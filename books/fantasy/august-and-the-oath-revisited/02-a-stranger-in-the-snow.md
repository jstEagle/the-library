## 2. A Stranger in the Snow

The snow came down the way it always did above the treeline — sideways, mean, and without any interest in whether you lived or died. Lena Blackwood had walked the Widow's Stair in worse. She'd walked it with a broken rib and a bounty on her name and half a keg of Thornfell firewhisky strapped to her back while three of Vask's toll-men followed her trail like hounds. She knew this road the way she knew the lines of her own palm: every switchback, every wind-carve hollow where a body could shelter, every place the mountain dropped away into nothing.

So when she saw the shape lying across the high road at the third bend, she did not think *man*. She thought first: *obstruction*. Then: *trap*. Then, as the wind gusted and the shape shifted — a hand dragging itself an inch through the drift — she thought, with a sour twist of her stomach: *trouble*.

She crouched behind a shelf of granite and let the storm cover her while she counted. One figure. No horses. No movement but that slow, terrible crawl. The road east of him was empty, white, unbroken — which meant whoever had put him here had come from the west, her direction, and had already passed.

Or they were still coming.

Lena pulled her hood lower and told herself to keep walking. That was the courier's rule, older than the guild, older than the passes themselves: *the road gives you nothing, and everything it offers costs double.* A dying man on the Widow's Stair was not cargo. He was not coin. He was a weight, and weights were how couriers died — slowed down, dragged down, buried under someone else's emergency.

She stood up out of the lee of the rock anyway.

"Stupid," she muttered, and meant it, and went to him anyway.

* * *

He was younger than she'd expected, or maybe the blood made him look young, stripped down to something raw. His cloak had been grey once — ash-grey, the kind of undyed wool that soldiers wore when they didn't want to be seen against winter stone — but now it was mostly dark, frozen stiff at the hem where the blood had soaked and refrozen and soaked again. There were three wounds she could count without touching him: a long slice along his ribs, a puncture low in his side that bubbled faintly when he breathed, and something wrong about the way his left leg lay beneath him.

And there were boot prints. Many. Coming up the road behind him, already filling with snow, but recent — an hour old, maybe less. Riders who had dismounted to chase him into the rocks, then given up, then gone back for their horses. Which meant they would be riding around by the switchbacks, and the switchbacks met this road again two miles west.

Two miles. In this weather, with a dying man, that was four hours if she was lucky.

"You're not dead yet," she said, kneeling beside him. "That's inconvenient for both of us."

His eyes opened. They were pale, almost colourless, and they found her face with a focus that startled her — no fever-glaze, no drifting. He looked at her the way a man looks at the last door in a corridor.

"Blackwood," he said.

The word went through her like a splinter. She hadn't given him a name. Her cloak bore no sigil; the Thornfell badge had been sewn inside the collar years ago, where only another courier would know to look.

"How do you know that name?"

"Your grandmother," he rasped. Each word cost him visibly, breath fogging and failing. "Maren Blackwood. Carried for the guild forty years. I'm sorry — I looked for anyone else. There wasn't time."

"Maren Blackwood has been dead eleven years." Lena's voice came out flat and cold, and she was grateful for it, because underneath the flatness something was moving that she didn't want to look at. Her grandmother's name, spoken by a stranger bleeding out on her road, in a voice that sounded like he'd known her.

"I know." His mouth moved into something that might have been a smile. "I went to her grave first. It's why they found me."

Wind screamed down the pass. Somewhere below and behind them — east, the way he must have come — Lena heard it: a horn. One long note, flattened by the storm, more felt than heard. Then silence. Then, fainter, a second horn answering from further down the mountain.

Riders. Coordinating. Closing a net.

The man's hand shot out and caught her wrist, and even dying his grip was strong enough to bruise. "Listen to me. They can't take it off me. Do you understand? If they take it from my body, they win. Everything — everyone who died getting it this far — for nothing."

"Take what?" But she could see the shape of it now, pressed flat against his chest under the ruined cloak: a leather case, long and narrow, sealed with wax that had been stamped and then deliberately smeared.

"The map." He fumbled at the case with fingers gone clumsy from cold. "It goes west. Over the Sunder, down into the Vale of Nine Rivers. There's a woman there — a keeper — she'll know what it is when she sees your face."

"My face?"

"Your blood." He got the case free at last and pushed it against her chest, and she took it without deciding to, her hands closing around it because some instinct older than judgment told her that letting it fall into the snow was unthinkable. It was lighter than she expected. Warm, somehow, though that had to be his body heat. Had to be.

"I'm not a keeper," she said. "I'm a courier. I carry things. I don't carry causes."

"Then carry it as cargo." Blood flecked his lips when he laughed, a dry, awful sound. "Charge yourself whatever you like. But swear it, Blackwood. Swear it the way your guild swears — on the road, on the passes, on the last honest thing left in these mountains. Swear you'll carry it west and never open it and never let it be taken."

"I don't make oaths for strangers."

"You're right not to." His eyes held hers, and there was something in them that frightened her more than the horns did — not desperation. Certainty. This was a man who had already done all his arithmetic and accepted the total. "But I've seen what happens when this reaches the wrong hands. I watched a city burn for it. Not sacked — *burned*, all at once, from the inside out, like the mountain exhaled. Three hundred years the stone holds, and everyone forgets what it's holding, and everyone starts thinking they're clever." His breath hitched, wet and ragged. "You have to be clever later. First you have to be stubborn."

Another horn, closer now. And under it, threading through the wind, the sound she dreaded most: hooves on stone. Rhythmic, patient, more than one horse.

Lena looked down the road toward the sound, then back at the man. "They'll find you either way."

"They'll find a body." He was already working himself backward, dragging his ruined leg, wedging his shoulders into the crevice between two slabs of fallen rock where the snow drifted deep. "Help me pack the wound with snow. Slow the bleeding. I bought myself an hour with the leg — told them I couldn't go far. They'll want to take me alive. That's their mistake. Take yours somewhere else."

"Why me?" The question tore out of her before she could stop it, raw as the wind. "There are a hundred couriers—"

"There are nine," he said quietly. "I counted. Corvan's guild is nine people and a debt, and half of them would sell this case before it crossed the first river. I spent a month choosing. Your grandmother's letters chose for me, in the end." His colourless eyes didn't waver. "Every Blackwood woman for three hundred years, Lena. Ask her letters why. When you're safe, ask them why."

"I don't have any letters."

"No," he agreed, and smiled again, and this time it looked almost gentle. "Not yet."

The hoofbeats were close enough now that she could hear the shingle grinding under the shoes. Minutes. Maybe less.

He caught her hand and pressed the leather case into it a final time, folding her fingers closed over the wax seal. His hands were freezing. "Swear it. Just the words. You don't have to mean them yet. Meaning comes later — that's what oaths are *for*."

Everything in her — the guild training, the courier's rules, the hard little economy of survival she'd built her whole life inside — said *drop it and run*. Run east, down the goat track to Millbrook, vanish into the low country, let the mountains keep their own dead.

But she knelt there holding a stranger's warmth through leather, and she thought of her grandmother's grave, dug small and plain on the south slope where Maren Blackwood had asked to be laid facing the passes. Facing the roads she'd walked her whole life. And Lena understood, with a grief that arrived all at once and fully formed, that she had been lonely for years without ever naming it — that the guild was a hearth going cold room by room, and that this dying fool with his colourless eyes was the first person in longer than she could remember to say her name like it mattered.

"On the road," she heard herself say. "On the passes. On the last honest thing left in these mountains. I swear to carry it west. I swear never to open it. I swear it will not be taken while I draw breath."

Something in the man's face loosened — pain and relief arriving together, indistinguishable. "Good," he whispered. "Good. Now go. West. Don't stop for weather, don't stop for wounds, don't stop for anything with a human face asking nicely. The keeper's name is Sera Ilvane. Tell her—" A cough took him, deep and terrible, and when it passed he was greyer than the wool of his cloak. "Tell her the August line is ended. She'll understand."

"The August line? What does that—"

"Go." He shoved her, weakly, with both hands. "They're taking me alive because they think I'll talk. Let them think it. Every hour they spend with a corpse that won't confess is an hour you're over the Sunder."

Lena stood. Snow filled her hood, her collar, her boots. Down the road, through the white curtain, she could see them now — shapes resolving out of the storm, riders in ash-grey cloaks, five of them, moving at that unhurried pace of hunters who believe the quarry is spent.

The wounded man saw them too. He settled back into the rocks, arranged his cloak over the leather case's absence, and looked up at her one last time.

"What's your name?" she asked. It seemed suddenly obscene that she was carrying a man's life's work and didn't know what to call him.

"Aldric," he said. "Tell the story however you like. But tell it true."

Then he turned his face to the storm and closed his eyes, and became, with a discipline that hollowed her out to watch, just another shape in the snow.

* * *

She ran.

Not along the road — the road belonged to the riders now — but up, onto the knife-back ridge above it that only couriers used, the one with the drop on the left side that killed two of Corvan's best in the winter Lena was sixteen. The wind tried to peel her off the mountain with every step. The case rode inside her coat, bound tight with her scarf, and through the wool she could feel it against her sternum like a second heartbeat. Warm. Still warm. That was impossible, and she made herself stop thinking about it.

At the ridge's shoulder she allowed herself one look back.

Below, the riders had reached Aldric. They'd dismounted — she could see the horses stamping, steam rising off them — and gathered around the rocks in a loose ring, careful, wary, the way men gather around something they've been warned about. One of them knelt. Another raised a hand, and even at this distance, even through the storm, Lena saw the gesture for what it was: someone working magic, quiet and controlled, probing.

The kneeling rider straightened. Turned his head — not toward the body, but upward, along the ridge, directly at the place where she stood.

Even at two hundred yards, even blind in the screaming snow, she felt the look land on her like a hand on the back of her neck.

Lena dropped off the ridge's far side and ran, and did not look back again.

* * *

She made the Sunder crossing at dusk, or what passed for dusk inside the storm — that hour when the light simply gave up and quit. The rope bridge over the gorge swayed and snapped like a living thing, and she crossed it with the case strapped to her back and her teeth clenched so hard her jaw ached, and on the far side she kept walking because stopping meant thinking and thinking meant feeling and feeling was a luxury with five riders somewhere behind her.

It was full dark when she finally found the hollow she'd used a dozen times before — a wind-carved pocket beneath an overhang, big enough for one, invisible unless you already knew. She got a fire going small enough to hide in her cupped hands, melted snow, forced herself to eat. Her body shook with cold and reaction, and she let it shake, because fighting it cost more than it saved.

The case sat against her knee.

She had sworn not to open it. She had sworn it on the road, on the passes, on the last honest thing left in the mountains — words she'd said a hundred times over cargo and never once meant like this. The oath sat in her chest alongside the case, heavy, warm, impossible.

*Aldric*. A dead man's name now, or near enough. *Tell the story however you like. But tell it true.*

"Idiot," she said aloud, and wasn't sure if she meant him or herself.

She turned the case over in her hands. The wax seal was smeared, deliberate, anonymous — but beneath the smear, where her thumb found it by accident, there was an impression too deep to destroy. A circle. And inside the circle, a mountain with a crack running through its heart.

Her grandmother had drawn that mark once, in the margin of a recipe book, in flour-dusted ink, when Lena was small enough to sit on the counter and steal dried plums. *What's that, Gran?* And her grandmother had gone very still, and wiped the page clean with her apron, and said, *Nothing, love. An old mason's sign. Forget you saw it.*

Lena sat in the dark under the overhang with the storm howling and the fire guttering, holding a dead man's map against the ghost of her grandmother's voice, and understood with perfect clarity that her life had just divided into before and after.

West, then. Over the mountains, down into the Vale, to a woman named Sera Ilvane who would know her face — her *blood* — on sight.

Behind her, far to the east and below, a horn sounded one last time across the passes. Long. Flat. Patient.

They knew she was out here. Or they would, soon enough.

Lena banked the fire, wrapped her cloak around her, and put the case where she could feel it against her heart. Sleep came slowly, and when it came, it brought her grandmother standing at the edge of a field of ash, saying something she couldn't quite hear.

She woke before dawn and walked west.
