## 4. The Guildmaster's Summons

The summons came at dusk, carried by a boy with frostbitten ears and a guild seal pressed into soft wax that had already begun to crack in the cold.

Lena read it twice by the light of the safehouse's single tallow candle, and both times her eyes snagged on the same phrase, written in Corvan's precise, upright hand: *Come alone. Come now. Bring the stranger's effects.*

*Effects.* Not *the man* — though surely word of a dying courier on the high road had reached him by now; nothing moved through the Thornfells without Corvan knowing. Not *the map*, though any reasonable guildmaster would have asked for it first. *Effects.* As if the stranger were already dead. As if whatever he'd carried had already been sorted into parcels, weighed, and entered into the guild ledger like so much contraband salt.

She looked across the room. The stranger — Aldric, he'd finally gasped out, before fever took him back under — lay on the cot beneath her spare cloak, his breathing shallow but steady, each rise of his chest a small victory against the arrow wound in his side. She'd drawn the shaft out an hour after finding him, packed the wound with moss and yarrow, and stitched it closed with the same waxed thread she used to mend boot leather. It was ugly work. He was alive anyway.

The bloodstained map sat rolled inside her shirt, against her skin, where it had lived since the high road. She hadn't taken it off since. She slept with it. She was beginning to suspect she always would.

"Come alone," she murmured to the empty room. "Come now."

Outside, the wind worried at the shutters, and somewhere below in the valley, faint as memory, dogs were barking. They had been barking since morning. Ash-grey riders had passed through the low village two days ago asking questions, and last night she'd seen firelight on the western ridge that wasn't any shepherd's hearth. The Court was looking for something. The Court was looking for *him*.

And Corvan wanted his effects.

Lena folded the summons into quarters, then eighths, until it was a hard little square she could have swallowed. She didn't swallow it. She tucked it into her belt beside her knife, because some part of her — the part that had spent twenty-two years learning that paper outlasts flesh — understood that this letter would matter later. Letters from guildmasters always mattered later. That was how they worked. That was how *he* worked.

She crossed to the cot and stood over Aldric for a long moment. In sleep his face lost the desperate urgency of the road and became simply what it was: a man perhaps ten years older than her, hollow-cheeked, grey-stubbled, with a scar through one eyebrow and hands that even unconscious seemed curled around the ghost of reins. A courier's hands. She knew those hands. She'd seen them every morning of her childhood in the mirror of her mother's washbasin.

"You're going to be the death of me," she told him quietly. "You know that."

His breathing did not change. But something in the room seemed to lean closer, listening — the shadows in the corners, the candle flame bending sideways without any draft. Lena shook off the feeling, pulled her hood up, and went out into the dark.

* * *

Corvan kept his quarters above the counting house in Hollowbridge, which told you everything you needed to know about the man. Other guildmasters of the old line had held court in taverns, in wayhouses, in the open air of the high passes where the wind could carry their voices to every ear that mattered. Corvan held court over ledgers. He believed, and had said so often enough, that a guild died not when its couriers stopped running but when its accounts stopped balancing.

Lena climbed the outside stair with snow squeaking under her boots and counted the lit windows. Two. His study and the archive below it. No guards posted on the landing, which meant either that he trusted no threat to reach him or that whatever he intended required no witnesses. With Corvan, those had always been the same thing.

She knocked once.

"Enter, Blackwood."

The study smelled of ink, tallow, and the particular dry rot of old vellum — a smell Lena had loved once, when it had meant safety, contracts honored, debts forgiven. Now it just smelled old. Corvan sat behind his desk with a single lamp turned low, and the light caught him the way he liked to be caught: silver at the temples, jaw clean-shaven, rings on three fingers, the guild signet heavy on his thumb. Fifty years old and built like the door of a strongroom. He had taught her to read a trail at nine. He had taught her to hold a lie behind her teeth at eleven. He had carried her father's body down from Whitecap Pass at fourteen, and she had never once asked him what her father's face had looked like at the end, because Corvan had volunteered nothing and she had known better than to ask.

That was the shape of their whole history: things given, things withheld, all of it balanced in a ledger neither of them ever opened aloud.

"Lena." He said her name the way he said *invoice*. "Sit."

She sat. The chair across from the desk was deliberately lower than his own — an old trick, and she'd known it was a trick since she was twelve, and she sat in it anyway, because tonight she needed him comfortable.

"You found a man on the high road." Not a question. "Two days past. Near the cairns above Wolfjaw Col."

"You have eyes everywhere."

"I have *bills* everywhere," Corvan said. "There is a difference, and the difference is why this guild still exists while the others are selling kindling. A shepherd saw you carrying a man down from the col. A shepherd sold me the sight of it for two bits of silver and a promise about his son's apprenticeship." He laced his ringed fingers together on the desktop. "Tell me about him."

"There isn't much to tell. He'd taken an arrow through the ribs. Riders in ash-grey cloaks chasing him. I brought him to the safehouse and I've been keeping him alive."

"And?"

"And what?"

"*Effects,* Blackwood." Corvan's voice stayed level, pleasant, almost bored — and that was the first wrong note, the first hair-thin crack in the glaze. Guildmasters who were genuinely bored did not summon couriers at dusk in winter. "What did he carry? Papers? Seals? Coin?"

"A knife. Boots. The clothes he was freezing to death in."

The pause that followed lasted exactly long enough to be measured. Lena had grown up measuring Corvan's pauses the way other children learned psalms. This one was too long, and at the end of it he smiled, and the smile was the smile he used on debtors he'd decided to ruin gently.

"He gave you nothing else. A man runs three days ahead of the Ashen Court's own riders, takes an arrow meant for his spine, crawls through a blizzard rather than turn back — and he carries nothing worth the chase."

"He was delirious. People say things when they're dying. Half of it's fever."

"What did he say, Lena?"

And there it was. Her name again, but softer this time, worn smooth, the voice he'd used when she was small enough to sit on the floor of this very study and practice her letters while he worked late. He was reaching for the girl she had been, and they both knew it, and he was doing it anyway, because it had always worked.

Lena felt the map against her sternum like a second heartbeat.

She had lied before, of course. Every courier lied — to toll-keepers, to tax-men, to jealous husbands and suspicious sergeants. But those lies were tools, clean and disposable, part of the trade the way lockpicks were part of the trade. She had never lied to Corvan. In eleven years of service, through botched runs and lost cargo and the worst winter in a generation, she had never once put a false thing between herself and this desk. It wasn't loyalty, exactly. It was architecture. He was one of the load-bearing walls of her world, and you did not carve your name into a wall just to see if it would hold.

"He talked about a delivery," she heard herself say. "West. Something he had to get to someone past the Sundered Ford. He wouldn't say what. Kept calling it *the weight.* Just — the weight. Like it was too heavy to name."

It was a good lie. It was built from true materials — Aldric *had* said those things, feverish, half-conscious, pressing the map into her bleeding hands with words she still couldn't repeat without her throat tightening. She had simply left out the only part that mattered. A lie made of ninety percent truth. Corvan had taught her that construction himself, years ago, over a card table: *"Never invent, child. Omit. An invented lie breaks under pressure. An omission bends."*

He nodded slowly, and for one absurd moment she thought — *it worked, it bent, we're through* —

Then he said, "Show me the body."

"The—"

"The man. The wounded man. If he's carrying a delivery west, the delivery is on him or near him, and I want to see it with my own eyes before the Court burns another village looking for it." He rose, unhurried, and began pulling his riding cloak from its peg, and the lamplight slid across his face and for just a moment Lena saw past the silver temples and the strongroom jaw to the thing underneath, and the thing underneath was *hungry.*

Not ambitious. Ambition she knew; ambition was the guild's daily bread, ambition was Corvan negotiating tolls and territory and the slow salvage of a dying trade. This was different. This was the look of a man standing at a locked door he had spent his whole life walking past, hearing, for the first time, something moving on the other side.

"They're calling it the Oathstone," Corvan said quietly, turning his back to her to fasten his cloak. "You've heard the fireside stories. Everyone has. Three hundred years buried under the mountains, and now ash-cloaks are riding our passes in winter, burning waystations, paying shepherds for gossip. Something has come loose, Lena. And when something that old comes loose, there are only two kinds of people afterward: the ones who held it, and the ones who held the power." He shrugged the cloak straight. "I have spent thirty years holding this guild together with spit and ledger-lines while the world forgot us. I am owed the second kind."

The candle flame leaned. Somewhere below them, in the archive, a shutter banged.

"Show me the body," he said again, "and we'll speak of this as partners. You'll want a partner in what's coming. Everyone will."

Every instinct Lena owned was screaming the same word, and the word was *no* — no, don't take him there, don't show him the door, don't let this man stand in the same room as a sleeping stranger and a hidden map, because she had just watched hunger move behind his face and hunger did not partner, hunger *consumed.* But refusal had its own price. Refusal confirmed. Refusal told him the omission was a lie, and Corvan did not forgive lies; he collected them, filed them, and presented them when they would do the most damage.

So she did what couriers did. She bought time with terrain.

"He's feverish and torn open," she said. "If you ride up tonight you'll find him dead by morning, and then whatever he knows dies with him, and the Court gets what it wants for free. Give me two days. Let me keep him alive, and when he can talk sense, I'll bring you everything — him, the delivery, all of it. Partners." She made herself smile. "You taught me never to deliver damaged goods."

Something flickered across Corvan's face — she would spend many nights afterward trying to name it, and would fail. It might have been pride. It might have been disappointment. It might have been the exact moment a man decides that a tool he has relied upon for eleven years has become an obstacle.

"Two days," he said.

"Two days."

"And Blackwood—" She had reached the door. She stopped, hand on the latch, not turning. "The shepherd also mentioned you haven't taken off your riding coat in two days. Even to sleep, the old woman says. Even to sleep." A pause, mild as milk. "Cold winter."

"Freezing up there," Lena said. "Goodnight, Guildmaster."

She went down the outside stair with her heart hammering against the map, against the vellum, against the secret that had suddenly become a target painted on her own chest. At the bottom she paused in the shadow of the counting house and looked back up. Corvan stood in his lit window, motionless, watching the street — and as she watched, he turned away from the window, toward the interior of the room, toward the desk, and lifted something.

A horn. Small, black, banded in silver.

Lena knew that horn. Every courier in the Thornfells knew that horn. It was the guild's oldest relic, older than the guild itself, carried by the first masters in the wars nobody talked about. It hung above Corvan's desk as decoration, as heritage, as proof that the Thornfell Guild had once mattered.

It was not supposed to come down off its peg.

She stood in the snow and watched his silhouette bend over the desk, and she thought of ash-grey cloaks and burned waystations and a shepherd bought for two bits of silver, and a cold new understanding settled into her, quiet and complete as a stone dropped into a well:

Someone had told the Court about the high road. Someone had told them where to aim their arrows. And the man who had taught her to omit rather than invent — the man whose pauses she could read like scripture — had just picked up the oldest horn in the mountains, and horns were for *calling.*

Lena pulled her hood low and walked out of Hollowbridge without looking back, and behind her, faint on the night wind, she thought she heard — though it might have been only the wind, though it might have been only the cold playing tricks — the sound of a door opening below the counting house, into the archive, where the oldest papers were kept.

By dawn she would be gone. By dawn she would understand that there was nowhere in the Thornfells left to hide, because the guildmaster had already begun to sell the passes — pass by pass, courier by courier, starting with the only one who had ever trusted him completely.

She just didn't know it yet.
