## 6. The Smuggler's Road West

The pass called the Shepherd's Needle did not appear on any map the Ashen Court had ever confiscated, because it did not exist until Lena decided it did.

She led Kael off the high road at a place where a rockfall looked like a rockfall, scrambled him down through a crack barely wider than his shoulders, and brought him up into a hanging valley where the morning fog pooled like spilled milk. Behind them, faint and far below, a horn sounded — the Court's patrol checking the road they'd abandoned in the dark.

"They'll find the trail splits here," Lena said, crouching to brush pine needles over the scuff of Kael's boot. "Three ways out of this valley, if you trust maps. Six, if you trust me."

"And which do I trust?"

She glanced up at him. He was crouched across from her, ash-grey cloak traded for a shepherd's smock she'd stolen from a drying line, his dark hair tied back with twine. He looked like exactly what he wasn't. That was useful. That was also the problem.

"Neither," she said. "Trust the route. It doesn't care what either of us wants."

She moved, and he followed. That was the arrangement, and it held for three days.

* * *

The Thornfells in high summer were a country of contradictions: snow in the shadowed couloirs, wildflowers rioting on the southern faces, meltwater roaring in every gully like the mountains arguing with themselves. Lena knew all of it the way other people knew their own hands. She took Kael down the Hollow Stair — a courier-cut channel in the cliff face, hidden behind a curtain of falling water — and across the Widow's Span, a fallen fir bridging a gorge so deep the river at its bottom was only a sound.

"You're enjoying this," Kael said on the second morning, watching her walk a knife-edge ridge without looking down.

"No."

"You're smiling."

"I'm remembering a woman who used to smile on this ridge. I'm deciding whether to be her." She kept walking. "Keep your eyes on your feet, Court. This ridge has killed better-balanced men than you."

"I defected," he said mildly. "You could use my name."

"I could use a lot of things. A hot meal. A night without one eye open." She stopped at the ridge's end and turned, and for a moment the wind pushed her braid back off her face and she looked, he thought, unbearably young for someone carrying a kingdom's worth of buried war in a satchel. "Your name doesn't change what you were."

"No," he agreed. "It doesn't. But it changes what I'm asking to be."

She didn't answer. She led him down.

* * *

The first campfire was a necessity, not a truce — though Lena built it in a hollow beneath an overhang, screened on three sides, smoke channeled through a crack in the stone the way couriers had done for a hundred years. Cold camp, warm hands. Kael watched her work with the focused attention of a man cataloguing weapons.

"You're memorizing my tradecraft," she said.

"I'm memorizing you. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"For me there is. For the Court, no. They taught us the same lesson: know a person's habits and you own them." He fed a twig to the flames. "I'm trying to unlearn which half of that is true."

Lena pulled the map from her satchel — she'd taken to sleeping with it against her ribs — and turned it toward the firelight without quite showing him the whole of it. "Then unlearn this. Why did you really intercept me on the pass? You had a crossbow. You had the high ground. You could have ended this in ten seconds and been a hero of the Court."

The fire cracked. Kael was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer.

"Because I've seen the vault beneath the Citadel of Ash," he said finally. "Not the throne room — the vault under it. Where the Court keeps the records no one is allowed to read. I was a records-warden for six years. Do you know what I found there, in a sealed folio older than the Court itself?"

"Tell me."

"A casualty ledger. From the last war. Three hundred years old." He looked at her, and the firelight made his face flicker between the defector and the man he'd been. "Lena, the ledger doesn't record an army being imprisoned. It records a *choice being kept*. The founders didn't seal an enemy under the mountain. They sealed something they couldn't kill and couldn't carry — and then they built an entire order on making sure no one ever asked what."

"So you believe the legend."

"I believe the Court is afraid of a map drawn by a dying man. That's enough for me." He leaned forward. "Your turn. Why carry it? You could burn it in any one of these gorges and be free of it by morning. The Court loses, Corvan loses, you go back to being a smuggler of ordinary secrets."

The question landed harder than he probably knew. Lena fed the fire and let the silence do some of the work.

"A man bled out in my arms on the high road," she said at last. "He had maybe a minute left, and he spent it not on a prayer, not on a name, but on making me swear. *Carry it west.* You know what that's like? Being the last person someone trusts with the last thing they have?"

"Yes," Kael said quietly. "That's why I'm here."

She looked at him across the fire. For a moment neither of them blinked.

"Sleep," she said. "First watch is mine."

* * *

On the fourth day she took him through the Undercut, a lava tube from some ancient burning that couriers had used for generations — half a mile of black stone where they walked by touch and by the phosphorescent moss the guild cultivated in clay pots set into the walls. Their footsteps echoed wrong, doubled, as if the mountain were repeating them back.

"You know what I can't figure," Kael said, his voice low in the dark. "You hate the guild. You've said so — the debts, the dying routes, Corvan hoarding what's left. So why do you love these roads so much?"

"I don't love the roads."

"You walk them like a prayer."

She stopped. The moss-light turned her face green and ghostly. "My grandmother taught me these. Every crack, every hidden stair. She'd walk me through them in August, when the passes were open, and she'd tell me the mountain was a house, and couriers were the ones who remembered all the rooms." Her hand rested on the stone wall, briefly. "She's dead. The guild is dying. Corvan is selling the rooms one by one. But the house is still here, and as long as I'm walking it, somebody remembers." She started forward again. "That's not love. That's stubbornness."

"In my experience," Kael said, "those are the same thing."

* * *

The second fire was in a ruined waystation chapel, roofless, its saint's niche empty except for pigeon nests. They risked a real flame because rain was coming down in ropes and no patrol would fly in it. Lena cooked barley and dried venison; Kael produced, from somewhere in his stolen shepherd's clothes, a twist of salt and a handful of dried plums, and she looked at him with genuine offense.

"You've been holding out."

"Courier's rule?"

"Smuggler's rule. You share the route, you share the food."

"So share the route, then." He pushed the plums across. "The real one. Not the courier's version. Why does a woman who resents her legacy carry it like scripture?"

Lena ate a plum and considered not answering. The rain drummed on the ruined roof. Somewhere in the dark, the mountain groaned — thermal shift, nothing more, but it made the silence after feel deliberate.

"Because my grandmother lied to me," she said finally. "All those bedtime warnings about the Oathstone — *never west of the river, never speak its name, the mountain sleeps and we must not wake it* — I thought they were stories. Superstition. Guild folklore to keep children off the high passes." She turned the map over in her hands. "Then a dying stranger hands me this, and every mark on it matches something she taught me. Every one. The Hollow Stair. The Undercut. The waystations. It's not a smuggler's map, Kael. It's a *family's* map. Which means my grandmother knew. Which means every Blackwood woman for three hundred years knew — and chose the bedtime stories instead of the truth."

"That angers you."

"It *terrifies* me. Because if she lied to protect me, then the truth is something a person needs protecting from. And I'm walking straight at it." She looked up. "Your turn, records-warden. What did the Court take from you? Nobody defects over a ledger."

The rain filled the pause. Kael turned his hands toward the fire, and Lena saw, for the first time, the faded scar across his palm — a thin white line, old, deliberate-looking.

"When I was nineteen, I was assigned to the Ashen Guard's escort detail," he said. "We escorted a cartographer. Old man, half-blind, kept in a tower. He'd spent forty years drawing the mountains, and the Court let him because his maps were wrong in ways they liked — roads that didn't exist, passes that were closed. Misdirection, carved into parchment." He smiled without humor. "One night he asked me to help him with his ink. I did. And while I ground it, he talked. He told me he'd once drawn the true map. The real passes, the real stone, everything. And the Court burned everything he'd ever made and blinded his teachers, and kept him alive only because a dead mapmaker is a martyr and a live one is a leash."

"What happened to him?"

"The escort detail happened. Orders came down — he'd become a liability. We were told it would be a quiet transfer." His jaw tightened. "It wasn't a transfer. I was nineteen and I followed orders, and I have spent eleven years learning what that makes me. The man who gave me the map on your high road — the one who died — he was the cartographer's son. I recognized his work before I recognized his face."

Lena stared at him. The rain, the fire, the ruined chapel — all of it receded a little, the way a room does when something true is said in it.

"You've been following this map longer than I have."

"I've been following my own mistake longer than I have. The map is just where it leads." He met her eyes. "I'm not asking you to trust me, Lena. I'm asking you to watch me. Trust is what's left when the watching runs out."

She was quiet for a long moment. Then she pulled the blanket from her pack, tore it in half, and threw one half at his chest.

"Watch this," she said. "First watch is yours. Wake me at moonset. If I wake up and you're gone, I will find you. I found a smuggler in the Salt Wastes who'd been missing for two years, and I found him *on purpose*."

"I believe you," Kael said, and the terrible thing, she thought, was that he sounded like he did.

* * *

They crossed the river at the Ford of Names on the sixth day, where the water ran shallow and bright over red stones, and on the far bank Lena allowed herself, for the first time, to look back east. The Thornfells stood in their summer haze, blue and vast and indifferent. Somewhere behind them, the Court was mobilizing. Somewhere closer, Corvan was selling her name.

"You're saying goodbye to something," Kael observed.

"I'm saying *not yet* to something. There's a difference." She turned west. "The gorge is two days on. After that, the waystations end and we're in country nobody knows — not even me."

"And after the gorge?"

"After the gorge, we find out what my grandmother was protecting me from."

They walked. The mountains fell away behind them by degrees, and the land opened into foothills gold with late grass, and on the evening of the seventh day they crested a ridge and saw the waystation of Millbrook in the valley below — or what had been Millbrook.

Even from the ridge, Lena could see the smoke still rising.

The waystation's roof was gone. The outbuildings were black skeletons. And the fields around it, which should have been bright with summer workers, were scattered with figures moving slowly, sorting through ash.

Kael went very still beside her. "That's not a raid," he said quietly. "Raids take. That's a *message*."

Lena's hand found the map through her satchel, over her heart. "They knew we were coming," she said. "Kael — nobody knew we were coming. Nobody *could* have."

Below them, in the ruins of Millbrook, something burned on.
