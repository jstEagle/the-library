## 7. Ashes at Millbrook

Kael smelled the smoke before he saw the village.

It drifted down the valley on the morning wind — not the sweet grey of hearth-fires banked for dawn, but the bitter reek of wet thatch and burned grain, of things smoldering that had no business burning. He stopped on the ridge with his hand out flat behind him, and Lena, a half-dozen paces back and three days wiser about reading his silences, halted without being told.

"Millbrook," she said.

"You know it?"

"Every courier knows it. Last warm bed between the Thornfells and the western passes." Her voice was level, but Kael had learned in three days of hard travel to hear what lived underneath her levelness. Right now it was dread. "The Harrow family keeps it. Kept it. Old Marta Harrow fed every runner who ever came through cold and hungry. She fed me when I was nine years old and lost my first route in a snowstorm."

He looked at her profile against the pale sky — the jaw set too tight, the eyes fixed on the valley floor where a smear of dark smoke rose from behind the tree line. He knew what she was doing. She was cataloguing everything she might lose, the way soldiers did before a battle, so that when the losses came they would already have been grieved once.

Kael knew that habit intimately. He had invented it, or something like it, in another life.

"We go around," he said.

"No."

"Lena—"

"If Marta's alive, she needs help. If she's dead, I need to see it." She was already moving down through the pines, quick and quiet, her boots finding the silent places in the snow the way only mountain-born people could. "And if you're worried about being seen — nobody looks twice at two travelers walking into a burned village to help dig. That's the one thing ash-cloaks never understand about ordinary people. We run toward smoke."

*We.* She said it easily now, and it still caught somewhere in his chest like a fishhook. Three days ago she had held a knife to his throat on the high pass and asked him one question: *Why shouldn't I kill you where you stand?* He had answered honestly — *because you'd be doing the Court's work for them* — and something in the honesty had bought him this: a fire shared, a road shared, a pronoun shared. Not trust. Trust would take longer. But the pronoun was a start.

He followed her down.

* * *

Millbrook had been a small place — thirty roofs, a mill with its wheel turning brown water into flour, a waystation house painted the deep green of the Thornfell Guild because the guild had kept it for four generations. Now the mill wheel hung black and split. The waystation's roof had fallen inward, and its walls stood charred like the ribs of some great animal picked clean. Snow fell gently into the ruin, hissing where it touched coals still alive beneath the wreckage, and the whole village smelled of endings.

Survivors moved among the ashes like ghosts who hadn't yet been told they were dead. A woman Kael didn't recognize was pulling nails from a collapsed beam, one by one, stacking them with terrible care as though nails were what mattered now. Two children sat on a stone wall, watching the smoke with the blank patience of children who had learned overnight that the world could take things away.

An old woman knelt in the mud near the well, and Lena made a sound low in her throat and broke into a run.

"Marta!"

The old woman looked up. Her face was seamed with soot and one cheek was bandaged with linen gone yellow-brown at the edges. For a moment she stared at Lena without recognition, and then her whole body changed — she reached up with both hands, gripping Lena's forearms, pulling herself upright as though the girl were a rope thrown to a drowning woman.

"Blackwood's girl. Oh — oh, child, they came asking after *you*."

The words dropped into the ruined street like stones into a well. Kael saw them land. He saw Lena go very still under the old woman's hands, and he saw the survivors nearby turn their heads — slowly, all together, the way a flock of birds turns — and look at the two strangers standing in their ashes.

"When?" Lena asked. Her voice had gone careful. "How long ago?"

"Four days. Before first light." Marta's grip tightened. "They rode up the west road with their banners wrapped so you couldn't see the sigil, but we knew them anyway. Everyone knows them now. Their captain walked into my kitchen and put a paper on my table and said, *Two travelers. A woman of the Thornfell Guild and a man who used to wear our grey. They will come here, because this is where the guild always sleeps.*"

Kael felt the world tilt slightly, the way it did when ground he thought solid turned out to be thin ice.

"They described me," he said quietly. "By name?"

Marta looked at him — really looked, taking in the travel-worn clothes, the sword worn plain, the face she might have known from rumor alone. Something old and tired moved behind her eyes.

"Not your name, son. Your *face*. They had it drawn on the paper, good as a portraitist could do it. And hers beside it." She turned back to Lena. "I told them I'd seen no one. The captain smiled at me like I was a slow child and said, *That's all right, grandmother. We'll wait.* And they waited. Two days, camped on the west road, until word came from—" she faltered, "—from somewhere. A rider in guild colors brought them a message, and then they left. Burned the waystation on their way out. Said it was for lying to them."

"A rider in guild colors," Lena repeated.

"That's what I said."

The silence that followed had weight. Kael watched Lena's face do several things at once — disbelief fighting arithmetic, loyalty fighting the cold clear part of her mind that had survived twenty years of smuggling routes and knew how to count evidence. He recognized the fight because he was having it himself, in the same instant, on the other side of the ledger.

Because there was only one explanation that fit. The Court had known the route. Not guessed it — *known* it, known it precisely enough to set an ambush at the one waystation any Thornfell courier would use, known it with a portrait of Kael's own face drawn by someone who had seen that face recently enough to remember it. And the message that called off the ambush had come wearing guild colors, which meant someone inside the guild had been talking to the Court before the fire and after it both.

Someone inside the guild was selling them.

And Kael knew exactly who inside the Court had been able to draw his face.

* * *

They worked through the afternoon because there was nothing else to do with hands and grief. Kael hauled beams off the collapsed granary while Lena sat with Marta Harrow by the rebuilt cookfire — the villagers had dragged a brazier into the open air, refusing to light fires under any roof, and around it the survivors drank broth and spoke in the low voices of people rehearsing what they would tell each other for the rest of their lives.

Kael carried wood. He listened.

"The captain," Marta was saying, "had a way of talking like the burning was a kindness. Like he was sorry it had to be done, and someday we'd thank him. Twenty years I've fed every ragged runner the guild ever sent over that pass, and he talked to me like I was kindling." She spat into the fire. "But it wasn't him that frightened me most. It was the rider. Guild green, guild pin, rode right up to the ash-cloaks' fire like he'd done it a hundred times. Young fellow. Soft hands. He handed over the letter and the captain read it and laughed, and the rider laughed too, and then the rider said something I'll carry to my grave."

Lena's spoon had stopped moving. "What did he say?"

"He said, *Tell your matriarch the guildmaster keeps his word.*"

The fire popped. Somewhere behind Kael a beam settled in the ruins with a groan. He kept his face still and his hands moving, stacking the split wood, because he had spent eleven years learning to keep his face still, and in those eleven years he had heard a hundred sentences that rearranged the world, and this was one of them.

*Corvan.* It had to be Corvan. The guildmaster who had raised Lena, whose name she spoke with the complicated tenderness people reserve for fathers who were also masters. Kael had never met the man, but he had built a picture of him from three days of Lena's stories: shrewd, weathered, loyal to the guild the way mountains are loyal to their own stone. And now that picture was burning the same way Millbrook had burned, and he was going to have to watch Lena watch it burn.

Except — no. He made himself stop. Made himself apply the discipline the Court had beaten into him and then starved out of him, the discipline of *verify before you believe*, because the Court had taught him that lesson with whips and he had never forgotten it. One sentence from a traumatized old woman. A rider in guild colors. It fit Corvan, yes. It fit perfectly. And it fit perfectly because it was designed to fit perfectly, because the Court's intelligence service did not survive three centuries by sending messages that couldn't be misread.

Or because it was true, said a colder voice, the voice that sounded like his old handler, Vessane. *You were one of theirs, Kael. You know how thorough they are. You know they don't need a traitor in the guild — they need one rumor, placed well, and the couriers will tear themselves apart.*

He didn't know which truth was worse: that Lena's guildmaster had sold her, or that the Court wanted Kael to believe he had, because a courier who trusted no one delivered nothing.

Across the fire, Lena was asking Marta one more question, and her voice had changed — flattened, professional, the smuggler's voice. "Which direction did the rider come from? Think carefully. Was there frost on his cloak, or dust?"

"Frost, child. North and east. From the high passes."

From the direction of Thornfell Hall. From home.

Lena stood up. She thanked Marta with a touch to the old woman's shoulder that seemed to cost her something enormous, and she crossed the trampled snow to where Kael stood with his arms full of wood, and she took the wood out of his arms and set it down, and looked at him.

"You heard."

"I heard."

"Say what you're thinking."

He almost lied. It rose in him smooth and ready, eleven years of Court training offering him the comfortable falsehood the way a tavern keeper offers the house wine. *Maybe it means nothing. Maybe it's a trick.* And then he remembered the high pass, the knife at his throat, the question — *why shouldn't I kill you* — and the answer that had bought him this road: *because you'd be doing the Court's work for them.* Honesty had been the only coin she'd accepted once. He wasn't going to debase it now.

"I'm thinking that whoever drew my face for that captain has seen me since I left the Court," he said. "I'm thinking there are perhaps six people alive who could do that drawing, and five of them are in the Court's fortress, and the sixth is a courier liaison named Dain Ferro who used to buy Court intelligence through me when I was still wearing the grey." He held her eyes. "I'm thinking that if the Court has a thread into the guild, it runs through contacts I built. Contacts I *vouched for*. And I'm thinking that everything burning in this valley may be my fault, Lena, no matter whose hand struck the match."

She was quiet for a long moment. Snow ticked softly on the brazier.

"Dain Ferro," she said at last. "I know the name. Corvan's favorite. He carries messages between the hall and the western routes." Her jaw worked. "You vouched for him."

"With my life. Which is why they'll have used him. Vouched contacts are the ones you burn first — it poisons the voucher." He heard the bitterness in his own voice and didn't try to sand it down. "This is what I was, Lena. This is what I built before I understood what I was building. You want to know why I defected? It wasn't one grand moment. It was a morning like this one. A village like this one. Ashes, and an old woman, and the understanding that my neat little network of informants had made it possible."

"And now you think it's happening again."

"I think the Court doesn't need to catch us," Kael said. "It needs us to distrust everyone who might shelter us. Every safehouse closed, every friendly face suspected, every old debt called in fear — and then we're alone on the mountain in winter, and winter does the rest." He glanced back at Marta Harrow, small and soot-streaked by her fire. "Your instinct was right, coming here. But from tonight, we can't sleep anywhere the guild has ever slept. Which means nowhere."

Lena looked at the burned waystation for a long time. When she spoke again, her voice was low and steady and more frightening than shouting would have been.

"Then we go to the one place the Court can't poison," she said. "We go find out the truth ourselves. Ferro runs the western circuit — he'll cross Staghorn Pass within the week, and Staghorn has a side trail no one uses but me." She turned to Kael, and in the firelight her eyes were the color of the ice on the high crags. "You're going to help me interrogate your own contact, Kael. And if he's guilty, you're going to tell me everything you know about how the Court runs its spies inside my guild. Every name. Every method. Starting with yours."

"My name?"

"How you were recruited. What they hold over you. Why a man walks away from the Ashen Court knowing they never let anyone walk away." She shouldered her pack. "Because someone in my life has been lying to me since the day that stranger died in the snow, and I need it to stop being everyone."

* * *

They left Millbrook at dusk, carrying two of Marta's loaves and a promise to send help when the roads were safe — a promise Kael suspected neither of them believed in, made anyway, because some promises were load-bearing. Behind them the village glimmered with its open-air fires, stubborn sparks against the gathering dark, and ahead the trail climbed toward Staghorn Pass under a sky going the deep bruised violet of early night.

Kael walked second, as he always did, watching Lena's back and the country beyond it, and he turned the day over in his mind like a stone with something underneath.

The Court had his face. Fine — he had assumed they would; he had planned for it. But the portrait detail gnawed at him worse than he'd admitted to Lena, because there was only one person in the Court's intelligence service who drew faces, and that was Vessane herself, and Vessane did not waste her own hand on a mere waystation ambush. She drew portraits for hunts she intended to finish personally. Which meant the ambush at Millbrook hadn't been the hunt. It had been the *herding* — pushing them west, pushing them toward Staghorn, toward whatever waited on the far side of it.

Unless it was simpler than that. Unless Corvan really had sold her, and the Court really was just hunting, and every cold calculation he made was only his old training looking for excuses to distrust the first person who had ever offered him a fire without wanting anything back.

The trail narrowed along a shelf of rock above the freezing river. Lena paused there, waiting for him, breath steaming, and for a moment the last light caught her face and he saw how exhausted she was beneath the iron — three days of flight, a burned childhood, a mentor suddenly suspect, and still she walked.

"Ferro crosses Staghorn within the week," she said. "So we move fast, and we sleep cold, and we trust no one but each other. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Kael said.

And then, because honesty was the only coin between them, he added the thing he had been carrying since the well:

"Lena. If it turns out the rot goes all the way up — if it's Corvan himself — what will you do?"

She looked north, toward the high passes, toward the hall where she had learned her trade and her pride, and the wind pulled a strand of hair across her face and she didn't brush it away.

"I'll ask him to his face," she said. "And then I'll decide whether he's a traitor or a prisoner. Because those are different problems, Kael, and I have a bad feeling the Court knows the difference better than we do."

She walked on. Kael followed, and behind them, far down the valley, a single rider in guild green crested the southern ridge and turned, unhurried, toward Staghorn Pass.
