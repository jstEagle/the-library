## 8. Corvan's Bargain

The rain found them two miles below the pass, and by then Lena had already made her first mistake.

She had taken them down through the Hollow Stair — a courier's path stitched into the cliffside above the Greywater, so narrow in places that Kael had to walk it with his back to the stone and his eyes fixed on her shoulders. It was a route no Ashen rider could follow and no Court map recorded, because no Court map was drawn by anyone who had ever been hungry enough to learn where the mountain kept its secrets. She should have felt safe on it. Instead she had spent the whole descent listening to the rain drum the slate ledges and thinking about Corvan's face when she'd lied to him — the way his hunger had sat behind his eyes like a lamp behind horn panes, bright and carefully shaded.

"You keep looking back," Kael said.

"I'm checking the weather."

"You're checking for riders."

"Same thing, this time of year." She said it lightly, and hated how easily the lie came now. Three days ago she had never lied to anyone who mattered. Now the words came out of her smooth as river stones, worn by use.

They came off the Stair at dusk and cut east along the Greywater toward Millbrook, where a waystation under the sign of the Crooked Antler would have beds, hot food, and — more precious than either — a guild board where messages moved between couriers without ever passing through Corvan's hands. Lena had used the board since she was sixteen. She trusted it the way she trusted rope: not because it couldn't fail, but because she'd tested it until failure seemed impossible.

That was the second mistake. Trusting anything as if it were rope.

* * *

Millbrook announced itself by smell before sight — woodsmoke and wet wool and the sour-sweet reek of the tannery upstream. Then by absence: no dogs barking at their approach, no lamplight in the windows that faced the road, no sound of the mill turning though the Greywater ran high and loud beneath the wheel.

Lena stopped at the edge of the last field, where the barley stood flattened in long strokes, the way wind lays it down or the way boots do when a lot of people move through it in one direction.

"That's wrong," she said.

Kael had gone very still beside her, and she realized he'd known before she did. Of course he had. He'd grown up reading villages the way she read passes.

"How many?" he asked quietly.

"Thirty households, maybe. The waystation keeps another dozen." She crouched, touched the flattened barley, rubbed the crushed stalks between her fingers. "This is days old. Not hours."

"And yet nobody's come out to chase us off. Nobody's posted watch." He turned slowly, scanning the dark shapes of rooftops against the grey sky. "A village that emptied itself doesn't stay empty, Lena. Someone comes back. Someone always comes back to loot what's left."

"Unless they were told not to."

"Unless they were told someone important was coming through, and they didn't want to be seen seeing it."

The rain thickened. Somewhere up the valley, thunder muttered like an old man disagreeing with himself. Lena looked at the waystation's dark bulk at the far end of the village street, its crooked antler sign hanging motionless, and felt the cold slide down past her collar and along her spine.

Her whole life, the Thornfell Guild had been the thing that knew the hidden ways. Every safehouse, every sympathetic farmer, every tannery man who owed a debt — all of it woven together by three generations of couriers who carried secrets because carrying secrets well was the only honest trade left in the mountains. If the Court could empty a village on her route, then the Court hadn't followed her.

Someone had told them where she'd be.

"There's a barn," Kael said. "Stone-footed, half-ruined, north end. We can watch the waystation from the loft until we know—"

"No." Her voice came out flat and strange. "I want to know now. I'm done walking into rooms after other people have arranged them."

"Lena—"

"The stranger died on my road, Kael. He bled out holding my wrist while I swore an oath I didn't understand to carry his map west. The least I can do is stop being a fool about how I carry it." She was already moving, low and fast along the hedgerow, and after a moment she heard him follow — because whatever else he was, whatever old allegiance still lived in him like a splinter, he had chosen to follow her, and she clung to that fact the way a drowning woman clings to driftwood, knowing it might not hold.

* * *

The waystation's common room door stood unlocked. That alone told her everything.

Inside, the room had been searched with a thoroughness that was almost respectful. Chairs stacked neatly in a corner rather than smashed. The hearth swept clean. The guild board — her guild board, the oak-backed lattice where sixty years of courier chalk-marks had layered over each other like tree rings — hung crooked on its single remaining nail, every scrap of paper stripped from it.

On the table in the center of the room, aligned precisely with the room's long axis, sat a single candle, unlit, and a folded square of ash-grey cloth.

Kael picked up the cloth and unfolded it, and his face did something complicated and terrible. It was a Court token — she'd seen its like once before, sewn inside the dead stranger's collar. A circle broken by a vertical line. The mark of the ash-cloaks.

"It's a receipt," Kael said. His voice had gone quiet in the way that frightened her more than shouting would have. "They're telling whoever comes next: we were here, we took what we wanted, we left this for you to find."

"Or they're telling *me*."

"They don't leave messages for quarry. They leave messages for—" He stopped.

"For people they've bought," Lena finished.

And that was when the lanterns lit.

They came up all at once around the room's perimeter — six, eight, ten points of flame behind horn panes, and behind the flames the broad, familiar silhouettes of men and women she had eaten with, trained with, bled with. Dree Halloway with her crossbow held low and correct. The Voss twins flanking the door. Old Tam Bracken, who had taught her to pick a lock with a fishhook when she was nine years old, standing with his hand resting on his belt knife the way he rested it when he hoped he wouldn't need it.

Every one of them wearing the guild's knot-and-thorn badge. Every one of them looking anywhere but at her face.

"Dree," Lena said. "Dree, look at me."

"Don't," Dree said, and her voice cracked on the word. "Don't make this harder than it has to be, Lena. Just give Corvan what he asked for. The stranger's effects. That's all. You walk out of here tonight and tomorrow you're just a courier again, same as ever."

"The stranger's effects." Lena laughed, and the laugh came out ugly. "You mean the map. Say the word, Dree. Say *map*."

"Give him the map," Tam said heavily. "Girl, whatever you think it is, it isn't worth this. There are ten of us and one of you, and the other one—" his eyes flicked to Kael, and something like pity crossed his seamed old face, "—the other one's Court, whatever he says otherwise. Corvan knows what he's doing. He always has."

"Does he?" Lena turned slowly, keeping them all in her sightline, feeling the map's weight against her ribs where it rode in the oilskin wrap beneath her shirt. "Then let him tell me himself. Let him stand in front of me and say what he's sold."

"He hasn't sold anything," Dree said, too fast, too smooth — and Lena heard it, the rehearsed quality of it, the line learned from a script.

The door at the back of the common room opened, and Guildmaster Corvan walked in.

He looked exactly as he always had: silver at the temples, broad through the shoulder despite his sixty years, dressed in good grey wool with the guildmaster's chain of office resting on his chest. He had carried Lena's father out of a rockslide on his own back. He had stood at her grandmother's funeral and wept openly, unashamed. When Lena had earned her full knots at nineteen, he had fastened them himself and told her, in front of the whole hall, that she was the finest courier the Thornfells had produced in a generation.

He closed the door behind him gently, as if the night outside were a sleeping child.

"Lena." Warm. Reasonable. The voice of a hundred campfires. "Thank you for making this easy."

"Did I?" She kept her hands loose at her sides. "It didn't feel easy from where I'm standing."

"You were always the stubborn one. Your grandmother was the same." He crossed the room unhurriedly, and the enforcers shifted to let him pass, opening a lane between him and her like water parting around a stone. "Sit down. Have a drink. Let's talk like family, because that's what we are, whatever else happens tonight."

"We're not family, Corvan. Family doesn't empty a village to set a trap."

His expression flickered — the smallest tightening around the eyes — and she knew she'd struck true. "Millbrook was warned, not harmed. The Court pays for discretion these days; they've learned burned villages make bad neighbors. No one died. No one will."

"The Court pays." She let the words hang. "So it's true. You're taking their coin."

"I'm taking *terms*, girl. There's a difference, and your grandmother would have understood it if you'd let me finish teaching you." He sighed, and for one treacherous moment he sounded genuinely tired, genuinely old, and Lena's heart lurched toward him the way it had lurched toward him her entire life — and then he went on, and the tiredness fell away like a mask slipping, and underneath it was the thing she'd seen in his eyes in the safehouse, the lamp behind the horn. "The world is changing, Lena. The guild is dying — you know it better than anyone, you've watched our routes close one by one, our numbers thin, our debts pile up like snow against a door. In five years there'll be nothing left but the name. In ten, not even that. Unless."

"Unless what?"

"Unless the guild becomes something new. Something the new powers need." He spread his hands, and the firelight caught the chain of office and made it gleam. "When the Oathstone breaks — and it *will* break, child, the Court has waited three hundred years and they will not be stopped by a dying smuggler's guild and one stubborn girl — there will be a new order in these mountains. And the man who delivered the key to that moment will have a seat at the table when the new order is built. Master of passes. Warden of roads. A guildmaster's title with a kingdom behind it, instead of a graveyard."

The room was very quiet. Rain ticked against the shutters. Somewhere in the walls, a mouse moved through old straw.

"You sold me," Lena said. "Not the map. *Me.* My location, my route, my habits. You fed it all to the ash-cloaks and let them run me across half the Thornfells like a hare, because a hunted courier leads them to things a cooperative one won't."

Corvan didn't deny it. That was the worst part. He simply looked at her with those warm, reasonable eyes and said, "You were always going to be difficult about this. So yes. I gave them your likely paths and let their riders shape your choices. It saved time. And it kept you alive — do you understand that? If you'd gone to ground, truly gone to ground, they'd have stopped being gentle. This way, every trap you escaped was a trap designed to miss."

"Designed to miss." Her voice shook, and she let it. "The man on the high road wasn't designed to miss. He died in the snow with his blood freezing in his beard while their riders circled for the kill."

"A necessary loss." Corvan said it without cruelty, which made it infinitely worse. "War has necessary losses. Even a war most people don't know is being fought."

"He wasn't a loss. He was a *courier*. He was one of ours, or he might as well have been — sworn to carry, dying to deliver. And you handed his killers the road he died on." She took a breath, and found that beneath the shaking there was something hard and clean forming, something that felt almost like calm. "What did they promise you, Corvan? What's the seat called?"

"Warden of the High Passes." He said it plainly, and she saw — gods help her, she saw — that he believed in it. That some part of him had already furnished the title, hung it with honors, sat down in it. "And a place for the guild in whatever comes after. Protection for our people. That matters more than your pride, Lena. That matters more than any oath written on an old map."

"Our people." She thought of Dree, crossbow steady, eyes wet. Of Tam, hand on his knife, hating every second of this. Of the Voss twins, who between them had maybe four years of guild service and no idea what they were guarding or why. "Look at them, Corvan. Really look. You've already spent them. The moment you take that seat, they're not a guild anymore — they're garrison troops wearing our badge. You haven't saved the Thornfells. You've sold the corpse and called it a marriage."

"Enough." The warmth went out of his voice all at once, like a snuffed candle, and what remained was colder and older and more honest than anything he'd said all night. "The map, Lena. Last time I ask as your guildmaster. After that I ask as something else."

She looked at him for a long moment. Ten enforcers. One door, guarded. Two windows, shuttered. A hearth with a flue wide enough for smoke but not for a grown woman. She catalogued it all the way her grandmother had taught her, the way she'd taught herself on a thousand dark roads — and then she did the thing none of them expected, because none of them remembered that the first rule of the courier's craft was never taught in the hall.

*Never enter a room you haven't already chosen how to leave.*

"You know," she said conversationally, "you really should have searched me before you started talking."

Corvan's eyes narrowed. "Dree searched her at the—"

"Dree searched my *coat*." And Lena pulled the oilskin packet from inside her shirt — the decoy she'd prepared in the safehouse, weighted with folded parchment and a strip of lead foil to sit right against her ribs — and hurled it high and hard toward the rafters above Corvan's head.

Ten trained fighters looked up. It was instinct, beautiful and stupid and human, and it bought her exactly the second she needed.

She went over the table, hit the shuttered window with both boots, and crashed through into the rain and the dark beyond.

* * *

They came after her the way she'd taught half of them to move, and that was the only reason she lived through the next hour.

She took them down the tanner's race, along the sluice where the footing was grease-slick and only someone who'd run it blind drunk at seventeen could hold a pace. She doubled through the drying sheds, kicked a rack of hides down behind her, heard Tam Bracken swear as it tangled his legs and heard him shout — not *there she is* but *"she went left!"* — pointing the pursuit the wrong way, and she loved him for it even as her eyes burned, because loving him changed nothing now and he'd know that better than anyone.

Kael found her at the ford below the mill, chest heaving, hair plastered to her skull, blood running from a gash on her forearm where the shutter frame had caught her.

"You're hurt."

"It's nothing. Did they—"

"Tam's holding the bridge. The twins are 'searching the north fields.' Dree—" he hesitated. "Dree fired her crossbow twice. Both bolts went into fences."

Lena pressed her palm hard against the cut and stared at the black water sliding past. "They let me go. Some of them. They chose."

"Yes."

"Corvan didn't choose. He'll send real hunters after us by morning — ones who aren't ours, ones the Court lends him. He has to, now. I've seen his face. He can't afford to let me live." She said it steadily, and was surprised to find it was true: there was no rage left in her, or rather the rage had burned down overnight into something denser, something that would last. "Do you understand what happened in there, Kael? I'm not a courier anymore. Couriers belong to the guild. I'm a traitor now. Officially. With a price on me."

He was quiet for a moment. Rain ran off his jaw.

"When I left the Court," he said finally, "they struck my name from the rolls and burned my oath-paper in front of the assembled host. My mother was made to watch. That's how they say *traitor*." He looked at her, and in the dark his eyes caught the last grey light off the water. "It doesn't kill you, Lena. I used to think it would. But it turns out there's a kind of freedom on the far side of it. Nobody's rules left to hide behind. Only what you carry, and where you choose to carry it, and who walks beside you."

The words landed somewhere deep, in the bruised place where the guild had lived, and she found that they helped — not much, but truly.

"West, then," she said. "Through the Burnlands, if the maps are right. Past the flooded gorge." She started up the far bank, and paused, and looked back once at the dark shape of Millbrook, at the village that had emptied itself for her sake, at the whole warm life she had just climbed out of through a broken window. "Goodbye, you stubborn old liar," she whispered — to Corvan, to the guild, to the girl she'd been an hour ago — and then she turned her face west, where the mountains rose black against the last storm-light, and put the Thornfells behind her.

Behind them, far up the valley, a signal fire bloomed on a ridge top — one pulse, two, three. The Court's hunters, answering their master's call.

Kael saw it too. "How long do we have?"

Lena watched the fire pulse and did the arithmetic she'd been doing since she was nine years old, running routes in the dark with her grandmother's voice in her ears. Then she began to run.

"Less than we need. Move."
