## 11. The Ferryman's Price

The gorge announced itself long before they saw it — a low, ceaseless roar threading through the pines like something breathing in its sleep. Lena had heard it once before, years ago, when she was fourteen and her grandmother had brought her to the overlook at Duskwatch Point and told her never, under any circumstance, to go down to the water.

*The river remembers what people forget,* Gran had said, her hand heavy on Lena's shoulder. *And it charges accordingly.*

Now Lena stood at the edge of the gorge with the map pressed flat against her chest inside her coat, and the water below was nothing like the silver ribbon of her memory. The spring melt had come early and violent this year, and the Serpent's Throat — that was the old name for it, the name the couriers used when they wanted to frighten the new recruits — had swallowed its own banks whole. Brown water churned through the chasm forty feet below, thick with torn branches and the occasional pale flash of a drowned deer turning over and over in the current.

"There's no crossing this," Kael said behind her. He stood a careful three paces back, the way he always did now, as if he'd learned exactly how much distance she needed and refused to encroach on it. "Not by rope, not by ford. The far anchor points are underwater. I can see where the bridge used to be."

Lena followed his gaze. He was right. The great iron rings that had held the courier bridge were submerged, only their crowns breaking the surface like the backs of sleeping animals. The bridge itself was gone entirely — snapped cables whipping loose somewhere downstream, planks scattered for miles.

"The couriers called it the Throat," she said. "There was a bridge. There was always a bridge. My grandmother crossed it twice a year until the day she died."

"Your grandmother isn't here."

"No," Lena said quietly. "She isn't."

She turned away from the roar and studied the map instead, unrolling it against a boulder despite the wind trying to tear it from her hands. The ink still moved when she wasn't looking directly at it — she'd stopped mentioning that to Kael, because every time she did, he went very still in a way that frightened her more than the ash-cloaks did. The route west wound down into the gorge and up the other side, and where the crossing should be marked, there was a symbol she'd initially taken for a stylized wave.

Now, looking at it properly, she wasn't so sure it was a drawing at all. It looked like writing. It looked like an invitation.

"Kael." She kept her voice level. "What do you know about river-spirits?"

* * *

They made camp that night on the near rim of the gorge, in a hollow of granite that blocked the worst of the wind. Kael built the fire small — he was better at fires than she was, another thing she'd stopped resenting and started simply accepting — and they ate hard cheese and the last of the Millbrook bread, which had gone stale enough to double as a weapon.

Lena told him what her grandmother had told her, all those years ago at Duskwatch Point, in the tone adults use for stories meant to keep children safe. The spirit in the Throat was older than the guild, older than the Court, older maybe than the mountains themselves, or at least it had been here when the mountains were young enough to change their minds about their shapes. It didn't want worship. It didn't want sacrifice in the old bloody sense. What it wanted — what it *took*, always, as the price of passage — was memory.

"A toll?" Kael had been sharpening his knife; now his hands went still. "Memories aren't currency."

"Everything's currency if someone wants it badly enough. That's the first lesson of smuggling." She fed a stick to the fire. "Gran said the ferryman takes one memory per traveler, per crossing. You don't get to choose which one. Or—" she hesitated, "—you do, actually. That's the trap. You can offer one, and it will take it, but it chooses how much of it you lose. Sometimes a little. Sometimes all of it. And sometimes it takes something adjacent, something you didn't know was tangled up in the same knot."

"And you know this how?"

"I know this because my grandmother crossed twice a year for thirty years and came back each time a little less herself. By the end she couldn't remember my grandfather's face. She remembered that she'd loved him. She just couldn't remember why, or what he looked like, or whether he'd laughed." Lena heard her own voice go flat and careful, the way it did when grief got too close. "She said it was worth it. She said the guild was worth it. I used to think that was noble. Now I think it was just addiction with better vocabulary."

Kael was quiet for a long time. The fire cracked. Below them, the Throat roared on, patient as debt.

"You're thinking we find another way," Lena said.

"I'm thinking that I've already lost things to that river." His voice was strange. "When I left the Court, I crossed the Serpent's Throat at the shallows upstream, in winter, when it was ice. But there are stories among the defectors. Everyone who crosses water going west pays something. We used to joke about it. *Mind the toll.*" He looked up at her, firelight moving in his grey eyes. "I'm thinking that whatever it takes from me, I've earned worse."

"That's not brave, Kael. That's just sad."

"Maybe. But it's also true, and I've found true things make better traveling companions than brave ones."

Despite everything — the burned villages behind them, Corvan's betrayal like a splinter working deeper every day, the map humming faintly against her ribs as if it knew how close they were getting to its purpose — despite all of it, Lena almost smiled. Almost.

"We sleep," she said. "We cross at dawn. The spirit is supposed to be... calmer, in the morning light. Gran's word, not mine. She said it liked to watch the sun come up the way old men watch children play."

* * *

Dawn came grey and gold through the pines, and the gorge below had changed overnight in some subtle way Lena couldn't name. The water level had dropped — barely, a handspan, no more — but the character of the sound had shifted. Less rage in it. More attention.

"It knows we're here," Kael said.

"Of course it does. It's probably known since Millbrook." Lena rolled the map and tied it down. Her hands were steady. She noted that with distant surprise, the way you note weather. "Come on. Down the switchbacks. If we're doing this, I want it done while my nerve is fresh."

The descent took two hours. The trail was old courier work, cut into the rock in tight switchbacks, and Lena took the lead because this, at least, was hers — the reading of stone, the placement of feet, the small arrogant competence of a woman who had spent her whole life learning passes nobody else could walk. Behind her, Kael moved well for a Court man, economical and silent, but he moved like a soldier, not a courier. Soldiers trusted the ground. Couriers negotiated with it.

At the bottom, the river filled the world.

Up close the Throat was deafening, a brown thunder of meltwater compressed between walls of black granite furred with moss. Spray hung in the air like rain that couldn't commit. And there, moored against the near bank in an eddy that shouldn't have existed in water that violent, was the boat.

It was smaller than Lena expected. A simple wooden craft, flat-bottomed, dark with age, and utterly dry despite the deluge around it. No oars. No pole. No ferryman visible — though as they approached the bank, Lena felt the air thicken, the way it does before a summer storm, and understood that *visible* was not the same as *present*.

A figure formed at the stern. Not appeared — *formed*, the way frost forms on glass, gradually and then all at once. It was tall and narrow, wrapped in something that might have been robes or might have been riverweed, and where its face should have been there was a smoothness like wet stone, featureless except for two depressions that suggested eyes without committing to them.

Lena had faced down guild enforcers, ash-cloaks, and once, memorably, a bear. None of it prepared her for the specific humiliation of standing in front of something ancient and feeling like a child caught out past curfew.

"We want to cross," she said. Her voice sounded thin against the roar.

The figure inclined its head. When it spoke, the voice didn't come from it so much as from the water, from the stones, from the space between Lena's ears: a voice like current over gravel.

*TWO TRAVELERS. TWO TOLLS.*

"We know," Lena said. "We know the price."

*DO YOU?* The smooth face tilted. *THE LAST ONE WHO STOOD WHERE YOU STAND WEPT AND CALLED IT FAIR. THE ONE BEFORE HER LAUGHED AND CALLED IT CHEAP. BOTH WERE WRONG.*

"What do you take?" Kael asked. He'd stepped up beside her, close enough that his shoulder nearly touched hers. "Exactly. I want to hear it from you, not from stories."

*MEMORY,* the spirit said. *ONE FROM EACH. YOU MAY NAME THE MEMORY YOU OFFER. YOU MAY NOT CONTROL HOW MUCH OF IT I TAKE, NOR WHAT HANGS FROM ITS ROOTS. MEMORY IS NOT A FRUIT. IT IS A ROOT SYSTEM. PULL ONE THING AND OTHER THINGS COME LOOSE.*

Lena's mouth had gone dry. "And if we refuse?"

*THEN YOU REMAIN ON THAT BANK UNTIL THE MELT ENDS. THREE MONTHS, PERHAPS FOUR. THE ASHEN COURT RIDES FASTER THAN THAT.*

She felt Kael go rigid beside her. They hadn't spoken of pursuit since the Burnlands, hadn't needed to — the silence behind them was its own kind of message. Three months was a death sentence wearing a polite coat.

"I'll pay first," Kael said.

"No." Lena turned to him. "We pay together or—"

"Lena." He said her name quietly, and something in the quiet of it stopped her. "Let me do this."

* * *

Later — much later, on the far bank, walking west with a hole in him shaped like an answer — she would replay this moment again and again and hate herself for what she didn't see. But in the moment itself, all she saw was a tired man squaring his shoulders the way he did before anything difficult, and she thought, foolishly, that he was merely being gallant.

"I'll offer the reason I left the Court," Kael said to the spirit. "That's the memory. The reason. The night I decided. Whatever it costs — take it."

"Kael, no—" Lena grabbed his arm. "You can't. If you lose that, you lose—" She faltered. What did he lose? Why did the thought of it terrify her more than the gorge, more than the Court, more than the god sleeping under the mountain?

*I lose nothing I haven't already lost,* he said — no. He didn't say it. Did he? Or was that later, pieced together from fragments, from the way he looked at her?

What she knew for certain was this: he gently removed her hand from his arm, held it for a moment longer than necessary, and stepped toward the boat.

*Kael Veyran,* the spirit said, and the use of his full name — a name Lena had never heard, a name he had never given her — landed on the bank like a dropped stone. *FORMER BLADE OF THE ASHEN COURT. SWORN TO THE MATRIARCH'S THIRD CIRCLE. YOU OFFER THE NIGHT OF YOUR TURNING.*

"I offer everything attached to it," Kael said. His voice was steady. Too steady. "All of it. Take the root, not the fruit."

*THAT IS NOT USUALLY HOW THEY ASK.*

"I'm not usually how they are."

The spirit regarded him for a long moment. The river roared. Somewhere upstream, a branch the size of a man went under and didn't come up.

Then it extended one long, weed-draped hand, and touched Kael's forehead.

Lena saw it happen — or rather, she saw the aftermath happening, the way you see the wake of a thing too fast to witness. Kael's knees buckled. She lunged forward and caught him before he hit the stones, and he sagged against her, and his eyes when they opened were —

Wrong. Not empty. Worse than empty. *Searching.* He looked at her face the way you look at a word you almost remember, and Lena felt something crack open in her chest that she suspected would never fully close.

"Do I—" His voice came out hoarse, younger somehow. "Do I know you?"

"You know me," Lena said, and her voice held, which she would count later as the greatest act of strength in her life. "You're Kael. You're coming west with me. That's all you need to know right now."

He stared at her. And then, slowly, he nodded, and something in his posture settled, some deep instinct overriding the absence where his reasons used to live.

"All right," he said. "All right. Coming west. With you." He frowned, pressing his palm against his chest as if checking for a wound. "There was something important. About why. I can feel the shape of it, like a hole in a tooth. Was it — did I leave because of you?"

The question hit her like a physical blow. Because underneath the loss, underneath the amnesia, some part of him had reached for the nearest true thing and found *her*, and Lena realized with terrible clarity that she could tell him anything right now. She could shape his reasons. She could make herself his cause.

*No.* Gran's voice, sharp as a whetstone. *That's not love, girl. That's forgery.*

"No," Lena said softly. "You left for your own reasons. Good ones. The best ones. And when you remember them — and you will, pieces of you are stubborn enough to survive anything — you'll know I never lied to you about it."

Something flickered across his face — gratitude, or grief, or both braided together. He looked at the spirit, who had returned to its stillness at the stern.

"And her?" Kael asked. "Her toll?"

*HERS IS HER OWN TO PAY,* the spirit said. *OR HER OWN TO REFUSE. THE BOAT TAKES NO PASSENGER WHO HAS NOT SETTLED.*

Lena stepped forward. Her heart was hammering, and not from fear — or not only from fear. Because she had spent the entire descent deciding, and the decision had crystallized somewhere around the fifth switchback, cold and clear as spring water:

She would not pay.

Not because she was stingy. Not because she thought herself exempt. But because she had watched Kael give up the root of himself to spare her a smaller loss, and she understood now — with the same clarity that had carried her through every pass, every storm, every desperate run — that someone had to remain whole enough to carry what was coming. The map. The oath. The truth about what waited beneath the mountain.

One of them had to remember why they were doing this.

And God help her, some selfish, buried, human part of her whispered: *and one of us has to remember this. Him. This moment. Someone has to hold it, because I cannot carry it alone.*

"I refuse," Lena said to the spirit.

The smooth face tilted. *REFUSAL HAS A PRICE TOO.*

"Name it."

*THE RIVER WILL CHOOSE FOR YOU. IN ITS OWN TIME. IN ITS OWN WAY. NOTHING IS FREE, DAUGHTER OF BLACKWOOD. ONLY DEFERRED.*

The words slid through her like ice water. *Daughter of Blackwood.* The spirit knew. Of course it knew. Everything in these mountains knew what she was running toward.

"Then defer it," Lena said. "Bill me later."

For a moment — one strange, suspended moment — she could have sworn the featureless face *smiled*. Then the spirit gestured toward the boat, and the eddy widened, and the impossible vessel slid toward the bank like it had been waiting three hundred years for exactly this fare.

* * *

The crossing itself took eleven minutes. Lena counted. Counting kept her from screaming.

The boat moved through water that should have swallowed it, should have shattered it against the rocks, and did neither. The spray parted around them. The roar dimmed to something almost courteous. Kael sat in the bow, gripping the gunwale, watching the spirit with the wariness of a man who has just learned the world is larger and stranger than his training covered.

Halfway across, he spoke without turning around.

"I don't remember why I trust you," he said. "But I do. Completely. Isn't that strange? Trust without a reason. It feels like flying."

Lena's throat closed. She thought of all the careful distances he'd kept, all the wary truces, the slow negotiation of two enemies learning each other's edges — and understood that he had given all of that away. Not just his reasons. His *reasons for her*. Whatever careful architecture of evidence and instinct had built his faith in her, the spirit had pulled it out root and branch, and what remained was simpler and more terrible: belief with no foundation, love with no history.

"Yes," she managed. "It's strange."

"Was I good?" he asked suddenly, turning. "Before? At the Court. Was I a good man?"

The question hung in the mist. Lena thought of the scars she'd glimpsed on his back, the flinches at certain words, the way he sometimes woke at night with his hand already closing around a knife that wasn't there.

"You're becoming one," she said. "That's better."

He nodded slowly, accepting this, and turned back to the water, and Lena sat in the stern of a dead man's boat with a living man's trust resting in her hands like something breakable, and she did not let herself cry until they reached the far bank.

* * *

They climbed out of the gorge as the sun climbed with them, and by midday the Throat was behind them, its roar fading to a rumor, then a memory — *someone's* memory, at least.

Kael walked beside her in silence for a long while. Then, abruptly:

"There's a word stuck in my head. *Oathstone.* I don't know why I know that word. Do I know that word?"

"You know it," Lena said carefully. "It's where we're going."

"Why?"

"Because it matters. Because what happens there decides what happens to everyone. That's all I can tell you for now."

He considered this with an expression she was beginning to recognize as his thinking-face, and which she was also beginning to dread, because every time he wore it, he asked a question she couldn't answer honestly without breaking something.

"All right," he said finally. "I'll trust the hole where the answer used to be. It seems to point west." He glanced at her sideways, and there — just for a heartbeat — was a ghost of the dry humor she'd come to rely on across a hundred miles of hidden roads. "You're very easy to follow, did you know that? I feel like I've known you for years. Is that normal?"

Lena looked at the road ahead — the western passes rising blue and vast, the last leg, the mountain itself waiting somewhere beyond them with its buried god and its broken oath.

"No," she said. "Nothing about this is normal."

And then, ahead of them, where the trail bent around a shoulder of granite, she saw it: smoke. Thin and deliberate, a signal fire, burning in a pattern she recognized from her courier days.

A guild pattern. Thornfell code, three puffs and a pause.

Someone was waiting for them on the road west. And since everyone she'd ever trusted from the guild had either betrayed her or died, Lena drew her knife and quickened her pace, and did not see the look Kael gave her back — the look of a man following a woman into danger for reasons he no longer possessed, and following gladly.
