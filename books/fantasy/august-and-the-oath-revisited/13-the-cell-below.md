## 13. The Cell Below

They took her from me in the great hall of Cindermarch, and for three days I did not know if she still breathed.

I had been given a room, which was its own kind of insult. A narrow chamber on the fourth tier of the fortress, with a real bed, a basin of clean water, a window of thick glass that looked out over the ash-fields toward the mountains I could no longer reach. The door was not locked. It did not need to be. The Ashen Court had never needed locks for its own; it had something better than iron. It had the shape of your life before you left it.

I sat in that room the way I had once sat in a hundred rooms like it — spine straight, hands quiet, wearing the grey they had returned to me because my own clothes had been burned as contagion — and I listened to Cindermarch breathe.

That was the first thing they taught us, back when I was nine years old and the word *defector* had no meaning to me at all: a fortress is an animal. Learn where its heart beats and you will never be lost inside it. Cindermarch's heart was the furnace-hall below the third tier, where the hunt-mages tempered their instruments; its lungs were the ventilation shafts cut into the eastern cliff face, breathing cold mountain air down through channels so old the Court's own architects had forgotten them. Its blood was water, drawn from a cistern deep beneath the foundation stone, pumped by chains and counterweights through pipes of lead and copper. And its bones —

Its bones were the cells beneath the third tier, carved from the living rock when the fortress was raised on the ruins of something older.

I knew every corridor. That was my curse. Matriarch Veyra had not put me in a cell because she believed there was nowhere I could run. She had put me in a room with a window because she wanted me to look at those mountains and remember that I had helped build the machine now hunting the woman I loved. Every hour I sat in comfort while Lena Blackwood sat in stone was a lesson, and she intended me to learn it well.

On the third night, I stopped sitting.

* * *

The guard rotation changed at midnight. I remembered that too. The second watch came up from the furnace levels sweating and half-asleep, resentful, eager for the warmth of the brazier at the fourth-tier junction. For eleven minutes — I had counted them a thousand times in another life, standing post myself with my fingers going numb — the stair between the third and fourth tiers stood empty.

I went down it barefoot, boots in hand, wearing the grey they had dressed me in like a knife wearing a sheath.

The old knowledge rose in me the whole way, unbidden, treacherous, precise. *Left at the junction, past the brazier, take the servants' stair because it has no sightline to the watch-post.* My body moved through Cindermarch the way water moves through a channel it cut itself, and I hated how easy it was, hated the part of me that felt almost grateful, the part that whispered *you are home* — because this was exactly what Veyra wanted. Not my obedience. My return. The Court did not punish defectors by killing them. It punished them by making defection impossible, by keeping the doors unlocked until the defector understood that every road out of Cindermarch ran through the middle of it.

But tonight the roads led somewhere useful.

The third tier smelled of hot metal and tallow. I crossed the gallery above the furnace-hall with the orange light washing over me, and no one looked up, because no one looks at a man wearing the right clothes moving with the right stride. Fear does that. Three years outside the Court, and my body still carried its discipline like a scar carries the memory of the wound.

The cells began behind a door of blackened iron that I had walked past ten thousand times without ever being permitted to open it. Now the two guards before it glanced at me, read the grey, read the stride, and nodded.

"Business?" the older one asked.

"Inspection," I said. "Matriarch's order. There's been a question about the prisoner's condition."

It was a good lie because it was the kind of lie the Court actually told. Inspections were constant, arbitrary, designed to keep everyone uncertain. The guards stepped aside. One of them had a face I almost knew. I kept my eyes forward. In another life I would have known his name, his family, the debt his brother owed the quartermaster. That was the other thing the Court built into you: intimacy as architecture. You betrayed one person and the whole structure groaned.

The stairs went down farther than I remembered. Or perhaps I simply remembered them as shorter, because when I had descended them before, I had always been the jailer.

* * *

She was in the last cell, the deepest one, the one they called the Quiet because the rock around it drank sound. When I reached the barred door she was sitting against the far wall with her knees drawn up, and for one terrible moment I thought they had already begun — because there was a stillness to her that Lena Blackwood had never once shown me on the passes, on the ferryman's shore, in any of the hard miles between. She was a woman made of motion, of argument and stubbornness and laughter sharp as flint. This stillness was not hers.

Then she lifted her head, and her eyes found mine through the bars, and the stillness broke like ice under a boot.

"You," she said. "Of course. They sent *you*."

"No one sent me."

"Don't." She unfolded herself and came to the bars, and even now, even hollowed, there was heat in her voice. "Don't stand there in their colors and tell me you came on your own. I watched what this place does, Kael. I watched Corvan walk in here a guildmaster and walk out a creature. Whatever they've promised you—"

"They promised me nothing. They gave me a room and a window." I heard my own voice go flat, and I hated it, and I let it, because flat was safer than the alternative. "Lena. Are you hurt?"

She hesitated. That hesitation told me more than any answer would have.

"What did they do?"

"Nothing yet." Her jaw tightened. "They're patient. That matriarch of yours — she doesn't ask questions. She just talks. Two days of talking. About order. About the war ending properly this time. About how much easier everything would have been if I'd been born Court instead of guild." Something flickered across her face that frightened me worse than bruises would have. "And the worst part, Kael, is that some of it almost made sense. That's what they do, isn't it? They don't break you. They make you *agree*."

"I know what they do. I was their work for twenty years."

"Then why do you smell like them again?" Her eyes dropped to the grey cloak, and I saw her read it the way she read weather — instantly, totally, and with a courier's instinct for the coming storm.

So I told her. Quickly, quietly, the words pressed low against the bars. The room with the window. The unlocked door. Veyra's patience. And then the rest — the furnace hall, the servants' stair, the eleven minutes at midnight, the inspection lie, the fact that I was standing in front of her only because Cindermarch had never managed to burn its own map out of me.

When I finished, she was silent a long moment.

"You swore to forget all of it," she said finally. "At the gorge. After the ferryman took—" She stopped. The ferryman's toll hung between us, the way it always did now: the reason Kael had turned against the Court, paid away in river-water, gone somewhere none of us could follow. He had bought our crossing with the memory of why he'd chosen her side, and neither of us ever spoke of it directly, because speaking of it made it a debt and debts demand repayment and there was no repaying this one.

"The ferryman took one memory," I said. "He didn't take the others. I swore to forget, but oaths are words, Lena, and the stone under this fortress remembers better than I do." I pressed my palm flat against the cold rock of the doorway. "My feet know these halls. That's enough."

"And if it's a trap? If she let you come?"

"Then she's spent three days guessing wrong, because if Veyra wanted to test whether I'd crawl back, she wouldn't have let me near the one person who makes crawling back impossible." I pulled a thin strip of iron from inside my cloak — taken from the guardroom, bent and filed during two nights of pretending to sleep. "Hold still."

She held still. Of course she did. I worked the lock with hands that shook only after it opened, which seemed a fair arrangement.

When the door swung wide, Lena Blackwood stepped out of the Quiet and stood in the corridor of her enemies, and the motion came back into her like a tide returning — shoulders rolling, weight shifting to the balls of her feet, eyes already mapping the passage. She looked at me, and there was something new in her expression. Not gratitude. Something harder and more useful.

"They'll kill you for this," she said.

"They'll try."

A ghost of a smile. "Good answer."

* * *

We went up through the bones of the fortress, and the old knowledge carried us.

I will not pretend it was clean. The servants' stair was watched — I had gambled wrong on the rotation, or Veyra had doubled it since my day, and we lost six minutes pressed into an alcove behind stacked cordwood while a pair of ash-cloaks argued about dice within arm's reach of us. Lena's hand found mine in the dark, not for comfort but for coordination, her fingers tapping the rhythm of the guards' pacing against my knuckles the way she must have tapped signals on the passes. *Two steps. Pause. Turn. Go.* When they finally moved off, we moved with them, shadows folded into the noise of their departure.

At the furnace gallery we nearly died. A hunt-mage was working late, alone at her tempering bench, and she looked up as we crossed the catwalk above her — and I saw recognition begin in her face, the slow dawn of *that is Kael, that is the traitor Kael* — and then Lena dropped from the catwalk onto the bench beside her, and whatever happened in the next four seconds happened too fast and too quietly for me to render honestly except to say that the mage ended up bound with her own tempering-cords, gagged with her own hood, and entirely unhurt, because Lena had looked at me across the bench and shaken her head once. *No killing. Not here. Not yet.*

"She's someone's daughter," Lena said later, climbing. "Maybe she's even someone who'd have liked me, on a different road. We're not them, Kael. That's the whole point of not being them."

"That point may get us killed."

"Then we'll die pointing at it."

I had forgotten — or perhaps the ferryman had taken this too, or perhaps I had simply buried it — how exhausting it was to hope around her. Hope was a labor. Hope meant imagining mornings after this one, and I had trained myself out of mornings years ago. But she climbed ahead of me through the dark with her borrowed grey cloak tied back like a courier's sash, and I caught myself doing arithmetic about sunrise, and I understood that defiance, for her, was not a decision. It was a metabolism.

We reached the ventilation shafts an hour before dawn. They opened onto the eastern cliff face through a grate of rusted iron that I had crawled through at age eleven on a dare, back when Cindermarch was still the only world I knew and the world beyond it was a story the elders told to frighten us. The grate gave to Lena's knife in less than a minute. Beyond it, the wind came off the ash-fields carrying the smell of frost and distant pine, and it hit my face like open water hits a drowning man.

Lena went first. I followed. And for the length of one breath, hanging on the cliffside of the fortress that had made me, with the whole sleeping machinery of the Ashen Court at my back, I felt the strangest sensation — not fear, not triumph, but *permission*. As though some part of me that had been holding a door shut for twenty years had finally set the bar down.

Then a horn sounded from the third tier, and permission ended.

"They've found the mage," Lena said. "Or the empty cell. Doesn't matter. Run."

* * *

We ran east through the ash-fields as the horns multiplied behind us and the sky went from black to bruise-grey. The Court's response was everything I remembered and worse: disciplined, layered, patient. They did not scatter riders after us in panic. They closed a ring — slow, wide, mathematically certain — because Veyra did not chase people. She arranged circumstances from which there was no exit, and waited for her prey to notice the geometry.

"We won't make the foothills," I gasped. "The ring closes by midmorning."

"Then we don't make for the foothills." Lena skidded to a stop on a ridge of hardened slag, scanning the broken country ahead — the Burnlands, the scorched foothills where the Court's old wars had cooked the earth down to glass and cinder. Everyone fled Cindermarch west or south, toward green country and cover. No one fled into the Burnlands. Which meant, of course, that it was the one direction the ring would be thinnest.

"It's open ground," I said. "No water. No shade. Hunt-mages can read a trail in that ash like writing."

"I know." She was already moving, angling northeast, and there was something in her stride I hadn't seen since Millbrook — since the razed waystation, since the word *traitor* started following her. Fury, banked and burning clean. "They razed Millbrook because someone sold our route. They locked me in a hole because someone sold my name. Everything I loved got traded away piece by piece, and now they think they've got us in a jar." She glanced back at me, and the dawn light caught her eyes, and I swear the fire in the horizon was the lesser blaze. "Let them close the ring, Kael. Let them spend their riders chasing two ghosts across ground they poisoned themselves. Every mile they chase us east is a mile they aren't watching the mountain road."

Understanding landed in me like a struck bell.

"You want them looking at us."

"I want them *only* looking at us." She lengthened her stride, and I matched it, and behind us Cindermarch's horns wailed their patient, certain song. "Corvan sold me once. Veyra thinks she owns the board. So we give them a game worth watching — loud, desperate, doomed — and while every eye in that fortress follows two runaways into the Burnlands, the truth walks west on legs they forgot existed." She did not say whose legs. She did not need to. Somewhere beyond the ring, beyond the ash and the horns and the geometry of the hunt, there were passes only couriers knew and a mountain road only the willing could climb, and Lena Blackwood had already mapped our deaths so precisely that our survival fit inside them like a key fits a lock.

The sun broke the ridge behind us, gilding the ash, and the first riders appeared on our backtrail — small, dark, inevitable. Lena laughed. Actually laughed, breathless and wild, the sound torn out of her by the wind.

"There you are," she said to them, almost tenderly. "Come on, then. Let's give you something to report."

And I ran beside her into the Burnlands, into the open fire-colored morning, hunted by everything I had ever been — and for the first time since the ferryman's shore, since the toll, since the blank place where my reasons used to live — I did not need to remember why.

She was the why. She was running, and I was running with her, and that was oath enough.

Behind us, the horns changed their note — from pursuit to alarm — as somewhere in Cindermarch, a matriarch realized her cage had been measured from the inside.

Ahead of us, the Burnlands opened like a throat, and we went down into the fire.
