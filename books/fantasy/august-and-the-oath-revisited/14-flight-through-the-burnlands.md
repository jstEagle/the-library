## 14. Flight Through the Burnlands

* * *

The foothills had been green once. Lena could see it in the bones of the land — the fossilized rows of terraces, the black ribs of orchard fences, a stone well standing alone in a sea of ash like a tombstone for a village no one had bothered to name. The Ashen Court's old wars had done this, centuries ago, and nothing had grown here since.

Now the Court was coming to finish the job.

"They've loosed the hounds," Kael said, pulling her down behind the collapsed wall of a shepherd's hut. Below them, across three miles of grey scrubland, riders moved in formation — and ahead of the riders walked figures that made Lena's stomach turn over.

They were tall, robed in white that refused to stay clean, ash clinging to them and sloughing off in a constant grey snowfall of their own making. Where they walked, the dead ground *smoked*. Hunt-mages. She'd heard smugglers' stories about them — that they could smell a name spoken aloud, taste fear on the wind, follow a heartbeat through solid rock.

"Four of them," Kael breathed. "That's not a hunting party. That's an execution."

"Then let's be difficult to kill." Lena checked the map against her chest, felt its warmth through the oilcloth — it had been warm ever since the caverns, ever since her blood touched it, a faint pulse like a second heart riding under her ribs. "There's a dry riverbed two ridges east. Couriers used it before the burn. It runs under the old causeway —"

"I know the one." He said it too quickly. Then he frowned, pressing two fingers to his temple as though something had struck him from inside. "I — yes. I know the one."

"Kael?"

"Nothing." But his hand stayed pressed to his skull, and when he looked at her his eyes were wet and bewildered. "I remember the causeway. I remember *riding* it. Lena, I shouldn't remember that."

* * *

*Kael.*

The memory came back on the third ridge, mid-sprint, and it took him at the knees.

He went down hard among the burnt bracken, and the world folded away, and he was twenty-three years old again, kneeling on marble floors in the Court's citadel with his forehead pressed to cold stone while Matriarch Serevan walked a slow circle around him.

*"You were born to serve the seal," she said. "But you have begun asking questions. So we will take the questions, Kael. We will take them out of you one by one, and what remains will be a good instrument. You'll thank us for the quiet."*

And there had been a chair. There had been silver needles and a chanting circle and a light behind his eyes like a dying sun, and the last thing he had chosen to hold onto — the last thing he'd hidden so deep they couldn't find it — was a face. Dark hair. Grey eyes. A woman laughing in a doorway of the Court's library, holding a stolen book, saying: *you're not supposed to be here, but I'm glad you are.*

Lena's face.

Kael came back to himself gasping, nose bleeding freely onto the ash, and she was crouched over him with an arrow nocked and terror in her expression.

"What happened? Talk to me."

"The riverbed," he managed. "It floods. In spring — there was a flood, the year I scouted this route for them, we lost two riders—" He stopped. Blinked blood out of his eye. "I scouted this route *for them*, Lena. I led the purge through these hills. I know where every body is buried because—"

"Because you helped bury them." Her voice was flat, but her hands, gripping his arm, were not. They were shaking.

"Yes."

She looked at him for a long moment. Behind them, faintly, came the sound of the hunt-mages' bells — each mage wore iron chimes that rang only when they caught a scent of their quarry, and the ringing had been getting closer all morning.

"Then you know how to avoid your own traps," she said finally, and hauled him up by the arm. "Get up. Bleed later."

* * *

*Lena.*

The riverbed saved them and nearly killed them.

They dropped into it at noon, walls of fused glassy stone rising on either side, and for two hours it was the closest thing to safety the Burnlands offered — no scent trails surviving on baked rock, the bells fading behind them until Lena dared to hope.

Then the water came.

Not real water — she understood that a half-second too late, the moment she saw it sliding toward them upriver against all sense, a wall of grey mist shaped like water, moving like water, roaring like a flood that had died three hundred years ago and never known it. A hunt-mage's working. Memory given form, weaponized.

"*Flash flood!*" Kael screamed, and grabbed her, and threw them both onto a ledge of rock six feet above the channel floor as the ghost-water hit.

It didn't drown. That was the horror of it. It poured past them and where it touched, things *left* — lichen gone from the stone, a hawk's shadow erased from the sky overhead, and when a thin tongue of it licked across Kael's trailing boot he convulsed like a man electrocuted.

"It takes memories," he choked. "It's mine — part of what they took from me — they turned it loose as a hound—"

"Lie still," Lena snapped, dragging him fully onto the ledge. The grey water surged below them, hungry, circling. And she saw it then, clearly, in the way it kept orienting toward him: the thing wasn't hunting both of them. It was hunting *him*. A fragment of Kael, sent to devour the rest.

Something in her chest went very quiet and very cold.

She drew her knife. Cut her left palm — the same palm, the map pulsed hot against her ribs in answer — and held the bleeding hand out over the water.

"You want memories?" she said. "Come and get mine instead."

The flood rose toward her blood like a dog scenting meat — and stopped. Hung in the air. Somewhere beneath the roar she heard, impossibly, a voice like many voices braided together: *the key bleeds true. The key is not ours to take.*

The water collapsed. Fell apart into ordinary ash and drifted away on the wind, leaving the riverbed empty and silent and smelling of rain that would never fall.

Lena stared at her cut hand, shaking now, and didn't hear Kael come up beside her.

"It knew you," he said quietly.

"Everything knows me lately." She bound her palm with a strip of shirt without looking at it. "Your mages. Your ghosts. My own grandmother's map." She turned and found him watching her with an expression she'd never seen on him before — stripped bare, all the defector's armor gone. "What?"

"You bled for me," he said. "You didn't even hesitate. You didn't even ask."

"You'd have done the same."

"That's not why I said it." He reached out and took her wounded hand, careful as a man handling a live coal, and pressed his lips to the knuckles above the bandage. "I paid a river to forget why I betrayed everything I was. And every day since, pieces come back, and every piece is worse than the last, and I understand now — I understand that whatever reason I started with doesn't matter anymore. Because I'd do it all again. Not for the stone. For you."

The bells rang again, far off, insistent. The world did not care about confessions.

"I know," Lena said. "Me too. Now run."

* * *

*Kael.*

They ran until dark, and the memories kept finding him.

A ravine crossing, and suddenly he knew the exact depth of the gorge below because he'd watched the Court fill it with the bodies of a village that had sheltered couriers — he vomited, wiped his mouth, kept running. A stretch of flat ground, and suddenly he remembered training hunt-mages here, teaching young acolytes to shape their grief into weapons, and one girl, sixteen, who had cried the whole time and been praised for the quality of her tears. He remembered her name. That was the cruelest part. The Court had taken his reasons but left him everyone's names.

By full dark they'd put the bells behind them — or so Lena insisted — and made camp in the shell of a watchtower whose top had melted like candlewax in some ancient burning. No fire. Cold rations. Lena took first watch, sitting in the broken window frame with her bow across her knees, silhouetted against a sky full of stars the Burnlands' dead air made mercilessly bright.

Kael couldn't sleep. Every time he closed his eyes, another door opened.

So instead he watched her, and tried to fit together what remained of himself around the shape of her.

"You're staring," she said, without turning.

"I'm trying to remember something worth keeping."

"Find anything?"

"One thing." He sat up, back against the melted stone. "Before the Court, before any of it — I was a boy in the mountain villages. North side. There was a festival in August. Everyone climbed the high road with lanterns, all the way to the treeline, and left them burning for the mountain's sake. Nobody remembered why. Three hundred years, and nobody remembered *why* — they just did it, because some oaths sink deeper than memory." His voice cracked. He let it. "I think that's what they took first. The oldest thing. And I think it's trying to come home through the cracks."

Lena turned then. Starlight caught the edge of her face, the set of her mouth — grim and soft at once, the way she looked when she was deciding something.

"When this is over," she said, "we'll climb that road ourselves. Lanterns and all. And if the mountain wants a reason from us, we'll give it one."

"And if the mountain asks a price?"

She didn't answer that. She looked away, back to the dark eastern horizon, and Kael saw her hand drift unconsciously to her chest, to the map sleeping under her shirt, pulsing its slow second heartbeat.

He was learning, slowly, to recognize the look of a person carrying a cost they hadn't told anyone about yet.

"Lena. What aren't you telling me?"

"Sleep, Kael." Gentle, and final, and terrified. "Tomorrow we reach my mother's house."

* * *

They came for them an hour before dawn.

No bells this time — the hunt-mages had learned, or been replaced by something that had. The first warning was silence: the night insects of the Burnlands, sparse but persistent, cutting off all at once along the southern ridge like a snuffed candle.

Lena was moving before Kael finished waking. "South. Multiple. Big."

Then the ridge lit white, and he saw them.

Six mages in a crescent, and between them, walking with the terrible unhurried grace of something that had never needed to hurry, a figure in robes of woven ash that moved like living smoke. Kael had seen one once, as a child, from a great distance, and been told never to speak of it. An ash-wraith. The Court's founders' own magic, worn like a coat. It had no face until it wanted one, and when it raised its arm, the stars themselves seemed to lean away.

"*Down!*"

The working hit the watchtower's ruined crown and unmade it. Stone didn't shatter; it *forgot* how to be stone, crumbling into powder that sighed away on the wind. Kael and Lena fell through the disintegrating roof into darkness, rolled, ran — out the western arch as the tower behind them dissolved into a drifting cloud of grey dust.

They ran. The wraith glided. The mathematics of it were brutal and simple.

"We can't outrun it," Lena panted.

"No. But I know what it is." Kael's head was splitting, memory after memory slamming home with each stride — the archives, the forbidden vaults, a treatise on the Court's founding weapons read by candlelight and punished with a week in the dark. "It's made of surrendered memory. Every wraith is built from what the Court strips from its servants. It's *us*, Lena. It's everyone they ever hollowed out." He grabbed her arm, pulled her behind a shelf of black rock. "Which means it has a hunger. And a shape. And a shape can be filled."

"With what?"

He pulled the silver needle from inside his collar — the one relic of the Chair he'd kept all these years, the tool that had emptied him, kept as proof and promise. He'd sworn never to use it. He turned it over once in his fingers.

"With everything I got back today," he said. "Every body in that gorge. Every crying girl. All of it goes home to its maker."

"Kael, no — you don't know what that will do to *you* —"

"I'm starting to." He smiled, and it was the realest thing he'd given her in days. "When the wraith takes it, it chokes. One minute, maybe two. That's our window — there's a gully west, leads down into the green country. Go."

"Not without you."

"Lena—"

"*Not without you.*"

For a moment neither moved, and the wraith's ash-light grew brighter over the rocks, close enough now that he could hear it breathing — a sound like a thousand people exhaling at once.

Then Lena did the thing he should have expected, the thing he was beginning to understand she always did: she ignored the argument entirely, took the needle from his hand, pricked her already-cut palm, and pressed her bleeding fist against his forehead.

"Whatever you send it," she said, "you send carrying my oath. Blackwood blood rides with it. Now *throw*."

Kael laughed — one short, astonished bark — and let go of everything.

The memories tore out of him in a torrent of light and grief: the gorge, the girl, the chair, the lanterns in August, the woman laughing in a doorway. He hurled it all at the wraith like a spear made of names, and the creature *stopped*, staggered, its smoke-shape convulsing as hundreds of stolen lives flooded home into it at once, screaming silently with borrowed voices—

"Now!"

They ran. Down through the gully, through ash into scrub, scrub into heather, heather — gods be thanked — into grass, real grass, wet with real dew, as behind them the dawn lit up a pillar of dissolving grey and the screaming faded into wind.

They didn't stop until the trees closed overhead. Old growth, deep-rooted, the first living forest in three days. Kael collapsed against a trunk, empty, wrung out, lighter than he'd felt since the ferryman — hollowed a second time, but this time by choice, and this time with something planted in the space the memories left. He could feel it. Small, steady, warm.

Her oath, riding in his blood.

Lena crouched in front of him, checking his eyes, his pulse, her hands unsteady. "Still with me?"

"Barely." He caught one of her hands. "But what's left is yours."

She held his gaze for a long moment — and then, for the first time since he'd known her, Jonah's celebrated courier, the woman who lied to guildmasters and bargained with rivers, she leaned forward and rested her forehead against his, and just breathed.

After a while she stood. "We're close now. Half a day through these woods." She looked north, toward a house she hadn't spoken of in years, and her jaw tightened. "My grandmother's letters are there. And Kael — after what that wraith showed me tonight, after what it *knew* — I need to read them before the Court does. Because I think my family has been running from this mountain longer than either of us has been alive."

She started walking. Kael rose on shaking legs and followed her into the green dark, toward the house called Blackwood, where every answer waited — and every one of them, he suspected, would cost more than the last.
