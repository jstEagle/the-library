## 15. Grandmother's Letters

The house had not changed.

That was the first cruelty of it. Lena had imagined ruin — roof fallen in, snow through the shutters, foxes denning in the pantry where the dried apples used to hang. Instead the Blackwood cottage stood exactly as she'd left it nine years ago: the blue door with its iron latch worn smooth by three generations of thumbs, the herb bundles still swaying from the crossbeam, her grandmother's rocking chair angled toward the hearth as though Maren Blackwood had only stepped out to check the snares and would be back before the kettle sang.

Dust had claimed everything else. It lay over the chair like grey fur, over the table, over the little clay figures on the mantel that Lena had shaped as a child — a bear, a bird, a woman with no face. Kael stood in the doorway behind her, snow melting off his shoulders into dark spots on the floorboards, and said nothing. He understood, somehow, that this was a threshold of a different kind than any they'd crossed on the road.

"I was born in the back room," she said. Her voice sounded strange in the stillness, too loud, then too small. "There's a scorch mark on the floorboard by the bed where my mother's birthing candle tipped. Grandmother never let anyone sand it out. She said a house should remember what happened in it."

Kael closed the door behind them and dropped the bar. "Then this house remembers you."

She almost laughed. Almost. Instead she hung their wet cloaks on the pegs by the fire — her hands knew the motion without looking, hook second from the left — and set water on to boil because there was nothing else her body knew how to do here except the small domestic liturgies she'd been raised in. The kettle's whistle, when it came, was the same note it had always been. She had to turn away from it.

* * *

They found the letters on the second day.

It should have been sooner. But the first day had been spent boarding the broken window, laying snares above the treeline, and sleeping in shifts — Lena could not stop listening for hoofbeats on the high road, for the particular silence of hunt-mages moving through snow. The Court had lost them in the Burnlands, but *lost* was a courier's word, and couriers knew better than anyone that lost things were usually just things nobody had found yet.

On the second evening, while Kael slept his watch out by the cold hearth, Lena went up to the loft for a blanket and saw the loose plank.

She had known about it since she was seven. That was the age when Maren had caught her prying at it with a dinner knife and had taken the knife away, knelt down, and looked at her with an expression Lena had spent twenty years misreading. Stern, she'd thought. Disapproving. *Some things are locked for a reason, little wren.* Now, holding her palm flat against the same plank, feeling the grain of wood her grandmother's hands had polished, she understood the look had been something else entirely. It had been grief. It had been a woman passing a weight to a child and hating herself for it.

The letters were wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord gone brittle as old cheese-rind. There were dozens of them. More than dozens. Bundles upon bundles, sorted by year in Maren's cramped, slanted hand — and beneath them, older packets in two other hands she did not recognize until she read the signatures.

One was her mother's. Sable Blackwood, dead eleven years in a rockslide on the Wethering pass, a death the guild had called bad luck.

The oldest bundle was signed simply *E. Blackwood*, and the date on the topmost letter made Lena sit down hard on the loft floor.

Three hundred and twelve years ago.

* * *

"You should sleep," Kael said from below, sometime later. She didn't know how long she'd been reading. The candle had burned to a stub; outside the window, the sky had gone the deep bruised purple that came before true dark.

"Come up," she said. Her voice didn't sound like hers. "You need to see this."

He climbed the ladder and settled across from her among the dust, and she handed him the oldest letter without a word, because she could not yet make her mouth say it aloud.

Kael read. She watched his face do what hers must have done — the small tightening at the corners of the eyes, the slow drain of color, the way his jaw set and reset as though he were chewing on something too large to swallow.

*"To my daughter, and to every daughter after her,"* E. Blackwood had written, *"I leave the truth, which is also the burden, which is also the love. Do not thank me for it. I did not choose to receive it either."*

"She was there," Lena said. "Or close enough to it. Ellena — that was her name, I think, the name got shortened down through the generations until it landed on me like something dropped from a height. She was one of the oath-keepers. One of the women who carried the words up the mountain the first time."

"The first time." Kael set the letter down with exaggerated care, the way a man handles a blade he doesn't trust himself not to fall on. "Lena, the Court's histories say the Oathstone was sealed by the founders. Nine mages of the first circle. Men, mostly. The records name them—"

"Your records were written by the men who built their throne on top of the truth. Read the next one."

He read. She watched him read, and let the words wash over her again from memory, because she already knew them by heart now and suspected she always would have known them, somewhere under her skin, the way she'd known the passes:

*"The stone does not hold an army. Let no daughter believe the pretty lie. What sleeps beneath the mountain is older than armies — it is rage itself, the god's own fury at what was done to it, and it cannot be killed and cannot be reasoned with and cannot be freed without unmaking half the world. So we bound it. Not the mages — they took the credit and built their Court on the taking — but us. The women. We put our blood into the binding, generation after generation, a thread running unbroken beneath three hundred years of ordinary lives. Every Blackwood bride, every Blackwood birth, fed the oath whether we knew the words or not. Knowing was reserved for the eldest daughter. Silence was the whole of the law."*

"There are letters here from every generation," Lena whispered. "Every single one. My great-great-grandmother writing about how she nearly told her husband and couldn't. My grandmother writing to my mother about the dreams — the mountain dreaming, the heat under the stone, all of it. They knew, Kael. All of them. Three centuries of women going to their graves carrying the end of the world in their chests and smiling at their neighbors over the garden fence."

"And telling no one."

"And telling no one." She heard the edge come into her voice and did not blunt it. "Do you understand what that means? When your Court hunted the map, when they burned Millbrook, when Corvan sold me — every step of it, they were hunting a secret that a line of farmwives and herbalists had kept safe with nothing but stubbornness and silence. No walls. No fortress. Just—" she gestured at the bundles around them, "—this. Paper and obedience."

"Not obedience." Kael picked up one of Maren's letters, the ink faded to the brown of dried blood. "Look at the hand. This last page was written fast, pressed hard. She was angry when she wrote it."

Lena took it. She knew the letter. She had read it six times, and each time it landed like a blow in a slightly different place.

*"Sable wants to tell her girl when she comes of age. I have forbidden it, and I hate myself for forbidding it, and I will forbid it again tomorrow. This is the shape of the inheritance, daughter: we are given a truth we may not share and a duty we may not refuse, and the cruelest part is that we are also given the choice — every generation, the choice — to break the chain. Burn the letters. Speak the words. Let the world take its chances. And every generation so far has chosen the silence, because the alternative is a wager with a god's fury, and none of us has ever loved our own courage more than we loved the sleeping world.*

*I do not know if this is wisdom or cowardice. I suspect it is both, braided together so tightly they cannot be picked apart. I only know that someday a Blackwood woman will read these letters and decide for herself, and whatever she decides, she must decide freely — for the binding answers to choice, never to force, and a forced renewal would shatter the stone faster than any hammer. Remember that, whoever you are. Whatever you do: it must be yours."*

The last line was underlined twice, the pen pressed so hard it had torn the paper slightly.

Lena lowered the page. The candle guttered. Down in the valley, far off, a wolf began to sing, and the sound rose up through the floorboards like something rising from very deep underground.

"My grandmother knew about me," she said. "Not me-me. But the me who would sit in this loft someday with a stranger's blood drying on her sleeve and a map in her pack. She wrote to me, Kael. Across thirty years and a grave. *Whatever you do, it must be yours.*"

"What are you thinking?"

She was quiet for a long moment. Below them the wolf finished its song and the silence came back in, thick as wool.

"I'm thinking about my mother," Lena said finally. "She died on the Wethering pass. Rockslide, they said. Bad luck. But she was a Blackwood, and Blackwoods don't die of bad luck — we die of mountains, or we don't die at all." She turned her grandmother's last letter over in her hands. "And I'm thinking that every woman in this stack chose the silence. Chose to carry it alone. Chose to let their daughters inherit the weight without warning them it was coming. And I love them for it and I am furious at them for it, and I don't think those two things can be separated any more than the wisdom and the cowardice can."

"That sounds like inheritance."

"It sounds like a chain." She stood. Her legs were stiff from hours on the loft boards; she felt every one of her twenty-nine years and then some. "My grandmother chose silence because the alternative frightened her. My mother never got the chance to choose anything. And I—" she stopped at the window, looking out at the white shoulder of the Thornfells rising against the stars, the mountain they'd been walking toward for weeks now, the mountain that had been walking toward her her whole life without her knowing. "I've been a courier my entire adult life. You know what the first law of the guild is?"

"Deliver or die trying."

"*Tell no one what you carry.*" She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Three hundred years of Blackwood women keeping the guild's oldest law without ever swearing to it. I thought I chose this life, Kael. The passes, the routes, the silence. I thought it was mine. But it was bred into me like the color of my eyes. Even my rebellion was inherited."

Kael rose and stood beside her at the window. He didn't touch her. He had learned, somewhere on the road between Millbrook and here, when not to touch her, and she both appreciated it and wished he wouldn't.

"So break it," he said quietly. "The pattern. The chain. Tell the story. Tell everyone — the villages, the lowland towns, the guilds. Let the world know what sleeps under the mountain and who has kept it sleeping. End the silence."

"And start a war," Lena said. "Because the moment people know, the Court can't pretend anymore, and Corvan can't scheme in the dark, and every ambitious fool from here to the coast starts asking why the Blackwood women get to be the keepers. Silence protected the secret, but it also protected *us*. Break the silence and you break both." She shook her head slowly. "No. There's a third thing. My grandmother wrote it and didn't know she'd written it — or maybe she did, maybe she was cleverer than all of us. *It must be yours.* Not silent, not shouted. Mine."

"Which means?"

"Which means I stop being the last link in someone else's chain." She turned from the window and looked at the letters spread across the loft floor, three centuries of women looking up at her in faded ink. "I renew the oath. In my own words, not theirs. Not because the letters command it, not because the blood demands it, not because a dying stranger made me swear on a frozen road — but because I've read every word these women left me and I understand now what they were actually protecting. Not the secret. Not even the world, exactly." Her voice steadied. "The choice itself. The right of whoever comes after me to stand in this loft and decide freely. Someone has to keep that alive, and it might as well be someone whose family has been keeping things alive for three hundred years without thanks."

Kael was silent for a long time. When he spoke, his voice was rough. "You understand what it costs."

"I understand it costs freedom. I haven't decided yet what it gives back." She crouched and gathered the letters, bundling them with a gentleness she usually saved for wounded birds. "But I know this much: my grandmother asked the question and flinched from the answer. I'm done flinching."

She had reached the bottom of the ladder, letters hugged to her chest, when Kael's hand closed on her shoulder.

"Lena." His voice had gone flat and careful, the voice he used for reports from the field. "Your snares."

She froze. She'd set four lines above the treeline that morning. A snared hare meant meat, meant another quiet day. But the snare lines ran along the game trail, and the game trail was the only approach to the cottage that couldn't be seen from the road—

"—they're singing," Kael said. "All of them. At once."

Down in the valley, the wolf had started again. And now a second voice joined it from the eastern ridge, and a third from the north, high and thin and harmonized in a way wolves were not, and Lena felt the hair rise along her arms as she recognized the sound for what it was — not animals at all, but hunt-mages calling to each other across the dark, triangulating, closing.

And beneath them, faint but unmistakable, carried up the frozen air from the direction of the high road: hoofbeats. Many horses. Moving unhurried, the way riders move when they already know where they're going.

Someone had sold them. Again.

Lena pressed the letters tighter against her chest, felt three centuries of women press back, and reached for the iron latch her thumb had worn smooth as a child.

"They wanted the key," she said quietly. "Let's show them what it opens."
