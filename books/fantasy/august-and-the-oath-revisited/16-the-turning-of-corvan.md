## 16. The Turning of Corvan

The Ashen Court did not sleep so much as it smoldered. Corvan had learned that in his first month beneath the mountain fortress of Cinderhold — learned it in the way the corridors never went fully dark, in the way the matriarch's servants moved through the night hours with lamps hooded to a dull red glow, as if even light here were something to be rationed and controlled. He had told himself, in those first weeks, that he would grow used to it. That a man who had spent thirty years running a smuggling guild out of the Thornfells could grow used to anything.

He had been wrong about many things lately.

He stood now at the window of the chamber they had given him — a fine chamber, by any honest measure, with a real bed and a brazier that never went cold and a view down the long grey shoulder of the mountains toward the burnlands where the Court's hunt-mages were even now combing the foothills for two fugitives. Fine chambers, Corvan had discovered, were just cages with better upholstery.

In his hand he turned a coin. Old guild mint, worn nearly smooth, the thorn-and-bridge sigil of Thornfell rubbed to a ghost of itself by thirty years of thumbs. He flipped it. Caught it. Flipped it again.

*You sold her,* said the voice in his head that sounded uncomfortably like his dead wife. *You sold the girl you taught to read a trail at nine years old.*

*I saved the guild,* he answered it, as he always answered. *What was left of it.*

*There is nothing left of it. You know that. You signed its death warrant and called it mercy.*

The coin stopped mid-flip. He caught it, closed his fist around it until the edges bit.

Below, in the courtyard, torches moved. A rider coming up the switchback road through the last of the evening light — one of the hunt-mages' outriders, ash-grey cloak stiff with frost, dismounting before his horse had fully stopped. Corvan watched the man cross the courtyard at a run and vanish into the keep below, and felt the old courier's instinct stir in him like a scar aching before rain.

Bad news traveled fast. Good news walked.

This news was running.

* * *

They summoned him within the hour. Not to the matriarch's hall — he was not granted that honor often, and he had learned to be grateful for its absence — but to the war room on the third level, where a table of black slate held a map of the western mountains carved in relief, and where Veyra, the Court's hunt-captain, stood with her gauntlets off and her hands flat on the stone like a woman holding down something that wanted to rise.

"You've seen her work," Veyra said without preamble. She did not look up. "The passes. Tell me where she goes."

Corvan kept his face still. It was a skill he had taught Lena, once, in the early years — *your face is a door, girl; learn what's behind it before you let anyone open it.* He wondered if she remembered. He wondered if she was using it against these people right now, somewhere in the dark, and the thought gave him a flicker of something he refused to name.

"She'll take the Shepherd's Stair," he said. "If she's made it past the burnlands. It's the only route west that doesn't funnel through open ground. She knows it better than anyone alive."

"Better than you?"

"I taught her." The words came out flatter than he intended.

Veyra looked up then. Her eyes were the pale grey of wood-ash, and they had the particular patience of someone who had already decided how this conversation would end. "Then you understand our difficulty. The matriarch wants the courier taken alive if possible. The map intact. But the defector—" her mouth tightened around Kael's name the way a mouth tightens around a rot, "—Kael is to be killed on sight. No capture. No negotiation. He has seen the inner vaults. He has seen *her*. And now, we believe, he has seen the map."

"And me?" Corvan asked. "Where do I fit in your difficulty?"

Veyra straightened. From a fold of her cloak she drew a folded paper — Court paper, heavy and cream-colored, sealed with grey wax — and laid it on the slate between them.

"The matriarch has reviewed your service," she said. "Your information regarding the courier's likely routes proved accurate. Your cooperation is noted. When the stone is broken and the new order is established, there will be need of men who know the mountains. Roads. Guilds." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "There will be need of a guildmaster."

The word landed in Corvan's chest like a dropped stone. *Guildmaster.* Not the hollow title he'd clung to over a dying guild of eleven souls and mounting debts — a real one. A guild reborn under the Court's banner, with roads and routes and authority, with the Thornfells finally, *finally* brought to heel instead of slowly bleeding out. Everything he'd told himself he was buying when he'd sent that message to the Court from Millbrook waystation. Everything he'd told himself was worth the price.

"There is a condition," Veyra said.

He had known there would be. He had known since the moment she unrolled the paper.

"The matriarch requires certainty. The courier cannot be permitted to reach the Oathstone. If the hunt fails — if she slips the net on the mountain road — you will be the one who closes it." Veyra slid the paper toward him. "You will be given a blade and a position on the road above the Shepherd's Stair. Alone. A familiar face opens doors that soldiers cannot. And when the moment comes—"

"You want me to kill her myself."

"I want you to *prove* yourself," Veyra said. "The killing is incidental."

*Incidental.* Corvan looked at the paper. At the seal. At the elegant, patient handwriting of the matriarch's clerk offering him a kingdom built on a child's corpse.

Lena Blackwood was twenty-three years old. He had pulled her out of a snowdrift when she was six, blue-lipped and furious about it. He had taught her the difference between a game trail and a smuggler's cut. He had stood at her mother's funeral and promised — not aloud, never aloud, but promised all the same — that the guild would be her family now, that he would be, in whatever cold and inadequate way a man like him could manage, her father.

And three weeks ago he had sold her location to the people hunting her for the price of a chair.

"Yes," Corvan heard himself say. "I'll do it."

His voice did not shake. That was the worst part. Thirty years of lying to toll-keepers and tax-men and dying clients' families, and his voice had learned to carry a lie so smoothly that even he sometimes couldn't hear the crack in it.

But it was there. He could feel it. Thin as a hairline fracture in ice, running under everything, waiting for weight.

* * *

They gave him a horse and a blade and sent him up ahead of the host, because a familiar face travels lighter than an army. The mountain road above the Shepherd's Stair was old beyond memory — a ledge cut into the cliff face by hands no chronicle recorded, wide enough for one mule, dropping away on the left into a gorge full of cloud. In summer it was the fastest way west. In winter it was a graveyard with a view.

It was neither season now. It was late autumn, and the wind came up the gorge smelling of snow that hadn't fallen yet, and Corvan rode the ledge alone with a Court blade at his hip and his old guild knife hidden in his boot, and he did not examine too closely why he had brought both.

He found their camp at dusk on the second day.

Not because he was tracking well — though he was, some part of him still was, reading the broken spiderweb and the scuffed stone with the automatic ease of decades — but because they had *let* themselves be found. He understood it only as he reined in at the edge of the firelight: the camp sat in a hollow with one approach, and that approach was the one he'd come by, and there was no sign of alarm, no movement in the shadows, nothing.

Either they were exhausted past caution, or Lena Blackwood knew exactly who was walking into her fire, and had decided to meet him standing up.

She was sitting on a stone with her palms out to the flames, and she looked — gods, she looked *thin*. Cheekbones he could have mapped by touch. A healing cut along her jaw. Her eyes lifted to his and held there, and there was no surprise in them at all, and that absence of surprise was more damning than any accusation.

"Hello, Corvan," she said.

Kael rose from the far side of the fire, slow and deliberate, one hand nowhere near his sword, which was its own kind of message. His face was drawn too, and there was something wrong with his eyes — a hollowness behind them, like a house with the furniture removed. Corvan had heard the reports. The ferryman's toll. The defector had paid a memory, and no one knew which one, and the Court's mages found the uncertainty more frightening than any weapon.

"Guildmaster," Kael said. The title was precise. An insult delivered as courtesy.

"Lena." Corvan dismounted. His knees cracked. He was fifty-eight years old and suddenly every one of those years was sitting on his shoulders like wet wool. "You're not going to ask why I'm here."

"I've been asking why you're here for three weeks," Lena said. "Millbrook answered it." She turned her hands back to the fire. "Sit down or don't. I'm not going to beg you to stay, and I'm not going to beg you to leave. I'm done begging from you."

He should have done it then. That was the moment — he saw it clearly afterward, the way a man sees the exact plank that broke under him. She was tired. Kael was watching but unhurried, and Corvan had the element of surprise and a blade and orders, and if he had crossed that fire fast and low, the way he'd taught her to cross a fight, it might have been done. One motion. One life. One guildmaster's chair, bought and paid for.

Instead he sat down on the stone across the fire from her, because his legs chose it for him, and the blade stayed in its sheath, and the silence stretched.

"You look like her mother," he said, and immediately wished he had swallowed it.

Lena's jaw tightened. "Don't."

"You have her way of sitting. Like the world owes her an apology and she's waiting to collect." He heard himself talking and could not stop, words spilling out of the crack in the ice like water finding it. "I used to tell her she'd get us all killed someday, being right about everything. She'd laugh. You laugh the same. Did you know that? Same laugh."

"Corvan." Her voice was quiet. "Why are you here?"

And there it was. The question, laid across the fire between them like the blade he wasn't drawing. He could lie. He had lied to her once already, in the guildhall, with his hunger showing through his teeth while she lied back — the first lie of her career, she'd probably thought herself so clever, and she'd been right, and he'd known the map was in her coat the whole time. He could lie again. Tell her the Court was behind him, that he'd come to warn her, that the pass was watched. Half of it would even be true.

Or he could sit in the truth for one moment, one single moment, before the moment passed.

"They offered me the guild," he said. "Rebuilt. Charters, roads, authority. Everything Thornfell was before the wars gutted it. All I had to do was sell you." He looked at the fire. "So I did."

Kael shifted, but Lena didn't move. She just watched him with her mother's eyes, and Corvan understood that she had already known this too — had known it since Millburn, since the razed waystation, since the enforcers on the pass — and had carried the knowledge alone for weeks the way couriers carry everything, silently, because the cargo doesn't get to complain.

"Why are you telling me now?"

"Because they ordered me to kill you." He said it plainly. There was a terrible relief in saying it plainly. "Tonight. On this road. A familiar face, they said. Doors open easier for family." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged. "*Family.* Thirty years in the guild and I never once used that word out loud until the Court handed it to me as a murder tactic."

"And the blade?" Kael asked quietly. "Is it in your boot, or your belt?"

Corvan looked at him. "Both. Habit."

"Habit," Kael repeated, and something in his ruined eyes suggested he understood habits better than most.

Lena stood. She was swaying slightly with exhaustion, and still she managed to look down at him from the height of twenty-three years and one unforgivable betrayal, and what she said next was worse than any curse.

"I'm not going to kill you, Corvan. I want you to know that, so you can stop waiting for it. Whatever you decide in the next five minutes, I'm not going to be the one who does it." She picked up her pack. "Because you taught me that a courier delivers what she carries, all the way to the end, and I still believe you meant it when you taught me that. Some part of you meant it. I'm betting my life that some part of you still means it."

"That's a fool's bet."

"I know," she said. "I'm a Blackwood. We bet foolishly and win anyway. Grandmother's rule."

Behind her, Kael was already kicking dirt over the fire, moving with the economy of a man who had decided the conversation was over before it started. "The ledge continues west another mile," he said. "Then the climb to the high col. If the Court's host is behind you—"

"It's half a day behind me. Hunt-mages with the scent." Corvan rose. His joints protested. "They'll reach this camp by midmorning."

"Then we ride now." Lena swung her pack up. Then she paused, and looked back at him, and for a moment — just a moment — the mentor and the student stood on either side of a fire's dead ashes, and the years fell away, and she was nine years old in a snowdrift again, furious and alive.

"Coming, guildmaster?" she said. "Or staying?"

* * *

He did not answer her. He answered the mountain.

That was the truth of it, though it took him the rest of the night to understand. They rode west along the ledge in the dark, single file, hooves wrapped in sacking to mute the sound — her idea, his old trick, returned to him like a coin coming home — and somewhere in the second mile, when the gorge opened below them full of cloud and moonlight and the high col showed its white shoulder against the stars, Corvan understood that he had already chosen. Not tonight. Not at the fire. Perhaps thirty years ago, when he'd pulled a blue-lipped child out of a snowdrift and felt something in his chest crack open that he'd spent the rest of his life pretending was ambition.

The Court's promises had filled that crack with ash. But ash is not stone. Ash blows away.

They reached the mouth of the climb as the sky went grey, and there — filling the valley behind them like smoke poured uphill — came the first torches of the hunt. Too early. Faster than half a day. Someone had ridden ahead of him, or the matriarch had never trusted him at all, and the choice he thought he'd made was being taken out of his hands.

"They'll cut us off at the col," Kael said, scanning the heights. "There's a scout line already on the ridge — see the glint, left of the snowfield?"

"I see it." Lena's face was pale in the dawn light, but steady. "Corvan. The ledge forks ahead. There's a way up they won't expect — the old quarry stair. It's brutal, and it's exposed, but it puts us above their line instead of below it."

"How do you know it's still passable?"

"Because you showed it to me when I was twelve," she said, "and told me never to show anyone, ever, and I'm showing two people right now, which means I'm trusting you with the only secret I ever kept from you." She kicked her horse forward. "Prove me right."

The quarry stair was everything she'd promised — brutal, exposed, a ribcage of cut granite climbing the cliff's face with the whole grey morning yawning beneath. Halfway up, lungs burning, Corvan looked back.

The hunt had reached the fork. He could see them spreading through it, ash-grey cloaks among the pines, and at their head a figure in white-grey armor who raised one arm, and the glint of a spyglass found the stair, found them, held.

Veyra. She had come herself. And she was looking directly at him, at the traitor crawling up her mountain with the people she'd sworn him to kill, and even at this distance Corvan could feel the question in her gaze:

*Which side of the crack are you standing on, old man?*

He turned away from her. He put his head down. He climbed.

Above him, Lena's hand reached back down through the thin cold air — not asking to be taken, exactly. Just there. Offered. The way a hand is offered across a fire, across a betrayal, across thirty years of ash.

"Almost there, old man," she called down. "Keep up."

And Corvan, guildmaster of nothing, father of no one, keeper of one cracked and buried promise, took her hand, and climbed.
