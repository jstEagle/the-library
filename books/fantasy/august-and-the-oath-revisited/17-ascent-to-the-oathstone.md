## 17. Ascent to the Oathstone

The storm found them at the treeline, as if the mountain itself had been waiting to test them one last time.

Lena stood at the edge of the pines and looked up. Above her, the peak of Thornfell vanished into a churn of grey cloud, lightning flickering inside it like something breathing. The old road — the courier's road, the one her grandmother had traced in flour on the kitchen table — wound up out of sight, switchbacking into the white. Wind came down the slope carrying ice and the smell of deep stone.

Behind her, the valley burned. She could see the Ashen Court's host strung out along the road from the Burnlands, a thousand points of torchlight in the dusk, patient as a tide. They had stopped at the treeline. For now.

"They're waiting for the storm to break us first," Kael said beside her. His voice was rough; the fragments of returned memory had cost him a fever that still hadn't fully burned out, and he leaned on a staff cut from ash wood — a bitter joke, he'd said, that Lena hadn't laughed at. "The matriarch knows this mountain. She knows the storm up there is older than her throne."

"Then we don't wait for it to be kind." Lena tightened the straps of her pack. The map rode against her chest, wrapped in oilcloth, and she could feel it now in a way she never had before — not as paper and ink, but as a warmth, a second heartbeat pressed to her own. Since the blood had woken it in the hollow beneath the world, it had never been quiet. It pulled. It had always been pulling, she understood that now; every pass she'd ever run, every smuggler's road she'd mastered, had been the mountain teaching her the way home.

*Not a courier,* her grandmother's letters had said. *A key. And keys are not carried. Keys are used.*

"Your hands are shaking," Kael said.

"It's the cold."

"It isn't."

She looked at him. Wind tore at his dark hair, at the grey cloak he still wore because it was the warmest thing he owned, and his eyes — the eyes of a man who had once knelt in the Court of Cinders and meant it — held something she had stopped trying to name weeks ago. She'd stopped because naming it would have been a promise, and promises on this mountain had a way of becoming permanent.

"When we reach the stone," she said, "I have to turn it myself. No one can force it. That's the whole design — the Blackwoods, the oath, all of it. The key only works if it's *chosen*." She flexed her fingers inside her gloves. "Which means the Court can't take the mountain by taking me. They can drag me up there bleeding and it will do them nothing."

"Then why are they climbing?"

"Because they don't believe it. Because their whole order is built on the idea that everything can be taken, held, controlled." She turned back to the slope. "And because if they can't force the key, they can break the lock. They'll try to shatter the stone around me and let the god's rage out wild, and rule whatever's left."

Kael was quiet for a moment. Then: "Then we'd better be fast."

Lena almost smiled. "Almost" — because the last time she'd smiled fully had been in her grandmother's kitchen, and that was another country now. "Keep up, Court man. The passes only forgive couriers."

She stepped off the treeline and into the storm.

* * *

The first three hours were the kind of climbing Lena had been born into. Hidden ways, her grandmother had called them — ledges behind waterfalls of ice, a chimney of cracked stone that looked impassable until you knew to wedge your shoulders left, a shelf where the wind itself seemed to part around a groove worn by three hundred years of Blackwood boots. She moved through them with the calm of long habit, and behind her Kael climbed with the grim competence of a man who had learned mountains as a soldier learns walls: thoroughly, and without love.

The storm thickened as they rose. Snow came sideways, then came from below, torn up off the slopes in white sheets. Twice Lena had to rope Kael across a gap where the path had fallen away since her last crossing — since, she realized, her mother's last crossing, and her grandmother's before that. The mountain kept its own ledger.

It was on the fourth hour, on a ridge barely wide enough for two, that the first ambush came.

No warning. No shout. Just a flicker of ash-grey against the white ahead, and then a crossbow bolt hissing out of the murk. Lena dropped flat; the bolt went over her shoulder and shrieked away into the void. Kael was already moving, staff sweeping low, and a Court hunter came out of the snow with a blade of grey steel and a face wrapped to the eyes.

"Vess," Kael said. The name carried something old and tired. "Stand down. It's finished. You know what she is now."

The hunter — a woman, by the voice that answered — did not stand down. "The matriarch says the key comes down the mountain or the mountain comes down on the valley. Those are the terms, Kael. You of all people know we keep our terms."

"You kept terms at Millbrook, too," Kael said, and there was something in his voice Lena had heard only once before — in the hollow beneath, when he'd spoken of the god's rage and the lie beneath the Court's throne. Grief wearing anger's clothes. "How many waystation families, Vess? How many burned to keep the route quiet?"

The hunter's blade wavered — a hair's breadth, no more. In that hair's breadth, Lena acted. She didn't fight; fighting on a knife-edge ridge in a whiteout was dying with extra steps. She did what couriers do. She threw her weight against the hunter's leading leg, felt the woman stagger, and rolled past her down the ridge on the downhill side, into the wind-shadow of a boulder, pulling Kael after her by the wrist.

Bolts chased them. Then the storm itself intervened, a gust like a thrown wall of snow, and when it cleared the ridge was empty in both directions.

They didn't stop moving until the ridge gave onto a sheltered col, a bowl of stone where the wind died and the snow fell straight down, soft as ash.

Lena leaned against the rock, chest heaving, and realized her hands had stopped shaking.

Not from the cold leaving. From something settling. Somewhere between the treeline and here, between the bolt and the boulder, the last of her fear had burned off like morning fog — not because the danger was gone, but because she had finally stopped being afraid *of the wrong things*. Not of dying. Not of the Court. What remained was only the thing itself: the stone, the oath, the choice she had already made and was simply walking toward.

"Vess will report we took the ridge," Kael said, checking the fletching of a spent bolt with a professional's detachment. "There will be more. Half the host is on the mountain by now — the matriarch won't wait for the storm after all. Not once she knows the key is climbing."

"Good," Lena said.

Kael looked at her.

"I said *good*." She heard her own voice and barely recognized it — level, quiet, the voice she'd used on the day she'd lied to Corvan and the day she'd refused him in the cell below. "I spent my whole life running routes so that nothing would ever catch me. Every pass, every hidden way — it was all about *not being found*. And I was good at it, Kael. I was the best the guild ever had, and I hated every mile of it, because a courier who's never caught is a courier who's never *arrived*." She pushed off the rock and straightened. "I'm done running. Let them come. Every one they send up this mountain is one fewer burning a village in the valley, and every step they take, the mountain is deciding whether they belong here."

"The mountain decides," Kael repeated. Not mocking. Testing.

"You felt it in the hollow. You told me yourself — the god's rage isn't a prisoner, it's a *wound*, and wounds know who comes to them with hands open and hands closed." She held out her palm, the old cut from the blood-rite, half-healed and aching in the cold. "The stone's been teaching me since the map woke. Every pass I know, I didn't learn them. I *remembered* them. My grandmother didn't teach me the mountain, Kael. The mountain taught my grandmother, and she taught me, and now it's done teaching and it's asking."

"Asking what?"

Lena looked up through the falling snow, toward the summit she could not see but could feel the way you feel a hand on the back of your neck. Warmth. Attention. Patience three hundred years deep.

"Whether I'll keep the oath my own way," she said. "Come on. We're losing the light."

* * *

The last ambush found them an hour below the summit, on the shoulder of the peak where the old shrine stood — a ring of standing stones, frost-sheathed, that Lena's grandmother had called the Doorstep. The Court's hunters came out of the storm in force this time: six of them, grey cloaks stiff with ice, blades drawn, and at their center a figure Lena knew.

Corvan.

He looked hollowed. The fine guildmaster's coat was gone, replaced by Court grey, and the fire she'd known in him all her life — the hunger, the ambition, the sharp bright heat of him — had guttered down to something she almost pitied. Almost.

"Lena." His voice carried strangely in the wind, flattened by the storm. "Give me the map. The matriarch will let you walk. I have her word."

"You had my guild's word once too," Lena said. "You sold it for a seat at her table."

"I sold *nothing* that wasn't already dying." For a moment the old fire flickered — grief in ambition's clothes, this time. "The Thornfell Guild is three men and a ledger of debts, girl. I buried it with open eyes. You'd have buried it with your head in the snow. One of us was honest."

"Honest," Kael said softly, and stepped forward, and the six hunters' blades came up — and stopped. Because Kael had gone very still, and the storm had gone very still with him, snowflakes hanging in the air like held breath, and Lena understood that the mountain was listening now. That it had been listening since the Doorstep. That her calm was not hers alone anymore.

"Corvan," she said, and her voice came out of the stillness like a bell. "I'm not going to fight you. I'm not going to give you the map. I'm going to walk past you, up that slope, and you're going to let me — or you're going to stop me, and the mountain is going to decide what you are."

"You're a courier, Lena. You carry things. That's all you've ever—"

"No." The word came out of her without effort, without heat, the way a blade leaves a sheath. "I was never carrying it. I was *becoming* it. And you can't take what someone has become."

The stillness deepened. Snow hung unmoving between them, a thousand white points, and in that silence Lena walked forward — past the hunters' blades, past their wide eyes, straight at the man who had been her mentor, her guildmaster, her whole world's north star for fifteen years.

Corvan's hand came up. The hunters tensed.

And Corvan looked at her — at the calm in her, at the storm refusing to touch her — and his hand closed on empty air and fell back to his side.

"Go," he said. Barely a sound. Then, louder, turning to the hunters, his voice cracking like river ice: "The storm's too thick. We hold the Doorstep. *We hold the Doorstep* — that's the order."

Lena did not look back. But she heard it — the hunters' confusion, Kael's boots behind her, and under it all, faint and rising, the sound of the mountain exhaling.

* * *

The summit opened before them like a wound in the world.

The storm stopped at its edge — not faded, *stopped*, a wall of whirling white ringing a circle of impossible calm, and inside it the peak lay bare under a sky of stars that had no business being visible at any hour, let alone this one. And at the center of the circle stood the Oathstone.

It was not what the legends said. It was not a monolith, not a jewel, not a throne. It was a seam — a place where the mountain's stone had been parted and bound again, a scar in the world the height of a woman, glowing faintly from within with a light like embers under ash. And it was *warm*. Lena felt it from thirty paces, felt it against her skin like standing before a hearth, and beneath the warmth, vast and slow and old, something turned in its sleep.

The god's rage. Not caged. *Held.* The way you hold a grieving thing. The way her grandmother must have held her, all those nights, telling bedtime warnings that were really instructions.

Behind them, at the edge of the circle, the storm wall churned and spat figures — hunters, then more, then the vanguard of the host itself, grey cloaks pouring through the gap the mountain allowed them. The matriarch would come last. Lena knew it the way she knew the passes.

"Kael." She didn't turn. "When I touch it — whatever happens — don't try to pull me back. Promise me."

"Lena—"

"*Promise me.*"

A long breath behind her. Then, quietly: "I promise. But I'm staying right here until you're done. Whatever done turns out to mean."

She smiled then — fully, for the first time since the kitchen, since the flour-map, since the world was small and warm. And she walked to the stone.

The map against her chest was burning now, not with heat but with *rightness*, and she drew it out and unwrapped it and held it up, and the ink rose off the vellum like smoke and poured into the seam of light. The dying stranger's map. Her grandmother's map. The mountain's map, coming home at last.

Lena Blackwood, last courier of the Thornfell Guild, laid her cut palm against the Oathstone.

The warmth took her hand like a handshake. Like a door opening. Like a question three hundred years old, finally asked aloud:

*Do you choose this?*

"I do," Lena said, to the mountain, to the god, to every Blackwood woman who had ever carried the secret in silence. "But I choose it *my* way."

The light rose. The mountain drew breath.

And through the storm wall, robed in ash and starlight, the matriarch of the Ashen Court stepped into the circle, and her voice, when it came, was almost gentle:

"Child. Before you bind yourself — there is something you must know about the oath. Something even your grandmother never learned."
