## 22. August, Again

The high road was supposed to be haunted.

Everyone in Millbrook said so. Old Tam said the wind up there spoke in a dead woman's voice. The baker's wife said travelers who took the pass after dark came down the other side with white streaks in their hair and no memory of the crossing. Wren's own grandmother — who was not her grandmother at all but the woman who had taken her in when the fever year emptied her parents' house of everything but silence — said only: *the mountain keeps what it loves, and it loves what keeps it.*

Wren had decided, at eleven years old, that this meant the road was safe. Haunted things kept things. Kept things were not lost things.

So on the last hot morning of August, when the barley stood gold in the valley and the air smelled of cut grain and woodsmoke, she filled a satchel with bread, a skin of water, and her grandmother's letters — the real ones, the ones hidden under a floorboard for thirty years — and she climbed.

She told herself she was going to see the view. She told herself she was going to prove the village wrong. But the letters pressed against her hip like a second heartbeat, and she knew, in the way children know things they cannot yet say, that she was going because she had been called.

* * *

The first hour was easy. The road wound up through pine shadow, the river flashing silver far below, and Wren sang to keep her courage up — the old courier songs, the ones about passes and promises, though half their verses had been worn smooth by generations of mouths that no longer remembered what they meant.

The second hour was harder. The trees thinned. The sun climbed and the air grew thin and bright, and the path narrowed to a shelf of stone above a drop that made her stomach fold like wet paper. Twice she nearly turned back. Twice she thought of the letters, and of the last line of the last one, written in a hand that shook:

*If you are reading this, child, then the road has found you, as roads do. Walk it.*

In the third hour, she met the woman made of stone and starlight.

* * *

Wren saw her first as a trick of the light — a figure standing at the bend of the pass where the morning sun struck the granite face of the mountain, and the granite seemed to have gathered itself up into a shape. A woman's shape. Tall, still, wearing a cloak that was not cloth but weather: slow clouds moving across her shoulders, and beneath them skin like pale stone veined with faint blue fire, and eyes —

Wren stopped walking. Her satchel strap bit into her palm. The eyes were the worst and best thing, because they were not frightening at all. They were kind, and old, and lit from within like windows of a house where someone is always home.

"You're late," the woman said. Her voice was the sound a river makes under ice. "I've been waiting since spring."

"You've been—" Wren's voice cracked. She swallowed and tried again. "You've been waiting for me?"

"For someone." The stone woman smiled, and the smile moved across her face like dawn moving across a mountainside. "It is usually someone. It is rarely who I expect." She tilted her head, studying Wren the way Wren studied interesting beetles. "You have Blackwood hands."

"I don't know what that means."

"No," the woman said softly. "But you will. Come here. Show me your palms."

Every sensible part of Wren screamed at her to run. She did not run. Later, she would understand why: some meetings feel less like encounters than like returns. She crossed the last twenty paces of the pass and held out her hands, and the woman took them, and her touch was cool and solid and gentle as evening.

"Calluses," the woman murmured, tracing Wren's fingers. "From climbing. From gripping. You climb things you shouldn't, don't you? Walls. Trees. Other people's fences."

"The baker's roof," Wren admitted.

"And here—" the thumb paused over Wren's left palm, over a scar Wren had carried since she was six, a thin white line she'd gotten falling through a greenhouse window and never been able to explain to anyone, least of all herself, why she'd been reaching for the glass in the first place. The woman went very still. The blue veins in her wrists brightened.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, you clever, stubborn blood. You found your way back."

"Found my way back from where?"

The woman looked at her for a long moment. Wind moved through her cloud-cloak. Far below, a hawk rode the thermals off the crags, and its cry came up thin and lonely.

"Sit," the woman said at last. "Eat your bread. And I will tell you a story about a courier, and a map, and an oath. It is a long story, and it ends with you, though you don't know it yet."

* * *

Her name was Lena.

That was the first thing she gave, before anything else, as if names were debts and she paid hers promptly. *Lena Blackwood*, she said, and watched Wren's face for the flinch, and got it.

"Blackwood," Wren whispered. "Like the letters. Like—"

"Like the woman who wrote them, yes." Lena settled onto the stone beside her, arranging her impossible cloak around her with the ease of long practice. Up close, Wren could see that she was not entirely stone — there was movement in her, warmth even, deep down, like embers banked in granite. "Your grandmother's mother carried those letters out of these mountains thirty years ago. Before that, her mother carried them. Before that—" she smiled wryly, "—well. It is a very old satchel, that family. We have always been good at carrying."

"Carrying what?"

"Stories. Secrets. Sometimes both in the same bundle." Lena looked out over the valley, gold and green and small below them, threaded with smoke from morning fires. "Do you know what the Thornfell Guild was?"

"Couriers," Wren said. "Smugglers, mostly. They died out before my mother was born. There's a ruin up past the mill with their sign carved on it. A thorn branch."

"A thorn branch," Lena agreed. "A crooked one. Because honest roads get watched, and crooked ones get walked." She was quiet a moment. "I was the last sworn courier of that guild. The very last. And on a night much colder than this one, on this very stretch of road, a dying man put a map in my hands and made me swear to carry it west."

"What was on the map?"

Lena turned her hand over. In her stone palm, faint as frost on a window, lines appeared — ridgelines, a gorge, a spiral of peaks closing on a single point high in the heart of the range. Wren caught her breath. She had seen this map. She had seen it a hundred times, traced in ink gone brown with age, in the letters under the floorboard.

"That's—"

"Yes."

"But that's just a story. Grandmother said— my grandmother said the Oathstone was a story mothers tell to keep children off the high road."

"It is." Lena's eyes glinted. "It was also the truest thing in these mountains. Both can be so. That is rather the point of stories." She closed her hand and the map faded. "There was a war once, child. Older than any kingdom you have a name for. A god's rage, buried under the peaks, sealed by an oath spoken by people who loved the world enough to give up their places in it. For three hundred years the seal held, because couriers carried the knowledge of it quietly, and wardens renewed it faithfully, and everyone else had the mercy of forgetting."

"And then?"

"And then people grew hungry, and proud, and impatient. A court rose that wanted the rage released, believing they could rule what came after. A guildmaster betrayed his own courier for a seat at their table. And a girl from a dying guild ran the passes with a map burning a hole in her heart, learning too slowly that she wasn't carrying the secret at all." Lena touched her own chest, over the place a heart would be. "She *was* the secret. Her blood was bound into the oath before she was born. Every Blackwood woman for three centuries knew it, and every one of them chose silence, because silence kept the world safe."

Wren looked down at her own hands. At the thin white scar across her left palm.

"What happened to her?" she asked. "The girl. The last courier."

"She climbed," Lena said simply. "In a storm, with an army at her heels and the man she loved bleeding beside her, she climbed to the stone and she spoke the oath in her own words — not the old words, because the old words belonged to dead people, and oaths live only in living mouths. And the mountain accepted her. And the rage beneath it settled like a storm settling into snow. And the armies turned away, because the land itself had chosen, and land always wins in the end."

"Did she die?"

Lena laughed — a low, warm sound, like water over stones, utterly unlike anything a mountain should make. "No. That was the strange mercy of it. She did not die. She simply stopped being free. The stone needed a warden, and a warden must stay. She has been here ever since. Watching the passes. Keeping the oath. Waiting for the road to find someone new."

The wind picked up, combed through Wren's hair, and she shivered — not from cold, but from the sudden vertiginous sense of standing at the top of a very long staircase, all of it built by hands she was somehow related to.

"The letters," she said. "Grandmother's letters. They say— the last one says the guild isn't dead. It says it's sleeping. It says *a guild is not its buildings or its sign or its ledger. A guild is whoever carries the next parcel.*"

Lena's expression changed. Something moved behind her eyes like light behind cloud — grief, maybe, or joy, or the place where the two had lived together so long they'd become indistinguishable.

"I wrote that line," she said quietly. "Thirty years ago, dictated to a friend with better handwriting than mine, and sent down the mountain with the last parcel I ever carried." She looked at Wren. "What was the parcel, do you know? What did your grandmother's mother carry out that day?"

Wren shook her head. "Just the letters."

"Just the letters." Lena nodded slowly. "Yes. That would be right. That would be exactly right." She drew a breath that stirred the air around them both. "Wren. Child with Blackwood hands and a thief's relationship with rooftops. Will you hear the rest? Not the story — the rest. The part that comes after stories. The part with work in it."

* * *

They talked until the sun stood straight overhead, and then until it leaned west, and Wren forgot to be afraid, and forgot to be hungry, and forgot everything except the shape of what Lena showed her.

She showed her the passes. All of them — not just the high road but the crooked ones, the courier ways, the paths that existed only in the memory of the mountain itself, which Lena was now, and which she could open like a hand opening. She showed Wren where the scree could be crossed at dawn and never at dusk. Where the springs ran sweet and where they ran bitter. Which cairns marked graves and which marked caches and which marked nothing at all except that someone, long ago, had wanted a later walker to know they had passed this way and noticed the world.

"Why teach me?" Wren asked, somewhere in the golden afternoon. "Why me? I'm nobody. I'm just—"

"You are a girl who climbed a haunted road alone because a dead woman's letters told her to," Lena said. "That is not nobody. That is the entire job description." She was quiet a moment, and when she spoke again her voice had gentled. "And because someone taught me. Not the passes — I knew the passes. Someone taught me that carrying a thing is not the same as being burdened by it. That a secret given freely is a gift, and a gift hoarded is a grave. I learned it too late to save myself my freedom. Perhaps you'll learn it early enough to spend yours well."

"Is that what happened to you? You spent yours?"

"I gave mine," Lena corrected. "Spending implies you got something back." She smiled, and it was the saddest and most contented smile Wren had ever seen. "Though I did get something back. I got him, for a while. Kael. He carried our story west, the way he swore he would, and he told it to anyone who would listen, even when the Court's remnants hunted storytellers. He told it until the telling wore the fear away. He told it until there were more listeners than hunters." Her gaze went far away, down the valleys, across decades. "He came back every August, you know. Climbed as far as the treeline. We'd talk until dark. He'd bring news, and wine he couldn't share with me, and complaints about his knees. Forty years of Augusts." The blue fire in her veins flickered. "The last one, he told me he'd found a baby girl whose family had died of fever, and he couldn't stop thinking about her hands."

Wren's breath stopped.

"He said her hands reminded him of mine," Lena went on softly. "Climber's hands. Courier's hands. He placed her with a woman he trusted — a woman who owed him her life and her brother's life and a great many other things besides — and he asked her to raise the girl with the letters nearby, and the floorboard loose, and the road close." She turned to look at Wren fully, and the whole mountain seemed to lean in. "He died three winters ago. Peacefully. An old man in a warm bed, which for men like us counts as a miracle. His last words were a question. He asked whether the road had found you yet."

Wren was crying. She didn't remember starting. The tears came down her face and dropped onto the stone between them, and where they fell, tiny flowers opened — white stars of some alpine bloom that had no business growing at this height, in this season, blooming anyway, because the ground they fell on was not entirely ground.

"I never met him," she managed.

"No. But he met you. He used to watch you from a distance, on market days. He said you walked like the road belonged to you." Lena reached out and, with exquisite care, wiped a tear from Wren's cheek with a thumb of warm stone. "He was right. It does."

* * *

They descended together as the light went long and amber, and Lena taught her the first lesson of many: how to read the mountain's moods in the behavior of birds, how the swifts flying low meant rain within the hour, how the marmots' alarm calls mapped danger better than any patrol ever could. By the time they reached the treeline, Wren's legs ached and her mind was full and her satchel felt lighter, as if the letters inside had finally set down something they'd been carrying.

At the edge of the pines, Lena stopped.

"This is where I end," she said. "Stone doesn't do well among roots. And this is where you begin." She crouched to Wren's level, which took a long time, unfolding like a cliff deciding to be a doorway. "I'm going to ask you something, and you mustn't answer tonight. Sleep on it. Dream on it. Talk to your grandmother — she knows more than she's ever said, and it's eating her alive. Ask her about the guild. Ask her about the man with grey eyes who visited every autumn and never stayed for winter."

"Then what do I ask myself?"

Lena smiled. "Ask yourself whether you want to carry. Not the map — the map has done its work. Whether you want to carry the *work*. There are passes to keep open, child. Messages that need moving that no king's road can carry. People who need help that only the crooked ways can reach. The Court is broken, but broken things leave shards, and shards find hands. Somewhere out there, someday, someone will need a courier again — someone with Blackwood hands, or hands like them, who understands that a guild is not its buildings or its sign or its ledger." She took Wren's small hand in her large stone one and pressed it gently closed around nothing, around the shape of a promise not yet made. "A guild is whoever carries the next parcel."

"And if I say yes?"

"Then next August, you climb again. And the August after that. And I will teach you every pass, every cache, every name the mountain remembers. And when you're grown — if you still want it, if the road still wants you — we'll speak an oath. New words. Your words. Oaths live only in living mouths." Lena rose, and the sunset caught her, and for a moment she blazed like the peak itself, granite and gold and starlight waiting underneath. "And if you say no — if you go down and live a soft life in the valley with a baker's roof under your feet and never climb again — that too is an answer, and I will love it equally. That is the difference between an oath and a chain, Wren. An oath waits to be chosen."

Wren looked up at her. At the woman made of stone and starlight. At the last courier of the Thornfell Guild, who had given everything and been given everything back in a different shape.

"August," Wren said. "I'll come in August."

"August," Lena agreed. "Again and again, until again becomes always." She stepped back into the shadows of the pass, and the mountain began, gently, to take her — stone recognizing stone. "Now run home, little courier. Your grandmother will be worried. And tell her—" the voice was fading now, becoming wind, becoming the mountain's own low hum, "—tell her the parcel arrived safely. Tell her the guild received it."

* * *

Wren ran.

Down through the pines with the swifts wheeling overhead and the first stars pricking through the violet sky, down the switchbacks two at a time with her satchel bouncing and her heart hammering out a rhythm she would keep for the rest of her life, down to the lamplit windows of Millbrook where her grandmother stood in the doorway scanning the dark, and relief and terror and something older than both broke across her face all at once.

"You climbed the high road," the old woman said. "Alone. In one day. You little fool. You wonderful little fool."

"Grandmother." Wren caught her breath, gripped her hands, looked up into eyes that had waited thirty years without knowing it. "There's a woman on the mountain. She's made of stone. She says to tell you the parcel arrived safely."

Her grandmother made a sound Wren had never heard her make — half laugh, half sob, wholly undone — and pulled her inside, and that night, for the first time, told her everything.

And high above, under the wheeling stars, the mountain kept what it loved.

And in a valley below, in a house with a loose floorboard, a guild that had been sleeping for thirty years opened one eye, stretched, and began — quietly, crookedly, in the shape of a girl with climber's hands and a head full of passes — to wake.
