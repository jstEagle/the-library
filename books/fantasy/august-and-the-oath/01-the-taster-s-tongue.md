## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The broth arrived at dawn, as it always did, in a covered silver bowl carried by a page whose hands never quite stopped trembling.

Freya Thackeray had learned to read the tremble the way other men read weather. A slight shake meant nerves, nothing more—a boy too new to his duties, still afraid of the kitchens and their heat. But when the shaking came in small, rhythmic pulses, like a heart beating too fast beneath the skin of the hand, Freya knew what waited under the lid: something bitter, something slow, something the cook had been paid well to add and the physician had been paid better to deny.

He lifted the cover. Steam rose, carrying the smell of leeks and marrow and, beneath it all, faint as a whisper through a wall, the almond-bitterness of arsenic. Three years he had been tasting for the Crown of Vellmark, and three years he had trained his tongue to find that whisper in anything—wine, soup, honey-cakes, the rainwater the servants drank. The poison sat on the broth's surface like oil on a pond, invisible unless you knew to look, and Freya looked, and found it, and was not afraid.

That was the whole art, really. Not the tasting. Anyone with a clean palate could taste. The art was being unafraid.

He took the spoon. He let the broth sit on his tongue a moment longer than courtesy required—the physicians liked to see him savor, liked to imagine that a man who enjoyed his work could be trusted—and then he swallowed. The arsenic went down like an old acquaintance. It settled into him the way winter settles into a house: not comfortably, but familiarly, finding the rooms already prepared for it. His body had been learning this language since he was nine years old, fed drops and grains and powders by a taster-master who believed that immunity was built the way a wall is built, one stone at a time, each stone a little death survived.

"Clean," he said to the page. "Tell them the king may eat."

The boy ran off with the word *clean* held before him like a lantern, and Freya set down the spoon and stood alone in the tasting alcove while the great kitchen roared around him—chopping, basting, shouting, the percussion of a hundred copper pots. He was invisible here. That was the gift Vellmark had given him without knowing it: a man who tasted poison for a living was a man no one bothered to look at twice. Cooks nodded to him. Pages saluted him. Nobody asked him where he'd come from, or who his people were, or why his eyes were the grey-green of sea glass, or why he sometimes went quiet in the middle of a conversation, listening to something none of them could hear.

Nobody asked about Aurelan. Nobody in Vellmark asked about Aurelan, because nobody in Vellmark remembered Aurelan. That was rather the point.

Freya remembered. He was fairly sure he was the only one.

* * *

The market at midmorning smelled of wet wool and frying onions and the particular sourness of fish left too long in sun. Freya walked it the way he walked everywhere: unhurried, hood up against a light drizzle, a plain man in a plain coat with a plain face that made itself forgettable on purpose. He had a stall to visit and a customer to meet, and between those two errands lay forty minutes of freedom that he hoarded like a miser hoards coin.

The laundress was waiting by the fountain, as arranged. Her name was Marta; she had forearms like ship rope and a laugh that could be heard three streets off, though today she wasn't laughing. Today she was holding her left hand close to her chest, and the hand was wrapped in linen gone yellow with old blood.

"Needle," she said, when Freya asked. "Three weeks now. Won't close. The physic says there's rot in it."

She said it plainly, without drama, the way poor people say terrible things—as if the saying of them were just another chore on the list. Freya had noticed that about the low quarter. Grief here was not an event; it was furniture. You lived around it.

"And you want—"

"Luck." She said the word quickly, guiltily, glancing toward the church steps where a friar was arguing with a fishmonger about tithe. "I know what the criers say. I know it's not— I'm not asking for magic. Just luck. A charm. Something to keep the rot from spreading till I can pay the barber-surgeon."

What she was asking for, of course, was exactly what she said she wasn't asking for. Everyone in the low quarter did this dance. They had all learned the steps after the Gift-Hunt edicts began appearing nailed to church doors two winters past—registration, reward, pyre—but the learning hadn't stopped them needing. Need didn't care what was nailed to doors.

Freya reached into his coat and brought out a small thing of stitched leather, no bigger than a thumbnail, filled with salt and a thread pulled from cloth woven in a country that officially did not exist. He pressed it into her good hand.

"Wear it against the skin. Don't take it off to wash. When the wound closes, bring me a loaf and we'll call it even."

"A loaf? That's all?"

"That's all."

Marta looked at him the way customers look at a merchant giving change—checking the arithmetic, suspecting kindness of hiding a cost. Then she squeezed the charm once, hard, the way you squeeze a thing to know it's real, and hurried off toward the wash-houses with her bad hand cradled like a candle flame.

Freya watched her go and felt the old ache begin behind his eyes, the one that always followed a working. Because it *had* been a working—he could pretend otherwise, tell himself the salt was folk remedy and the thread was sentiment, but he knew better. He had spoken three words over that leather packet in the dark of his room last night, three words in a language with no living speakers, and the words had done what such words do. Small magic. A needle-wound's worth of magic, barely enough to tip a scale. But magic, in a city that burned people for it.

Every kindness left a trail. He knew that. Marta would tell her sister. Her sister would tell the woman who mended their nets. Someone, someday, would say to someone else: *the quiet man by the fountain, the one who sells charms.* And then the wrong pair of ears would hear it, and the trail would lead back to a door in the Tanner's Row tenement, and Freya Thackeray would learn what the inside of a pyre smelled like.

He told himself, as he told himself every time, that he would stop. That this was the last charm. That survival meant smallness, meant silence, meant walking past the Martas of the world with your eyes forward and your hood up and your hands in your pockets where they couldn't be read.

He never stopped. That was the trouble with being what he was. You could starve the magic, cage it, deny it—but you couldn't make it not be true. It was in him the way the arsenic was in him, woven through, permanent, patient. All he controlled was how much of it leaked out into the world, and how much evidence it left behind.

* * *

His room above the Tanner's Row tenement was one door, one window, one bed, one chair, and one loose floorboard that he had spent his first week in Vellmark prying up and planing down until it lifted without sound. Beneath it: a tin box, and in the tin box, a notebook.

Not a fine notebook. A chandler's ledger, half-used, its remaining pages soft with tallow-smell. He had bought it for a farthing from a stationer who assumed he kept accounts. In a sense he did. He kept the only account in the world that mattered, and he was, so far as he knew, its sole entry.

He lit the stub of candle, lifted the board, took out the book, and began to write.

Today's entries, in his small cramped hand:

*The harbor of Aurelan faced south, which everyone said was wrong, and which is why the morning light came across the water instead of behind it. The fishing boats came home at dusk with their lamps lit, dozens of them, strung out along the dark water like a necklace being fastened.*

*There was a bell in the Salt Market that rang only when a ship came in free of plague. The ringers were children. It was considered the highest honor a child could hold, and they took it so seriously that they washed their hands before touching the rope.*

*Bread was broken over salt at every table, every meal. My grandmother said the salt remembered what the bread forgot. She said a lot of things like that, and I did not write enough of them down, and this is the debt I am paying.*

He wrote until the candle guttered, then wrote a little more by the light of the banked fire, because there was never enough writing. Three years of entries, and the notebook was barely half full, and some nights this felt like victory—he was keeping it, keeping *it*, the country, alive in ink if nowhere else—and some nights it felt like watching a man bleed out slowly into a cup, measuring what was lost against what remained.

Because memory was not a storehouse. He had learned that. Memory was a fire, and it needed feeding, and every day he failed to speak Aurelanish aloud, every day he failed to taste the bread-over-salt, every day the country existed only in this tallow-smelling ledger, another log burned away and turned to ash that no one would ever warm themselves by.

Somewhere out there, presumably, other keys existed. His grandmother had implied as much, in the years before the erasure, before she went into the ground in a country that was about to stop having ever existed. *The oath keeps itself,* she had said, *as long as someone carries it.* But implication was not confirmation, and hope was not a plan, and Freya had spent three years being very careful not to wonder whether he was the last man in the world who could look at the word *Aurelan* and feel it mean something.

Tonight, for the first time in months, the wondering got loose.

He sat by the dead fire with the notebook open on his knees and thought: *I am thirty-one years old. I have a body full of poison and a head full of country. If I die tomorrow—if a cart clips me on Tanner's Row, if a fever takes me, if the Gift-Hunt finds me before I find a use for any of this—then it all goes into the ground with me. Every boat lamp. Every child bell-ringer. Every word my grandmother ever said. Gone, again, worse than gone—not even erased this time, just wasted.*

And underneath that thought, quieter, older, the one he usually managed to keep buried:

*I am not keeping this country alive. I am keeping myself alive long enough to avoid deciding what to do about it.*

Safety, he understood suddenly, sitting there in the tallow-light, was not a thing he owned. It was a thing he borrowed—night by night, charm by charm, swallow by swallow—from a city that would reclaim it the moment it learned what he was. The palace paid him to put poisons in his body. The market paid him in loaves to put magic into the world. And both employers, both ledgers, both debts, were converging on the same narrow street where he slept, and he had known it for a long time, the way you know a storm is coming when the gulls go strange and silent.

He closed the notebook. He slid it under the floorboard. He blew out the candle and lay down in the dark and listened to the rain start up against the window, and told himself, as he fell asleep, that tomorrow would be the same as today, and the day after that the same again, and that this was a life, and that he was lucky to have it.

He almost believed it.

But sleep, when it finally came, brought him a dream he had not had in three years: his grandmother's kitchen, the smell of rising dough, her hands dusted white to the wrist, and her voice saying the Oath-words over a loaf while he stood on a stool too small to reach the table, and the words were fading even as she spoke them, dissolving like sugar in warm water, and he woke before dawn with his heart hammering and the certain, sick knowledge that somewhere in Vellmark, someone else had begun to smell the smoke.
