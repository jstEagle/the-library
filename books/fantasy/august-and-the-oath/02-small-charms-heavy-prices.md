## 2. Small Charms, Heavy Prices

The dream would not leave him. It clung like the smell of woodsmoke to wool, and Freya carried it down through the grey streets of the low quarter, past shuttered bakeries and the fishwives already crying their wares in voices like gulls, all the way to the market square, where the morning fog was lifting off the cobbles in slow ribbons and the lamps were being snuffed one by one, each flame dying with a small hiss like a whispered secret.

He set up his corner by the fountain of Saint Verity, whose stone face had been worn smooth and featureless by a century of rain, so that she looked less like a saint than like a suggestion of one. He laid out his wares on a square of oilcloth: copper charms shaped like acorns, knotted red thread, little glass bottles of river water that he swore—only to those who asked twice—had been blessed. Nothing that worked. That was the trick of it, the whole architecture of his survival: he sold things that did nothing, and the doing-nothing was his armor. A man who sells luck-charms is a fraud, and frauds are ignored, and being ignored in Vellmark was the closest thing to grace the city offered.

But the magic was in his hands the way the arsenic was in his blood, and it wanted out.

He felt it this morning more than usual, prickling along his fingertips like the memory of frost, restless, insistent, as if the dream had woken it too. Three years of practice had taught him to hold it the way a taster holds broth in his mouth—carefully, briefly, and never swallowing. But holding it was not the same as being rid of it, and there were days when it pressed against the inside of his skin like water against a dam.

The market filled around him. He sold a knotted thread to a carter for his horses' bridles, a copper acorn to a girl who wanted luck in love, a bottle of ordinary river water to an old man with gout who would swear by it by evening, because belief was the cheapest medicine in the world and Freya dealt in it honestly enough. He took their coins and gave them nothing but tin and confidence, and the morning passed the way mornings were meant to pass: unremarkably, invisibly.

Then the child came.

She could not have been more than seven, small and sharp-faced, with her hair braided tight and her dress patched at both elbows, and she stood at the edge of his oilcloth for a long time before she worked up the courage to cross it. Freya watched her come the way he watched everything now—with two sets of eyes, the seller's and the survivor's—and he knew before she spoke what she wanted, because they always wanted the same thing.

"Please," she said. Her voice was very small. "My mama's sick. She's got the fever, and she's been coughing blood, and the physicker wants two silver just to look at her and we haven't got two copper, and Marta next door says you're a charm-seller and maybe—" She stopped, breathless, and looked at his wares with an expression that broke something in him quietly, without sound. "Maybe you've got something that works."

"Child," Freya said, and his voice came out gentler than he intended, "everything I have is luck and river water."

The girl's face did something terrible then. It did not crumple—she was too proud for that, or too practiced—but it tightened, the way a fist tightens, and she dug into her pocket and pulled out a button. It was brass, or had been once; now it was dull and worn smooth, with a lion stamped on it that time had rubbed down to a shadow of a lion, and she held it out to him on her flat palm as if it were a crown jewel.

"It's from Papa's coat," she said. "He's dead. It's the best thing we've got that isn't Mama's."

The market went on around them—carts rattling, fishwives crying, the fountain muttering its endless stone syllables—but for Freya the noise receded, as it always did at the worst moments, as if the world stepped back to watch him choose.

He should have sold her a knotted thread. That was the safe thing, the sane thing, the thing the survivor in him demanded: take the button, give her the thread, tell her to boil the water well and keep her mother warm, and let medicine or chance or God do the rest. That was what a man did who had spent three years learning to be no one. That was what kept a man alive.

He took the button.

"Where do you live?" he asked.

"Coalheaver's Lane. The blue door with the broken hinge."

"Go home," Freya said. "Put water on to boil. When it's boiled, drop this in it." He pressed the button back into her hand and closed her small fingers over it. "Then make your mother's tea with that water, and stir it seven times, widdershins, and don't tell anyone—not Marta, not anyone—what you did with the water. Can you do that?"

She nodded, eyes enormous.

"Seven times, widdershins."

"Seven times," she repeated, solemn as a novitiate. "What's widdershins?"

"Backwards. Against the sun." He almost smiled. "It's how you ask for something back from the dark."

She ran. He watched her go, braids flying, small shoes slapping the wet cobbles, and he stood at his empty stall with a dead man's button in his fist and the taste of his own folly in his mouth, and he knew—knew with the cold certainty of a man who has already swallowed the poison—that he would go to Coalheaver's Lane tonight and finish what he had started, because a charm was only a key, and the key was useless without the hand that turned it.

* * *

He closed the stall early and walked, taking the long way, the careful way, doubling back through the tannery row where the stink covered all other smells and all other tracks, and came to Coalheaver's Lane as the light was failing. The blue door hung on its broken hinge exactly as promised. Inside, one candle, one room, a woman on a pallet by the hearth with her cheeks burning red above grey lips, and the girl, whose name turned out to be Nettie, hovering with the teapot like a small anxious priest.

The water had been boiled with the button in it. Freya could feel that from the doorway—the faint residue of his own working clinging to the steam, thin as breath on a mirror. He had sent the charm with the button the way you throw a rope across a gap: enough to hold, not enough to cross. Now he would have to cross.

He knelt by the pallet. The mother's name was Bess. Her breath crackled in her chest like paper burning, and when she coughed into the rag he saw the rust-brown spots and did not let his face change, because he had spent three years not letting his face change, and it was the one art he had mastered completely.

He took her wrist. Her pulse fluttered like a moth against a window. And he let the magic out.

It went through his palms and into her the way warmth goes into cold hands—slowly, with a kind of gratitude. He did not know what his gift was, exactly; his grandmother had called it *small mending*, and had said it was common as sparrows in Aurelan, and that the sparrows had all flown when the country died. It could not cure. It could only coax—nudge a fever down when the body was already fighting, remind a lung how to clear itself, give a failing thing one more hour in which to find its strength. It was the magic of the last inch, the final hand's-breadth, and it had never in his life saved anyone who could not already have been saved by luck.

But it was real. He felt it leave him, felt the cost of it too—the familiar small hollowing, as if he'd given blood—and he stayed kneeling there in the candlelight with his hands on a stranger's burning skin, doing the one thing in Vellmark that could get him burned, and he could not make himself stop.

The fever did not break. Fevers did not break; that was for physickers and miracles. But an hour after he left, it eased. The crackle in her chest loosened by a degree. The moth-flutter pulse found a fraction more wing. Bess slept, and in her sleep her breathing evened, and Nettie watched from the corner with the teapot clutched to her chest and an expression on her face that Freya would carry for the rest of his life: not wonder, exactly. Recognition. The look of a child who has just learned that the world contains one more thing than she had been told.

"You're like the stories," Nettie whispered. "Marta said there used to be people like you. Before."

"There are no people like me," Freya said. "I'm a charm-seller. I sell luck."

"You made the water work."

"I sold you a button, and your mother's body did the rest. Boiled water is good medicine." He put a copper in the girl's hand—his own coin, given back with interest—and made himself smile. "Buy bread with it. And Nettie: you never saw me. Not tonight, not ever. A man who sells luck can't be seen doing it, or the luck turns."

She nodded. But children's promises are made of the same material as children's buttons, and he walked out of the blue door with the broken hinge knowing that he had left something behind in that room that no promise could hold: a trail. Bright, warm, unmistakable to anyone who knew how such trails smelled.

Every kindness leaves a trace. His grandmother had told him that too, in the kitchen that no longer existed, kneading bread with flour to her wrists. *The gift is a lamp, little one. You cannot light it without showing the dark where you stand.* He had been seven. He had not understood. He understood now, walking home through the dark with the hollow feeling of spent magic in his chest, that he had lit a lamp in Coalheaver's Lane, and that in a city full of hunters, lamps were visible for miles.

* * *

Odell's shop smelled of brass polish and old lies. It sat at the end of Fetter's Court, wedged between a coffin-maker and a purveyor of secondhand teeth, and its windows displayed the harmless inventory—lockets, candlesticks, a taxidermied owl with one eye—while the real trade happened in the back room, where Odell received stolen goods, moved contraband, and, in Freya's case, converted a charm-seller's modest earnings into coins that could not be traced to a stall by Saint Verity's fountain.

He was a fat man, Odell, with rings on every finger and a face like a contented toad, and in three years he had never asked Freya a question he did not already know the answer to. That was their arrangement, and it had worked because both of them understood that the arrangement was built on mutual usefulness and nothing else. Freya brought him goods; Odell made them disappear. Sentiment did not enter into it.

So when Freya pushed through the back room door that night and found Odell not counting coins but sitting very still, with his fat hands flat on the table and his toad's face unreadable, Freya's stomach dropped before a word was spoken.

"You're late," Odell said.

"The tannery row was slow."

"Sit down, boy."

Freya sat. The candle between them guttered in a draft, and the shadows of the pawned goods on the shelves leaned in like a jury.

Odell turned a ring on his middle finger, around and around, a habit Freya had never seen him indulge. That, more than anything, told him the shape of what was coming.

"I've been in this trade thirty years," Odell said. "Thirty years. I've fenced crown silver and smuggler's opium and once, God rest her, a bishop's mistress. And in thirty years I have learned that there are two kinds of questions in this world. The kind you can answer, and the kind that answer *you*." He stopped turning the ring. "Someone's been asking about you."

The candle hissed. Somewhere in the front of the shop, the taxidermied owl shifted on its perch—or seemed to; it was stuffed, it could not shift, and yet Freya would have sworn later that its one glass eye had moved.

"About me," Freya said. Keeping his voice flat was easy. He had practiced flatness in a palace, tasting death by the spoonful.

"A man came round yesterday. Well-dressed. Soft voice. Paid for a locket he didn't look at." Odell's eyes, small and wet and older than his face, held Freya's. "He didn't ask where to find you. Smart men don't. He asked what kind of goods I take from my suppliers. He asked if any of them had *unusual* talents. He asked—" and here Odell's voice dropped, and the fat man's hands, flat on the table, pressed down as if holding something in, "—he asked whether I'd ever handled anything that came to me *warm*. Things that carry a charge, boy. Charms that actually work. He used the word 'residual.' Who uses a word like 'residual' in a pawnshop?"

Freya said nothing. His heart was a slow, heavy drum.

"I told him I take what I take and ask nothing," Odell went on, "which is true, and which is also what I tell the crown's men when they come sniffing, and it satisfied him the way it satisfies them, which is to say it bought time and nothing else. But he left a coin on the counter when he went. Gold. Vellmark mint, new." Odell slid it across the table with one finger. "And stamped on the back was a flame."

The pyre's mark. The crown's new sigil, the one the criers had not yet spoken aloud but that every gutter in the city was already whispering: a hunt coming. A registration. A reckoning.

"I don't ask what you are," Odell said. "Thirty years, I haven't asked, and I'm not asking now. But I'll tell you what I told my own son before they took him for the navy, and it's this: when the water starts rising, the clever man doesn't argue about whose fault the rain is. He counts his exits." He leaned forward, and the candlelight made his toad's face suddenly, terribly grave. "Whatever you've been doing, Freya—whatever kindnesses you've been handing out in the dark like a rich man handing out bread—you need to stop. Every gift leaves a trail, and trails are all the same to a man with a flame on his coin. They don't care what you gave. They care that you gave."

Freya took the gold coin. It was heavier than it should have been, or perhaps his hand was lighter. "Why warn me?" he asked. "What's it to you?"

Odell smiled, and for once there was no contentment in it at all. "Because you're the only supplier I've ever had who brings me honest goods," he said, "and because I'm an old fat man who has outlived everyone he ever liked, and I find I don't care to outlive you too." He waved a ringed hand toward the door. "Go home. Burn anything that burns. And boy—" Freya paused at the threshold. "The man with the soft voice. He asked one more question. He asked if I knew anything about a country that used to exist."

The draft died. The candle flame stood perfectly still, as if listening.

"I told him," Odell said, "that I don't deal in places that don't exist."

* * *

Freya did not go home. He walked, and the walking had no destination, only a direction, which was away—away from Fetter's Court, away from the harbor with its black water slapping at the pilings, away from the fountain of Saint Verity and the palace on its hill where tomorrow he would sip broth and smile and taste for poison with a tongue that had been ruined into a weapon.

*A country that used to exist.*

He stood at last on the bridge over the Vell, the river running dark beneath him, and he took out the thing he always carried: a small notebook, oilcloth-wrapped, that lived against his ribs where no searcher's casual pat would find it. He did not open it. He only pressed his hand flat against it, feeling the shape of it through the fabric, the way you'd feel for a heartbeat.

Inside were the last forty-one pages of a country. The color of the roofs in Aurelan's capital, which were blue—not slate, not grey, but blue, fired with a mineral that no longer had a name because the mineral had been Aurelan's. The words to a lullaby whose tune he could hum but whose meaning he had begun, shamefully, to lose. His grandmother's recipe for bread, and beneath the recipe, written in her hand and copied in his, the Oath-words, which he could recite in his sleep and could not, anymore, fully translate. The name of the door. The door itself, which he had never seen, which lived in his inheritance the way the lion lived on Nettie's button: worn down to a shadow, but still, unmistakably, a lion.

A man with a soft voice was asking pawnbrokers about residual magic and vanished countries, and Freya stood on a bridge in the dark with a country folded against his ribs, and he understood at last what the dream had been trying to tell him. The smoke he had smelled was not memory. It was warning.

Below him the river ran on, carrying the city's reflections out to sea—lamp-light and moonlight and the orange smudge of some distant fire—and Freya thought of Nettie's face in the candlelight, that terrible, wonderful recognition, and he thought: *I will not stop.* He thought it with the same stubborn, doomed clarity with which he swallowed the palace's poisons every morning. *I gave the child the charm. I eased the mother's breath. If they come for me for that, then let them come, and let the ledger show one honest entry before it closes.*

Then, because he was a survivor first and a sentimentalist only in the dark, he went home by three different roads, and he checked the street twice, and he slept with the notebook against his ribs and a knife under his pillow, and he dreamed of nothing at all.

Which was, in Vellmark, the closest thing to grace there was.
