## 3. The Proclamation

The bells woke the city before the criers did.

Freya heard them from his room above Odell's shop—six strokes instead of the usual five, and a seventh that hung in the air like a question no one wanted to answer. He lay still in the grey light and listened to Vellmark stir beneath the sound: shutters opening, dogs answering, the groan of cart wheels on cobbles still slick with night rain. A city waking is a language. Three years in it had taught him most of its words. This morning it was speaking a new one.

He dressed quickly, in the dark, the way he always did—not from poverty but from habit, because a man who moves through rooms unseen lives longer than one who announces himself. The notebook went into its place against his ribs, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with the same cord he'd used since the day he arrived. He touched it once, the way another man might touch a saint's medal, and went down into the street.

The criers were already at the fountain square.

There were four of them, standing on the low stone lip where fishwives argued and children played at knucklebones, and they wore the king's colors now—white and iron-grey, the livery of the new reign—and each held a scroll sealed with wax the color of dried blood. A crowd had gathered the way crowds do: slowly, then all at once, drawn by the wrongness of white livery in a market square. Freya folded himself into the edge of it, near a stall of winter apples, and let a barrel shadow take his shoulders.

The first crier unrolled his scroll. His voice was trained, carrying, empty as a bell.

"Hear the will of the Crown. By decree of His Majesty, all persons possessing the forbidden gift—the art of charms, the craft of tasting, the knowing of things unseen—are commanded to present themselves for registration within thirty days."

A murmur moved through the crowd like wind through wheat. Freya kept his eyes on the apples. Red ones, waxy-skinned, arranged in a pyramid by someone who took pride in small orders. He counted them. Forty-one. He counted them again.

"Those who register shall be recorded, examined, and assessed," the crier continued. "The Crown offers honest work to those whose gifts serve, and mercy to those who surrender their arts willingly."

*Assessed.* The word sat in Freya's stomach like a swallowed stone. He knew what assessment meant. He had watched Corvane assess a kitchen boy once—a boy whose bread never burned, whose ovens forgave him—and he had watched the boy walk out of the palace with an escort of guards and never come back, and the next week there had been a new boy, and no one asked questions, because asking questions in a royal kitchen is a way of losing your tongue.

"Further," said the crier, and here his voice changed, taking on a relish that no training could quite disguise, "the Crown offers a reward of fifty crowns silver for information leading to the discovery of any person concealing the forbidden gift."

Fifty crowns. Freya heard the number land in the crowd the way a coin lands in a beggar's cup. He didn't look up. He didn't need to. He could feel the arithmetic happening all around him—the widow calculating her winter, the apprentice calculating his freedom, the neighbor calculating the neighbor. Fifty crowns was a year of bread and rent. Fifty crowns was a daughter's dowry. Fifty crowns was the price of a man's name spoken aloud in a square like this one.

"And be it known," the crier said, and now even the market noise seemed to lean away from him, "that any who refuse registration, or who are discovered after the term of grace, shall face the judgment appointed for servants of forbidden arts."

He paused. The pause was theatrical. Somewhere behind Freya, a child asked her mother what the man meant, and the mother said *hush*, and the hush spread outward like spilled water until the whole square held its breath.

"The pyre," said the crier.

The word dropped into the silence and broke it. The crowd made a sound—not a gasp, not a cry, something lower and more animal, a sound like the city itself exhaling—and then the noise came back doubled, everyone talking at once so as not to have heard what they'd heard. The apple-seller began rearranging fruit that needed no rearranging. A man near Freya laughed, high and thin, and stopped laughing almost immediately, as if embarrassed by it.

Freya stood very still and thought about broth.

This was the discipline Corvane had beaten into him, back when beating was still part of the training: *when the poison is on your tongue, think of nothing but the taste.* Fear makes the pulse quicken; a quickened pulse carries poison faster. So you breathe slow, and you fix your mind on some small neutral thing, and you let the horror pass over you like weather. Arsenic tastes of almonds and cold metal. Belladonna tastes of nothing at all until it kills you. And a proclamation naming everything you are tastes of—

Of nothing. Of forty-one apples. Of the slow breath going out.

The criers rolled their scrolls and stepped down, and the crowd parted for them without being asked, and Freya understood, watching that parting, that the decree had already done its work. It didn't need the pyre lit yet. The pyre was lit in every mind in the square, and every mind in the square was now measuring every other mind for kindling.

He bought two apples with a copper he couldn't afford, because buying things is what innocent men do, and walked home the long way, past the harbor, past the Saltgate district where the streets smelled of brine and tar, past the shrine of the Drowned Saint where an old woman was lighting a candle with hands that shook too much for the task. He stopped and steadied the candle for her. She thanked him without looking at his face. In Vellmark, gratitude had learned not to look at faces either.

*Thirty days,* he thought. *Then they come for the ones who don't sign.*

And under that thought, quieter, older, the other one, the one that lived in him like a second heartbeat: *They have been coming for me for three years. I only just now heard their footsteps.*

* * *

It happened at the bakery on Tanner's Row, two days later, and afterward Freya would understand that he had been walking toward it all along—that the whole city had been walking toward it, the way water walks toward a drain.

He was there for bread. That was all. The morning was bright and cold enough to bite, and the bakery smelled of rye and woodsmoke, and there was a line of six people ahead of him, and he stood in it with his collar up and his thoughts on the notebook hidden at home, on the pages he had not yet decided what to do with, when the wagons came.

Two of them. Not the city watch—something else, men in plain dark clothes with the king's badge sewn small at the shoulder, the way you'd sew a label inside a coat rather than display it. They came down Tanner's Row at a walk, unhurried, and the row went quiet ahead of them the way water goes still before a stone lands.

They stopped at the bakery.

The hedge-witch's name was Maren. Freya learned it afterward, from the talk, the way the city always gave up names after it was too late to matter. She was old, or old the way poor women get old early, with a shawl the color of wet slate and flour on her knuckles, and she had worked the bakery's ovens for years—everyone knew it, everyone bought her charm-bread on feast days, little loaves scored with a spiral that kept milk from souring and fevers from climbing. Half the street owed her something. Half the street had eaten her bread.

The dark men went in without knocking. Through the bakery's fogged window, Freya saw the shapes of it: the sudden interruption, the oven-girl backing away, Maren setting down her peel with the deliberate slowness of a woman deciding, in the space of a breath, whether to run or burn. She chose neither. She stood still. And when they brought her out, she came with her hands open at her sides, empty, showing them to the street—look, nothing, no charms, no knives, nothing but an old woman's palms.

"By order of the Crown," one of the dark men announced, in a voice pitched for the windows overhead, "this woman stands accused of the forbidden gift. She refused registration. Let all see the consequence."

Maren looked at the street. Freya was close enough, at the edge of the gathering line, to see her face properly. What he expected was terror. What he found instead was worse: she was *looking*. Moving her gaze along the faces, unhurried, the way a woman counts her own spoons. And when her eyes reached Freya—just reached him, just touched him, a glance of perhaps half a second—he saw her understand him. Saw her read the collar turned up, the stillness, the careful blankness of a man practicing at being furniture.

She didn't name him. Her mouth didn't even change shape. But something passed between them in that half-second, something with weight and temperature, and then the dark men turned her toward the wagon and she went, and the door of the wagon closed, and the street breathed again.

No one moved. No one spoke. A woman clutched a loaf to her chest as if it might be next. The oven-girl appeared in the bakery doorway, wiping her hands on her apron, looking at the space where Maren had been, and then she went back inside and the smell of baking continued, because bread does not care.

Freya stood in the line. The line re-formed around him, closing like water over a stone, and when his turn came he bought his loaf from the oven-girl and paid and said thank you and walked away, and his legs carried him with perfect steadiness, and his hands did not shake, and this—more than the wagon, more than the pyre they were all imagining—was the thing that shamed him. That he could do it. That Corvane's training ran so deep in him that even now, watching an old woman be taken to be burned, his body performed innocence flawlessly, without being asked, like a servant who has long ago stopped waiting for wages.

*I did nothing.*

He turned the thought over all the way home, examining it from every side like a coin he suspected was false. He had done nothing, yes—but what was the something he had failed to do? Step forward? Name himself? Take the wagon's bench beside her and make it two pyres instead of one? He thought of Aurelan, of the country that lived folded in oilcloth against his ribs, and understood with a clarity that felt like cold water that his silence had a price tag too, and that the currency was exactly this: an old woman's glance, half a second long, weighing him and finding him absent.

*Survival is complicity,* his grandmother had told him once, though she'd been talking about something smaller then, about the year the tax collectors came to their village and everyone hid their grain and let the poorest family starve. *Every mouth that stays fed while another goes empty has agreed to the emptying.* He had been nine. He hadn't understood. He understood now, walking through a bright cold morning with warm bread under his arm, that the decree had made the whole city into a kitchen and every citizen into a taster, and the dish set before them all was each other, and he had just swallowed his portion without flinching.

That was the worst of it. Not that he'd done nothing. That doing nothing had tasted like nothing at all.

* * *

That night he burned half the notebook.

He didn't decide to. Deciding implies a moment of choice, a fork, a weighing. There was none of that. He climbed to his room, barred the door, lit the candle, and unwrapped the oilcloth, and the notebook lay open on his knees—cheap paper, sewn binding, the ink faded where his sweat had salted it over three years—and he read what he had written, and somewhere in the reading the decision made itself, the way rot makes itself in fruit.

The first pages were the oldest. His grandmother's voice lived in them still; he could hear her cadence in the lines. *The Oath-words, first teaching:* and beneath it the phrases she'd made him repeat over bread and salt, the syllables he'd carried in his mouth since before he could write them down. Those pages he set aside with hands that were careful, almost tender. Then the lists. The lists he had made in his first year in Vellmark, terrified of forgetting, cataloguing Aurelan the way a drowning man catalogs breaths:

*Rivers: the Senn, the Aldiss, the Merrow (the Merrow runs backward at the equinox tide—Grandmother said this is why we salt our doors).*

*Kings: the line of Aurel, eleven of them, ending with—* and here the ink blurred, not from age but from the pressure of the pen, pressed hard enough to nearly tear the page, *ending with the last one, whose name I will not write, whose name I say to myself every night before sleep, whose name I am.*

*Cities: Carreth, with its bridge of white stone. The terraces of Ondel. The harbor quarter of—*

He stopped. He stared at the entry. *The harbor quarter of*—and then a smudge, a word worn illegible by three years of being touched, traced, worried at by a thumb trying to raise the ghost of the letters. He remembered the place. He could see it: the crooked lanes, the fish-smoke, the bell tower leaning like a drunk uncle. But the *name*—the name was gone, rubbed out not by any thief but by his own devotion, loved off the page.

That was when he understood what the burning was for.

Not fear—or not only fear. Arithmetic. Every page he kept was a page that could be found. And what was written there was no longer just memory; it was inventory. Names of rivers. Names of kings. A country, itemized, ready for confiscation. If the dark men came for him—if Corvane finally closed his patient hand—they would not need to torture anything out of him. They would need only to open the oilcloth. Everything he was, laid out in his own handwriting, organized for their convenience.

He fed the candle flame with the middle years of the book. The lists went first—all those rivers, all those harvest customs, the words for snow in the mountain dialect, the recipe for funeral bread. The paper caught reluctantly, the way old ink resists, and the flames ate blue-green where the writing was densest, and the smell was sharp and bitter, like burning hair, like burning something alive. He watched the harbor quarter's name go. He watched the eleven kings go. He watched a drawing he'd made of his grandmother's kitchen—the hearth, the salt dish, the window with the sea beyond it—curl and blacken and become a shape with no country in it at all.

He saved the Oath-words. He saved the map fragment he'd copied from the flyleaf, the one with the mark he didn't yet understand, the curved line like a gate. He saved the last page, which held only one sentence, written the first week he arrived in Vellmark, when the fear was freshest and the resolve hardest:

*A country survives in whoever remembers it. As long as one remembers, it is not lost. Remember.*

The rest he burned down to the wire of the binding, and crushed the ash fine between his fingers, and washed it down the drain of the communal sink downstairs, alone, at an hour when no one would wonder why the taster's boy was washing ash from his hands at midnight.

Then he sat on his bed in the dark with the surviving pages against his chest and listened to Vellmark breathing around him. Somewhere a dog barked and was silenced. Somewhere a cart passed, bound for the harbor before dawn. Somewhere, perhaps, a dark man was writing a list of his own—names, addresses, the people who had stood too still in the crowd outside a bakery—and somewhere on that list, or soon to be, was a line that read: *the boy who tastes.*

He should run. The thought arrived plainly, without drama, the way true thoughts do. Thirty days. Less, now. A city turning its pockets inside out, hunting for exactly what he was, paying fifty crowns for the finding. Run north, run anywhere, take the notebook and the Oath and vanish into some place where the king's writ thinned out.

But the map fragment said otherwise. The curved line, the mark like a gate—it pointed not away from Vellmark but into it, down toward the harbor, toward the salt-stink streets he'd walked past a hundred times without knowing why his feet slowed there. Whatever his grandmother had left him, whatever the Oath was for, it wasn't across the border. It was here. Under the hunters' noses. Waiting.

Freya wrapped the remaining pages in their oilcloth and tied the cord and held the small hard bundle a moment in the dark, feeling it beat against his palm like a second heart.

*Remember,* the last page said.

Outside, far off toward the river, someone was singing—an old song, wordless from this distance, rising and falling. He listened until it stopped, and then he slept, and dreamed of bread and salt, and woke with the taste of ash still in his mouth.
