## 4. Master Corvane

The summons came the way weather comes to Vellmark in autumn: first a stillness, then a pressure behind the ears, then the thing itself arriving whether you were ready or not.

A page in palace livery found Freya at his morning station beside the king's breakfast table, standing with the small silver spoon that was both his tool and his badge. The boy couldn't have been more than ten, all elbows and nervous eyes, and he held out a folded card sealed with grey wax.

"From Master Corvane," the page said. "He asks you come at the ninth bell."

Freya took the card without letting anything move in his face. Three years of tasting had taught him that much: the face is a door too, and it pays to keep it shut. He broke the seal with his thumbnail and read four lines of Corvane's precise, upright hand—*an inventory of the spice cellars, your assistance required*—and understood immediately that there would be no inventory of any spice cellar, because Master Corvane did not use subterfuge for small things. He used it for large ones, and only when he wished the largeness of them to be felt later rather than sooner.

"Tell him I'll come," Freya said.

The king's breakfast went on around him: quail eggs, honeyed bread, a compote of plums that smelled faintly of something bitter underneath, as plum compotes sometimes did and sometimes didn't. Freya tasted each dish before it passed the royal hand, holding it on his tongue for the count of seven heartbeats, reading it the way other men read letters. Almonds. Wine dregs. A whisper of laurel from the kitchen garden, nothing more. Safe. He nodded the nod that meant *serve*, and the footmen moved, and the king ate, and no one looked at the thin man in the plain grey coat who stood slightly apart from everything, guarding a life with his tongue.

But all through the meal, the summons sat in him like a swallowed stone.

*Master Corvane.*

There were men in Vellmark who commanded fear, and men who commanded love, and a very few who commanded something stranger than either: the particular dread of a student called back into the presence of the teacher who made him. Corvane was one of those. He had trained every taster in the palace for thirty years, and he trained them the way falconers train birds—not cruelly, never cruelly, but with an intimacy so complete that the bird forgot it had ever been wild. Freya owed him his position, his immunity, his very tolerance of poison, which Corvane had built into his body drop by drop over two years of careful, patient dosing. Arsenic in the morning broth. Belladonna in the evening wine. A curriculum written in the body itself.

And now, with criers shouting the Gift-Hunt from every corner of the city—with registration lists opening in every ward and a pyre already cold in the square where they'd burned the hedge-witch's bakery stall three days past—now Corvane wanted to see him privately.

The ninth bell found Freya climbing the narrow stair to the taster-master's rooms above the old counting-house, where the air smelled of ink and dried herbs and, beneath it, the faint mineral ghost of the poisons Corvane kept like a miser keeps coin.

* * *

Corvane received him in a room Freya had not entered since his training ended. It was smaller than he remembered, or perhaps he had grown; the shelves of labelled jars crowded close, each one neat as a psalm, each one deadly. A fire burned low in the grate though the day was mild. Two chairs faced each other across a small table, and on the table sat a decanter of dark wine and two glasses.

Two glasses. That detail arrived in Freya's chest before any words did.

"Freya." Corvane rose from his chair with the unhurried grace of a man who had never once needed to hurry. He was sixty, maybe older, silver at the temples, with a long face and pale eyes the colour of watered milk. His hands, when he extended one, were soft and clean and steady. "Sit. You look well. The work agrees with you."

"Master." Freya took the offered chair because refusing it would have been its own confession. "You sent for me about the spice cellars."

"I did." Corvane poured the wine himself, a service no taster-master performed for anyone, and set a glass before Freya. "Though I find my memory of the cellars has grown unreliable. Age." He smiled, thin as paper. "Drink. It's from the south vineyards. You'll remember it."

Freya looked at the wine. Then, deliberately, he lifted the glass, swirled it, and drank—a full swallow, unhesitating, the way a man drinks who has nothing to fear from a glass in this room. Because he didn't. Because if Corvane had wanted him dead there were a hundred quieter ways, and because showing hesitation here would be worse than any poison.

Corvane watched him drink with an expression of quiet, scholarly satisfaction, the way he used to watch Freya hold belladonna on his tongue at fifteen without weeping.

"You've kept your discipline," he said. "Good. I'd hate to think those years were wasted."

"They weren't wasted, Master. I taste for the king every morning."

"Yes." Corvane settled back into his chair, glass untouched in his own hands. "You do. Faithfully. Unremarkably. For three years now, isn't it? Three years, two months, and—" he paused, consulting some interior ledger, "—eleven days. Since old Tammas retired to his sister's farm and you stepped into his place without a single question asked by anyone."

The stone in Freya's stomach turned over.

"A man could take pride in that kind of consistency," Corvane went on mildly. "Or a man could wonder at it. A taster who never stumbles. Who never falls ill in winter when half the palace coughs. Whose hands stay steady through the fever-year, through the bad harvest, through the assassination scare two springs ago when every servant in the household was questioned twice." He sipped at last, delicately. "You understand I mean no accusation. I mean only that I taught you, and teachers notice what their students become. Even at a distance. Even across three years of deliberate silence."

"Master, I don't—"

"Freya." Corvane said his name the way he used to say it over the training table, that gentle, terrible patience, and Freya's mouth closed on its own, some old obedience waking in the muscles of his jaw. "I am going to tell you a story. I'd like you to listen to all of it before you decide what to say. Will you do that?"

It was not truly a question. Freya nodded.

"When you came to me," Corvane began, "you were sixteen and starving, and you lied beautifully on your application. You wrote that you had no gifts, no history, no family. You wrote it in a hand you'd practiced, I imagine, because your real writing slants left—you were left-handed once, weren't you? You trained yourself out of it. I noticed that in your second month, when you thought no one was watching. I notice most things. It's the whole of my profession." He turned his glass slowly, watching the wine climb the sides. "Your application claimed you were born in Vellmark, in the Tanner's Ward. But you flinched—only slightly, only once—the first time you heard the word *saltmarsh*. You flinched the way people flinch at the name of a place they loved. And you pronounced certain words strangely. *Harbour.* *Hearth.* Little accents, worn almost smooth. Almost."

The fire ticked. Somewhere below, the counting-house clerks murmured over their sums, ordinary men leading ordinary days.

"There are only a few places left in the world," Corvane said softly, "where people say *harbour* that way. Places that were erased so thoroughly that even their names are contraband. Places a boy might have come from, carrying an accent like a smuggled letter." He looked up, and his milk-pale eyes were not unkind, which was somehow the worst thing about them. "I have known what you are since your third week in my training. Perhaps longer. I knew it when I began building your immunity, drop by drop, year by year. I knew it when I placed you in the king's household. Every kindness I ever showed you, Freya, was also a chain—and I fastened the first link myself, gladly, because I believed you were worth keeping."

Freya's mouth had gone dry. The wine sat in him like a lit coal. "You're saying—" His voice came out lower than he intended. "Three years. You let me believe I was invisible."

"Invisible?" Corvane smiled again, and there was something almost tender in it, which made it obscene. "No one in this palace is invisible. There is no such thing. There is only *observed* and *unobserved*, and I promise you, boy, you have been observed since the day you walked through my door smelling of salt and secrets." He set down his glass. "Now. Here is why I've summoned you, and here is where I offer you the only honest thing I have ever given you: a choice, freely described."

He rose and went to the window, hands clasped behind his back, silhouetted against the grey light of the harbour.

"The Gift-Hunt is not a frenzy," he said. "Whatever the criers say, whatever the mob believes—it is not a frenzy. It is a policy. Someone above us has decided that the gifted are a threat to the succession, and someone above *them* has decided that a list is worth more than a pyre. Registration first. Compliance rewarded. Refusal punished. It will be orderly, and it will be thorough, and it will take years, and at the end of it there will be a ledger with every gifted soul in Vellmark written in it—or ash where their names should be." He turned back. "I have been appointed to administer the palace wards. My name goes on the commission tomorrow."

Freya felt the floor tilt beneath his chair, though the room was perfectly still.

"And I am offering you protection," Corvane continued. "Real protection. Your name entered on the rolls as *cleared*—vetted, immune, loyal. No questions. No watchers. You go on tasting for the king until your teeth fall out, and no one ever looks at you again. In exchange—" he returned to the table, leaned down, and laid one soft finger on the wood between them, "—you give me names. Not yours. Others'. The hedge-witches. The charm-sellers. The laundress who buys luck-charms at the market, if you know her. The child who pays in buttons. Anyone whose gift you have seen, sensed, or suspected. Names, Freya. That is the entire price. Names."

The room seemed to shrink toward him. Freya heard his own heartbeat, slow and enormous, the way it went when he tasted something bitter under the sweetness.

"You've been watching me sell charms," he said quietly. "In the market."

"I've been watching you *be kind*," Corvane corrected. "Kindness leaves tracks, boy. It always has. Every kindness you've done in that market is a thread, and threads can be pulled, and right now there are men being paid to pull them." He straightened. "I am offering to cut those threads before someone else finds the ends. Do you understand what I'm telling you? I am the only man in this city who wants you alive *and* free. Everyone else wants one or the other."

"And if I refuse?"

Corvane's expression did not change, but the temperature of the room did; Freya would have sworn the fire guttered.

"Then tomorrow my commission begins, and I must do my duty, and my duty includes reporting a taster of irregular background whose accent does not match his papers." He said it without heat, without malice, the way he had once announced doses. "I would regret it. Genuinely. I have invested a great deal in you. But I chose erasure once myself, long ago, and I learned what it costs to hesitate." Something moved behind his eyes then—brief, buried, gone—something that for one unguarded instant looked less like menace than grief. "Do not mistake me for a monster, Freya. Monsters choose easily. I have never chosen easily in my life."

Freya stood. His legs held, which surprised him. "I need time."

"You have until the equinox." Corvane resumed his seat and lifted his glass as though the conversation had been about wine all along. "That is more generosity than the commission will tolerate, so spend it wisely. And Freya—" the milk-pale eyes caught him at the door, "—burn whatever notebooks you're keeping. Yes, I know about the notebook. I told you: I notice most things."

* * *

Freya walked home through streets that had changed shape around him without moving an inch.

The market was open. The laundress was selling soap. Children ran between the stalls with paper windmills, and a fishwife argued cheerfully about herring prices, and none of it had changed—but Freya saw it now as a man sees a room after learning the walls are painted over a door. Every kindness he had done here was a thread. Odell's shop, where he fenced his little charms. The button-girl and her mother's tea. Three years of small mercies, each one a line in someone's ledger, and the man who held the ledger had held it since Freya was sixteen and starving.

His invisibility had never been his. It had been a leash, and he had worn it like a shadow wears a wall.

At his lodging he climbed the stairs, barred the door, and knelt at the loose board beneath the bed. The notebook was there, half of it ash already, the surviving pages wrapped in oilcloth: the catalogue of Aurelan. The white terraces above the harbour. The bread and salt. His grandmother's voice saying the Oath-words over his head like a roof.

They want names, he thought. They'll get ash.

But even as he gripped the notebook, another thought arrived, colder and sharper than any of Corvane's poisons: *he knew what I am for three years and never reported me. Why? What does a man like that want badly enough to protect the last key to a dead country?*

Outside, far off toward the harbour, a bell rang once—the tide-bell at the old seawall, marking low water.

Freya had never paid it much attention before.

He paid attention now.
