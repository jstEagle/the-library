## 5. What the Notebook Kept

The ashes of half a notebook weigh nothing.

Freya learned that the night before, feeding page after page to the kitchen hearth until the fire had eaten every word of the first three years—the charm-work, the names of clients, the careful inventory of his own small treasons—and what remained was a stub of paper and string no bigger than a sparrow's heart, which now sat in the bottom of his flour tin beneath a false layer of rye, waiting for him like something breathing.

He had burned the notebook to save himself. He woke knowing he had burned it to lose something else.

The palace kitchens woke around him in their usual order: the banked ovens exhaling their first heat, the scullery boys hauling water up from the cistern with the rhythm of men still half asleep, the great spits being cranked for the morning roasts. Freya moved through it all with his hands doing the work his hands had done for three years—skimming the fat from the king's broth, holding the tasting spoon at the correct angle, letting the arsenic-laced mouthful sit on his tongue while his body catalogued it the way other men catalogued weather. Bitter almond underneath. The slow copper bloom at the back of the throat. His pulse did not so much as stumble.

*You are a lock they built,* his grandmother had said once, though she hadn't meant the palace. *And locks forget they were ever doors.*

He rinsed the spoon. He set it down. And all through the morning service, while he tasted and nodded and tasted again, one thought turned over in him like a stone in a streambed: *I kept the wrong half.*

It was true, and he had known it was true even as the pages curled and went orange in the firelight. He had burned the years of Vellmark—his safety, his trail, his small commerce in luck-charms—and kept the older pages without deciding to, the way a drowning man keeps hold of the heavier thing because it is also the warmer thing. The surviving stub held what his grandmother had left him. It held Aurelan.

By midday he could not stand it any longer. He told the sous-chef he had an errand in the fish market—he was owed an errand; he had not taken a free hour in a month—and walked out into the cold bright noise of the city with the flour tin's false bottom pressing against his ribs where he'd strapped it under his coat like a second heart.

* * *

There was a place behind the tannery row where the wall of an old granary made a pocket of windless shade, and the tanners' dogs had learned to sleep there and let a man sit among them if he was quiet. Freya had found it in his first winter in Vellmark, when he had still been learning the city's grammar—which streets were spoken softly, which doors knocked twice. He sat down now with his back against stones that smelled of oak bark and lime, and took out the surviving pages.

They were few. Eleven leaves, gone soft at the edges from years of handling, written in two hands: his grandmother's early entries in her tall slanted script, and then his own, younger, rounder, copying her out like a boy copying scripture. He had not read them since the night of the proclamation. He had been afraid to. Now he smoothed the first leaf on his knee and let the fear break over him and pass, the way you let a wave pass if you cannot stop it.

Her handwriting began the book, as it had begun everything.

*Aurelan is not gone. Say it plainly, child, because no one else will say it at all: a country is not its borders on a map. A country is bread and salt and who you share them with. They can burn the maps. They cannot burn the recipe.*

Below that, in her hand, the Oath-words. Not all of them—never all of them on one page, even here, even hidden; she had split them across three leaves like a man splitting a fuse—and Freya felt his lips move along the fragment without his permission, felt the old syllables settle into his mouth like water into a dry channel. They were not in any language spoken in Vellmark. They were not, he suspected, in any language at all anymore except this one, carried by one mouth at a time down the years like a flame passed hand to hand in the dark.

He read until the light shifted. He read the lists of plants that grew only in Aurelan's river valleys—*silverharrow, queen's sorrow, the blue wheat we milled at Saltford*—and the measurements of her kitchen, and the names of neighbors dead thirty years whose faces he could still summon if he closed his eyes: Marta with the burn-scarred forearm, old Tam who whistled through the gap in his teeth, the twins from the mill road whose names he suddenly realized he had never once said aloud in Vellmark, because there was no one to say them to.

That was the thing about memory that no one warned you. It did not fade evenly, like a painting in the sun. It faded the way a language fades when no one speaks it—first the everyday words go soft, then the grammar, and last of all, stubborn past all sense, the prayers.

He was deep in the third leaf when his thumb caught on something that was not writing.

A fold. A tight fold, pressed flat years ago and forgotten, at the base of the page where his own young handwriting trailed off mid-sentence. He worked it open with a fingernail, expecting nothing, and a scrap of paper came loose into his palm—a fragment, torn roughly, smaller than his hand, done in faded ink and one line of red.

Not his grandmother's hand. Older. Official, almost—the ruled precision of a surveyor or a harbor clerk. It showed a section of coastline he did not recognize and then, abruptly, did: the curve of the headland, the bite of the bay, the little comb-teeth marks along the waterline that meant quays. Across the top, in the same clerk's hand, a label half worn away but legible if you tilted it toward the light:

*SALTGATE — LOW TIDE ONLY*

And below the drawing of the gate itself—a door, drawn in section, set into what the labels called *the harbor wall*, black-inked and hatched dense as iron—one more line, in a different hand entirely. His grandmother's. Hurried, as if she had written it in a room where someone might walk in:

*It opens for the one who remembers. Nothing else will do. Nothing else is enough.*

Freya sat very still among the sleeping dogs, and the tannery smells, and the distant racket of the city that had outlawed everything he was, and understood that his grandmother had known. Had known the door would be needed someday. Had hidden the map inside the Oath-pages the way you hide a key inside a loaf—not to conceal it, but to make sure it traveled together with the thing it opened.

She had never gone through herself. He had asked her once, a child's blunt question, and she had looked at him for a long time and answered, *Because the door takes a price, love, and I had already paid mine in staying.* He had thought she meant something simple. He had been seven. He had thought most things were simple.

Now he folded the map fragment along its old creases and put it inside his shirt, against his skin, and the grief that had been sitting in him since the burning changed shape. That was the only way he could describe it, even to himself. Grief was heavy and formless, a wet coat you carried. This was different. This had edges. This pointed somewhere.

* * *

That night he dreamed the kitchen at home, and knew it was a dream, and stayed anyway.

In the dream he was small again, six perhaps, standing on a stool so he could reach the table, and the evening light came through the east window the color of honey left too long in the sun. His grandmother stood across from him with a round of dark bread and a dish of coarse salt, and outside the window the river ran silver, and somewhere beyond the hill a bell was ringing the hour in a pattern no bell in Vellmark had ever rung.

"Bread first," she said. "Always bread first. You don't make an oath on an empty mouth—it's rude to the words."

She tore the loaf and gave him the heel, and dipped it in the salt, and made him eat it slowly, chewing while she watched, and only when he had swallowed did she begin.

"Say after me. Don't try to understand yet. Understanding comes later, the way fruit comes after blossom. First you plant."

And she spoke the Oath-words, the first fragment of them, slow as a lullaby, and he repeated them with his mouth clumsy around sounds that had never been made in that kitchen by anyone younger than her. Some of the syllables he got wrong. She corrected him without impatience, the way she corrected his knife-grip or his kneading—*no, from the wrist, from the wrist*—and when he finally said the phrase whole and true, something happened that he had forgotten until this dream returned it to him: the salt on the table shivered. Just shivered, a fine tremor across the crystals, like the surface of water touched by breath.

His grandmother smiled, and it was not a triumphant smile. It was tired and proud and sad all at once, the smile of a woman handing a weight to a child because her arms were failing and there was no one else.

"There," she said. "Now it knows you. That's all an oath is, in the end. Something that knows you better than you know yourself, and holds you to it." She tapped the dish of salt. "People think remembering is easy. Remembering is the hardest work there is, love. Forgetting happens to you. Remembering—you have to *do* it, every day, like kneading. Every day, or the dough goes flat."

"Who am I remembering?" he had asked, mouth full, six years old, oblivious.

"Everyone," she said. "Everyone who can't anymore."

The dream held there a moment longer—the honey light, the silver river, the shivered salt—and then, as dreams do, it thinned at the edges and began to carry him elsewhere, toward some darker corridor of the night, and Freya did what he had trained himself to do across twenty years of dreaming: he seized the image and refused it passage. He would not spend the rest of the night in whatever the dream wanted next. He spent it instead walking the streets of the dream-Aurelan, door to door, saying the names aloud—Marta, Tam, the twins from the mill road—like a man going house to house relighting lamps.

He woke before dawn with the names still in his mouth and the map fragment warm against his chest, and lay in the dark of his rented room listening to Vellmark's bells ring the hour in their plain foreign pattern, and felt something in himself finish turning over, like a boat coming about.

* * *

He spent the day preparing, because preparation was the one discipline Corvane had beaten into him that he intended to keep.

He ate a normal breakfast in the servants' hall and laughed at the right moments. He tasted the noon service flawlessly, flagging nothing, flinching at nothing, giving the steward's clipboard nothing to record. He bought a lamp of cheap oil and a coil of good rope from the chandler on Tanner's Row, paying separately, hours apart, the way Odell had taught him—*never buy your escape kit in one shop, boy, shops talk*—and felt a hot coal turn over in his chest at the thought of Odell, alive this morning, and made himself keep counting change.

At dusk he went up to the widow Maren's roof, where he sometimes went to be alone, and looked east.

Vellmark's harbor lay below him in the last grey light, masts and mooring lines and the flat oil-slick shimmer of low tide baring the mudflats beyond the sea-wall. Somewhere along that wall—behind the fish-stalls, behind the customs sheds, behind three decades of new construction piled carelessly against the bones of the old—there was a door of black salt, and it opened at low tide, and it opened for exactly one person in all the world.

Him. Only, apparently, him. *It opens for the one who remembers.* He turned the words over and felt both the lift of them and the terrible arithmetic underneath. If he was the only one who remembered, then he was the only one who could open it—and everyone who had ever hunted a rare thing knew what you did with a unique key. You didn't leave it lying around.

And the door took a price. His grandmother had paid hers "in staying," whatever that meant. What would his be? He flexed his hands in the cold and thought of the taster's discipline, the daily small poisons, the body taught to survive what should kill it. Perhaps the door was another dose. Perhaps he had been training for it all his life without knowing.

Below him, a watchman's lantern swung along the quay, and the light caught—for just a heartbeat—on something moving near the base of the eastern sea-wall. A figure, stooped at the waterline where no honest person stooped at this hour. Then the lantern passed on, and the dark took the figure back, and Freya told himself it was a mudlark, a scavenger, one of the tide-pickers who worked the flats for coins and copper nails.

But he watched the spot a long time after the lantern was gone, and the watching cost him his ease, and he climbed down from the roof with the map fragment against his chest and the Oath-words already moving silently in his mouth, rehearsing, planting, kneading—every day, or the dough goes flat.

Tomorrow was a market day. Tomorrow the tide tables nailed up at the harbormaster's office would tell him when the water fell lowest. Tomorrow he would find the door his grandmother had died rather than open.

Tonight, he fed the stove one more page—not from the notebook, which was nearly nothing now, but a blank leaf, a rehearsal of burning—and watched the fire take it, and whispered the first fragment of the Oath into the flames like a man whispering into a well to hear how deep it went.

Somewhere across the sleeping city, he could not have said why, he felt the words land.
