## 6. The Memory-Thief

The pawnbroker's memory tasted of copper and candle smoke, and underneath it, faint as a watermark, the shape of something old.

Sable sat in the back room of Quill & Pawn on Tanner's Row with her boots crossed at the ankle and her fingers laced over her knee, the picture of a woman waiting for nothing in particular. The pawnbroker—Hask, a widower with gout and a weakness for brandy he watered to make it last—sat across from her with his eyes half-closed and his mouth slightly open, wearing the expression of a man dreaming with his face on. To anyone looking through the doorway they would have seemed like two people sharing a silence. Only Sable knew that she was three layers deep into Hask's Tuesday, the one where his dead wife had stood in this very room counting buttons, and only Sable could feel the edges of the memory the way a tongue finds the socket of a missing tooth.

She was not stealing it. That was the first thing people always got wrong about her. She was *tasting* it.

There was a difference, and the difference was the whole of her trade. A thief took and left a hole. Sable drank shallow and left the cup full—or nearly full, or full enough, depending on what the client had paid for and how honest she was feeling that week. Memory, properly handled, was like good wine: you did not drain the cask, you drew off a glass, and if your glass came away carrying more than the client had agreed to part with, well. Wine merchants were not famous for their scruples either.

The old thing underneath Hask's Tuesday was what had brought her here. She let the wife and the buttons dissolve and sank deeper, past the pawnshop's ordinary sediment—debts, resentments, the smell of the harbor at low tide—and found it again: a sliver of something that did not belong to Vellmark. A color that wasn't any color Vellmark had ever flown. A word spoken in a cadence the city's tongue had forgotten how to make.

*Salt,* said the sliver. *Bread. The door in the wall.*

Sable opened her eyes.

Hask blinked, shivered, and looked at her the way men always did after—a little emptier around the mouth, though he'd never be able to say why. "Well?" he said. "You get anything?"

"I get paid," Sable said pleasantly, "and then I get anything." She set three silver marks on the table between them, counted out slow so he could watch each one land. "You told my contact you once held goods from a house that burned. Goods nobody would name."

Hask's jaw worked. "That was years ago. A clerk brought them. Rings, mostly. And a book." His eyes flicked away, which told her the book still existed somewhere, and that he was afraid of it, and that fear was a flavor she could work with. "I sold the rings. The book I couldn't read. Letters in it I've never seen before or since."

"Where is the book now?"

"Why do you want it?"

"Because," Sable said, leaning forward, letting him see her smile—the one that made strong men suddenly generous and clever men suddenly stupid—"I collect things that can't be read. And I pay better than anyone who might ask you that question with less charm."

He told her. Of course he told her. They all told her eventually; charm was simply the gentler form of what she did for a living, and she saw no reason to apologize for using the tools of her craft across both trades.

When she stepped out onto Tanner's Row an hour later with the pawnbroker's directions folded into her memory alongside everything else she'd lifted from him—his mother's maiden name, the combination to his strongbox, the exact weight of his guilt over a drowned apprentice, none of which she needed tonight but all of which might keep—she paused under the eaves and let herself do the thing she did perhaps twice a year, the thing that made her hands shake and her mouth flood and her heart beat like a trapped bird.

She thought about the prize.

Not the book. The book was a crumb, a breadcrumb dropped by some fleeing hand years ago. She meant the thing behind the crumb. For six weeks now she had been catching traces of it the way a hound catches traces of fox: a whiff off a drunk sailor in the Rope Walk who mumbled in his cups about a country that wasn't on any chart; a ghost of it in a confessional booth at Saint Ferren's, where a penitent had wept over sins committed in a place the priest had never heard of; a thread of it in the registry office, of all places, buried in the marginalia of a tax ledger whose earliest pages had been scraped and rewritten so many times the vellum had gone grey. Every trace was faint. Every trace was *old*. But every trace pointed the same direction, inward, down, toward the center of the city like rings around a stone dropped in a pond, and every trace carried the same impossible signature.

It was not a person's memory. People's memories were small. Even the richest life fit in a cupped pair of hands—a childhood, a grief, a love, a shame, sixty years of Tuesdays. What Sable had been smelling for six weeks was not a life. It was a *country*. Harvests and coronations and border wars and lullabies, generations deep, a whole nation's worth of remembering gathered up and hidden somewhere inside Vellmark like a pearl inside an oyster the size of a city.

She had eaten memories of countries before—well, of moments in them. A general's recollection of a battlefield, once, which had taken three days to digest and left her weeping for soldiers whose names she never learned. A queen's memory of her own coronation, bought from a disgraced chamberlain at an obscene price, worth every mark. Those were slices. This was the loaf. This was the field the wheat grew in.

If it existed—if she hadn't spent six weeks chasing a rumor born of bad brandy—it would be the largest meal of her life. It would be enough to buy her out of the trade entirely. Enough to buy anything. Enough to *be* anything, because that was the secret no one understood about her gift: you became what you ate. Take enough of a man's memories and you could walk through his front door and his own dog would wag for you. Take enough of a country's—

Sable pressed her palms flat against the cold brick of the alley wall and breathed until her hands stopped shaking.

Patience, she told herself. Patience was the whole art. Anyone could snatch a purse. The trick was to stand in the market for hours with your hand open and let the purse come to you believing it had chosen you.

* * *

Saint Ferren's smelled of wet stone and tallow, and its confessionals smelled of the people who used them, which was a bouquet Sable appreciated more than any incense.

She came on Thursdays, when Father Aldous heard the late penitents, and she sat in the pew nearest the booths with a prayer book she never opened, doing what she thought of as fishing. Grief and guilt loosened memory the way heat loosens a jar lid. A city full of frightened people was a pantry, and Vellmark had been growing more frightened by the week—first the whispers about the Gift-Hunt, then the proclamation itself, criers on every corner promising reward for registration and fire for refusal. Fear made people confess. Confession made people remember. And remembering, lately, kept brushing against the scent she was hunting.

Tonight the middle booth was occupied. She felt him before she heard him—a man in his fifties by the timbre of the cough, merchant class by the wool, terrified by the rhythm of his breathing. She eased sideways along the pew until she sat directly behind the thin partition, closed her eyes, and opened herself the way she'd been taught to open herself, decades ago, by a teacher whose name she had long since digested along with the rest of him.

The man's penance drifted through the lattice like smoke. Adultery, first, perfunctory, a decade stale. Then the real thing arrived, and Sable went still.

"—I registered a family," the man whispered. "The Vessicks. Cloth merchants. I put their names in the ledger because Oskar Vessick beat me to a contract in the spring and I hated him for it, and the bounty is forty marks a head, Father, forty marks, and there are four of them, and the youngest is nine—"

"God will weigh your heart," Father Aldous said, in the voice of a man who had weighed many hearts lately and found most of them wanting.

But Sable had stopped listening to the priest, because the merchant's guilt had wrapped itself around a memory, and the memory had brushed the scent again—that same old salt-and-bread signature, fainter than a fingerprint, there and gone. Not from the merchant himself. From something he'd seen. She leaned in, drinking carefully, delicately, a hummingbird at a flower, and caught it: a room in the registry office. A ledger. And standing over the ledger, a clerk she did not know—young, fair-haired, unremarkable except for the way he'd touched the page, the way a man touches a thing he loves—and around the young clerk, for half a heartbeat, the smell of a country that had been erased so thoroughly that even the erasers had forgotten what they'd erased.

The merchant finished his confession, received his absolution, and shuffled out into the nave lighter by nothing at all, because Sable had already taken the useful part. She sat in the emptying church while a vermin snuffed candles one by one, and she turned the clerk over in her mind like a coin of uncertain minting.

A clerk. Young. Fair. Touching a ledger with love. The registry office kept the Gift-Hunt's lists now—every name surrendered, every name sold, every name priced. If the country-scent clung to a clerk of that office, then either the clerk was gifted and hiding it, which was interesting, or the clerk was something rarer, which was delicious.

Sable did not believe in coincidence. Coincidence was what small minds called pattern when the pattern frightened them. Six weeks of traces, all pointing inward, and now a boy at the center of the hunt's own machinery who smelled of the prize. The city was converging on him from every direction—the witch-hunters below, the bounty-hunters above, the informants seeping through every floorboard—and none of them knew what he was. None of them knew what he *carried*. They were hunting a gifted man when they should have been hunting a kingdom.

Which meant, if she moved quickly, the entire apparatus of the state would conveniently flush her quarry toward her, and she need only stand at the downstream edge with her hands open.

She rose, nodded to the verger, and stepped out into the rain, which came down soft and grey over Vellmark, washing the streets, filling the gutters, running toward the harbor the way everything in this city ran toward the harbor. Toward the sea. Toward the walls where the tide went in and out twice a day against stones older than anyone remembered.

Older than anyone remembered. She smiled at her own thought. Almost true. Almost no one.

* * *

Her rooms above the chandler's on Coil Lane were the only place in Vellmark where Sable allowed herself to be hungry in public, so to speak, because hunger was the one appetite she could not afford to show clients. Hunger made you take too much. Hunger made you sloppy. Hunger, unmanaged, turned a craftsman into a glutton, and gluttons ended on pyres like the ones the proclamation had promised, because the crown's new witch-finders had a particular interest in her kind—memory-workers sat high on their list, just under hedge-witches and just above luck-charmers, and the reward for one of her was a hundred marks, which she found flattering.

She lit no lamp. She didn't need one. In the dark she laid out on the table the things she'd gathered in six weeks of patient fishing: not objects, but tastes, each one pinned in her mind like moths in a case. The sailor's mumbling. The penitent's registry room. The pawnbroker's unreadable book, whose location she had extracted and whose retrieval she had delegated to a burglar named Finch, who would have it to her by tomorrow night or discover what else she'd taken from him the day they met. The tax ledger's scraped margins. A beggar woman's nonsense song, learned secondhand off a drunk, containing a word that matched no language in Vellmark but rhymed, impossibly, with a word in the general's battlefield memory she'd eaten eleven years ago.

She arranged them in order of age. Oldest to newest. And when she had them all in a row she did what she always did at the end of a day's hunting: she lay back on her cot, folded her hands on her breastbone like a woman in a coffin, and let the tastes speak to one another.

This was the part no other practitioner could do. Others of her kind consumed; Sable *composed*. A memory was not a single note but a chord, and chords resonated, and if you held enough fragments of the same lost thing close together, they began to harmonize, and the harmony filled in what the fragments lacked. She had reconstructed whole evenings this way, whole affairs, one murder, once, that had made her a great deal of money and cost her two nights of sleeping with the lamp lit.

The fragments began to sing.

Salt first, always salt—the taste of it in bread, in air, in wounds. Then green hills going down to white beaches, terraced orchards, a road of pale stone running along a coast that no chart admitted to. Bells. A language with liquid consonants, worn smooth as river stones. A king's face, blurred, because kings were the first thing erasure ate. And threaded through all of it, everywhere, in every fragment, the same refrain, the way a name threads through a song:

*Aurelan. Aurelan. Remember Aurelan.*

Sable lay in the dark with her hands folded and let the dead country wash over her, and she was aware, distantly, that she was smiling, and that the smile had been on her face for some time.

So it was real. Not a rumor, not a drunkard's dream. A whole nation, unmade from maps and records and minds, surviving in exactly one hiding place—one vessel, one carrier, one keeper of keys. The traces were old because the keeping was old, passed hand to hand like a coal passed down a line of runners, and the coal had come to rest, recently, here, in Vellmark, in the hands of someone the witch-hunters were already circling.

Someone young. Someone fair. Someone who touched ledgers with love.

Tomorrow she would go to the registry office. She had a talent for playing clerks—she'd eaten enough of them to do the ink-stained fingers and the stoop to perfection—and offices were her natural hunting ground, all those tired minds stacked like cordwood, all those secrets filed within arm's reach. She would find the boy. She would stand near him in some corridor, brush his sleeve, and drink one sip, one single delicate sip, just enough to confirm.

And if the sip tasted of salt and bread and bells—

Sable rolled onto her side in the dark, and outside the rain thickened, and down at the harbor the tide was turning, sliding black and patient against the base of the old seawall, against stones that remembered what no one else did.

"Sleep well, little key," she murmured to the city, to the boy somewhere in it, to the whole sleeping country folded up inside one mortal memory. "Eat your supper. Mind your steps. I'm coming to taste what you're made of."

She closed her eyes, and dreamed—not her own dreams, she had traded those away years ago, but the country's: green hills, white beaches, a door of black salt waiting in the wall for a hand that remembered.

By morning she had a plan, a disguise, and an appetite like a winter wolf's.
