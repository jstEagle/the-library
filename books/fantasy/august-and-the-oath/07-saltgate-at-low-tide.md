## 7. Saltgate at Low Tide

The tide went out an hour before dawn, and Freya went with it.

He had learned the harbor's moods the way he had learned the palace kitchens: by repetition, by patience, by keeping his head down and his eyes moving. Low tide was when Vellmark showed its bones. The mudflats beyond the breakwater gave up their drowned cargo—snapped hawsers, a child's shoe gone green with rot, wine bottles sealed with wax older than the city's charter. Mudlarks worked the flats with lanterns and iron rakes, and no one looked twice at another bent figure moving through the grey.

But Freya was not looking for bottles. He had the map fragment folded against his chest, wrapped in oilcloth, and he had read it so many times in the three days since finding it that he could see it with his eyes closed. His grandmother's hand, or a hand like hers—older, harder, the letters pressed deep into the paper as though the writer feared the ink might be unmade too, like everything else from Aurelan. *Where the salt remembers, walk to it. Where the wall drinks the sea, knock.*

*Knock.* As if it were a neighbor's door. As if there were anyone left inside to answer.

He moved along the base of the harbor wall where it curved away from the fish-market, past the tar yards and the rendering sheds whose stench could strip paint, into the stretch the mudlarks avoided—the old quarter, they called it, though no one could say what it was old *from*. The stones here were blacker than elsewhere, veined white, and the mortar between them glittered faintly when his lantern caught it. Salt. Salt worked through everything, salt bleeding out of the rock itself, and Freya felt something turn over in his chest, slow and enormous, like a whale shifting in shallow water.

His grandmother's voice, three years dead and still the clearest thing he owned: *Salt keeps what water cannot hold.*

"Here," he said aloud, and his breath smoked in the cold, and the word fell flat against the stone and died.

He walked on. The tide was far out now, the flats sighing and glistening under a sky the color of dirty pewter. Somewhere behind him the city was waking—gulls screaming over the fish-market, the first carts rattling down toward the quays—and every sound of it reminded him that he was exposed, that the Gift-Hunt had made the whole coastline a pair of open eyes. Corvane's hunters wore grey sashes and carried iron-tipped staves, and they had learned, in the weeks since the Proclamation, to watch for exactly the kind of man who walked alone before dawn with a lantern and a purpose.

Freya kept walking anyway. That was the thing about hope, he was learning. It did not feel like wings. It felt like a debt you couldn't stop paying.

* * *

He found it by falling.

The mud near the wall's base hid a hollow—a depression worn smooth, deliberate, circular, like the bottom of an enormous cup. His boot slid into it and he went down hard, lantern spinning, and as he caught himself against the wall his palm pressed flat against stone that was not stone.

It was warm.

Not sun-warm, not animal-warm. Warm the way bread is warm when you pull it from the oven and hold your hand above it—that living warmth, the warmth of something *made*. Freya knelt there in the filth of the tidal flat with his heart slamming and laid both palms against the surface, and the surface gave back nothing, no light, no sound, only that impossible gentle heat and a texture like fine grain, like flour, like—

Like salt pressed so long and so hard it had forgotten it was ever the sea.

He scraped the mud away with his bare hands, working by lantern-light, and the door emerged from the wall the way a face emerges from shadow. It was perhaps seven feet tall, arched, seamless, black as the inside of a closed mouth, shot through with veins of white that might have been salt or might have been light trying to get out. There was no handle. There was no keyhole. There was only, set into the center at the height of his chest, a shallow depression the size of a spread hand.

Freya put his hand into it.

Nothing happened. And then—not nothing. A feeling came up through his arm like a question asked in a language he almost spoke. Not words. Weight. The sensation of standing on a scale and watching the needle tremble, waiting for a number. The door was *weighing* him.

"I remember," he whispered. "I remember the orchards above Callowmere, I remember the bells of the Salt Cathedral ringing for the harvest, I remember—" His voice cracked. "I remember my grandmother's kitchen. Bread and salt. She taught me the Oath-words over the table, I remember them all, every one—"

The weight shifted. Grew heavier. The door was listening, and the listening itself was terrible, because it was not enough. He could feel it wanting more than memory, wanting something he could not name, some coin he did not carry or did not know how to count. The Oath-words rose in him unbidden, his grandmother's cadence, her hand heavy on his shoulder:

*By bread and salt, by seed and shore,
by what was lost and lost once more—*

and the door drank the words and gave back only that patient, hungry weight, and Freya understood with cold, sinking certainty that it was waiting for him to finish a sentence he didn't know. The Oath had a price written into it somewhere, in some page burned or buried or eaten by time, and until he knew the price, the door would stand here in the dark of the harbor wall forever, warm as bread, silent as the drowned.

"No," he said. "No, please." He pressed his forehead to the black salt. It smelled faintly, impossibly, of his grandmother's kitchen—woodsmoke and yeast and the mineral bite of coarse salt crystals—and the smell undid him more thoroughly than any grief had in three years. He wept against the door of a country that no longer existed, in the mud of a city that wanted him dead, and the door held its heat against his skin like a hand that would not quite close around his.

That was when he heard the boots.

* * *

They came along the top of the wall first—he heard them up there, two sets, unhurried, the pace of men working a grid. Then one descended the harbor stairs at the far end of the curve, lantern high, and the light swept the flats in a long yellow blade.

Grey sash. Iron-tipped stave. Corvane's livery.

Freya killed his own lantern and pressed himself into the wall beside the door, and the door's warmth soaked into his shoulder like a conspirator's breath. *Move,* said every instinct he owned. The mudlark's path ran back toward the tar yards, forty feet of open flat, no cover but the dark and the tide-smell. The hunter below was coming closer, boots sucking in the wet sand, humming something tuneless. Above, his partner's lantern swung its blade across the wall-top, closer, closer.

Freya thought, absurdly, of Odell. Of the button in his pocket, paid by a child for charmed tea. Every kindness leaves a trail; every trail leads somewhere; and this trail led to a door that would not open, and he was going to die six feet from his own country's threshold with his memories intact and useless as coins in a closed fist.

The hunter below stopped.

Raised his lantern.

Light crawled across the mud toward the hollow where Freya crouched, across the scraped-clean patch of wall, across the—

Across the door, which had gone cold. Utterly, perfectly cold, and dark, and indistinguishable from the rest of the wall, its veins of white dimmed to nothing. As if it had never been found. As if it were keeping him secret even now—or keeping itself.

The hunter frowned. Freya watched him frown, watched the lantern waver, watched the man take a step closer and then pause, struck by some hunter's instinct he couldn't name, and Freya's hand crept toward the little knife in his boot with a steadiness that surprised him. Three years of tasting poison had taught him this much: panic was a flavor, and he had learned to keep it off his tongue.

Then a hand closed over his mouth from behind.

He nearly drove the knife backward on reflex—but the grip was iron and the body behind him was already moving, pulling him sideways into a gap between the wall's foundation stones that he would have sworn a moment ago had not existed, a slot of darkness barely wider than a coffin. The knife stayed sheathed. The hunter's lantern passed within an arm's reach of them, close enough that Freya could hear the man breathing, could smell the tallow smoke and the leather of his stave-grip—and then the light moved on, and the boots crunched away toward the stairs, and the humming faded.

Above them, the second lantern swept past and kept going.

For a long moment neither of them moved. The gap smelled of salt and old rope and, beneath it, something Freya had not smelled since childhood—something that made his throat close. Woodsmoke. Yeast. Home.

Then the hand left his mouth, and a voice said, low and dry, close to his ear:

"You knocked. It listened. And then you cried on it." A pause. "Amateurs always cry on it."

Freya turned. In the dark he could make out little—a lean frame, a travel-stained coat, and eyes that caught even the smallest light and held it, pale as weathered pewter. Grey eyes. Eyes the color of the sky over the flats at low tide, and looking at him now with an expression that was equal parts irritation and something older, something that hurt to look at directly.

"You know what it is," Freya said. Not a question. His voice came out rough.

"I know what it is." The stranger glanced back through the gap, toward the place where the door hid itself in the wall. "More to the point, I know why it wouldn't open. And unless you want Corvane's grey-sashes to learn the same lesson over your corpse, you're going to come with me, right now, quietly, and you're going to tell me who taught you the Oath-words."

Cold flooded through Freya—cold and hot at once, fear and fury braided together. "I don't know you."

"No," the stranger agreed. He was already moving, easing out of the gap into the shadow of the wall, gesturing Freya after him with two fingers. "You don't. But your grandmother owed me a life, and I've spent thirty years wondering how to pay it back." Those grey eyes fixed on Freya, and in them Freya saw—for just a heartbeat, before the stranger turned away—something that looked unbearably like grief.

"She told me once," the man said, "that if the day ever came, I'd know her grandson by the way he knocks."

The tide had begun to turn. Far out on the flats, the first thin tongue of water came sliding back across the mud, licking at the harbor wall, climbing toward the door of black salt that no longer showed itself—as if the sea itself were coming home to wait outside it.

"Come on," the stranger said. "Before it comes back in. We have a great deal to talk about, and very little time to do it in—and somewhere behind us, Master Corvane is about to learn that someone found his door."

* * *

They went up through the old quarter as the sky went from pewter to ash, the stranger leading with the certainty of a man who had walked these streets in another century. At the mouth of Rope Walk he stopped, listened to the distant bell of the fish-market counting the hour, and looked back once toward the harbor.

"They'll sweep it again at full tide," he said. "Corvane doesn't know what's in the wall yet. But he knows something is, and he has hunters who can taste magic the way you taste poison." He turned those grey eyes on Freya. "Which means you have days, boy. Perhaps less. So we'd best begin."

"Begin what?" Freya asked.

The stranger smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile, but there was relief in it, raw and exhausted, like a man setting down a weight carried thirty years.

"Begin," he said, "with your grandmother's name. And then everything she ever told you about the price of doors."
