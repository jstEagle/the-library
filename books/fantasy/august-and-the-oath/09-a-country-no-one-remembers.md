## 9. A Country No One Remembers

Ives had spent eleven years learning how to forget, and one night discovering that forgetting was a thing you could be paid to do.

He had been the junior cartographer of the royal survey when the order came down from the Chancellery: burn the coastal charts. Not correct them—burn them. He remembered standing in the map room with his sleeves rolled to the elbow, feeding vellum into the iron stove while the Chancellor's men watched him with the flat patience of men who had done this in forty other rooms. The ink went up green, because Aurelan's cartographers had mixed their pigments with seawater, and there was salt in it still, and salt burns green.

That was the first lesson. A country does not die when its people die. It dies when its name stops fitting in anyone's mouth.

* * *

The safehouse above the chandler's shop smelled of tallow and old rope, and Ives had chosen it for exactly those reasons—no one lingered where the light was bad and the air was greasy with fat. He had lit two lamps anyway, because what he meant to do tonight required seeing.

Freya Thackeray sat across the scarred table with his hands wrapped around a cup of barley tea he wasn't drinking. In the lamplight he looked younger than he had at the harbor wall, younger than a man who'd survived Corvane's hunters by a knife's width deserved to look. There was a bruise coming up along his jaw where the cobblestones had caught him, and he kept touching it absently, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

"You said you'd explain," Freya said. "The door. The price. You said my grandmother—"

"I said a great many things while we were running." Ives set a sheet of blank paper on the table between them, and beside it a stick of charcoal worn to the length of a thumb. "I'd rather show you first. If you'll allow it."

"Show me what?"

"Tell me about Aurelan."

The silence that followed had weight. Ives had seen men flinch at the word before—old soldiers who woke shouting names they couldn't explain, widows who kept setting an extra place at table without knowing why. The erasure had not been clean. It never is. Memory is water; you can dam it, divert it, poison the well, but somewhere it always seeps. What the Chancellery had done was not kill the memory but orphan it—took away every anchor, every document, every grave-marker, so that what seeped through had nothing to hold to and drained away within a generation.

Except in this boy, apparently. Except in the boy who tasted poison for a living and sold luck-charms for buttons.

"I don't talk about it," Freya said finally. "There's no one to talk to about it. People look at me like I'm mad if I say the name."

"I won't look at you like that." Ives turned the paper toward himself and began, without letting himself think, to draw. Coastline first—the northern coast, the hook of the Vess Peninsula, the shallow bite of the Sallow Bays. His hand remembered even now, eleven years later, the way a scar remembers the blade. "I drew these shores four times before they made me burn them. I was good. I was the best young hand in the survey, which is why they made me do the burning myself. They said it was an honor." His mouth twisted. "Draw me something. Anything. A harbor. A road. Don't tell me what it is first. Just draw."

Freya took the charcoal the way a man takes a knife offered hilt-first—warily. For a long moment he didn't move. Then he bent over the paper, and Ives watched his face change as he drew: the wariness draining out of it, replaced by something older and more private, the expression of a man reading a letter from the dead.

The lines came slow at first, then faster. A crescent of quay. Warehouses with stepped gables. A lighthouse—and Ives's breath caught, because Freya drew it without hesitation, a squat tower with a lantern room ringed in black-and-white chevrons, exactly right, exactly the Saltlight of Varen's Mouth, which appeared on no surviving chart in the world.

"The tide-stair," Ives said quietly. "You put a stair on the seaward side."

"Everyone used it at low water. The fishermen came up it instead of walking around through the gate—it saved ten minutes and everyone in Varen's Mouth would break the law for ten minutes." Freya stopped. Looked up. "I've never said that sentence out loud. I didn't know I knew it."

"What else?"

"I know the smell." Freya set the charcoal down and pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "Gods, I know the smell. Low tide in the harbor—rot and brine and the tar they boiled in the ropewalk, and under it something sweet, the sugar-loaves going stale in the customs house. I could find that city blindfolded by the nose alone. Why do I know that? Why has that stayed when I can't—" His voice cracked and steadied. "I can't remember my mother's face. I've tried. It's gone soft, like a print left in wax too long. But I can smell a rope-walk I haven't stood in since I was six years old."

"Because no one ever told you to forget it." Ives leaned forward. "Listen to me, Freya. What was done to Aurelan was done in three layers. First the records—registries, deeds, parish books, ship manifests. That took the Chancellery two years and a hundred clerks. Then the maps and the names—every chart, every signpost, every gravestone. That took longer, because names live in stone and stone argues. But the third layer—" he tapped the table, once, hard— "the third layer was the minds. There was a working. I don't know its shape or its cost; that wasn't given to men like me to know. But I stood in the map room the morning after it was cast, and a colleague of mine, a man named Aldous Bray who had surveyed the Sallow Bays with me for six years, looked me in the eye and asked me why the eastern shelf of my chart was blank. He'd sailed those waters forty times. He'd eaten oysters from the bay with his own hands. And the working had gone into him so gently he didn't even feel the hole where his memory had been. He thought the blankness was my error. He corrected me. Kindly."

Freya had gone very still. "And you? Why do you remember?"

"Because I owed your grandmother a debt, and she paid it in the only coin that mattered." Ives held up his hands, turning them palm-up. Cartographer's hands, ink-stained at the cuticles still, after all these years. "Maren Thackeray came to me three nights before the working was to be cast. She knew—I have never learned how she knew, but she knew. She gave me a charm to wear, a knot of red thread and salt, and she told me: *When the fog comes, hold the knot and count your borders.* And when the fog came—because it did come, it came like weather, rolling off the eastern sea at dawn, gray and sweet-smelling, and every man in the survey office breathed it and smiled—I sat under my desk with the knot in my fist reciting coastlines until my lips bled. Aldous Bray walked past my hiding place whistling. He whistled a tune I later realized was an Aurelan work-song, and he didn't know it, and he never would again."

Ives stopped. The lamps hissed. Somewhere below them the chandler's dog turned over in its sleep.

"I kept the knot for years," he said. "Until the thread rotted. But by then the habit of remembering was carved deeper than the charm. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Everyone else who remembers Aurelan remembers by accident—a scrap here, a song there, a dream that won't resolve. You remember on purpose. Someone built that in you."

"My grandmother." Freya's voice was barely above a whisper. "The bread and salt. She made me say words over it. Every week. I thought it was just—superstition. Old women's ritual."

"It was a maintenance schedule." Ives allowed himself a thin smile. "Your grandmother was keeping a country alive in your skull the way a lighthouse-keeper trims a wick. Weekly. Without fail. And now—" he pulled the paper back toward himself and studied the drawing again, the quay, the stepped gables, the black-and-white lighthouse, the little tide-stair clinging to the seaward wall like a secret— "now I need to know how much survived her. Because there is a difference, Freya, between a man who carries a country's memory and a man who *is* one."

"What does that mean?"

"It means the Oath isn't a spell. Spells are cast and finished. An oath is carried." Ives rose and went to the window, checking the street out of eleven years of habit—empty, wet cobbles, the chandler's sign creaking. "When the Chancellery unmade Aurelan, they could not unmake everything. Some things were woven too deep. The door at Saltgate is one. The Oath-words are another. And the keys—the living keys—are the third. Your grandmother was a key. So was her mother, going back, I don't know how far. Keys aren't born knowing they're keys. They're taught. Word by word, meal by meal, until the country lives in them so thoroughly that the door will recognize its bearer the way a lock recognizes—"

He stopped. Turned. Freya was holding the drawing at arm's length, staring at it, and his face had gone the color of the tallow-light.

"Say the rest," Freya said.

"When you stand at that door and speak the Oath, it will not open because you know a password. It will open because for as long as you stand there, you are the last living citizen of Aurelan. Every border in your head. Every street. Every name. The door doesn't want a key it can turn. It wants a country it can recognize." Ives came back to the table and sat, and took the boy's wrist—not gently, because gentleness would have been a lie. "So I need to test you. All of it. Tonight. Because if there are gaps—if the weekly rite slipped, if your grandmother died before she finished—then that door will taste the gaps and stay shut, and we will both die in a harbor town for nothing."

* * *

They worked until the candles drowned in their own wax.

Ives tested him the only way a cartographer knows: by measurement. Names of towns—Freya gave him nine, and Ives, who had drawn the country four times, knew all nine and had expected five at most. Rivers. The Freywater, yes, and the Ald, and the third one whose name Ives himself had lost and which Freya supplied without hesitation—*the Merren*, he said, *it floods in the thaw and the eel-fishers hate it*—and Ives wrote it down with a hand that was not quite steady, because he had spent eleven years thinking that name was gone from the world, and here it was, alive in the mouth of a food-taster.

Roads. Freya described the coast road from Varen's Mouth south to the salt-pans, and then corrected himself—no, there was a cut-through behind the chalk cliffs, a drover's path, you could save half a day but the sheep hated it—and Ives laughed, actually laughed, a rusty sound that startled them both, because he had walked that drover's path in the rain at nineteen and sworn at the same sheep.

But it was the small things that undid him.

"Tell me what people ate," Ives said, near the end, when his voice had gone hoarse from listening. "Not the feasts. The ordinary food."

"Bread with caraway in it, on the coast. Rye inland. They baked it in rounds and broke it, never cut it—cutting bread was for funerals." Freya's eyes had gone distant, fixed on some middle distance no lamp could reach. "Herring, obviously, but there was a way of doing it—with mustard and a little burnt honey, and you ate it standing up, at the harbor wall, and it was bad manners to sit. Children drank milk warmed with a bay leaf. My grandmother—" his voice caught, mended itself— "my grandmother put a pinch of salt in cider. She said sweet without salt is a lie."

Ives sat back. He found that his eyes were wet, and did not trouble to hide it, because there was no one in the room to perform for except a dead country and its last keeper.

"Do you understand what you are?" he said softly. "I have spent eleven years among men who remember fragments. A song. A surname. A woman in the low quarter who makes a pastry she calls *aurel-cake* and cannot say why. They are echoes. Echoes fade. But you—" he spread his hands over the pages they had filled, nine pages, dense with Ives's cartographer's script and Freya's slower additions— "you are not an echo. You are the archive. When you die, Freya—when any of us die—Aldous Bray's whistled tune dies with him, and the woman's cake loses its name, and the fog wins by inches. But while you carry it whole, Aurelan is not erased. It is *exiled*. Waiting."

"That's a terrible thing to put on a person."

"Yes," Ives said simply. "It is. Your grandmother should have had a dozen children and trained them all, and instead the burden came down to one boy in a palace kitchen tasting broth for tyrants. I didn't build the world. I only draw it."

Freya looked down at his hands. Taster's hands, Ives thought. Steady hands. Hands that had learned to shake without spilling.

"There's something else," Freya said at last. "Something I've never told anyone. When I taste the poisons—the training doses, the ones Corvane gives us to build immunity—sometimes, right at the edge, before my body pushes it out, I hear it. The country. Like a voice under the water. It says the Oath-words back to me. In my grandmother's voice." He raised his eyes. "Is that madness?"

Ives considered lying to him, and rejected it, because lies were heavy luggage for a short journey.

"No," he said. "I think it's the Oath answering. I think the country knows its key is being tested, and holds on tighter each time. Which means, Freya, that every dose Corvane feeds you is teaching the door to trust you more. The man hunting you is sharpening the very thing he's trying to catch."

Outside, the tide was turning; they could hear the harbor breathing against the sea-wall, long and slow, like something asleep. Ives gathered the nine pages, squared their edges, and looked at the boy across the ruins of the candles.

"Tomorrow I'll teach you the rest of the Oath—the parts your grandmother may never have gotten to, the clauses that name the price. We'll need every word before the equinox." He paused at the door, hand on the latch, and could not help himself: "One last question. The lighthouse you drew. The Saltlight. Did you draw it because you remembered it, or because you could see it?"

Freya went pale. "What do you mean, see it?"

"Nothing," Ives said, though it was not nothing, because in eleven years of exile he had never once dreamed of Aurelan, and last night, after touching the boy's shoulder to pull him clear of the hunters, he had dreamed of a black-and-white tower burning green against a dawn sky. "Sleep. Tomorrow we work."

But as he climbed the stairs to his cold bed, Ives understood that the country was already reaching through its last key—that the erasure had never been complete, that doors, once tasted, begin to open from the other side.
