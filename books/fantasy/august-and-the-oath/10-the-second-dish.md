## 10. The Second Dish

The palace smelled different when you came back to it as a traitor.

Freya had noticed it the moment he passed the outer gate—the same beeswax and woodsmoke, the same river-damp rising from the flagstones of the lower court, but underneath it all a new note, faint as arsenic in wine: the smell of a house being searched. Servants moved with their eyes down. Two of the King's Gift-Hunters stood at the kitchen stair, their grey sashes the only color in the corridor, and one of them watched Freya's hands the whole way down, as if hands were where magic kept itself.

*Three years I walked these halls invisible,* he thought. *Now every stone knows my name is written somewhere.*

He carried his tasting-spoon in its leather sleeve at his belt, and the weight of it was different too. Once it had been a tool. Now it was a costume, and costumes are heavier than tools because you must hold them up yourself.

The morning's first dish waited in the small stone chamber off the royal kitchens: a pike quenched in sorrel sauce, sent up from the fishmongers on the canal with the palace's compliments to no one in particular. Freya uncovered it, bent close, and let his tongue do the work three years of Corvane's training had taught it.

Bitterness at the back edge—sorrel, expected. Salt. Butter gone slightly brown from a careless pan. And there, threading through it like a hair in milk: something vegetal and sweet-rotten, a ghost of hemlock water where the fish had been rinsed.

Not enough to kill. Enough to sicken a king for two days and start questions that would end with someone on a pyre.

"Take it away," Freya said to the under-cook hovering at the door. "The fish was washed in bad water. Have them boil the second pike in fresh, and tell the fishmonger his canal bucket has been standing."

The boy stared. "You can taste *that?*"

"I can taste what doesn't belong." Freya said it flatly, the way Corvane had drilled into him years ago—no wonder, no pride, nothing for anyone to chew on. But inside, the old dread turned over like a sleeper. Because it was true, and truth was the one thing he could no longer afford to be caught holding. The gift they hunted was his gift. Every dish he saved the king from proved it, quietly, to anyone clever enough to ask how.

And Corvane was the cleverest man Freya had ever known.

* * *

Corvane received him at midday in the taster-master's office above the spice stores, a room Freya knew better than his own bed. Nothing in it had changed: the racks of labeled vials, the mortar scarred by thirty years of grinding, the window that looked out on nothing but wall, so that no passerby could study the master's face while he studied his poisons.

Except the ledger.

It lay on the desk between them, closed, bound in grey calfskin, and Freya felt it the way you feel a stove before you touch it. Names. He'd heard about the registration lists from Odell, from the market whispers—the Gift-Hunt keeping its book of the marked, the pardoned, the *pending*. Somewhere in those pages were children. A hedge-witch's daughter who could make cream keep. A cooper's son who spoke to rats. Names gathered like kindling, waiting for the season to turn.

"You look well," Corvane said. "The city agrees with you."

"The city doesn't notice me, Master."

"No," Corvane agreed, and poured two cups of wine from the decanter he kept for guests he wished to unsettle. "It never has. That was always your finest quality, Freya. Sit."

Freya sat. He drank when Corvane drank—a courtesy between poison-men, each trusting the other's self-interest—and let the silence do its work.

"You've been to the harbor," Corvane said at last, mildly, as though remarking on weather.

The wine went cold in Freya's mouth. "I walk everywhere. It's how I learn what the city eats."

"Mm." Corvane opened a drawer, took out a folded paper, and set it beside the ledger without unfolding it. "Two of my hunters saw a young man matching your description near the Saltgate at low tide last week. Running. There was some trouble with a pickpocket, they thought." His eyes came up, mild as milk. "You weren't robbed, I hope?"

"Nothing worth stealing."

"No," Corvane said softly. "There isn't. Not yet." He folded his hands over the ledger. "The equinox is coming, Freya. The King wants the hunt finished by then—a clean country by the turning of the year. I would prefer my best student not be in it. Bring me names. Anyone. The laundress who buys your little charms. The fence on Tanner's Row. Give me five names and this paper stays folded, and you taste for the crown until your tongue gives out, honored and safe, exactly as you have always been."

*Exactly as I have always been.* As if safety had ever been anything but a leash measured out link by link.

"I'll consider it," Freya said.

"Do more than consider." Corvane rose, and for a moment—only a moment—something moved behind his face, some old grief wearing the mask of patience. "The pyre does not care how small your gift is. It burns the same either way. Go home. Think."

Freya bowed and left, and it was only on the stairs, with his heart hammering against his ribs like a fist on a locked door, that he understood what Corvane had actually given him: an invitation, a deadline, and ten unguarded minutes alone with a room where the ledger lived.

* * *

He came back after dark.

This was the part his grandmother had never taught him—not the Oath-words, not the salt and bread, but this: how to walk back into the wolf's den wearing the wolf's own calm. He told himself it was for the names. For the cooper's boy and the witch's daughter and all the others stacked like cordwood in grey calfskin. But beneath the noble arithmetic ran a simpler current: *if I know what Corvane knows, Corvane cannot surprise me.* Survival dressed up as mercy. He was learning to stop telling the difference.

The kitchens slept. Banked fires breathed in the dark like great sleeping animals, and the night-taster—a heavy woman named Maren who trusted Freya completely, who had once covered for him when he'd fainted from a dose of foxglove—dozed in her chair with her spoon still in her hand. He hated using her sleep as cover. He added her to the growing list of debts he intended to survive long enough to repay.

Corvane's office lock was old iron, older than the hunt, older than the new king's grandfather. Freya had picked harder locks picking Odell's strongbox for practice. It gave with a click that sounded, in the sleeping dark, like a bone breaking.

Inside, the moon through the high window laid a pale stripe across the desk. The ledger sat where it had sat all day, patient as a trap.

Freya opened it.

Names, in Corvane's small precise hand. Not just names—*assessments.* *Marta Ellin, laundress, minor charm-work, cooperative, watch.* *Tomas Reed, cooper's apprentice, age eleven, animal speech, unconfirmed, parents registered under protest.* Page after page, the city's quiet magic catalogued like inventory, each entry ending in one of three words: *watch*, *summon*, or *burn*.

Eleven. The cooper's son was eleven years old, and Corvane had written *unconfirmed* beside his name the way another man might write *unverified vintage.*

Freya's hands were shaking. He made himself copy—not everything, he couldn't risk everything—but the *burns*. Thirty-one entries marked for the equinox pyres, thirty-one lives reduced to ink, and he copied them onto a strip of paper thin enough to swallow, in a cipher of his own devising, his pen scratching in the dark like a mouse in a wall.

He was folding the strip into his boot when the door clicked shut behind him.

He did not turn around. Turning around was for people with options.

"Well," said a voice he didn't know—low, amused, a voice like smoke curling from a snuffed candle. "A clerk leaves a ledger open, and look what walks in. The city is generous tonight."

Freya turned slowly. A woman leaned against the door he was fairly sure he had locked. Clerk's grey dress, clerk's plain coiled hair, a clerk's ink-stained fingers—and eyes that held none of a clerk's boredom. They were dark and quick and hungry, and they were looking at him the way he had seen starving men look through bakery windows.

"You're not a clerk," he said.

"And you're not a thief," she said pleasantly. "Thieves take silver. You're taking *paper*. Paper with names on it. Which means you're either very brave or very frightened, and frightened men are far more interesting." She pushed off the door and drifted closer, circling, and Freya felt something he had no word for—a pressure behind his eyes, a sensation like fingers leafing through the pages of him. "Hold still. This won't hurt. Much."

"What are you—"

The pressure *closed*, and Freya was somewhere else.

*A summer kitchen. His grandmother's hands dusting flour. The smell of bread and salt—*

Gone. Not hidden. *Gone*, the way a tooth is gone, leaving a smooth aching place where the root used to be. He staggered, caught the desk's edge, and the world snapped back to the moon-striped office, and the woman was watching him with her head tilted, tasting something on the air.

"Bread," she murmured. "Salt. An old woman's kitchen. Old words in an older language." Her hunger sharpened into something almost reverent. "Oh, you lovely, *loaded* thing. Do you know what you're carrying? I've eaten a hundred memories this year—first kisses, dying fathers, a queen's coronation once, from a senile chambermaid—and none of them sang like that. You're not carrying a memory. You're carrying a *country*."

Every instinct screamed at Freya to run, to fight, to deny. Instead he did the thing Corvane had trained into him across a thousand tasting-tables: he showed nothing.

"I don't know what you mean," he said. "You've stolen from the wrong man. I'm a food-taster. My head is full of broths."

Her laugh was soft and genuine and utterly without mercy. "Your head is full of broths the way a vault is full of dust." She straightened her clerk's dress, smoothed it, became—in one uncanny shift—a tired civil servant again. "Here's what happens now. You'll finish your copying—I saw which page you wanted; I'm not a monster—and you'll walk out of here, and you won't speak of me, because speaking of me means explaining why you were here, and we'd both hang. And I will find you again, taster. When I'm hungry. And I am *always* hungry."

She unlocked the door—it had been locked; she had simply been standing behind it—and paused on the threshold.

"Sable," she said, as though he'd asked. "In case you want to know what to be afraid of."

Then she was gone, and Freya stood alone in the moonlight with a stolen strip of paper against his leg and a hole in his head where bread and salt used to be.

* * *

He did not go home. Home was a known address, and known addresses were coffins with better light. He went instead to the one place left in Vellmark where a hunted man could think: the roof of the old customs house, where Ives had said to come if ever the word *equinox* reached him, and it had, twice over.

Ives was already there, wrapped in a boatman's coat, mending a chart by lantern-light with the concentration of a man stitching a wound. He looked up when Freya climbed over the parapet, read his face in one glance, and put the chart away.

"They know about the Saltgate," Freya said. "Corvane knows I was there. He's given me until the equinox to name other gifted—or join them on the pyre. And I got the ledger. Thirty-one names marked for burning. Children, Ives. There are *children* in it."

"Give me the names. We'll move them." Ives took the strip of paper, and his jaw tightened as he read. "Reed. The cooper's boy. I mapped his grandfather's shop, back when his grandfather's shop was in a country that existed." He looked up. "What else?"

Freya hesitated. The ache where the summer kitchen had been throbbed like weather coming. "Someone found me in Corvane's office. A woman. She—" He made himself say it plainly, because plainly was the only way it wouldn't hollow him further. "She ate a memory. Out of my head. Just reached in and took it. She said—" His voice failed and restarted. "She said I'm carrying a country. She could *taste* it."

Ives had gone very still. Lantern-light moved in his grey eyes.

"A memory-thief," he said quietly. "Sable. They call her that in the low quarter—she trades in other people's pasts. I thought she was a rumor." He rose, pacing the narrow roof, and Freya watched fear move through him—not fear for himself; fear doing arithmetic. "Freya, listen to me. Corvane wants your body on a pyre. That's terrible, but it's *simple*. This woman wants what's in your head. And if she takes enough of it—if she tastes Aurelan whole—she won't need the door to open for you. She'll have the key's shape. She could make a wax impression of your soul and press it to that gate."

"Can she?" Freya's mouth was dry. "Could she open it?"

"I don't know." Ives stopped pacing. "No one knows. The Oath was written for rememberers, not thieves. But I'll tell you what I do know." He gripped Freya's shoulder, and his hand was cold from the river wind. "You have maybe days. Corvane's deadline, Sable's appetite—they're converging on the same date, the same door, the same man. Whatever you meant to decide, decide it now."

Below them, Vellmark lay spread out under a fat white moon, ten thousand windows, ten thousand sleepers, and somewhere among them a thief licking her lips over the flavor of bread and salt.

Freya looked north, toward the harbor, toward the black salt door set in the seawall that would not open for him yet.

"Then I go back," he said. "One more time. Tomorrow I taste the king's breakfast, smile at Corvane, and play the loyal dog until I find out what the door actually costs." He swallowed. "Because I'm not feeding that woman another bite of my life. And I'm not giving Corvane a single name."

Ives studied him for a long moment. "You understand what you're choosing between."

"I understand I'm running out of *not* choosing."

Far off across the roofs, a bell began to toll the midnight hour, and Freya counted the strokes—one, two, three—like a man counting the coins left in a purse, knowing that soon, one way or another, he would have to spend them all.
