## 11. Odell's Choice

The knock came at the wrong hour.

Freya knew the rhythms of Vellmark the way he knew the rhythms of a kitchen: when the fishwives rose, when the tallow-chandlers opened their shutters, when the watch changed at Saltgate and again at the Bellows Gate. He knew which hours belonged to which sounds, and so he knew that no honest visitor knocked on Odell's door in the grey hour before dawn, when even the gulls had not yet found their voices.

He was not there to see it. That was the thing he would carry afterward, the thing that would sit in his chest like a swallowed stone: he was three streets away, asleep in his rented room above the cooper's yard, and the first he knew of it was the sound of boots—many boots, moving in step, the way soldiers move when they have stopped caring whether anyone hears them.

He dressed in the dark. He did not know why. Habit, perhaps—the taster's instinct, the sense that something in the air had turned, like milk on the edge of spoiling. He climbed to the window and looked out over the rooftops toward Candlemaker's Row, where Odell kept his shop behind a façade of mended kettles and secondhand locks, and saw the smoke.

Not chimney smoke. Chimney smoke rises straight and thin in still air. This smoke leaned, and it was the wrong color, grey-white with a sweetness under it that Freya could taste from three streets away because his tongue had been ruined by three years of poisons and now tasted everything too well. Witch-ash, the low quarter called it. They burned what they confiscated, and what they burned left a residue that got into the rain and came down on the roofs for days afterward, so that children said you could tell a raid by the way the puddles tasted.

Freya stood at the window until his legs went cold, and then he did the most cowardly and most sensible thing he had ever done: he sat down on the floor with his back against the bed and waited for daylight, because daylight was when a man could walk abroad without being remembered.

* * *

Odell Fenn was not a good man, if goodness meant kindness freely given. He was a fence—a buyer of stolen goods, a mover of contraband charms, a man who weighed everything twice and paid in thirds. He had a face like a closed ledger and hands that smelled always of metal polish, and he had never once in three years said anything to Freya that could be mistaken for warmth.

But he had taken Freya's luck-charms without asking what they were. He had taught him which pawnbrokers asked questions and which ones only counted coin. And once, in the first winter, when Freya had been new to the city and starving and stupid enough to charm a beggar's cough away in broad daylight, Odell had grabbed him by the collar and dragged him into an alley and held him there, nose to nose, breath sour with gin, and said: *You don't do that. Not here. Not ever. You want to spend your gift, you spend it through me, and I decide who sees the change.* And then he had bought Freya a bowl of stew, because Odell's cruelty and Odell's care were poured from the same jug and neither could be told from the other.

Now Freya stood in the crowd outside the Assize Yard and watched them bring Odell out in a cart.

It was market day, which was why there was a crowd at all; the magistrates had learned that executions drew better attendance when people were already abroad with money in their pockets. The criers had worked the quarter since dawn: *Harborer of forbidden charms. Refuser of registration. Traitor to the Crown's peace.* They had not needed to say more. Everyone in the low quarter had bought something from Odell, or sold something, or known someone who had, and everyone understood that this was the point—not justice but instruction. The gallows was a lesson, and the whole city was the schoolroom, and attendance was compulsory in every way but name.

They had not beaten him much. Freya noted that with a professional eye, the way he noted flavors. A split lip, crusted brown. One eye swollen to a slit. His hands were bound in front of him, and they were shaking—but old men's hands shake, and Odell was fifty if he was a day, and Freya told himself that was all it was, the shaking. He was very good at telling himself things. It was, after all, how he had survived: *the broth is only broth, the charm is only a charm, the notebook is only paper.*

The cart stopped beneath the scaffold. A magistrate read the charges in a voice flattened by repetition, and the crowd made the noise crowds make—not a roar, not a jeer, but a low shifting murmur, the sound of several hundred people each privately deciding how much of this they were going to look at.

And then the interrogator stepped forward, and everything changed.

Freya knew him. Not by name—names were dangerous currency in Vellmark and Freya spent none—but by type. Grey coat, plain boots, the unhurried manner of a man whose work is done mostly sitting down. Corvane's people dressed like clerks because Corvane believed fear traveled better in an envelope than on a blade. This one carried a small wooden board with papers on it, and he held it up, and he turned—not to the crowd, but to the cart—and Freya saw his mouth move, and though the words did not carry across the yard, Freya had spent three years reading lips across banquet tables while pretending to sip soup, and he read them now.

*Last chance. Give us the name. Any name. Walk away.*

That was the offer. That was always the offer; it was cheaper than rope and it broke men faster. They had done it to the hedge-witch from the bakery, Freya had heard later—she had given up two names, both false, both of dead men, and they had burned her anyway, because the lesson required burning. But sometimes, with the right prisoner, they let the trade stand. Sometimes a man walked away from the cart with his life and someone else's death in his pocket, and lived out his years in some grey village, and never slept a full night again, and everyone called it mercy.

Odell looked up at the interrogator for a long time.

From where Freya stood—pressed against a shuttered stall at the back of the crowd, hood up, heart going like a triphammer—he could see the old man's face in profile, tilted toward the grey sky. He could see the mouth working. He could see Odell say something, one word or two, and shake his head, slowly, the way he shook his head over a counterfeit coin: not angry, not afraid. Just certain. *No. This isn't real. Take it away.*

The interrogator folded his papers. The magistrate nodded. The rope went up.

Freya did not close his eyes. He wanted to; every nerve in his body screamed at him to look away, to turn around, to be anywhere else, and he stood in the press of bodies and forced himself to watch, because he owed Odell that much and knew it was nothing like enough. He watched the trapdoor open. He watched the body drop and jerk and go still with a terrible mechanical swiftness, faster than he had expected, almost mercifully fast, and he hated himself for noticing that, for cataloguing it, for the taster's reflex that turned even this into data.

The crowd exhaled. Somewhere near the front a woman began to weep, quietly, without drama, the way people wept at prices. A child asked its mother something and was shushed. The guards moved along the barrier, nudging people back into motion, and the crowd obeyed, because crowds always obeyed, because obedience was the cheapest thing a person owned and the one thing the crown would take from you last.

Freya stayed until the end. He watched them cut Odell down and load the body onto a handcart for the potter's field, because paupers' graves were part of the lesson too. Then he walked home through the market with his hood up and his face arranged into the mild blankness of a man thinking about dinner, and he bought a herring he did not want from a fishwife who had known Odell twenty years, and she wrapped it in paper with steady hands and met his eyes for half a second, and neither of them said anything, because saying anything was how people died now.

* * *

His room above the cooper's yard smelled of oak shavings and cold ash. He lit no lamp. He sat on the floor with his back against the wall and the surviving half of his notebook in his lap—the half he had not burned, the pages about Aurelan, the Oath-words in his grandmother's hand, the fragment of map with the Saltgate marked in faded ink—and he tried to understand what had happened, and could not, and understood it completely, both at once.

Odell had known his name.

Of course he had. Three years of business. Charms passed through his hands with Freya's mark on them, or close enough to a mark. If the interrogators had squeezed—and they had squeezed; the split lip said so, the swollen eye said so—all Odell had to do was say one word. *Taster,* or the boy's street-name, or simply *the young one who brings the charms.* One word, and the cart would have rolled away empty, and Odell would have gone home to his kettles and his gin.

He hadn't said it.

Freya turned the notebook over in his hands. The leather was worn soft at the corners where his thumbs had worried it for years. Inside were the only memories of Aurelan that existed outside his own skull: the smell of the terraced orchards above the capital, the bell-voice of the tide-bells, his grandmother's kitchen with its salt dish and its black bread, the words of the Oath written out phonetically because his grandmother had refused to teach him letters she thought might be copied. A country, folded flat between two covers. A country that existed now in exactly two places in all the world: this book, and him.

And Odell had died rather than add a third place—or rather than empty the other two. Freya didn't know which. Perhaps Odell hadn't either. Perhaps it hadn't been loyalty at all; perhaps it had been spite, or stubbornness, or the simple arithmetic of a fence who knew that names sold cheap and silence cost exactly what it cost. People built monuments out of less. Grief would build a monument out of anything.

But it didn't matter. That was the thing he kept circling and kept landing on, the stone at the bottom of the well: *it didn't matter why.* Odell was dead. Odell was dead and Freya was alive, and the difference between them was not courage or cleverness or worth. It was silence. Other people's silence. The hedge-witch's two false names had bought the hunt a few wasted days. Odell's silence had bought him nothing at all and Freya everything. Every day Freya had survived in Vellmark had been purchased with someone else's discretion, someone else's blindness, someone else's decision not to see the boy who tastes. The laundress with the luck-charm. The child with the button. Odell, holding his tongue while they broke his face.

He had been saved by other people's silence for three years, and the bill had come due today, and it had been paid by a man with metal-polish hands who had never once said a kind word to him.

Freya set the notebook down. He went to the window. Below, the cooper's apprentices were sweeping shavings, and the gulls had finally found their voices, and the city went on being the city, vast and indifferent and hungry. Somewhere in it, Corvane was reading reports. Somewhere in it, the grey-coated interrogator was opening a fresh file. Somewhere in it, whoever had informed on Odell—because there had been an informer; there was always an informer—was spending whatever they'd been paid, and sleeping fine, or not.

He thought about the door of black salt under the harbor wall. He thought about the price it demanded, the price Ives had described in his careful cartographer's voice: *it takes something, Freya. It always takes something. We just don't know what yet.* He had flinched from that. He had told himself there would be time to learn the terms, time to find another way, time to be safe a little longer.

There was no other way. There was no *longer*. Safety was a debt, and the interest was other people's lives, and he had been living on credit since the day he came to this city with a dead country in his head and a poisoner's tongue in his mouth.

Freya picked up the notebook again. He opened it to the map fragment, to the little inked archway marked *Saltgate*, and below it, in his grandmother's cramped hand, the first line of the Oath:

*I remember, therefore the door remembers me.*

He read it aloud. His voice cracked on the second word and he read it again, steadier, and then a third time, and by the fourth time it was not a recitation but a vow, and he felt it settle into him the way poison settled into him—slowly, cell by cell, becoming part of the architecture.

He would go back to the palace. He would put on the white apron and the silver pin and stand at Corvane's shoulder and taste whatever was put in front of him and smile his bland taster's smile, and while he did it he would find the ledger—the registry of names, the list of the gifted, the children on it, the *children*—and he would take it. And he would go to the Saltgate and learn the door's true price and pay it, whatever it was, because he finally understood what his grandmother had been trying to tell him over bread and salt all those years ago, in a country that no longer existed anywhere but in him:

Memory was not a burden you carried. It was a debt you owed. And debts, in the end, were always collected—one way or another, from someone.

Outside, the witch-ash was starting to come down with the evening rain, sweetening the puddles over Candlemaker's Row. Freya blew out the candle he had never lit, and in the dark he began, quietly, to plan.
