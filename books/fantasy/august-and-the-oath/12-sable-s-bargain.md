## 12. Sable's Bargain

Sable had eaten the taste of a drowned man once, and it had stayed with her for a week: brine and terror and a strange sweetness underneath, like honey stirred into seawater. She still thought of him sometimes, that fisherman from the coast road, how his memories had gone down her throat like oysters—slippery, cold, each one opening with a small wet surrender. She had not thought of him in years. But tonight, standing in the shadow of the Saltgate district with the harbor breathing its rotten-sweet breath over the rooftops, she thought of him again, because Freya Thackeray tasted like that too. Like something drowning slowly and refusing to admit it.

She had been following his scent for nine days.

Not his body-scent—that was nothing, tallow and arsenic and cheap lye soap, the smell of a man who washed himself like an apology. His memory-scent was another thing entirely. Memory had a smell to those of her trade, though no two thieves described it the same way. Odors, Sable called them. And Freya's odor was a country.

It came off him in layers. The topmost layer was fear, sharp as vinegar, laid down fresh every morning since the proclamation. Beneath that, the palace kitchens, the broth, the long discipline of swallowing poison without flinching—that stratum tasted metallic, professional, almost boring. But under everything, so deep she had to press close to even catch it, there was something vast and green and salted, something with bread in it, and bells, and a language of doors. A whole nation folded into one man's skull like a letter pressed between the pages of a book.

Sable had eaten towns before. A village in the Vellmark hills, once, when the plague took everyone but her and the memories were going spare anyway; a garrison's worth of soldiers who'd rather forget a massacre than live with it. Towns were rich. Towns were a feast. But a *country*—a country erased from every map, unmade from every mind, surviving only because one stubborn taster's tongue kept rolling the word *Aurelan* around behind his teeth like a lozenge he refused to swallow—

Her mouth watered just thinking about it.

*Patience,* she told herself, the way her first teacher had told her, back when she was a gutter-child in Corvane's city and didn't yet know what her mouth was for. *A meal this size is not taken. It is earned.*

But patience, like everything else, could be spent.

* * *

She found him at the pawnshop on Tanner's Row, the one run by the fence called Odell—a fat, careful man whose odor smelled of candle-wax and guilt, and who had already sold three names to the registrars and hated himself for it in a way Sable found almost delicious. She had tasted Odell twice, lightly, through a handshake and a borrowed coin. He was a well of shame. Shame aged beautifully.

Freya came at dusk, hood up, moving like a man who had learned that crowds were cover and shadows were traps. He carried a wrapped parcel under his arm—bread, by the smell of it, and something else beneath the bread, paper and ink, a notebook's ghost. Sable drifted along the opposite side of the street, matching his pace, letting the evening crowd close around her like water around a stone. She wore the face of a tired clerk today, grey wool and ink-stained fingers, a face that said *I am nobody, please walk past me.* It was one of her best faces. She had eaten it off a dying tax assessor eleven years ago and it had never once failed her.

He turned down Cooper's Stair, the cut-through where the lamplighters hadn't come in three weeks. Narrow. Empty. Good.

Sable let him get halfway down before she spoke.

"You burn your notebook," she said, "and then you carry what's left of it under your arm like a sick bird. That's either very brave or very stupid."

He stopped. She watched his shoulders do the arithmetic—weight shifting to the balls of his feet, right hand drifting toward the knife he kept at the small of his back. Trained. Not a fighter, but trained. Tasters learned to survive the first three seconds of anything; after that they were supposed to be dead or saved.

"Who's asking?" he said, without turning.

"A friend." She came down the stair toward him, unhurried, her clerk's shoes clicking on the wet stone. "Or a customer. Or a creditor. Depends entirely on your answer."

He turned then, and she got her first proper look at his face in the half-dark: young still, despite the poison-years, with the kind of plainness that was itself a disguise. Only his eyes gave anything away, and what they gave away was exhaustion—the specific exhaustion of a man who has been holding a door shut with his shoulder for three years and has begun to hear it creak.

"I know you," he said slowly. "The registrar's office. You've been at the palace."

"Twice." She smiled with the clerk's thin lips. "You taste for the crown, Master Thackeray. I taste for myself. We're practically colleagues."

Something moved behind his eyes—recognition, then denial, then the cold drop of understanding. She felt it land on her own tongue, secondhand, the way strong emotion always bled off people near her: fear, going sour at the edges.

"The memory-thief," he said. "They say you eat what you take."

"They say I eat children." She clicked her tongue. "I've never once eaten a child. Children's memories are gristle. Everything is either sweets or terror, no middle notes whatsoever." She spread her hands, palms out, the universal gesture of harmlessness, which she had always found hilarious. "I'm not here to hurt you, taster. I'm here to make you an offer."

"There's a hunt on," he said. "Corvane's hunters. If you're seen with me—"

"If I'm seen with you, I'll be seen with a clerk talking to a food-taster about bread prices." Her smile widened a fraction. "I'm very good at being seen. Now. Are we going to stand in this stairwell all night, or will you walk with me to the harbor wall? There's a bench there. The view is better. And I promise—" she held up one ink-stained finger, "—that I will touch nothing you don't give me freely. On my trade."

"That's not a vow anyone trusts."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "It isn't. Walk anyway."

* * *

The bench sat where the harbor wall curved away from the fish-market, facing the black water and, beyond it, the dark hump of the Saltgate district. Low tide had pulled the sea back from the pilings, exposing the weeping green stone, and the air smelled of kelp and tar and old rope. Somewhere behind them a tavern fiddled through a song about a soldier's return, badly.

Freya sat at the far end of the bench, parcel on his knees, knife hand loose. Sable sat closer than courtesy allowed and pretended not to notice him notice.

"Do you know what I am?" she asked. "Actually know? Not the stories."

"I know enough."

"You know rumor." She tipped her head back, looking at the first stars pricking through the smoke-haze. "Rumor says I steal memories. Fine. Rumor is a blunt instrument. Here is the truth, sharpened: I don't take anything. Taking implies force, and force bruises the goods. What I do is *taste*. A handshake. A brush of hands over a coin. Skin to skin, a few seconds, and I can sip the top layer of what a person is—their morning, their mood, their mother's face if it floats high enough. Most people never miss it. The mind heals over a sipped memory like a tongue finds the gap where a tooth was. They feel lighter afterward and call it relief." She glanced sideways at him. "You, of all people, should understand the economics of tasting."

His jaw tightened. "That's not the same."

"It's exactly the same," she said softly. "You drink poison so your king can eat safely. I drink other people's lives so I can afford to keep mine. The only difference is that your customers consent and mine don't." She let that sit in the salt air for a moment. "Though yours don't consent either, do they? The king doesn't ask. The poison doesn't ask. You've simply arranged yourself between two hungers and called it a profession."

"You didn't drag me down here to insult me."

"No." She turned to face him fully, and let the clerk's face fall away—not into another mask, but into her own, the one she wore perhaps four times a year: plain, pale, patient, with eyes the color of tea left too long. People found her real face more frightening than any disguise. She'd never understood why. It was the only honest thing about her. "I dragged you down here because I want to make you rich, Master Thackeray. Richer than you've ever been. I want to give you the one thing you can't buy, can't charm, can't taste your way past."

"And that is?"

"The price." She watched the word land. Watched his breath go shallow. Ah, there it was—the door-odor flaring under his fear, green and salted, bread and bells. "You've found it. Don't bother denying it; I've followed your odor to the harbor wall three nights running, and a door of black salt doesn't hide from someone like me any better than you do. You stood in front of it and it wouldn't open. Because it wants something. And you don't know what."

Freya said nothing. His silence was very loud.

"I do," Sable said.

"How could you possibly—"

"Because I ate a man who knew." She said it flatly, without theater, and that was the cruelty of it. "Forty years ago there was a priest of Aurelan—one of the last—who carried the door's liturgy in his head. When the erasure came for him, he ran to a monastery in the mountains and paid the brothers to hide him, and when the erasure reached the monastery anyway, he did the only thing left. He gave himself to a memory-eater. My teacher. All of it, willingly, so that the knowledge would survive somewhere—even if that somewhere was a thief's appetite." She shrugged. "My teacher died fat and happy, and I inherited her pantry. I have a dead priest's last forty years sitting in me like a swallowed stone. Including the rite. Including the price."

The tide sighed against the pilings. Far off, the fiddle stumbled and began again.

"What price," Freya said. His voice had gone careful, the way voices go when they're carrying something breakable.

Sable smiled and shook her head slowly, savoring it. This was the part she loved—the moment before the bargain, when the other person wanted the answer more than air and both of them knew it.

"Not for free," she said. "Here's my offer, and I'll make it only once, so listen. You let me taste one memory of Aurelan. One. Chosen by me, given freely, skin to skin, a minute of your time. In exchange, I tell you the true price of the door—all of it, no riddles, no half-truths—and I swear on my trade that I take nothing else. No follow-up sips. No ambush later. One memory, one secret, fair trade between professionals."

"Why would you want one memory?" he asked. "If you're what they say, why not take the whole country?"

"Because I can't," she said, and the honesty surprised her as it left her mouth. "Not by force. A gift the size of a nation doesn't go down a thief's throat unwilling—it chokes. It would tear me apart from the inside, and the door would still be shut, because the Oath would die with me. No. A country has to be *carried*, not stolen. Which means the only way I ever taste Aurelan is if its key gives it to me." She leaned closer, and lowered her voice to something almost gentle. "One memory, taster. One taste of the country you're going to lose anyway. Because you will lose it—you must know that by now. Whatever the door takes, it takes everything. Your grandmother knew. Ask her ghost why she never walked through."

His head snapped toward her. "You knew my grandmother?"

"I know her odor," Sable said. "It's all over you. Bread and salt and a woman's voice saying words in a language that isn't on any map anymore." She let her smile fade into something that might, in a better woman, have been sympathy. "She's the reason you're still carrying it. And she's the reason you'll die carrying it, unless someone tells you what the door actually costs. So. One memory. One truth. Do we have a trade?"

For a long moment Freya Thackeray looked at her, and Sable looked back, and between them the harbor breathed its slow rotten breath.

"No," he said.

The word was quiet and absolute, a door closing.

Sable blinked. Of all the answers she had rehearsed against—bargaining, trembling, greedy yes—she had somehow not prepared for a clean refusal. "No?"

"No." He stood, parcel clutched to his chest, and for the first time all night his voice rose, low and fierce, the fear burning off into something harder underneath. "You think I don't know what you are? You think I haven't watched what happens to things people sell? Odell sells names. The registrars sell mercy. Every person in this city is trading pieces of themselves to stay alive one more week, and you want me to start handing out my country by the spoonful." His knuckles had gone white around the parcel. "Aurelan isn't mine to sell. It's mine to *carry*. There's a difference, and it's the only difference left in me."

"Sentiment," Sable said. "How expensive."

"It's the only thing I have left that isn't for sale." He stepped back from the bench. "Thank you for the offer. I mean that—I'd rather know the price than not. But not from you. Not that way."

And he turned and walked up out of the harbor dark, quick and straight, not running, because running would mark him, and Sable sat alone on the bench with the tide coming in and felt something she almost never felt.

Hunger with no plan attached.

* * *

She caught him three nights later.

Not because she'd planned to—she told herself that, walking home through the rain along the cooper's lane, telling herself she was merely passing, merely curious, merely keeping an eye on her investment. But her feet knew the shape of his route the way her tongue knew the shape of a good vintage, and when he came down the lane with his collar up and the rain streaming off his hood, she was simply there, stepping out of a doorway, and her hand closed around his wrist before either of them had decided anything.

Skin to skin.

Rain on both their arms, cold water bridging them, and the contact was enough—barely, deliciously enough. She felt him realize it a half-second too late, felt his whole body try to become a locked room, and laughed aloud into the rain because locking a door against her was like locking a door against weather.

"Don't—" he started.

"One sip," she murmured. "You refused to choose. So I'll choose."

She dove.

His defenses were good—three years of hiding teaches a person to pack their memories in straw and darkness—but she wasn't after the vault. She skimmed. That was her art: skimming, the light touch, the swallow that barely rippled the surface. She went through the top layers like a gull over water—fear, the kitchen, the broth, Corvane's cologne, the burned pages of a notebook, ash and grief—and then, at the very edge of the deep water, where the country-odor rose thickest, she hooked one bright thing and drank.

A summer kitchen.

It came into her whole: whitewashed walls sweating in the heat, a sloped roof of red tile, bunches of herbs hanging upside down from the rafters like sleeping bats. A long table scarred by forty years of knives. Through the open door, a garden blazing with poppies, and beyond the garden—there, impossible, unbearable—the blue line of the Aurelan coast, the true coast, the coast that existed nowhere on any living map. Someone was baking. The oven breathed heat and the smell of bread and rosemary, and a woman's voice was singing in a language made of doors and thresholds, and a child—small hands, flour to the wrists—was pressing a thumbprint into a round of dough, and outside, far off, the bells of a city that no longer existed rang the hour across the water, and the whole of it was drenched in afternoon light the color of honey and it was *alive*, it was so alive, a country in a single room—

Then it was in her, and it was hers, and it was gone from him.

Sable opened her eyes. Rain fell. Freya stood in front of her with his wrist still in her grip, and his face was doing something terrible and quiet: not rage, not yet. Recognition. He was feeling the shape of the absence, running his tongue over the gap the way a tongue finds a lost tooth, and finding that the tooth had been a whole summer, a whole kitchen, a whole coast, a woman's song.

"What did you take," he whispered. Not *what did you take from me*—he already knew. Just: *what.*

"A kitchen," Sable said. Her voice came out strange, thickened, drunk. The memory sat in her like a swallowed sun. She could still smell the rosemary. Gods, she could still smell the rosemary, and somewhere under her ribs the dead priest's liturgy stirred and turned over, recognizing the flavor of home. "A summer kitchen. Bread, and a garden, and the bells. I'm sorry." And the appalling thing, the thing she would examine later and find no remorse in, was that she meant it the way a diner means it when the meal is over: sorry it's finished.

Freya pulled his arm free. For a moment neither of them moved, and the rain came down between them like a curtain drawn on a stage.

"You swore," he said.

"On my trade, I'd take nothing you gave freely. You never gave anything freely." She flexed her fingers, watching the rain run off them. "I'm a thief, taster. I told you what I was. Believing me was your mistake, and it won't be your last."

He looked at her for a long time. And what frightened her—not much, but a little, a cold pinprick under the warmth of the stolen summer—was that he didn't look broken. He looked like a man adding a debt to a ledger. Like a man who had just learned exactly how much he had to lose, and was filing the number away.

"When I walk through that door," Freya Thackeray said quietly, "whatever is left of me is going to remember your face."

"Nothing will be left of you," Sable said. "That's the price. Didn't I tell you?"

And she smiled, turned up her collar, and walked away into the rain, carrying a summer kitchen inside her like a coal she could warm her hands on all the way home—while behind her, in the drowned dark of the cooper's lane, the last key of Aurelan stood motionless in the falling water, one memory poorer, and beginning, at last, to understand what the door would demand.

Three nights later, the summons came to the palace kitchens, sealed with Corvane's signet: *Come to my study. Come alone. We will speak of your grandmother.*
