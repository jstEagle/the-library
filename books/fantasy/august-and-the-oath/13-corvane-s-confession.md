## 13. Corvane's Confession

The summons came at dusk, folded inside a napkin of white linen that smelled faintly of rosemary and something beneath it—something mineral, like a cellar floor.

*Master Corvane requests the honor of your company. The west study. After the evening meal.*

Freya read it twice by the light of the kitchen's banked ovens, then held one corner to a coal until it curled and blackened and was gone. Around him the kitchens went on with their evening rhythm: copper pots being scoured, a boy hauling water, the head cook arguing about the morrow's sauces. No one looked at him. No one ever looked at him. That had been the whole architecture of his life for three years—invisibility as craft, invisibility as survival—and now a single line of Corvane's handwriting had made the invisibility feel like a costume he'd been wearing so long he'd forgotten it was a costume at all.

He ate the evening meal in his narrow room above the taster's quarters, chewing each bite thirty times out of habit, tasting for things that were not there. Then he dressed in his good coat—the dark grey one, unadorned, the coat of a man who is furniture—and went up through the palace's spine toward the west wing.

The corridors changed as he climbed. Down below, the stone sweated and smelled of onions and wet ash; up here the air was dry and cold and carried beeswax and old paper. Servants thinned out. Somewhere a clock ticked with the patience of a thing that has outlived everyone who wound it. Freya counted doorways the way he counted everything now—cataloguing exits, cataloguing witnesses, cataloguing the weight of the candlesticks in case candlesticks were what the evening came to.

The west study door was oak, banded with iron, older than the rest of the wing. It stood open.

"Thackeray." Corvane's voice from within, warm as a hand near a fire but not touching it. "Come in. Close it behind you."

* * *

The study was not what Freya expected, though he could not have said what he had expected. Perhaps instruments. Perhaps the tools of the hunt laid out like a surgeon's tray: pincers, ledgers, the little silver spoons they used in the interrogation rooms to feed suspects truth-sap until their tongues betrayed them. Instead there were books. Walls of them, floor to ceiling, spines cracked with use rather than neglect. A fire burned low in a grate big enough to roast an ox. And on the desk, between an inkwell and a brass scale, sat two cups and a small clay teapot, steam rising from its spout in a thin, patient thread.

"You'll take tea," Corvane said. It was not quite a question. He was pouring before Freya had finished closing the door, and Freya watched his hands—broad, scarred across the knuckles, the hands of a man who had once done work rougher than tasting—and noted that the pour was steady. Too steady. Steady the way a blade is steady.

"I know you'll want to taste it first," Corvane went on, setting a cup on the near side of the desk. "I'd be insulted if you didn't. Chamomile and valerian, a little honey. Nothing else. I prepared it myself, in this room, with these hands." He held the hands up, palms out, as if presenting them for inspection. "You have my word, which I know means very little to you. But taste anyway. Consider it a professional courtesy between colleagues."

"We're not colleagues," Freya said.

"No," Corvane agreed, and something moved behind his eyes—something old and tired and hungry all at once. "We're something else. Sit down, Thackeray. We're going to talk about Aurelan."

The name landed in the room like a dropped glass.

Freya did not move. His body did what three years of training had taught it: nothing. Face slack, breathing even, pulse—well, the pulse he couldn't govern, but no one in this room was holding his wrist. He let three seconds pass, four, five, long enough that a guilty man would have started inventing denials, and then he crossed the room and sat in the chair opposite the desk and picked up the cup and tasted the tea the way he tasted everything: first the tip of the tongue for sweetness, then the sides for sour, then the back of the throat where the poisons lived.

Chamomile. Valerian. Honey. Nothing else.

He drank.

Corvane watched him do it with an expression Freya had never seen on him before—not the flat professional approval of the tasting hall, not the cold courtesy of the summons. Something closer to grief, if grief had been left out in the weather for twenty years and come back as something harder.

"Do you know how I found you?" Corvane asked. "Three years ago. A starving boy off the mountain roads who claimed to have a palate. Do you know why I took you?"

"You needed a taster."

"I needed *you*. Or someone like you." Corvane settled back into his chair, and the firelight caught the grey at his temples, the deep lines around his mouth. He was not an old man, but he wore age the way some men wear armor—deliberately, piece by piece, each piece earned. "There's a stillness in the gifted. Did you know that? Poison-tongues, charm-menders, hedge-witches, weather-readers—they all carry it. A quietness. Like a house where the family has died but the furniture is still arranged. I've seen it in hundreds of faces over eleven years. But yours—" he tapped the desk once, softly, "—yours had something under the stillness. Grief. The specific grief of a man remembering a place that doesn't exist."

Freya set the cup down. His hands were perfectly steady. He was distantly proud of that.

"I don't know what you mean."

"No," Corvane said. "Of course you don't." He opened a drawer. What he took out was not irons or a writ or a vial. It was a spoon. An ordinary wooden spoon, dark with age, its bowl worn smooth and pale at the lip where a mouth had pressed against it ten thousand times. He turned it slowly in the firelight. "Do you know what this is?"

"A spoon."

"It's an Aurelan spoon. Beechwood. They carved them in the river towns—every household had them, every child got one at weaning, carved with their birth-month sign on the handle. August." He turned the handle toward Freya. There, worn nearly to nothing, was a leaf-shape. The August sign. "This was mine."

The room was very quiet. The clock ticked. The fire shifted.

"My mother gave it to me when I was six," Corvane said, "and told me never to show it to anyone, because soon there would be no one left alive who would know what it meant. She was right. She usually was, about the important things." He set the spoon down on the desk between them, gently, the way a man lays down a weapon he no longer has the strength to use. "My name wasn't always Corvane, Thackeray. In another country—a country that was real, that had rivers and harvests and bread baked with salt in the crust—I had another name. A name in a language that no longer exists anywhere except in the heads of maybe nine people alive, and most of them don't know they're carrying it."

Freya's throat had gone tight. He made himself say it. "You were born there."

"I was born there," Corvane said. "And when the erasure came—when the maps were redrawn and the records burned and the mind-wrights went province to province with their little silver instruments, taking the country out of people's heads like rotten teeth—I *let them take it.*" His voice did not rise. That was the terrible thing; it stayed level, conversational, a man discussing crop yields. "I stood in a queue in a market square in what used to be the Vellmark road district, and I handed my childhood to a stranger with kind eyes and a silver instrument, and I walked away lighter. Free. I forgot my mother's face. I forgot the smell of the river in August. I forgot the Oath-words my father taught me over bread and salt. And in exchange, I lived. Do you understand? *I chose to live.*"

"And now you hunt us."

"Now I hunt the gifted," Corvane said, "because hunting them is the only proof, day after day, year after year, that the choice was worth making. Every witch I drag to the registry, every charm-seller I hang, every gifted child I enter into that ledger—it's me telling the crown: look how loyal I am. Look how thoroughly I killed him. The boy from the other country. He's dead. I killed him myself." He laughed, short and airless. "Eleven years, Thackeray. Eleven years of proving it, and do you know what I've learned? You can kill a man. You cannot kill a memory you gave away yourself. It comes back. It sits in your study at night and drinks your tea."

He looked at the spoon.

"I knew what you were the day I hired you. I've known for three years. I watched you sell luck-charms in the fish market. I watched you charm a laundress's fever down for a button and a smile. I let you, because as long as you were hidden, you were mine—a secret I kept, proof that I could keep one, that I could hold a piece of Aurelan in my fist without opening my hand. And then the Gift-Hunt came down from the crown, and my keeping became a liability, and now here we are." He spread his hands. "Here we are."

"Why tell me this?" Freya asked. His voice sounded far away to himself. "Why not just take me to the registry? Why the tea?"

"Because I need to know what you know." Corvane leaned forward, and for a moment the mask slipped entirely, and what was underneath was not a hunter at all. It was a starving man looking at bread. "The door, Thackeray. The Saltgate. My agents say you've been to the harbor wall at low tide. Say you've seen it. Tell me what it wants. Tell me what it costs. Because I have spent twenty years being a dead man walking around in a loyal servant, and if there is a door that leads back—if there is any way to un-choose what I chose—" His voice caught, actually caught, and he mastered it with visible effort, the way he'd master a tremor in a spoon. "Tell me."

And Freya understood, suddenly, completely, the shape of the trap he was sitting in. Not irons. Not the pyre. This: a man across a desk offering him his own hunger reflected back, asking him to open his hand and show the last living key to a dead country—to the one man in Vellmark who wanted it more than he feared the crown, and who would use it, or burn it, or bury it, whatever kept his own choice intact.

"The door doesn't want anything from me," Freya said quietly. "It wants everything."

"What does that mean?"

"It means I'm not going to tell you."

Something closed in Corvane's face, shutter by shutter, until the hunter was back and the starving man was gone—or rather, locked in the same room, behind the same door, the way he had been for twenty years.

"No," Corvane said. "I didn't think you would." He rose, went to the window, stood with his back to Freya, looking out at the city where the lamps were coming on one by one, each one a small surrender to the dark. "Then hear my terms. You have until the equinox. Nine days. On the morning of the equinox, the registry closes, and every name in my ledger goes to the pyre-lists—including yours, Thackeray, including the name I've protected for three years by writing it nowhere. Nine days to bring me what I've asked for. The Oath-words. The way through. Whatever the door takes, I'll pay it—I've already paid worse." He turned. In the firelight his face was half gold, half shadow, and both halves were tired. "Give me back my country, boy. It's the only mercy either of us is going to get."

"And if I can't?"

"Then on the equinox morning, I will do my duty," Corvane said, "and I will hate myself for it, and I will go on hating myself, efficiently, for the rest of my life. Which is, I'm told, what loyalty looks like." He picked up the wooden spoon, turned it once in his fingers, and held it out. "Take it. You'll understand why later. Or you won't, and it won't matter."

Freya took the spoon. It was lighter than it should have been, the way all relics are lighter than they should be, as if the years had eaten the wood from inside and left only the shape of holding.

"The equinox," Corvane said. "Nine days. Go home, Thackeray. Taste your breakfasts. Be invisible. And decide which of us is the ghost."

* * *

He walked back down through the palace's spine with the spoon inside his coat, against his chest, and the corridors seemed different now—not safer for knowing their exits, but smaller, as if the walls had drawn in around the knowledge he carried.

In his narrow room he lit no lamp. He sat on the edge of his cot in the dark and took out the surviving pages of his notebook—the half he hadn't burned, the half with the map fragment pointing to the Saltgate—and read them by the light that came under the door from the corridor, the letters swimming, the ink like small black wounds.

*Aurelan. August. The river smells of cut hay and hot stone. Grandmother says the salt must go in the crust, not on top, or the bread forgets what it's for.*

He touched the spoon through his shirt. Beechwood, worn smooth, the August leaf nearly gone.

Somewhere below, in the city, Sable was hunting the scent of a country. Somewhere above, in the west study, a man who had sold his own childhood was counting nine days. And here, in between, a food-taster sat in the dark holding a dead man's spoon and understanding, at last, what Corvane had shown him tonight—not mercy, not confession, but a mirror. The man across the desk was what happened when you chose survival over memory. The man across the desk was the future Freya was being offered, dressed up as protection, dressed up as loyalty, dressed up as a cup of chamomile with nothing in it but honey.

*I chose to live,* Corvane had said.

*So am I choosing,* Freya thought. *But not like that.*

He wrapped the spoon in the notebook pages and pushed them into the loose brick behind the hearth, next to the Oath-words his grandmother had fed him over bread and salt, and lay down on the cot, and did not sleep.

Outside, the tide was turning. Nine days to the equinox. And somewhere in the harbor wall, behind forty feet of black salt, a country waited to see what its key would do.
