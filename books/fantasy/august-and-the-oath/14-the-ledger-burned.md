## 14. The Ledger Burned

I had spent my life drawing lines on paper and calling them countries. Borders are a cartographer's arrogance—the belief that ink can hold back the sea, that a ruler's stroke can tell a river where to run. I knew better. I had watched a whole kingdom be unmade by strokes of a pen, line by line, name by name, until Aurelan was nothing but a rumor with no one left to rumor it.

So when Freya Thackeray laid Corvane's ledger on my table and said, "We burn it," I understood at once that we were not destroying a book. We were redrawing a map.

* * *

The ledger itself was an ugly thing. Bound in grey calfskin gone green with damp, its corners furred like something drowned and dredged up. Corvane's hand marched across its pages in that terrible tidy script of his—each entry a small execution waiting for its date. Names. Districts. Ages. A column headed simply *Disposition*, as if these were goods in a warehouse awaiting shipment.

I turned the pages slowly while Freya stood at the shuttered window, listening to the street below with his taster's stillness—that quality he had of making himself furniture, of being present and entirely overlooked. He'd stolen it from the registry office three nights before, wrapped in oilcloth inside a delivery of pickled herring, because Corvane would never imagine anyone wanting to steal something so foul-smelling.

"Here," I said. "Aldous Merrin, chandler's son, nine years old. Gift: *warms what he touches.* Disposition: pending."

Freya didn't turn around. "How many children?"

I counted. It took a while. "Thirty-one under the age of twelve." My voice came out rougher than I intended. "Forty-one if you count the ones whose ages weren't recorded."

"Their parents will have been questioned by now," Freya said. "Corvane works outward from the family. Grandparents last—he finds they talk fastest, actually. They've had longer to be afraid." He said it flatly, the way you recite a recipe. Three years of tasting poison teaches a person to describe horror without flinching at it. But I saw his hand on the windowsill, and it was not steady.

"You've read this thing before."

"I helped him organize it." Now he turned. In the lamplight his face was the color of the paper we were about to destroy. "He liked my penmanship. That was the joke of it—I sat in his office copying names into columns while he drank his wine, and neither of us ever spoke about why a food-taster writes so neatly, or why he never sweats. Two liars keeping each other's secrets." He crossed the room and put his palm flat on the ledger, the way you'd touch a body to confirm it was dead. "Every night I told myself I was learning where the traps were so I could avoid them. Every night I copied another child's name. And every morning I woke up and I was still safe, and they were still in the book, and I called that survival."

"It was survival," I said. "Don't apologize for it. Fear is honest. Shame is just fear wearing better clothes."

He looked at me then with something close to gratitude, and I understood—for the first time, really understood—what my debt to his grandmother had bought me. Not a favor owed. A purpose. Maren Thackeray had once pulled me out of a burning archive in Aurelan with her own two hands, my maps under my arm, and she had said: *Keep them. Someone will need to know where things were.* She hadn't lived to see her country erased. But she had guessed, somehow, that erasure was coming for somebody, someday, and she had spent her life teaching her grandson to remember.

Now her grandson wanted to burn a book. There was a symmetry in that which I found almost holy.

"Not here," I said. "And not all at once. If the registry simply vanishes, Corvane assumes theft and doubles his guards. The families stay named; only the names go underground. We want something else. We want the city to *see* it burn."

Freya frowned. "You want witnesses."

"I want a crowd." I pulled my old survey maps toward me—the ones I'd redrawn from memory, the streets of Vellmark rendered in the same careful hand I'd once used for Aurelan's coasts. "Look. The registry office sits above the Saltmarket, and the Saltmarket opens onto Tanner's Row, and Tanner's Row runs down into the low quarter like water finding a drain. The low quarter is where Odell hanged. The low quarter is where every charm-seller, hedge-healer, and luck-peddler in this city lives, because rents are cheap and neighbors mind their own business—until they don't." I tapped the map. "Burn the ledger there, publicly, and you aren't freeing thirty-one children. You're telling forty thousand people that the list can die. Lists are only power while people believe them permanent."

"And after?" Freya asked quietly. "After the fire?"

"After the fire," I said, "we stop being invisible. You understand that. Whatever happens, whatever we do next, we will be the two men who burned the registry. Corvane won't hunt us anymore. He'll *besiege* us."

Freya was silent for a long moment. Then he did the thing I had learned to watch for in him—a small motion, half smile, half wince, like a man testing a wound.

"Good," he said. "I'm tired of fitting in."

* * *

We chose the eve of the salt-market holiday, when the stalls stayed open past dark and lanterns hung over the square like a second, lower sky. The plan was simple, and simple plans are the only kind that survive contact with a crowd.

I went in first. This was the part I insisted on—the cartographer's privilege, if you like. I had spent twenty years making myself forgettable: the grey man with the grey eyes who measured things and drew them and was never remembered afterward. Disgrace had taught me that skill long before the witch-hunt did. A disgraced royal cartographer is a man everyone has already decided not to think about.

The registry office kept its ledgers in an iron cabinet behind the clerk's desk, and the clerk—a soft, sweating man named Pell—kept the key on a chain around his neck, because Corvane trusted no lock alone. But Pell also kept a bottle in the bottom drawer, and on festival eves he drank from it steadily after sundown, and he also, I had observed on three separate visits under three separate pretexts, wore his coat too warm for the season and dozed at his desk with the window cracked open for air.

I did not steal the key. Stealing leaves a gap, and gaps get investigated. Instead I waited until Pell's breathing went long and even, reached through the cracked window with a wire I'd fashioned that afternoon, and lifted the chain gently over his head as he slept. He dreamed on. Somewhere in his dream, perhaps, a heavy weight left his neck and he felt lighter. I hope he remembers it kindly.

Then I opened the cabinet, and there it was—not just the master ledger but the whole gray family of it: district copies, the interrogation summaries, the reward schedules with their obscene arithmetic of souls per coin. I took everything. My satchel, built for carrying maps through burning buildings, carried them out instead.

Freya met me in the alley behind the fishmongers', where the smell covered everything and no one asks questions of men who smell of herring. He had changed clothes—borrowed laborer's slops, a cap pulled low—and without his palace bearing he was astonishingly easy to miss. That was always the tragedy of him, I thought. A boy trained so thoroughly to disappear that he'd nearly disappeared to himself.

"To the fountain," he said. "Middle of the square. Where the light is."

* * *

I want to record honestly what happened in the Saltmarket, because afterward the rumors made it into something grander and cleaner than it was, and I have spent enough of my life around maps to distrust anything drawn too neatly.

It was not clean. It was loud and hot and terrifying, and for the first several minutes it very nearly failed.

Freya climbed the rim of the fountain with the satchel, and I stood below him in the crowd, and he began—not with a speech, because Freya cannot give speeches, but with a reading. He held the ledger open against the lantern light and read names aloud. Just names. Aldous Merrin, chandler's son, nine years old. Wenna Cade, laundress, warms water with her hands. Tam Broadfoot, six, whose gift was that flowers bloomed out of season around him. On and on, in his clear taster's voice, the voice trained to announce poison at a king's table, each name landing in the crowd like a stone dropped down a well.

People stopped. Festival crowds stop easily, but this was different—this was the particular stillness of people hearing their own secrets spoken in public. I watched a woman near me go white and grab her son's shoulder. I watched a stall-keeper put down his ladle and drift closer, then closer again. I watched a pair of Corvane's plainclothes hunters at the edge of the square exchange a look and start pushing through the crowd, unhurried, professional, certain.

"They're coming," I murmured.

"I know," Freya said, and did not stop reading. He had maybe forty names left. He read them all. I will carry the sound of it to my grave: a young man standing in falling lantern-light reading out the condemned, while the condemned's neighbors pressed in around him, and nobody—nobody—moved to stop him, because every soul in that square was in the book or loved someone who was.

Then he finished, closed the ledger, and said, "This book says these people can be taken. Sold. Burned." He held it up. "So let's find out whether a book can burn."

He dropped it into the fountain basin, where the shallow water pooled around it, and struck flint to the lamp-oil he'd soaked the cover with—it was my contribution; I'd oiled the binding that afternoon, a cartographer knows how flammable treated calfskin is—and the thing caught with a soft whump that was far too small a sound for what it meant.

For one heartbeat, nothing. The flames stood up off the wet leather, orange and improbable, and the crowd stared, and the two hunters froze mid-stride, and I thought: *it's not enough, it's just a fire, they'll disperse us and rebuild the list by spring.*

Then someone laughed. A high, cracked, disbelieving laugh from somewhere in the middle of the crowd, and a voice said, "Burn it! Burn the rest!" and a woman threw her market basket onto the flames, and then it was not a fire in a fountain, it was a bonfire, and the square came apart like ice in thaw.

They didn't riot at the soldiers. That was the remarkable thing, the thing the ballads got wrong later. They rioted at the *paper*. People tore down the proclamation bills on the walls—the Gift-Hunt notices with their neat printed rewards—and fed them to the blaze. A chandler—I believe it was Merrin's father, though I never confirmed it—brought an armload of his own stock and hurled it in, candles and all, wax hissing like fury. The fire climbed ten feet, fifteen, throwing light up the facades of the guildhalls, and in that light I saw faces I will not forget: not rage, mostly. Relief. The look of people setting down a weight they'd carried so long they'd forgotten it wasn't part of their bodies.

The hunters tried to push through and the crowd simply—closed. Not violently. The way water closes. They were there and then they were elsewhere, and by the time they fought free, Freya and I were already moving, two more bodies in a river of bodies flowing down Tanner's Row, and behind us the low quarter heard the noise and came up to meet it, and where the two currents met, the whole district lit with thrown-open shutters and snatched-down lanterns and voices asking *what happened, what happened* and voices answering *the list is burning, the list is burning.*

By midnight there were fires in four places I could see from the roof where we crouched, and bells ringing out of order, and the garrison horn sounding the assembly that meant the authorities had lost the narrative of the night. By dawn, it was said, half the named families in the registry districts had packed their carts and gone—out through the marsh gates, down the coast road, anywhere the lists couldn't follow, because the lists, tonight, were ash floating on fountain water.

* * *

We sat on that rooftop until the sky went grey, sharing a loaf Odell's sister had pressed on Freya at the market—she'd recognized him, or thought she had, and wouldn't take no. Below us the city smoked and muttered. Somewhere a mob had tipped a tax collector's cart into the harbor, which had nothing to do with us and everything to do with us; a crowd that learns it can burn one book burns others.

"We're famous now," Freya said. He said it the way he announced poison. "By noon every wall in Vellmark will have our descriptions on it. Reward notices. *The taster and the mapmaker.*"

"Your description will be wrong," I said. "That's your gift, isn't it? Being overlooked. Half the witnesses will swear the reader was a woman."

"And half will swear it was you." He tore the loaf and handed me my share. "Ives. Say the true thing. You're the one who draws true lines."

I chewed. Watched the smoke. Drew the true line.

"We traded our safety for their head start," I said. "That's the whole ledger of it. Thirty-one children sleep in houses tonight instead of cells, and dozens of families are on the road ahead of the riders, and none of that happens quietly. Corvane has lost his list, so now he needs a spectacle. Two heads on the equinox pyre would restore everything the fire cost him—fear, obedience, the belief that the list comes back. We haven't escaped him, Freya. We've *scheduled* ourselves."

Freya nodded slowly, and I saw that he had known—we both had—but knowing a thing and hearing it aloud are different foods, and this one sat heavy.

"Then we don't run," he said at last. "Running gives him time. We go to the door. Equinox is eleven days away. High tide at Saltgate falls on the equinox dawn—I've checked the almanac twice." He looked east, toward the harbor, where the black salt door waited in the wall like a held breath. "Eleven days to learn what it takes. Eleven days to decide."

Below us, a new bell began to ring—not the alarm, but the palace bell, the deep one that rang only for proclamations. Voices rose in the streets beneath us, and a crier's distant voice threaded up through the smoke, and though we couldn't make out the words, we both heard the shape of them: the cadence of a state answering fire with fire.

Freya stood, brushing crumbs from the borrowed slops, and for a moment the dawn light caught him full in the face—no shadow, no furniture-trick, no disappearing. Just a young man with ash on his sleeves and a country folded up inside him like a letter no one had dared open.

"They'll come for us today," he said. "So today we go to the water."
