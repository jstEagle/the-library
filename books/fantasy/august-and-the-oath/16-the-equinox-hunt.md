## 16. The Equinox Hunt

The equinox came in on a wind off the harbor that smelled of iron and old rope, and with it came the riders.

Freya heard them before he saw them—hooves on cobbles at an hour when even Vellmark's drunks had gone home, the deliberate, unhurried rhythm of men who did not need to hurry because they owned the morning. He was awake anyway. He had been awake most of the night, sitting in the loft above Odell's shuttered shop with the surviving pages of his notebook spread around him like a fallen flock, and when the first horn sounded from the Saltgate district he knew, with the flat certainty of a man who has tasted enough poisons to know their arrival by their perfume, that the borrowed time was spent.

He went to the window and looked down into a street gone silver-grey with the hour before dawn. The riders came through it in pairs, torches guttering in the damp air, and behind them walked the footmen of the Gift-Hunt with their long poles and their iron collars on chains, and behind *them* came the carts.

Freya had seen carts like those before. Everyone in Vellmark had, these last months. But he had never watched one stop directly beneath his window, and he had never heard the voice that called up to the dark windows—not shouting, not threatening, but almost gentle, almost kind, the voice of a man reading a list:

*"By order of the Crown, all persons bearing the unsanctioned gift will present themselves for registration. Refusal is treason. Treason is the pyre."*

A door opened somewhere below. A woman's voice, cracked with sleep and fear, said something Freya could not make out. Then a child began to cry—the thin, exhausted wail of a child who had been crying long enough to have nothing left—and Freya stepped back from the window as though the sound were a hand reaching through the glass.

He thought of the ledger. Of the names in Corvane's careful hand, the children's names written smaller, tucked between their parents' entries like afterthoughts. He and Ives had burned the registry eleven days ago; he had watched the pages curl and blacken and told himself the word *freed* as they burned. But paper was only ever the shadow of a thing. Corvane had the names in his head. Corvane had always had them in his head, the way Freya had Aurelan in his, and no fire touched what lived behind the eyes.

The horns moved east along the waterfront, then north. By full light the city had changed its shape: doors barred, shutters closed, the markets empty except for soldiers moving among the abandoned stalls like gulls. Vellmark had pulled itself inside its own skin, and under that skin, Freya knew, hundreds of small magics were being smothered or hidden or swallowed—a hedge-witch eating her own charms, a luck-singer biting his tongue bloody, a child somewhere pressing her gift down so hard it would leave a bruise on her soul she'd carry forever.

*Every kindness leaves a trail,* Odell had told him, back when Odell was alive to tell him things.

Freya packed the notebook pages into the oilskin wrap against his chest, took up the plain satchel Ives had left with him, and went out through the roof.

* * *

Ives had said to meet him at the chandlery on Rope Walk if the hunt came early, and the hunt had come early, and so Freya made his way there through the back-bones of the city—over drying-sheds, down a rain channel, along the covered fish-market where the smell of yesterday's catch masked everything else. Twice he passed checkpoints. Both times he walked past with the loose-shouldered slouch of a laborer late for work, and both times the guards' eyes slid over him without catching, because invisibility was the one magic he had practiced longest and best.

Rope Walk was deserted. The chandlery's door stood open.

That was the first wrong thing. Ives was a cartographer; cartographers closed doors behind them the way other men locked them.

Inside, the smell of tallow and tar, and dust motes turning in a blade of light from the high window, and on the floor—nothing. No struggle, no blood. Just the open door, the empty room, and a scrap of paper weighted under a coil of hemp, written in Ives's quick slanting hand:

*Passage bought. Night boat, the *Kestrel*, master owes me. Equinox tide, north pier, after the bells. If I'm not there, go without me. Don't be noble about it.*

Freya read it twice. *If I'm not there.* Ives wrote contingency the way he drew coastlines—with every hazard marked and named. Which meant he had expected this possibility, which meant—

Which meant the night had teeth in it, and Freya had until the evening bells to decide how much of himself he was willing to feed them.

He was still standing over the note when he heard the bells—but not the evening bells. The alarm bells, from the direction of the harbor, ringing fast and ragged, and beneath them a sound he knew from the day they hanged Odell: the crowd-noise, the low animal murmur of people gathering to watch something terrible done properly.

He should have run. Every instinct trained into him across three years of tasting other people's deaths said *run*, said *the note said go without me, said don't be noble*. But Ives had pulled him out of the hunters' hands at Saltgate with no reason to and less to gain, had mapped a dead country from memory because a dead woman once saved his life, and Freya Thackeray had already stood in one crowd and done nothing. He would not stand in two.

He ran toward the bells.

* * *

They had taken Ives at the harbormaster's office, buying his own passage twice.

That was the story the crowd told itself in fragments as Freya pressed into its edge, hood up, shoulders bent, one more grey body among the grey bodies of the low quarter come to see. A clerk recognized him, someone said. The cartographer, the map-man, the one who'd helped burn the registry—everyone knew that face now, everyone had heard the rumors of the riots and the freed families and the two men who'd lit the fire. Ives had walked into the harbormaster's with coin in his fist asking for the *Kestrel*, and the harbormaster's clerk had looked at him and looked at the reward notice nailed beside the tide-tables, and that had been that.

They were holding him on the customs steps, and Corvane's riders had formed a ring around the plaza to keep the crowd at a distance it could see across but not cross.

Freya found a place beside a fishwife's stall and looked, because looking was all he could do and he had learned, since Odell, that refusing to look was its own kind of rot.

They had hurt him already. Not badly—not so anyone in the crowd would flinch—but Freya had spent three years learning to read damage in faces the way other men read weather, and Ives's face was a chart of small cruelties: the swelling at the jaw, the way he held his left arm, the careful blankness of a man conserving himself. His hands were bound in front of him, and even bound, even bleeding at the mouth, he stood the way he stood over his maps—like a man surveying territory.

Corvane sat a horse ten feet away, grey-cloaked, motionless, watching his men work with the patience of a falconer.

And then Ives lifted his head, and found Freya in the crowd.

It should not have been possible. The plaza was deep with bodies; Freya was hooded, half-turned, nothing. But Ives had spent his life reading ground, and perhaps a face in a crowd is just another kind of terrain, and perhaps he simply knew where Freya would be, because Ives always knew where the high ground and the exits and the one unwatched angle were. Their eyes met for half a heartbeat. Ives did not nod. Did not signal. He only looked at Freya the way he might look at a coastline he had drawn from memory and finally, after years, seen with his own eyes—and then he turned deliberately away, presenting his profile to his captors, giving Freya the gift of being unseen.

But Corvane had seen the glance. Freya watched him see it—watched the stillness sharpen, the head tilt a degree, the gloved hand rise to touch the horse's neck in a small calming gesture that was really for himself. Corvane turned in the saddle and let his gaze travel the crowd, section by section, patient as tide.

Freya melted backward into the fishwife's stall, took a basket, became a porter, became nobody, and was three streets gone before his hands stopped shaking.

*I'm not there. Go without me. Don't be noble about it.*

Too late for the second part. The first part was all he had left, and the evening bells were coming, and the *Kestrel* would sail on the equinox tide whether Freya Thackeray stood on the north pier or rotted in a cell waiting for Corvane's questions.

He cut through the ropewalks toward the harbor, and that was where Sable found him.

* * *

She was leaning against a bollard in the shadow of a net-drying frame, arms crossed, hood back, grey eyes catching the last light like coins at the bottom of a well. She did not step out to block his path. She didn't need to. The street ahead of him had acquired, somehow, two more of Corvane's footmen at its far end, walking slowly, looking into doorways, and Sable simply occupied the space between Freya and everywhere else.

"You," he said.

"Me." She smiled. It was a good smile; it had gotten her past a great many doors, and it did nothing now but show her teeth. "You look terrible, taster. Have you eaten? You strike me as a man who forgets."

"I've nothing you want."

"That's the third lie you've told me and we haven't even started." She pushed off the bollard and fell into step beside him—not grabbing, not forcing, matching his pace the way water matches a hull. "Walk with me. Walking looks innocent. Standing still looks guilty, especially today."

He walked. There was nothing else to do, and the footmen at the end of the street were coming closer, and some treacherous animal part of him noted that beside her, hooded and ordinary, he was invisible again. That was her real talent, he was beginning to understand. Not the tasting. The arranging.

"They have your cartographer," she said conversationally. "I watched. You watched too—I saw you in the crowd, under the fish-stall awning. You're better at disappearing than I gave you credit for." A pause. "He's good, your Ives. He gave them a location."

Freya's stomach dropped. "What location?"

"A false one. Oh, don't look like that—it was beautifully done, truly. He fed them the old salt warehouse on Gull Street, coordinates and tide-times and a little speech about his grandmother's debt, very moving, very specific." Her smile widened. "Specific is how you know it's a lie. Truth is vague. Lies have to be exact or nobody believes them. He'll hang for it, of course. Corvane will check Gull Street within the day, and when it's salt and rats instead of a door, he'll come back to the customs steps, and your cartographer will burn tomorrow night or the night after." She said it the way she might report the price of mackerel. "Unless."

"Unless what."

"Unless the door isn't on Gull Street. Unless it's where I think it is, and where *you* know it is, and where he knows it is, and none of you will say it out loud because saying it makes it findable." She stopped walking. They were in the lee of the net-frame, in blue shadow that smelled of hemp and brine, and she turned to face him fully, and for the first time since he'd known her the charm dropped away entirely, and what looked out of her face was hunger—clean, enormous, ancient. "Saltgate. Black salt in the harbor wall. Low tide. I've tasted it off you twice now, little crumbs, and I have dreamed about it every night since. Do you know what you carry? Do you have any idea?"

"A country," Freya said quietly.

"A *country*." She breathed the word like incense. "Not a memory. Not a life. A whole nation of lives—harbors and harvests and quarrels and songs, thousands of years of bread and salt and grief, and it's all sitting behind your eyes like a room with the door left open. I've eaten memories my whole career. Kings' secrets. Lovers' first nights. A dying woman's entire childhood, once, and I thought that was a feast." Her hand rose, almost involuntarily, toward his temple, and he stepped back and her hand fell, and she laughed at herself, softly, without shame. "Forgive me. You smell like a banquet to me. You have always smelled like a banquet. And now the city's burning down around the table, and you're going to lose everything anyway—Corvane will take you, or I will, or the pyre will, and the country dies with you either way unless someone opens that door."

"So you're offering to help me."

"I'm offering to *lead* you through the cordon tonight. Every checkpoint, every patrol, every rider Corvane has on the water—there are forty-one, I counted, I've spent the day counting. I can walk you past all of them, because I've been selling Corvane information for a month and he trusts my reports, and because I know the gaps in his lines better than his own captains do." She tilted her head. "In exchange, when the door opens—you go through, and I go through, and what's yours stays yours. I don't take. I *taste*. One taste of the whole thing, Freya. One true taste of Aurelan, on the far side, freely given. That's all. That's the price."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then tonight I follow you to the pier anyway, and I watch you get taken, and afterward I sit with Corvane while he works on you, and I eat Aurelan out of your dying head piece by piece, and I promise you—" her voice gentled into something almost tender, which was worse than any threat— "I will enjoy it. I will enjoy every crumb. I am not a cruel woman, taster, but I am a hungry one, and hungry women are honest. This is me being honest. This is the best bargain you will be offered in your short remaining life."

The bells rang vespers across the water, bronze rolling over bronze, and somewhere beyond the rooftops the alarm bells answered, still hunting, still sweeping. Freya thought of Ives on the customs steps, turning his face away to give Freya the crowd's blindness. Thought of Odell swinging silent in the cold. Thought of his grandmother's hands breaking bread over salt, and the words she'd made him say, and the door of black salt that wanted a payment he had not yet counted.

Every road out of this city led through someone's hunger. Corvane's hunger was erasure. Sable's was appetite. The door's—he was beginning to guess—was himself.

Sable watched him weigh it, and had the grace not to rush him, and that patience frightened him more than anything else she'd said.

"The cordon tightens at full dark," she said at last. "After that, even I can't thread it. So: yes or no, taster. And know that I'll know if you lie, because I've tasted you, and liars taste different."

Freya looked out past her, at the harbor going dark, at the black line of the seawall where a door of salt waited under the tide. Somewhere behind him a child was crying in a cart. Somewhere ahead of him a man was waiting for a pyre. And here, offering her hand like a dance partner, stood the hungriest thing in Vellmark, smiling.

"Yes," he said.

Her smile broke open like dawn. "Then hold my arm, love," said Sable, the memory-thief, drawing him into the shadows. "We're going to walk right through the middle of the hunt—and when we reach the door, you're going to give me a country."
