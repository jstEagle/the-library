## 20. The Last Thing He Keeps

The door of black salt drank him by degrees.

Freya had imagined it would be like drowning, or like the proving-doses Corvane had fed him in the tasting room—fire first, then numbness. It was neither. It was subtraction. One thing, then another, then another, each lifted out of him with a gentleness that was worse than violence, the way a pickpocket's fingers are worse than a knife because you feel yourself being *courted*.

He knelt at the threshold with his palms flat on the salt, and he paid.

His mother's face went first. He had thought he would fight for that one—had braced himself against losing her the way he'd once braced against the bitter almond note of cyanide in a consignment of marchpane. But when it left, it left whole and complete, and what remained was not grief but a shape where grief had been: the knowledge that he had loved someone, without the someone. A coat hook with no coat. He heard himself make a sound, low and animal, and the salt drank that too, or seemed to.

*The first winter,* the door asked, in no voice at all, only pressure, only tide.

He gave it. The winter he was six, when ice had sealed the gutters of Vellmark's low quarter into ropes of glass and his grandmother had melted snow for tea over a forbidden flame, humming an Aurelan lullaby with the words sanded down to nonsense so the neighbors wouldn't hear the shape of them. Gone. He kept the fact of cold and lost the memory of it, which was a strange amputation—to be someone who knew he had been cold once, and could not remember shivering.

*Your name.*

That one he held.

He held it the way he had learned to hold poison on his tongue without swallowing: a discipline of the whole body, jaw locked, breath shallow, every muscle agreeing at once. *Freya.* Not yet. There were things still standing between him and the door's far side, and he needed to be someone while he walked through them.

Behind him, Sable screamed.

Not the scream of a thief thwarted—the sound had gone past craft into something rawer. He did not turn. Turning was a luxury; he could feel how few luxuries he had left, counted them the way Odell used to count coins before paying out, touching each one. He had perhaps forty memories remaining. Perhaps fewer. He could not tell anymore which ones were gone and which were merely dark, and that uncertainty was its own horror: he kept reaching for things and finding smooth walls.

"You can't—" Sable's voice cracked against the harbor stones. "You don't even know what you're carrying! I've tasted it, boy, I've tasted *pieces* of it—you're not a man, you're a *country wearing a man's skin*, and you'd rather be hollow than—"

"Than give it to you." His own voice surprised him. It came out level. Taster's training: never let your face tell the kitchen what the broth told you. "Yes."

"It doesn't want willingness. It wants *worth*. And I am worth more than you—I've eaten a hundred lives, I've carried kings' childhoods, I could set Aurelan back on the water like setting a crown on a head—"

"The door says otherwise."

And the door did. Freya could feel its judgment the way he felt arsenic under the tongue—not a taste exactly, but a certainty. All through the long night of paying, Sable had clawed at the threshold, had pressed her grey-eyed hunger against the salt, and the door had simply declined her, politely, absolutely, the way a locked house declines a stranger. She had stolen memories from pawnshop madmen and confessing priests; she had taken his summer kitchen from him without permission or pain. But this door opened only for the willing, and she had never in her life willed anything but taking.

She understood it too. That was why she had stopped bargaining and started screaming.

"Then take me *with* you," she said, and here her voice changed, dropped its silk, became almost human, which was the most frightening transformation of all. "Freya. Whatever's left of you. I have pieces of your country inside me—your summer kitchen, your grandmother's market-day, a wedding I took off some dead woman in a confessional. You'll arrive there missing everything, and I *have* things. Let me carry them home. That's all I want now. Not to own it. To bring it back."

It was a beautiful lie. It might even have been half true—that was Sable's genius; she built her lies around real beams. But Freya had spent three years tasting truth for a living, and he knew the flavor of a lie wrapped around a wish. If she crossed, she would not carry anything home. She would eat the country again, bite by bite, until it fit inside her the way stolen goods fit inside a fence's warehouse: owned, silent, dead.

"I'm sorry," he said, and found he meant it, which cost him one more memory—he gave up the face of the laundress he'd sold luck-charms to, the only kindness he could name from his three safe years, and used the empty space to hold his sorrow for Sable. Grief for the thief who wanted to be a pilgrim and couldn't stop being a thief.

The salt beneath his palms softened.

*What do you keep?*

Here it was, then. The last question. The door had taken his mother, his winters, his city streets, the smell of the palace kitchens at dawn, Corvane's face (and that loss was mercy, though he hadn't asked for it), Ives reciting borders to a jeering crowd, Odell's silence hanging in the air like a struck bell. Nearly blank. Nearly nothing. A man-shaped room swept clean for someone else's furniture.

But the door required a keeper, and a keeper required one thing held fast.

He reached into what remained of himself and chose.

Not his grandmother's face—that was already gone; he had checked twice, horrified, and found only the bread-salt ritual floating free of her, orphaned, and he had wept without knowing for whom. Not Ives, though leaving Ives on the block was a debt he would carry into whatever came next. Not even his own name, because a name belonged to the man he was erasing, and that man had done his work.

He chose the Oath.

The words his grandmother had spoken over bread and salt when he was small enough to sit on her knee, the words he had copied into a hidden notebook in a hand disguised as merchant's cipher, the words the whole erased kingdom waited behind a wall of black salt to hear:

*I remember the fields that were, the hearths that burned, the names unwritten. While I keep them, they live. While they live, the door stands open. Salt remembers. Bread restores. I am the key and the keeping.*

He spoke them aloud, there on his knees, in a voice scraped down to its frame, and as he spoke the door stopped taking.

Everything else in him went quiet—no, not quiet, *reorganized*, the way a kitchen reorganizes around the one dish that must never fail. The Oath sat in the center of him, vast and warm and specific, and around it the emptiness arranged itself like flour waiting for water. He was almost nothing. But the almost-nothing had a spine now.

Sable saw it happen. He heard her go silent, heard the wet sound of her breathing change, and when he finally turned—for he could afford to turn now; there was nothing left in his face worth stealing—she was on her knees at the edge of the salt, and her grey eyes were wide, and for one unguarded moment she looked less like a predator than like a starving person outside a lit window.

"One word," she whispered. "One word of it. Give me one word of your country and I'll let you walk. I swear it on whatever I've got left to swear on."

"That's just it," Freya said gently. "You'd swear it true. And then you'd break it by morning. I've watched you work, Sable. Hunger isn't your flaw. It's your whole architecture."

Her face closed like a door. "Then go be holy somewhere else," she said, and spat, and then ruined her own fury by adding, very quietly, in the voice of the girl she might have been before someone taught her to eat people: "Was it worth it?"

He considered the question with what remained of himself. Somewhere in the emptied rooms of him, a shape moved—the shape of a grandmother's hands kneading dough, of salt pinched and scattered, of a country that existed now only because one stubborn taster had refused to swallow his forgetting.

"I'll tell you from the other side," he said.

The tide was turning. He felt it come up through the harbor wall, felt the black salt quicken like a sleeping animal scenting rain, and he stood—stiffly, an old man's rise from an old man's knees—and put his hand flat against the door.

It opened the way morning opens: gradually, then all at once. No light burst forth. What came through was better than light—it was *air*. Air that smelled of cut hay and woodsmoke and the particular green sweetness of a river valley in late August, air from a country that had been holding its breath for twenty years behind a membrane of salt and law and forgetting. The breath of Aurelan moved over his face, and the Oath inside him answered it like a tuning fork, singing in his hollow chest, *here, here, here.*

Sable lunged.

He had known she would. He stepped through anyway, unhurried, because the door was closing at the speed of tide and tide does not negotiate. Her fingers caught his sleeve—his mother's mended sleeve, the last object of his old life—and the salt closed on her wrist with a crack like ice going out, and she fell back screaming, not in pain but in refusal, screaming the way the drowned scream at a surface they cannot reach, and the last thing Freya heard from the world of Vellmark was her voice tearing itself hoarse on the words:

"*Come back! Come back and I'll give them back to you—all of them, every one, I'll—*"

The door shut.

* * *

Silence. Then birdsong.

Freya stood in a field of barley so tall it brushed his chest, under a sky the color of a freshly scoured pan, and the air moved across the grain in long silver strokes, wave after wave, the whole valley breathing. Hills rose to the east in soft blue folds. Somewhere below, out of sight, a river said its single syllable over stones. Smoke rose from chimneys—dozens of chimneys—a town, a real town, tucked into the riverbend like a secret kept so long it had become a habit.

Aurelan.

Alive. Unmapped. Waiting.

He tried to say it aloud and discovered he had almost no words left—only the Oath, whole and glowing, and a handful of practical fragments, tongs and spoons and *careful, it's hot*, the kitchen-words that lived deeper than memory. So instead of speaking he did the only thing the Oath asked of a keeper newly arrived: he knelt in the barley, took a pinch of salt from the small cloth bag at his belt—his grandmother's bag, though he no longer knew whose it was, only that it must not be dropped—and scattered it on the earth.

Salt remembers. Bread restores.

Far off in the town, a bell began to ring. Then a second bell, answering. Then voices, thin with distance, rising—one voice, then ten, then the whole river-valley waking to a sound it had not made in twenty years, a sound with no name in any surviving language except the one Freya carried in his chest.

They were calling the key home.

And down the hill, through the barley, already moving toward him, came figures—and among them, unmistakably, someone walking with the rolling gait of a woman who has spent her life beside an oven, and Freya's hollow heart, holding nothing but an oath, somehow recognized her anyway.
