## 22. August and the Oath

The kitchens of Aurelan smelled of bread and salt and woodsmoke, as they had every morning for six years, and every morning Freya Thackeray stood at the long stone bench before dawn and taught a child to die a little.

Not all at once. Never all at once. That was the first lesson, and he spoke it now as he had spoken it a thousand times, his voice low so it would not wake the ovens' tenders who slept in the loft above.

"Again," he said. "Smaller this time."

The girl on the stool before him was nine years old, sharp-elbowed and solemn, with her mother's dark brows and her father's stubborn chin. Her name was Wren, and she had been born under a roof that remembered itself, which made her luckier than anyone Freya had known at her age—and no safer, because the gift did not care where you were born. It only cared that you carried it.

Wren lifted the spoon with both hands, the way he had shown her, cupping the bowl of it like something that might startle. On the spoon: a mouthful of broth, pale gold, still steaming. In the broth: three drops of tincture from the small brown bottle Freya kept locked in a box beneath the floorboards, the same bottle whose twin had once sat in a locked drawer in a palace across the sea, in a country that had forgotten its own name.

She drank.

Freya watched her face the way he had once been watched—by a man named Corvane, in another life, in a kitchen with colder light. He watched for the flicker at the corner of the eye, the small involuntary tightening of the throat, the body's honest report of what it had been given. Wren's jaw set. Her eyes watered. She swallowed anyway, and then she sat very straight and very still, hands flat on her knees, doing the breathing he had taught her—four counts in, seven held, eight out—until the wave passed through her and broke somewhere below her ribs and was gone.

"Well?" he said.

"Bitter," she said. "Like pennies. Like—" She frowned, hunting for it. "Like the harbor wall in winter."

"The arsenic?"

"And the nightshade." She tapped her tongue against her teeth. "Both. You doubled it."

"I doubled it." Freya took the spoon from her and set it down. "And you're angry."

"I'm *fine*."

"You're fine because we started when you were six, with broth so weak your grandmother could have drunk it blindfolded, and because we have gone slowly, slowly, slowly, one grain at a time, for three years." He crouched so their eyes were level. The kitchen was warm around them; the first loaves were just going in, and the smell of them folded over everything else like a blanket. "Anger is part of it, Wren. Anger is the body saying *this is not fair*, and it isn't fair, and you are allowed to say so. But the anger is not what keeps you alive. What keeps you alive is patience, and measuring, and never—not once, not ever—letting anyone see how much you can bear."

She looked at him with those too-old eyes children of the gift always seemed to have, and asked the question he had been waiting three years for her to ask.

"Why do we have to hide it here? There's no hunt here. Nobody burns anybody."

Freya was quiet for a moment. Outside the kitchen's high window, August was coming up over Aurelan—the real Aurelan, the living one, its terraced gardens going gold above the harbor, its bells beginning the morning rounds, its streets filling with the smell of fish and honey and hot stone. Six years, and he still sometimes caught himself bracing, some animal part of him waiting for the criers, the boots, the pyre-smoke on the wind. Six years, and the bracing came less often. But it came.

"Because hiding is a skill," he said at last, "and skills keep people alive longer than safety does. Because countries forget. Because—" He stopped. He almost never told this part. But she was nine, and sharp, and she would find out anyway, the way he had found out, and better from him than from a stranger's cruelty. "Because I knew a man who thought being safe meant he could stop hiding. And when the forgetting came to his country, he had spent so long being safe that he'd forgotten how to be careful. They gave him a choice, in the end. He chose the fire rather than give up other people's names."

Wren absorbed this the way children absorb terrible things—completely, and without flinching, and then moving on to the practical question underneath.

"Was he gifted too?"

"He was a mapmaker." Freya stood, knees cracking, and went to fetch the second thing—the other lesson, the one that mattered more, though he had learned it later and at a price he was still paying. "Sit. Today we do words instead of poison. Poison can wait until your stomach's settled."

* * *

The Oath-words were best taught with bread and salt.

That was how his grandmother had taught him, in a kitchen that existed now only inside the Oath itself, and so Freya taught Wren the same way: a heel of yesterday's loaf, a dish of coarse grey salt from the Saltgate flats, the two of them sitting cross-legged on the flour-dusted floor while the ovens ticked and breathed behind them.

"Hold the bread," he said. "Don't eat yet. Say it after me, and say it *meaning* it, or don't say it at all. The words know the difference."

"They can't know anything. They're words."

"So is a door just wood," Freya said, "until someone remembers what it's for."

He began, and she followed, stumbling, correcting, stumbling again:

*Bread of the field and salt of the gate,*
*I hold the country in my mouth.*
*While I remember, it is not lost.*
*What I remember, I must carry.*
*What I carry, I must keep.*

The old words, in the old tongue, the tongue that had come back to Aurelan's streets the way grass comes back through cobblestones—slowly, stubbornly, wherever anyone bothered to speak it aloud. When Freya had first walked through the black salt door, hollowed out and nameless, carrying nothing but the Oath like a coat someone had left on his shoulders, these words had been nearly all he owned. He had spoken them to the empty road beyond the gate. And the road had answered.

That was the thing about the door that no one had understood—not Corvane, not Sable, not even Ives with all his maps—that Freya had only learned by walking through it: the door did not lead *out* of the erasure. It led *through* it, into the country as it had been kept, whole and waiting, in the one vault no thief and no king could breach. Memory itself. Aurelan had gone on existing in the place where it had been hidden all along—in him, and in the Oath—and when he stepped through, the country had unfolded around him like a letter finally opened. The terraced gardens. The grey-eyed fishermen. The bells. All of it preserved, all of it starving for someone to live in it again.

And the people—the people had come through after, in ones and twos over the years, guided by rumor and longing and the strange gravity of a country remembering itself. Not many. Enough. Enough to rebuild the ovens and relight the beacons and teach the children the words again.

"Wren," Freya said, when they had said it through four times and the bread was going stale between them, "eat it now. Both together. Bread and salt. That's the whole rite—it was never more than that. A mouthful, and the meaning behind it."

She ate. She chewed with her eyes closed, which she did when she was taking something seriously, and when she opened them she said, "It tastes different after the words."

"It always does."

"Did it taste different for you? The first time?"

Freya looked at the salt dish. Grey salt, harvested from the flats below the black door, which still stood in the harbor wall, sealed now, its work done—or paused, or sleeping; no one knew which, and no one dared knock.

"My grandmother taught me those words over bread and salt," he said. "In a kitchen much like this one. I didn't understand what I was holding until everything else was taken from me. Don't be like me, Wren. Understand it now. Every kindness you do with your gift leaves a trail, so be kind carefully. Every memory you keep is worth more than gold, so keep them generously. And if you ever find a door that asks you what you love—" His voice caught, just slightly, on the edge of an old grief. "Answer it honestly. But count the cost first."

* * *

Across the sea, in a city that had once erased a country from its maps and was now, quietly, without proclamation, beginning to un-erase it, a rumor moved through the low quarter like water finding a crack.

They said the cartographer had survived the pyre.

They said it in pawnshops, over tankards, in the confessional of Saint Verity's where a priest with kind eyes and a suspicious memory heard everything and repeated nothing. They said the rope had burned through, or the guards had been bribed, or the fire itself had refused him—which was the version the gifted liked best, though it was the least likely. They said a grey-eyed man had been seen in the border towns selling hand-drawn charts of roads that appeared on no official map, roads with names like *Aurelan Road* and *Saltgate Way*, and that when the magistrates seized the charts and burned them, the names somehow got into people's mouths anyway, and mouths were harder to burn.

Corvane heard the rumors too. He was older now, and harder, and he had stopped hunting the gifted openly—not from mercy, but because the Gift-Hunt had become an embarrassment, a ledger of ash and orphans that even the crown had grown tired of reading. He kept his own counsel in his rooms above the taster-master's quarters, where he still trained boys to sip poison without flinching, and where, on the shelf beside his bed, in a place no servant was permitted to dust, he kept a single object: a scrap of charred notebook paper, saved from a fire years ago, bearing half a line of handwriting in a dead language.

*...the salt remembers what the sea forgives...*

He did not know why he kept it. He told himself it was evidence. He told himself a great many things.

On certain nights—equinoxes, mostly—he would take the scrap out and hold it near the candle, not close enough to burn, and try to feel what the boy must have felt: that a country could live inside a person, that memory could be a load-bearing wall, that erasure was never as total as the erasers believed. He had been born in Aurelan. He had chosen to forget, and the choosing had worked, mostly, the way amputation works—you lived, and you learned not to reach for the missing thing. But some nights he reached anyway.

Some nights he whispered the fragment aloud, testing it against his teeth like a coin of doubtful metal.

*The salt remembers what the sea forgives.*

It meant nothing to him. It meant nothing, and he kept it anyway, and that was the closest thing to hope that Corvane ever allowed himself.

* * *

And in a fishing village on the far coast, where the charts with impossible road-names changed hands most often, there lived a woman who mended sails and asked no questions and paid in exact coin, and who was known to the village children as a soft touch and to the village adults as someone best not discussed.

Sable had come ashore five years ago with no memory of how she'd gotten there, which was not unusual for her—memory-thieves leak, it's the occupational hazard—and with a hunger in her that no pawnshop prize had ever satisfied since. She had tasted a summer kitchen once, stolen from a boy in a palace corridor, and it had ruined her. Everything since had been gruel. She had spent years chasing the scent of that kitchen through confessionals and attics and the dreams of old women, and the scent had led her, at last, to the coast, to the charts, to the rumor of a door of black salt that opened only for the willing.

She had gone to the harbor wall at low tide. She had found the door. She had pressed her palms against it until her fingers bled, and the door had stayed shut, and something in the salt had looked back at her—not with anger, which she could have handled, but with pity, which she could not.

Now she mended sails. Now she bought charts from smugglers and read the road-names aloud to herself at night like prayers in a language she was teaching herself to want honestly. Whether the door would ever open for a woman who had spent a lifetime taking what she should have been given—whether wanting could be learned late, the way immunity is learned drop by drop—she did not know.

But she kept the charts. And she practiced saying the names.

* * *

August again, in Aurelan. The harvest festival, the third one Wren would remember clearly, and the kitchens of the rebuilt hall ran hot and loud from dawn, every oven lit, every surface crowded with the food of a country relearning its own recipes—honey-bread twisted into gate-shapes, salt-fish baked in vine leaves, the thin bitter broth called *keeper's soup* that no one ate for pleasure and everyone ate once a year, because it was the taste of what had been carried through.

At midday, when the hall was full and the bells were ringing the hour, Freya stood at the head of the children's table with a spoon in his hand and a bowl before him, and the noise died down the way it always did, the way a tide goes out.

"You know the rite," he said. "Bread and salt. Words and meaning. One mouthful, and the country in it."

The children said the Oath together, dozens of young voices in the old tongue, some fluent, some stumbling, all of it rising into the rafters where the smoke-blackened beams—salvaged, some of them, from the original hall, from before—seemed to lean in and listen.

Then each child came forward in turn, and Freya fed them a spoonful of keeper's soup with his own hand, as his grandmother had once fed him, and looked into each face as he did it, and kept each face, the way he kept everything now: deliberately, generously, knowing the cost of keeping and paying it gladly.

Last of all came Wren, who took her spoonful without flinching—of course she did; he'd trained her—and who lingered after the others had run off toward the festival noise.

"There's a ship in the harbor," she said. "Came in this morning. The sailors are telling stories in the taverns. About a grey-eyed man. A mapmaker." She watched Freya's face carefully, the way he had taught her to watch a dish. "They say he draws roads that aren't on any map. They say one of the roads ends at our door."

Freya set down the spoon. For a long moment he said nothing, and the kitchen sounds rose around them—ovens, voices, the sea beyond the walls—and in that moment he felt the whole shape of his life settle into place like a key turning: the poison and the patience, the loss and the keeping, the boy who had once catalogued a dead country in a hidden notebook, standing now in a living one, feeding its future one mouthful at a time.

"Finish your soup," he said, and his voice was not entirely steady. "Then fetch your cloak. We're going to the harbor."

"To meet him?"

"To find out," Freya said, "if the sea has forgiven us both."
