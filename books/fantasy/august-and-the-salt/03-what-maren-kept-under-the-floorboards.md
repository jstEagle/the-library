## 3. What Maren Kept Under the Floorboards

The well water had told me one thing for certain: someone was writing in salt, and they were not writing to me.

I spent two days convincing myself otherwise. I laid the crystals out on slate in Maren's back room and copied their shapes into my field book until my hand ached—loops like riverbends, strokes like fence lines, a character that recurred eleven times and looked, uncomfortably, like the boundary stones themselves drawn small enough to hold between finger and thumb. It was not any script I knew. But it *was* cartography. I felt that in the same place a sailor feels weather coming: the shapes described edges, approaches, a way through something. A way *in*.

Which meant somewhere out there was a way out, and something on the other side of it had begun to knock.

Maren would know what to do with that knowledge. That was the trouble. Maren Okafor knew what to do with everything—smuggled brandy, stolen deeds, orphaned boys—and her answers always came with a price folded inside them like a razor in bread. So before I brought her anything, I wanted to bring her proof. And proof, I reasoned, might be found in the one place in our house I had never been permitted to search.

Under the floorboards of her room.

* * *

I want it recorded, even if only in the privacy of my own skull, that I did not go to those floorboards lightly. I went because the salt-script had used the shape of the boundary stones, and because Maren had once said—drunk, three winters ago, laughing at some joke I hadn't been part of—that she'd walked the northern ridge "when it was still honest." Because she kept a knife under her pillow and a second knife in the flour jar and had never once explained why a smuggler in a valley with no customs officers needed two knives. Because loyalty, I was beginning to learn, is not the same as honesty, and I had spent twenty years mistaking one for the other.

Her room smelled of tallow and pipe smoke and, beneath that, faintly, of the river—the same green-dark smell the well water had carried. The boards were pine, grey with age, except for four near the eastern wall where the grain ran wrong. Too straight. Too evenly spaced. I'd have missed them if I hadn't spent my life reading ground that didn't want to be read.

The pry bar was in the shed. I told myself I was only going to look. I told myself a great many things that evening; the floorboards heard all of them and gave up their secrets anyway.

Beneath the fourth board lay an oilcloth packet, tied with waxed cord gone brittle as old cheese rind. Inside: papers. Dozens of them, soft-cornered, handled often once and never since. Route-notes. I knew the genre intimately—I'd been raised on it, raised *as* it, in a sense, since Maren taught me to draw my first contour line by tracing the veins of her hand. These were hers from long ago, in a younger, harder hand than the one that wrote my supply lists now.

They were notes of a road I had never mapped.

The paper itself was wrong first. Not Vessian stock—not ours at all—but heavy, cream-colored, watermarked with a device I didn't recognize: a tower with its top broken off. The ink had faded unevenly, as though parts of it had been written in something other than ink. And the geography—

I sat down on the cold boards with the pages spread around me like a fallen hand of cards, and my breath went strange in my chest.

There was a valley north of the ridge. Not the Salt Valley—a different one, wider, terraced, threaded with aqueducts drawn in confident double-stroke. There were towns with names I half-remembered from lullabies Maren claimed she'd invented: *Vessia* written along the top margin of the largest sheet in letters that had been traced over so many times they'd nearly cut through the paper. There were roads, bridges, a lake shaped like a cupped palm, and every feature annotated in Maren's shorthand—*soft crossing here*, *guard rotation*, *the child must not cry*.

*The child must not cry.*

I read that note four times. Around me the house ticked and settled. Somewhere outside a dog began barking at nothing, which dogs in our village had been doing a great deal lately, and stopped as abruptly as if a hand had closed over its muzzle.

The last sheet was different from the rest. Smaller. Folded into eighths, worn soft as cloth. When I opened it, a pressed sprig of some herb fell out—silvery, scentless, dead past any revival—and I saw that the sheet bore no map at all but a single line of that same salt-script I'd spent two days copying from the well water. Written in Maren's hand. Dated, in our calendar, twenty years ago almost to the month.

She had answered them before. Or they had spoken to her before. Either way, the conversation had started long before the well water came to me, and she had never said a word.

That was when the floor creaked behind me, and the lamp in the hall went up, and Maren Okafor's voice came down the stairs like a dropped stone.

"Juniper."

Not a question. Not my name, really—an indictment wearing my name.

* * *

She stood in the doorway with the lamp held high, and I watched her take in the scene the way she took in a room she suspected of containing soldiers: doorways first, exits second, then faces. The open boards. The papers fanned around me. My face, which I could not arrange into innocence fast enough.

"You went through my floor."

"You went through a country," I said. It came out sharper than I meant it, and I didn't take it back.

She set the lamp on the washstand, slowly, and came into the room, and crouched down across from me among her own scattered secrets. Up close I could see what twenty years had done to her hands—the burn scar from the Kettle Row fire, the missing half-finger she'd always blamed on a winch. She gathered the papers without looking at them. She knew them by touch. That frightened me more than anything else in the room.

"How much did you read?"

"All of it."

"Then you read too fast." She squared the stack against her knee. "And you understood none of it."

"There's a country north of the ridge." My voice cracked on *ridge*, traitor that it was. "There are roads. There's a whole—Maren, there's a whole *place* over there and you've *been* there, you've crossed, and you let me spend my life mapping a border you already knew the far side of—"

"Keep your voice down." It wasn't a shout. It was worse—a whisper with iron in it, the voice she'd used on men with knives, and it worked on me exactly as it worked on them. I shut my mouth. The dog outside had started again, or another one had; the night was full of dogs uneasy at nothing.

"I was a courier," she said at last. "Twenty-two years ago. Before your time, before this house, before I got respectable." The word *respectable* came out with a self-mocking twist, because we both knew what she was. "A client hired me to carry a package north across the stones. Paid half up front, which should have been my first warning. Clients who pay half up front are buying silence, not delivery."

"What was the package?"

She was quiet long enough that the lamp flame leaned and righted itself twice.

"A child," she said.

The word landed in the room and stayed there. I thought of the route-note—*the child must not cry*—and understood suddenly, horribly, that it had not been a smuggling instruction about noise. It had been a mother's note. No. Not quite that either. Something older and stranger.

"The client," Maren went on, in the flat tone of a woman walking a plank, "was not a person. Or not only one. I met them at the boundary stones at moonset, and I will tell you honestly, Juniper, that I have smuggled for magistrates and murderers and one archbishop, and I have never been as afraid as I was shaking that man's hand—except he wasn't a man, his shadow fell the wrong direction, and when he spoke the words arrived a half-second after his mouth moved, like thunder after lightning. He paid me in coin that was warm. He told me where to cross. He told me the child would sleep the whole way and remember nothing, and the child did, and doesn't."

My skin had gone cold. "Where is the child now?"

Maren looked at me. Just looked. And twenty years rearranged themselves around that look like furniture in a darkened room becoming visible when the lamp is finally carried in.

"No," I said.

"You slept the whole way," she said gently. "You didn't cry once. I've never decided whether that was magic or whether you were just a good baby."

The floor seemed to tilt. I put a hand flat on the boards to steady myself against a house I had lived in my entire remembered life, and the boards were ordinary pine, and nothing anywhere was ordinary anymore. "Vessia," I managed. "The watermark. The tower. That's Vessia. That's where I'm from."

"That's where you're *from*," she agreed. "It isn't where you're *of*. You're of the Salt Valley. I made sure of it. I fed you our food, taught you our songs, buried your real name so deep even you couldn't dig it up. Every map you ever drew, I watched you draw it, and every year you drew the border a little more confidently, and I told myself that was the point. That was the whole point. A boy who maps the seal can't be a crack in it."

"But the client never paid," I said. The phrase from the chapter of her life she was telling me, the debt she'd named. "You said a client who never paid."

"He paid half." Her jaw tightened. "Half up front, and the promise of the rest on return. When I came back south with you in the basket, the crossing place was gone. Not hidden—*gone*. The stones had moved. The road I'd walked north wasn't there anymore, and neither was the country at the end of it, and when I asked after Vessia in the river towns, people looked at me the way you look at someone naming a dead relative kindly. There was no such country. There had never been such a country. Except on my papers, and in my arms, and—" she touched her own sternum briefly, "—here."

"And the other half of the payment?"

"Never came. Debts to gods don't come due the way debts to men do. They come due sideways." She stood, joints cracking, and carried the papers to the hearth in the main room, and I followed her because following her was the oldest habit I had.

"Maren. The well water. The salt—it's writing. It's the same script as your last page. Something is answering prayers in the valley, the seal is thinning, and you *knew*—you had to have known it might come to this someday—"

"I knew it might." She knelt at the hearth. "Knowing and being able to do anything about it are different countries, and I never found the road between them."

"Then don't burn them." I heard my own voice go high and young and hated it. "Those are the only maps of the far side that exist. If the seal fails—if the door opens—we'll need them, we'll need every line—"

"That is *exactly* why they burn." She struck the flint, and the kindling caught, and she held the first sheet over the flame without letting it go yet, so I could see the terraced valley, the cupped-palm lake, the traced-and-retraced name, all of it beginning to curl amber at the edges. "Listen to me, Juniper, because I'll only say this once. Everything I did, I did to make you invisible. Invisible to the Inquisition, invisible to whatever paid me in warm coin, invisible to the whole rotten miracle of it. Papers like these aren't maps. They're addresses. They're an invitation with your name pre-written on it. The moment anyone learns they exist, you stop being a cartographer and start being a key."

"I'm already a key," I said. "The well water told me so. Whatever's writing in the salt knows I can read it. Burning your notes won't unwrite me."

"It might buy you time."

"Time for what? For the Inquisition to arrive first?" I grabbed her wrist—not the burning one, the other—and she let me, which she never let anyone do. "Serah Vell came asking about pre-Fall Vessia. There *is* an Inquisition remnant, Maren, and they're asking questions I can't answer without these papers, and you're standing there feeding the only answers to a fire."

For a moment she wavered. I saw it—saw the smuggler in her run the arithmetic she was born running: what the cargo was worth, what the risk was, what the buyer would pay. Twenty years of habit fighting twenty years of fear.

Then she pulled free of my grip and fed the sheet to the flames.

One by one. She didn't tear them, didn't crumple them, didn't rush. She gave each page to the fire individually, the way you'd lower a body into the ground, and she watched each one go, and I stood in the doorway of the room where I'd grown up and felt something in my chest come apart along a seam I hadn't known was there. It wasn't the loss of the maps. It was the understanding that she had chosen, twenty years ago and again tonight, to protect me by deciding things *for* me—by making me safe the way you make a thing safe in a vault, which is to say by making it a prisoner.

"Loyalty," I said, when the last corner of the last page had gone to ash, "means secrets. Is that it? That's the lesson?"

She didn't turn around. The firelight moved on her face and made her look, for just a moment, like the woman in the route-notes—younger, harder, carrying a sleeping child across a border that no longer existed.

"Loyalty means secrets," she agreed quietly. "And mine may cost lives. I know. I've done the sums every night for twenty years, and they've never once come out clean."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat in my own room with the shutter open to the cold and my field book on my knees, and I copied out from memory everything I could recall of the burned sheets—the aqueducts, the lake, the terraces—knowing even as I wrote that memory is the least reliable ink there is, that I was already falsifying Vessia simply by remembering it imperfectly. Halfway through the lake, my pen stopped.

Because I realized what had been nagging me since the hearth. The last sheet—the one with the salt-script, dated twenty years ago—had not burned like the others. I had watched carefully. Paper curls and blackens and lifts; that sheet had lain flat in the flames a beat too long, and for a moment, as it went, the script on it had seemed to *brighten*, the way embers answer a bellows.

As if it had been read.

Outside, down the lane, the dogs had gone silent all at once, every one of them, the whole village holding its breath. And in the well at the end of the street—too far to see, close enough to feel—something began, very softly, to write.
