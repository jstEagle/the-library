## 6. Maren's Debt

The night Vessia died, Maren Okafor was drinking bad plum brandy in a border tavern called the Lame Goose, and she remembered it afterward as the last hour of her life in which she had been an honest woman.

Honest was a generous word for what she'd been. She was twenty-six years old, a smuggler with two forged passports and one real knife, waiting on a client who was late. The tavern smelled of tallow smoke and wet wool, and outside the window the rain came down in that fine, salt-heavy drizzle the border country was known for—rain that left white crusts on the sills by morning, rain that tasted faintly of tears if you were foolish enough to open your mouth to the sky.

Her client was a scholar from the capital, a thin nervous man named Aldous Ren who had paid half up front for something simple: passage across the boundary stones into Vessia, three days' travel, and passage back out again. He wanted soil samples, he said. He wanted to see the old shrines. He wanted, he kept saying, *to look at the dragon-fire while there was still some to look at*, and Maren had thought nothing of it then, because everyone talked about the dragon-fire that autumn. Everyone could feel it going out, the way you feel a fever breaking in someone you love—the heat still there but wrong, thinner, retreating toward the bone.

She drank her brandy and watched the door, and she did not know that she was waiting not for a client but for the rest of her life.

* * *

Aldous Ren arrived past midnight, soaked through, carrying a canvas satchel against his chest the way a man carries an infant or a wound. He didn't apologize for being late. He sat down across from her, put the satchel on the table between them like a hostage, and said, "They've started answering."

"Who has?"

"The gods." He said it quietly, the way you say a diagnosis. "Outside the valley. The prayers are getting through now. The seal is—" His hands made a shape in the air, cupped, then collapsing inward. "It's failing. The fire is almost gone. And when it goes, everything the seal held back comes through, and everything the seal protected—" He stopped. He looked at his hands. "I have a daughter," he said, as though this explained something, and perhaps it did.

Maren had smuggled silk, opium, fugitives, forged relics, and once, memorably, a live lynching victim rolled in a carpet. She knew every kind of cargo and every kind of fear. But she had never seen a man carry fear the way Aldous Ren carried it—not like weight but like light, leaking out of him at every seam.

"You want me to take you into Vessia tonight," she said. "Through a border that's about to stop existing."

"I want you to take my daughter."

The satchel stayed on the table. The rain ticked against the glass. Somewhere behind the bar, the tapman was arguing with his wife about money, their voices rising and falling like weather.

"She's four years old," Ren said. "Her name is Juniper. I know how it sounds. Her mother chose it." A terrible small smile crossed his face and drowned. "Maren Okafor. Yes, I know your name. I've asked after you for a month. They say you're the only runner who still knows the old route under the boundary stones, the one the Inquisition never mapped because the man who mapped it died drunk in a ditch. They say you're good. They say you're expensive. They say you don't ask questions."

"They say right on all three counts."

"Then here is the question I need you to answer instead." He leaned forward, and up close she could see that his eyes were bloodshot all the way to the iris, that he hadn't slept in days, maybe weeks. "Can you get a child across a dying border? Because I can pay you enough to retire, and none of it will matter if the answer is no."

Maren looked at the satchel. Then at the door. Then at the rain, silvering down past the lamplight, and somewhere beyond it, past the hills and the stones and the iron brazier burning on its hilltop shrine, the country of Vessia lay in its last sleep, and its people lay in theirs, and none of them—not one soul in that whole soft green country—knew yet what was coming for them at dawn.

"Yes," she heard herself say. "But not tonight. Tonight we prepare. Tonight you tell me everything, and I mean *everything*, because tomorrow I'm carrying your whole life through hell, and I don't work blind."

So he told her. All of it. And that was the last hour of her honest life, because everything she learned that night, she would spend twenty years keeping from a boy who deserved better, and she would do it anyway, and she would call it love.

* * *

Juniper Sandoval was four years old and furious about the dark.

He rode in a wicker basket padded with sheepskin, strapped to Maren's chest, and he had opinions about the arrangement which he expressed at intervals throughout the first day with a clarity and stamina that would have impressed a parliament. By the second day he had discovered that the woman carrying him would answer questions, and so the questions began, and they did not stop.

"Why are we walking?"

"Because horses can't go where we're going."

"Why can't horses go where we're going?"

"Because the ground forgets it's ground."

He considered this. Above them the pines dripped. The old smuggler's road ran along a streambed that wasn't on any map worth trusting, a seam in the hills where the world's stitching showed, and Maren walked it from memory, counting landmarks the way other people counted rosaries: the split boulder shaped like a kneeling monk, the lightning-struck fir, the spring that ran cold and then, without changing course, ran warm.

"Are you my mama?"

"No."

"Where's my mama?"

"In Vessia."

"Where's Vessia?"

Ahead of her, Aldous Ren stumbled on a wet stone and caught himself against the rockface. He had insisted on coming as far as the boundary itself. He had insisted, in fact, against every argument Maren could mount, and she understood why and hated it: he wanted to walk his daughter to the edge of the world and hand her over at the very last possible inch.

"Vessia," Maren said carefully, "is home."

"Is it nice?"

"It's the nicest place there ever was."

That was true, and it was the cruelest thing she said on the whole journey. Vessia had been the nicest place there ever was: orchards terraced down hillsides like green amphitheaters, villages with names like lullabies—Sorrelmere, Anvet, the twin towns of High and Low Candlewick—shrine-fires burning blue-gold on every hilltop, and over it all the patient amber glow of the great dragon-fire at the valley's heart, the flame that had burned since before anyone's grandmother's grandmother, the flame that held the old hungry gods on the far side of the world's skin the way a held breath holds a scream.

And the people of Vessia had known, that final autumn, that the breath was running out. That was the worst part, the part Maren would carry longest. They had *known*. There had been time for farewells. The whole country had spent its last month saying goodbye to itself—mending fences that would never be sat on, planting orchards for children who would never eat the fruit, writing letters to relatives abroad that read, in retrospect, like wills. Aldous Ren had spent his month finding a smuggler. His wife, Sefa, had spent hers packing a basket with a wool coat two sizes too large, a carved wooden horse, and a bundle of letters, and pressing her forehead to her daughter's hair, and letting go.

Maren had watched her do it. She had stood in a farmhouse kitchen smelling of woodsmoke and bruised apples, and Sefa Sandoval had kissed her child's curls and handed her over with hands that did not shake until after, and then shook so hard she had to sit down, and Maren had thought: *I will remember this woman's face for the rest of my life,* and she had been right, and she had wished with her whole heart to be wrong.

"Why does the lady cry?" Juniper asked, from the basket, on the morning they left.

"Grown-ups leak," Maren said. "Don't worry. It stops."

It didn't. Not for twenty years, it wouldn't.

* * *

The boundary stones stood in a ring on a bald hilltop, twelve of them, older than any nation, each carved with a word in a language that predated language—or so the scholars claimed; Maren figured they were just rocks that had gotten lucky. Around the ring the air did something wrong. It thickened. Sound went strange, arriving a half-beat late, like thunder after lightning stretched into forever. And through the middle of the ring, faint as breath on glass, hung the shimmer of the seal itself—a curtain of nothing, a wall made of the absence of magic, and beyond it, the ordinary gray hills of the empire, and safety, of a kind.

Beyond it also, Maren knew, was the trap. Because the seal worked both ways. What it kept out, it also kept in—and what it erased, it erased completely. Anything that crossed out of Vessia would be leaving a country that, within days, would no longer exist. Not conquered. Not destroyed. *Erased.* Every map amended, every record rewritten, every memory softened and slid loose, until Vessia was a rumor of a rumor, a hole in the world shaped like a homeland. The Inquisition called it mercy. The gods it imprisoned had written the terms long ago, and the terms were absolute: a country that sheltered the seal's anchor could not survive the seal's failure, and its fate was to be unwritten rather than devoured.

Aldous Ren stood at the edge of the ring with his daughter in his arms, and the shimmer lit his face with a light like the inside of a shell.

"Maren." His voice came out scraped. "If anything happens—if the crossing hurts her—you turn around. You bring her back. Promise me."

"If the crossing hurts her, the alternative is worse."

"Promise me anyway."

"I promise."

He knelt in the wet grass. He held his son—no, his daughter, she corrected herself, the name mattered, the name was the one thing they could give her that couldn't be unwritten—he held little Juniper Sandoval and spoke to her in low Vessian, too fast for Maren to follow, words that were clearly rehearsed and clearly falling apart anyway. The child patted his cheek with one small mittened hand and said something that made him laugh, a broken bright sound, and then he stood up so quickly it was like watching a man decide to jump.

"Go," he said. "Now. Before I'm a coward about it."

Maren took the child. The basket settled against her chest, warm and heavy and alive. Juniper looked back over Maren's shoulder and waved, the way children wave, without any understanding whatsoever that waving can be a funeral rite.

They walked into the shimmer together.

Crossing the seal felt, to Maren, like being born. Pressure, darkness, a roaring silence, the sensation of being turned inside out gently and then not gently at all. She heard the child scream—one raw note of pure animal outrage—and felt small fists hammering her ribs, and then the pressure released and they were through, stumbling onto imperial grass under imperial stars, and the scream cut off mid-note.

Not because the child stopped crying. Because the sound had stopped *reaching* them. Maren spun. On the far side of the shimmer, Aldous Ren stood with his mouth open, shouting something she could not hear, would never hear, his voice sealed away with his country. The shimmer rippled between them like the surface of deep water. She saw him press his hand flat against the nothing. She pressed her own hand flat against her side of it, palm to palm through a wall of law, and watched him say, clearly, silently, in the lip-reading silence of the damned:

*Keep her invisible.*

Then the light changed. Dawn was coming up over Vessia, gold and rose and unbearably beautiful, and beneath that beauty, far off at the valley's heart, the great dragon-fire guttered—and as it guttered, the world behind the shimmer began, softly, like frost forming in reverse, to unwrite itself.

Maren stood there for six hours. She would always claim it was two. The child slept against her chest, exhausted, safe, already forgetting. And Maren watched a country die the gentle way: orchards fading like breath off glass, hilltops smoothing into anonymous hills, the shrine-fires going out one by one until the last blue-gold point of light winked away and there was only grass, only stone, only a landscape with a hole in it shaped exactly like everything that had been taken.

By noon there was no Vessia. By noon there was no reason for a woman to be standing on a hillside crying, and so she stopped, because smugglers learn to cry on schedule, and she turned south toward the Salt Valley with a sleeping child and a debt she hadn't agreed to and would spend the rest of her life paying.

* * *

The Salt Valley took them in the way valleys take in weather: indifferently, and then completely.

It was a pocket of the world the old gods had never touched, sealed so long ago that its people had forgotten there was anything to be sealed against. No magic worked inside it. No god answered in it. Its borders were marked by standing stones its inhabitants polished annually and understood not at all, and its people were practical, pious in a small domestic way, and deeply suspicious of strangers—which meant Maren needed a story, and fast, and the best stories are mostly true.

She gave them one. A sister dead of fever, a brother-in-law lost on the roads, a nephew orphaned and delivered to her care. She rented a room above a chandler's shop in the town of Brackish Ford, and she hung out a sign that said CARTOGRAPHY AND SURVEYING, because maps were legal, maps were dull, and maps were the perfect cover for a woman who needed to know every road, ford, and cellar in a valley she intended never to leave.

The lie about the nephew lasted eleven minutes. The chandler's wife took one look at the way Maren carried the child—not like cargo, not like duty, but like the last load out of a burning house—and said, "Yours, then?" and Maren, who had lied to magistrates and customs officers and once to a saint, heard herself say, "Yes."

It was the first lie she told him, and it was the kindest, and it was the beginning of all the others.

Because invisibility, she learned, was not a single act but a practice. You did not make a child invisible once, at a border, in a moment. You made him invisible daily, in ten thousand small erasures. You gave him a surname that fit the valley's parish records. You burned the letters from Vessia—read them first, forty times, until you knew Sefa Sandoval's handwriting better than your own, until you could recite the last line of the last letter in your sleep (*tell her the orchard trees are blooming early this year, tell her we planted one for her*)—and then you fed the pages to the stove and told yourself it was protection. You never sang the lullabies you'd heard on the far side of the stones. You corrected his accent when it drifted toward vowels that belonged nowhere on any living map. You taught him to love the valley, truly love it, its salt flats and its stubborn stones and its well water that tasted of nothing but itself—because a boy who loved where he lived would never wonder what he'd lost.

And he loved it. That was the miracle and the punishment both. Juniper Sandoval grew up salt-crusty and map-mad, a quiet ferocious child who drew the world the way other boys threw stones, and he loved the valley with the whole uncomplicated heart that Maren had engineered for him, and every year that heart grew bigger and more certain, and every year Maren's guilt grew a floor deeper beneath it.

He was seven when he found the first letter. She'd missed one—a scrap tucked in the lining of the wooden horse, Sefa's hand, three lines about blooming trees. He brought it to her at the kitchen table, puzzled, holding it upside down. "This isn't valley script," he said. "Where's it from?"

Maren looked at the handwriting of a dead woman in a country that had never existed, and she smiled the smile she'd practiced for three years, and she said: "Old stock paper. Probably came wrapped around a shipment. Toss it in the stove, love."

He did. He was seven. He trusted her absolutely.

She watched the page curl and blacken, and she thought: *someday he'll hate me for this,* and she thought: *let him live long enough to.*

* * *

Twenty years is a long time to keep a country secret.

Maren got good at it, the way you get good at carrying water with a cracked jug—by never stopping moving. She built a life above the chandler's shop, then a house on Lantern Row, then a reputation as the valley's finest mapmaker-of-record, the woman whose surveys the town council actually trusted. She watched the boy become a young man who outmapped her by fifteen and forgave her slowness with the patience of the truly gifted. She watched him grow careful with instruments and careless with himself, watch him stand on ridgelines in weather that would kill a goat, and every time, every single time, her heart performed the same arithmetic: *he is the last of them, he is the last of Vessia, and he doesn't even know what he's the last of.*

She kept the wooden horse. She kept the coat two sizes too large, folded in oilcloth under the floorboards with the things she could not burn and could not show: a marriage ribbon, a handful of Vessian coins already going blank in her palm, and one letter she had never managed to feed to the stove, the last one, the one that ended *tell her we planted one for her.*

Some nights she took them out. Some nights she sat on the floor with the boards lifted and the oilcloth open and let herself grieve on schedule, quietly, the way smugglers do everything. And some nights—more lately, this last year, as the well water began tasting faintly wrong and the boundary stones hummed at dusk and her old surveying instincts, the ones that had kept her alive through three empires, whispered *the seal is thinning, Maren, the fire is going out, the bill is coming due*—some nights she sat in the dark and understood that the twenty-year lie had an expiration date, and that when it ended, she would lose either the boy's trust or the boy, and she did not know which frightened her more.

Tonight was one of those nights. She had the floorboards up. She had the letter in her hand. And she was so absorbed in the old ritual—reading, grieving, resealing—that she did not hear the front door, did not hear his boots on the stairs, did not know anything at all was wrong until the lamp at the top of the steps went dark, and the house went silent, and she understood that Juniper Sandoval was standing in her doorway, looking down at the open floor, at the oilcloth, at the evidence of a country that did not exist—

—and that in his hand, catching the last of the streetlight, was a page of salt-script he had carried all the way back from the northern wells, and that whatever question he had climbed these stairs to ask her, the floorboards had just answered it first.
