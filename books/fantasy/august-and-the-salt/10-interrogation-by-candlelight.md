## 10. Interrogation by Candlelight

The cell they gave her in the garrison at Salt Hollow had once been a tithe-office, and it still smelled of ink and tallow and the particular dust that collects in rooms where men count other men's losses. Serah Vell had requisitioned it herself, which was the first small dishonesty of the night. The second sat rolled on the table before her, tied with plain twine, smelling faintly of juniper smoke and river-damp: the map.

She did not untie it yet.

A single candle burned between her and the empty chair across the table—the chair she had ordered brought in for the cartographer, though he was not in it, would not be in it tonight. She had told Captain Dross that she preferred to interrogate alone; prisoners talked more freely without an audience, she'd said, and Dross, who believed cruelty worked best in numbers, had shrugged and gone to drink himself stupid on the garrison's thin wine. That was the third dishonesty, and the largest: there would be no interrogation. There was only this—her, a candle, a map that might be a lie, and twenty years of a silence she had helped to enforce.

Serah took off her gloves. Her hands looked older without them, the knuckles scarred from a lifetime of gripping things: reins, pikes, ledgers, the edges of burning documents. She flexed them once, twice, the way her mother had taught her before prayer, back when prayer was still legal, back when Vessia—

Back when Vessia existed.

She untied the twine.

* * *

The map unrolled like something breathing.

It was good work. That was the first thing she felt, before suspicion, before hope—a cartographer's honest admiration rising in her chest like warmth near a hearth. The linework was confident without being arrogant. Rivers ran with the right hesitancy where they braided through marshland. The hills were hatched in that old style, the pre-Fall style, each stroke laid down as if the draftsman had walked every contour himself. And along the eastern margin, where any honest map of the Salt Valley should simply stop—should trail off into blank vellum and the words *here the world declines to continue*—this map continued.

Vessia spread across the last third of the parchment in ink so fine it seemed less drawn than remembered.

Serah's breath went shallow. She made herself look at it the way she'd been trained to look at everything: as an officer of the Inquisition's remnant, as a woman whose commission still read, in letters no one had rescinded, *to seek out and suppress all vestiges of the fallen countries.* She looked for errors. Deliberate ones. A forged map betrayed itself in the details—in a village placed on the wrong bank, a road that bent where roads don't bend, a shrine given the wrong dedication.

She found none.

That frightened her more than any error would have. Because she knew these places. Not from maps—from *before*. She had been nine years old when the gods fell, ten when the erasure came through like a tide going out and never coming back, taking the country's name off every chart, every deed, every tongue that could be compelled by law or fear. But she had been born in Vessia. She had splashed in the Vessa Sera when it was still called by its true name. She had watched her grandmother light the little salt-lamps on the feast of the Undrowned, and she had wept without knowing why for twenty years afterward, grief with its object amputated, mourning a wound shaped exactly like a homeland.

And here it was. The Vessa Sera, bending south past Ondrel's Ford. The terraced vineyards above Callowmere. The shrine-road climbing toward the high pass where, her grandmother had sworn, a dragon-fire still burned in iron, holding the sky shut over all of them.

Either Juniper Sandoval had forged the most perfect counterfeit in the history of counterfeiting—or he had done the one thing the Inquisition had spent two decades swearing was impossible.

He had mapped the unmappable.

* * *

She had met him only twice, and both times she had watched him lie to her face with the serene competence of a man who has been invisible his whole life and finds invisibility comfortable.

The first time was in the market at Salt Hollow, three days after her arrival. She had approached him openly, shown her writ, asked—politely, almost gently—for any records of pre-Fall geography. He had blinked at her with those gray eyes and said, "Pre-Fall? You mean the old survey charts? Most of those rotted in the tithe-house flood, years back. I've got fragments. Nothing worth an officer's time." And he had smiled, and the smile had been warm, and she had believed him for nearly four hours, until she saw him at dusk on the northern ridge with his instruments out, measuring a stretch of empty hillside with the intensity of a man taking the pulse of something alive.

The second time was yesterday, when her soldiers had brought him in—not arrested, not yet; she had been careful about that—merely *invited* to answer questions regarding seditious materials. He had sat in the chair across from this very table, candlelight moving on his face, and lied again, beautifully, about a master map destroyed in a fire, about apprentices who scattered, about how a cartographer's work was mostly guesswork anyway, officer, mostly guesswork and wishful thinking.

But his hands had trembled. Not much. Just enough that the candle flame's reflection shivered in his eyes when he said the word *fire*, and Serah Vell, who had spent twenty years reading faces the way other people read scripture, had understood:

He wasn't lying because he was a coward.

He was lying because he was protecting something. Someone. Perhaps everyone.

Tonight, alone, she turned the map under the candlelight and let herself admit what she had known since the ridge: the boy was not the enemy. The boy was the door.

* * *

There was a knock. She stiffened, then relaxed—one knock, then silence. Corporal Ives, bringing what she'd asked for. "Leave it outside the door," she called. "You're relieved for the night."

"Yes, Officer."

Boots receding. Then the small sounds of the garrison settling: a dog somewhere, wind worrying the loose shutter of the guardhouse, the creak of the well-rope in the courtyard. She fetched the tray herself. Bread, hard cheese, a flask of wine, and—wrapped separately, at the bottom, beneath a napkin—the thing she had actually wanted: a stub of chalk-white salt crystal, harvested from the well water that half the valley now swore performed miracles.

She set it beside the map and looked at the two of them together. Evidence and miracle. Crime and prayer. In the Inquisition's doctrine they were the same thing, and always had been: the fallen countries were heresy given geography, and anything that remembered them was contraband.

Her whole life, Serah had been very good at believing the doctrine. It had structure. It had purpose. It had explained why she woke some nights tasting riverwater and hearing a language she was forbidden to speak. *You grieve a heresy,* the seminary masters had told her. *Grief is the devil's argument against order.* So she had buried the grief. She had risen through the ranks by being the most thorough suppressor of vestiges in the eastern command. She had burned libraries. She had stood in doorways while older men did worse than burning, and she had told herself it was mercy, that forgetting was a kind of anesthesia, that a people who could not remember their gods could not be damned by them.

Then the prayers started leaking through.

She had read the reports from Salt Hollow with the detachment of an auditor: a child's fever broken overnight; rain arriving on the feast-day schedule the valley hadn't observed in a generation; a widow's loom mending itself thread by thread while she slept. Small mercies. Old gods, small mercies—and the phrase had come to her unbidden, in her grandmother's voice, in the forbidden tongue, and she had sat at her desk in the garrison with her hands flat on the table and understood that the seal was failing, and that everything she had spent her life suppressing was coming back whether the Inquisition willed it or not.

The question was not whether the door opened.

The question was who stood beside it when it did.

* * *

She ate a little, without tasting anything, and then she did the thing she had been avoiding since sundown. She took out her own papers—the real ones, not the decoys she kept for Dross's inspection—and unrolled them beside the boy's map.

Her route-notes. Twenty years old, carried through four commands and two purges, hidden inside the binding of a doctrinal text no one ever opened because no one ever read doctrine anymore, they only enforced it. She had copied them from memory at seventeen, the night before her final oath, kneeling in a seminary cell with a stolen pen, writing down everything the erasure had left in her: the shape of the coast, the names of the rivers, the road her family had taken east when the sky began to close. Child's geography. A child's map of home, rendered in an adult's disciplined hand.

She laid it next to Juniper Sandoval's work.

Hers was a ghost. His was a body.

Where her memory had guessed, his instruments had measured. Where she had written *hills here, many, wooded*, he had drawn each ridge with its own name—names she had not heard spoken aloud since she was small enough to be carried, names that rose off the parchment and struck her in the sternum like cold water. *Carrow Fell. The Sisters. Under-Ondrel.* She pressed her palm flat against the map as if she could take its pulse, and the vellum was cool, and coolness was somehow the worst part, because it meant this was real, this was *documented*, this was a country that existed right now on the other side of a border drawn in dragon-fire, waiting.

"I'm sorry," she said aloud, to no one, to the map, to a grandmother thirty years dead in a country officially never born. "I'm sorry it took me so long."

The candle guttered. She let it.

* * *

Here was the truth she had carried up the mountain passes and into this valley like a stone in her boot, and which she had told absolutely no one, because telling it meant the end of everything she had built, rank and writ and purpose alike:

She did not serve the Inquisition anymore.

She had stopped serving it eleven months ago, in a archive cellar beneath the provincial seat, when she had been sent to catalog and destroy the last seized collection of pre-Fall materials. Among the ledgers and burnt hymnals she had found a prisoner's confession, decades old, from a smuggler interrogated at the border—the same border, the same season, the same operation. The smuggler had crossed into Vessia before the erasure finished setting. The smuggler had seen people alive in there. Had carried a child out. The confession named no names—it had been redacted, badly—but it described the crossing in detail: the boundary stones, the way reality thickened like ice near them, the moment of passage described as *stepping out of a held breath.*

And at the bottom of the page, in the interrogator's hand, a note that had ended Serah Vell's career of belief, though no one had thought to tell her career:

*Subject insists the country is not dead but sleeping. Recommend no further action. If they are sleeping, they may wake.*

They may wake.

She had sat in that cellar until dawn with the confession in her hands, and somewhere in the dark hours the doctrine had quietly died in her, the way a fire dies when the last log is consumed—not with a crash but with a cooling, a slow dimming, until what remained was ash and the shape of what had burned. She had reported the mission complete. She had requested transfer to the field. She had begun, with the patience of a woman defusing something, to steer her own assignments toward the Salt Valley, toward the ridge, toward the rumors of a cartographer whose instruments drifted near the old stones.

She had told herself she was hunting him. She had even believed it, some days.

But hunting and seeking are neighboring trades, and she had crossed from one to the other without noticing the exact step.

* * *

The candle was half gone when she finally spoke to the empty room, because saying it aloud—even alone, even here—made it load-bearing.

"I want the door open too."

The words hung in the tallow-smoke. She listened to them the way you listen to a confession you're giving yourself, checking for heresy, finding instead only relief, vast and terrifying, like a rope going slack after years of bearing weight.

Not conquest. Not restoration of the writ. Not the Inquisition's fantasy of marching into a resurrected country and putting it back under the heel of lawful silence. She wanted—Gods forgive her precision, but precision was all she had left—she wanted the Vessa Sera to run under its true name again. She wanted to stand on Carrow Fell and smell woodsmoke that belonged to someone. She wanted to find her grandmother's house, if houses survived being erased, and sit in whatever remained of the kitchen, and finish grieving properly, with the object restored to the wound.

And she wanted the boy to live.

That was the part that surprised her, when she finally let herself think it plainly. Juniper Sandoval was a means to an end, strategically speaking—the only living soul who could find a door no one else could see. But somewhere between the market and the ridge and yesterday's careful lies, he had stopped being a target and become something else: proof. Proof that the erasure had failed. Proof that a child carried out of a dying country could grow up whole on the wrong side of the border and spend his life drawing it back into existence stroke by stroke, whether he knew that was what he was doing or not. He was what happened when you refused to let a country die. He was the counter-argument to everything she had enforced, walking around in a worn coat with ink-stained fingers.

If the Inquisition's remnant got to him first—if Dross and his kind got their hands on the true map—they would not open the door. They would burn it. They would salt the ground around it and call it peace, and Serah would be required by her own signature to stand witness, and she would rather cut off her own commissioned hand.

So. The plan assembled itself in her mind with the clean inevitability of a military maneuver: get the true map before Dross did. Get the boy away from the villagers, who were already looking at him sideways, already weighing their safety against his, already deciding—as frightened people always decide—that one stranger's ruin was a fair price for the valley's quiet. Get to the border. Open the door. And pray—actually pray, first prayer in twenty years—that whatever waited on the other side was more merciful than the men behind her.

She would need him to trust her. That was the difficulty. Trust, from a man raised by a smuggler in a valley that survived by keeping secrets, would not be bought with rank or honesty alone. It would have to be bought the way all real trust is bought: with a cost paid visibly, in front of witnesses, in currency he valued.

She looked down at the map on her table—the beautiful, impossible, seditious forgery-or-truth—and understood, with a grim little laugh, what the currency would have to be.

* * *

Near midnight, she heard footsteps in the corridor and knew them before the door opened: Dross, heavy and wine-sweet, letting himself in without leave because rank was a habit he wore like armor over a smaller man.

"Any progress, Officer?" He nodded at the map, the candle, the untouched wine. "Has our cartographer confessed his sedition yet?"

"He will," Serah said. "These artistic types crumble slowly. I need another day. Two."

"Two days." Dross picked up the salt crystal from her table, turned it in his thick fingers, and grinned at her with the confidence of a man who has never once doubted the world he was handed. "For a piece of paper. You know what I think, Vell? I think paper's paper. Burn it, hang him if he argues, and the valley goes back to sleep. That's the old way. That's the *right* way."

"Of course," she said mildly. "I'll have my report ready."

He dropped the crystal back on the table—irrelevant to him, a pebble—and lumbered out. Serah sat listening to his footsteps fade, and her hands, scarred and steady, lay flat on either side of the map like a border's own stones.

Two days. Then she would do what she had spent twenty years punishing others for doing: she would choose the memory of a fallen country over the law that felled it. She would burn the decoy in front of God and Dross both, if it came to that. She would become, in the eyes of every man she'd served beside, a traitor—and in the eyes of one ink-stained cartographer, hopefully, something rarer.

An ally with nothing left to hide.

She pinched out the candle, and in the dark the map seemed to hold its faint luminescence a moment longer, like a country refusing, even on paper, to go out.

Outside, far to the north, over the ridge where the boundary stones stood their ancient watch, the sky flickered—once, softly, gold.

Dragon-fire. Guttering.

Whatever window was opening, it was opening faster than her two days.
