## 13. The Village That Forgot Its Name

Serah Vell had burned a great many things in her years wearing the grey coat of the Inquisition, and she had learned to read fire the way other officers read ledgers. A confession burned fast and bright, all crackle and self-importance. A map burned slowly, curling in on itself like something trying to hide its own edges. And a lie—she had learned this only lately, lately being these past few months since she'd stopped sleeping properly—did not burn at all. It simply changed hands.

She thought about that now as the three of them came down out of the hills toward the hamlet at dusk, because Juniper Sandoval was walking ahead of her with his decoy map sewn into his coat lining, and Maren Okafor walked behind him with a rope coiled over one shoulder like a farmer who had forgotten which animal she meant to tether, and Serah herself carried nothing but the memory of a country she had never seen.

That was the thing no one understood about the Inquisition's work, she wanted to tell them. They thought erasure was clean. They thought a writ signed by nine hands could unmake a place the way you unmake a table—saw off the legs, carry it out, sweep up the sawdust. But erasure left residue. It left grief with nowhere to set itself down. She had spent eleven years confiscating maps and burning route-notes and questioning old women about borders that officially did not exist, and in all that time she had never once found anyone who remembered Vessia.

She had found plenty who wept when she said the name.

The hamlet sat where the road gave up pretending and became a suggestion—a scatter of low stone houses with turf roofs going silver in the last light, smoke standing straight up from two chimneys in the windless evening air. There were perhaps thirty people visible, most of them drawing water or bringing in goats, and every single one of them stopped moving when the strangers appeared on the track. Not hostile, Serah noted. Not afraid, exactly. Something worse. They looked at the three travelers the way people look at a door they're certain they've never opened but dream about anyway.

"They know us," Maren said quietly. "Or they know what we are."

"They can't," Serah said. "This is twenty miles past the boundary stones. Nobody lives here. Officially."

"Officially." Maren's mouth did something that was almost a smile and entirely bitter. "You and your official things, officer."

Juniper said nothing. He hadn't said much in two days—not since the landscape had begun editing itself around their footsteps, not since he'd stood at the boundary stones turning his brass instruments over in his hands with an expression Serah had last seen on a man watching his house burn. She had expected him to be triumphant at the threshold. The invitation had been his; the drifting needles had been singing to him for chapters of his life before he knew how to listen. But triumph required a solid place to stand, and the ground here refused to hold still long enough for anyone to stand on it.

A woman came down the lane to meet them, sixty or so, broad through the shoulders, with flour dusted to her elbows as though she'd been interrupted mid-bread and would return to it when the world made sense again. Her eyes went from Juniper to Maren to Serah, and Serah watched her do the arithmetic everyone in these borderlands did: smuggler, boy, soldier. Her face tightened at the third term but did not close.

"You'll be needing shelter," the woman said. It wasn't a question. "Nights come strange out here. Best you're indoors for them."

"Strange how?" Serah asked.

The woman looked at her for a long moment. "Strange like someone walking around inside a room looking for furniture that isn't there anymore," she said. "Come on, then. I'm Odile. We'll feed you. It's what we do."

* * *

Odile's house smelled of woodsmoke, yeast, and underneath both, faintly, of salt—the same salt that had been crystallizing into handwriting in Juniper's well water, Serah realized, though here it was only a scent, a ghost of a ghost. The common room held a long table worn smooth by generations of elbows, and the walls were hung with objects that made Serah's professional heart stutter: fishing nets knotted in patterns she didn't recognize, a child's coat with buttons of green glass, and above the hearth—

Above the hearth hung a framed piece of embroidery, decades old, the thread faded from whatever color it had been to a soft anonymous grey. It depicted a city gate. Two towers, an arch, cobbles fanning out from the threshold. Beneath the gate, stitched small, was a word.

She couldn't read the word. She looked at it and looked at it and the letters slid apart like minnows, and she felt the specific vertigo she'd felt only once before, standing in the Inquisition's archive vault holding the last surviving fragment of a Vessian census while her superior told her to put it in the fire and she'd obeyed, she'd obeyed, she still woke at night with the smell of it in her nostrils.

"That's been there since my grandmother's time," Odile said, following her gaze. She set down a board of dark bread and hard cheese and didn't look at the embroidery. "Nobody remembers doing it. Nobody remembers what the letters say. My mother used to dust it every week and cry while she did, and when I asked her why, she said—" Odile paused, and Serah heard the effort of a woman reaching for a memory that had been amputated cleanly, without scar tissue. "She said she didn't know. She said it was just something her hands did."

"Grief without object," Juniper said softly.

Serah turned. The boy was staring at the embroidery too, and his instruments case was open on the table beside him, and his compass needle was trembling—not drifting, not pointing anywhere useful, but shivering, the way a dog shivers when thunder is still too far off to hear.

"What did you say?"

"My master taught me that maps record three things," Juniper said. "Land, distance, and feeling. He said feeling was the hardest layer to survey and the only one that never lies. This village has the feeling part. It's the land and the name they lost." He touched the compass needle gently and it stilled under his finger, or pretended to. "Whatever erased Vessia took the facts. It couldn't take the ache. The ache got written into everything it couldn't reach—embroidery, recipes, the way people stand facing east without knowing why."

"The way people stand facing east," Serah repeated.

Odile had gone very still by the hearth. "How did you know that?" she said. "Every evening. All of us. We stand in our doorways facing east until the light goes, and none of us knows what we're waiting for, and if you ask anyone they laugh at you, but we do it. Every evening. Like saying grace to a god whose name we—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "There's a word for it," she said finally. "I keep thinking there's a word for what we're mourning and it's right there and I can't—"

"Vessia," Serah said.

She said it plainly, without ceremony, the way she'd said it during interrogations for eleven years, watching faces. And Odile's face broke.

It was not dramatic. That was what Serah would remember later, writing her reports she no longer had anyone to report to: it was not dramatic at all. The woman simply began to cry—quietly, terribly, the way people cry at funerals for someone they loved more than they ever admitted aloud—and she pressed the back of her flour-white hand against her mouth and shook, and around the room the other villagers, drawn in from the lane by some instinct, all of them standing in the doorway facing east even now, began to weep too. Not one of them asked why. Not one of them said the name back. They wept the way a body flinches from a wound it cannot see, and Serah stood in the middle of it holding a name like a lit coal, and for the first time in her career she understood that she had not been an instrument of justice.

She had been an instrument of surgery. And the patient had never stopped bleeding.

* * *

Later, when the bread had been eaten and the crying had spent itself into the ordinary exhaustion of a village accustomed to weeping without knowing why, Odile took Serah aside to the small back room where Juniper was asleep—or pretending to sleep—curled around his instruments case like a child around a dog. Maren was outside somewhere, checking sightlines with a smuggler's thoroughness, muttering about approaches and escape routes as though the enemy were still the Inquisition rather than the sky.

"He shouldn't have said the name," Serah said quietly. It wasn't quite an apology. Nothing she'd done in the last year had been quite anything.

"He should have said it years ago," Odile answered. She was winding a length of candlewick between her fingers, and Serah noticed her hands: strong hands, baker's hands, and on the right wrist a scar, old and white, in the shape of—Serah leaned closer—a shape she recognized. A fragment. A shard of script, the same script that had crystallized in Juniper's well water, the same that had crawled up the boundary stones, burned into flesh so long ago the skin had grown around it like bark around a nail.

"Where did you get that mark?" Serah asked.

Odile stopped winding. For a moment Serah thought she wouldn't answer, that the whole village would fold shut like a fist. Then the woman pulled back her sleeve fully and studied the scar with the detached expression of someone examining a stranger's hand.

"I was born with it," she said. "We all have one, more or less. Look at Tam's shoulder next time his shirt slips. Look at the miller's daughter. Marks like writing, our mothers called them birth-blessings, and nobody ever questioned it because what else would you call them?" She let the sleeve fall. "But my grandmother, near the end, when her mind had gone soft and the erasure couldn't find purchase in her anymore—she grabbed my wrist once, hard enough to bruise, and she read it. Out loud. In a language I've never heard before or since. And then she looked at me with her eyes clear as bells and she said: *child, this is your name, and it comes from over the wall.*"

"Over the wall," Serah said.

"There's a wall," Odile said simply. "Everyone here knows there's a wall. Nobody knows where it is or what it keeps in or out. But when the nights come strange—and they've been coming stranger this season, officer, don't think we haven't noticed your kind walking the high passes—we hear it. Under the ground. Something vast turning over in its sleep, or waking up, or burning. And the marks itch. All of ours itch, all at once, same hour, every few weeks now instead of every few seasons." She met Serah's eyes. "So. You'll tell me what my grandmother read off my wrist. Or you'll tell me why you can't. Either way, you owe me a truth, because I fed you my bread."

From the corner, in the voice of a boy who had very much not been asleep: "Her name means *keeper of the eastern gate.*"

Serah turned. Juniper was sitting up, hair wild, eyes fever-bright, and in his hands he held his own fragment—the salt-script rubbing he'd taken from the shrine upstream, the scrap of crystallized prayer he'd been carrying folded against his chest like a relic. He laid it on the table beside Odile's scarred forearm, and Serah felt her breath stop in her throat, because the two fragments matched. Not approximately. Precisely. The broken edge of his rubbing fit the raised edge of the woman's scar the way two halves of a seal fit together, the way a key fits the lock it was cut for.

"That's impossible," Serah said. "You've never been here. She's never left. These border hamlets don't travel."

"No," Juniper agreed, and his voice had gone quiet and strange, the voice of a man reading terrain that hasn't decided to exist yet. "But names do. Names leak. Whatever wrote us—wrote the valley, wrote the seal, wrote the wall—it used the same ink on both sides." He looked at Odile. "Your family isn't from here. Your family is from *through*. Someone crossed, a generation or five ago, and brought the script with them in their blood, and the erasure couldn't scrub it out of living bodies any better than it could scrub the ache out of the embroidery."

Odile looked down at her wrist for a long time. When she spoke again, her voice had changed—steadier, harder, the voice of a woman deciding something.

"My grandmother also said a thing I've never told anyone," she said. "She said the wall has a door, and the door is under the stones, and the door is *hungry*, and one day a man would come carrying the rest of the sentence." She lifted her marked wrist, and pointed with her other hand at Juniper's fragment, at the missing half of the word, at the space between them where the meaning lived. "Is it you? Are you the rest of the sentence?"

Outside, far off across the darkening valley, something flared—briefly, horribly, a red-gold bloom behind the ridgeline like a second sunset arriving hours early, and every person in the hamlet stepped out into their doorways at once and faced east, and every mark on every wrist in the village began, audibly, to itch.

And Serah, standing in the doorway between them, felt the old scar on her own palm—the one she'd always told herself was a training accident—begin, for the first time in her life, to burn.
