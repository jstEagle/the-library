## 16. The Inquisition's Last Writ

Serah Vell had burned a great many things in her years of service. Writs, mostly—orders she disagreed with, confessions extracted under lamplight that she'd signed and then regretted signing, the occasional letter from her sister in the capital that said too much about how thin the walls of their family's respectability had grown. Burning was easy. Paper wanted to burn. It went up with a sound like a sigh, like something relieved to be finished.

She told herself this on the morning the reinforcements came down out of the northern pass, forty riders in ash-grey cloaks with the sun-wheel stitched at their shoulders, because she knew—she had known since the night she'd sat alone with Juniper Sandoval's forged map and admitted what she was—that before this was over, she would burn something that did not want to burn at all.

The column arrived the way the Inquisition always arrived: too early, too loud, and with the particular confidence of men who had never once been asked to explain themselves. Serah stood in the square of Salt Hollow as they rode in, boots striking cobbles still damp with well water, and watched Captain Dossin dismount his grey mare with the unhurried precision of a man who considered every village a room he had been given permission to rearrange.

"Vell." He did not salute. Rank between them was a formality neither of them believed in anymore; he outranked her by two grades and half a lifetime of better connections, and they both knew the writ he carried made her position decorative. "You've been here three weeks."

"Four."

"Four." He smiled the way frost forms—slowly, and all at once. "And no master map. No Vessian cartography recovered. One decoy, which you assure me is authentic, which you have not surrendered, and which you now tell me requires—" he consulted a paper he did not need to consult, "—*further verification*. I begin to wonder whose side of the sun-wheel you're standing on, Serah."

"The side that doesn't start fires it can't put out," she said. "Captain. The valley is delicate. The seal—"

"The seal is a story old women tell children so they'll come home before dark." He handed the reins to a trooper and looked past her, at the village: the low stone houses, the drying racks hung with salt-white fish, the well at the center with its iron lid chained shut since the wishes started coming true. "The writ says otherwise. The writ says there is a door, and a country behind it, and a map that shows the way, and that anyone withholding such materials is guilty of heresy against the Concord of Ash. Do you know what the penalty for that is, Vell? I'll remind you, since your memory seems selective. It's fire. It has always been fire. It is the one thing we have never once changed our minds about."

Behind him, his troopers were already moving through the square with the practiced economy of men who had done this in a dozen towns. Kicking open doors. Pulling families into the street. A woman screamed somewhere near the bakery—not in pain yet, only in the anticipation of it—and Serah felt the sound go through her like a fishhook.

"You were sent to find a map," Dossin said quietly, for her alone. "You were not sent to grow roots. I've read your reports. They're very good reports. Too good. You describe these people like a poet describes weather." He folded the paper away. "Tonight we take hostages. Three of them, to start. If the cartographer hasn't produced his master work by tomorrow's dusk, we take three more. And so on, until either the map appears or the village runs out of people worth taking. That is the last writ of the Inquisition, Serah, and you will stand beside me when I read it, because your name is on it too."

She had written her name on it. That was the worst part. Three days ago, when the courier bird had come down through the pass with orders she could have lost—could have claimed the hawk was taken by weather, could have let the message die in the reeds where she'd actually dropped it—she had instead retrieved it, dried it by the inn's fire, and signed beneath it in her careful clerk's hand, because a signature bought her another week inside Juniper Sandoval's confidence, and she had convinced herself that a week was what mattered. That trust was a currency she could spend without ever really paying.

Now the bill had come due, and it would be paid in other people's children.

"Where is the cartographer?" Dossin asked.

"Gone to ground," Serah said. It was even true; she hadn't seen Juniper since the night in the shrine, since the voice in the dragon-fire had spoken to him and not to her, since he'd come back down the mountain looking like a man who had been shown the back of the world. "He knows you're coming. They always know."

"They don't know anything," Dossin said. "They only believe. And belief, Vell, bends." He turned toward the well, toward the crowd that had begun to gather at the edges of the square the way water gathers at a crack, and raised his voice into the cold morning air. "People of Salt Hollow! By authority of the Concord of Ash and the writ of the Inquisition remnant, this settlement is placed under interdict!"

The word fell on the village like a stone into still water. Serah watched the ripples move outward: the baker's hands going still on his doorframe, the ferryman pulling his daughter behind him, old Widow Asche crossing herself with the wrong hand—the old way, the pre-Fall way, the gesture that had survived twenty years underground in a valley that thought itself immune to everything except forgetting.

"Your wells have been answering prayers," Dossin continued, and Serah marveled, distantly, at how he could make even truth sound like an accusation. "Your dead have been whispering. Your border stones have been *sweating salt*. These are not blessings. These are symptoms. Somewhere in this valley is a document—a map—which, if destroyed, will close whatever wound has been opened here. Produce it, and the Inquisition leaves you to your salt and your silence. Withhold it—" he paused, letting the square hold its breath, "—and we will help you remember what you owe."

* * *

They took the miller's twin daughters first.

Serah stood at the edge of the square and watched it happen, and made herself watch, because she had decided—if decided was the right word for a thing that happened to you the way rot happens to fruit—that she would not look away from a single consequence of her own signature. The girls were fourteen. They had flour on their sleeves. Their father put himself between them and the troopers and was struck across the mouth with a gauntleted hand, and the sound of it carried across the square like a branch snapping, and then the twins were bound and set on a wagon bed under guard, and the miller knelt in the road holding his broken lip and making a noise Serah had never heard a man make before, low and continuous, like a bell with its clapper removed.

The second hostage was the ferryman's son. The third was Widow Asche, taken not for leverage but for example—Dossin had seen the way she crossed herself, and something in his face had gone bright and hungry, the way Serah had once seen a colleague's face go bright over a heretic's pyre in the capital, before she'd stopped attending executions, before she'd started telling herself that paperwork was a kinder weapon than fire.

By noon the village had arranged itself around its grief the way a body arranges itself around a wound. Doors stayed shut. The well chain rattled in a wind that wasn't blowing. And at the inn, in the back room where the lamp burned low and the floorboards kept their secrets, Juniper Sandoval sat across from her with his hands flat on the table and his eyes gone the flat grey of the sky before snow.

"You signed it," he said.

"I signed it."

"Why?"

Because a signature buys time. Because I thought I could steer them. Because I have spent four months telling myself I am not what the sun-wheel says I am, and every day I sign my name to something that proves otherwise. She said none of it. What she said was: "Because if I hadn't, they'd have sent someone who wouldn't warn you. Someone who wouldn't have left you three days' lead. Someone who'd have taken the whole village hostage on the first morning instead of waiting for me to fail."

"And did you fail?" His voice was very quiet. "Or was this the plan? Take the children, force my hand, get the map—"

"There is no plan," Serah said, and heard the truth of it land in the room like a dropped glass. "Juniper. There hasn't been a plan since the night I told you what I serve. There's just—me, standing in a square, watching a man break a miller's face, knowing that I wrote the order that put the miller there. That's what I have. That's what I am. And tomorrow at dusk, if you haven't given them the map, Dossin takes three more, and he will keep taking until this valley is a graveyard of examples, because that is what the writ authorizes and I—" her voice caught, and she hated it, hated the small human crack in the middle of her clerk's precision, "—I put my name on it."

Juniper looked at her for a long moment. Then he reached beneath the table, into the satchel he never let out of arm's reach, and drew out a roll of vellum tied with waxed cord, and set it on the wood between them with a care that made her breath stop.

It was beautiful. Even unrolled only to its first third, it was beautiful—the coastlines of a country that officially had never existed rendered in ink fine as hair, the mountains of Vessia shaded in cross-hatching so dense it looked like smoke, rivers labeled in a script the Concord had spent twenty years burning out of every archive and every memory. Serah had spent her whole career learning to detect forgery, and if she had not known—if she had not sat across from this boy in this room while he confessed the truth of it—she would have sworn on the sun-wheel itself that this was the master map of Vessia.

"It's the decoy," Juniper said. "You know it's the decoy. I know you know. But Dossin doesn't, and more importantly, the village doesn't. And the village is what matters now." He pushed it across the table. "Give it to him. Tell him you extracted it from me. Tell him whatever makes it real. And then let him do what the Inquisition does with heretical documents."

Serah stared at the vellum. "Burn it."

"Burn it. Publicly. Where everyone can see." His jaw was tight, and she understood suddenly what this was costing him—weeks of work, ink and sleep and fear layered into every line, a lie built to be destroyed in front of the people who would believe its destruction meant their safety. "They need to see it die, Serah. They need to see the thing Dossin says is poisoning their wells go up in smoke, or they will spend the rest of their lives wondering what they might have traded for their children. Give them ashes. Ashes end arguments."

"And when Dossin discovers the seal isn't thinning? When the wishes keep coming true and the dead keep whispering and nothing changes?" She met his eyes. "He won't leave. He'll decide the map was false, that there's a true one still hidden, and he'll start again—with worse methods. You're not buying peace. You're buying days."

"I'm buying what I can afford," Juniper said. "Days are what I have."

* * *

At dusk, in the square of Salt Hollow, Serah Vell committed the performance of her life.

Dossin had the hostages displayed on the wagon bed, pale and silent, the miller's twins holding hands through their bindings. He had the village assembled at pike-point, forty troopers forming a corridor of ash-grey wool and oiled steel. He had a brazier lit at the square's center—an Inquisition habit, a stage prop, the promise of fire made visible—and he stood beside it with the writ in his hand and waited for Serah to bring him the world.

She walked the length of the corridor with the forged map under her arm and her face arranged into triumph, and she let herself feel it, because the feeling had to be real or the performance would fail: the cold clean satisfaction of the hunt concluded, the quarry run to ground, the heretic's work seized at last. She let Dossin see it in her eyes. She let the village see it too, and watched them understand what it meant—that the cartographer had broken, that the map was taken, that their children might come home.

"Found where?" Dossin asked, taking the vellum from her.

"Hollowed into the floor of his workroom," Serah said. Lies came easily now; that frightened her more than anything else in this long terrible season. "Beneath the boards. He'd wrapped it in oilcloth. He'd planned to run with it."

"And him?"

"Gone. Out the back, into the reed beds. My men are searching." Another lie, and behind her, unseen, the actual Juniper Sandoval was at that moment sitting in Maren Okafor's back room with the true map broken into fragments scattered among people who loved him, and Serah was the wall between those two facts and the man who would kill to collapse them.

Dossin unrolled a hand's width of the vellum, glanced at the coastline, at the impossible mountains, at the script the Concord had outlawed, and something moved through his face that Serah recognized from her years in the capital archives: the scholar's hunger, briefly, before the zealot closed over it like water closing over a stone. He held the map up so the village could see it.

"This," he called out, "is the wound in your valley. This is why your wells speak and your dead walk in your dreams. This is the door the fallen gods have been scratching at." He turned to the brazier. "The Inquisition does not negotiate with heresy. It does not preserve it, study it, or fear it. It burns it."

He fed the vellum into the fire.

Serah watched the decoy catch—the oilcloth sizing in the vellum took the flame beautifully, exactly as Juniper must have known it would—and she watched the fire climb up through the cross-hatched mountains, and she thought: *I have burned a great many things, and this is the first one that was a gift.* Some child in this square would sleep tonight because this paper was dying. Some father would get his daughters back. And none of them would ever know that the map was false, that the true one lived in pieces in their own houses—in the lining of the ferryman's coat, sewn into Widow Asche's quilt, rolled inside the miller's eldest son's flute—hidden by a boy who had trusted her, of all people, with the scattering of his life's work.

The shame arrived on schedule, punctual as a debt. She stood at attention beside the brazier with her hands clasped behind her back and let it move through her, wave after wave, while the village wept with relief and Dossin smiled his frost-forming smile and the forged coastline of a murdered country curled and blackened and rose into the darkening air as sparks.

"Good work, Vell," Dossin said quietly, watching the fire. "I misjudged you. You're still one of us after all."

*Yes,* she thought. *That is precisely what I needed you to believe.*

Aloud, she said: "The hostages, Captain. The writ promised their release."

"The writ promises what I say it promises." But he waved a hand, magnanimous in victory, and the troopers began cutting the twins loose, and the miller ran into the square with his ruined mouth and his arms out, and the sound the village made when the children came down off the wagon was the sound Serah would carry out of this valley if she carried nothing else—grief and gratitude tangled together, relief priced honestly, nothing free.

She turned to go, and Dossin caught her sleeve.

"One more thing." His voice had dropped, gone private, gone cold in a new way. "The fire's behaving strangely tonight. Have you noticed? The braziers. The lamps. They gutter—like something's breathing on them from underneath." His eyes were fixed on the brazier, where the last scraps of vellum had burned away but the flames themselves were doing something Serah had never seen fire do: leaning, all together, all at once, toward the north. Toward the boundary stones. Toward the shrine in the high hills where a dragon-fire guttered in its iron bowl.

"The seal," Dossin said softly, and for the first time since he'd ridden down out of the pass, Serah heard something in his voice that was not confidence. "It's not a story, is it, Vell. You knew. You've known all along what's really under this valley." He released her sleeve. "Find me the cartographer. Tonight. Because if that fire goes out—and I think you know, as I know, that it's going out—then the map won't matter anymore, and neither will mercy. Not mine. Not yours."

Above the square, the wind changed, and came down out of the north smelling of salt and hot metal, and every flame in Salt Hollow leaned toward it like grass bending before a storm.
