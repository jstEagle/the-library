## 1. The Thirteenth Turn

The kettle had a whistle like a dying goose, and Mara Galloway had long ago decided this was the gatehouse's one concession to personality. Everything else about the place was stone and silence: the squat round tower, the iron-banded door, the gate itself behind it—a slab of black rock set into the mountain pass like a tooth in a jaw, carved over with sigils that no one living could read and no one dead would explain.

Twelve years she had kept it. Twelve years of the same morning: light the stove, wind the clock, walk the circuit of the wall, check the gate, mark the ledger, eat, sleep, repeat. The King's clerks called her a Warden of the Marches. The villagers in Lowmere, eight miles down the valley, called her the Gate-Witch, which she suspected was less about magic and more about the fact that she once refused to sell a man eggs because he had insulted her goat.

The goat had been right to be insulted. He had a face like a disapproving bishop.

"Morning, Your Grace," she told the bishop-goat now, scraping frost from the water trough. He blinked at her with the serene contempt of all goats everywhere. Snow was coming down in fat, unhurried flakes, the kind that meant business, and the mountains on either side of the pass had gone the soft grey of unwashed wool.

It was, by her count, the eve of the moon's thirteenth turn.

She had not wanted to count it. But the ledger had a way of making you count. The old wardens' book—three hundred years of cramped handwriting, the last third of it in her predecessor Aldous Vey's increasingly shaky script—had a page near the front, ink gone brown as old blood, where some warden long dead had written:

*The moon makes twelve turns in a year, as is proper. When the reckoning falls so that a thirteenth turn must be counted before the year closes, the gate grows restless. Let the warden on watch mark it well: the thirteenth turn comes twice to no one. Be awake for it. Be sober for it. Be alone for it.*

*—and may whatever gods the old country kept have mercy on whoever is warden when it comes.*

Mara had read that entry perhaps a hundred times over twelve years, and every time she came away with the same two thoughts: first, that Aldous Vey had been a dramatic old man who enjoyed a good scare; and second, that she had never once seen him sober, so his opinion on the matter was compromised.

Still. She checked the gate twice that morning instead of once.

The gate was the same as ever: black stone, cold as a riverbed in January, its carvings worn to suggestions. Whatever it had once sealed—and the official histories, such as they were, said only *a border of no further interest*—it sealed it still. Nothing came through. Nothing went through. In twelve years the most exciting thing to happen at Thornfell was the winter a marten got into her flour.

She marked the ledger, ate bread and hard cheese, and told herself the prickling feeling at the back of her neck was just the weather.

* * *

By late afternoon the snow had thickened into a proper white hush, the kind that swallowed sound so completely that her own footsteps seemed rude. Mara did the evening circuit with a lantern, kicking icicles off the wall-walk out of habit, and paused—as she always paused—at the north corner where the stone was scorched in a pattern she had never examined too closely.

There was a scar in the masonry there. Old, deliberate, the size of a spread hand. Five lines radiating from a circle, like a child's drawing of a sun that had decided to be something else instead.

She knew it the way you know a freckle. She had never once wondered about it, in the way that you can go twelve years without wondering about a thing if the not-wondering is deep enough.

Her left hand, she noticed, ached. The one in the glove. The one she kept gloved even in summer, even sleeping, because under the wool there was a scar of her own, and she had made a private arrangement with herself long ago that she would think about it never.

"Evening," she told the scorched stone, because talking to goats was one thing but talking to walls was a habit she was not yet ready to admit to. "Behave yourself tonight."

The stone, characteristically, said nothing. But the ache in her hand said something, low and persistent, like a word on the tip of the tongue.

* * *

She was banking the stove when the knock came.

Three knocks. Small ones. The kind of knocks a fist the size of a pear makes.

Mara stood very still, poker in hand, and ran through the possibilities. Travelers did not come to Thornfell. That was rather the point of Thornfell. The pass beyond the gate led nowhere—officially nowhere, cartographically nowhere, a blank stretch of parchment where the mapmakers drew their little sea-monsters and left it at that. Eight miles down the valley, Lowmere. Beyond the gate, nothing. So whoever knocked at the gatehouse door on the eve of the thirteenth turn was either lost, mad, or sent.

She opened the door with the poker held like a sword, which would have been more impressive if the poker were not also a poker.

A child stood on her step.

A girl, six years old perhaps, seven at most, standing barefoot in three inches of snow as though snow were a rumor she had declined to believe. She wore a nightdress. A fine one, once—white linen gone grey with travel, hem scorched, a singed smell still clinging to it like a bad memory. Her hair was black and cut raggedly, someone in a hurry with a knife. Her lips were blue. She was shivering so hard her whole body rang with it, and she was looking up at Mara with an expression of such ferocious, furious hope that it stopped Mara's breath.

"You'll freeze," Mara said. "Get in. In, in—gods, your feet—"

The girl did not move. She said something instead.

It was a language. Mara knew that much immediately, the way you know a song from a country you've never visited. The sounds were round and old and deliberate, vowels held like held breath, and they should have been nothing to her—she spoke the King's Tongue and enough Lowmere dialect to haggle over eggs—and yet.

And yet she *half-understood*. That was the horror of it. The words went past her ears and something under her ears leaned in and caught them, like a dog hearing its name in a stranger's mouth.

*"—the door,"* the girl was saying, or something like *the door*, or like *the door* wearing a costume. *"I was told—the door, the warden, the thirteenth—"* and then a word Mara's mind flinched from, a word that slid off her understanding like a fish off wet stone, though her hand ached when it was said.

"Slowly," Mara said, and heard her own voice come out strange. "Slowly. I—"

*I understand you*, she almost said, in a language she did not know she knew, and bit it back so hard she tasted copper.

She got the girl inside by the simple method of picking her up, which the girl allowed with the dignity of a queen permitting a chair to be carried. Her feet were blocks of ice; Mara chafed them by the stove, wrapped them in wool, fed her broth spoonful by spoonful while the girl watched her with those ferocious eyes and did not stop shivering for a long time.

"Name?" Mara tried. "Your name?"

The girl pressed her lips together. Then, slowly, she raised her right hand, palm out, the way children do when they've been taught a thing is important.

Mara looked at the palm.

The sigil was burned there, old and white against the small brown skin: a circle, and five lines radiating out from it, like a sun that had decided to be something else instead.

The poker clattered out of Mara's other hand. The broth went cold. Outside, the snow kept falling on the pass, on the gate, on the scorched stone at the north corner, and inside the gatehouse the kettle began its dying-goose whistle and no one moved to stop it.

Because under her left glove, in the exact same arrangement, in the exact same hand-drawn lines, Mara Galloway had carried that mark for twelve years—carried it without wondering, carried it the way you carry a stone at the bottom of a well—and she had never, not once in twelve years, shown it to a living soul.

The girl looked at Mara's gloved left hand.

Then she looked up at Mara's face, and said—clearly, deliberately, in that round old language that Mara's bones understood and her mind refused—

*"Sister of the seal. I found you."*

The clock on the mantle, which had kept perfect time for twelve years, ticked twice and stopped.

Outside, the moon was rising over the pass, one night shy of its thirteenth turn, and it came up the color of a scar.
