## 2. What the Ledger Forbids

The ledger lived in the third drawer of the warden's desk, beneath a false bottom that had taken Mara two years and one broken fingernail to find, back when she was young enough to be curious and foolish enough to act on it.

She had not opened it since.

Twelve years of discipline, undone in a single night by a barefoot child who was currently asleep in the warden's chair with her boots—no boots, Mara corrected herself; the child owned nothing but the dress she stood up in and a look of terrible patience—tucked under her like a cat claiming territory by right of occupation.

Mara sat across from her at the desk, the ledger open between them, and told herself she was only checking. Confirming. The way you check a latch twice because the wind is howling, not because you're frightened.

The ledger itself was unremarkable: a fat volume bound in grey leather, its pages filled with her predecessor's cramped hand. Warden Aldous Vane had kept meticulous records. Turn counts. Weather. Ration inventories. The gate's hum, noted daily, in a column all its own, as though it were livestock.

*Hum steady. Hum low. Hum like a word half-said.*

It was the margins that were forbidden.

Mara had read them once, at twenty-three, and had gone to bed that night telling herself they were the ravings of an old man alone too long in the mountains. She had spent twelve years not thinking about them, which was a skill she'd honed to a fine edge, the way you stop hearing a clock.

Now she turned to the pages near the end, where Aldous's handwriting changed—tightened, went crooked, as if his hand had learned fear.

*The gate does not seal,* he had written. *I have been saying it wrong for thirty years. A seal keeps something in. This keeps nothing anywhere. It unmakes the keeping.*

Below that, pressed so hard the nib had torn the paper:

*Vessia is not behind the gate. Vessia is being eaten by it. Slowly. A memory at a time. It feeds on remembering—the country's, ours, anyone's. That is why no map holds it. That is why mothers here cannot tell their children the old songs without losing the tune midway. We are not guarding a door. We are feeding a mouth.*

Mara looked up.

The child was watching her. Not asleep at all, then. Dark eyes over the edge of the chair arm, unblinking, the way deer watch a lantern—not afraid exactly, but measuring how afraid they ought to be.

"You should be sleeping," Mara said.

The child said nothing, because the child said nothing in any language Mara could properly name, though the language she'd spoken on the doorstep last night had slid into Mara's ears like water into cracks, familiar in a way that made her scalp prickle. Half-understood. Half-*remembered*, some traitorous part of her whispered.

She looked back down at the page before the whisper could finish its sentence.

Aldous again, further along:

*I asked the gate what it eats. Foolish. You cannot ask a mouth. But I have counted, and on the thirteenth turn of the moon—the turn that comes once in a generation, the turn the first wardens swore would never come twice—it does not eat. On the thirteenth turn, it opens its eyes.*

*If you are reading this, warden, and the thirteenth turn has come: do not go out to it. Do not let anything through. And above all, do not—*

The sentence stopped. The rest of the margin was scraped clean, deliberately, the parchment fuzzed pale where someone had worked it with a knife. Below the scrape, in fresher ink, smaller, almost apologetic:

*I have decided you are better off not knowing. Forgive me. Some doors are kinder shut. — A.V.*

"Charming," Mara muttered. "Leave me a warning and eat the actual warning."

The child made a sound. It took Mara a moment to recognize it as a laugh—a small, dry, single-note thing, like a bird deciding against singing. But it was unmistakably a laugh, aimed with devastating precision at Mara's joke, and Mara felt something in her chest shift its weight, which was inconvenient, because she had plans for this child and none of them involved liking her.

* * *

The plan was simple. Plans should be simple; complexity was how people died in the mountains.

The smugglers' road began a quarter mile below the gatehouse, where the cliff folded inward around a seam of scree that didn't look passable and wasn't, unless you knew where to put your feet. Mara knew. She'd found it her first winter, following a fox, of all things, and had used it twice since—for supplies, once, and once to carry down a warden's body, which was the other thing wardens' roads were for.

She packed the child a satchel: bread, cheese, a flask of broth, a wool blanket rolled tight. Sturdy boots from the stores, three sizes too big, laced double. A compass she didn't need, because the road needed none, but because giving a traveler a compass felt like a blessing you could hold.

"The road goes down to Harrow Ford," she said, kneeling to check the bootlaces for the fourth time. "There's a tannery at the ford. Ask for a woman named Bess Corrigan. Tell her—" Mara paused. What did one tell Bess Corrigan? "Tell her the warden sent you. She owes the wardens a favor older than either of us."

The child watched her with that patient dark gaze.

"You'll be safe there," Mara said. "Safer than here. There's—" soup, she almost said, there's soup, there are other children, there is a whole world that doesn't hum at night—and heard how thin it sounded even inside her own head.

The child reached out and took Mara's gloved hand.

Not grabbed. Took—deliberately, both small hands wrapping around Mara's fingers, the way you hold something you've been told is breakable. And then the child spoke.

The sounds were the same ones from the doorstep, that sliding, crack-water language—but this time Mara's mind did the thing it had done last night, the thing she'd been refusing to examine. The sounds came apart into meaning, the way a knot comes apart when you find the one end.

"*Mara.*"

Her name. Except not the name everyone used—*Warden Galloway, Mara Galloway, the woman at Thornfell*—but something underneath it, older, rounder, pronounced with a softness at the back that made it a different word entirely.

"*Mara vesh Annelin. Thiriel o Vessa.*"

And Mara's mouth, without asking her permission, answered: "*Yes.* Yes, that's—that's my name. How do you know my—"

She stopped.

Because she had never known that name. She had been Mara Galloway since the orphanage at Cael Ford, since before, since always. No one had ever called her anything else. There was no room in her life for another name, no hollow where one had been removed—

Except there was. Now that the child had spoken it, Mara could feel the shape of the absence, the way your tongue finds the gap of a lost tooth. *Mara vesh Annelin.* A mother-name. A naming-line. Something given, something inherited, something that belonged to a place—

To a country that wasn't on any map.

"No," Mara said aloud, and pulled her hand back, and stood up too fast so that the desk corner caught her hip. "No. Whatever game this is, whatever you were taught, whoever sent you—I don't know that name. I've never known it. Take the satchel. Go down the road. Don't come back."

The child did not move. The child did not cry. The child simply sat in the warden's chair, three sizes too big for her and somehow too small, and looked at Mara with an expression no child should have had occasion to learn: the expression of someone watching a person they love walk into a wall and knowing the wall will win.

"I mean it," Mara said, and hated how much it sounded like she didn't.

The child lifted her hand. Opened her palm.

In the firelight, the sigil stood up off the skin like a brand gone silver with age—a circle crossed by seven lines, six radiating outward and one bent back upon itself, a shape that hurt slightly to look at, the way certain chords hurt to hear.

Mara's left hand, still gloved, began to ache.

She had hidden the scar for twelve years. Under the glove, under wraps in summer, under the flat lie of *a rope burn from the winch*. She had never shown it to anyone. She had never, in twelve years, allowed herself to look at it longer than the time it took to change the dressing.

She looked at it now—or rather, she looked at the child's palm, and her own palm answered, throbbing in time, twin bells struck together.

"It's mine too," Mara heard herself say. Her voice came out strange, flattened, like sound through a closed door. "Isn't it. That's why you came to my door. Out of every light in these mountains—you saw mine."

The child nodded, slow and grave.

And then she said, in that sliding language that Mara's blood understood better than her head ever would:

"*Thiriel. O vessa. Eremma.*"

*Home. Come home. It's time.*

The word *home* landed somewhere in Mara's chest and detonated quietly, the way old charges detonate, years after the war. She gripped the back of the chair. The ledger lay open between them, Aldous's scraped-away warning grinning up from the margin, and beyond the shuttered window the moon was rising—three nights shy of full, three nights shy of the thirteenth turn, already too bright, already humming.

The gate hummed. She could feel it through the floor, through her boots, through the scar on her hand. And for the first time in twelve years, Mara admitted to herself that the hum had a direction. It wasn't a sound filling the mountain.

It was a sound leaning toward the house.

Toward them.

"Three days," Mara said finally, and was appalled to hear that her voice had decided something without consulting her. "You stay three days. You sleep behind the bar, you eat what I eat, and you don't touch the gate. Then we talk about the road again."

The child's face did something complicated and luminous, and Mara turned away from it quickly, busying her hands with the kettle, because self-preservation, she had always believed, was mostly a matter of not looking directly at the things that would undo you.

Outside, the snow had stopped. The moon climbed over Thornfell, white and patient and one turn short of thirteen.

And far below, down the smugglers' road she had meant to send a child into exile by, something was climbing—slowly, without hurry, in no need of hurry—with footsteps that made no sound in the snow at all.
