## 3. Unminders

The fire had a sound. That was the first thing Ivet knew, before she knew her own name again: the fire had a sound like a thousand small mouths, and it was eating the books.

She was very small in the memory. Small enough that the shelves went up past the sky. The library of Vessia had been tall as a mountain and old as a grandmother, and it was burning, and everyone was screaming in a language that was hers, that was *hers*, that lived under her tongue like a second heartbeat.

*Run,* someone said.

Hands on her shoulders. A woman's hands, shaking. The woman had ash in her hair and blood at her collar and eyes like doors slamming shut, one after another after another, and she knelt down so their faces were level, which meant Ivet could see that she was crying without making any noise about it, the way important people cry.

*Ivet.* Her name, said like a key turning. *Listen to me. You are going to forget this. That is not a punishment. It is a mercy, and it is also a lie, because you will not forget, not all the way, not where it counts.*

The woman took Ivet's hand. Turned it over. Pressed something into the palm — hot, hot, *hot*, a brand shaped like a coiled serpent biting its own tail with a star caught in its mouth — and held it there while Ivet screamed, and did not let go, and said:

*This is your country. It is small enough to carry. When you find the door, show them this, and they will know you. And if they do not know you — if they have forgotten how to know you — then make them remember. That is the whole duty of a queen. She remembers for people who cannot.*

*What if I can't?*

*Then you come home anyway, and we will be ashamed of you together.* The woman smiled then, a terrible wonderful smile, and kissed her forehead, and it tasted like smoke. *I love you. That is the last thing. Hold onto that even when you lose everything else, because it is the only thing no Unminder can reach.*

Then — running. Snow. A road that smelled of iron. A man with kind hands lifting her onto a cart, saying words she half-heard: *the gate, the warden, the thirteenth turn, go now, go now—*

And then white. White like a page with nothing written on it.

Ivet woke in the gatehouse loft with her heart going like a rabbit's and Mara's blanket tangled around her ankles, and below her, through the floorboards, Mara's voice was rising and falling the way it did when she talked to herself, which was often, which Ivet had decided was because Mara had been alone so long that talking to herself was just talking to someone she hadn't met yet.

Ivet lay still and pressed her burned palm flat against her own chest, over the place where the promise lived.

*I remember,* she told the woman in the burning library, across all the white. *You said hold onto it. I'm holding.*

* * *

Three days later, horses came up the mountain road.

Ivet heard them first — she always heard things first, being a creature made mostly of listening — and she was at the window before Mara finished frowning at the fire. Two riders in grey. Not the grey of weather or work; the grey of *deliberate*, the grey of clothes that wanted to be forgotten the moment you looked away from them. Their faces were smooth in a way faces shouldn't be, like bread dough, like something that had been kneaded too long.

Mara went still in a way Ivet had learned to read. Stillness number four: danger, but from the front door.

"Stay upstairs," Mara said, and then, catching herself, said it again in the other language, the old one, the one that lived in Ivet's bones: *stay above, little ember.*

Ivet stayed above. Mostly. She lay flat on the loft floor with her eye to the gap between boards, which was not staying above in any way that mattered, but Mara hadn't specified, and Ivet had found that grown-ups left enormous loopholes if you were patient.

The knock came. Mara opened the door.

"Good morning," said the taller rider. His voice was pleasant the way a frozen lake is pleasant. "Lovely remote posting you have. Terrible roads. One wonders why anyone would choose them."

"One does," Mara said.

"We're looking for a child." He tilted his head, and Ivet felt the tilt in her teeth. "Small girl. Dark-haired. Possibly mute. Possibly not mute — reports differ, and reports are such slippery things." He smiled. "Have you seen anything you shouldn't have?"

And here is what Ivet saw, through the gap in the boards, and what she never forgot as long as she lived, which was longer than anyone expected:

Mara opened her mouth to say *no* — Ivet could see the *no* forming, simple and solid as a stone — and then the word *died*. Died right on her lips. Mara's brow creased. Her mouth worked. She looked, for one horrible moment, like a woman reaching into her pocket for a coin that had been stolen while she slept.

"No," she said at last, but slowly, like the word was borrowed. "No... child."

"You hesitated," said the second rider. She had a voice like wet paper. "Hesitation means the question touched something. May we come in and touch nothing?"

"The kettle's on," Mara said, which Ivet understood was either hospitality or a threat, and possibly both.

They came in. They sat. They asked questions, and Ivet watched each question land on Mara like a small grey bird pecking at a thread, pulling, pulling — and with every pull, something came loose. Mara reached for the word *ledger* and got *le... le... the book, the big book, the warden's book* — she had to build the word out of splinters. She reached for the name of the pass below the gatehouse and found a hole where it should have been, smooth-sided, freshly dug. She laughed at a joke the tall one made, and mid-laugh her face went confused, because she'd lost the joke, lost the whole middle of it, and covered by drinking tea.

Ivet's chest hurt. It hurt in a way that had nothing to do with fear for herself.

Because here was the thing the white had tried to take and failed: Ivet knew what forgetting looked like. Forgetting had a face. Forgetting had sat across from her mother at a table and eaten her brothers' names off the air. And watching these two grey birds-of-prey pick at Mara thread by thread, Ivet felt something rise in her that was not small, was not quiet, was not the frightened animal the snow and the cart and the white had made her.

It was the thing the woman had pressed into her hand along with the brand.

*Make them remember,* the woman had said. *That is the whole duty of a queen.*

Ivet was six years old and queen of a country that was on fire behind her, and she could not say a single word aloud, and none of that mattered, because Mara was losing pieces of herself into these people's teacups, and Ivet had decided — there on the loft floor, with dust in her hair and her heart hammering out a war-drum rhythm — that Mara Galloway was *hers* to protect. The way the sigil was hers. The way the promise was hers.

The tall Unminder set down his cup. "One more question," he said pleasantly. "Where is the child *now*?"

And Mara opened her mouth, and Ivet saw it happen — saw the hole open behind her eyes, saw her reach for the last three days and find them gone, smoothed over, a page with nothing written—

Ivet moved.

She came down the ladder faster than dignity allowed, crossed the room, and put herself between Mara and the riders, feet planted, chin up, one small hand fisted around the sigil scar. And she did the only weapon she had: she looked at the tall Unminder, straight in his kneaded-dough face, and she *remembered at him*. Deliberately. Furiously. She remembered the burning library at him, and the woman's terrible smile, and the taste of smoke, and the word *home*, and she pushed it all forward like a shield, like a wall, like a small girl standing in a doorway with her whole country held out in front of her.

The Unminder blinked.

For a moment — just a moment — his smooth face *flickered*, like a candle guttering, and something moved behind his eyes that might have been grief, might have been a memory of his own trying to surface through all that grey. His hand went, briefly, to his own palm.

Then the second rider stood. "We're done here," she said, in her wet-paper voice, and there was something almost gentle in it, which was worse. "Thank you for the tea, Warden. Do keep well. Forget nothing."

"Forget *nothing*," the tall one echoed, and the flicker was gone, ironed flat, and they rode out into the snow, and the mountain swallowed them.

* * *

The door closed. The silence came down like snowfall.

Mara sat at the table with her hands around a cold cup, and Ivet climbed into the chair beside her, and for a long time neither of them did anything at all.

"I've forgotten something," Mara said finally. Her voice was careful, the way you walk on ice. "Something important. I can feel the shape of the hole." She looked at Ivet, and her eyes were wet and furious and afraid. "They were in this house and I cannot tell you what they asked me. I can't — there's a gap, right *there*, and it's got my handwriting all over it, like I agreed to everything."

Ivet reached across the table and took Mara's hand. Turned it over. Looked at the glove-covered palm, then up at Mara's face, and held her gaze the way the woman in the library had held hers.

Slowly — watching Mara's eyes the whole time — Ivet pulled off her own mitten and pressed her bare palm against the back of Mara's gloved hand. Sigil to hidden sigil. Serpent to serpent. Star to star.

Mara went very still.

"What are you—" she started, and stopped, because Ivet was guiding her, tugging the glove loose finger by finger, and Mara let her, which was its own kind of answer, until the two scars lay together on the table, identical, matching, twelve years apart and exactly the same.

"Oh," Mara said. Very quietly. The way people say *oh* when a floor opens.

Ivet nodded once, grimly, a general confirming a map. Then she pointed at the door the riders had gone through, and at Mara, and at the space between, and drew in the spilled tea with one finger the Old Vessian sign for *danger-comes-twice*: a line, crossed by a line, crossed again.

"They'll be back," Mara translated, hoarse. "And next time I won't see them coming."

Ivet shook her head. Tapped the table. Drew the sign again — and then, beneath it, drew the winding mark for the smugglers' road, the one Mara's body knew and her mind had been stripped of, the one that ran south through the flooded mines toward a country that wasn't on any map anymore.

Mara stared at the little wet spiral for a long time.

"You want me to run," she said. "Take you and run."

Ivet shook her head again, harder, and jabbed the mark once more, insistent, and then — because some things could not wait for language — she scrambled onto her chair, took Mara's face in both small hands, and said, clear as a bell, in the language of the burning library, the first full sentence anyone had heard from her:

*"We don't run from the door. We go through it."*

Mara's breath caught. Somewhere under twelve years of frost, something turned over in its sleep.

And far down the valley, so faint that only a creature made mostly of listening could have heard it, the gate began — very softly — to sing.
