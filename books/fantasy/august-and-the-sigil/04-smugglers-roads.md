## 4. Smugglers' Roads

They left the gatehouse before dawn, while the frost still held its silver and the Unminders' lanterns were only a rumor of orange somewhere down the valley road.

Mara had packed the way she always imagined she would pack for this day, though she'd never once admitted to imagining it: bread wrapped in waxed cloth, a skin of water, the warden's ledger pressed flat against her ribs where it couldn't be seen. She'd taken down her father's old climbing rope from the beam without letting herself think about why her hands went straight to it, past three other coils that were newer and better kept.

"Can you walk?" she asked Ivet for the fourth time.

The girl nodded, then shook her head, then made a small gesture—*a little, but don't stop*—that Mara understood perfectly and could not have explained to anyone. That was becoming a theme of the morning. Of the whole year, if she was honest. Twelve years at the gate, and in two days she'd learned more about herself than in twelve winters of ledger-keeping, and almost none of it was comfortable.

The smugglers' road began behind the woodshed, which was exactly the sort of joke smugglers enjoyed. A loose board, a gap in the stone, and then a path so narrow the mountain seemed to have grown around it deliberately, closing its fingers. Mara squeezed through sideways, breath fogging, and came out into a fold of the hills no map had ever troubled itself with.

She stopped.

The path forked ahead—three ways, choked with bramble—and she knew, without knowing how she knew, that the left-hand way was death by bog, the middle way was watched from the ridge, and the right way would take them up through the Hanging Pines to the old drovers' shelf above the river.

Her feet were already moving toward the right-hand path.

"Mara." She said her own name out loud, just to hear something sensible. "Mara Galloway, you have walked this road maybe six times in your life, and never in winter."

Ivet looked up at her with an expression of profound, patient skepticism—the look of a child waiting for an adult to catch up to something obvious.

"I know," Mara said. "I know."

But she didn't know. That was the trouble. Her mind was a tidy little house of twelve rooms, all accounted for, all swept. And here was her body wandering through a thirteenth room she'd never been shown, touching things on the shelves like it owned them.

---

By midmorning they reached the first waymarker, and Mara's denial died quietly of natural causes.

It was a standing stone, waist-high, crusted with lichen, unremarkable to any king's surveyor who'd ever passed it. But carved into its shaded side, worn nearly smooth, was a spiral—a line coiling inward like a snail shell, like a ear listening to the center of the world.

Mara's hand rose and touched it before she decided anything at all.

And under her glove, the scar on her palm *woke*. Not pain, exactly. Recognition. The way a dog lifts its head before the knock comes. The spiral under her fingers and the sigil in her flesh answered each other across twelve years of forgetting, and a word arrived whole in her mouth, in a language she did not speak:

*Vess-ala-tir.* Waymark true. The road remembers what the walker cannot.

She said it aloud without meaning to. Ivet's head snapped up, eyes wide, and the girl grabbed her sleeve and pointed at her own mouth, then at Mara's, then made a sound—not a word, but a shape of delight, a note of pure astonishment.

"Yes," Mara said faintly. "Apparently we're both full of surprises."

She marked the stone in passing the way her hand insisted on doing it—two fingers, spiral to spiral, left to right—and felt something settle in her chest, some small door unlocking in a house she'd thought fully furnished.

They walked. The drovers' shelf clung to the mountainside above a river gone gray-green with snowmelt, and Mara found she knew where every handhold was, where the shelf narrowed, where the loose stone waited to betray the careless. Twice she caught Ivet's arm before the girl herself had seen the danger. Once she stepped over a crack in the rock that her eyes rated as trivial and her feet refused absolutely.

"You're showing off," she told her own legs.

Her legs declined to comment.

---

They sheltered that night in a hollow beneath the roots of a fallen pine, and Mara lay awake listening to the mountain breathe, cataloguing what else her body knew that she didn't. How to read the stars off by one season and correct for it. Which mushrooms were the lying kind. A lullaby, half-formed, in Old Vessian, that surfaced when she dozed and sank again when she woke, leaving only the feeling of it, warm as bathwater.

Someone had taken these things from her. Someone had folded them away behind a wall, and the wall was starting to leak.

She should have been terrified. She was, a little. But underneath the fear was something worse and warmer: *homesickness*, sharp and specific, for a place she had no memory of ever being.

Beside her, Ivet slept with the burned sigil curled into her fist, and in her sleep she murmured sounds that might have been words, might have been a name, over and over, like a prayer or an apology.

---

* * *

The mine found them on the second day, or rather they found the mine, because the storm drove them to it.

It came up the valley like a living thing, a white roaring wall, and Mara made the call every mountain walker dreads: shelter or die exposed. The mine mouth gaped in the hillside above the drowned workings of Kell's Deep—an old silver cut, abandoned since before the erasure, since before *anything*, its lower levels flooded black and still.

"We go in, we stay near the mouth, we wait it out," she said. "In and out. Like tea."

Ivet gave her a look that said, clearly, *you and your sayings*, and followed her in anyway.

They got perhaps forty paces deep when the light behind them changed.

Not the storm. Lanterns.

Three figures at the mine mouth, gray-cloaked, faces smooth as eggs, and the foremost one spoke in a voice like a page turning: "Warden Galloway. You are carrying an irregularity. Please set it down and step away."

Mara's mind did the thing the Unminders' voices made it do—stuttered, lost its footing. She opened her mouth to answer and found the word *irregularity* sliding out of her grasp even as she heard it, the meaning draining away like water through fingers.

But her body—her traitor, her savior, her thirteenth room—did not stutter.

Her body put Ivet behind her. Her body drew the knife she'd carried so long it had become part of her silhouette. And her body turned her palm upward and pulled the glove off with her teeth, because there was no time, because the Unminders were walking forward, because the girl behind her was breathing fast and small.

The sigil on her palm was burning now, actually burning, white-gold, and the spiral carvings in the mine walls—old Vessian waymarks, she saw them everywhere now, they'd been here all along, the whole country riddled with them like a secret written in the margins of another book—began to glow in answer.

*I don't know how to do this,* she thought.

*Yes you do,* said the rest of her, the buried part, calm as deep water.

She pressed her palm flat against the wall.

The world *folded*.

There was no better word for it. The mine, the lantern-light, the smooth gray faces—all of it bent inward like paper creased by a giant thumb, and the darkness between the folds filled with the smell of—

Bread.

No. Not bread. A kitchen. A low-ceilinged kitchen with a fire going, and someone humming, and the smell of onions and caraway and woodsmoke and rising dough, a smell so specific and so beloved that Mara's throat closed around it like a hand—

And then it was gone.

Not faded. *Taken.* Lifted cleanly out of her, the way you'd lift a stitch out of cloth, leaving the weave intact and the shape of the absence aching where it used to be. She knew she had owned that smell. She knew it had been the best thing in the world. She could not, for the life of her, remember a single note of it.

The folds closed. When the light steadied, the Unminders stood at the mine mouth looking at empty air, their heads tilting slowly, like birds that had lost the thread of why they'd come. Then they turned and walked back down into the storm.

Mara slid down the wall until she was sitting in the wet dark, her palm still glowing faintly, her chest heaving.

"That's the price," she said aloud. Her voice sounded scraped. "That's the toll. It takes a memory to pay for the magic. One for one."

Ivet knelt in front of her. The girl took Mara's face in both small cold hands and made her look up, and then—Ivet pointed at her own nose, then at the dark air where the kitchen smell had been, and frowned ferociously, and shook her head, and pointed at Mara's heart, and pressed her fist there, hard, twice.

*I can't give it back. But it mattered. You mattered.*

Mara laughed, or sobbed, or produced some undignified hybrid of the two. "Twelve years I've guarded a door," she said, "and nobody told me the door was me."

She pulled the girl against her shoulder and held her while the storm shouted outside, and she tried, desperately, greedily, to hold the memory's shape in her mind—to keep the ghost of the kitchen, the hum, the warmth—but it was like holding smoke, like trying to remember a dream after waking, and within an hour even the grief had softened into something stranger: a hole she could feel but not map.

She knew one thing with total certainty, though, and she said it into Ivet's hair like a vow:

"No more magic unless it's life or death. We spend nothing else. Whatever's left in me, we're saving it—for the gate. For whatever's coming."

Because something was coming. She could feel it the way she now felt everything Vessian: in her bones, in her blood, in the scar that pulsed gently in the dark like a second heartbeat.

Far to the east, past the drowned levels of the mine, past the mountains, the moon was already climbing toward its thirteenth turn.

And in her pocket, the ledger—silent for two days—had begun, very softly, to hum.
