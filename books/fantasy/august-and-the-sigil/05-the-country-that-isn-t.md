## 5. The Country That Isn't

The shrine has no roof.

Ivet knows this is how it should be — the sky is the roof, the sky was always the roof, and the hole where the stone fell in is shaped like a moon on purpose, because *Selvane* watches through it, the pale sister, the one who counts. The knowing arrives in her chest before it arrives in her head, warm as a swallowed coal, and she stands in the doorway of the ruined shrine with snowmelt dripping off her hood and feels, for the first time since the burning library, *placed*.

Behind her, Mara stops on the threshold like a dog at an invisible fence.

"It's just a ruin," Mara says. Her voice does the thing it does when she is frightened and pretending not to be — flat, careful, each word set down like a cup that might crack. "Old border shrine. They're all over these mountains. We should keep moving."

Ivet does not move. She looks up at the moon-shaped gap and lets the light fall on her face, and she thinks: *this is a door too.* Everything here is doors. The whole country is a house with all its doors bricked shut, and she is the key somebody loved enough to throw over the wall.

Mara sighs the long-suffering sigh of a woman who has already lost an argument she hasn't finished having, and steps inside.

* * *

The floor is mosaic. Most of it is broken, frost-heaved, furred with moss, but near the fallen altar there is a patch the size of a hearth-rug where the tiles still hold their colors: a river, blue as a bruise going green, and along its banks little golden figures, walking, dancing, carrying sheaves of wheat and baskets of fish and one — Ivet crouches, breath fogging — one figure carrying a goose under each arm, which is so funny that her whole body shakes with silent laughter until she has to point, urgently, seven times, before Mara leans down to look.

"A goose thief," Mara says gravely. "Vessia's greatest criminal."

Ivet nods solemnly. Then she spoons the thought out into words on the frozen ground with her finger, in Old Vessian, letter by letter: *goose-thief is a holy job.*

Mara reads it. Ivet watches her read it — watches her lips move around shapes, watches her brow fold — and then watches something stranger happen, which is that Mara's mouth keeps moving after the reading is done, shaping sounds without sound, like a woman talking in her sleep.

"I know this," Mara says slowly. "Not — I don't *know* it. But my mouth knows it. Like a song you haven't heard since you were small and then someone hums two notes and suddenly you know all of it."

Ivet's heart does a thing she has no word for. She sits down cross-legged on the cold mosaic, pats the tile beside her — the goose-thief tile, obviously — and looks up at Mara with what she hopes is her most teacherly expression. She has never been anyone's teacher. She has mostly been cargo, and then a fugitive, and then a girl holding a burned hand in the dark. But the woman who pressed the sigil into her palm had also said, *you are Vessia now, little one, carry it,* and carrying means handing things to people.

She points at herself. Taps her own chest twice.

"*Ivet*," she mouths, exaggerating every shape. Then she points at Mara.

Mara hesitates. "You know my name."

Ivet shakes her head firmly. Points again. *Your other name.*

And here is the thing Ivet has noticed, the thing she has been turning over for three days like a smooth stone in her pocket: when Mara says *Mara Galloway*, she says it the way you read a name off a tax ledger. But when Ivet had gripped her hand back at the gatehouse and spoken the true name — the old name, the name from before — Mara had cried without knowing why, and her hand had known Ivet's grip the way a lock knows its key.

So they play the game. Ivet taps her chest: *Ivet*. Points: *you?*

Mara opens her mouth. Closes it. Opens it again.

"Ma—" She frowns. "It starts the same. It's — there's a sound in the middle of it, a soft one, like—" She makes a shape with her lips and nothing comes out, and her face goes white, and Ivet realizes with a lurch that this is not a game anymore, that somewhere inside Mara Galloway there is a locked room and someone has taken the name off the door.

Ivet takes her hand. Writes on the frost-dusted tile between them, slowly, in letters that glow faintly gold where the sigil-warmth leaves her finger:

*M-a-r-a.*

"That's not it," Mara whispers. "That's the warden-name. The gatehouse name."

Ivet writes more. *A-u-g-u-s-t?* No — wrong, she knows it's wrong even as she writes it, though she doesn't know how she knows. She closes her eyes. Somewhere in the burned library there was a book of names, names of the smuggled children, and hers was in it, and next to hers—

Her eyes open. She writes four letters.

*A-v-e-l.*

Mara makes a sound like a hinge. "Avel," she repeats, and her voice cracks down the middle of it, and she presses both hands over her mouth, and above her fingers her eyes are streaming. "Why does that hurt? Why does a *name* hurt?"

Because, Ivet writes, and then stops, because she doesn't know yet. But she will find out. She is very good at finding out. Finding out is the only thing she has done since the fire.

* * *

They stay at the shrine three days, because the snow seals the pass and because neither of them suggests leaving.

On the first evening Mara builds a fire in the fallen altar, which Ivet suspects is sacrilege and decides to allow, because the firelight catches the mosaic river and makes it run. On the second day Ivet begins the lessons in earnest.

She cannot speak, so she teaches the way water teaches: patiently, everywhere at once. She scratches words in the ash. She mimes. When Mara mispronounces *selvane* — moon-sister — so badly it comes out meaning *wet boot*, Ivet laughs so hard she falls off the altar step, and then draws a wet boot wearing a crown, and after that neither of them can say the word without grinning, which is, Ivet reflects, probably not how the priests intended the moon-sister to be honored.

But the songs are the strange part.

Ivet hums them — she has no voice for words, but humming lives lower down, in the chest, and the chest still works. She hums the sowing-song her mother sang, the one about the seed that argues with the dirt, and Mara goes still as a hare.

"I know the harmony," Mara says wonderingly. "Ivet. I know the *harmony*. How do I know the harmony?"

Ivet shrugs, elaborate, innocent. *Maybe you were a famous singer. Maybe geese followed you everywhere.*

Mara throws a pinecone at her. Ivet catches it and puts it in her pocket, where it joins a button, a blue tile shard, and a crow feather: her museum of important things.

And when Mara finally, haltingly, hums the low line beneath Ivet's melody — wobbling, half a beat behind, but *there*, rising out of her like a fish surfacing — Ivet feels tears startle out of her eyes, hot and sudden, and she doesn't know if she's crying for the song or for the woman singing it, who is crying too, laughing and crying, saying "*I don't remember learning this, I don't remember, so where is it living?*"

In you, Ivet wants to say. It's living in you. They took your remembering but they couldn't take your *having-had*, and there's a difference, and the difference is a country.

* * *

It happens on the third night, by the fire, over the game.

The game is Ivet's invention: *two truths and a tile.* One person holds the blue tile shard and tells two truths and one lie; the other must catch the lie, in Old Vessian, or lose the tile. Mara is terrible at lying in any language, which makes the game easy and delightful. Ivet is excellent at lying, which makes it dangerous.

Then Mara holds the tile, turns it over in her gloved fingers, and says, in careful, accented, newly-born Old Vessian:

"When I was small, I lived in a house with a red door."

True. Ivet feels it land true, somehow, the way the shrine-knowledge landed true.

"My mother made bread on Thursdays and sang while she kneaded."

True — and Mara's face crumples as she says it, because it isn't imagination, it's *returning*, it's coming up through her like groundwater.

"And I came over the mountains when I was six years old, with my father."

Lie.

Ivet is already reaching for the tile, triumphant, when she sees Mara's expression — not defeated, not playful, but *shattered*, staring at the fire as if it has shown her something.

"No," Mara says. "No, that's the lie, isn't it. Not six. Not with my father." Her hand rises slowly to her gloved right hand, the one with the hidden scar. "An infant. I was an infant. Someone carried me over the mountains in a — a basket, a pack-basket, and the crossing cost — the ferryman, the smuggler, whoever — the price was—"

She stops. The fire pops. Above them, through the moon-shaped hole in the roof, Selvane slides into view, thin and counting.

"The price was my memories," Mara finishes, very quietly. "Someone paid for my life with my life. My whole — everything before the mountains. Paid to get me out." A terrible pause. "Paid by whom?"

*I don't know*, Ivet writes in the ash. And then, because it is true and because truth is the only currency she has left: *but they loved you. Nobody pays that much for a stranger.*

Mara looks at her for a long moment. Then she laughs — wet, cracked, real — and pulls Ivet against her side, and Ivet lets her, stiffly at first and then not stiffly at all, tucking her cold nose into the warmth of Mara's shoulder.

"Home," Mara says suddenly, testing the Old Vessian word like a new tooth. "*Domu.* This is the first time I've said it since—" She shakes her head. "It doesn't taste foreign. Isn't that strange? Twelve years at that gate and no place ever tasted like home, and this freezing ruin with a hole in its head—"

*Domu*, Ivet agrees against her shoulder. And then, because the word is loose in her now too, running free for the first time since the library burned, she writes it one more time in the ash between them, big, underlined twice, surrounded by a lopsided heart:

*DOMU.*

* * *

Near dawn, Ivet wakes to Mara standing at the shrine's threshold, very straight, looking north through the falling snow.

"Mara?" she signs — their own private sign now, quick and fluent, invented over three days of firelight.

"There's a light in the pass," Mara says. "Down-valley. Moving. Lanterns, maybe four — five." Her voice has gone flat and careful again, the cup-that-might-crack voice. "Travelers don't come this way, Ivet. Smugglers travel high and quiet. Whoever walks the open road with lanterns lit wants to be seen."

Ivet comes to stand beside her. Far below, tiny as altar-candles, the lights bob through the snow. And leading them, at the front, unhurried, there is a figure whose shadow falls wrong — too long, too many-jointed, the way shadows fall in the bad stories the Unminders never managed to burn.

Ivet slips her hand into Mara's glove, onto the scar, and squeezes once.

*Time to go home faster,* she signs.
