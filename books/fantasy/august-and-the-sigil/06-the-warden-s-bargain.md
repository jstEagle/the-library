## 6. The Warden's Bargain

The shrine they'd slept in had a hole in its roof shaped like a closed eye, and through it the morning came down in a single pale column that Ivet insisted on standing in, arms out, face tipped up, as though she were being poured into. Mara watched her from the doorway with a tin cup of snowmelt tea going cold in her hands and thought, not for the first time, that twelve years of solitude had not prepared her for how loud one small child could make silence.

"Drink your tea," Mara said.

Ivet stuck out her tongue, which was blue from cold, which was apparently meant to be an argument.

"It's chamomile. It grew wild by the gatehouse. I used to—" Mara stopped. The sentence had a hole in it too. *I used to* what? She could see the flowers, yellow-eyed and nodding along the gatehouse wall; she could almost smell them. But the *used to* was gone, scooped out like pulp from a fruit. She drank the tea to have something to do with her mouth.

They had three days' walk back to the gate, if the smugglers' roads held. The moon was fat now, a day or two from full—no. Not full. *Thirteenth.* The ledger's word for it kept rising in her mind like a fish surfacing: *the turn that never comes twice.* Whatever it meant, it was coming, and the Unminders knew it too. They'd seen sign of them twice since the mine—a burned waymarker, hoofprints in the melt—and Mara had begun walking the way she imagined hunted animals walked, all ears and no humming.

Which was why, when they reached the threshold of Thornfell at dusk on the second day and found a man sitting on the lowest step of the gatehouse as if he'd been waiting since before the stone was laid, Mara put Ivet behind her and drew the knife she'd carried for twelve years without once using.

The man did not look up right away. He was old in the way that mountains are old—not bent, exactly, but worn smooth, his gray hair tied back with a cord, his coat the same warden's gray as hers only older, softer, like cloth that had been loved past its usefulness. When he finally raised his head, his eyes were the color of the gate itself, that deep unlit iron, and Mara understood two things at once: he was dead, and he had been expecting her.

"You're the first warden," she said.

"I'm *a* first warden," he said. "There's been more than one of us who couldn't quite leave." He smiled, and it was a kind smile, tired around the edges. "You can put the knife away, Warden Galloway. It won't do anything to me but embarrass us both."

She didn't put it away. She did sit down on the step opposite him, because her legs had decided the matter without consulting her, and Ivet crept out from behind her and sat down between them, watching the ghost with the frank, unblinking interest of a cat presented with a moth.

"You know my name," Mara said.

"I know all our names. That's rather the problem." He laced his fingers over one knee. "My name was Aldous Vey. I kept this gate for thirty-one years. I sealed it. And then I stayed to watch the seal, because I didn't trust anyone else to, and then I stayed to watch myself stay, and somewhere in there I died without noticing, which I recommend against."

"Aldous," Ivet said, trying the shape of it, and beamed when it came out right.

"She speaks," Aldous said, eyebrows up. "To me, no less. Do you know how long it's been since anyone spoke to me? There was a fox, once, but I don't think it counted."

"What do you want?" Mara asked.

"To offer you a bargain." He said it simply, without theater, and somehow that was worse. "The moon turns in two nights. You've read the ledger's margins—I know you have, you have ink on your left ring finger and the guilty look of a woman who's been reading where she shouldn't. So you know the seal thins. You know what comes through when it does. And you know"—his voice gentled—"what it costs to keep it shut."

"The memories," Mara said. Her hand went, without permission, to her gloved palm.

"The memories," Aldous agreed. "The gate doesn't eat stone or blood. It eats remembering. Vessia fell, and the world agreed to forget it, and the forgetting has to be fed every day or the door swings open. Every warden pays. Some pay small—a name here, a face there. Some pay large." He looked at her glove. "You paid large once already, I think. Before you ever stood ward. Someone bought you passage out of a burning country with everything you knew of it."

Mara's throat closed. "That's not possible. I was born in Harrowmere. My mother—"

"Was a kitchen with a smell you can't find anymore?" Aldous asked softly. "A voice with no words attached?"

Ivet took her hand. Small fingers, cold, curling into her palm over the sigil scar. Mara breathed.

"So here is the bargain," Aldous said. "Two doors, Warden. Door one: seal the gate fully, tonight, while the moon is still turning toward its thirteenth. It will take everything the gate has left, and after that it will sleep for good—it will never need feeding again, because there will be nothing left behind it to remember. Vessia dies. Truly dies. Not erased—*ended*. But you live. The girl lives. No Unminders, no thirteenth moons, no more paying. You grow old in a quiet house with a garden, and neither of you ever loses another thing."

"And door two," Mara said.

"Door two: hold it open. Let the moon turn. Let Vessia come back through." His eyes were very steady. "And one of you pays the whole price. A life's remembering. Everything either of you has ever been, poured into the hinge, so the door stays open wide enough for a country to walk through."

The wind moved through the pass, carrying the smell of snow and, faintly, impossibly, woodsmoke and baking bread—the smell of a country that wasn't there yet, waiting on the other side of the mountain like a guest listening at the door.

"No," Mara said.

Aldous tilted his head. "No to which?"

"No to choosing. Not yet. Not here, not on your schedule, sitting on my own doorstep being managed by a dead man." She heard her own voice come out flat and hard and recognized, distantly, her mother's tone—no. Someone's tone. A voice with no words attached. "You've been dead a long time, Aldous Vey. You've had years to make this tidy. Two doors, one choice, nice and clean. But I've spent twelve years keeping this gate, and I'll tell you what I've learned: nothing about it is clean. It's a cage somebody built because they were tired and frightened, and you're offering me the same cage with nicer bars."

Something flickered across the ghost's face—hurt, quickly smoothed. "I built it to save people."

"I know," Mara said, and hated how gentle she sounded. "That's what frightened people always say."

* * *

They did not sleep in the gatehouse. Mara couldn't have said why, except that the ledger felt like it was watching her, and Aldous's absence—he'd faded with the dusk, promising to return—sat in the room like his presence had. Instead she took Ivet up the goat track behind the gatehouse to the warden's old watch-hut, a dry-stone bothy half-swallowed by gorse, where they lit a fire small enough to hide and ate hard cheese and the last of the waybread.

"He wants you to choose fast," Ivet said. She'd been quiet through supper, chewing her lip, and now she spoke with the sudden decisiveness of someone who has finished a long calculation. "Fast choices are bad choices. Everyone knows that."

"Do they?"

"That's how they get you. In Vessia—" she stopped. Started again, smaller. "In the library, before. The men in gray came and everyone ran and nobody thought, and that's why people got left." She looked at the fire. "The woman who gave me the sigil told me: *when they make you hurry, stop.*"

Mara reached over and tucked a strand of hair behind the girl's ear. "Your woman sounds wise."

"She was scared too," Ivet said. "But she stopped. For me. That's why I'm here."

Outside, the moon rose, nearly round, wearing its thin dark veil like a hood pulled partway up. Mara lay awake beside the sleeping child and turned the two doors over in her mind until they wore grooves. Seal it and live empty-but-safe. Open it and pay everything. And underneath both options, so quiet she'd almost missed it, a third thing she had no word for yet—something about the way Ivet had said *she stopped*, something about cages and the people who build them meaning well.

She was still turning it when she heard the horses.

Not on the road—*off* it, down in the black folds of the pass, muffled hooves on wet turf, many of them, moving without lanterns. Mara was up and awake in the space between one heartbeat and the next, hand over Ivet's mouth before the girl could gasp, finger to her own lips. Through the gap in the shuttered window she counted shapes below: eight riders fanning out across the slope, and among them figures on foot moving wrong, too smooth, faces tilted upward like hounds scenting.

Unminders. Here. Now—at the gate, on the eve of the turn, exactly where Aldous had wanted her cornered into choosing.

"They followed us," Ivet whispered against her palm. Her eyes were enormous. "Or they were always coming."

"We run for the gate. The wards will—"

The hut door burst inward.

Mara moved without deciding to—shoved Ivet toward the back wall, threw herself sideways, and the first Unminder's hand closed on air where her shoulder had been. The man's face was calm, terribly calm, the calm of deep water, and where his fingers brushed her arm the words she was shouting simply *left*, mid-syllable, like coins dropped through a torn pocket. She staggered. Behind her Ivet screamed—not a frightened scream, a furious one—and then the girl did something Mara would spend the rest of her life being grateful for having seen.

She raised her marked palm, and the sigil *burned*.

Light like white iron filled the hut. The Unminder recoiled, his calm cracking for the first time, and in that crack Mara found her knife-hand and her voice at the same moment. But there were others coming through the door, and the light was guttering, and Ivet made a small sound—

Mara spun. One of the foot-figures had gotten past her, silent as spilled water, and its hand was pressed flat against Ivet's side, and where it touched, the girl's scream cut off into silence, into a look of pure confusion, into a slow crumple—and beneath the Unminder's fingers, dark and spreading, was blood. Real blood. Ordinary, mortal blood, welling from a wound Mara hadn't seen made, as if the touch itself had opened her.

The Unminders withdrew as one, unhurried, their work done. At the door the last of them paused and looked back at Mara, and its calm face arranged itself into something almost like courtesy.

*"Choose soon, Warden,"* it said, in a voice like pages turning. *"The moon is impatient."*

Then they were gone, and the hut was firelight and blood and Ivet pale in her arms, breathing shallow, the sigil on her palm flickering like a lamp running dry.

"Hold on," Mara said. "Hold on, little one, hold on—"

Beyond the shuttered window, the veiled moon cleared the ridge, one night from its thirteenth turn, and far below in the gatehouse, faint as breath on glass, a dead man's voice seemed to say: *now you understand why I hurried.*
