## 7. Thirteen Moons Rising

*Mara*

The moon had been wrong for three nights.

Mara noticed it the way she noticed everything now—through Ivet. The girl kept stopping on the smugglers' road to stare up at it, her small face pinched with a worry too old for her, and when Mara asked what was the matter, Ivet only pressed her burned palm flat against her own chest and then pointed, urgently, back the way they'd come. Toward Thornfell. Toward the gate.

"She's right," Mara muttered, mostly to herself. "Of course she's right. She's eight and she's right."

The ledger at the gatehouse had been clear in its dry, disapproving way: *the thirteenth turn comes but once in a warden's keeping, and never twice.* Twelve years of ordinary moons, twelve years of Mara counting them like coins into a jar—and now the thirteenth, the one the old wardens had written about in handwriting that got shakier with every line, the one that came when the seal thinned to gauze and everything Vessia had been began pressing against it like a face against a window.

They had four days' walk behind them and two days' walk ahead, if the passes held, if the flooded mine stayed flooded, if the Unminders didn't find them first.

Ivet tugged her sleeve and pointed again. Then, with the fierce economy of a child who could not waste a single gesture, she drew in the snow-dusted dirt: a circle, a line through it, an arrow toward the mountains.

"I know," Mara said. "I know we're going back. I'm not an idiot, I'm just slow."

The girl gave her a look that said, with devastating clarity, *one of those things is true.*

Mara laughed despite herself—a rough, rusty sound, like a door opening after years. That was the trouble with Ivet. In five days the child had dismantled twelve years of careful solitude as though it were a fence made of paper. Mara had spent those years being nobody's anything: not daughter (her mother's face lived somewhere behind a wall of fog she couldn't climb), not Vessian (a word that meant nothing until it meant everything), not even properly a warden anymore, since wardens, apparently, were cages built by tired frightened people and she'd been living inside one without ever seeing the bars.

Now she saw them. Now the bars were all she could see.

They walked through the night. Mara's body knew the way even where her mind didn't—her feet found the cairns under the snow, her hands knew which stones were honest and which would roll, and twice she caught herself humming a walking song in Old Vessian, a song she did not remember learning, whose words arrived in her mouth like birds returning to a house they'd once nested in.

*Home,* Ivet had signed at the ruined shrine, and then pointed at Mara, and then at herself, and then at the space between them, which was maybe the truest thing anyone had ever told her.

Near dawn they crested the last ridge before Thornfell, and Mara stopped so abruptly that Ivet walked into her legs.

Below, in the grey half-light, the valley around the gatehouse was full of lanterns. Dozens of them. Hundreds. A constellation of cold white lights arranged in perfect, patient rings around the little stone building where Mara had spent twelve years being alone, and every light stood beside a figure in the King's grey, motionless as chess pieces waiting for a hand to move them.

At the center of the rings, directly before the gatehouse door, a single lantern burned gold.

"That's either very good or very bad," Mara whispered.

Ivet grabbed her hand hard enough to hurt, and Mara understood: it was both.

* * *

*Ivet*

The lights below looked like the library burning, seen from far away. That was the first thing. Lights in the dark meant running, meant the woman with kind terrible eyes pressing the sigil into Ivet's palm, meant *go, go, don't look back, you are Vessia, do you hear me, you are all of us now.*

But these lights weren't moving. They were standing still, and stillness was worse.

Ivet pressed closer to Mara's side. Mara was warm and smelled of woodsmoke and old wool and, faintly, of the shrine—the good smell, the singing smell. When Mara held her hand, the world made a shape. It wasn't a mother-shape exactly. But it was near enough that Ivet had stopped checking, at night, whether the shape was still there. It always was.

Below, the grey figures parted, just slightly, and someone walked out of their middle toward the gatehouse door.

Even from the ridge, even across the whole dark valley, Ivet felt it like a hook behind her ribs. The woman moved wrong. Not limping-wrong, not hurt-wrong. *Empty*-wrong, the way the Unminders moved in Vessia's last days, the ones who had come for the library, who had smiled while books screamed. A person-shaped hole wearing boots.

She tugged Mara's sleeve and made the sign they'd invented on the road, the one that meant *bad-empty-danger*, and Mara went stiff all over.

"Yes," Mara breathed. "I see her. Stars help me, I know her."

* * *

*Mara*

She knew the walk before she knew the face.

Twelve years ago—no. Before that. Before the fog. There had been a woman who came to the gatehouse when Mara was small, or when Mara was new, the memories wouldn't line up, they slid past each other like wet stones. A woman who had sat with her by this very fire, in this very valley, and spoken gently, and taken—

And taken.

"Oh," Mara said aloud, and her voice cracked down the middle. "Oh, you *thief.*"

The commander of the Unminders was Warden Ellery Vance. Her predecessor. The woman whose marginalia filled the forbidden pages of the ledger, whose handwriting went from confident to cramped to, in the final entries, barely legible: *it takes more than you think it will. it takes everything you think is load-bearing. i am sorry. i am free.*

Free. Mara looked down at the woman in the gold-lantern light—straight-backed, silver-haired, serene as a frozen lake—and understood, with a fury that started in her scarred palm and spread outward like fire through dry grass, what *free* had cost, and who had paid it.

Not Vance. Vance had chosen. Vance had handed over her remembering like a coat she was tired of wearing, and then, because an empty heart cannot bear to see a full one, she had spent the rest of her life making sure no one else got to keep theirs either.

"She took my mother's kitchen," Mara said. The words came out low and shaking. "The smell of it. Bread and rosemary and rain on hot stone. I've been trying to find it for twelve years and it's been *in her pocket* the whole time, or burned, or—" Her breath hitched. Beside her, Ivet wrapped both arms around Mara's arm and held on, and Mara put her free hand over the small burned palm and felt the sigil pulse there, faint and steady, like a second heartbeat.

"Thirteen moons," she said. "She's here for the thirteenth turn. She's going to seal it. Seal it and erase the girl and erase me and call it mercy."

Ivet looked up at her. And Ivet—who could not speak, who had crossed a vanished country barefoot, who had taught a grown woman her own name in a shrine of broken stones—signed something slow and deliberate, holding each shape long enough that Mara couldn't pretend to misunderstand:

*You choose. Now. Choose.*

The moon crested the eastern ridge behind them, swollen and strange, the color of a bruise healing yellow. Below, the Unminders raised their lanterns in unison, hundreds of small cold flames tilting toward the gatehouse like iron filings answering a magnet. The gold lantern flared.

Mara thought about the ledger. About duty. About the rules she'd kept for twelve years the way a drowning woman keeps hold of a plank that is also, she can finally admit, the thing that's holding her under.

Then she thought about bread and rosemary and rain on hot stone. About a song her feet remembered. About a small hand gripping hers in a flooded mine and refusing, absolutely refusing, to let go.

"The rules say one of us pays," she said quietly. "Fine. Here's my answer, Warden Vance."

She lifted Ivet onto her hip—the girl was lighter than she should be, the land already pulling at her, calling her home—and turned her back on the valley, on the lanterns, on the woman she had once trusted with everything and who had collected.

"They don't get to take her," Mara said, to the moon, to the mountain, to whatever old gods kept the smugglers' roads. "They don't get to take *us.*"

The sigil on her palm woke like a struck bell.

Far below, every lantern in the valley guttered at once—and Warden Ellery Vance turned her head, slowly, precisely, and looked directly up at the ridge where they stood, as if she had been waiting twelve years for exactly this.

"Run," Mara told Ivet, already moving. "Hold on. We're going through the front door."
