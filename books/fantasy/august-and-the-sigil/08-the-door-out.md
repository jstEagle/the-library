## 8. The Door Out

The gate had never looked like a gate. That was the first cruelty of it.

Mara had spent twelve years tending what she'd believed was a wall—a seam of black stone in the mountainside, older than the road, older than the ledger, humming faintly under her palm on cold nights like a sleeping animal. Now, with thirteen moons crowding the sky and Ivet's blood drying on her sleeve, the seam had begun to open whether she willed it or not, and Mara could finally see what it had always been: a door. Tall as a cathedral's promise, thin as a knife laid edge-up in the rock.

And between her and its heart stood forty Unminders in grey, and one woman in warden's black who wore Mara's stolen childhood like a coat.

"Step aside, Sable," Mara said, using the name from the ledger's margin—the name of the warden who had come before her. "You've done your duty for thirty years. Let someone else be tired."

Sable's face didn't move. That was the terrible thing about the Unminded; they were so *restful* to look at. No grief, no guilt, no weather at all behind the eyes. "The gate closes tonight," Sable said, in a voice like a well-mannered avalanche. "It has always closed on the thirteenth turn. You are not required to understand. You are only required to forget."

"I'm rather done being required," said Mara, and pulled the sigil from beneath her glove.

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about magic that runs on memory: you do not get to choose which memories are heavy enough to spend.

Mara had learned this three days ago in the flooded mine, when she'd burned the smell of her mother's kitchen to crack open a collapsed tunnel—bread and woodsmoke and something green, maybe rosemary, gone now, a hole shaped exactly like dinner. She'd learned it again at the shrine when she spent the sound of her father laughing to hold the Unminders off while Ivet slept. She was learning it now, running up the smugglers' switchbacks toward the gate with Ivet's hand locked in hers, spending memories like a drunkard spends coins, and every single one hurt more than the last.

An arrow took the boulder beside her head. Behind them, boots on scree, the disciplined rhythm of people who did not tire because they did not hope.

"Mara." Ivet's voice was small and furious. The girl had found words again since the land began leaking through the opening gate—Old Vessian mostly, but some Common, arriving like birds blown off course. "Mara. *Run better.*"

"I am running *beautifully*," Mara gasped, "for a woman carrying an entire country in her pocket," and she reached into herself for the next coin.

She spent the taste of honey-cakes on a market day in a town whose name she couldn't recall anymore—which was fine, apparently, because neither could anyone else, ever again—and the memory went out of her like heat from an open window, and where it struck the air it became light, and the light became a wall of thorns across the path behind them. The Unminders stopped. Even the Unminded, it seemed, respected thorns.

"That one cost you something big," Ivet said. Not a question. The girl had learned to read the flinch.

"A festival," Mara said. "I think. There was dancing." Her chest felt strangely roomy, the way a house feels after the furniture is carried out. "It's all right. It was probably a boring festival."

"It wasn't."

"No," Mara agreed. "It wasn't."

* * *

The gate's heart was a chamber behind the black stone, and no map showed it because no map remembered it. Mara knew the way the way she knew the way her own blood moved: badly, on faith, in the dark. The last stretch was a crawl through a fissure that the old wardens' ledger called the Throat, and Mara went first, because if the mountain decided to bite she would rather it bit her.

Inside, the chamber was small and round and impossibly lit, moonlight coming from nowhere, thirteen moons' worth of it pooled on the floor like spilled milk. And there, in the center, stood the mechanism.

Not gears. Not levers. A table of black stone, and on it a bowl of clear water, and above the bowl an inscription in Old Vessian that Mara could suddenly read—could read *fluently*, the way you can read your own handwriting—and the inscription said:

**A DOOR OUT OF VESSIA COSTS ONE LIFE'S REMEMBERING. ALL OF IT. PAID IN FULL, AT THE THRESHOLD.**

"Well," said Mara. "That's blunt."

Behind her, Ivet came through the Throat, pale, limping, the wound in her side already knitting closed where the land's returning power touched her—Vessia pouring back into the world beyond the gate, and some of it spilling over onto its heir like a mother licking a cub clean. The girl looked at the inscription. Then at Mara. Then at the inscription again, doing the arithmetic children should never have to do.

"No," Ivet said.

"Yes," said Mara. "But listen—listen—it's *fine*. It's actually fine. Do you know what I've got left? Half a childhood I didn't even know about until last week. Some songs. Your name. A truly embarrassing amount of feeling about ledgers." She was talking too fast and she knew it. "Ivet. Twelve years ago somebody paid my passage out of Vessia with my whole life, and I built a new one anyway. A good one. Cold, but good. I can build another."

"You won't remember me."

The words landed like the arrow had. Mara opened her mouth to lie—Ivet deserved one lie, surely, after everything—and found she couldn't. Not to this child. Not with thirteen moons watching.

"No," she said gently. "I won't remember you."

Ivet's face did something complicated and ancient, the face of a girl who had watched a library burn and a kingdom vanish and a woman she loved press a sigil into her hand and say *go*, and had promised herself never again. She grabbed the front of Mara's coat with both fists.

"Then we don't pay," she said. "We seal it. Like the ghost said. We live. We go south and eat honey-cakes and you teach me Common properly and nobody remembers anything ever again and it's *fine*—"

"Ivet."

"—it's *fine*, it worked for thirty years, everyone said—"

"*Ivet.*" Mara knelt, which put them nearly eye to eye, and took the girl's burned palm in both hands, careful of the sigil. "Do you know why I'm going to pay?"

Ivet shook her head. She was crying without any noise at all, which broke Mara's heart in a way she suspected she'd soon be unable to recall, and which she decided, fiercely, to look at directly while she still could. Brown eyes. A smudge of ash that had survived three rivers and a mountain. The particular stubborn set of a jaw that had once belonged, Mara now understood, to a grandmother she'd never meet.

"Because a country isn't a wall," Mara said. "It's a *remembering*. And because somewhere past those mountains there are people waking up right now who don't know their own names yet, and their queen is standing in front of me refusing to let a gatekeeper buy them back." She smiled. "Also because I've always wanted to see what I'd be like with nothing in my pockets. Lighter, probably. Better posture."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

Boots echoed in the Throat. Sable's voice, closer now, calling words that made the air itself feel thinner, words designed to unmake. Mara stood up. Above the bowl, the water had begun to rise on its own, reaching for the moonlight, patient as tax.

"How much time?" Ivet whispered.

"As much as love takes," said Mara, "and not a moment more."

* * *

She paid it standing up, facing the door, because she refused to do this kneeling or weeping or anywhere near the ledger.

The first thing to go was small, mercifully: the name of the innkeeper in Thornfell village who slipped her extra bread. She felt it leave like a splinter drawn out. Then larger things, faster, the water rising to meet each payment—the layout of the smugglers' roads, spent gladly, let the Unminders choke on those; the twelve years of gatekeeping, the long cold winters, the ledger's marginalia, her predecessor's warnings; her mother's face, which she'd only just gotten back, and which she held up to the light one last time before letting it go, saying thank you, thank you, thank you.

The Unminders reached the chamber mouth and stopped, because the door was opening, and even the Unminded could not look away from a country being born.

Beyond the black stone, where there had been rock and rumor, there was now morning. Actual morning, Vessian morning, gold on terraced hillsides, a river remembering how to run, a city un-ruining itself street by street like a wound healing in fast motion. People stood in the fields staring at their own hands, at each other, weeping and laughing and calling names across the valleys—names that had been erased and were now, with a sound like a great inhale, given back.

And the land poured through the gate, and where it touched Ivet it healed her utterly, and the girl straightened like a sapling finding the sun, and turned, and looked at Mara with eyes going wide and wet and royal all at once.

"Mara—" she started.

"Don't," Mara said, smiling, most of herself already gone, holding on by fingertips to exactly one thing, saving it for last, savoring it. "Let me keep you till the end. That's the order of operations. Gatekeeper's privilege."

"But—"

"*Ivet.*" One word. Her whole hoard. She spent it last, deliberately, pressing it into the rising water like a coin into a wishing-well, and the door swung wide, and Vessia came home.

The last thing Mara Galloway knew was warmth—moonlight and morning mixed, a small fierce hand gripping hers, a throne-side threshold glowing under a child's bare feet—and then the water took the rest, kindly, all of it, in full.

* * *

Later—how much later she could not have said—there was a woman standing alone in a gatehouse in the mountains, holding a broom she did not remember owning, looking out at a horizon that seemed, somehow, fuller than horizons usually were.

She felt like a house after the furniture was carried out. Roomy. Strange. Not unhappy, exactly. Just—expectant. As if she were waiting for someone, though she couldn't have said who, and the waiting ached in a way that was almost pleasant, like the ghost of a song.

On the doorstep, catching the early light, something glinted.

She bent down. It was a carving, fresh-cut, in the shape of a flame inside a circle, and when her bare fingers brushed it, warmth climbed her arm and settled behind her ribs—an ache, a fullness, a name-shaped hole that somehow felt less like loss than like a door left thoughtfully ajar.

"Huh," said the woman, to no one, smiling at she-knew-not-what.

Far below, on the road out of the pass, a young queen in traveling clothes kicked her horse into a canter, grinning like a girl late for the best lesson of her life.
