## 9. All That I Kept

The gate does not look like a door.

Ivet has known this since the night she came through it, small and barefoot and burning, when the world on the other side was a smear of snow and a woman with a lamp. The gate is a ring of standing stones, older than the wall around them, older than the ledger that forbids them, and in its center there is nothing at all—only air, only moonlight, only the thirteenth turn pouring down like water through a broken roof.

Mara stands in the middle of the nothing.

She is holding Ivet's hand so tightly that it hurts, and Ivet does not pull away, because hurting is a kind of holding too.

"You'll go through," Mara says. Her voice is steady. Her hands are not. "When it opens all the way, you'll walk through, and you won't look back, because looking back is how people turn into pillars and sad stories, and I have read enough sad stories for both of us."

Ivet shakes her head. She cannot say *no* in any language anymore; her voice went somewhere on the smugglers' road, spent like a coin, and what she has left are her hands and her eyes and the sigil in her palm, which is glowing now, which has been glowing since they crossed back over the mountains into the country that was not a country until tonight.

"Stubborn," Mara says fondly. "You're as stubborn as—" She stops.

Ivet watches the stopping happen. Watches it the way you watch a candle gutter: the light bends, flickers, steadies. Mara frowns, chasing something.

"As stubborn as my mother," she finishes, but slower, like a word remembered from a song whose tune has gone.

The stones begin to sing.

It is not a sound, exactly. It is more like remembering made audible—a low thrumming in the bones, in the teeth, in the scar beneath Ivet's glove. Beyond the ring of stones, where there should be nothing, something is arriving. Air first. Then color. Then the smell of woodsmoke and river-water and bread, an entire country's worth of smell, rolling through the opening like a tide coming home.

Vessia is coming back.

And Mara is paying for it.

* * *

Ivet sees the first loss before anyone could name it a loss.

Mara laughs—at something, at the absurdity of it all, at the singing stones—and then the laugh stops halfway out of her mouth, and she says, "What was I laughing about?" and her brow creases, and then she shrugs and smiles anyway, because whatever it was, it was funny, and some part of her knows that even if the knowing has no shape anymore.

That is how it goes. That is how it goes, and Ivet stands at the threshold of a returning kingdom and watches a woman be unmade one kindness at a time.

The stones take the roads first. Ivet knows this because Mara, who led them through flooded mines and frozen passes without a map, who navigated by waymarkers her body remembered and her mind did not—who said, three days ago, *my feet know things I don't*—looks down at her own boots and says, puzzled, "How did we get here? I mean here-here. This exact stone."

"We walked," Ivet signs.

"Did we?" Mara says. And then, lighter, covering the crack in her voice: "Well. My feet are very clever. They can have the credit."

Then the songs. The shrine songs, the counting songs, the rude marching song about the warden's hat that Ivet taught her at the ruined shrine while they shared a heel of bread. Mara hums it now, absently, waiting for the gate to finish waking—and gets to the third line and stops, mouth open, humming a note that belongs to no song at all.

"I knew that one," she says quietly. "Didn't I? I knew that one."

Ivet takes her hand and taps the rhythm against her palm. *Here. Here. Here.* Giving it back the only way she can.

Mara hums along. For four bars she has it. On the fifth bar it slides away from her like water through fingers, and she looks at Ivet with such naked apology that Ivet's chest cracks clean down the middle.

"It's all right," Mara says. "It's all right, little queen. Some things are worth forgetting."

*I'm not,* Ivet signs, fierce, tears finally coming. *You said. You promised. You said you'd remember me.*

"I did say that." Mara's smile wobbles. "I'm sorry. I appear to be a liar."

* * *

Beyond the stones, Vessia arrives.

Ivet turns to look because she cannot help it—the pull of it is older than her fear, older than her grief. The air beyond the gate has thickened into place. There are hills there now, and terraced orchards silvered with frost, and a river running bright under the thirteenth moon, and a city rising out of its own ruins the way a bruise rises, slowly, painfully, inevitably becoming itself again. Towers knitting their own stones. Roofs un-collapsing. Windows lighting one by one, as though someone inside each house had just struck a match and was not entirely sure why, but was glad of it.

And people.

They come through the gate in ones and twos and then in rivers—people who have been standing at the edge of erasure for twelve years, half-remembered by cousins in other countries, missing from portraits, forgotten mid-sentence by their own mothers. They spill across the threshold weeping, calling names, falling to their knees on Vessian soil and pressing their palms flat against it as if checking whether it is real. An old man carries a cat. A woman carries a baby who was old when the country fell and is somehow still old, blinking at the moonlight, alive, impossible. A boy of maybe ten runs in circles shouting his own name over and over, practicing it, wearing it out like a new coat.

Someone starts singing. It is the counting song. Ivet's counting song, the one Mara just lost, and it comes rolling up from a hundred throats at once, whole, unbroken, and Ivet understands with a lurch that the song was never hers—it belonged to everyone, always, and it survived because countries keep their songs even when they lose everything else.

Mara listens. Her face does something complicated.

"That's beautiful," she says. "I feel like I should know it."

"You do," Ivet signs. "You did."

Mara looks at her then—really looks, the way she looked that first night in the gatehouse doorway, barefoot child in the snow, before either of them knew anything—and whatever is happening behind her eyes, whatever the stones are taking, she holds onto this one thing with visible effort, gripping it like a rope over a cliff.

"Your name," she says. "Tell me your name. Quickly. Before I—"

*Ivet.*

"Ivet." She says it twice, three times, carving it somewhere deep. "Ivet. That's a good name. That's—I gave you soup, didn't I? The first night. You were too thin. You held the bowl with both hands like it might run away."

Ivet nods, sobbing, laughing. *Yes. Yes, that was you. That was us.*

"Good," Mara says. "Good. Keep that. One of us should."

* * *

The last of it goes gently, which is the cruelest thing.

Mara does not scream. She sits down on the threshold stone, tired the way a person is tired after carrying something heavy a very long way, and she watches the city glow, and she says, "Oh, it worked," with wonder in her voice, plain wonder, as if she had no part in it, as if the miracle simply happened near her.

Then she looks at her own hands, at the sigil scar fading there, going pale, going quiet, its work done.

"I think I was someone," she says thoughtfully. "I think I was someone important. A warden? There's a word—warden—that feels like mine." She pats the stone beside her. "Do you want to sit? You look like you've been crying. Someone should tell you it's all right. Everything's all right now. Look how bright it is."

And Ivet sits beside her, because what else is there, and holds her hand, and Mara smiles at her the way you smile at a kind stranger, warm and empty and utterly without recognition, and Ivet thinks: *this is what she bought. This is what it cost. Every memory, traded for a door.*

The gate flares white-gold behind them. The last stone settles into place. Somewhere in the restored city, bells begin to ring—bells that did not exist an hour ago, ringing a peal that no one taught them.

People come for Ivet then. Of course they do. The sigil in her palm is blazing openly now, and the old woman with the cat kneels first, and then the boy with his new-worn name, and then dozens, hundreds, a whole returned nation turning toward the child with the burned hand like flowers toward sun.

"The heir," someone breathes. "The royal line. The last of them."

They lift her up. She does not ask them to. She is small and mute and nine years old and they put her on their shoulders anyway, and carry her down through the gate-road toward the city that remembers itself again, and the crowd crowns her the only way a people with nothing left can crown anyone: by acclamation, by roaring her name—her true name, her full name, the one the burning woman whispered into her ear as the library fell—until it echoes off towers that grew back in the dark.

Queen Ivet of Vessia. By the will of the returned, so let it be.

She should feel triumphant. She does, in flashes—when the river catches the moonlight, when the boy sings, when an old soldier weeps into his beard and calls her by her mother's name. But every flash of joy has a shadow shaped like a woman on a threshold stone, smiling kindly at a stranger.

* * *

Near dawn, they bring her back to the gatehouse.

Mara is there. Of course she is—she lives there, someone explains, the warden, or she used to be the warden, or perhaps she simply never left. She has built a fire. She has, inexplicably, made soup, following some instinct her mind cannot source, and when Ivet comes through the door Mara holds out a bowl without being asked.

"You look hungry," Mara says. "Hold it with both hands. It likes that."

Ivet takes the bowl. Her hands are shaking. She sits across the fire from the woman who gave everything, who does not know her, who does not know herself, and she drinks the soup, and it tastes exactly the same as the first night, and that is the worst mercy of all.

Mara tilts her head, studying her across the flames. Something moves behind her eyes—not memory, not quite, but the ghost of a groove worn deep by years of feeling.

"Do I know you?" Mara asks softly.

Ivet sets down the bowl. She reaches into her coat and draws out the piece of chalk she has carried since the shrine, and on the wide hearthstone, in careful strokes, she draws the sigil—the gate-sigil, the royal sigil, the one burned into her palm and once, long ago, into another palm entirely.

Mara stares at it. Her gloved hand drifts, slow and wondering, toward her own hidden scar.

"No," she says slowly. "But I think... I think I'm going to."

Far off in the east, past the pass, a young queen's road home is already being planned—and it begins, Ivet decides, right here, at this fire, tomorrow, and the day after, and every day after that, for as long as it takes for love to find its way back through a door that has no memory but has never once been locked from the inside.
