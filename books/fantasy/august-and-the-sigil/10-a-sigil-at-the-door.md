## 10. A Sigil at the Door

The gatehouse had no business being comfortable. It was a squat stone fist of a building at the end of a road that went nowhere, with a door that hadn't opened in living memory and a stove that smoked unless you spoke to it kindly. Mara spoke to it kindly. She didn't know why. It was simply the sort of stove that deserved it.

She had lived there for—well. She didn't know how long, exactly, and this didn't trouble her the way it might have troubled another person. When she tried to count the years, the number slipped through her fingers like water through a wicker basket, and she would find herself instead thinking about the way the light came through the eastern window at dusk, or the particular whistle the wind made through the pass, a long low note like someone humming an old song they'd half forgotten.

Someone had half forgotten a song. The thought arrived, sat down at the table, and left again without taking its boots off.

She kept the gate. That was the one thing she knew with the certainty of a stone knowing it was a stone. Every morning she rose, banked the stove, and walked the short path to the great door set into the mountainside—a door of grey wood bound in iron, carved over with symbols that meant nothing to her and everything to her hands, which sometimes rose of their own accord to trace them, the way a tongue returns to the socket of a missing tooth.

"You're still shut," she told the door each morning. "Good. Stay that way."

She didn't know what was behind it. She didn't know what she was keeping in or keeping out. The King's surveyors had come once, years ago—she thought it was years ago; time was a soup in which she occasionally found unexpected bones—and asked her questions with soft, apologetic voices, and she had answered what she could, which was nothing, and they had left looking vaguely disappointed, like men who had come to arrest someone and found only an empty chair.

She was not an empty chair. She was quite sure of that. She simply couldn't have said what filled her.

* * *

She had a routine, and the routine had rooms in it, and she lived in all of them.

Mornings: the stove, the door, the tracing of symbols. She had learned not to fight the tracing. Her fingers knew the shapes the way her feet knew the path down to the spring, and fighting knowledge that lives in the body is like arguing with a river.

Midday: she walked. There was a road behind the gatehouse, narrow and switchbacked, that climbed into the high passes. The villagers from the valley below—the ones who brought her cheese and flour and, once a season, an enormous and unrequested wheel of something they called "the good stuff," which was always slightly rancid—told her never to take that road. It went nowhere, they said. It was smugglers' ground, they said. Bad for the knees, they said, and on this last point at least they were unimpeachable.

She took it anyway. Every day. Her feet found holds in the rock that her eyes hadn't seen, ducked beneath ledges, stepped over stones that looked unremarkable but that her body treated with a strange reverence, pausing over each one as if reading it. Once she had caught herself pressing her palm flat against a boulder and whispering something in a language she did not speak, a word like *velth-irren*, and she had stood there in the wind with her heart going like a hammer, not frightened, exactly, but the way you're not frightened when someone calls your name from another room in an empty house.

She had tried, in her first months, to write down what she didn't know. She'd filled three pages with questions—*Who was I? Why here? Why does the stove respond better to flattery than coal?*—and then she had found the pages again a week later and read them like letters from a stranger, and the stranger had seemed so sad that she'd burned them, gently, in the kindly stove, and made tea instead.

Some things, she decided, were better carried than counted.

* * *

The winter of the sigil came on soft, with snow that fell in fat, unhurried flakes, as if the sky were taking its time.

She was sweeping the doorstep—she swept it every morning, though no one had walked it in living memory, and she had stopped asking herself why, because the answer was always the same: because it wanted sweeping—and that was when she saw it.

Carved into the second flagstone, small enough to cover with a palm: a shape she knew. Not recognized—she had nothing to recognize it *with*—but knew, the way you know a stair in the dark. A circle, and within it a branching line like a tree, or a river seen from above, or the path a crack takes through ice. Her hands had traced this shape on the great door a thousand mornings. Her hands had never once traced it on her own doorstep.

She crouched. The carving was fresh; the snow had fallen after it was made, and the flakes in its grooves had not yet melted, so it had been cut in the night, in the cold, by someone who had stood exactly where she was standing.

She should have been afraid. She understood that distantly, the way you understand that fire is hot even when you're not touching it. A stranger had come in the night to her door, the door of the last gate in the world, and left a mark that matched the marks on a sealed door that sealed she-didn't-know-what.

She put her hand on it.

The warmth went up her arm like tea going down on a cold morning. Not heat, exactly—more the memory of heat, the way a coat holds the shape of a body after it's been taken off. It pooled in her chest, behind her breastbone, in a place that had always felt to her like a room with the furniture covered in sheets.

Something in her wanted to weep. She didn't. But it was a near thing, and she stood there in the falling snow with her hand pressed to the stone, letting the warmth pour into that sheeted room, and one corner of one sheet lifted, and she glimpsed—nothing. A color, maybe. A sense of someone laughing, though she couldn't have said whose laugh, or at what, or whether the laughing one had been herself.

"Whoever you are," she said to the empty pass, "you'd better come back and explain yourself. I don't keep marks on my doorstep for strangers."

The pass said nothing, in the way of passes. But the wind came down it, and the wind was carrying, very faintly, that half-forgotten hum again, and this time—for just a moment, before it frayed away—she could have sworn it was a song, and a child's voice singing it, and that the words, whatever they were, meant *come home, come home, the door is open.*

* * *

She went inside and made tea and had a conversation with the stove, which was going through one of its sulky phases.

"Someone carved on my step," she told it.

The stove smoked.

"I know it's not a crime. I'm just saying. A person likes to be asked."

The stove, mollified by the attention, settled into a respectable crackle, and she sat with her tea and let the warmth of it sit alongside the warmth of the sigil, the two of them getting acquainted in her chest.

That was the thing she kept circling, the way her feet circled the waymarkers on the smugglers' road: she was full of warmth with no story attached to it. She loved the gatehouse and didn't know why. She loved the high road and didn't know why. She had woken, more than once, from dreams of a burning library with tears on her face and a word in her mouth—*velth-irren, velth-irren*—that meant nothing and grieved her like a funeral.

There was a shape in her, is what it came down to. A person-shaped absence, the way a snowbank holds the shape of the one who fell into it long after the one has been dug out. Somebody had been there. Somebody had filled her so thoroughly that even with everything taken, the taking had left a mold, and the mold ached on cold nights, and the ache was—she tested the word, turned it over, found it fit—*fond.*

She was fond of someone she had never met. She was homesick for a place she had never been. She was grieving a loss she couldn't name, and the grief was the best thing she owned.

"Mad as a March hare," she informed the stove, and drank her tea.

* * *

Three days later, she heard the bells.

Not the valley's bells—those were dull, cracked things that rang for sheep and funerals and could not always be told apart. These came from high up the pass, thin and bright, and they were ringing a pattern her body knew before her mind did: three, pause, three, pause, one. Her hand was on the door latch before she had decided to move.

She stood in the doorway with the snow coming down and watched the road.

They came out of the pass in ones and twos at first, then in a stream—people, dozens of them, hundreds, wrapped in furs and road-cloaks, some carrying children, some carrying nothing at all because they had left with nothing at all, all of them walking down the smugglers' road that went nowhere, weeping and laughing and singing in a language that Mara did not speak and that her bones understood every word of. Behind them, beyond the great grey door, the mountains were—different. She couldn't have said how. There were towers where there had been crags, and green where there had been stone, and a light on the far peaks like morning arriving from the wrong direction.

And at the head of them, riding a shaggy mountain pony far too small for the gravity of her bearing, came a young woman in a crown that looked, to Mara's eye, like it had been made that afternoon, possibly by an enthusiastic child, out of whatever had been to hand.

The column stopped at the foot of her path. The young woman did not. She rode up it, dismounted in the snow, and stood in front of the gatehouse door with the sigil-crown slightly crooked and her breath steaming, and she looked at Mara the way you look at a house you grew up in.

"Hello," she said. Her Vessian accent was thick; her trade-tongue was careful, each word set down like a cup on a saucer. "I'm told you don't remember me. That's all right. I've come to introduce myself."

Mara's hand had gone, without her permission, to the warmth behind her breastbone, which had stood up like a dog hearing its name.

"I'm Mara," she said. "I keep the gate."

"I know," said the young queen, and her voice broke in the middle of the two small words, and she laughed at herself for it, wiping her eyes with a fur-mittened hand, entirely unroyal, entirely certain. "I know you do. You always did. My name is Ivet, and I'm going to tell you a story, and it's going to take a while, and you're going to make tea while I do it, because you always made tea when things were hard, and I would know, because I was there."

The sheeted room behind Mara's breastbone stirred. Somewhere in the covered furniture, something was smiling.

"Well," said Mara, stepping back, holding the door wide. "You'd better come in, then. It's cold, and the stove only behaves for company."
