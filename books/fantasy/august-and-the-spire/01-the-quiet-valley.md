## 1. The Quiet Valley

The trouble with Hollowmere, Marguerite's mother used to say, was that it was too good at being forgotten. The valley sat in the crook of two grey mountains like a coin dropped into a pocket and never missed, wrapped so deep in mist that even the crows flew around it rather than over. Travelers who stumbled in by accident — a lost peddler once, a runaway bride another time — always described the same sensation: as though they'd walked through a curtain of cold water and come out somewhere the world had decided to stop shouting.

That was the point of the place. That had always been the point of the place.

Magic could not follow you into Hollowmere. Whatever old bargain had been struck — and no one living knew its full terms, though Odile claimed she'd read fragments of them in a book whose pages were made of pressed moth wings — the valley stood outside the reach of every spell and hex and haunting that plagued the rest of the realm. No witch could scry past its borders. No curse could cross the ridgeline. The gifted came here when the world grew dangerous, and here they became simply people: the woman who ran the bakery and happened to read smoke; the ferryman who could talk to rivers but mostly talked about the weather; the girl with moss growing in her hair who was, everyone agreed, entirely ordinary except for the moss.

And Marguerite Quintana, who carved charms from bone and heard the dead whisper through them — except in Hollowmere, where the dead were quiet, and her gift was quiet, and she was almost, nearly, at peace.

Almost. Nearly. The gap between those words was where she lived.

* * *

On the morning the widow Halloran came calling, Marguerite was already at her workbench, sleeves rolled to the elbow, arguing with a piece of sheep bone.

"You're being difficult," she told it. The bone said nothing, which was typical. "I know what you want to be. You want to be a button. I can see it in you. Well, I'm sorry, but buttons don't help anyone, and there's a woman coming up my path who needs more than a button today."

The workshop smelled of beeswax and juniper smoke and the faint mineral tang of bone dust, which Marguerite no longer noticed but which visitors always remarked on, wrinkling their noses and then apologizing for wrinkling their noses. Shelves lined every wall, crowded with finished charms strung on twine: small vertebrae beads for mothers with colicky babies, a rib-slat wind chime that played the particular note that soothed nightmares, a whole cabinet of tiny carved animals that did nothing at all except make children feel brave, which Marguerite maintained was a kind of magic too, just one that didn't require her.

She turned the sheep bone over in her hands. It was a good bone, dense and pale, from a ewe that had lived fourteen years and been mourned properly, which mattered. Bone that had been loved held its shape better under the knife. She'd tried working with butcher-shop stock once, early on, and the charms had come out sullen and short-lived, like they resented the whole arrangement.

The knife was her grandfather's, antler-handled, worn smooth as river stone. She set the blade against the bone and began, as she always began, with the name.

"Edmund Halloran," she murmured, carving the first curve. "Husband of Petra. Father of three, one of whom still writes letters. Died in the ice-thaw, four years past, when the millrace took him." Each fact went into the bone with the blade, not written but *worn*, worked into the grain until the charm hummed faintly against her palm. "Loved plum brandy. Sang badly. Sang anyway."

Outside, the mist was burning off the way it did by midmorning, unraveling into rags that drifted up the mountainsides. Through the window she could see the village proper: slate roofs, the bakery chimney already smoking, Widow Prue hanging washing that would take until sundown to dry because the valley air kept everything damp and slow. A cart rattled past on the low road, and the driver raised two fingers to her window without stopping, because that was how Hollowmere said hello — economically, and with the assumption of many more hellos to come.

Marguerite had been born here. She had never left it. She thought about this more often than she let on.

"Carving again?" said a voice from the doorway, and Odile came in on a breath of cold air, carrying a basket of late apples and wearing the expression of a woman who had opinions about something and was deciding whether to share them.

Odile was seventy if she was a day, thin as a winter branch, with white hair she wore in a braid thick as a ship's rope and eyes the color of iron nails. She had taught Marguerite the craft — the names, the knives, the listening — back when Marguerite was small enough to sit on this very workbench and swing her feet. She was, as far as anyone knew, the last person alive who remembered what lay outside the covenant, and she guarded that knowledge the way a dragon guards gold: jealously, and with occasional ominous remarks.

"I'm carving," Marguerite agreed. "Petra Halloran wants to speak to Edmund. One last conversation. She's been saving up things to say for four years."

"Hm." Odile set the apples down and inspected the half-finished charm with the critical squint she reserved for apprentice work, which was insulting, since Marguerite had been a journeywoman for a decade. "You're using sheep?"

"It's what I have."

"You have elk horn in the third drawer. I brought it back from the high pass myself."

"The elk horn is for emergencies."

"A widow's grief isn't an emergency?"

Marguerite put down the knife. "It's different. Elk horn holds a voice clearer, truer. If I use it for a single conversation, it's like... spending a whole harvest on one meal. And then when someone truly desperate comes—"

"When someone truly desperate comes," Odile said, "you'll carve whatever's in front of you, because the material matters far less than you tell yourself it does, and you know it." She picked an apple out of the basket, polished it on her sleeve, and handed it over. "But go on. Use your sheep bone. See if I say anything when it works perfectly."

"You'll say something."

"I'll say several things." Odile settled onto the stool by the window, which was her stool, uncontestably, and began paring her own apple with a small knife she produced from somewhere in her shawl. "Now. Tell me what you hear."

Marguerite looked down at the half-carved charm. In Hollowmere, the dead were silent — that was the covenant's great mercy, or its great cruelty, depending on who you asked and how recently they'd buried someone. Her gift didn't switch off inside the valley; it simply went quiet, like a radio reduced to static. But bone remembered. Bone carried echoes the way a seashell carries the sea, and when she carved a name into it with enough care, enough patience, enough love honestly spent, she could catch the shape of a voice that had once filled it.

Not the dead themselves. Never quite the dead themselves. An echo of an echo. But for the grieving, an echo was everything.

"I hear him," she said quietly. "Or the memory of him. He's impatient. He thinks Petra has been ridiculous about the fence."

"The fence," Odile repeated.

"The east fence. He built it crooked on purpose, apparently, so the morning sun would hit the vegetable bed first. Petra's been meaning to straighten it for four years and he finds the idea unbearable." Marguerite smiled despite herself. "He also wants her to stop watering the rosemary. She's drowning it."

"There," Odile said, with satisfaction, as though Marguerite had passed an examination. "You see? Sheep bone is fine. Elk horn is vanity." She pointed her paring knife at her student. "Now listen to me, because I'm only going to say this once, and then we're going to pretend I never said it. Tonight, when Petra speaks to her husband, you will stand behind her, not beside her. You will hold the charm, not hand it over. And when it's done, when the echo fades, you will let her thank you, and you will not wave it off, and you will not make a joke about buttons."

Marguerite felt her face do something complicated. "I wasn't going to—"

"You were always going to. You deflect like water off slate. It's your worst quality, and I include the year you decided to dye your hair with walnut ink." Odile returned to her apple. "One day you'll understand why I'm telling you this. I hope, for your sake, it's a gentle day when you do."

* * *

Petra Halloran arrived at dusk, in her good shawl, wringing a handkerchief into a rope.

Marguerite's workshop had a second room behind the bench — small, round, windowless, lit by a ring of juniper candles that threw more scent than light. There was a chair in it, one chair, facing a low table where the charm would rest. Everything about the room was designed to feel less like magic and more like a kitchen conversation, because grief, Marguerite had learned, could bear almost anything except ceremony.

"Sit," she said. "Breathe. He's not going anywhere."

"He might have moved on," Petra whispered. "Four years. People say—"

"People say a lot of things about the dead, and most of them are wrong, and the rest are guesses." Marguerite set the finished charm on the table between them. It was the size of a thumb joint, carved into the shape of a small coiled snake — Edmund's joke, per his echo, that he'd wanted to come back as something with no chores. "I can't promise you Edmund. What I can promise is that some part of what he was is still in this bone, the way warmth stays in a stone after the fire's out. You talk. It listens. Sometimes it answers. That's all. That's everything."

Petra sat. She looked at the little snake for a long moment. Then, in the voice of a woman stepping off a ledge, she said: "Edmund. You absolute fool. You built our fence crooked."

The candle flames leaned, very slightly, all in the same direction, as though a door had opened somewhere.

Marguerite stood behind her, as instructed, and held the charm, and listened to a dead man's echo tease his widow about rosemary and fences and the letter their son kept promising to write, and heard Petra laugh and sob in the same breath, and watched four years of unsaid words pour out of her like water from a tipped jug. It took an hour. It took no time at all. The echo faded gently, the way warmth fades from a stone, and Petra sat in the candlelight with wet cheeks and, unbelievably, a smile.

"He says the sun should hit the beans first," she said. "He *planned* it. All this time I thought he'd rushed the job."

"He planned it," Marguerite confirmed.

"I'm going to leave the fence exactly as it is forever." Petra stood, pressed both of Marguerite's hands between her own, and gripped hard. "Thank you. Thank you. You've given me—" Her voice failed, recovered. "You've given me my husband back for an hour. How does anyone thank you for that?"

"They don't," Marguerite said warmly. "They pay me in bread and complain about my prices, like civilized people."

Petra laughed, hugged her, and left. The door closed. The candles burned.

And Marguerite stood alone in the round room, in the smell of juniper, holding a charm that had gone silent and would never speak again, and felt the loneliness arrive right on schedule — punctual as the evening bell.

This was the shape of her life, and she knew it exactly: she gave other people their dead back, and she was not permitted her own. Not in Hollowmere. The covenant kept the dead quiet here, kept the valley safe, and the safety was real and precious and non-negotiable, and Marguerite had agreed to it before she was old enough to understand what she was agreeing to. Her mother's voice — an echo she could never legally chase — her mother's laugh, her mother's terrible singing: all of it sealed away beyond the reach of her own craft, unless she broke faith with the only home that had ever sheltered her kind.

She served the grieving. She was not allowed to grieve. She had made her peace with it, or a treaty, at least — the uneasy kind, with skirmishes.

Marguerite blew out the candles, pocketed the spent charm to grind down for powder, and stepped out into the blue evening. Mist was rising off the mere in long silver banners. Somewhere downhill, Widow Prue was yelling at a goat. The world was soft and safe and utterly, deliberately asleep.

Which was why, when she saw Odile standing at her gate in the dark, lantern unlit, face pale as the moon above her, Marguerite's stomach dropped before her teacher said a single word.

"Come inside," Odile said quietly. "There's something we need to burn."
