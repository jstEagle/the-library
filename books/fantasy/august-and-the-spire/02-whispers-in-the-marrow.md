## 2. Whispers in the Marrow

The trouble with bone, Marguerite had learned by her fourteenth year, was that it remembered everything and apologized for nothing.

She sat at her workbench in the back room of Odile's cottage, a rabbit's femur braced in the little iron clamp, her carving knife moving in slow curls along its length. Outside, October was doing what October did best in Hollowmere — raining sideways, rattling the shutters, and making everyone agree it was excellent weather for staying indoors, which was the valley's favorite kind of agreement.

"Steady," she murmured to the bone, or possibly to herself. The two had become difficult to tell apart over the years.

The charm was for Widow Anselm, down in Lowmarsh, whose husband Bertrand had died in the spring when his cart horse decided, mid-ford, that it had opinions about rivers. Bertrand had been a talkative man in life — the sort who could turn a sentence about turnips into an evening — and his widow had come to Odile's door three weeks ago with red eyes and a firm jaw and said, "I don't want him *back*. I want one more conversation. One. Then he can go on to whatever comes next and stop haunting my pantry."

Marguerite understood the distinction better than most people would have guessed. The dead didn't want to stay. They wanted to be *heard*, once, properly, and then they wanted to rest — and it was the living who couldn't let go, who kept them circling like moths around a lamp they'd mistaken for the moon. Her whole craft was really just good manners applied to the boundary between worlds. *Please. Thank you. You may go now.*

She blew away a curl of bone dust and held the femur up to the window light. The spiral she'd carved caught the grey afternoon and seemed to hold it, the way a cup holds water. Good. The lattice was sound. Now came the part no teacher could demonstrate, because it happened somewhere behind the ribs rather than between the ears: the listening.

She closed her eyes. She let her breath slow until it matched the rain. And she reached — gently, always gently, the way you'd knock on a neighbor's door rather than batter it down — into the marrow.

*Bertrand?*

The dead man was there at once, the way he'd probably been there all along, hovering near the surface of the bone like a face pressed to frosted glass. His presence felt the way his voice had sounded, if that made any sense — round and warm and slightly out of breath, as though he'd hurried to answer.

*Is that the girl? The carver?*

*It is. Your wife has asked for you.*

*Agnès! Is she—* A fluttering, a gathering, the sensation of someone smoothing down their coat before company arrived. *Tell her the third jar on the left shelf isn't plums. Never was plums. I may have mentioned this while dying but she wasn't listening, on account of the cart being on top of me.*

Marguerite bit down on a smile. *I'll pass it along.*

But before she could open the door between them properly — before the ritual words, the candle, the single hour of speech that the craft allowed — something went wrong.

It began as a wrongness in the bone itself. A vibration, faint as a hairline crack in ice, running up through her fingers and into her wrists. Then the marrow *shivered*.

Dead men did not shiver. Dead men were past shivering, the way stones were past thirst. Marguerite had held charms through thunderstorms and earthquakes and one memorable night when a cow had wandered into Odile's herb garden and eaten half the wolfsbane, and never once had the dead inside them done anything but wait with the bottomless patience of their kind.

*Bertrand?*

The warmth in the bone guttered. When his presence returned, it was pressed flat against the far side of the marrow, small and cold, the way a child hides under a bed.

*They're coming,* he said. *Oh, oh — girl, they're coming, and we can feel them from here.*

*Who's coming?*

*Not the quiet ones.* His voice — and it was a voice now, spilling up through the carved spiral and out into the room, thin and urgent — *not our kind. The loud ones. The ones that were torn. Riders. So many riders, and they're wearing — I can't see colors, girl, I'm dead, but the others can, the ones in the older bones, and they say — they say there are no colors they know. No livery of any house. Grey. All grey, like ash, like—*

The charm cracked.

Not broke — cracked, a single fine line splitting the spiral from end to end, and every dead whisper within it came apart into a scream. It lasted only a heartbeat. It was enough. Marguerite was on her feet with her chair skidding backward across the flagstones before she knew she'd moved, the broken femur clutched in her fist, her heart going like a drum in a parade.

From the front room came the sound of Odile's teacup meeting its saucer with great precision.

"Marguerite," her mentor called, in the tone she used for everything from spilled ink to house fires, "come here, dear, and bring whatever it is you've just broken."

* * *

Odile Vasse was seventy-one years old and had the posture of a woman who intended to disapprove of things well into her nineties. She was small, sharp-faced, and wore her white hair in a braid coiled at her neck so tight it looked structural. She had taught Marguerite the craft for eleven years, and in all that time Marguerite had seen her frightened exactly twice: once during the rockslide that closed the eastern pass, and once when the tax assessor from the lowlands had tried to charge duty on imported candles.

This was the third time.

Marguerite set the cracked charm on the table between them and told it all — the shiver, the scream, the riders in colors no one knew. Odile listened without touching the bone, which was itself alarming; Odile touched everything, because Odile believed that information lived in surfaces and only cowardice kept hands in pockets.

When Marguerite finished, the old woman stood, went to the window, and pulled the curtain aside one inch.

"The pass road?" Marguerite asked.

"No." Odile let the curtain fall. "They'd come up the river track, if they're clever. Which inquisitors usually are."

The word landed in the room like a stone dropped into still water. Inquisitors. Marguerite had heard it the way you hear the names of distant storms — King Aldric's hunt for the gifted, the edicts read aloud at market days in the lowland towns, the stories traders told with lowered voices and then laughed off as exaggeration. Hollowmere had always been safe from such things. That was the entire point of the valley. Its founders had built it inside a fold of the world where magic thinned to nothing, where a bone-carver's whispers faded to silence at the borders, where the gifted had hidden for nine generations behind fog and superstition and the lowlanders' own conviction that nothing worth stealing lived up here.

"They can't find us," Marguerite said. It came out steadier than she felt. "No one finds us."

"Nothing finds us, dear. We are found anyway, eventually, by everyone." Odile was already moving, and it was the quality of her moving that frightened Marguerite most — not hurrying, not panicking, but *efficient*, the way she moved when a patient was dying and there was no time left for gentleness. She swept the knitting from her chair and knelt before the hearth, dragging out a wooden chest Marguerite had never once seen opened.

"What are you doing?"

"Burning papers."

"You're — *what?*"

"You heard me." Odile lifted out an armful of ledgers, letters, and loose pages tied with string, and fed the first ledger into the fire without ceremony. The cover curled. Marguerite caught a glimpse of the title page — *Registry of the Gifted, Hollowmere Valley, Vol. III* — before the flames took it.

"That's eleven years of names!" Marguerite crossed the room and grabbed her mentor's wrist. "Every family, every child we've trained, every—"

"Every name Aldric's grey riders would very much like to have read aloud." Odile did not pull her wrist free, but she stopped burning, and looked up, and her eyes were dry and terribly clear. "Yes. I know what I'm burning, child. Sit down."

Marguerite sat. The fire ate the ledger with a soft, greedy sound.

"I've known this day might come since before you were born," Odile said, feeding in another armful, page by page now, deliberately, watching each name catch. "Your grandmother knew. Her mentor knew. We keep the valley quiet and the borders dull and we pray the world stays lazy. But kings who break covenants need scapegoats, and gifted folk make such *convenient* ones." She glanced at Marguerite sidelong. "You felt the riders through a charm. Through the *dead*. Do you understand how unusual that is?"

"It means the dead are frightened."

"It means the dead are *loud*, which they haven't been since the covenant was struck, and it means your gift has grown teeth whether you wanted them or not." Another page into the fire. A child's name, Marguerite thought. Little Perrin, who heard his grandmother in the wind chimes. "If they take records, they take leverage. If they take leverage, they take the valley. So the records go first, and then—" Odile paused, and for the first time her voice did something complicated. "Then we discuss what happens to you."

"Nothing happens to me. I'll hide in the caves below the mill, we've rehearsed this, you made me rehearse it every autumn like a fire drill—"

"And you'll do exactly that, if it comes to it." Odile wiped her hands on her apron, leaving ash streaks, and fixed Marguerite with a look that had ended many adolescent arguments before they began. "But first, tea. Dread is like weather, Marguerite. It arrives when it arrives, and shouting at the clouds does nothing, and the sensible thing is to put the kettle on and bring in the washing before it breaks."

It was, Marguerite would think later, the most Odile sentence ever spoken. So she put the kettle on. And while the water heated, she brought in the washing — the sheets stiffening already with cold, smelling of rain and rosemary — and the whole time her hands worked, some animal part of her brain was counting. How long to saddle the mule. How far to the mill caves. How much bread was in the crock.

Dread arriving gently, like weather. Just as her mentor said.

Just before the storm.

* * *

They drank the tea. Odile burned the last of the registry, then the correspondence, then — with visible regret — a folder of recipes annotated in three generations of handwriting ("the plum brandy dies with me, then," she said, "a genuine tragedy, arguably greater than the king's"). By full dark the chimney was smoking ash and secrets, and the cottage smelled of burnt paper and bergamot, an oddly domestic combination for the eve of a catastrophe.

Marguerite washed the cups. It seemed important to do ordinary things correctly.

"Tell me about the covenant," she said, drying her hands. "You've never told me about the covenant. Every time I ask, you say it's 'accounting,' and I've decided I no longer accept that answer."

Odile was standing at the window again, watching the dark where the river track would be, if the dark were honest enough to show roads. For a long moment Marguerite thought she wouldn't answer.

"When the valley was founded," Odile said at last, "the dead agreed to sleep. That was the price of this place — not the fog, not the folded borders. Those are just geography. The true wall is a bargain: the living of Hollowmere tend the graves and speak the names, and in exchange the dead consent to quiet. No whispers. No hauntings. Rest, real rest, given freely. That's why your charms barely work inside the valley, why you have to carve so deep and listen so hard. You're not breaking rules, dear. You're asking for exceptions, and the dead grant them because we've kept faith with them for nine generations." She turned. Firelight moved on her face. "Aldric broke the covenant in the capital. Tore it up to feed his war. And a torn covenant doesn't stay politely in the capital, any more than a torn dam stays politely upstream."

"So the dead waking — that's not because of me."

"No. Though it speaks *through* you, which will matter, later, in ways I genuinely cannot predict." Odile crossed to the chest by the hearth — emptied of papers now, light as a lie — and rested her hand on its lid. "There's one thing left in here that doesn't burn."

And that was when the dogs started.

Not the village dogs — those were miles downvalley, and besides, Hollowmere's dogs were famously undramatic, having achieved a collective agreement over the decades that barking was beneath their dignity. These were strangers' dogs, big-voiced, coming up the river track through the rain, and beneath their voices came another sound that raised every hair on Marguerite's arms though her gift was nearly silent here:

Hooves. Many hooves. Riding hard.

Odile's hand shot out and gripped Marguerite's arm, hard enough to bruise, and her face in the firelight had gone from pale to something beyond pale — the color of the bone Marguerite carved, the color of paper before it burns.

"Back door," Odile said quietly. "Now. Don't run for the mill — too far, they'd see you on the open ground. Go up the goat path to the shrine cave, and whatever happens, whatever you *hear* happening in this house — you do not come back down. Do you hear me? Whatever you hear."

"Odile—"

"*Whatever you hear.*" The old woman pushed her toward the door, and then, at the last moment, caught her sleeve and pressed something into her palm — small, angular, warm from the chest it had waited in. Marguerite looked down. A shard of pale stone, no bigger than a thumbnail, veined with lines that seemed almost like writing, almost like veins, almost like both.

"North," Odile whispered. "When you run — and you will run, child, I'm sorry for it — you run north. Find the free cities. And whatever the shard tells you, believe it faster than you believe me."

Outside, the hoofbeats reached the gate.

Inside, Odile Vasse straightened her apron, smoothed her braid, and turned to face her front door with the expression of a woman preparing to disapprove of something well into eternity.

Marguerite ran.
