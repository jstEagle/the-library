## 3. Riders in Grey

The trouble with hunting witches, Tomas Vael had decided somewhere around the fourth day of rain, was that it required you to be outside.

He sat his horse at the head of the column and watched the valley of Hollowmere open before them like a held breath. It was beautiful, which annoyed him. He had expected something grim — a place that sheltered the gifted ought to look guilty about it, all crooked trees and crows and low sky. Instead there were orchards going gold with late summer, a river the color of hammered tin, and smoke rising from chimneys in thin, domestic threads. Somewhere down there a woman was baking bread. Somewhere down there a woman was carving charms out of dead men's bones and listening to corpses talk, and she was probably also baking bread, because evil never announced itself; it smelled like yeast.

"Orders say alive," said Sergeant Brakke, riding up beside him. Brakke was a large man built along the lines of a well-fed door, with a mustache he oiled daily and a soul Tomas suspected had been repossessed years ago. "Alive and unspoiled. The Lord Inquisitor's words."

"I can read, Sergeant."

"Just checking, Captain. You've got that face."

"What face?"

"The one where you're about to ruin somebody's afternoon."

Tomas did not answer, mostly because Brakke was correct. He had spent eleven years in the grey coat of the King's Inquisition and had developed, over those years, a private taxonomy of orders. There were orders that were merely tedious. There were orders that were cruel but comprehensible. And then there were orders like *take the bone-carver alive*, which were neither — they were careful, the way a man is careful with a tool he intends to use until it breaks. Aldric's hunt had swept up forty-one of the gifted since midsummer. Thirty-nine had gone to the capital in wagons. Two had gone in boxes. Nobody had explained what made the bone-carver worth the extra effort, and nobody would, because asking questions in the Inquisition was like asking a river to run uphill: technically possible, deeply unwise.

He put his heels to his horse and led them down into the valley.

* * *

Hollowmere did not fight them. That was the first thing wrong with it.

Tomas had taken towns before. Towns screamed, or went silent and slammed their shutters, or — once, memorably, in a fishing village whose name he'd made a point of forgetting — threw fish at him. Hollowmere simply opened its gates, such as they were, and stood in its doorways watching the grey riders pass with an expression he could not immediately name. It took him half the ride down the high street to identify it. Pity. They looked at the Inquisition with pity, the way you might look at men wading into a river to catch smoke.

"They know we're coming for someone," Brakke muttered. "Why aren't they running?"

"Because they think we can't do anything," Tomas said. "Look at them. They're not brave. They're *certain*."

It unsettled him more than resistance would have. He had a theory, old and unexamined, that fear was the load-bearing wall of the whole enterprise — that the hunt worked because people were afraid, and if a valley full of the gifted declined to be afraid, then the entire architecture of his life was standing on nothing. He pushed the thought away the way you push away a bill that hasn't come due yet.

They found the shrine by mid-afternoon. It sat on a knoll above the village: a low stone building with a turf roof, ringed by wooden posts, each post crowned with a small carved figure. Bones, he realized as he dismounted. Not decorative carvings — actual bone, polished smooth by weather and handling, dozens of little faces looking down at him with expressions of mild, patient interest. One of them, he could have sworn, was smirking.

"This is it?" Brakke said. "This is the great den of sorcery? Looks like my grandmother's garden shed."

"Your grandmother kept bones on posts?"

"She kept cats. Same energy."

Tomas almost smiled. That was the trap of Brakke, and of the whole grey machine: it was staffed by ordinary men who told jokes, who shared tobacco, who had mothers. Evil, when you lived inside it, did not look like a black tower. It looked like a workday.

Inside the shrine it was cool and dim and smelled of beeswax and dried herbs. Shelves lined every wall, and every shelf held charms — hundreds of them, carved from bone and antler and river stone, each one tagged with a scrap of paper in a careful hand. He picked one up. The tag read: *For Maren Ostrow, to speak with her husband, three nights only, then let him rest.* Below it, smaller: *She says thank you. He says the fence needs mending.*

Tomas stood there holding a dead man's opinion about a fence, and felt something shift in his chest — not pain exactly, more the sensation of ice making its first sound before it cracks.

"Captain!" A voice from outside, young and overexcited. Corporal Dennet, sixteen if he was a day, bursting through the door with his torch already lit. "We found the witch's mark! On the posts!"

"Dennet—"

"The Lord Inquisitor said cleanse any site of the craft—"

"Dennet, put it *out*."

But the boy had already touched flame to the nearest post, and dry summer wood caught the way dry summer wood does — greedily, all at once, with a sound like applause. Within a minute the whole ring was burning, and the little carved faces were blackening and splitting, and Tomas stood in the doorway of a house of grief and watched it happen and did not stop it. That was the thing he would remember later, in the long insomniac arithmetic of his life: not that he failed to act, but that he watched, catalogued, and wrote it down inside as *regrettable but within commission*. Eleven years of training, and his first instinct had been paperwork.

The villagers came up the hill while the posts burned. They didn't shout. An old man took off his hat. A woman held her children back by the shoulders, gently, the way you hold back someone from a grave. And in the middle of them stood a tall woman with white streaks in her dark hair — Odile, though he didn't know her name yet — and she looked at Tomas across the fire with an expression of such level, unhurried sorrow that he felt flayed by it.

"You burn the doors between us," she said. Her voice carried easily; she didn't raise it. "Do you know how many of your own dead you just locked out? Soldiers. Boys. Men who died calling for their mothers." She shook her head slowly. "You think this protects the living. It only orphans the dead."

"We have our orders," Brakke said, because Brakke always said it, the way other men said *good morning*.

"Yes," the woman said. "I imagine you do."

* * *

That night they camped in the village square, because the Inquisition camped where it pleased; that was rather the point of the Inquisition. Tomas sat apart from the fires, cleaning a sword that did not need cleaning, and listened to his men.

"—swear on my mother, it moved. The charm. I set it down and it turned to face me."

"It's bone, Dennet. Bone doesn't turn."

"This one did. Little smirk on it and everything."

"You've been drinking."

"One drink. And it *turned*, Rostik."

Tomas should have corrected them. Should have reminded them that superstition was the enemy's currency, that the gifted worked through suggestion and sleight and the credulity of tired men. That was the doctrine. He had recited it a hundred times. Instead he found himself thinking about the tag in the shrine. *He says the fence needs mending.* Whoever had written that had known the dead man. Had known him well enough to report his opinions on fencing. That wasn't sorcery as the catechisms described it — no pacts, no corruption, no rot. That was a widow being allowed to hear her husband complain about a fence one last time, and Tomas could not, try as he might, locate the crime.

The problem, he was beginning to understand, was that he had joined the Inquisition at nineteen for reasons that had seemed structural at the time. His father had died in the border wars, and grief had needed somewhere to go, and the recruiters were very good at offering angry boys a shape for their anger: *the gifted steal the voices of your dead, boy. They traffic in what should rest.* It had sounded like justice then. It had sounded like justice for eleven years, right up until tonight, when a valley full of the gifted had looked at him with pity and an old woman had told him he was orphaning the dead, and the terrible thing — the thing that sat in his chest now like a swallowed stone — was that she hadn't sounded like a liar. She'd sounded like a doctor delivering a diagnosis he'd been avoiding for a decade.

"Captain." Brakke dropped onto the bench beside him, uninvited, and handed him a cup of wine. "You've got the face again."

"I don't have a face."

"You've got a whole collection. This one's new. This one's the one where you're about to do something stupid and noble." Brakke drank. "Don't. Stupid and noble gets men killed, and worse, it gets me writing reports."

"Whose side are you on, Sergeant?"

"Yours, obviously. Which is why I'm telling you not to be stupid and noble." He nodded toward the darkened hillside where the shrine still smoked. "The old woman. The Lord Inquisitor wants the bone-carver, but the scouts say the old one's the master here. Teacher, whatever. We take her tomorrow, question her, get a name and a direction. Clean work. Then we go home and you stop making that face."

"And if she doesn't give a name?"

Brakke shrugged, vast and comfortable. "Then the Lord Inquisitor's people ask louder. Not our problem. We deliver, we don't unpack." He drained his cup and stood. "Get some sleep, Captain. You look like a man being haunted, and in this line of work that's either a liability or a promotion."

He lumbered off toward the fires, and Tomas sat alone in the dark with his wine and his stone-swallowed chest.

Haunted. It was meant as a joke. But the word stuck, because that was precisely what he was, in the most literal sense available to a man who officially did not believe in ghosts: haunted by a fence. Haunted by a scrap of paper in a careful hand. Haunted by the old woman's voice saying *you only orphan the dead*, and by the fact that some traitorous cellar of himself had answered, quietly, *she's right.*

He thought about the order again. *Take the bone-carver alive.* Alive and unspoiled. Tools were kept unspoiled. People were kept alive. The distinction mattered, and he had spent eleven years not noticing it, and now he noticed it, and noticing, he discovered, was not a thing you could un-do. It was like the crack in ice: silent, hairline, invisible to anyone standing safely ashore — but the man standing on it heard it perfectly, and knew, in the wordless way of the body, that the lake beneath him was very deep and very cold.

Tomorrow they would take the old woman. Tomorrow he would stand in his grey coat and watch it done, and write his report, and call it regrettable but within commission.

Unless.

He looked up at the hills, where the shrine smoked faintly against the stars, and past it, deeper into the valley, where a single window still burned gold in a farmhouse at the valley's far end. The scouts had marked it on the map. *Likely residence of the bone-carver.*

One window, lit late. Someone down there was awake, working — carving, maybe, or packing, or simply sitting with a lamp and dreading the morning, the way he was.

Tomas finished the wine, set down the cup, and made himself a promise so small it barely qualified as one: tomorrow, when it happened, he would watch. Truly watch. And if the crack spread — if the ice gave — then he would find out, once and for all, what kind of man stood underneath the uniform.

Across the square, Corporal Dennet shivered violently in his sleep and muttered something about a smirking bone.

In the farmhouse at the valley's end, the lamp went out.
