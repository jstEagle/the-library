## 4. What Odile Knew

Odile was burning papers in the kitchen when I woke, which was the second strange thing about that morning. The first was that the kettle hadn't been put on, and in forty years of shared roof and shared work, Odile Vane had never once let a morning pass without tea. She said it was the only civilized ritual left to the living. She said it twice a day, minimum, and usually with an accusation in it — *Marguerite, the kettle, are your hands decorative?*

So I stood in the doorway in my nightdress, watching my mentor feed her life's correspondence to the stove, and I said, "You've forgotten the kettle."

"I haven't forgotten anything," she said, without turning around. "That's rather the problem."

The firelight did unkind things to her face. Odile was seventy-one and wore her years the way she wore her shawls — draped, defiant, slightly askew. But that morning she looked older than her years. She looked like a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like the sum of.

"Papers," I said. "You're burning papers. You've told me a hundred times that paper is the poor man's memory and burning it is a small murder."

"I've told you a hundred times not to repeat everything I say, it makes me sound like a fool with a following." She fed another page to the flames — I caught the corner of a sketch, a charm design, the one for speaking with drowned kin — and watched it curl. "Marguerite. Go and dress. Properly. Boots, not slippers. The ones that lace."

"Odile—"

"The laced ones," she said, in the voice she used on apprentices who tried to carve against the grain. "And put your hair up. And don't argue, because I am old, I am right, and I have already had my tea."

She hadn't. The kettle sat cold on its hook. I didn't say so. Some lies are mercy, and Odile had taught me that too, though she'd have denied it.

I dressed. I laced the boots. I put my hair up with shaking hands and came back down to find her sweeping ash into the grate with a broom, humming — actually humming — an old valley tune about a miller's daughter, the one with the rude verse everyone sang and no one admitted to knowing.

"Tell me what's happening," I said.

"Riders," she said. "Coming up the valley road. Wearing grey, and no colors I know, and no colors any of us know, which in Hollowmere is the most frightening thing a stranger can wear." She set the broom aside. "The dead told you first. I heard you in the night, talking in your sleep. You said *riders* three times, and then you said a word I didn't recognize, and then you wept. Do you remember?"

I didn't. But the charm — the cracked charm from the widow's ritual, the one whose fracture had screamed — I had wrapped it in linen and put it in the drawer where I put things I couldn't bear to look at. I went to it now. Through the cloth I could feel it, faint as a heartbeat through a coat sleeve. Still whispering.

"They're close," I said.

"They were close at midnight. They stopped at the ford — horses won't cross the mere after dark, even grey horses, even grey men — and they made camp, and now they're breakfasting, and now they're coming." Odile said it the way she announced the weather. Rain by noon. Frost by week's end. Soldiers by breakfast. "Which gives us perhaps an hour. Perhaps less. Sit down."

I sat. She took both my hands in hers, and her hands — I had held them a thousand times over a thousand charms, guiding my knife, steadying my breath — were cold.

"Marguerite Quintana," she said. "You were eleven years old when you came to me, all elbows and grief, and you tried to steal a chicken from my yard."

"I was not stealing it. I was negotiating with it."

"You were losing to it. It had you pinned against the fence. I took you in because any child who can be bested by a hen and still come back the next day has either no sense at all or iron in her, and I've made my living telling the difference." Her thumb moved over my knuckles, back and forth, the way she soothed frightened spirits. "You have iron in you. I have known it thirty years. Today you're going to need every scrap."

"Odile, you're frightening me."

"Good. Fear laces boots faster than courage."

She crossed to the shelf above her workbench — the shelf I had dusted every week for thirty years and never once been allowed to touch beyond the dusting — and took down the clay jar with the chipped lid. Inside, wrapped in wool gone soft with age, was a shard of pale stone. Not quite white, not quite grey. It caught the firelight and gave something back that wasn't quite light. When she set it in my palm, the kitchen went quiet in a way kitchens cannot go quiet. The fire still burned but made no sound. The wind outside still moved but had nothing to say.

The dead, everywhere in Hollowmere, had stopped to listen.

"What is it?" I whispered.

"Part of the keystone," she said. "The keystone of the covenant, child. The old bargain. The reason this valley is quiet and the dead here sleep easy and the magic that would swallow your gift slides off us like rain off oiled wool. Do you remember when you asked me why Hollowmere was founded, and I said it was founded by tired people?"

"You say that every Founding Day."

"I say it because it's true and because the full truth is a Founding Day sermon I have spent my whole life hoping never to give." She closed my fingers over the shard with both her hands. "The valley was founded to shield the gifted. But the quiet — the true quiet, the peace the dead rest in — that isn't geography, Marguerite. That's a promise. A covenant between the living and the dead, sealed at the Spire at the world's edge, and held together by a keystone. And this is a piece of it."

"Why do you have a piece of it?"

"Because someone has to," she said, "and I was younger once, and braver than I looked, and someone I loved very much trusted me." For a moment her eyes went somewhere I couldn't follow — somewhere far north, I'd have said, if you'd pressed me, somewhere cold and high — and then came back. "It has been my honor and my burden for fifty years. This morning it becomes yours. I'm sorry. I had hoped to leave it to you in a will, with a nice long letter explaining everything, and a list of complaints about your carving technique."

"Odile—"

"Run north," she said. "Out of the valley, past the mere, up the old shepherd road. Don't stop for anyone, don't charm anyone, don't carve anything — your gift is louder outside the valley and they will hear it like a bell. Keep the shard against your skin. It will muffle you. And when you can, find the free cities, and find someone who remembers what I'm about to tell you, because I only know the half of it."

"The half of what?"

"The covenant is broken." The words came out of her like teeth. "Or breaking. The dead outside these borders have been restless for years — you've felt it, in every charm carved from bone that came from beyond the mere, all that pressure, all that whispering. I told you it was the world being the world. I lied. Something is feeding on them, Marguerite. Something is *using* them. And if grey riders have come to Hollowmere, then whoever is doing it has finally remembered we exist."

I heard it then — faint, far off, at the very edge of hearing. Hooves. Not many. A handful. Coming up the valley road at a walk, unhurried, the way men move when they believe nothing in the world can stop them.

Odile heard it too. She didn't flinch. She straightened her shawl, which was the most Odile thing she could possibly have done, and looked at me the way she looked at a finished charm — checking for flaws, finding none worth mentioning.

"The back door," she said. "Through the herb garden, over the wall, into the reeds. The mere will hide you till noon if you keep low. Then the shepherd road. North. Do you hear me? North."

"You're not coming."

"I'm going to answer the door," she said, "because it's my door, and I am an old woman, and old women answering doors are the last innocence left in this world. If I run, they chase us both and catch us both. If I stay, they get one tired carver of no particular importance and a house full of ash." She smiled, and it was a real smile, warm as the stove, and it broke my heart in a place I didn't know hearts could break. "Besides. Someone has to tell them nothing. I'm very good at it. Ask anyone."

"Odile, please—"

"Marguerite." She took my face in her cold hands. "Thirty years ago I took in a child who wouldn't give up a fight with a chicken. Don't you dare give up this one. Run north. Live. That's not an order. Orders are for soldiers. It's a request, from someone who loves you, and you will honor it because you have iron in you and because I said *please*, which I have said perhaps four times in my life and I'll not make it five."

The hooves were closer. I could hear voices now, low and disciplined, and the creak of leather, and beneath it all — beneath everything — the shard in my hand was whispering, and the whisper was a woman's voice, and the woman was afraid.

I kissed her cheek. She smelled of ash and lavender and fifty years of tea.

"North," I said.

"North," she agreed. "And Marguerite — the rude verse in the miller's song. It's about you. It was always about you. I want you to know I found it hilarious the entire time."

And then she pushed me, gently, toward the back door, and turned to answer the front one.

* * *

I did not go out the back door. I want that on the record of whatever court judges my life. I went as far as the herb garden, I put my hand on the wall, and then the front door opened and I heard the voices, and I stayed — crouched in the rosemary like a fool, like a child, with a shard of the covenant keystone burning cold against my palm.

There were six of them. Grey riders, grey cloaks, grey everything, and at their head a man I would come to know far too well — tall, dark-haired, with a face that was trying very hard to be stone and not entirely succeeding. He carried a commission scroll in a black case and he held it the way a man holds a thing he's already begun to doubt.

"Odile Vane?" he said.

"Who's asking?" said Odile. "Only I get a great many callers, and I like to sort them by whether they're worth tea."

"By order of King Aldric, I have a warrant for the arrest of the bone-carver of Hollowmere."

"Then you've had a long ride for nothing. I'm the bone-carver. Well — bone-*worker*, if we're being precise, and we're never being precise in this valley, it's considered showing off. I carve buttons. Spoons. Little birds for children's windowsills. Would you like a little bird? I have a lovely wren."

"Madam." A pause. I could hear him choosing his words like a man crossing ice. "We seek a carver of charms. Of... speaking pieces."

"Speaking pieces." Odile's voice went up with magnificent innocence. "You mean whistles."

"Madam—"

"Because I do make whistles. Out of bone. They speak, of a fashion — a sort of *wheeee* — children adore them—"

"Madam, I am authorized to use force."

"And I am authorized to be seventy-one and unarmed and standing in my own kitchen, which I find is authorization enough to say whatever I like. Come in, then, if you're coming. Mind the step. And the cat — well, there's no cat, but there's usually a cat, and it's the principle of the thing."

I heard them enter. I heard chairs. I heard the terrible, gentle sound of an interrogation conducted by a man who did not yet know he was a monster and an old woman who knew exactly what she was. He asked his questions. Where is the carver. Who has she taught. What has she made. And Odile — Odile told him about buttons. Odile told him about spoons. Odile told him, at length and with what I swear was genuine technical detail, about the proper curing of chicken bone, and I pressed my fist to my mouth in the rosemary and wept and laughed and hated myself for both.

Then the tone changed. Someone — not the tall one, one of the others, a voice like a wet rope — said something I didn't catch, and the tall one said, "That won't be necessary," and the wet-rope voice said it *would*, and then there was a crash, and a cry that was not Odile's, and then a sound I will not write down.

When I crept to the window, they were dragging her out. Her hands were bound. There was blood at her temple and she was — she was *laughing*, gods help her, laughing at something, and the tall one was walking beside her saying, quietly, "You don't have to make this harder," and Odile saying, "Harder? Captain, I'm seventy-one, everything is harder, that's what seventy-one *means*—"

And then, as they passed the herb garden — as they passed *me*, hidden in the rosemary with her inheritance cutting a circle of cold into my palm — Odile Vane turned her head, found my eyes through the leaves, and winked.

They put her in a wagon. They rode south, out of the valley, toward the capital, toward whatever they did to the gifted in the capital. And I stayed in the garden because she had told me to run and I had not yet found the courage to obey her, and because some part of me still believed — gods, I *believed* — that she would talk her way out of it. That she would bore them to death with buttons. That she would come home and put the kettle on and say, *well, that was tiresome, where were we?*

She never came home.

I learned later — much later, from the tall one himself, who carried it the way soldiers carry shrapnel — what happened in the questioning. How they asked her, over and over, for the name of her student. How she gave them the names of everyone in the valley, the miller's sons, the widow Bramble, the reeve, the priest, a chicken she described as a known associate. How the wet-rope man grew inventive, and Odile grew *funnier*, and how there is a kind of courage that looks so much like insolence that cruel men mistake it for weakness right up until it kills them with shame.

How she died in the end refusing to name me, and how her last recorded words, spoken to a room full of grey men, were:

"You can burn every paper in this valley and it won't matter. She's already carved. She's already *made*. And you cannot unmake what was made with love. Go north, if you like. Go anywhere. But you'll find she's ahead of you, and always will be."

I did not hear those words that morning. That morning I heard only the wagon wheels fading south, and the shard whispering against my palm, and the dead of Hollowmere — all of them, every soul the valley had ever sheltered — rising at once, not in anger but in something like salute, and then settling again into the quiet Odile had spent her life keeping.

Then I went over the wall.

North, she had said. I went north. I went north with a shard of the keystone, a knife, a loaf I stole from my own kitchen in a fit of practicality, and a grief so large I could not look at it directly, the way you cannot look at the sun.

I did not run fast enough. I will say that plainly, because the record should be plain. I was thirty years old and I had iron in me and I had been trained my whole life to move quietly through a world that listened, and I still did not run fast enough. The valley road bent at the standing stones. The grey riders had left two men behind to watch it.

But that is the next part of the story, and I was not the one who told it first.

What matters is this: Odile Vane pressed a shard of the covenant keystone into my hand and told me to run north, and I ran, and everything that followed — the wagon, the prison, the queen in the stone, the Spire, the terrible arithmetic of memory — everything grew from that single breath, from her cold hands closing my fingers over the stone, from the last thing she ever gave me.

She died refusing to name me.

I have spent every day since trying to be worth the silence.

* * *

The two men at the standing stones were not good at their work. I want that recorded too, for balance, because the grey riders were many things and incompetent in only a few, but watching from cover was one of them. I had gotten within forty paces, wrapped in a hedge-shadow with the shard muffling whatever noise my gift made, when the taller of the two said, conversationally, to the other:

"Do you hear that?"

And the other said: "Hear what?"

"Exactly," said the first, and turned, and looked directly at the hedge where I was hiding.

I ran.

The valley opened ahead of me — the mere flat and silver in the morning light, the shepherd road climbing north into hills I had seen every day of my life and never once crossed — and behind me a whistle blew, sharp and thin, a whistle made of bone, and I remember thinking, absurdly, *that's good craftsmanship, that carries beautifully* —

— and then the world tilted, and a hand closed on my arm, and the quiet valley of Hollowmere, which had held me like a cupped palm for thirty years, let me go.
