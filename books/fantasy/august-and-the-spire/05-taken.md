## 5. Taken

I had always imagined that if I ever left Hollowmere, it would be the way pilgrims left: on foot, at dawn, with bread in my satchel and Odile's blessing pressed to my forehead like a coin. I would walk out through the western gate where the birches grew crooked from leaning toward something they couldn't name, and the valley would let me go the way a mother lets go of a child's hand at a schoolhouse door — reluctantly, but with pride.

Instead, I left bound at the wrists, in the back of a supply cart, between a barrel of salted herring and a man who smelled like both.

The rope around my wrists was new. I could tell because it still had that raw-hemp sweetness, and because I kept worrying at it the way you worry at a loose tooth — not to escape, exactly, but because the alternative was thinking about what had happened, and my mind refused that the way a horse refuses a bridge.

Odile was dead. I knew this the way you know weather is coming: not by seeing it, but by the ache behind my eyes, the pressure in the marrow of me. The keystone shard she'd pressed into my palm was hidden now in the lining of my left boot, wrapped in a strip of her shawl, and every time the cart jolted over a stone I felt it shift against my ankle like a small, insistent heartbeat.

*Run north*, she'd said. *Don't look back. Don't be clever.*

I had run north for perhaps four hundred paces before the cleverness failed me entirely. In my defense — and I offered no defense aloud, because the grey-cloaked men did not seem interested in hearing one — I had never been good at running. I was a carver. My hands were my legs, in a sense. And my hands were tied.

So here I was, riding out of the only home I'd ever known as cargo.

* * *

Hollowmere does not let you leave easily. This is not sentiment; it is physics, or whatever word the old books used for the thing that physics wishes it were. The valley was founded on a hollowing — a great scooping-out of magic, generations ago, when our ancestors decided that the gifted should have one place in the world where their gifts could rest. Inside the borders, the dead are quiet. Charms hold their shape. Whispers stay whispers.

Outside the borders, everything I had spent my life learning to muffle came back all at once.

It happened at the boundary stone, the old moss-eaten marker shaped vaguely like a kneeling woman, though centuries of rain had worn her down to more of a crouching suggestion. The cart rolled past her, and I felt it — the world pressing in against my skin like water against a diver's ears. Not pain, exactly. Pressure. Old wounds remembering they were wounds.

The dead, who inside the valley had been a murmur so constant I'd stopped hearing it — the way you stop hearing your own blood — suddenly became a crowd.

They weren't shouting. That was the terrible part. They were *talking*. All of them, everywhere, in the low conversational register of people who have been waiting a long time to be heard and have given up expecting it. A woman two fields past the stone, murmuring about a loom she'd never finished warping. A soldier under the road itself, counting his marching days over and over like prayer beads. A child somewhere north, humming a song with no words left in it.

Inside Hollowmere, I had spent twenty-six years learning to turn the volume of the world down. Outside it, someone had turned every dial to its limit and then sat on the controls.

"Carver." One of the grey men rode alongside the cart. He was young, with the kind of face that hadn't yet decided what it believed, and he kept looking at me the way people look at a stove they've been told is hot. "You're pale."

"I'm always pale," I said. "It's the profession. Bone dust gets into everything, including, apparently, the complexion."

He didn't laugh. None of them laughed. I was beginning to suspect that humor had been confiscated along with my carving knives, which stung more than the ropes, honestly. Those knives had been my mother's mother's, and the handles were worn to the exact shape of a Quintana grip, and some sergeant with hands like fence posts had stuffed them into an evidence sack without even looking at them.

I decided, in the manner of people deciding small defiant things to keep from drowning in large ones, that I would hate him specifically and forever.

* * *

The road north wound up out of the valley through pine country, and with every mile the pressure grew. By midday I had learned to sort the dead the way you learn to sort voices at a crowded market — not listening to any one, but mapping the whole. There were layers. The freshest dead spoke loudest and made the least sense, still tangled in the grammar of dying. The old ones had settled into repetition, saying the same few things until the words wore smooth as river stones. And beneath all of it, faint as a note held under water, there was something else — a wrongness in the pattern, a discord, like a choir where one voice has been taught to sing against the others.

I didn't know yet what it was. I want to be honest about that. I lay in the swaying cart among the herring barrels and I thought: *the dead are louder than Odile ever said, and they are not just sad, they are* ***arranged*** *somehow, and I don't know by whom.*

The shard against my ankle pulsed once, softly, like a hand squeezing mine.

"Hush," I whispered, so quietly it was barely breath. "I know."

The young rider glanced over. "Talking to the fish?"

"The fish and I have an understanding."

That time, almost — *almost* — the corner of his mouth moved. He turned away quickly, as though the movement had cost him something, and I filed him away in the small cabinet I keep for people who might be more than their uniforms. It was a nearly empty cabinet. But it existed.

* * *

We stopped at dusk in the ruins of a waystation — a coaching inn burned years ago, its chimney standing alone in a field of nettles like a monument to somebody's supper. The soldiers built a fire and ate hard sausage and argued about dice. They gave me sausage too, which surprised me, and untied my hands long enough for me to eat, which surprised me more. The man with the fence-post hands — Sergeant Brakk, according to the shouts — watched me the entire time with the expression of a man watching a cat he'd been told might be a demon.

"You really hear them?" he asked finally. It was the first thing he'd said directly to me. His voice was surprisingly gentle, the way big men's voices sometimes are, as if the gentleness had nowhere else to fit.

"I hear the dead," I said. "Yes."

"And what do they say? Generally."

I looked at the fire. Beyond it, past the nettles, the evening was filling up with whispers — dozens of them, hundreds, the unquiet residue of a countryside that had seen too many wars to count its graves properly.

"Generally," I said, "they say *it hurts*. Or they say *I'm lost*. Or they ask after someone specific — a wife, a son, whether the harvest came in the year they missed." I turned the sausage in my fingers. "Once, a man spent three hours asking me to tell his brother the joke he'd died before finishing. I never learned the punchline. He couldn't remember it either. He just remembered that there had been one, and that his brother had needed to hear it."

Brakk chewed slowly. "And you do that? Deliver messages?"

"When I can. It's most of the work, honestly. People think bone-carving is curses and portents. Mostly it's post."

Something happened to his face then — a small collapse, like a wall losing a single brick. He looked at the fire and said nothing for a long while, and I understood that somewhere behind his eyes a letter was being written that he would never send, or had already failed to send, or had sent and been too afraid to think about since.

"They told us you were dangerous," he said at last. "Corrupted. That the gift rots a person."

"It's not a gift," I said. "It's a trade. Like anything. You give something, you get something." I flexed my stiff fingers. "What I give is mostly sleep."

He almost smiled again. Then the young rider called something about the watch rotation, and the brick went back into the wall, and Brakk stood up and was a sergeant again, and I was a prisoner again, and the fire crackled on between us like a conversation neither of us could finish.

* * *

I did not sleep. Prisoners in stories always sleep, exhausted and tear-streaked, and wake with resolve. I lay awake instead, listening to the dead grow louder as the night deepened, and somewhere past midnight I heard the discord again — that wrong note beneath the chorus — and this time it was closer, or I was closer to it, and it had a texture I recognized.

It sounded like hunger.

Not the hunger of the restless dead, which is a longing hunger, a homesick hunger. This was appetite. Something feeding. Something that had found a way to make the dead's unrest into fuel, the way a mill makes flour of wheat, and the millstone of it was turning somewhere far to the south, in the direction of the capital, and every whisper I heard was grain going under the stone.

The shard pulsed against my ankle. Harder this time. Almost urgent.

"All right," I breathed into my collar. "All right. I'm listening."

And there — threading up through the boot leather, through the wool wrapping, faint as light through a shut door — came a voice that was not one of the field-dead. It was clear. Composed. It had the particular authority of someone accustomed to being obeyed, worn now thin as gilding on old wood.

A woman's voice. Younger than Odile's, older than mine, and cold in a way that made me think of winter palaces and locked doors.

"*So,*" it said. "*You're the one carrying my stone.*"

I lay very still among the herring barrels, wrists raw, home shrinking behind me mile by mile, and answered the voice in my boot the only sensible way a bone-carver answers a ghost she's never met.

"Well," I whispered. "You'd better come out and introduce yourself, then. Because I don't work for strangers."

The voice laughed — short, sharp, genuinely startled — and somewhere far to the south a king who did not yet know my name felt, for no reason he could name, a chill walk up his spine.
