## 6. The Queen Who Wouldn't Rest

The wagon smelled of wet straw and old fear, which Marguerite supposed was fair, since both belonged to her.

They had been on the road for two days. The valley of Hollowmere was behind her now — she had stopped looking back somewhere past the second ridge, because every glance felt like pressing a bruise — and ahead the road unspooled through country she had never seen except in Odile's maps. Hills the color of old wool. Rivers that ran loud where Hollowmere's streams ran quiet. And everywhere, everywhere, the dead.

That was the thing no one warned you about, leaving the valley. In Hollowmere, magic could not follow, and neither could the whispers. The whole valley sat inside a silence so complete that Marguerite had spent her entire life mistaking it for emptiness. It was not emptiness. It was shelter. Out here, past the border stones, the world pressed against her like deep water, and the dead pressed back, hundreds of them, thousands, a low ceaseless murmur at the edge of hearing — *cold, cold, who will carry word to my wife, cold, I dropped it, I dropped my name somewhere, find my name* —

She had wedged herself into the corner of the wagon with her wrists chained loosely enough that the guard, a boy with chilblains and a guilty conscience, pretended not to notice. She kept her hands closed around nothing. Around the memory of nothing.

Because the shard was gone.

Odile had pressed it into her palm — *run north, little wren, run north and don't look back* — and Marguerite had run exactly as far as the goat path behind the smokehouse before the net took her legs out from under her. She'd had time for exactly one decision: swallow the shard, or let them find it. She had chosen swallowing, mostly because it was small and smooth and there wasn't time to be clever about it.

So now the keystone shard — the last piece of an ancient covenant, the thing a queen had died near, the thing Odile had bled out protecting — sat in Marguerite's stomach, doing absolutely nothing useful, and occasionally making her burp faintly of chalk.

"You could have told me it would taste like this," she muttered, to no one.

The dead murmured. They were not, as a rule, helpful.

* * *

It happened on the third night, camped in a hollow below a ruined waystation, the wagon's wheel clamped, two guards playing cards by a fire that smoked too much. Marguerite lay curled on the straw, hungry, aching, and so saturated with whispering dead that her skull felt like a kettle left too long on the heat.

And then, beneath all of it — beneath the *colds* and the *find-my-names* and the general gray clamor of the restless — a voice said, distinctly:

"Well. This is undignified."

Marguerite sat up so fast she cracked her head on the wagon's frame.

The voice came from inside her own chest. From lower than her chest. From, she realized with a lurch, the shard.

"Can you hear me?" the voice asked. It was a woman's voice, low and dry, with the particular clipped patience of someone accustomed to being obeyed and recently, catastrophically not. "Nod if you can hear me. Actually — don't nod. You'll look mad, and they'll take you off the road to some cell where I can't reach you. Blink twice."

Marguerite blinked twice.

"Good." A pause. "You swallowed me."

"I didn't know what else—" Marguerite began aloud, then caught the nearest guard glancing over and turned it into a coughing fit. When he looked away she whispered into her own collarbone: "I panicked. There were nets."

"There are always nets," said the voice, with the weariness of long experience. "My husband used to say a crown is just a net you agree to wear. He was wrong about most things but right about that." Another pause, shorter, sharper. "You're the bone-carver. The one they want alive."

"They killed my mentor," Marguerite whispered. "Odile. An old woman who never hurt anyone. They burned her shrine and they killed her, and they put me in this wagon as bait, and I don't even know your name, voice-in-my-ribs, so if you're going to haunt me, at least—"

"Isolde."

The word landed in the wagon like a dropped coin. Marguerite went still. Every child in the valley learned the name eventually, the way children learn the shapes of clouds: Queen Isolde, dead nine years, dead before Aldric ever wore the crown properly, dead in a hunting accident so convenient that even the ballads about it sounded embarrassed.

"You're the queen," Marguerite said.

"I was the queen. Now I'm a splinter of pale stone in the stomach of a carver of charms, which is a career trajectory I intend to discuss with whoever runs the afterlife, assuming anyone does." But under the dryness Marguerite heard something else — something taut and tired, like a wire strung too long. "You're shivering."

"I'm cold."

"You're frightened. Don't bother denying it, I've been dead nine years; I know fright when I hear it. It sounds like teeth." A pause. "I'm not going to hurt you. Whatever you've heard about ghosts — and I imagine Odile told you true things, she always did — I am not here to drag you anywhere or trade your eyes for passage. I'm here because you're carrying the only piece of me left, and because you're going north whether you like it or not, and because we may be the only two creatures in this kingdom who want Aldric Vane destroyed."

Marguerite's breath caught. "How do you know Odile?"

Another pause. Longer. When the voice came again, the dryness had thinned.

"Everyone who ever held a piece of the covenant knew Odile, in one way or another. She was the valley's keeper. The last of the keepers, now." Something moved through the words like weather. "I'm sorry. About how she died."

"She wouldn't give me up." Marguerite found her eyes stinging and hated it, here, in a cage, in front of a ghost. "They asked her where I'd gone and she just... smiled at them. Like they were slow students."

"That sounds like her." Quietly. Then, brisk again, the queen reassembling herself syllable by syllable: "Listen to me, bone-carver. We have perhaps an hour before the guards change watch, and there are things you must understand, because everyone is counting on you not understanding them. Are you listening?"

"I'm in a prison wagon talking to my own stomach," Marguerite said. "Listening is the only entertainment available."

For the first time, the voice laughed. It was a short laugh, surprised out of her, and it made her sound suddenly, terribly young.

"Good," said Queen Isolde. "You'll need that. Now. What do you know of the covenant?"

* * *

What Marguerite knew was what every child of Hollowmere knew, in the way children know the liturgy without knowing the meaning: that the valley was founded long ago by people who feared magic, and that the dead beyond its borders did not rest, and that this was simply the shape of the world, the way rivers ran downhill and winters came.

"Tell me what's wrong with that," she said.

"Everything," said Isolde. "It's backwards. The dead don't refuse to rest because they're wicked, or cursed, or hungry — though they are hungry, poor things, hungrier than the living will ever understand. They don't rest because the thing that lets them rest was shattered. Deliberately. Nine years ago."

"The covenant," Marguerite said slowly. "The keystone. Odile called it that. She only said one word about it before they took her. She said *keystone*, and then she said *north*, and then she bled."

"Then let me say the rest, since she paid for the right to say it." The voice drew itself up, and Marguerite had the strange sensation of a woman standing straighter inside her ribs. "There was a covenant, once. Older than any kingdom. Between the living and the dead. The living remembered the dead — truly remembered them, spoke their names, kept their places at the table, tended their graves — and in exchange, the dead rested. Not vanished. Rested. Slept the way a field sleeps under snow, waiting. The keystone was the heart of it, the anchor-stone, the promise made solid. As long as it stood whole, death was a door, not a wound."

"And Aldric broke it."

"Aldric broke it." Flat. Cold. "He didn't stumble into it, whatever his priests claim. He sought it out. He learned what I learned too late — that a broken covenant doesn't just leave the dead restless. It leaves them *available*. All that grief, all that unfinished longing, all those souls with nowhere to go — it pools, bone-carver. It pools like blood in a cupped hand. And power can drink from it."

Marguerite thought of the whispers pressing against her since the border stones. *Cold. Find my name.* Thousands of them. Tens of thousands.

"He's drinking them," she said, horrified.

"He's feeding on them. His armies march without supply lines because the dead carry their provisions. His war-machines run without coal. His wounds close overnight. He hasn't aged a day since the night he shattered the keystone — do you understand? He murdered me nine years ago and he has not grown one grey hair since, because he pays for his years with other people's deaths. With other people's *unfinished* deaths." The wire-strung tension in her voice pulled tighter. "Every soldier who falls in his wars doesn't rest. He gets harvested."

The wagon seemed suddenly very small. Marguerite wrapped her arms around her knees.

"Why tell me? Why me?"

"Because you're the first person in nine years to hold a piece of the keystone and hear me speak. Because the valley's silence kept you clean when every other gifted soul in this kingdom has been hunted down or bent to his service. Because Odile trusted you with the one thing she died protecting, and Odile's trust was worth more than my crown ever was." A pause. "And because I am running out of time, and so are you."

"What do you mean, you're running out of time?"

"I mean I am a splinter, not a soul. I cling to the stone the way frost clings to glass. Each year there is less of me. Soon there will be none, and the last witness to what Aldric actually did will be gone, and the ballads will win." Bitterness, then, sharp as vinegar. "He's very good at ballads. He wrote half of them himself. Did you know that? He composed the one about my hunting accident personally. He plays the harp badly and insists on performing it at every feast. I have spent nine years dead and that is still the single most humiliating fact of my existence."

Despite everything — despite the chains and the straw and the guards and the vast dark weight of the dead pressing in — Marguerite laughed. It escaped before she could stop it, a small cracked sound, and she smothered it in her sleeve.

"I'm glad my murder amuses you," said Isolde, without heat.

"It's not — I'm sorry. It's just that Odile used to talk like that. Like the world was ending but the jokes were still mandatory." Marguerite wiped her eyes with the back of her chained wrist. "She'd have liked you."

"No, she wouldn't. She thought I was a fool. She told me so, once, to my face, at a treaty signing, in front of three ambassadors." A beat. "She was right. That's rather the point of everything I'm about to ask of you."

The fire outside had burned low. One of the card players yawned; the other gathered the deck. Somewhere down the road, a wolf sang and got no answer, and Marguerite felt the dead lean toward the sound like flowers toward light, and it made her chest ache.

"What are you about to ask of me?" she said.

"North," said Isolde. "You were already going. Good. North, to the free cities, and then further — to the Spire, at the edge of the world, where the keystone was first forged. The shard in your belly is a piece of it. There are others. Scattered, hidden, guarded by people who remember the old promises — fewer every year, but some. If the keystone can be reforged, the covenant stands again. The dead rest. Aldric's armies starve, his machines die, his borrowed years come due all at once." The voice dropped, and for a moment it was not a queen speaking but a woman, tired and furious and afraid. "He knows this. It's why he hunts the gifted. It's why he wants *you*. Not because you carve charms for widows. Because you're the kind of person who might actually manage it."

"And if I refuse? If I just — stay in the wagon, go to the capital, keep my head down, survive?"

Silence. Marguerite braced for scorn.

"If you refuse, you'll live," Isolde said at last. "Perhaps. He finds the gifted useful, briefly. You'd spend your years pulling whispers out of bones for a man who eats the dead, until you were used up, and then you'd join the pool yourself, and someday some other frightened girl would hear you saying *cold, find my name*." A breath that was not a breath. "I won't pretend there's safety in the other road. There isn't. There's Odile's fate, or worse, most likely. I only ask that you choose with your eyes open. I chose mine shut, and it cost the world nine years."

Marguerite stared at the wagon's slatted ceiling, through which a few indifferent stars showed. She thought of Odile smiling at men with swords. She thought of the widow Marta, holding the charm of her husband's voice, laughing and crying at once. She thought of the murmur all around her, endless, patient, starving.

*Find my name.*

"I want one thing first," Marguerite said.

"Name it."

"When we do this — when I go north and play courier for a dead queen — nobody gets to pity me. Not you, not the free cities, not the ghosts. I've spent my whole life being the valley's sad little bone-carver, the orphan they feed and fret over, and I am done with it. If I'm going to be bait and thief and heretic, I'll be it standing up." She heard her own voice shake and kept talking anyway. "So don't call me brave. Don't call me poor girl. Just tell me what needs doing."

Another of those short, startled laughs. "Oh, Odile trained you well."

"She trained me to carve straight lines. The rest I learned myself."

"Then here is what needs doing," Isolde said. "Tomorrow the road forks at a burned chapel. The left fork goes to the capital and everything you fear. The right fork goes nowhere anyone important looks — abandoned quarry road, three days to the river country. Your guard, the young one with the cough — he sleeps deepest, and he dreams of home, and homesick men sleep like the dead. Which, these days, is saying something." The voice turned wry. "I can't unlock chains. I'm a splinter in a stomach. But I can tell you that the pin on your left cuff is worn nearly through, and that fear makes fingers stupid, and that you swallowed me once already, so we know you can act fast when it matters."

Marguerite flexed her left wrist. The pin rattled, loose as a milk tooth.

"And after?" she whispered. "After the quarry road?"

"After that, we steal back what was stolen — starting with the charms they took from your workshop, because there are voices in those bones that deserve better than an evidence locker. And then north. And then the Spire." A pause, and when Isolde spoke again, all the queen had gone out of her voice, leaving only the woman. "One more thing, bone-carver. Before you decide. Ask me why I'm really helping you. Go on."

Marguerite hesitated. "...Why are you really helping me?"

"Because I signed the original terms," Isolde said quietly. "Nine years ago, when the covenant was renegotiated — before Aldric, before any of this — I was on the council that set its price. And the price fell hardest on the ones with no one to speak for them. The unnamed dead. The paupers' graves. I signed, and I slept well, and I told myself it was the best bargain possible." The words came slow and level, like a confession rehearsed for nine years. "Aldric didn't break the world, child. He just stopped pretending to pay for it. So no — don't pity me, and I won't pity you. We'll owe each other instead. That's cleaner."

Outside, the fire collapsed into embers. The young guard with the cough rolled over in his bedroll and began, softly, to dream of home.

Marguerite closed her hand around the loose pin, and in her belly, the keystone shard hummed like a struck bell.

"All right, Your Majesty," she breathed. "Let's owe each other."
