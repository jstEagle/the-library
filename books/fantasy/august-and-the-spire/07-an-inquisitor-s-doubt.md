## 7. An Inquisitor's Doubt

The interrogation room in the garrison at Marrow's Ford had once been a tithe office, which explained the shelves. Someone had thoughtfully left the ledgers of grain collection bolted to the walls, and now they served as backdrops for confessions. Tomas Vane had spent three years watching frightened people confess against that backdrop of agricultural arithmetic, and he had developed a private theory that the ledgers were the most honest thing in the room.

Today's subject was nine years old.

Her name was Wren Halloway, according to the intake sheet, though she had not spoken since the soldiers brought her in from the mill village two days ago. She sat on a chair built for grown men with her feet swinging an inch above the floor, and she watched Tomas with the flat, patient stare of a cat deciding whether the thing approaching it was worth running from.

"Water?" he asked, setting a cup on the table between them.

She looked at the cup. She did not take it.

"You're allowed to drink," he said. "That's not a trick. If I wanted to trick you, I'd be better at it."

One corner of her mouth twitched — not quite a smile, but the memory of where smiles went. She took the cup with both hands and drank like a small bird, in quick sips, never taking her eyes off him.

Tomas pulled out his own chair and sat, unhurried, arranging his papers as though this were a meeting about fence lines. In truth he had read the intake report four times. *Subject observed speaking to well water. Subject claims grandmother answers. Grandmother deceased eleven months.* A miller's granddaughter who talked to wells. The hunt had come to this: children and their griefs, dragged into garrisons so that clerks could write *confirmed* or *unconfirmed* in columns, and the columns could be sent south to the capital, and no one would ever ask what happened to the confirmed ones after the ink dried.

Except Tomas was starting to ask.

"Wren," he said. "Do you know why you're here?"

She shook her head. Her hair was fine and brown and had been cut badly recently, in the way hair gets cut when there is no mother to do it properly.

"Do you know what an inquisitor does?"

Another shake. Then, quietly, with the terrible directness of children: "Do you?"

It was such a good question that Tomas laughed before he could stop himself — a short, surprised sound that made her flinch, and then, seeing that the flinch had been unnecessary, look faintly offended.

"That's fair," he said. "Honest answer? I ask questions. I write down what people tell me. I send the writing to important men in tall buildings." He tapped the blank report form on the table. "This is what I do it on. You'll notice it has very little room for anything true."

She frowned at the form. "It's got a box for 'manifestations.'"

"It does."

"What's manifestations mean?"

"It means—" He stopped. He had written the word *manifestations* perhaps six hundred times in three years, and no one had ever asked him what it meant, least of all the people filling the boxes in. "It means when something happens that shouldn't be able to happen. When the rules break."

"The well talks," Wren said. "Is that a rule breaking?"

"I don't know. Does it?"

She studied him for a long moment, doing the arithmetic of trust, and whatever sum she arrived at, she arrived at it the way children do — completely, without hedging. "It doesn't talk-talk. It's more like... if you put your ear on a train track. You can't hear words but you know someone's coming." She wrapped both hands around the cup again. "Gran always said the water remembers. She said everything remembers, that's the trouble with the world. Nothing knows how to forget."

Tomas kept his face still, because his face had been trained to stay still, but inside his chest something turned over slowly, like a body in sleep.

"And your grandmother," he said gently. "You talk to her through the well."

"She talks first, mostly." Wren's chin came up, defiant, braced for the thing that came next — the blow, the shout, the *confirmed*. "Go on, then. Write it down."

He looked at the form. The box waited. *Manifestations: yes / no / suspected.*

Here is what Tomas Vane knew, and had known for some months, in the way one knows a roof beam is cracked — knowing it every time the wind blows, saying nothing, sleeping under it anyway. He knew that the reports he sent south did not go into files. He knew that certain prisoners — the strong ones, the vivid ones, the ones whose gifts came off them like heat off a road — were transferred instead of released, and that the transfers went to places that did not appear on any map he was permitted to see. He knew that Aldric's war-machines ran without coal or men pulling ropes, and that the army moved at night now, and that the dead did not rest anywhere along the supply roads, and that when he had once said to his commander, half-joking, *the whole country's haunted*, Commander Brase had looked at him with something too serious for a joke and said, *yes, and thank the King for it.*

He knew that the hunt was not hunting witches. It was harvesting.

And he knew that if he wrote *confirmed* in that box, a nine-year-old girl who missed her grandmother would be loaded into a wagon with black curtains, and he would never learn what happened next, and some part of him would spend the rest of his life not learning it on purpose.

"Tomas?" Wren said. Because she had learned his name from the soldiers' talk, the way children collect everything.

He picked up the pen. His hand did not shake; three years of training saw to that. What shook was somewhere deeper, in the marrow, where the training didn't reach.

*Unconfirmed,* he wrote. *Subject exhibits grief-related fantasy behavior common to bereaved children. Recommend release to kin. No further action warranted.*

His pen hovered over the signature line. Then, because a lie is only as good as its details, he added: *Well investigated. Water ordinary.*

"There," he said, and blotted it, and turned the paper over so she couldn't read it, which was foolish, because she couldn't have read it anyway, and because turning it over was really for his own benefit — proof that the thing existed, done, irreversible.

Wren watched him do it. "You're not sending me away?"

"No."

"To the bad place?"

Tomas went cold. "What bad place?"

"The soldiers said." Her voice dropped to a whisper, as if the walls had ears, which — he thought of the ledgers, of the garrison's night wagons — they might. "They said gifted ones go south to serve the King. My gran said serving the King is how you end up *in* the King." She shrugged, a child's shrug, terrible in its casualness. "I don't know what that means."

Neither did he, precisely. But he had stood outside the transfer wagons. He had heard the sound they made when the curtains were drawn — not crying, mostly. A kind of humming. He had told himself it was fear.

"Drink your water," he said, standing. "I'll arrange for your uncle to collect you tomorrow. He's a cooper in Thornbury, yes? Good trade. Better than talking to wells."

"He says so," Wren agreed solemnly. Then, as he reached the door: "Inquisitor?"

"Yes?"

"You've got somebody following you."

Tomas turned. The room was empty except for the girl, the cup, the ledgers, the light coming grey through the high window.

"Not a person," Wren said, pointing at a spot roughly at his shoulder. "A sort of... cold. Like when you walk past a doorway into a cellar. It's been there since you came in. It keeps looking at your hands."

He looked at his hands. Ink-stained, steady, ordinary.

"They're sad about something," Wren said, with the merciless clarity of the very young. "Whoever it is. They're sad about your hands."

* * *

He did not sleep that night. He sat in his quarters with a candle and the accumulated paperwork of three years, and he read his own reports the way a man reads letters from a stranger.

*Confirmed. Transferred south.* Forty-one times.

Forty-one names. He made himself say each one aloud, because it seemed the least he owed them, and by the twentieth his voice had gone rough, and by the thirty-fifth he had stopped pretending he was reading and started remembering.

The cooper's daughter from Thornbury, transferred last spring, who had wept and thanked the inquisitors for taking her seriously at last. The old ferryman who claimed the river spoke to him and who, on the wagon, had smiled — smiled — and said, *about time somebody wanted us.* The twins from Harrow Fen, ten years old, who finished each other's sentences even though only one of them was reported gifted, and who had held hands the entire way to the wagon, and whose humming he could still hear if he let himself, which he had spent a year not letting himself.

And beneath all of it, older than the reports, the thing he had actually seen and filed away unexamined, the way one files away a scar: the night the King's army passed through Marrow's Ford two winters ago, and the machines rolled silent through the frost, and every dog in the town went mad, and every candle guttered toward the road as if the flames themselves were bowing. And Aldric's herald riding at the front, fresh-faced, and the herald's shadow moving wrong — lagging, the way a sound lags its echo — as though the man were being worn rather than walking.

*Thank the King for it,* Brase had said.

Tomas blew out the candle and sat in the dark and understood, with the calm of a man stepping off a ledge and discovering the ledge was the ground all along, that he had known for years. That knowing and admitting were different rooms in the same house, and he had lived his whole career in the first one, keeping the second door shut, and a nine-year-old girl with a cup of well water had just walked through it whistling.

*Nothing knows how to forget,* she'd said. *That's the trouble with the world.*

He was going to be the exception. Starting tonight, he intended to remember everything.

* * *

Commander Brase received him at midmorning in an office that smelled of ink and, faintly, of something sweeter and worse underneath — incense, maybe, or the memory of incense, though the garrison burned none. Brase was a broad, comfortable-looking man with a farmer's hands and a clerk's eyes, and he greeted Tomas with genuine warmth, which somehow made everything harder.

"Vane. Good work on the mill village. Quick turnaround." He shuffled papers. "The Halloway child — I see you marked her unconfirmed."

"The evidence was grief, sir. Nothing more. I interviewed her twice."

"Mm." Brase looked up, and for a moment the warmth thinned and something else showed through, something tired and careful. "You're a thorough man, Vane. That's why I'm going to say this plainly, officer to officer." He leaned back. "There's pressure from the capital on the northern circuit. Numbers are down. Confirmed cases, I mean. Either the gift is fading from the blood — which the theologians doubt — or our methods are going soft." He let that sit. "Which do you think it is?"

"I think," Tomas said, "that we're finding fewer because there are fewer to find, and that a nine-year-old missing her grandmother is not a threat to the realm."

"A nine-year-old today is a bone-carver tomorrow." Brase said it lightly, but his eyes didn't. "You heard about Hollowmere."

Everyone had heard about Hollowmere. The valley that magic couldn't enter, breached at last; the shrine burned; the old woman Odile dead resisting arrest; the bone-carver herself taken alive, by special order, alive, which everyone knew meant *important*, and nobody knew why.

"I heard," Tomas said carefully.

"The bone-carver's being brought to the capital. There's a request for a reliable escort officer to oversee her custody en route and after. Someone patient. Someone who follows procedure." Brase smiled. "Someone thorough. I thought of you immediately."

Tomas felt the trap close around him and recognized, with a strange lurch that was almost relief, that it wasn't a trap at all. Or that it was, but the door was open, and through it he could see exactly what he'd been asking for without knowing how to ask.

"Why me?" he said. "I'm a field interrogator. Custody detail is a step sideways."

"It's a step *south*," Brase corrected. "Closer to the center. Men have ruined careers asking to go south, Vane. You should want less."

With respect, sir, Tomas thought, I should want nothing at all, and wanting nothing is how forty-one people ended up humming behind black curtains while I slept soundly ten feet away. Aloud he said: "I'll take the assignment."

Brase studied him a moment longer than comfort allowed. Then he stamped the transfer order and slid it across, and as Tomas took it, the commander said, almost idly:

"The prisoner. The bone-carver. She hears the dead through carved bone — that's the report. You believe that sort of thing, Vane?"

Tomas thought of a cold spot at his shoulder, sad about his hands. Thought of candles bowing toward a silent road. Thought of a girl wrapping both arms around a cup like it was the last warm thing in the world.

"I believe," he said, "that the dead are the one population this kingdom refuses to count. And I've started wondering what happens to things that aren't counted."

Brase's pen stopped moving. For just a moment the commander looked at him the way a man looks at a crack in a roof beam — and Tomas understood that Brase had known about the beam for years too, and slept under it anyway, and hated himself in the specific quiet way of men who have chosen their wages over their souls.

"Guard her well," Brase said at last, and returned to his papers, and that was the whole of it.

* * *

The prison wagon stood in the garrison yard, black-curtained, and beside it, chained at the wrist to a soldier's pike, stood the woman he had crossed half a country to avoid thinking about.

Marguerite Quintana was thinner than the reports suggested, and younger, and did not look dangerous. She looked like a woman who had buried her teacher eight days ago and been given no time to grieve, and who was carrying the grief anyway, all of it, because there was nowhere to set it down. Her eyes found him across the yard — quick, assessing, cataloguing his uniform, his rank, his distance — and he watched her decide he was an enemy, file him accordingly, and look away.

He deserved the filing. He knew that. He had earned the uniform and everything the uniform had done.

But as they led her past him to the wagon, she stumbled on the yard's uneven cobbles — exhaustion, chains, eight days of grief — and he caught her arm before he had decided to, steadied her, let go. And she looked up at him with those quick eyes, and something in her face flickered, some recognition of the hesitation in his grip, the half-second of *I'm sorry* that his hand had said without his permission.

She said nothing. He said nothing. The soldiers said nothing, because soldiers miss nothing and understand less.

But as the black curtain closed behind her, Tomas stood in the yard with the transfer order in his pocket and the cold spot — her? someone? — still sad about his hands at his shoulder, and he made himself a promise in the plain language of a man done lying to himself:

*I will look. Whatever they're doing down south, whatever the King is feeding, I will stand where I can see it. And when the moment comes — hers, mine, anyone's — I will not be the man who writes* unconfirmed *and sleeps soundly.*

The wagon lurched forward. Somewhere south, past the hills, the capital waited with its tall buildings and its columns of numbers, and inside the wagon a bone-carver rode with a shard of pale stone hidden somewhere against her skin — though he wouldn't learn that until later, much later, when everything had burned and been rebuilt.

For now there was only the road, and the curtain, and a man finally, deliberately, looking.
