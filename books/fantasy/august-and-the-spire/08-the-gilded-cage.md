## 8. The Gilded Cage

The capital announced itself to Marguerite long before she saw it — first as a smell of smoke and river rot, then as a sound like a hive the size of the sky, and finally as a wall so tall it seemed less built than grown, pale stone rising out of the morning haze like the jawbone of some dead god.

She stood in the prison wagon with her wrists bound in iron-threaded cord and watched the gates swallow them, and she thought: *So this is what Odile died to keep me from seeing.* It seemed a strange thing to die over. The city was magnificent in the way a fever is magnificent — too much gold, too many banners, everything burning bright with its own importance.

They did not take her straight to a dungeon. That was the first surprise. Instead they took her to a bathhouse, where women with bored faces scrubbed the road off her and dressed her in a grey wool gown that fit her badly at the shoulders, and then they marched her up four flights of stairs into a tower room with tall windows, a real bed, and a fire already lit.

"Prisoners don't get fireplaces," Marguerite said to the guard who unlocked her door.

"This one does," he said, and had the grace to look embarrassed about it.

*Because you're not a prisoner,* Isolde's voice murmured from the shard hidden in the seam of Marguerite's sleeve. *You're an exhibit.*

She was right, of course. The next morning they paraded her through the Hall of Petitioners — a vast chamber where citizens came to beg favors of the Crown — and stood her on a dais while a herald with a voice like a cracked bell announced that *the bone-witch of Hollowmere, taken alive by His Majesty's loyal inquisitors*, was proof that no gifted soul could hide from the King's justice. Three hundred people stared at her. A few spat. One old woman crossed herself twice, which Marguerite found almost touching; she hadn't been worth two crossings since she was a girl.

She kept her eyes down and her face blank and let the shard against her wrist grow warm with the queen's silent fury.

*Look up,* Isolde hissed. *Make them see your face. Let them remember there is a person under the word 'witch.'*

*And get my jaw broken for it?* Marguerite thought back. *I've survived worse than being ignored. I was ignored professionally for twenty years.*

There was a pause, and then — reluctantly, as if it cost her something — the ghost laughed.

That night, alone in the tower room, Marguerite took stock. Fire, food, a window too narrow to climb through but wide enough to watch the sun set behind the Spire-road's distant hills — north, always north, the direction Odile had pointed her. And a chessboard on the table by the window, ivory and ebony, set up mid-game as though someone had been interrupted.

She was studying it when the door opened and the inquisitor walked in.

Tomas. She remembered him from the valley — the one who'd caught her arm instead of striking it, whose men had burned Odile's shrine while he stood very still and looked at the flames like a man reading a letter he wished were addressed to someone else. He wore his grey uniform neatly and his guilt badly, which was to say he wore it everywhere, in the set of his mouth, in the way he didn't quite meet her eyes.

"You play?" he asked.

"No," said Marguerite, who had played Odile every winter evening for fifteen years and lost gracefully perhaps a third of the time.

"Good." He sat down across from her, turned the board so the white pieces faced her, and moved a pawn. "Then I'll have to teach you."

"I'm told prisoners don't get fireplaces," Marguerite said, sitting. "Do they get chess lessons?"

"This one does." He didn't smile. But something at the corner of his mouth considered it, weighed the option, and filed it away for later.

*Careful,* Isolde whispered. *Inquisitors don't teach games out of kindness.*

*I know,* Marguerite thought, moving a knight because it looked brave. *But kindness is the only weapon in this room I might actually be able to steal.*

* * *

Tomas told himself it was strategy.

That was the official version, the one he wrote in his reports: *Subject responds well to structured engagement. Rapport established. Recommend continued observation by this officer.* Inquisitor-General Veyra wanted the bone-carver cooperative before she was presented to the King; a snarling witch made poor propaganda. Chess was engagement. Chess was observation. Chess was, if he was honest with himself at three in the morning, the first hour of any day when he didn't feel like a man slowly drowning in his own uniform.

She was better than she claimed. He knew it by the fourth game, when she "accidentally" forked his queen and rook and then apologized so sincerely that he nearly believed the accident. By the eighth game she was losing on purpose whenever a guard lingered near the door, and winning like a knife whenever they were alone, and he understood that she was mapping him — learning which version of himself each audience got, and how far apart the versions stood.

He should have reported that too. He didn't.

"You hesitated," she said one evening, nodding at the move he'd just made. "Before you took my bishop. Why?"

"Because taking your bishop was a trap."

"And you took it anyway."

"The trap was badly built," Tomas said. "Whoever set it assumed I'd value the bishop more than the position."

Marguerite studied him across the board with those grey eyes that saw too much, the way her mentor must have seen, the way all of them saw — the gifted, the cursed, whatever the proper word was this season. "And do you? Value things more than positions?"

"That's not a chess question."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "It isn't."

He moved a pawn and changed the subject, and hated how obviously he did it, and noticed that she let him — that she was kind enough, or cruel enough, to let him.

* * *

From the shard, Isolde offered commentary.

*He lies with his left hand,* she observed during the second week, as Tomas reached to castle. *Watch. Every time he's about to say something true, he touches the table first. Grounding himself, like a man stepping onto ice.*

*You were married to a king,* Marguerite thought. *Did you learn to read people or did they come pre-read?*

*I learned by being lied to beautifully for eleven years. He lies badly. It's almost restful.*

Marguerite moved her rook and said aloud, "Your General Veyra sent a letter today. I saw the courier."

Tomas's hand paused over the board — left hand, brushing the table edge first. "You have sharp eyes for a prisoner."

"I have sharp eyes for a bone-carver. We spend our lives watching hands." She tipped her head. "What does she want?"

"To know why I haven't requested your transfer to the interrogation wing."

"And why haven't you?"

"Because," Tomas said, "you're more useful here."

It was a good answer. It was even mostly true. What it left out was the interrogation wing's new methods, which Tomas had seen applied to a cooper's daughter in the capital's lower cells, and which he would describe to no one, ever, except perhaps someday to a priest, if he could find one whose god hadn't been conscripted into the King's service.

Marguerite looked at him for a long moment. Then she reached out and moved his knight for him — a blunder, a terrible one, leaving her own queen hanging — and said, "Your move."

He stared at the board. She was giving him the game. She was giving him the game in front of God and the fireplace and the ghost in her sleeve, and her face said *I know what you didn't say, and I'm paying you for it the only way they haven't taken from me yet.*

"Take the queen," he said quietly. "I played it wrong."

"You played it exactly right," Marguerite said, "for someone who has to live in this building," and took her own queen back off the board and set it beside the box, out of play, and neither of them mentioned it again.

*Oh,* said Isolde, in the tone of a woman revising an opinion she'd rather keep. *Oh, this is going to be a problem.*

* * *

The trouble with chess, Tomas discovered, was that it taught you to think in the enemy's shape.

By the third week he could predict her openings. By the fourth he could predict her silences — the particular quiet she went into when a bell rang somewhere in the city, the way her fingers found her left sleeve when she thought no one was looking. He catalogued these things in the part of his mind that used to file heresy reports, and the catalogue kept turning into something else. Concern, maybe. The word for it in the old tongue of his grandmother's village had been *cared-for-from-a-distance*, and there had been no word for it in Grey uniform training at all.

"You never ask me questions," Marguerite said one night, midway through a game she was losing on purpose for the benefit of the changing of the guard. "Interrogators ask questions. You bring tea and move little horses around."

"Inquisitors," Tomas corrected. "And I did ask you questions. Week one. You told me the weather in Hollowmere was none of my business."

"It wasn't."

"It rained there the day we arrived," he said. "All day. My men complained. I remember thinking it sounded like a place that grieves properly." He moved a pawn without looking at it. "Here it hasn't rained in two months. The King says the weather is loyal."

Marguerite laughed — a short, startled sound, quickly smothered, but real. It was the first time he'd heard it. Something in his chest performed a maneuver he declined to examine.

"The weather is loyal," she repeated. "Does he say that before or after dinner?"

"After. Everything's after dinner with Aldric. He likes his omens digested." Tomas realized he'd said too much — said *Aldric* the way a man says a name and not a title — and covered it by reaching for the teapot. Left hand. Table first.

Marguerite's eyes flicked to the gesture and back up, and he knew she'd seen, and knew she knew he knew, and the whole architecture of their mutual dishonesty shuddered like a bridge taking weight.

"Check," she said softly, and he looked down and discovered she'd checkmated him six moves ago and had simply been waiting, politely, for him to notice.

* * *

That night, in the dark of the tower room, Marguerite lay awake and did arithmetic.

Three weeks. Twenty-one days of tea and chess and a man who touched the table before telling the truth. Isolde had gone quiet after sundown — ghosts, apparently, needed rest, or pretended to — and in the silence Marguerite turned the problem over the way she'd turn a fresh-carved charm, feeling for the grain.

He was lying about what he believed. So was she. They both knew it, and the knowing had become a kind of language, more honest than anything either of them said aloud. When he falsified the guard rotation so her window faced north. When she lost games in front of witnesses and won them alone. When he'd said, last Tuesday, apropos of nothing, "The Spire road closes with the autumn snows," and she'd said, "Then travelers should leave soon," and he'd said, "Yes," and poured the tea.

*You trust him,* Isolde said suddenly, from the sleeve.

Marguerite didn't jump. She'd stopped jumping at the queen weeks ago. "I trust his patterns."

"That's what trust *is*, girl. Patterns that hold." A pause, and when Isolde spoke again her voice had the careful flatness she used when a memory pressed too close. "I trusted a pattern once. Eleven years of breakfasts. Then one night the pattern picked up a knife."

"Aldric."

"Aldric." The word came out like a stone dropped down a well, falling a long way before it landed. "He smiled at me across a chessboard too, once. Did I tell you that?"

"You didn't."

"He was terrible at it. All attack, no patience. I let him win anyway, because queens do that, we spend our whole lives letting men win games they've already lost." The shard grew cold against Marguerite's wrist. "Don't let yours win, Marguerite. Whatever else you do. Make him earn it."

"He's not mine," Marguerite said.

"No," Isolde agreed. "Not yet. But I watched you give him your queen last week, and I watched him refuse to take it, and I am very old and very dead and I know what I saw."

Marguerite closed her eyes. Outside, the city bells rang the midnight hour, and somewhere below, boots on stairs — the guard's round, familiar as rain. Except the boots stopped outside her door. Paused. Moved on.

Then paused again.

She sat up. Through the door's iron grate she could see torchlight, and the shadow of a man standing very still in the corridor, and after a moment the shadow spoke, low, through the grate:

"Don't sleep deeply tomorrow night. Veyra's moving you to the interrogation wing at dawn. Officially it's because the King wants a full accounting before the equinox presentation." A breath. "Unofficially, Veyra read my reports. All of them. She says I've gone soft, and she's right, and soft men don't get to guard you anymore."

Marguerite was at the door before she'd decided to stand. "Tomas—"

"There's a loose bar in the washroom window," he said, faster now, words tumbling like spilled coins. "Third floor drop onto the laundry cart line if you cut left. The east stair is unwatched during the changing of the watch — I've seen to that for three weeks, though I told myself it was sloppiness." His laugh was barely a sound at all. "I told myself a great many things. Checkmate, by the way. You had it again tonight and you let me find it myself."

"Tomas." Her hand found the cold iron of the grate. "Why?"

The silence stretched. Down the corridor, a torch guttered.

"Because I took your bishop in the first game knowing it was a trap," he said at last, "and I've been trying to take it back ever since. Dawn comes early. Don't sleep deeply."

The boots receded. The torchlight slid away along the wall, and Marguerite stood in the dark with her heart hammering and the shard burning cold against her wrist, and Isolde's voice rose one final time, dry as old paper:

*Well. I suppose we're leaving tonight.*
