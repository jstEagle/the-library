## 9. Aldric's Bargain

The map room of the Citadel had once been a chapel.

Aldric had ordered the stained glass pulled down in his first year — *too much kneeling light*, he'd said, and his architects had obeyed with the alacrity of men who suspected that disagreeing with the new king was a career measured in inches of rope. Now the great east window was iron lattice, and through it the morning came in grey and orderly, like everything else he had built.

He stood over the map of the realm as he did every morning, a cup of black tea going cold at his elbow, and counted the lights.

That was what Veyra called them, anyway. *Lights.* Each one a village where an inquisitor's report confirmed a gifted soul: a woman who dreamed true, a boy who could quiet a frightened horse with a touch, a carver of bone in some hidden valley who let the grieving speak to their dead. Small flames, scattered across the parchment like embers shaken from a hearth. His generals saw threats. His clerks saw quotas.

Aldric saw fuel.

"Your Majesty." The chamberlain's voice came from the doorway, careful as a man walking on river ice. "The Master of Coin begs five minutes."

"He may have three."

The Master of Coin was a round, sweating man named Pellwick who had learned, in two years of service, to say unpleasant things very quickly and then leave. He crossed the room at a trot, papers clutched to his chest like a breastplate.

"Sire. The war-engines on the northern front. The—the animating principle." He swallowed. "It's failing again. Three colossi went inert outside Varnholt yesterday. The engineers say the—say the *resonance* is thinning. They're requesting more of the... the substance."

"The substance," Aldric said mildly, "being the dead."

Pellwick went the color of porridge. "I would never presume to—"

"You may presume. It saves time." Aldric turned back to the map. "Tell the engineers they'll have their resonance. Tell them the hunt is proceeding ahead of schedule. And Pellwick—" he glanced back, and enjoyed, in a distant way, the way the man flinched, "—the next time you call them *the substance* in my presence, I'll have you reassigned to the colossi yourself. Understood?"

"Perfectly, sire." A bow, a scurry, a door clicking shut.

Alone again, Aldric permitted himself a small, dry smile. It was not kindness that moved him — or not only kindness; he was no longer entirely sure what moved him, which was rather the point. But he had found that precision of language kept a court honest. If the men around him were forced to say *the dead* out loud, every day, they might someday be forced to think about them too.

He touched the map, lightly, over the western mountains. Hollowmere. The reports described a valley where magic could not follow — a hole in the world's fabric, stitched shut by some ancient covenant. For two years the valley had sat there like a missing tooth, defying him simply by existing outside his reach.

Now, according to last night's dispatch, it did not defy him any longer.

"Inquisitor Renn," he said to the empty room, "you have made me a gift."

He should have felt triumphant. He noted, with the detached curiosity of a scholar observing his own symptoms, that what he felt instead was hunger. A deep, structural hunger, like the ache of a house settling on a cracked foundation.

*Soon,* said something in the marrow of him. Not in words. It hadn't used words in a long time.

"Not yet," Aldric told it, the way one tells a dog to sit. "Patience. We do this properly."

The thing in his marrow settled, sullenly, to wait.

* * *

He had been a different man when he first understood what the covenant cost.

That was the trouble with memory — his own memory especially, these days. It arrived in fragments now, whole scenes sheared off at the edges like paintings cropped by careless hands. But some scenes remained entire, and this was one: twenty-two years old, standing in the crypt beneath the old cathedral while his father's body cooled above, listening to an archivist explain why the crown's oldest duty was also its most useless.

"The covenant binds the living to the dead," the archivist had said. She was a small, sharp-eyed woman named Maren, and she was the only person in the palace who spoke to the new king without flinching. "Every soul that dies at peace passes through the keystone and is *spent* — gently, usefully, into the land. Rain, harvest, luck. That is why the valley folk prosper. That is why the old kings never wanted for anything."

"And the price?" young Aldric had asked.

"Restraint. Nothing more dramatic than that. The dead must rest, and the living must let them. No king may call a soul back, not for counsel, not for comfort, not for war." Maren had smiled thinly. "Your father called it the most boring miracle in the world. He was proud of it."

Boring. Yes. Aldric remembered how the word had tasted — like a door closing on a room he'd only just learned existed. His father had died at peace, surrounded by family, and passed through the keystone like water through a sieve, and that was that. All that wisdom, all that stubbornness, gone into the rain.

And Aldric had stood in the crypt with a war already forming behind his eyes — border lords circling, creditors circling, the whole realm a carcass waiting for stronger teeth — and thought: *there is enough power in that stone to unmake every enemy I have. And my father spent it on weather.*

He had not broken the covenant in a night. He wanted that recorded somewhere, even if only in his own head, which was becoming an unreliable archive. It had taken years. Study, negotiation, a certain amount of blood. The keystone was guarded by keepers who had held their office since before the kingdom had a name, and they did not respond to gold or threat. So he had done it differently. He had learned the covenant's grammar. He had found the flaw in its phrasing — there is always a flaw in phrasing; lawyers and gods are made of the same material — and he had written himself into the contract as a clause no one had thought to forbid.

*A king in need may draw upon the rest of the dead.*

In need. He had been very needy indeed. The border war. The famine year. The rebellion of the eastern lords, which had ended so decisively when his enemies' own honored ancestors rose from consecrated ground and marched against their descendants' supply lines. The dead did not fight well — they fought terribly, in fact, shambling and confused and weeping, which was somehow worse — but armies do not require their weapons to be skilled. Only present.

After that, the war-machines had seemed almost a courtesy. Why raise corpses when you could bind the unrest of ten thousand souls into iron and let it walk? The engineers called it resonance. The priests he hadn't yet purged called it abomination. Aldric called it efficiency, and slept soundly, mostly.

Mostly.

* * *

The trouble — and here he paused, that evening, alone in his apartments with a decanter he wasn't drinking from — the trouble was the interest.

Power borrowed from the dead accrued interest like any other debt, but the currency was peculiar. It didn't take coin. It took *interiority*. Room by room, the thing inside him was evicting him from his own house.

He had noticed it first with small things. The taste of plums — gone, one autumn, between one bite and the next. The name of the nurse who had raised him — he knew he had known it, knew the shape of her hands, and the name itself was simply a smooth blank, like a wall painted over wet paint. Then larger rooms. His coronation oath: he could recite it perfectly and remember nothing of saying it. The face of his first horse. The smell of the sea at his childhood estate — he owned the memory of owning the memory, a label on an empty drawer.

His scholars would have found it fascinating. His physicians, had he allowed any near him, would have prescribed bleeding and rest. Aldric had instead done what he always did with problems: he had commissioned a study, read it twice, and burned it.

Because the study had confirmed the pattern, and the pattern was simple, and simplicity was the cruelest quality a horror could have. Every soul he drew upon took something. Not randomly — *specifically*. The dead took what the living valued, because value was the only coin they recognized. And Aldric, who valued almost nothing anymore, was being paid out in the few things he had left.

He set down the untouched decanter and walked to the window. Below, the capital glittered, obedient, lit by lamps and by the pale glow of the resonance-works where the newest engines drank. Somewhere in that city was a prison wagon carrying a bone-carver, and somewhere in his chest was a hunger wearing his name.

*Elira.*

He thought the name deliberately, the way a man probes a tooth to learn how deep the rot goes.

His daughter. Dead nine years now — fever, the physicians said, though he had long suspected the physicians of saying whatever let them keep their heads. Nine years, and he had drawn on the dead within a month of her funeral, because grief was intolerable and power was right there, and because — this was the part he admitted only at night, only to the window — because he had wanted to find her. In the unrest, in the churn of stolen souls, surely she would surface. Surely he could reach into the great dark river and pull out one small girl.

He never found her. Of course he never found her. The dead who came to his call were the restless, the angry, the torn-away — and Elira had died loved, in a peaceful house, and had gone through the keystone clean and quiet before he ever broke it. He had shattered the door and then ransacked the room looking for someone who had already left by the window.

But he had kept one thing of hers. One memory, defended like a fortress, walled around with all the discipline of a man who had organized his own damnation: her face. Round-cheeked, gap-toothed at six, solemn at nine, laughing — he had a hundred moments of it catalogued, and he reviewed them nightly like a treasurer counting the last coins in a failing bank.

Except.

He stood at the window and reached for the fortress, and found the gates open, and the treasury empty.

There was a shape where her face had been. He knew its outline the way one knows a country from maps never visited: small, warm, beloved. He knew that he had known it. He knew the exact weight of her head against his shoulder — that memory survived, bodiless, a sensation with no owner. But the face itself, the particular arrangement of her — her mother's brow, his own stubborn chin, the small scar on her left eyebrow from falling out of the pear tree he'd told her not to climb —

Gone. Spent. Fed into a colossus at Varnholt, perhaps, or into the suppression of some rebellion, or simply eaten by the slow machinery of his immortality, which was not immortality at all but a loan renewed nightly at compound interest. He did not age. That was the joke of it, the exquisite joke: the most powerful man in the world, undying, and he could not remember his daughter's face, and every year he would remember less and less that there had ever been anything to forget, until one morning he would wake as a smooth, hungry, eternal nothing wearing a crown, and the realm would finally have the king it deserved.

Aldric laughed. It came out wrong — too short, too high — and he pressed his knuckles to his mouth until it stopped.

"No," he said aloud, to the window, to the city, to the thing in his marrow. "No. I'll finish it first. I'll reforge what's needed, I'll close the ledger, I'll pay it all back and keep what's mine. You don't get her. Whatever else you take, you don't get her."

The thing in his marrow stirred, amused. It did not believe him. He was beginning to suspect it had good reason.

* * *

Veyra found him at dawn in the map room, which was where he increasingly lived, sleeping in the chair like a clerk instead of a bed like a king. She entered without announcement — the only officer permitted the habit — and set a dispatch on the map with two fingers.

"Hollowmere," she said. "Renn has the bone-carver. Alive, per orders. There's a complication."

"There's always a complication. Complications are how the realm says good morning."

"A shard of the keystone is missing. Renn believes the carver has it. And—" Veyra paused, and Veyra did not pause; pausing, in her, was the equivalent of another man screaming, "—the resonance around the prisoner is anomalous. Stronger than any reading since the eastern campaign. Whatever she carries, the dead are *drawn to it*. Sire, if we can secure the shard intact—"

"Then I could draw on the covenant directly. Without the engines. Without the intermediaries." Aldric looked at the map, at the western mountains, at the small valley that had defied him for two years, and felt the hunger lean forward inside his ribs like a hound scenting meat. "Yes. Bring me the carver. Bring me the shard. Gently, Veyra — gently. Break the woman and the stone means nothing. Treat her as you'd treat..." He searched for the comparison and found, absurdly, the ghost of his daughter's hand in his, leading him somewhere important, and could not for the life of him remember where. "...as you'd treat something irreplaceable."

Veyra studied him a moment too long. "You're well, sire?"

"I'm immortal," Aldric said. "It's the fashionable ailment. See to the escort. Double it — the free cities will have heard by now, and the free cities are sentimental about their witches."

She saluted and went. Alone again, Aldric spread his hands flat on the map, over the road north, over the route the prison wagon would take, and let himself imagine it: the keystone whole in his grasp, the covenant rewritten, the debt converted from memory into something cheaper — other people's memories, other people's daughters, other men paying his interest forever.

It was a beautiful plan. It was airtight. It was exactly the kind of plan he had made before every triumph of his reign.

And underneath the hunger, underneath the cold arithmetic, some small surviving room in the ruined house of himself whispered what it always whispered, in a voice that sounded less like ambition every year and more like warning:

*The dead are patient, Aldric. You taught them that.*

Far to the west, in a prison wagon rattling toward the capital, a dead queen was already talking.
