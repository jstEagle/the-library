## 10. Escape Through the Bone Market

The Bone Market opened at dusk, as it did every year on the eve of the Long Fair, and the whole capital turned out to celebrate its dead.

I learned this from Isolde, who had attended forty-one of them in life and remembered every one with the particular sharpness of a woman who had loved a festival and been murdered by its host. *They hang lanterns carved from antler,* she told me that morning through the shard in my palm, her voice like wind moving through a cold room. *And they sell sugar skulls. And everyone wears white, even the beggars, because for one night the living are supposed to dress as ghosts so the dead can find them.*

*That's either beautiful or obscene,* I said.

*It's both. That's what makes it a festival.* A pause. *It is also, little carver, the only night of the year when half the guards in this city will be drunk and the other half will be looking at anything except the prison wing, because no one wants to be the man who missed the fireworks over a jail cell.*

I lay on my cot in the gilded cage they'd built me — a tower room with velvet curtains and a view of the river, because Aldric wanted his bone-carver comfortable until she was useful — and I laughed. Actually laughed, alone, at nothing, like a madwoman. It felt wonderful.

*You find something amusing?*

*I was just thinking that if Odile could see me taking escape advice from a queen, she'd say I'd finally learned to network.*

Isolde made a sound I had come to understand was her version of laughter — dry, brief, startled out of her. *Your mentor sounds insufferable.*

*She was.* The past tense sat wrong in my mouth. It always did. *She is. She's just also dead, which complicates things.*

*Welcome to my entire existence,* said the ghost-queen, and for once there was no frost in it at all.

* * *

The plan, such as it was, had three parts, and two of them belonged to other people.

Part one was Isolde: she knew the tower, knew the guard rotations, knew which stairwell had been bricked up after a scandal involving a chamberlain and someone's wife, and knew — this was the part that made my skin crawl — exactly how many men Aldric had posted to watch me, because she had spent three years as a ghost learning to count her murderer's soldiers.

Part two was Tomas, and I still didn't know what part he intended to play. That was the trouble with Tomas. He stood in doorways with his hands folded and his face arranged into professional blankness, and he asked questions during our chess games that were not quite questions, and twice now he had looked away at precisely the moment looking away mattered. Once when a guard struck me for speaking out of turn. Once when I asked him, idly, whether the confiscated charms from Hollowmere were kept somewhere with a lock or merely somewhere with a rule.

He hadn't answered. But he hadn't reported me either, and in the capital, silence was its own kind of confession.

Part three was me, and mine was simple: steal back the bones.

Because here is what I had not told anyone — not Isolde, not the guards, certainly not the king who visited me twice with honeyed words and hungry eyes. The charms they'd taken from my workshop were not empty. Every charm I carve holds a whisper of the dead it was made for, and thirty-one of them had come south in an inquisitor's crate, and thirty-one voices had been pressing against their bone walls for weeks, calling a name they couldn't quite remember.

Mine.

I could feel them from my tower, faint as a headache coming on. Tonight I meant to answer.

* * *

The festival began at sundown with bells, and the bells undid everything.

Not by accident — Isolde had planned it that way. When the great antler lanterns were lit along the Processional and the first drumline came down from the hill districts, the bells of the Citadel rang the old mourning peal, twelve strokes for the honored dead, and every soldier in the garrison stopped what he was doing to stand at attention, because Aldric had made irreverence toward the mourning peal a flogging offense and soldiers have long memories about floggings.

Twelve strokes. Twelve minutes, give or take, in which the corridor outside my door would hold exactly one man.

I heard his boots before I saw him. Of course I did; I'd spent six weeks learning the sound of them, steady and unhurried, a man who walked like he had nowhere better to be and nowhere worse.

Tomas unlocked my door himself.

"Captain Veyra wants you on the balcony for the lantern release," he said, flat as slate. "Official duty. The king likes his prize displayed."

"The king," I said, "likes his prizes leashed."

Something flickered across his face — too fast to name, gone before it landed. He stepped aside and gestured me through with a formality that would have convinced anyone who wasn't watching for it. Anyone who wasn't me, counting the seconds between bell-strokes, noticing that his hand rested nowhere near his sword.

*Tell him,* Isolde murmured. *No. Don't. If you don't tell him, then whatever happens tonight isn't treason. It's negligence. There's a difference, and the difference is a rope.*

We went down the stairs together, past the landing where the second guard should have been standing and wasn't, past the window where the whole city glowed gold and white, and Tomas said, without turning his head, in a voice pitched under the bells:

"The storeroom under the east armory. Crate marked HOLLOWMERE CONFISCATE, third shelf. The lock is old. The sentry is new."

My heart did something complicated and painful. "Captain—"

"Don't." He kept walking. "Whatever you're about to say, don't. I haven't done anything. I'm walking a prisoner to a balcony. If she slips away between here and there, that will be a failure of discipline I'll regret for the rest of my career."

"Which will be very short."

"Probably." He almost smiled. It was the closest I'd ever seen him come to one, and it transformed his face from a locked room into a room where someone had recently lived. "The balcony's ahead. I'll present you, you'll wave, the crowd will cheer the merciful king. Then there'll be the lantern song, and during the lantern song, Captain Veyra always goes down to the forecourt to review the honor guard, because she trusts no one else to do it properly. The balcony stairs on the north side come down behind the kitchens. No one uses them during the festival. The cooks are all drunk."

"And you?"

"I," said Tomas, "am going to stand on this balcony and look extremely surprised."

* * *

I waved. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger the dead keep of ridiculous human behavior: on the night I fled the capital of a tyrant, I stood on a velvet-draped balcony beside a man whose armies had burned my valley, and I smiled and waved at ten thousand strangers while they sang the lantern song, and several of them waved back, and one small girl blew me a kiss, and I nearly wept at her, which would have ruined everything.

Then the last lantern rose — a great whale of bone-white paper carrying a written wish, some child's hope scrawled in charcoal — and the crowd gasped it upward, and every face in the square tilted to the sky, and I turned and left my life standing on a balcony.

The kitchen stairs smelled of onions and spilled wine. I took them two at a time with Isolde's voice in my ear — *left at the bottom, through the wash-yard, mind the trestle tables, the drunk ones sleep early on festival nights* — and she steered me around sleeping bodies with the ease of a woman who had sneaked out of a hundred feasts herself.

*You're enjoying this,* I accused her, vaulting a woodpile.

*Immensely. Three years of drifting through walls, and finally someone runs where I tell her to run. Do you know what I'd give for legs right now?*

*A body?*

*Don't tempt me, little carver. I've seen what your charms can do.*

The east armory squatted at the edge of the inner ward, and the sentry outside it was exactly as advertised: new, young, and asleep sitting up with a festival cup still in his hand, dreaming whatever boys dream who signed on to hunt the gifted and got assigned instead to guard a locked room through the one drunken night of the year. I stepped over his boots and felt, absurdly, guilty about it.

The lock was old. I'd known it would be, because Isolde had told me, and Isolde did not lie about locks. What she hadn't mentioned was that the key hung on a nail directly beside the door, because the quartermaster of the Citadel had the security instincts of a trusting grandmother.

I let myself in. The dark inside was total, and full of the smell I knew better than my own heartbeat: old bone, oiled steel, dust, and beneath it all, faintly, the sweet-marrow scent of charms waiting to be used.

Third shelf. CRATE MARKED IN STENCILED LETTERS: HOLLOWMERE — CONFISCATED GOODS.

I pried the lid off with my fingernails, breaking two of them, caring not at all.

Thirty-one charms lay in straw, wrapped in cloth like corpses prepared for burial, which in a sense they were. I gathered them into my satchel with shaking hands, and as each one touched the leather, the whispers rose — not words yet, not names, but recognition, the way a sleeping person stirs when someone they love enters the room. By the time I cinched the bag shut, it hummed against my hip like a hive.

"Hold on," I whispered to them. "I'm getting you home."

*Little carver.* Isolde's voice cut sharp through the dark. *Feet. Now. Someone's coming.*

I doused the shuttered lamp I'd brought, pressed into the shadow between shelving and wall, and held my breath as torchlight swung through the high window and boots crossed the yard outside. A voice — Veyra's voice, iron scraped over iron — called something to another soldier, too far to catch the words. Then silence. Then more silence than silence should reasonably contain.

When I finally exhaled, my knees were water.

*She's heading for the balcony,* Isolde said grimly. *She'll find your inquisitor standing alone.*

*Will he be all right?*

There was a pause. Ghosts, I was learning, paused when the honest answer was ugly. *That depends entirely on how surprised he looks,* Isolde said, *and I confess I have never once been able to read that man's face, and I was married to a diplomat.*

* * *

Getting out of the Citadel was easier than it had any right to be, because the Bone Market swallowed the streets.

The whole city had poured itself into white. Lanterns swayed on poles above the crowds; sugar skulls melted on children's chins; a man dressed as Death jangled past with a tambourine, collecting coins for the temple of rest. Nobody looked at a woman in a servant's smock with a heavy satchel, because everybody was looking at everything else, and grief and joy had gotten so tangled together in the crowd that people were laughing and crying in the same breath, and I understood suddenly why Isolde had loved this night, and hated the man who owned it.

I bought two sugar skulls from a stall because it seemed sacrilegious not to, and ate one immediately, and it tasted like childhood I'd never had.

*Where to?* Isolde asked, as I drifted with the current of the crowd toward the river gate.

*North gate's sealed since the hunt started. River gate has a chain across it after dark. So—*

*So the drain under the fish market,* she finished. *Yes. I know. I helped pay for that drain, actually, after the flood year. Worst money I ever spent and now the best. Turn left at the shrine of the drowned.*

The shrine of the drowned was a niche in a wall with a chipped statue and a bowl for offerings, and I stopped despite myself, because the bowl was full of little carved tokens — fish, boats, hands — and among them, unmistakable even in lantern light, a charm. Cheap work, apprentice work, the kind peddlers sold to grieving families who couldn't afford better. Someone had left a charm at a shrine like a prayer.

Someone in this city still believed.

I touched it, just briefly, and felt the faintest stir inside — an old woman's voice, worn smooth by years, humming a lullaby to no one. I couldn't take it; it wasn't mine, and stealing prayers is a theft even I won't commit. But I bent close and whispered into the bone, the way I do, the way Odile taught me: *You're heard. You were always heard. Rest easy till the road opens.*

The humming faltered, then steadied, warmer. Like a candle relit.

*Sentimental,* said Isolde, but softly.

*You married a diplomat and haunt a keystone shard. You don't get to judge me.*

*Fair.*

The drain under the fish market was exactly as glamorous as it sounded. I went in on my hands and knees through a gap behind a stack of barrels, into a brick throat that smelled of low tide and older sins, and crawled by touch while Isolde counted junctions — *second fork, bear right, the masons cut corners in '61, trust the left wall* — until gray light appeared ahead, and then silver, and then the river, sliding black and broad under a sky full of rising lanterns.

A rowboat waited among the pilings. Not mine. Isolde's arrangement — she'd spent weeks nudging a ferryman's dreams, and the ferryman, a widower who talked to his late wife every evening over dinner, had woken three nights running with the unshakable conviction that he owed a debt to a lady in white, and had left his spare boat tied where the lady in white would need it.

I climbed in. I untied the line. And that was when I heard the bells change.

Not the mourning peal. The alarm — fast, ragged, urgent, ringing from the Citadel across the water, and beneath it, thin with distance, shouting.

They'd found the empty tower. They'd found the open storeroom. They'd found—

*Him,* Isolde said quietly.

I shipped the oars and sat in the dark boat, in the middle of the river, with the burning city reflected around me like a crown of embers, and I thought about a man standing on a balcony arranging his face into surprise while Veyra's boots came up the stairs behind him.

"He knew what it would cost," I said aloud.

*Yes.*

"He did it anyway."

*Yes.*

"Why?" My voice cracked on it. "Why would he— he burned shrines. He led the hunt into my valley. He—" I ran out of accusations, because the truth underneath them was simpler and worse: he had looked away twice, and then he had looked all the way, and I had no idea what a man does after that except burn his own life down and call it surprise.

*People are not their worst orders,* Isolde said. *I have reason to know. I gave some terrible ones myself, before the end.* Her presence shifted, the way she did when she was choosing honesty over comfort. *Row, little carver. Grieve later. The free cities are nine days north and Veyra is faster than grief.*

I rowed.

Behind us, the alarm bells chased each other across the water, and the lanterns went on rising anyway, hundreds of them, carrying wishes up past the towers of the man who had broken heaven to keep his throne. Somewhere in that glow, a soldier was answering hard questions, and I could not make myself believe he was lying well enough.

* * *

Dawn found me hauled out on the north bank, soaked to the waist, hiding the boat in reeds, eating the second sugar skull because fear burns calories and I refuse to apologize.

That was when I heard hoofbeats on the towpath.

I had the satchel strap in my fist and a carving knife in the other before my brain caught up — and then the rider came around the bend, mud-spattered, cloakless, riding a borrowed post-horse at a pace that had clearly killed at least one horse's opinion of him, and it was Tomas.

He reined in. We stared at each other across ten feet of river mist, two fugitives discovering they'd committed the same crime independently.

"You're supposed to be under arrest," I said.

"I was." He dismounted stiffly. His knuckles were split and there was a bruise blooming along his jaw. "Veyra asks thorough questions. I answered badly on purpose. Then I answered worse on purpose. Then I took a horse." He said it all evenly, like reading a supply manifest, but his eyes went to the satchel at my hip and stayed there, and something in his face eased — the thing under the locked-room face, the living thing. "You got them out. All of them?"

"Thirty-one."

"Good." He looked back the way he'd come, toward the city, where smoke from the morning fires mixed with festival haze, and I watched him decide something. He reached inside his coat and drew out a folded document, heavy paper, sealed with grey wax — his commission, his name, his rank, his whole self written in an inquisitor's hand.

"What are you doing?"

"What it looks like." He struck flint to the char end of a dropped festival torch someone had abandoned in the reeds, and held the flame to the corner of the paper. "I can't unburn the shrine at Hollowmere. I can't unwrite the reports. But I can stop signing new ones."

The fire took his name first. He held it until the flames reached his fingers, then let the last piece go spinning out over the water, a scrap of orange light dwindling among the ash-grey dawn.

"There'll be a price on your head by noon," I said.

"On both our heads. Yours is higher. I intend to hide behind you extensively." And there it was again, that almost-smile, closer to real this time. "Nine days north, the ghost says?"

"Nine days, if the weather's kind and Veyra's slow."

"She won't be slow." He glanced north, where the road ran up out of the riverlands toward hills I'd only seen in Odile's maps, and his voice went quiet. "She took the loss personally. She'll ride through the night. Which means we should too."

We stood a moment longer, two liars freshly unemployed, while behind us the capital's bells rang on and the lanterns burned out one by one in the brightening sky.

Then I put my shoulder to the boat, Tomas took the bow line, and we went north together — carrying thirty-one whispers, one ghost-queen, and the smoking ashes of everything he'd been — into a country that had no idea yet what was coming for it, or what was coming after us.

Somewhere behind, faint on the wind, I thought I heard Veyra's war-horns begin to cry.

But that, as Odile used to say, is tomorrow's weather.
