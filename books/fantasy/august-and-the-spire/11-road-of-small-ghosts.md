## 11. Road of Small Ghosts

The road north was not a road so much as an argument the land was having with itself — cart tracks dissolving into mud, mud hardening back into ruts, hedgerows leaning in as if to whisper. Marguerite walked it with her hood up and the keystone shard warm against her sternum, and by the third day she had learned to read the countryside the way she once read bone: by what it refused to say.

Villages, mostly. Or the shapes of villages.

The first one they passed through had a name on no sign anymore, because there was no sign, because there was no smithy wall to hang it on. There were foundations. There was a well with its bucket rope cut and coiled neatly beside it, the way people leave things when they mean to come back. There was a church — or the ribs of one, its roof gone to fire, its bell still hanging in the blackened frame like a tongue in an open mouth.

And there were graves. Not the tidy kind with stones and names. These were long, shallow trenches behind the churchyard wall, filled in hurriedly, marked with nothing but the trampled grass that hadn't yet decided to grow over them.

Marguerite stopped walking before she consciously decided to.

"Margo," Tomas said quietly, using the road-name they'd agreed on. He'd taken off his grey coat somewhere between the capital and here; without it he looked younger and wrong, like a chair missing one leg. "We can't stop at every—"

"Yes we can," said Isolde, from inside the shard, in the voice only Marguerite heard — dry, imperious, and, these days, faintly amused by Tomas's habit of stating the impossible as policy. "Tell him yes we can."

"She says yes we can," Marguerite translated.

"I didn't hear anything."

"You never do. That's rather the point of you." She was already moving toward the trench, kneeling in the wet grass. She drew her knife, and then, because some habits are older than caution, she paused. "I need something small. A button. A splinter of wood. Anything that belonged to one of them."

Tomas crouched at the edge of the grave, arms on his knees, and looked at the churned earth for a long moment. Then he reached into his boot and came out with a knife of his own, and began, methodically, cutting buttons off his own coat — the grey one, the one he'd carried folded in his pack like a snake's shed skin, because burning it had felt too much like pretending he'd never worn it.

"What are you doing?"

"They're bone buttons," he said. "Close enough." He set three of them in her palm. His hand was steady. His face wasn't. "Will they work?"

"They'll work better than most things would." She looked at him. He was staring at the trench. "Tomas."

"Don't," he said. Not unkindly. Just flat, the way a man sets down something heavy. "Just do whatever it is you do."

So she did.

She carved while Isolde talked her through the old forms — the queen had been dead eleven years and had apparently spent them reading everything in the royal archive out of spite, and it showed. The charm took shape: a small disc of bone, a spiral scored around its edge, words pressed into it not with ink but with intention. When it was done, Marguerite held it over the trench and spoke the names she didn't have.

"You don't know their names," Isolde murmured. "That's the cruelty of it. The covenant was built so that no one would go unnamed. He's turned the whole kingdom into a graveyard that forgot itself."

"Then we start remembering," Marguerite said aloud, and pressed the charm into the earth.

What happened next was quiet, which somehow made it worse. No screaming, no rising shapes. Just a sound like a long-held breath finally let go — a sigh moving through the ground, through the grass, up through her knees and into her chest. The trampled grass shivered. Somewhere a bird started singing again, having apparently been holding its opinion for some time.

And then the pressure she hadn't realized she'd been carrying since Hollowmere — the dead pressing against her senses like faces against glass — eased. Not everywhere. Just here. Just this field.

Tomas sat back on his heels. He didn't say anything for a while. When he did, his voice came out sanded down. "There were four hundred conscripted from this valley. The requisition rolls. I signed the transport orders for two of them myself. I remember because one of them had my mother's surname."

"Tomas—"

"He was a cooper's son. Seventeen." He stood, brushed off his knees with more force than the gesture required, and picked up his pack. "We should make distance before dark. Veyra doesn't camp."

* * *

They made a rhythm of it, over the following days, though none of them ever called it that. Walk until the land grew loud — and Marguerite always felt it first, a buzzing behind her teeth, the shard warming like a stone left too near a fire — then stop, dig, carve, release. Walk again.

Isolde named the practice, because Isolde named everything. "You're a ferryman now," she announced on the fifth morning, while Marguerite tried to eat porridge that had achieved a texture best described as philosophical. "A grim little ferryman with terrible boots. In my day the gifted kept to the shrines and the dead came to them politely. Now look at us. Walking the country like peddlers."

"You were a queen," Marguerite said. "Everything is beneath you."

"Everything *was* beneath me. That was the problem." A pause, and then, quieter, in the tone Isolde used perhaps twice a month: "Keep doing it anyway. The villages. Even if it slows us."

"Why?"

"Because Aldric counts on the dead staying restless. Restless dead feed him. Every grave you quiet is a coin taken out of his purse." The ghost's voice hardened. "And because I signed the document that made this possible, girl, and I have eleven years of interest to pay. You're not slowing us down. You're paying faster than I ever did."

Tomas, across the fire, pretended very hard to be cleaning his weapons. He'd gotten good at pretending. He'd also, Marguerite noticed, gotten good at the digging — he could open a trench grave in a quarter hour now, working silently, efficiently, with the same competence he'd once used for other purposes entirely. He never asked whether the dead approved of his help. He simply assumed they didn't, and worked anyway, and that, Margueride thought, was its own kind of penance.

The sixth village had children's graves.

She knew before she knelt. The buzzing was different — thinner, higher, like wind through a keyhole. The trench behind the burned granary was short. That was all she allowed herself to think about it: the trench was short, and the earth over it had been patted down by hands, many hands, the way you pat earth over something you couldn't bear to leave rough.

She got halfway through the carving before her own eyes blurred, and Isolde said nothing at all, which from Isolde was a kind of embrace.

When the charm went into the ground, the silence that followed had a different quality. Lighter. And for one moment — she would swear this later, and Tomas would neither confirm nor deny it — the grass in the trench bloomed with small white flowers that had no business blooming in autumn.

Tomas stood apart, facing away. His shoulders moved once, and stilled.

"My orders built this," he said, to the trees, to nobody. "Not Aldric. Aldric signs things. I was the man who arrived in the square and read the names off the list. I was the face they'll remember, if anyone's left to remember."

"The face who just dug up a granary floor with his bare hands," Marguerite said.

"That doesn't balance the ledger."

"No," she agreed. "But it opens a new page." She held out the carved disc, the last of his coat-buttons spent. "Here. Keep this one."

"I don't want it."

"I know. Keep it anyway. That's how ledgers work — you carry them."

He took it. He put it in his breast pocket, over the heart, where a man keeps things he intends to answer for someday, and they walked on.

* * *

On the ninth night they found someone alive.

An old woman in a root cellar beneath a collapsed byre, living on turnips and stubbornness, who came up swinging a fire poker at the first sound of footsteps and caught Tomas squarely across the forearm before Marguerite could shout a warning.

"Good swing," Tomas said, cradling his arm, sounding genuinely admiring. "Elbow more, wrist less. You'd have broken it."

"Don't you lecture me, boy, I buried two sons." But she lowered the poker, squinting. "You're not soldiers. Soldiers don't apologize with their posture."

They shared her turnips. Her name was Berta, and her village had emptied eight months ago, and she'd stayed because, she said, "someone has to tell them where the fence posts go when they come home, and they will come home wrong, and they'll need pointing at their own houses." She said it matter-of-factly, the way you report weather.

Marguerite asked, gently, if she'd seen anything. After dark. Near the trenches.

Berta's jaw tightened. "Every night. Lights. Like lanterns under water. I put out food on the wall sometimes, the way you would. My mother did it for the fair folk; I do it for my boys." She looked at Marguerite with sudden fierce attention. "You're one of them, aren't you. One of the gifted. They said the King's men were hunting your kind. Said you were the reason the dead walk."

"The dead walk," Isolde said coldly, "because your king broke a covenant older than his bloodline, and if you repeat that slander I shall haunt you personally, madam, and I assure you I am extremely annoying."

Marguerite laughed — surprised into it, the first real laugh since Hollowmere — and covered it by coughing. "No," she told Berta. "We're the reason they can rest. If you'll let me, I'll see to your sons tonight."

Berta looked at her for a long time. Then she nodded once, briskly, the nod of a woman adding a chore to a list. "Wash your hands first. My mother's rules. Dead don't mind being spoken to, but they mind being handled dirty."

The work that night was easy, almost sweet. Two graves side by side, marked with crossed sticks and a strip of blue cloth Berta had tied there herself, every week, for eight months. When the charms settled and the lights under the earth went out — went *home*, Marguerite thought, not out — Berta stood at her fence in the moonlight and wept without any noise at all, and then made them all tea, and told Tomas his arm would scar, and that he should count himself lucky it was only her and not her late husband, who'd had better reach.

In the morning she pressed a parcel on them: bread, salt pork, and a wooden whistle she claimed her youngest son had carved. "For the road. When it gets quiet — and it will get quiet, dearie, that's the worst part — blow on it. Noise reminds the world somebody's still here."

Tomas carried the whistle in his pocket next to the bone button. Marguerite saw him touch both, sometimes, walking. Checking they were still there. Checking he was still the kind of man who carried such things.

* * *

It was on the twelfth day that the land changed.

Not gradually. They crested a hill at midmorning and the valley below was simply *wrong* — green going grey in patches, as if frost had fallen selectively, and the stream at its bottom running sluggish and dark. And the buzzing behind Marguerite's teeth rose to a whine so sharp she dropped to one knee.

"There are hundreds," she gasped. "Hundreds of dead, all at once, and none of them resting. None of them even trying."

"A garrison town," Tomas said, and his face had gone the color of the hills. "Calverston. It resisted the levy. It—" He stopped. Started again. "It stopped resisting."

They went down into it because Marguerite insisted and Isolde insisted harder, and because Tomas, when it came to it, no longer argued with either of them, which Marguerite suspected was its own kind of surrender.

Calverston had walls, and the walls had been breached, and inside the breach the air itself felt thick, like wading through cold soup. The dead here weren't whispers. They were a crowd. They pressed against Marguerite's senses from every direction — hungry, confused, furious, *counted*, she realized, they had been counted and catalogued and *used*, and something in the shard flared hot against her skin as if Isolde had drawn breath to scream.

"Marguerite." The queen's voice, stripped of all its dryness. "This is a harvest field. This is what he does with towns that refuse. Look at the keep."

The keep's doors stood open. Inside, in the great hall, someone had drawn a circle on the flagstones in channels — grooves cut deep and stained dark — and around the circle, arranged like cordwood, like inventory, were bones. Sorted. Labeled in Aldric's neat administrative script.

Tomas looked at the sorted bones for a long time. When he spoke, his voice was almost conversational, which is how Marguerite knew it was the worst thing he'd ever said.

"I requisitioned the wagons for this transport. Twelve of them. I remember thinking it was efficient. Twelve wagons, one trip." He turned to her, and his eyes were wet and absolutely level. "I need you to understand that I'm not telling you this so you'll forgive me. I'm telling you so that when we get to the free cities and someone asks why a former inquisitor deserves shelter, you'll have the true answer ready."

Marguerite took his hand — the burned one, the one Berta had promised would scar — and pressed it flat against the keystone shard at her throat.

"Feel that?" she said.

"It's warm."

"It's *angry*. It's angrier than I've ever felt it. Do you know why? Because the dead in this room aren't angry at you, Tomas. They don't know you. They're angry at the man who ordered this, and he lives in a palace eating off gold plate, and every restless soul here feeds him another year of it." She held his gaze. "You want to balance the ledger? Help me empty it. All of it. Right now, today, this room first."

It took them until dusk. Hundreds of charms — she ran out of bone and used the labeled tags themselves, scraping Aldric's script blank and scoring spirals in their place, which Isolde declared "the single most satisfying act of vandalism I have witnessed in life or death." Tomas dug. Marguerite carved. Isolde directed, calling the old forms like a conductor, and somewhere past midnight the pressure lifted — all at once, like a storm breaking — and Calverston's air went thin and clean and ordinary for the first time in a year.

They slept in the byre of a house whose owners would never return, and Marguerite dreamed of nothing at all, which felt like a gift.

* * *

They crossed out of the harvested lands on the fifteenth morning, and the change was immediate: birdsong returning, the shard cooling to a comfortable hum, the world remembering its colors. By afternoon they could see, far off across the plain, the first watchtowers of the free cities' borderlands — squat stone fingers against the sky.

"We'll make the gates in three days," Tomas said, shading his eyes. "If the roads hold."

"If the gates open," Marguerite said.

He glanced at her. "You think they won't."

"I think," she said carefully, "that we're three refugees with no papers, one of whom is the most wanted woman in the kingdom, one of whom is a defected inquisitor whose face is on notices in every market square, and one of whom is dead." She ticked them off on her fingers. "I think the gates might take convincing."

Behind her, Isolde made a sound that was suspiciously close to a snort. "Convincing. Girl, I ruled half this continent. Their grandparents bowed to me. If the council elders have any sense of history—"

"You were also murdered by the current king," Marguerite pointed out, "which makes you politically complicated."

"I am *dynamically* complicated, thank you."

Tomas smiled — briefly, crookedly, the smile he still seemed surprised to find he owned — and then it faded, because he'd gone still, head tilted, listening to something beyond the range of human ears.

Or beyond the range of *most* human ears. Because Marguerite heard it too, a moment later: hooves. Many. Behind them, back along the road they'd come, back toward the smoke-grey valleys and the quieted graves.

Back along the trail of released dead they had left stretching behind them like a lit fuse.

"Veyra," Tomas said quietly. "She's been following the quiet. Every village we've freed is a marker on a map. We haven't been hiding, Margo. We've been *signposting*."

The hoofbeats grew louder. Far off, at the edge of sight, dust rose against the southern sky — organized, deliberate, advancing.

Marguerite's hand closed around the shard, and Isolde's voice came sharp and clear as a war-horn:

"Run."
