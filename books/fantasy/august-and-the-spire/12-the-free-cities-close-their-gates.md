## 12. The Free Cities Close Their Gates

The walls of Corvane rose out of the morning mist like the jaw of some enormous, patient animal, and Marguerite thought, with a weariness that had stopped being dramatic and become simply a fact of her body, *of course they're closed.*

She had imagined this moment differently, back when she'd still had the energy for imagining. In her better fantasies — which were fewer and shabbier these days than she cared to admit — the free cities would be a door opening. Lanterns. Warm bread. Someone saying *you're safe now* in a voice that meant it.

Instead there was a wall, and a gatehouse, and a line of refugees stretching back down the road into the fog like beads on a string that someone had dropped and couldn't be bothered to pick up.

"They're not letting anyone through," Tomas said quietly beside her. He'd been talking to the people in the queue for the better part of an hour, moving along it with his hood up, listening. He was good at that — at being unremarkable in crowds. A lifetime of wearing grey would do that to a man. "A farmer's family has been here nine days. Nine days. They're sleeping in their cart."

Marguerite leaned against the milestone and let the stone take her weight. Her legs had opinions about standing. So did her back, her shoulders, and the deep bruise along her ribs from the ford, which had turned the color of a stormy sunset and complained whenever she breathed too fully.

"Nine days," she repeated.

"There's a woman two carts back with a fever. I gave her my cloak."

"You'll freeze."

"I run hot," Tomas said, with the small, stubborn smile he used when he was lying about something minor so he wouldn't have to argue about it. "Inquisitor's constitution."

"You're not an inquisitor anymore."

"I'm working on my new constitution. It's a work in progress."

Despite everything — despite the road, the hunger, the dead murmuring at the edge of her hearing like rain against a window — Marguerite felt her mouth twitch. It was a terrible thing about Tomas, that he could do that. That even now, with Veyra's army somewhere behind them eating the countryside mile by mile, he could find the exact seam in her despair and slip a joke into it.

*Don't get used to him,* said a voice in her head that sounded unpleasantly like prudence.

*Too late,* said another voice, which sounded like hers, and was honest about it.

*Oh, stop both of you,* said Isolde, from the keystone shard at Marguerite's throat. The queen's voice always arrived with a faint chill, like air off a glacier. "If you two are finished flirting over the refugee queue, I'd like to point out that the gate is opening."

*We're not flirting,* Marguerite said, and heard how unconvincing it was even inside her own skull.

*Mm,* said Isolde, in the tone of a woman who had ruled a court full of liars and had developed a professional appreciation for bad ones.

The gates did open. A horn blew — three short notes, flat and joyless — and a company of city guards came out in mail the blue-grey of Corvane's livery, forming a line across the road. Behind them, on a wooden platform hastily erected against the gatehouse, stood a row of figures in robes: the Council of Corvane, or a delegation of it. At their center was an old man with a white beard cropped close as a hedge and eyes like two coins left in a well — you could see the shine, but not the bottom.

He raised his hands, and the murmur of the queue went quiet, because queues learn quickly who holds the bread.

"People of the road," the elder called. His voice was surprisingly strong, worn smooth rather than cracked by age, like a stair worn concave by centuries of feet. "I am Elder Cassian. The Council has asked me to speak, because I am old enough that no one will mind if I say what everyone else is thinking."

Someone in the crowd laughed. It was thin, but it was real, and Marguerite felt the whole line lean toward him slightly, the way plants lean toward light.

"The Council knows why you have come. Aldric's hunt grows bolder. His armies burn east of the river. And you come to us asking for our walls." He paused. "And the Council says no."

The silence that followed was worse than any sound. Marguerite watched it move down the queue like wind through wheat — heads bowing, shoulders settling, a child starting to cry and being hushed before the cry could become hopelessness.

"Aldric has sent envoys," Cassian continued. "He offers us continued peace if we refuse harbor to the gifted and turn away those who shelter them. If we open these gates, we make ourselves a target. Some among you will say: then let us be targets! Honor demands it." He shook his head slowly. "Honor does not feed four thousand mouths behind this wall when the granaries burn. I have buried one city already. I will not bury this one."

*He's not wrong,* Isolde said, and there was something bitter in it. *That's the filthiest trick of it. Aldric doesn't need to breach these walls. He only needs to make closing them feel like wisdom.*

Tomas had gone very still beside her. She could see his jaw working, the muscle jumping under the skin. This was the arithmetic of his old life, she realized. This was the ledger every inquisitor carried: the few burned so the many might sleep. He had spent years believing that sum came out even.

"It's not wisdom," Marguerite said aloud, barely above a whisper. "It's just fear wearing wisdom's coat."

"Then we'll have to take the coat off it," Tomas said.

* * *

They didn't get near the platform, of course. Guards moved the queue back, and the delegation withdrew behind the gates, and the great doors swung shut with a boom that Marguerite felt in her teeth. But Tomas, who had a talent for finding the crack in any wall — physical, bureaucratic, or moral — spent the afternoon doing what he did best, which was talking to people whose job made them invisible.

By evening he returned to the inn-yard where they'd rented space in a hay barn — paid for with the last of the coin they'd taken from a waystation strongbox, and Marguerite tried not to think about whether that counted as theft anymore, because they were well past the country where such distinctions mattered — carrying two bowls of barley soup and an expression she'd learned to read as *I have news, and you won't like all of it.*

"Elder Cassian will see petitioners tomorrow," he said, handing her a bowl. "One hour after dawn. But—" he held up a finger, "—only petitioners with a case the Council deems material. Refugees seeking asylum? Not material. Traders with toll disputes? Material. Land claims? Very material."

"So we lie about what we are."

"We don't lie. We *reframe*." He sat down on the hay bale next to her, close enough that their shoulders touched, and she didn't move away, and neither did he, and they both pretended not to notice, which had become its own kind of language between them. "You're not a refugee. You're an envoy bearing intelligence regarding the realm's military situation. I'm your escort, formerly of the inquisition, defecting with actionable knowledge of Aldric's command structure. And Isolde—"

*Isolde,* said the ghost dryly, *is a shard of rock in a girl's necklace.*

"You're the intelligence," Marguerite said.

There was a pause.

"That," Isolde said, "is either very flattering or very insulting, and I haven't decided which."

"Both," Marguerite and Tomas said together, and then looked at each other, and looked away, and Marguerite concentrated very hard on her soup.

* * *

Dawn came grey and cold, and the council chamber smelled of tallow smoke and old decisions. It was a round room — Marguerite noticed that first, the way the architecture curved around them, seven chairs set in a ring beneath a dome painted with constellations so faded they'd become suggestions rather than stars. Seven elders, and Cassian at the center, and guards at every archway, and Marguerite standing on the mosaic floor feeling like a specimen pinned under glass.

She was so tired. That was the thing no one warned you about — not the danger, not the grief, but the sheer grinding cost of staying upright, day after day, when every part of you wanted to lie down in the road and let the world go on without you. She could feel the dead pressing at the edges of the city, hundreds of them, refugees of a different kind, crowding against whatever boundary the free cities maintained between themselves and the unrest beyond. They weren't hostile. They were just *there*, the way weather is there, and she hadn't slept properly in weeks, and the keystone shard hummed against her sternum like a second heartbeat.

"State your business," said an elder to her left, a woman with rings on every finger and the expression of someone who had audited better liars than this one.

Tomas stepped forward and delivered the introduction they'd rehearsed — smooth, measured, the inquisitor's cadence repurposed. He named the garrisons he'd served. He named the officers. He described the structure of the hunt, its supply lines, its quotas. Marguerite watched the elders' faces and saw the words land where he wanted them: not with horror, but with *calculation*. These were people who understood armies. Armies they could weigh.

"And the girl?" Cassian asked at last. His well-eyes settled on her. "Your escort speaks of war. What do you speak of?"

Marguerite opened her mouth to give the rehearsed answer.

What came out instead was the truth.

"I speak of the covenant," she said. "The old one. Between the living and the dead. Your city stands on it — all the free cities do. Why do you think the unrest hasn't crossed your borders? Why do you think your graveyards sleep while the rest of the realm's dead walk? Someone built a wall against that long before your ancestors built this one, and Aldric broke it, and the broken pieces are the reason his machines run and his soldiers don't tire and his spies hear whispers in the dark." She lifted the cord at her throat, and the shard caught the dome-light, pale as moonstone. "This is a piece of it. The keystone can be reforged. I know how. I'm the one who has to do it."

Silence. The kind that has weight.

Then Elder Cassian leaned forward, and his voice, when it came, was gentle in the way that surgeons are gentle.

"Child," he said, "do you know how many gifted souls have stood on that floor and told us the world could be saved if we only helped them? Do you know how many of them were right, and how many were mad, and how we told the difference afterward?" He spread his hands. "Proof. That is what the Council requires. Not testimony. Not relics. Proof that the covenant can be reforged — proof that aiding you buys the realm anything except Aldric's retaliation. Bring us proof, and Corvane opens more than its gates. Fail, and you'll wish you'd never knocked."

* * *

They walked out through the archway into the grey morning, and Marguerite made it exactly eleven steps past the gate before her legs informed her they were done negotiating.

She sat down on the mounting block outside the gatehouse, and Tomas sat beside her, and for a long while neither of them spoke. The refugee queue shuffled behind them. Somewhere a cart wheel needed grease and was announcing it to the world.

"Proof," she said finally. "Of course. Proof. As if I'm carrying a merchant's sample case." She laughed, and it came out wrong, brittle at the edges. "I have a rock and a ghost and a road full of graves, and they want *proof*."

"You have more than that."

"Do I?" She turned on him, and the exhaustion tore the guard off her voice, and everything underneath came out raw. "Because from where I'm sitting, Tomas, I have nothing. Odile is dead. The valley is burned. Every city locks its doors, every village hides its children, and the one person who might have taught me what I actually am died refusing to say my name to men with torches. I'm twenty-three years old and I am so tired, and the dead don't stop, they never stop, and I don't even know if reforging the keystone will *work*, or what it costs, or—" Her breath hitched. "Or whether anyone out there will ever open a single door for us again."

The words hung in the cold air. She hadn't meant to say most of them. Saying them changed nothing and changed everything; that was the treachery of despair, that it felt like honesty and behaved like surrender.

Tomas didn't tell her it would be all right. She loved him a little for that — for knowing the difference between comfort and lies.

Instead he said, quietly: "When I falsified my first report, I told myself it was a small thing. One child. One name left off a list. And it was small, and it changed nothing, and I did it anyway, because it was the only true thing I could do that day." He looked at her. "Maybe proof isn't something you show a council, Marguerite. Maybe it's something you leave behind you. Village by village. Grave by grave. Every dead soul you put to rest is a door that opens somewhere — maybe not here, maybe not today, but somewhere, for someone."

*He's right,* Isolde said, and her voice had lost its chill. *I built a kingdom on the belief that power must be proven before it can be trusted. It nearly destroyed everything. Don't learn statecraft from me, girl. Learn from him.*

Marguerite sat with it. The cold seeped through her clothes. The cart wheel squeaked. And slowly, grudgingly, like a fire catching in wet wood, something rekindled — not hope, exactly. Hope was too grand a word for what she had left. More like the stubborn ember underneath hope, the part that refuses.

"Grave by grave," she repeated.

"Grave by grave," Tomas agreed. "Besides — I may have mentioned to Elder Cassian's steward that we possess intelligence regarding movements of Aldric's northern garrisons. Intelligence he'd find... persuasive. He seemed interested."

Marguerite stared at him. "You booked us a second audience."

"I reframed," Tomas said serenely. "Also I may have implied you were the bone-carver the entire hunt is looking for, and that delivering you safely to allies is worth considerably more than delivering you to a wall that closes."

"You *what*?"

"He was going to figure it out eventually. Better he hears it from a charming defector than from Veyra's scouts." He shrugged. "Desperate times, Marguerite. Even honesty needs a good salesman."

She should have been angry. Instead, absurdly, she felt the corner of her mouth lift — the first time in days. "You're impossible."

"I'm *resourceful*. There's a difference. Impossible people die interesting deaths; resourceful people die boring ones, much later, in bed."

* * *

They left Corvane the next morning, before the second audience — because Isolde, in the night, had gone quiet in a way that meant she'd seen something, and when she finally spoke, her voice was urgent as winter:

*Veyra's outriders reached the river ford two days ago. They're asking after a bone-carver and a defected inquisitor. The Council knows. Cassian knows. And Cassian, girl, is deciding right now whether selling you to Aldric buys Corvane another year of peace.* A pause. *Whatever proof you're going to give anyone — give it fast, and give it running.*

So they went north, out the pilgrim's postern before the gates woke, two small figures on a road full of locked doors, carrying a rock, a ghost, and the stubborn ember of a promise: that somewhere ahead, someone would believe them without needing proof at all.

Behind them, Corvane's walls sank back into the mist.

Ahead, the road bent toward the shrine country — and toward whatever the dead were waiting to ask of her there.
