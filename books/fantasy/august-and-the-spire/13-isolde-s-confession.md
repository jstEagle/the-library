## 13. Isolde's Confession

The dead do not sleep, but we pretend to.

I had been pretending for eleven years when the wagon jolted over a rut and Marguerite Quintana woke with my voice still caught between her ears like a splinter of ice. She sat up too fast in the dark, cracked her head on the wagon's low beam — I heard it, felt something like an echo of it, ghosts are sympathetic instruments whether they wish to be or not — and said, into the blackness, "You're still here."

"I'm always here," I said. "That is rather the whole of my tragedy."

She fumbled for the keystone shard on its cord around her neck. In the faint grey light that came through the slats, her face was drawn and young and furious, which was, I will admit, a face I recognized. I had worn it myself once, in a palace far from this road, arguing with men who believed that grief was a currency only kings were permitted to spend.

"You could have told me sooner," she said. "That you were in here. That you were *her*."

"I could have," I agreed. "And you would have done what? Thrown the shard into a river? Prayed? Screamed at a stone?" I let a pause sit between us, the way one lets a pause sit before delivering news that will bruise. "I am a coward, Marguerite. I have been one for a very long time. It seemed prudent not to add honesty to my list of failings until I knew you wouldn't simply leave me in a ditch."

"Leaving you in a ditch has occurred to me," she said, and there it was — the ghost of a smile under all that fear. Good. She would need it. What I had to tell her required a spine, and spines are kept strong by laughter the way walls are kept strong by mortar: invisibly, and only noticed when absent.

* * *

We traveled by day and hid by night, or rather Tomas hid us, because Tomas had a talent for looking official in places where officialdom was fatal. He had burned his commission in the Bone Market and yet somehow the world still arranged itself around him as though he carried papers. Men stepped aside. Dogs did not bark. I found this fascinating and told him so, once, through Marguerite, and he went the color of old brick and said nothing, which told me a great deal about how often anyone spoke kindly to him at all.

But this story is mine, and I have put off telling it for eleven years, and the dead are owed their debts even when the debt-collector is a girl of twenty with bone dust under her fingernails.

So: I will tell you how Queen Isolde of the Vale died, and worse, I will tell you what she did before it.

* * *

You must understand what the covenant was, truly was, before Aldric broke it — because everyone speaks of the breaking now, the shattering, the theft, and no one speaks of the signing. The signing was mine. My hand held the pen. My seal melted into the wax. And I knew — I want that written somewhere that cannot be burned — I *knew* what the terms cost, and I signed anyway, because the terms were written so that the cost fell on people who could not refuse them.

The covenant was older than my grandmother's grandmother. It bound the living and the dead into a single conversation. The dead gave counsel; the living gave remembrance. A farmer who died was remembered by name at his village shrine each season, and in exchange his wisdom stayed near his fields, gentle as weather. A queen who died was remembered by her people, and in exchange she might whisper warnings down the long corridors of the living world. Nothing passed between the worlds without payment on both sides. That was the covenant's heart: reciprocity. No taking without giving. No silence imposed.

The Spire held the keystone, and the keystone held the balance, and the balance held everything.

And then came the war I helped start.

* * *

Ah, you flinch. Good. Flinching means you are listening.

There was a border dispute — there is always a border dispute; borders are where nations keep their excuses — and the eastern lords wanted the river valleys, and my husband, King Edvard, who was a decent man and a terrible king, wanted peace, and I wanted him to be *strong*, because I was thirty-one years old and I had been told since childhood that a queen's worth was measured in her husband's victories. I pushed him toward war. I wrote letters. I chose the words that made hesitation sound like weakness. I was, in the language of your valley, a carver myself — I carved my husband out of wood and then wondered why he moved like a puppet.

The war went badly. Wars begun for vanity go badly; wars begun for anything go badly; that is the first lesson of war and the last. Our dead came home in wagons, and then they stopped coming home at all, because there were too many, and the shrines filled past holding, and the dead grew restless — not wicked, mind you, never wicked, only *unheard*, which the living cannot distinguish from wickedness if they try.

And into that restlessness walked a young general named Aldric Vane, with a proposal.

He had found, he said, a way to draw strength from the unrest itself. The dead's agitation, properly channeled — properly *harvested* — could power engines, ward armies, extend life. All it required was a revision to the covenant. A small amendment. An efficiency.

I should have had him hanged. I want you to know that I know that. There were laws for men who trafficked with the restless dead; they were old laws and good ones, written in blood that was not metaphorical. Instead I listened, because his proposal came wrapped in the one thing I could not resist: it promised victory, and victory would make Edvard strong, and Edvard's strength would be mine, reflected, the way moonlight belongs to the sun.

The amendment was elegant. That is the horror of it — it was *elegant*. Instead of every dead soul being remembered individually, the covenant would consolidate. Remembrance would flow upward, pooled, administered. The dead of the poor — the farmers, the soldiers, the laundresses, the children taken by fever — their names would be held in trust by the crown rather than spoken at their village shrines. In trust. Do you hear it? Even now, eleven years dead, I hear it, the soft bureaucratic hiss of that phrase. *In trust.* Their remembrance would be stored, managed, allocated to purposes of state.

And the purposes of state, it turned out, were Aldric's purposes.

Because here is what the amendment actually did, though it took me two years and my own murder to understand it: it silenced the voiceless dead. A queen remembered by ten thousand mourners keeps her voice. A soldier remembered by no one — his mother dead, his village burned, his name struck from a ledger mislaid in some archive — a soldier remembered by no one has no purchase on the world at all. He becomes raw material. The pooled remembrance became a reservoir, and Aldric built his machines on the shore of it, and drank from it, and grew.

I signed it because the cost fell on people who could not refuse. That is the sentence. That is the whole confession, and everything else is decoration. I signed it because the price would be paid by those with no power to say no, and I thought — I *thought*, God forgive the arithmetic of cowards — that as long as the ledger balanced, as long as someone paid, the covenant held, and what did it matter whose coin was spent?

It mattered. It was the only thing that mattered. A covenant is not a contract between crowns. It is a promise between every living soul and every dead one, and you cannot spend another person's portion of a promise and call the account settled.

* * *

Marguerite had gone very still across the campfire. Tomas was checking the horses, pretending — badly — not to listen, though I noticed his hands had stopped moving some minutes ago.

"So the hunt," she said slowly. "Aldric hunting the gifted. It isn't only fear of magic."

"No," I said. "Fear is the banner. The harvest is the purpose. Every gifted soul he kills is a soul whose connection to the dead ran deep — carvers, hearth-speakers, veil-touchers. They were the last ones still paying the old covenant's price directly, still remembering the dead by name, still keeping small accounts open that his great reservoir couldn't swallow. Each one silenced makes the pool deeper. Each one makes him stronger." I paused. "And each one makes the dead louder, because unheard things do not vanish, Marguerite. They accumulate. He is building a dam across a river that remembers being a river."

"And the keystone?"

"The keystone was the lock on the original terms. As long as it stood whole at the Spire, the amendment could be repealed — any heir, any council, any honest clerk with the right authority could restore the old covenant, name by name, shrine by shrine. Aldric couldn't allow that. So he didn't repeal it and he didn't destroy it, because destroying it would have unmade the covenant entirely and left him with nothing to harvest. He *shattered* it and scattered the pieces, and declared himself the sole administrator of remembrance. King of the ledger. Keeper of the pool." My laugh came out bitter, and ghosts' laughs carry strangely; Marguerite hugged her arms against it. "I made him possible. Understand that. He did not corrupt my covenant. He *used* it, exactly as designed, exactly as I signed it — a system where the powerful administer what the powerless owe. He simply stopped pretending the administration served anyone but himself."

* * *

Now the murder. You may as well have all of it.

By the third year of the amended covenant, I had begun to hear them. That is the irony that will follow me past the end of the world: the amendment that silenced the voiceless dead gave *me* — keeper of the seal, signatory, queen — a clearer channel than any queen had ever had. Their remembrance pooled through my crown, and I heard them at night, in the marble corridors, a sound like wind through a keyhole that was also, unmistakably, weeping.

I tried to repeal it. I want that recorded too, though I know it buys me little. I drafted the restoration. I gathered three lords I trusted. And Aldric — who had been drinking from the pool for three years by then, who had grown tall and strange and smooth-faced in a way that made the court ladies whisper — Aldric learned of it, because I had taught him well where power lived, and I had foolishly left it living in me.

They say poison. The histories say poison, a fever, a sad accident of the season. It was neither. He came to my chambers himself, at night, with the courtesy he always wore like armor, and he thanked me. That is the detail no history records and I cannot stop repeating: he *thanked* me. For the amendment. For teaching him that a covenant could be revised by whoever held the pen. "You showed me the shape of the thing," he said. "I've only completed it."

Then he pressed his palm to my forehead, and he drew my remembrance out of me while I still breathed — all of it, the pool's first great withdrawal, the proof of concept. I felt my name leave me. I felt my mother's face go. I felt Edvard's face go, and our daughter—

Our daughter.

Her name was Maren. She was six when I died. She had a gap between her front teeth and a habit of naming things twice — "horse-horse," "moon-moon" — as though once weren't enough to hold them in the world. Aldric took my memory of her along with everything else, and I have been dead eleven years, and I cannot tell you what she looked like. I can tell you she existed. I can tell you I loved her. The love remained when the face went, the way a hook remains when the fish is landed, and I have hung on that hook for eleven years in the dark of a shard of pale stone, and I am telling you this now, Marguerite, because you deserve to know what you are carrying.

You are carrying a woman who sold the voiceless to buy a victory, and was murdered by the man she armed, and forgot her own child's face, and deserved it, and did not deserve it, both, forever, in the same breath.

That is my confession. I have no absolution to request. The dead don't get absolution; we get *witnesses*. So witness me. Witness the signature. Witness the thanks he gave me. Witness Maren, horse-horse, moon-moon, six years old, unremembered by the one person whose remembering was supposed to be her birthright.

Witness it, and then finish what I started and could not: bring the keystone home.

* * *

The fire had burned to embers. Marguerite was crying without noise, the way people cry who learned early that noise costs something, and I hated that I had taught her nothing gentler than that.

Tomas crouched at the fire's edge, feeding it a stick he didn't need to feed it. When he finally spoke, his voice was rough. "My report on Hollowmere said the shrine was empty. I wrote that the carver's apprentice was never identified." He stared into the flames. "I've been telling myself that was the first dishonest thing I ever did for a good reason. Listening to her—" he nodded at the shard, at me, awkwardly, the way men nod at ghosts they can't see, "—it wasn't the first. It was just the first one I noticed."

"Welcome to the ledger," I said, and Marguerite, wet-eyed, startled, actually laughed — a small broken sound, but real.

"That's not comforting," she said.

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be true, which is rarer." I softened, as much as a voice in a stone can soften. "Listen to me, both of you. I am not asking you to forgive me. Forgiveness is for the living to give and I have robbed most of mine of the chance. I am asking you to do better than I did. When the moment comes — and it is coming, it rolls toward us like Veyra's army, which, incidentally, is closer than any of you think, three days perhaps, four, I can feel her scouts the way you feel weather in an old wound — when the moment comes and the price of doing right lands on someone who can't refuse it, look at the ledger. Look at whose coin is being spent. And refuse, even if refusing costs you everything you were planning to keep."

Marguerite looked down at the shard in her palm. "What did it cost you to tell me this?"

"Everything I had left," I said honestly. "And it was heavy, and I am lighter now, and lighter is not the same as free. But it is a direction."

Tomas fed the fire another stick. Somewhere east, beyond the dark hills, I felt the hunt turning toward us like a slow wheel, and within it a cold bright point — Veyra, and the shard-sense she carried, drawing a line through the night straight to the pale stone warming against a tired girl's breastbone.

Three days. Perhaps four.

I did not tell them that part after all. Some ledgers, I decided, could wait until morning.
