## 14. The Hunt Closes

The map room of Aldric's palace had once been a chapel.

He remembered this because he had ordered the conversion himself, eleven years ago, and because remembering was his profession now. The stained glass had come down — too much color in it, too much human joy — and in its place stood a table the length of three wagons, carved from black oak and inlaid with silver rivers and tin mountains. On it, the whole of the realm lay flat and obedient, the only country he had ever truly loved.

He stood over it each morning with a cup of wine that no longer tasted of anything, moving little brass markers across the tin mountains like a man playing a game against an opponent who refused to sit down.

Today's marker was a sliver of bone-white stone, no bigger than a thumbnail, that he had placed himself on the northern road between the ford at Callowmere and the free cities' border. It represented the keystone shard. It represented, though he would never say so aloud, everything going wrong.

"You're brooding," said Veyra from the doorway.

"I'm strategizing. Brooding is for men with fewer armies."

Veyra crossed the room the way she did everything: without wasted motion, without apology. She was sixty if she was a day, grey-cropped and iron-spined, with a face like a cliff that had decided long ago not to be scenic about it. She had served his father before him, and had buried two husbands and one king, and Aldric trusted her more than he trusted his own reflection — which, these days, was a low bar. His reflection had begun to look tired in ways sleep could not fix.

"Report," he said.

"The shard moved north through the hill country. Slowly. They stopped." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile. "They stopped to bury people."

Aldric set down his cup. "Bury whom?"

"Conscripts. Deserters. The unmarked dead along the road." Veyra picked up a brass marker — one of his own garrison towns — and turned it idly in her fingers. "The bone-carver has been pulling splinters out of my war as she runs. Every village she passes through, the restless quiet down. My supply lines through the hill country have stopped needing escorts, because the ghosts there have gone to sleep instead of screaming the mules off the cliffs."

"That sounds like good news."

"It would be," Veyra said, "if I were running a country. I'm running a hunt. And a hunt needs its quarry frightened, Your Majesty, not *comforted*." She set the marker down with a small click. "There's a waystation at Harrow Fen. Old coaching house, run by a widow named Sef and her brother. The fugitives stayed two nights. The widow fed them. The brother — a former quartermaster of mine, as it happens — gave them horses and didn't report them."

Aldric looked at the map. Harrow Fen was a small notch of ink on the northern road, the kind of place that existed on maps the way freckles existed on faces: technically, and without consequence.

"And your recommendation?"

Veyra met his eyes. She always did. That was the thing about Veyra; everyone else in the palace had learned to look slightly past him, at his crown or his shoulder or the middle distance, as though meeting his gaze directly might cost them something. She looked at him the way she'd look at any officer whose orders she intended to follow and whose soul she intended to note the condition of.

"My recommendation is that we burn it," she said. "The waystation, the widow, the brother, the barn, the well rope, all of it. Publicly enough that the next village thinks twice about feeding strays. Quietly enough that the free cities can't make a sermon of it." A pause. "You should know what you're ordering, sire. Sef's brother lost a leg at Redwater. Fighting for you."

"I know what I'm ordering, General."

"Do you?" she asked, not cruelly. Veyra was never cruel. Cruelty required heat, and there was nothing warm left in her questions — they came out of her like stones from a sling. "Because I've watched you order things for eleven years, and lately you sign the orders the way a man signs for bread. I'd like to know whether that's confidence or hunger."

It was, of course, hunger. But a king does not say so.

"Burn it," Aldric said.

* * *

Here is what the histories will never record, because Aldric made sure of it: the covenant between the living and the dead was not broken by accident.

Eleven years ago, kneeling in the crypt beneath the old cathedral with his brother's crown still warm from the coronation he had stolen rather than inherited, Aldric had taken up a hammer and shattered the keystone that anchored the boundary between the quick and the quiet. He had been told — by the thing in the dark that wore his father's voice — that the dead's unrest could be harvested. That grief was a fuel like any other, if you built the right engines. That a kingdom powered by its own mourning would never grow cold.

And it worked. Gods forgive him, it worked. The war-machines drank the unrest of ten thousand unquiet graves and ran hotter than any furnace. His armies marched without fear, because fear requires a future, and the dead had taught the living what happened to futures. And Aldric himself —

Aldric had stopped aging.

That was the part he had wanted most, in the beginning, when wanting was still something he recognized. He had told himself it was for the realm: a king who did not weaken, who did not die mid-reform, who could steer the ship of state for a century without the rot of succession. He had believed it the way young men believe things — completely, and in the direction of their appetites.

Now he was fifty-one years old and had the hands of a man of thirty, and he could not remember his daughter's face.

Not misplaced it. Not mislaid it among the clutter of a busy reign, where a determined man might dig it out again. It was *gone*, eaten, spent — poured into the engines with everything else, because the power took payment first from whatever its holder loved most, and he had loved her more than the crown, more than the throne, more than the clever machinery of his own survival. Lira. Seven years old when the fever took her, twelve years dead now, and he had fed her face into a war-engine somewhere around year four without even noticing the meal going down.

He kept a portrait of her in the west tower. He visited it sometimes, standing before it like a man reading a letter in a language he used to speak fluently. *This is my daughter,* he would tell himself. *I am told I loved her.* The portrait showed a girl with dark hair and a serious mouth. It could have been anyone's daughter. It could have been a stranger's child hung there by some sentimental servant.

Some nights he caught himself trying to reconstruct her from the painting the way scholars reconstruct extinct animals from a single tooth, and some nights he thought: *this is the price, and I knew the price, and I paid it, and the realm is strong.*

And some nights — fewer now, every year fewer — he thought: *what have I done.*

The nights were getting shorter. Not the literal nights; those behaved normally. The nights inside him, where the doubt lived. Each year the doubt had less room, the way a garden has less room when the ivy is patient. The power did not merely feed on grief. It fed on his capacity to grieve, which meant it fed on his capacity to mind, which meant that every horror he committed made the next horror easier to commit, and easier after that, a staircase descending so gently that a man could walk down it for a decade believing he was still on level ground.

He hated himself less each time. That was the true horror of it. Not that he felt nothing — he could remember feeling nothing, back when feeling nothing had been a relief. No: he felt *less bad*. The guilt arrived on schedule, smaller each season, like a bird returning to find its forest felled. Eleven years ago, the thought of burning a waystation full of civilians would have sickened him for a week. Five years ago, for a night. Now he signed the order and his hand was steady, and the steadiness itself was the wound, and he could not even properly feel the wound, because the power had already drunk it.

Somewhere in the palace, he supposed, there was still a man who would have wept for Sef the widow and her brother with the soldier's leg. Aldric occasionally went looking for that man, the way one checks on a house left empty. The rooms were cold. The furniture was covered in sheets. But the house still stood, and some animal part of him insisted that a house which stands can be lived in again.

He clung to that the way drowning men cling to driftwood that is visibly pulling them under.

* * *

The report came three days later, carried by a rider who had ridden hard enough to kill the horse, which Veyra's quartermasters noted with the resigned efficiency of men who had stopped expecting their general to spare anything.

Harrow Fen burned. The waystation, the barn, the widow, the brother. Two other souls besides — a tinker who had been sleeping in the loft, and a boy of perhaps fifteen who had been mucking stalls for his supper. The fugitives themselves had gone a day earlier. Veyra's riders found cold ashes and a road heading north.

Aldric read the report standing at the great map, and here is the thing he would have given anything — anything except, at this point, another memory — to be able to describe honestly:

It tasted good.

Not the deaths themselves. He was not so far gone as that; some flickering pilot light of the man he'd been still flinched at the boy in the stable. No — what tasted good was the *power*. Because that was the engine's design, the thing-in-the-dark's masterstroke: the harvest did not wait for the grave. Every death caused in service of the hunt fed the hunt's master directly, a tithe collected at the moment of taking, warm and sweet and immediate. Somewhere in his chest, behind the sternum, something drank, and warmed, and settled.

He felt the widow Sef go the way a man feels the first swallow of wine after a long thirst. He felt the brother, the quartermaster, the loyal legless soldier, and hated how much better that one tasted — betrayal-flavored, rich. He felt the tinker. He felt the boy, and the boy was the worst, because the boy was small and brief and bright, a candle rather than a lamp, and the sweetness of it made Aldric's eyes sting, and he could not tell anymore whether the stinging was grief or appetite.

Both, probably. The engine did not distinguish. That was the whole point of the engine.

"There it is," he murmured to the empty map room, and heard his own voice with a stranger's ears: calm, resonant, a voice built for proclamations. "There it is, and I hate that I love it, and I love that I hate it less, and tomorrow I will love it less still."

The words made a kind of arithmetic he could no longer solve.

He pressed his palm flat against the map, over the notch of ink where Harrow Fen had been. The oak was cool. Nothing under his hand but wood and tin and the geography of a country that fed him whether it wished to or not.

*One day,* he promised the house-with-sheets inside him. *One day I will stop.*

He had been promising it for six years. The promise, too, was getting quieter.

* * *

Veyra returned at dusk, smelling of woodsmoke and horse, and found him still at the map. She reported the operation in flat, complete sentences — casualties, distances, the disposition of her riders — and then, instead of leaving, stood there with her helmet under her arm like a woman working up to something.

"Out with it, General."

"Sire." She set the helmet on the corner of the map, which no one else in the realm would have dared. "The boy in the stable. Fifteen, maybe. When my sergeant put the torch to the hay, the boy ran for the door, and Corporal Denn cut him down." She paused. "Denn laughed."

Aldric waited. He had learned that Veyra's silences were load-bearing.

"Denn laughed," she repeated, "and I have known Denn nine years, and Denn does not laugh at killing. Denn cries at dogs in films. Something is coming off this army of ours, sire, like heat off a forge, and my soldiers are drinking it without knowing what it is, and they are starting to enjoy their work." Her cliff-face eyes held his. "I've followed you through worse than this. I'll follow you through this, if you order it. But I want to hear you say what it's for. Not the realm. Not the hunt. What it's *for*, in the dark, where you keep it."

And Aldric — who had lied to councils and parliaments and priests, who had lied so long and so smoothly that lying had become simply the shape his mouth made — opened his mouth to give her the standard answer, *the stability of the realm, General, the peace of the living*—

—and what came out instead, quietly, in the dusk-lit room that had once been a chapel, was:

"I don't remember my daughter's face."

Veyra went very still.

"The power takes memories," he said. "First the ones you love best. It's how it's paid for. And I have been telling myself for eleven years that the price was worth it, and every year I believe that a little more easily, and I no longer know whether that's wisdom or just the engine digesting me." He looked down at his hands, which were smooth and strong and thirty years old. "You asked what it's for. Honestly? I no longer remember. I think I may have eaten the answer."

For a long moment Veyra said nothing. Then she picked up her helmet, and Aldric braced for the sound of armor leaving, for the defection, for the knife.

What she said was: "Then we'd best catch these fugitives quickly, sire. Before there's nothing left of you to interrogate." A beat. "And when we do — I'll want to watch what the bone-carver does to that shard of yours very closely. Because if it can be reforged, it can be *unmade*. And I'd like to know, before I burn another waystation, whether I'm serving a king or a meal."

She left. Aldric stood alone with the map, and the brass marker on the northern road, and the fading warmth in his chest where a boy with a pitchfork had briefly, terribly, nourished him.

North, then. The shard was moving north, toward the Spire, toward the thin place at the world's edge where the covenant had first been struck and might yet be struck again. Toward the one outcome the thing in the dark feared, and therefore the one outcome Aldric, in the cold rooms of the house he still kept inside himself, secretly hoped for.

He lit a candle against the dusk and placed it, absurdly, ceremonially, on the map at Harrow Fen.

"For the boy," he told the empty room, and could not feel whether he meant it — and that, more than anything, was how he knew the hunt had to end.
