## 15. What the Dead Ask

The shrine found them before they found it.

They had been walking for two days through a forest that seemed to lean in around the road — old trees, older than any kingdom, their roots braided across the path like knuckles. Isolde had gone quiet since morning, which was itself an omen; the queen's ghost chattered when she was comfortable and went silent as a held breath when she was afraid.

"Here," Isolde said at last, her voice arriving inside Marguerite's skull like a cold fingertip against the temple. "Stop here. It's under the moss."

Tomas looked up from checking the straps on their packs. "Under the what?"

"The shrine." Marguerite was already kneeling, brushing leaf-rot away from a shape in the ground that her fingers recognized before her eyes did. Bone-pale stone, worn smooth, carved with the interlocking rings of the old covenant — the same sigil that circled the keystone shard in her pocket, warm as a sleeping bird. "There are hundreds of these. There used to be thousands. Odile said they marked the roads the dead were allowed to walk."

"And now?"

"Now they're buried." She worked the moss free with her thumbnail. "Like everything else about them."

The stone came clean all at once, and the forest changed.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the thing about the dead that living people never understood — they didn't announce themselves. The light simply went a shade more attentive. The wind stopped being weather and started being listening. And beneath everything, faint as a pulse through a wrist, Marguerite felt the press of presences gathering, drawn up out of the soil like worms after rain.

"Oh," she said quietly. "Oh, they're *here*. All of them."

"How many?" Tomas had moved to stand at her shoulder, hand on his knife out of habit rather than need. A knife, she'd told him twice, was about as useful against ghosts as a strongly worded letter.

"I don't know. More than I've ever felt in one place." She settled cross-legged before the shrine, laid the keystone shard on the stone, and did what Odile had taught her to do when the dead came crowding: she opened the door in herself and left it swinging.

The whispering began.

* * *

They came slowly, the way shy animals come to a feeder. First a shepherd who'd died four hundred years ago and still worried about his flock. Then a woman in the dress of a kingdom that no longer existed, then three children holding hands in a chain, then dozens, then more than dozens — faces surfacing in the air above the shrine like reflections in dark water, some sharp and some smudged, some ancient enough that their features had worn down to suggestions.

Marguerite had spoken with the dead her whole life. But always one at a time, or in small grieving clusters — a widow, a father, a drowned boy. Never like this. Never a *congregation*.

"You came," said a voice near her ear, dry as paper. An old man stood beside the shrine, or the shape of one, his robes the style of a priesthood extinct for centuries. "We wondered if any of the carvers remained."

"I'm the last I know of," Marguerite said. Her mouth had gone dry. Beside her, Tomas stood very still, watching her speak to empty air, and she loved him a little for not flinching.

"Then you are the one who carries the stone."

She touched the shard. "I carry part of it. Isolde says—"

"We know what Isolde says." No malice in it, only a vast tired patience. "Isolde says many things. She was a queen; saying things was her profession."

From somewhere over Marguerite's left shoulder came a sound like a ghost sniffing indignantly, which, given that ghosts had no noses, was impressive.

"But you have questions," the old priest said. "You've been carrying questions since the valley. Ask them here, where the answers can't be softened."

Marguerite looked at Tomas. He gave her the smallest nod. She turned back to the congregation of the dead, took a breath, and asked the question she had been carrying since the ford, since the pass, since Odile pressed the shard into her palm and died with her student's name locked behind her teeth.

"What happens at the Spire? Truly. Not what Isolde hopes happens. What *is*."

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight. Somewhere in it, the three ghost children stopped holding hands.

"The keystone is broken," the priest said. "You know this. Aldric broke it twenty years ago, deliberately, because a covenant binds both sides — and he wanted the dead unbound. Unrest is fuel. Grief is fuel. Every soul that cannot rest becomes a coal in his furnace." He gestured, and the air above the shrine filled with images: war-machines striding on legs of black iron, their joints glowing with a light that hurt to look at, a light made of screaming. "He feeds them with the restless dead. And they feed *him*."

"I know this part," Marguerite whispered.

"Then you know the covenant must be remade whole, at the place where it was first made. The Spire. The world's oldest bone." The priest's image-self flickered. "And you know that nothing is forged without payment."

"Isolde said the price would be steep."

"Isolde," said the priest, with the weariness of a man who has been dead long enough to be tired even of queens, "did not pay the original price herself. She signed the terms. Others paid. So let us be plain where she was diplomatic." He folded his hands. "The keystone is a bond between the living and the dead. To reforge it, the bond must be fed. And the only currency the bond accepts — the only thing a living soul possesses that the dead truly lack — is memory."

The word dropped into Marguerite's chest like a stone into a well.

"Memory," she repeated.

"Given willingly. Every recollection surrendered feeds the bond and seals it. The covenant of old was paid by many hands, each giving a little. But the carvers were always the ones who gave most, because the carvers could hear what the bond needed." His eyes, if eyes they were, fixed on hers. "To seal it alone, at the Spire, you would give... nearly everything."

* * *

Afterward she couldn't say how long she sat there. The light changed. Tomas built a fire without being asked, and she heard him moving around her like a man moving around a sickbed, careful and quiet, and she was grateful and hated being grateful.

"Nearly everything," she said finally, to the fire.

"That's what he said." Tomas crouched across from her. In the firelight his face was doing the thing it did when he was choosing words with tweezers. "But 'nearly' isn't 'all.' You could choose. Couldn't you? Choose what to keep?"

"They said every recollection surrendered feeds the bond." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "Which means I get to decide what I lose. Which is somehow worse. If it just took everything, at least it wouldn't be my fault."

"It wouldn't be your fault either way."

"Wouldn't it?" She laughed, and it came out wrong. "Tomas. Think about what I am. My whole self is memories. Take my mother's face, take Odile's voice, take the smell of the workshop, take—" she stopped, because the next item on the list was sitting right there poking the fire, and she couldn't say it while looking at him. "Take enough of those and what's left? Some girl with clever hands and no idea why they're clever. A stranger wearing my life."

Isolde rose out of the shard without being summoned — a pale figure by the fire that only Marguerite could see, though Tomas had learned to read the direction of her gaze. "I will tell you what I told no one while I lived," the queen said. "I was afraid of exactly this. That is why I signed the covenant's terms onto others. Fear makes cowards of the powerful and martyrs of everyone else. Don't be me, bone-carver."

"You're being helpful," Marguerite said. "It's unsettling."

"I'm dying again, in slow motion, and I've decided to be useful about it. It's very fashionable among ghosts."

Despite everything, Marguerite snorted.

* * *

She went back to the shrine alone when the fire had burned to coals. This was carver's business, and some doors Tomas could not follow her through, however much he wanted to.

The congregation had thinned but not dispersed. They stood in the dark between the trees like frost waiting for morning, and the old priest waited by the stone.

"Ask," he said.

"If I pay," Marguerite said, "if I sit at the Spire and pour myself out into that stone until the covenant holds — does it work? Does it actually hold? Or do I spend everything I am and buy five years before the next Aldric comes along?"

This time the answer came from everywhere, from all of them at once, a chord instead of a single voice:

*It holds.*

"The covenant held for three thousand years," the priest said. "It did not fail. It was broken — by a living hand, from the living side. That is the difference, child. A kept covenant doesn't end. It can only be betrayed. And betrayal requires the living to stop paying attention." His form steadied, sharpened, became almost solid in the moonlight. "You carve charms so the grieving can say goodbye. Have your charms ever failed?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because I listen," she said. "Really listen. Before I cut the bone, I ask what the dead person needs, not what the living want. Most carvers skip that part. That's why most charms crack."

"And that," said the priest, and something in his voice warmed, "is why it will be you. Not because you are strong. Because you listen." He paused. "Now ask the other question. The one underneath."

Her throat tightened. "What happens to the people who love me? If I don't remember them?"

The dead were quiet for a long moment. Then a woman stepped forward from the treeline — the one in the old kingdom's dress — and her voice was gentle. "My daughter forgot me within a year of my death. Living forget the dead anyway, carver. It is the natural direction of things. We fade from your minds whether or not you fade from ours." She smiled, and it was a real smile, sad and whole. "But my daughter kept one thing. Not my face. Not my voice. Just — how it felt to be loved by me. A feeling with no name attached. She carried it her whole life and never knew whose it was, and it was enough. It was more than enough."

"A feeling," Marguerite whispered.

"Memories are the wrapping," the priest said. "But some things inside you aren't memories. They're deeper than remembering. Trust. Kindness. The instinct of your hands. Those are cut into you like grain in wood — you can sand the surface and the grain remains." He inclined his head. "Choose carefully what you pay. Pay the wrapping. Try to keep the grain."

"And if I can't tell the difference?"

"Then you are honest," he said, "and honesty is the first requirement of the covenant. It was never strength we asked for. Only truth, freely given."

The congregation began to thin then, dissolving back into the soil the way breath dissolves into cold air, and the last thing Marguerite heard, from everywhere and nowhere, was the chord again, softer now:

*Come to the Spire, listener. Come, and pay, and be paid.*

* * *

Tomas was awake when she came back to the fire. Of course he was. He was always awake when it mattered, in that stubborn, unglamorous way of his — keeping watch over a woman who talked to ghosts, guarding her from dangers a knife couldn't touch, and never once making her feel foolish for needing it.

"Well?" he said.

She sat down beside him. Close. Closer than strictly necessary, and she noticed herself doing it and didn't stop.

"They told me the price. Memory, given willingly. Nearly all of it." She watched the coals breathe. "And they told me something else. That some things survive the losing. The grain under the wood." She turned to look at him, and found him already looking at her, and the firelight did something kind to his tired face. "So I've been sitting out there making a list. Everything I might keep. Everything I might give."

"And?"

"And I realized the list has a problem." Her voice wobbled and she let it. "Because the thing I most want to keep isn't a memory at all. It's this. Right now. Being looked at like that by someone who stayed. And that's not something I can put in my pocket, Tomas. That's just... how you've made me. It's grain."

For a moment he said nothing. Then, very quietly: "Even if I'm not in the list at all? Even if you wake up at the bottom of that mountain and I'm a stranger with a scar and bad camping habits?"

"Especially then." She almost smiled. "You'll just have to earn it again. You seem like the persistent type."

He huffed something that was almost a laugh. Above the trees, far to the north, the sky had begun to pale toward a dawn that felt less like an ending than an appointment.

"We should sleep," he said. "The pass starts tomorrow."

"Yes," Marguerite said, and did not sleep, and did not mind. She sat watching the coals and feeling, for the first time since Odile died, not afraid of what she would lose — but fiercely, tremblingly determined about what she would carry north: not a list of faces, but a handful of grain.

Behind them, deep in the earth, the dead settled back to wait — and far to the south, unseen, something with Veyra's seal on its orders rode through the night, following the warmth of the stone.
