## 16. Veyra at the Ford

The river had no name on any map they could find, which Isolde declared typical of everything north of the capital.

"Cartographers are cowards," she said, drifting above the water so that her translucent hem trailed in the current without disturbing it. "They name the rivers that flow through cities and leave the rest blank, as though a river only exists when someone important is standing beside it."

"This one exists," Marguerite said grimly. "I can hear it. It's very loud about existing."

She meant the dead beneath it. The ford was old — older than the road, older than the free cities, older perhaps than the kingdom itself — and the riverbed held its dead the way a coat holds dust. Drowned travelers. Soldiers from wars whose names had worn smooth. A woman who kept repeating *the ice was thinner than I thought* with the patience of someone who had been saying it for two hundred years.

Tomas crouched at the water's edge, testing the current with a stick. "Knee-deep at the crossing, waist-deep either side of it. We can be across in ten minutes if we go now."

"And then what?" Isolde asked. "Walk into the mountains with Veyra's cavalry an hour behind us? No. If we're going to be cornered anywhere, better here. A river at your back is a wall. A river behind your enemies is a grave." She said it without relish, which somehow made it worse.

Marguerite looked back down the road they'd traveled. The waystation's smoke was still visible as a grey smudge against the morning, three miles off. Bren and Sella had died there. The ferryman's family who'd hidden them for a night. The old soldier who'd given up his boots. Veyra had burned all of it for the crime of sheltering them, and Marguerite carried every name like stones sewn into her hem.

"She'll reach us by midday," Tomas said.

"Then we have until midday," Marguerite said, "to decide whether to run or stand."

Isolde turned from the water. For a moment her ghost-light flickered — the closest thing she had to a facial expression that wasn't contempt or irony. "You already know," she said.

Marguerite did know. She reached into her shirt and drew out the keystone shard. It was warm against her sternum, warmer than it should have been, and through it she could feel them: not just the drowned under the ford but the freed ghosts of the last hundred miles, the ones she'd laid to rest in unmarked graves, the conscripted boys and burned villagers. They were *watching*. She couldn't explain how she knew, only that the shard hummed faintly whenever they gathered near, like a struck bell remembering its note.

"They came because of what I did at the graves," she said quietly. "If I run, they'll have bled for nothing."

"If you stay, you might bleed for something too," Tomas said. He didn't say *for nothing*, and the care he took not to say it made her chest ache.

* * *

They spent the morning preparing, which mostly meant arguing.

Tomas wanted to fortify the near bank — dig in, make the crossing costly, hold until dark and slip away upstream. Isolde wanted exactly the opposite: let Veyra commit to the ford, then hit her flanks while her soldiers were wet and crowded. "Water is the great equalizer," she said. "I won three battles this way. Well. My generals won them. I took credit beautifully."

"You were a queen," Marguerite said.

"I was a queen," Isolde agreed, "which is why I know how kings fight. Aldric doesn't send Veyra to capture you anymore, girl. He sends her to retrieve his property. That means she will not be cautious. Cautious is for people who expect to lose something."

It was Tomas who noticed the volunteers first — or rather, it was the shard that noticed, warming suddenly against Marguerite's skin like a hand pressed flat over her heart, and she stood up so fast she knocked over their cookpot.

"What is it?" Tomas had a knife out before she finished standing. Three weeks of running had taught him that.

"They're coming," Marguerite said. "From the north. From the free cities." She frowned, listening to something neither of them could hear. "A lot of them. And they're—" She stopped, bewildered. "They're singing?"

An hour later, over the rise beyond the ford, came perhaps sixty people on foot and a dozen on plow horses, armed with hunting bows, wood axes, pitchforks, and one truly magnificent fowling piece that its owner carried like a scepter. At their head walked a broad woman in a dyer's apron gone stiff with old bloodstains, and beside her — Marguerite's breath caught — floated a ghost.

Not Isolde. A young man in a conscript's uniform, faded almost to transparency, walking at the woman's shoulder with the easy familiarity of an old friend.

The woman stopped ten paces away and looked Marguerite up and down with the frank appraisal of someone buying a horse.

"You're the bone-carver," she said.

"I'm Marguerite."

"Dagny Holt. This is my brother Oskar." She jerked her thumb at the ghost, who bowed. "He was taken in the third conscription. Two years ago. We got his effects back in a box with a form letter." Her jaw worked. "Then word came down the refugee road about a woman who walks grave-lines and asks the dead their names, and the dead *answer*, and then they rest. Oskar followed the stories south until he found you. Or found where you'd been." Her eyes went to the smoke smudge on the horizon, and hardened. "We heard about the waystation."

"We couldn't save them," Marguerite said. The guilt was old and heavy by now; she carried it the way she carried the shard.

"No," Dagny said. "But you saved the ones before them, and the ones after them will be saved because of what those people bought you. That's how it works. That's how it's always worked. Somebody pays first." She turned and raised her voice across the assembled volunteers. "This is her! The one the ghosts talk about!"

And the volunteers — farmers, fishwives, a seminary of defrocked novices, two brothers who ran a toll bridge and had apparently decided to stop collecting tolls — began to cheer.

Marguerite stood in the middle of it feeling like a fraud holding a stolen sword. Beside her, Tomas had gone very still, the way he always went still when something hurt him.

"They shouldn't be here," he said quietly, only for her. "Veyra will kill them. These aren't soldiers."

"No," Marguerite agreed. "They're people who heard testimony from the dead and believed it. Which makes them braver than most soldiers I've met." She touched his elbow. "Including some inquisitors I've met."

He huffed — half laugh, half surrender — and went to organize the archers.

* * *

Veyra arrived at midday exactly, because of course she did. Marguerite would learn later that the general was famous for punctuality, that her soldiers joked bitterly that you could set the hour of your death by her watch.

She came down the southern road with four hundred men and a battering confidence that needed no ram. Grey cloaks, black lacquer, the sunburst-and-shackle of Aldric's hunt on their banners. At the head rode a tall woman with iron-grey hair cropped short and a face like a closed ledger.

Isolde drifted up beside Marguerite on the near bank. "That's her," the queen said. "Veyra. She was a captain under my husband, you know. Before Aldric. She once held a bridge against triple numbers for a day and a night." A pause. "She was loyal to the crown, then. Whatever crown happened to be standing."

"That's supposed to comfort me?"

"It's supposed to inform you. Comfort is your own business."

Veyra reined in at the far bank and studied the crossing. Studied the volunteers. Studied the muddy earthworks Tomas had spent the morning directing — shallow trenches, sharpened stakes pulled from a fence line, the fowling piece's owner positioned on a knoll with a clear field of fire. Her expression suggested she was reading a poorly written report.

Then she raised one gauntleted hand, and her army halted in perfect silence, and she called across the water in a voice built for parade grounds:

"MARGUERITE QUINTANA. YOU ARE ACCUSED OF HERESY AND UNLICENSED NECROMANCY BY ORDER OF KING ALDRIC THE FIRST OF HIS NAME. SURRENDER YOURSELF AND THESE PEOPLE WILL BE SPARED. RESIST, AND THEY WILL BE CHARGED AS YOUR ACCOMPLICES."

Her voice carried perfectly across the water. So did the silence afterward.

Dagny Holt stepped forward to the bank, planted her pitchfork, and shouted back: "WE KNOW WHAT HAPPENS TO PEOPLE WHO SURRENDER TO THE HUNT! WE'VE SEEN THE GRAVES!"

"The graves," Veyra called back, unmoved, "are full of the stubborn."

"The graves," Dagny returned, "are full of the *innocent*, and we've finally started asking them about it."

Something flickered across Veyra's closed-ledger face. Marguerite felt the shard pulse, and understood: the dead of the burned villages, the unmarked pits, the conscript columns — their testimony had traveled ahead of them like weather. Even into the grey ranks. Even, perhaps, into the general herself.

Veyra gave the order.

* * *

Later, Marguerite would remember the battle in pieces, the way a charm holds a voice in fragments.

She remembered the arrows first — a ragged volley from the volunteers' bows that fell short, and Veyra's crossbow answer that did not, and the man with the fowling piece firing once with a crack like the sky splitting and dropping the lead rider's horse, and shouting an apology to the horse.

She remembered the ford churning grey with soldiers, shields locked, wading into water that came to their waists, and Isolde at her ear saying, low and fierce: "*Now*, girl. Ask them."

So Marguerite knelt at the water's edge, pressed both palms to the river, and spoke to the drowned.

She didn't use a charm. There was no time to carve, and besides — the shard was singing against her chest, and the veil here was thin as winter ice, and when she opened her mouth the words came out in a voice that was hers and also much older: *"You who have waited under this water longer than there have been roads — the covenant is broken, but not ended. I am going to mend it. Help me hold the crossing."*

The river rose.

Not violently — that was the terrible, beautiful thing. It rose the way a sleeper turns over, slow and immense, and the current that had been knee-deep became thigh-deep became chest-deep in the space of six heartbeats, and the grey column in the middle of the ford simply *stopped*, boots slipping on old stones, shields knocking, men gasping. Shapes moved beneath the surface — hands, faces, the patient dead of two hundred years — not seizing anyone, just *present*, just there, the way a crowd is present at a trial.

The advance faltered. Wavered. Broke back to the southern bank in a clatter of armor and curses.

On the knoll, the man with the fowling piece whooped. Dagny Holt laughed like a door swinging open.

And then Veyra did the thing Marguerite would think about for years afterward. She dismounted. She walked to the water's edge, alone, out of range of the bows, and she looked directly across the river at Marguerite — found her, singled her out, forty yards of churned water between them — and she said, in a voice pitched only loud enough to carry:

"The dead in that river are traitors to the crown. As are you. As are *they*—" a gesture at the volunteers, "—and I will drown every one of them in their own fields if that is what my king requires. But understand me, bone-carver: I do not enjoy it. I remember when the dead rested. I remember what it was like before the king broke whatever he broke." Her jaw tightened. "Give me the stone you carry, and I will let every soul on this bank walk away. You have my oath. It is worth more than you think."

Marguerite's heart slammed. Because the offer was real — she could see that, could feel it through the shard the way you feel truth through a floorboard — and because it was monstrous, and because both things lived in the same woman without contradiction.

"She means it," Isolde murmured. "Which is the saddest thing about her."

Marguerite stood. Her legs shook. She made them hold anyway.

"I believe you," she called back. "And I'm sorry for what he's done to you — to all of you. But the stone isn't mine to give, and the covenant isn't mine to sell. It belongs to everyone living and everyone dead, and I'm taking it home."

Veyra held her gaze a long moment. Then she inclined her head — a small, precise motion, almost respectful — turned, and walked back to her lines.

"Girl," Isolde said softly, "she's not withdrawing."

"I know."

* * *

What came next was not a battle so much as a grinding. Veyra fed companies into the ford in rotation, disciplined waves that the risen river slowed but could not stop entirely, and the volunteers' arrows thinned them, and the trenches caught them, and slowly, horribly, the mathematics began to work in the general's favor. Sixty farmers cannot hold a river against four hundred professionals forever. By late afternoon the eastern flank had bent, then cracked, and grey-cloaked soldiers were coming ashore on the north bank in numbers.

Marguerite was in the thick of it without ever quite deciding to be — dragging wounded behind the trench line, pressing her palm to a dying novice's forehead so the boy wouldn't cross over alone, the shard blazing hot against her skin as the dead of the ford fought beside her in ways no one could see but everyone, somehow, could feel.

She saw the rider too late.

He'd come around the eastern flank with the breakthrough — a sergeant on a big bay, cutting through the scattered defenders toward the bank where she knelt, toward the shard, toward her. She saw him raise his saber. She had time to think, absurdly, of Odile's hands pressing the stone into hers, *run north, child* —

— and then Tomas was there.

He didn't shout. He didn't hesitate. He simply stepped between her and the blade the way a man steps between a candle and the wind, and the saber came down across his shoulder instead of her neck, and he folded onto his knees in the mud with a sound that Marguerite would hear in her sleep for the rest of her life.

Everything after that happened quickly and distantly. Dagny's brother Oskar — a ghost, a dead man, a conscript two years gone — threw himself bodily into the sergeant's horse, and the animal screamed and bolted, and the fowling piece spoke from the knoll, and the sergeant did not get up. Marguerite was on her knees in the shallows with Tomas's head in her lap, blood spreading through his grey cloak, her hands pressed to the wound and shaking too hard to be useful.

"Stay," she said. "Stay, stay, look at me —"

"Caught... the good side of the shoulder," Tomas managed. His face was white as bone. "Mostly."

"You absolute fool, you *idiot*, why would you —"

"Because," he said, and his eyes found hers, and there was so much in them that he had never once said aloud, three weeks of chess games and shared watches and careful silences all crowding behind them at once, "someone has to pay first. Isn't that how it works?"

And then he passed out, which was probably for the best, because Marguerite was crying too hard to pretend otherwise.

* * *

Twilight saved them. Not dramatically — Veyra did not flee in terror, and the dead did not rise in glory. Twilight simply made a night assault suicidal and a dawn assault inevitable, and Veyra, professional to her marrow, pulled her surviving companies back to the high ground to wait for morning. She had lost perhaps eighty men to the river, the arrows, and the trenches. The volunteers had lost eleven dead and twice that many wounded, and Dagny Holt organized the burying herself, digging the first spadeful of every grave, saying each name aloud, refusing help.

Marguerite sat by the fire with Tomas's head bandaged and his fever climbing, and watched the opposite bank, where Veyra's campfires bloomed in the dark like a second, colder constellation.

"She'll come at first light with everything she has left," Isolde said. She had grown dimmer as the day wore on — spending herself, Marguerite realized, holding the river's dead together through the fighting. "And we cannot win a second day. The math is against us. The math was always against us."

"Then we go tonight," Marguerite said. "Upstream. Into the pass." She looked down at Tomas's sleeping face, at the bandage going rust-dark at the edge. "Can he travel?"

"He can be *made* to travel," Dagny said, sitting down heavily across the fire. She was still wearing her brother's ghost at her shoulder like a scarf. "Whether he should is another question. But listen to me, carver." She leaned forward, firelight carving deep lines in her face. "Eleven of mine are in the ground because we chose to stand here. I don't regret it. But I'll tell you what Oskar told me at noon, when the fighting was worst and I was sure we'd all die." She glanced up at the ghost, who nodded. "He said: the dead don't need her to win. They need her to *arrive*. Every mile she gets closer to the Spire is a mile the covenant gets closer to being whole. Winning battles isn't the work. Arriving is the work."

Marguerite looked into the fire for a long time.

"Then we arrive," she said.

They broke camp in the dark, the wounded lashed to plow horses, the able carrying the unable, sixty-odd survivors melting northward into the foothills while Veyra's sentries watched the ford for a dawn attack that never came. Marguerite walked at the rear of the column, one hand steadying Tomas's litter, the other closed around the shard.

And it was burning.

Burning hotter than it had ever burned, pulsing like a second heartbeat, and through it she heard — faint, from far ahead, from somewhere up in the black shoulders of the mountains — a voice she knew.

*"Come, child,"* said Odile, from beyond the grave, from the thin place in the pass. *"There is something you must see before you climb."*

Marguerite lifted her face to the mountains and walked faster.
