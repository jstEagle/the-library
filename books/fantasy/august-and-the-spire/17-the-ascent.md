## 17. The Ascent

The pass had a name once. The maps called it the Shepherd's Stair, though no shepherd had climbed it in living memory, and by the third day I understood why: it was less a road than a wound in the mountains, stitched shut with scree and old snow and the occasional prayer-flag gone grey as cobweb.

I climbed it with a fever.

Tomas counted my steps out loud for a while — I think he believed I didn't notice — and when he reached four hundred he stopped counting and started lying instead. "Nearly there," he said, at a ledge that was very clearly not nearly anywhere. "You're doing well," he said, while I leaned against the rock and shook like a wet dog. Isolde, drifting beside us in her tattered shimmer of light, said nothing at all, which from Isolde was its own kind of mercy.

"You look terrible," she told me on the second evening, as I sat wrapped in both our cloaks watching Tomas coax a fire out of damp juniper. "Like something the river spat back out."

"Thank you," I said. "You look lovely."

"I was crowned in front of ten thousand people," she said. "I know exactly what lovely looks like, and this isn't it." But she settled closer to my shoulder, cold as a draft under a door, and stayed there through the night.

The wound in my side was Veyra's parting gift from the ford — a shallow slash that should have been nothing, except that everything since the valley had been nothing stacked on nothing until the pile came up to my chin. Tomas had cleaned it with the last of the spirits meant for his own leg, which still bore the bruise-black print of a warhorse's hoof. We were, the two of us, a matched set of damage limping toward the edge of the world.

"Tell me about the Spire again," I said that night, because his voice kept me anchored the way a rope anchors a climber, and because asking him to talk was easier than admitting I was afraid of sleeping.

"It's older than the maps," he said, feeding the fire another stick. "Older than Aldric's line, older than the free cities. The scholars in Carreth used to say it wasn't built so much as *agreed upon* — that the world grew it the way an oyster grows pearl around a hurt." He glanced at me. "That's what you are, isn't it? The hurt. And the keystone's the pearl."

"That's the least romantic thing anyone has ever said to me."

"I'm an ex-inquisitor. Romance died in my family three generations back." He almost smiled. Almost. His smiles had been coming more easily since the ford, since strangers had bled beside us for a cause they'd only heard about through the mouths of ghosts. But they still arrived shy, like cats approaching a stranger's hand.

Isolde made a sound above us that might have been a laugh. "He's not wrong, girl. The Spire doesn't want romance. It wants payment." She said it lightly, but I felt the weight underneath, the same weight I'd been carrying since the shrine, since the dead had told me what the reforging would cost. *Memory given willingly.* Every recollection surrendered to feed the bond between the living and the dead.

I had been trying not to make a list. That night, feverish, I made one anyway, scratched into my mind the way Odile had taught me to scratch charms into bone: quickly, before the material could refuse you.

My mother's hands, flour to the wrist. My father humming off-key at the lathe. The smell of Hollowmere in autumn. Odile's kitchen. The first charm I ever carved, and how proud I'd been, and how Odile had looked at it and said, *good bones, bad intentions — try again.* The widow Marta's face when she heard her husband's voice one last time. The sound of Tomas laughing — actually laughing — at the Bone Market, when Isolde had possessed a festival puppet purely to heckle a fruit-seller.

I stopped there. My eyes were burning, and I told myself it was woodsmoke.

* * *

On the fourth day we entered the place where the veil wore thin.

I felt it before I saw it — a hush, like the moment after snowfall, like the held breath inside a church. The air itself seemed to have opinions. Tomas felt it too; I watched his hand drift to a sword he'd stopped carrying weeks ago.

"What is this?" he asked quietly.

"A door left ajar," Isolde said. Her form had brightened as we climbed, her edges sharper, her voice losing the underwater quality it carried in the lowlands. "Places like this exist all over the high passes. The dead come here to be near the living without frightening them. Or the living come here to be near the dead without admitting it." She looked at me. "Grief needs somewhere to stand, Marguerite. This is standing room."

The path narrowed into a corridor of stone pillars, wind-carved into shapes that might once have been figures. And between them —

Between them, the dead were waiting.

Not the hungry restless dead I'd freed along the road, not the whispering crowds who pressed against every charm I carried. These were quiet. They stood among the pillars like people waiting out rain, and they turned to look at me as I passed, and every single one of them was mine.

There was the miller's boy who'd drowned the summer I was nine, whose charm I'd carved badly and wept over. There was old Fenwick the cooper, who'd taught me to whittle and whose last words to me had been an argument about sandpaper. There was a woman I didn't recognize until she smiled, and then I did: my grandmother, dead before I could walk, wearing the face my mother had described to me a hundred times over soup.

They didn't speak. They simply *were*, present and patient, and walking among them felt like walking through a house where every room held someone who loved me.

Tomas couldn't see them. I knew it by the way he watched me — wary, tender, the way you watch someone sleepwalking near a cliff edge. "Marguerite," he said carefully. "Talk to me. What do you see?"

"My whole life," I said, "apparently. Standing in a row."

His hand found my elbow, steadying me on the loose rock. He didn't ask me to hurry. That was the thing about Tomas I could never explain to anyone who hadn't seen him in his grey uniform: he had a genius for not hurrying people. It was, I suspect, the exact skill that had made him a good interrogator, turned inside out into something kind.

And then, at the far end of the pillar-corridor, where the path bent upward into cloud, she was there.

Odile.

She looked as she had on the morning the riders came — grey-streaked hair pinned carelessly, ink on her fingers, that expression she wore when a student gave her an answer that was technically correct and spiritually useless. Not a ghost the way Isolde was a ghost, all fury and unfinished business. Just a woman, standing in thin air, waiting for me the way she'd waited a thousand evenings in her kitchen.

I stopped walking. I don't remember deciding to stop.

"You're dead," I said, which was the stupidest sentence of my life, and I've said some contenders.

"Mm," said Odile. "You always did lead with your strongest observation."

The tears came without permission, hot and sudden, and I wiped at them furiously because I was twenty-six years old and a fugitive and my side hurt and I did not have time — but Odile crossed the space between us and took my face in her ink-stained hands, and her touch was cool as well-water and solid as bread, and I stopped wiping.

"I couldn't save you," I said. "I ran and I still got caught and they killed you anyway, and everything since — the child in the cells, the waystation, all of it — it's because of me, because they wanted—"

"Stop." Not unkind. Firm, the way she'd say it over a mis-cut charm. "Marguerite Quintana. Look at me."

I looked.

"You were nineteen years old and they brought fire and forty men. What exactly was your plan? Reason with them? Out-carve them?" One corner of her mouth lifted. "You did the only thing I trained you to do. You survived, and you carried the work north. If you'd stayed, you'd be ash in my yard and the keystone would be on Aldric's desk, and every ghost from here to the sea would still be screaming. So no, child. There is nothing to forgive. But if it will let you put the weight down—" she pressed her forehead to mine, the old gesture, the one that meant *enough now, go eat something* — "then hear it. I forgive you. I was never anything but proud of you. Now climb."

"I'm going to forget you," I whispered against her brow. "At the Spire. That's the price. All of it. Maybe even you."

Odile drew back and studied me with those eyes that had catalogued every lie I'd ever told her, and found, as always, the true thing underneath.

"Then forget me *well*," she said. "Forget me the way a river forgets the rain — not as loss. As what made the channel." She stepped back, and the other dead were gathering behind her, my whole ragged congregation, the drowned boy and the cooper and my nameless grandmother, and none of them reached for me or clung or begged. They only bowed their heads, one by one, like a field bowing under wind.

"Grace first," Odile said. "Payment after. That's the order of things. It always has been."

The cloud took them. Or they took the cloud. With Odile it was never entirely clear who dismissed whom.

* * *

I don't remember much of the rest of that day's climbing. Tomas later swore I walked it myself, refused his shoulder twice, and ate half his rations without noticing. I remember warmth, oddly, in a place of ice. I remember feeling lighter than I had since the valley — not healed, not happy, but *carried*, the way a melody is carried after the instrument stops.

We camped that night in the lee of a cairn older than any kingdom, and for the first time since the ford, my fever broke clean.

"You spoke to someone up there," Tomas said, when the fire was down to embers. He said it without pushing. He'd learned that from me, maybe, or I'd learned the opposite from him; by then our habits had started trading places like coats.

"Odile," I said. "She forgave me."

"For what?"

"Existing, mostly." I pulled the cloak tighter. "For surviving when she didn't. For being the reason they came." I looked at him across the small fire, at the man who had burned his commission and his old life in a market square and followed a bone-carver and a dead queen to the edge of the map. "She said there was nothing to forgive. Do you believe that? That some things just... aren't debts?"

Tomas was quiet long enough that I thought he wouldn't answer. Then he said, "I falsified reports for two years. Every name I softened was a debt I owed someone I couldn't save. I used to think the ledger would drown me." He turned a stone at the fire's edge, over and over, a habit. "But at the ford, when those volunteers came over the hill — farmers, a ferryman, a girl with a pitchfork — nobody asked me what I'd done. They asked what needed doing next." He looked up. "So yes. I believe some things aren't debts. I have to. Otherwise the ascent is just punishment with better views."

Above us, Isolde flickered against the stars, and for once she didn't mock either of us. "Get some sleep, both of you," she said softly. "Tomorrow the air gets honest, and the Spire gets close, and you'll want your strength." A pause. "And Marguerite. Whatever you paid in that pass — spend it wisely from here. Grace is a loan, not a gift. The interest comes due at the top."

I slept, and dreamed of a kitchen that smelled of ink and rosemary, and woke with tear-tracks already dry on my face and no memory of why.

* * *

The last morning, the mountain ended its pretense of being a mountain.

The path opened onto a saddle of bare black rock, and beyond it, filling the sky like a second horizon, stood the Spire.

No picture had prepared me. No story had. It rose out of the cloud sea pale as bone — of course pale as bone, I thought, and almost laughed, because of course the world's oldest covenant would be written in the world's oldest material — and it was carved, all of it, every visible span, with names. Millions of names. Generations of names, spiraling upward past sight, the ledger of everyone ever bound or released, living and dead in one continuous script.

Tomas stood beside me and forgot, briefly, to breathe. I heard it catch.

"That's..." he began.

"Yes," I said.

"It's..."

"Yes."

Isolde drifted forward to the saddle's edge, and her light went strange — brighter and thinner at once, like a candle in its last inch. "There it is," she said. "I signed its founding terms, you know. In a room that smelled of lilies. I was very young and very certain that the price would land on someone else." Her voice frayed. "It's time, bone-carver. It knows you're here. Can't you feel it *looking*?"

I could. The whole vast column of it, attentive as a held breath. And beneath my feet, faint but unmistakable, rising through the black rock like heat through a floor:

Riders. Many riders. Far below, moving fast, wearing no colors at all.

Veyra had found the pass.

Tomas's hand closed around mine — calloused, warm, steady — and together we started up the last stretch of stone toward the Spire, while behind us the mountain began, very quietly, to fill with the sound of hooves.
