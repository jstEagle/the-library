## 18. The Spire

The last stretch of the climb was stairs — a staircase older than any kingdom, cut into the mountain's spine and worn smooth by ten thousand years of pilgrims who had come to the Spire for reasons no one alive remembered. Marguerite climbed it on legs that had stopped belonging to her somewhere around the two-thousandth step.

"Tell me again why ghosts don't have to climb," she said through her teeth.

Isolde drifted beside her, translucent as breath on glass, entirely unbothered. "Because we are unburdened by flesh. It's rather the whole point of being dead."

"You could carry my pack."

"I could *haunt* your pack. I could make it whisper dreadful things about you at night. Carrying is out of the question."

Tomas, one step below her, laughed — a short, cracked sound, like something breaking open in a good way. His shoulder was still bound from the ford, his left arm nearly useless, and he had climbed every step anyway without once asking to stop. "She's been like this since the veil thinned," he said. "The higher we go, the more of her there is."

That was true, and Marguerite tried not to look at it directly. Isolde had begun the journey as a voice in a shard of pale stone, thin as winter light. Now she cast a shadow on the stairs. Her outline flickered at the edges like a candle deciding whether it wanted to be a bonfire. The closer they came to the Spire, the more queen she became — and queens, Marguerite was learning, came with opinions.

"The summit opens onto the Ring," Isolde said, gesturing upward with a hand that had worn rings worth more than Hollowmere. "Aldric will be there. He will not be alone. And he will offer you something, bone-carver, because that is what he does. He offers. It is how he takes."

"What will he offer?"

Isolde was quiet long enough that the wind filled the silence. "Whatever frightens you most losing."

* * *

The stairs ended, and there was the sky.

They stepped out of the mountain's throat into evening — an August evening, absurdly gentle after everything, gold light pooling across a ring of white stone wide as a village square. And above them, impossibly, the Spire itself went on rising: a needle of pale stone climbing past the clouds into blue so deep it was almost black, its tip lost somewhere among stars that were already coming out despite the hour. The keystone shard in Marguerite's pocket grew warm as a sleeping bird. She could feel the rest of it up there. She had felt it for three days now, a pulling behind her ribs, patient and enormous.

"It's beautiful," she whispered.

"It's a door," said Isolde. "Everything beautiful here is a door. Mind that."

And then, because the world apparently refused to let them catch their breath, Veyra came out of the eastern stair.

She walked unhurried, sword sheathed, six soldiers fanning out behind her along the ring's curve. Her face was the same as it had been at the waystation, at the ford — calm as still water, and just as easy to drown in. "Marguerite Quintana," she called across the stone. "Tomas Ferren. You've made this very tiring, and I'd like to say so before the King arrives, because afterward there won't be time for pleasantries."

"Veyra." Tomas stepped forward, putting himself between her and Marguerite with a motion so automatic it was almost funny. Almost. "You don't have to do this."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "I get to. There's a difference, and it matters." She drew her sword anyway, and her soldiers followed suit, steel ringing softly against the ancient stone. "The King wants the carver alive. He didn't say anything about the defector."

What happened next happened quickly, and Marguerite spent years afterward trying to arrange it into order in her mind, and never quite managed.

Veyra moved first — fast, professional, no flourish. Tomas met her with his bad arm and his good one both on the hilt, and the sound of their swords was like a bell cracking. One of the six broke toward Marguerite and got three steps before Isolde simply *arrived* in front of him, blazing suddenly bright as a forge-door, and the man sat down hard on the stone and did not get up again so much as scoot backward on all fours until he fell off the ring's inner edge into cloud. Two others circled toward Tomas's flank, and Marguerite did the only thing she knew how to do: she pulled the charms from her coat — every scrap of bone she'd stolen back at the Bone Market, months of work, a widow's husband, a farmer's mother, a drowned boy whose name she'd promised to carry north — and threw them down on the stone between the soldiers and herself.

The dead stood up.

Not fully, not in bodies, but as *presence* — a pressure like standing too near a waterfall, cold air and voices layered over voices. The soldiers stopped as if they'd hit glass. One of them began to pray. Another dropped his sword and sat down next to the first man, and the two of them held each other and shook.

"They're just bones," Marguerite told them, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Just old griefs. Stand still and they'll pass over you."

Across the ring, Tomas and Veyra traded blows along the inner edge, and Marguerite saw with a lurch that he was losing — slowly, deliberately, giving ground he didn't need to give, keeping her turned away from the Spire's base, buying seconds with inches of his own blood. There was red on his bandage already. Fresh.

"Tomas—"

"*Go up,*" he shouted. "Marguerite, *go up*, I've got her—"

"He does not have me," said Veyra, and struck him across the thigh, and he went down on one knee, and Marguerite's heart went with him.

But then the light changed.

It didn't dim; it *bent*. Every shadow on the ring swung east at once, like iron filings finding their magnet, and the temperature dropped so fast that the soldiers' breath bloomed white. Out of nothing — out of the space between the stars and the stone — Queen Isolde Aldraine stepped into the world wearing her own body again.

Marguerite had seen her angry. She had seen her grieving, wry, tired, fierce in small ghostly ways. She had never seen her *royal*. This was different. This was a woman in armor of grey light with a crown that wasn't there and was, all the same, and when she looked at Veyra, Veyra's soldiers found urgent reasons to be elsewhere along the wall.

"Sister-in-law," Isolde said pleasantly. "Do mind the edge."

Veyra looked at her — truly looked, the first crack in that still water — and for one moment her sword arm wavered. Then she set her jaw. "You're dead," she said. "I put flowers on your tomb every year."

"I know," Isolde said, softer. "It was the only kindness anyone paid me in that palace, and I never learned your name. Tell it to me someday, if either of us survives this. But right now—" her gaze flicked to Tomas, bleeding and grinning up at her from the stones, "—right now I have an appointment with my husband."

* * *

He was already there.

No stair announced him. No door. Aldric simply stood at the Spire's base as if he had always been standing there, as if the evening had arranged itself around him. He wore black and silver and no crown, which was somehow worse than a crown, and he was — Marguerite realized with a small cold shock — handsome. Tall, silver-templed, with his daughter's dark eyes, except that when he looked at you the eyes did the arithmetic of a man counting coins he was running out of.

"Marguerite Quintana," he said. Her name in his mouth sounded like a legal document. "The bone-carver of Hollowmere. Do you know how long I have looked for someone who could hear them clearly? Years. Whole years. And here you climbed to me on your own feet. I find I admire that."

Behind her, faintly, she heard Tomas force himself upright, sword up, planting himself between Veyra and the Spire. She heard Isolde drift forward, light gathering around her like a storm deciding. She made herself look at none of it. Aldric was talking to *her*, and Isolde had warned her: he offers, and offering is how he takes.

"You want the keystone," Marguerite said.

"I want what it can do, which is not the same thing." He walked toward her, unhurried, hands loose at his sides, a man strolling in his own garden. "You know what I am now. You've seen the roads my armies travel on. You've felt what the restless dead feed. So you know the cost of stopping it — the Spire demands payment, girl, and the payment is memory, given willingly, all of it, every recollection poured into the bond. Your mentor knew. That's why she hid you instead of training you for this." He smiled, gently, terribly. "Odile loved you enough to let the world burn rather than ask that of you. I'm offering better than Odile did."

"Are you."

"I'm offering you *keep* them." He stopped an arm's length away, and up close she could see the wrongness under the handsome — a fine tremor, like a building settling on a broken foundation; a smell of old incense and something scorched beneath it. "Walk away. Give me the shard. Go home to your valley and carve your little charms and keep every memory you own — your mother's face, your valley, your friend bleeding on the stones behind you. All of it stays yours. In exchange, you serve. Quietly. Charms for my officers. Comfort for the families of the fallen. You'd be good at it. You'd be *kind* at it. And in fifty years, when you die warm in your bed, the dead will still be screaming — but not yours. Never yours. You'll have kept everything."

Marguerite's hand had gone to her pocket without her permission, to the shard, warm as a heartbeat.

And that was the trap of it, she thought. Not the fear. The *fairness* of it. He wasn't asking her to betray anyone. He was asking her to keep herself — and what kind of monster would refuse that?

"That's a lovely bargain," she said. "Did you make it to yourself too?"

Something flickered across his face.

"Because I heard what it cost *you*." She took her hand out of her pocket, empty, and was surprised to find it steady. "You don't remember her face. Your daughter. You stand there offering to help everyone keep their memories, and you can't remember your daughter's face, and some part of you knows that's the price tag still hanging off your own neck." Behind her, she heard Veyra's blade ring against Tomas's again — closer now, desperate. She kept her eyes on Aldric. "You're not offering me a deal. You're offering me company in the dark."

"Girl—"

"My name," said Marguerite Quintana, bone-carver of Hollowmere, who had spent her whole life being quiet in a valley built to keep magic out, "is Marguerite. And I have buried enough people who kept themselves safe. I'm done."

She turned from him — actually turned her back on the most powerful man in the world, which even she recognized as possibly the stupidest thing she had ever done, and which felt, frankly, magnificent — and ran for the Spire's base, where the keystone's socket waited in the pale stone, a hollow shaped exactly like the thing in her pocket.

Aldric moved faster than a man should move.

His hand closed on her shoulder and the cold of it went straight through her coat, through skin, into the marrow — and the whispers rose, all the dead he had ever spent, crowding up through his grip, and she heard them *screaming* and she heard, underneath the screaming, something that broke her heart: they sounded *tired*. They wanted to stop. Even his army of unrest wanted only what she wanted.

"Last chance," he said quietly, close to her ear.

"Take mine instead," said another voice.

Isolde.

She came across the ring like dawn — like the *idea* of dawn, light given a purpose — and where she touched the stone the shadows fled, and Aldric turned to face his dead wife with an expression Marguerite would spend the rest of a life she couldn't yet imagine trying to describe: fury and relief braided together so tightly they were the same thing.

"You can't touch me," he said. "I made myself untouchable. I paid for—"

"Yes," Isolde agreed. "You did pay. Year after year after year. And every year you bought was *mine*, Aldric. Every borrowed day has my name on the ledger. I signed nothing you didn't steal." She raised her hand, and the crown-that-wasn't blazed. "So I've come to collect."

She laid her fingers against his forehead.

And the years came *off* him.

Marguerite watched it happen and understood that she was watching something neither cruel nor kind — a debt being settled. The silver left his temples. The height stooped. The terrible vitality drained out of him like water from a cracked cup, decade by decade by decade, sixty borrowed years pouring away into the light of the woman he had murdered to hold them, and what was left when it finished was — a man. Old. Ordinary. Shaking, blinking, clutching at the air as though the world had suddenly gotten louder, which perhaps for him it had, because for the first time in decades he was hearing the dead without the power to spend them.

"Isolde?" he said. Small. An old man's voice. "Isolde, I can't remember her face—"

"I know," said the Queen Who Wouldn't Rest, and there was no triumph in it at all, only a grief so old it had gone smooth as river stone. "I know. Neither can I. That's what you took from both of us."

She was fading. Marguerite saw it — the blaze guttering down, the grey armor going thin, the crown dissolving into sparks. Unmaking his borrowed years had cost Isolde whatever had kept her burning this far, and she was going out like a candle in a large room, and she was smiling about it.

"Go," Isolde told her. "*Up.* The socket is waiting, and the Spire does not wait twice. And Marguerite—" her voice caught, and she laughed at herself catching, a queen embarrassed by her own heart, "—when it asks for your memories, it will take them gently. I promise you that much. I checked. It's the only thing I could check from where I stood."

Behind them, steel rang — once, twice — and then the sound of a sword hitting stone and staying there. Marguerite spun. Tomas was on his knees, Veyra standing over him with her point at his throat, and Veyra was looking not at him but at the ruined, ordinary, trembling old man who had been King Aldric, and her face was doing something complicated, like a lock turning over.

"Veyra," the old man whispered. "I don't— where am I?"

And Marguerite ran for the Spire, with the shard burning in her fist and every memory she had ever owned rising up inside her like a tide deciding which things to keep.

The socket opened for her like a mouth. She pressed the keystone home.

The light took her at the throat, gentle as Isolde had promised — and asked.
