## 19. The Reforging

The Spire's summit was a bowl of white stone, worn smooth as the inside of a shell, and the wind up there did not howl so much as *sing* — a long, low note that Marguerite felt in her teeth and in the marrow of every bone she had ever carved.

She knelt at the center of it, where a socket had been cut into the living rock, older than any kingdom, older than any word for kingdom. The keystone shard sat in her palm, warm as a held hand. Isolde stood at her shoulder, or rather hovered there, translucent in the strange light that poured down from a sky with no clouds in it at all.

"It will ask you," Isolde said. "It won't take. Remember the difference."

"I remember." Marguerite almost laughed. "For now."

Behind them, near the stair, Tomas was on his feet again despite the wound in his side, one arm braced against the stone, watching the horizon where Veyra's army crawled upward like ants toward sugar. He caught Marguerite looking and gave her the smallest nod — the same nod he'd given her across a chessboard in the capital, the one that meant *your move*.

*Your move,* she thought. And then she set the shard into the socket.

The Spire woke.

There was no other word for it. The singing note deepened, became a chord, became something like a voice made of ten thousand voices all speaking at once but somehow saying only one thing: *come.* The pale stone of the keystone drank the light and gave it back changed, and threads of it — silver, then gold, then a color Marguerite had no name for — began to rise out of the ground all around them, out of the air itself, out of the thin places where the dead pressed close.

And the dead came.

They came up the mountain road behind Veyra's soldiers, through the soldiers, indifferent to them as rain to stones. They came from the unmarked graves along the north road, from the waystation's ashes, from villages hollowed by conscription, from centuries of small injustices and great ones. They gathered at the rim of the summit-bowl in their thousands, and they were not frightening. That was the thing Marguerite would have told anyone who asked, if anyone could have asked afterward: they were not frightening. They looked *tired*. They looked like people waiting at a door they'd been locked outside of for a very long time.

Among them, near the front, she saw Odile. Her mentor's ghost wore the same shawl she'd died in, singed at the hem, and when their eyes met Odile smiled the way she used to smile when Marguerite got a carving right on the first try — no surprise in it, only satisfaction.

"Hello, little wren," Odile said.

Marguerite's throat closed. "I don't know what to do."

"You do. You've always known. You just wanted someone to tell you it was allowed." Odile folded her ghostly hands. "It's allowed."

The voice of the Spire resolved, then, out of the chord, into something she could understand — not words exactly, more like the sensation of a question being asked directly against the inside of her skull:

*WHAT WILL YOU PAY?*

Marguerite closed her eyes.

She had imagined this moment on the whole long road north, and in her imaginings it had always been abstract — *memories*, a currency, coins counted out. It was not abstract now. Now it was a ledger opening inside her, page after page, and the Spire turning those pages gently, patiently, asking of each one: *this? this? this?*

Her mother's face. She felt it go first, because it offered itself first — the face she had spent years trying to keep bright and had let blur anyway, grief doing the Spire's work early. She surrendered what remained: the particular way her mother's eyes crinkled, the smell of bread and tallow, the sound of her humming off-key while she worked. It left her like warmth leaving a cup, and where it had been there was not pain but a kind of clean space, like a room swept for a guest.

*This,* said the Spire, accepting.

"Yes," Marguerite whispered. "That. Take it."

Hollowmere went next. Not all at once — the Spire was strangely kind about order, taking the edges first. The smell of the valley in autumn. The name of the widow Marta, whose husband's charm had been the first she'd ever carved well. The path to Odile's cottage, every stone of it. The taste of the valley's water, which tasted of nothing special and was therefore irreplaceable. Each memory lifted out of her and streamed away in a thread of gold, joining the great rising weave above the keystone, and each departure left her lighter and more frightened, because she could feel herself becoming simpler, and simplicity felt dangerously like peace.

"Tomas," she said aloud, suddenly, without deciding to.

He was beside her before she finished the word — he must have crossed the summit-bowl without her hearing, wounded man moving quiet as a cat — and he knelt across from her, and took her hands in both of his. His palms were shaking. His grip was steady.

"Don't," he said. His voice cracked on it. "Marguerite. There has to be another—"

"There isn't. You know there isn't. You've known since the ford."

"I know it and I hate it." He laughed, wetly. "That's most of what I know these days."

She smiled at him. She found, to her horror, that she was already losing the memory of his face — not its fact, but its texture; she could see him and knew she was seeing him, but some part of her understood that the seeing would not survive the hour. So she did what she had done her whole life with things too precious to carve: she held it as hard as she could, and then she let it go, deliberately, the way you open your hand around a bird.

His hands around hers. The chessboard in the gilded cage, and his deliberate negligence dressed up as bad luck. The Bone Market, and the commission burning behind them like a second sunrise. *Your move.* The feeling — this was the last thread, the truest one — the feeling of being trusted by someone who had every reason not to trust her, and trusting him back, and how that had felt like the valley's old peace come back to her in human shape.

It left her.

The Spire took it with what she could have sworn was gentleness, folding it into the weave, and Marguerite heard herself make a small sound, not quite a cry, and felt Tomas's grip tighten as though he could anchor her to it by force.

"What did you give?" he asked. "What did it take?"

She looked at him. He was a man kneeling across from her. Kind eyes — she could still see that they were kind, even if the history of their kindness was draining away like color from a sunset. She searched herself for his name and found a smooth place where it should have been.

"I don't know," she said honestly. "Something. I think... I think something important."

His face did something terrible and brave. He nodded once, twice, and pressed her hands back toward the keystone. "Then finish it," he said. "Finish it, and whatever's left of you, I'll look after it. That's a promise. Even if you don't know who's making it."

Above them, the weave of gold was nearly complete — a vast slow-turning lattice in the shape of nothing so much as a lock, and the dead at the rim of the bowl had begun, all together, to sing. It was not a requiem. It was the sound of ten thousand people exhaling after holding their breath for a hundred years.

And then Aldric arrived.

---

He came up the last of the stair alone, which was somehow worse than an army. Veyra's soldiers had stopped climbing; they stood scattered along the switchbacks below, looking up at the golden weave with expressions Marguerite recognized from mirrors — the expression of people watching something they had been taught all their lives was impossible.

Aldric climbed like a man climbing to his own execution who has decided to arrive punctual. Up close, in the Spire's light, the wrongness of him was naked. The borrowed years hung off him like ill-fitting clothes; his skin had the stretched, translucent quality of parchment over flame, and where his eyes should have been there was a hunger wearing the shape of eyes.

"The bone-carver," he said. His voice was ruinous, beautiful, exhausted. "Do you know what I gave up to live long enough to be hated this thoroughly?"

"Everything," Marguerite said. "I'm about to find out what that costs."

"Then you'll fail where I succeeded." He raised a hand, and the hunger-eyes fixed on the keystone, and Marguerite felt the weave *shudder*, felt the dead nearest the rim flicker like candles in wind. "I can unmake this. I have unmade greater. Kneel, girl, and I'll spare your memories — all of them. Every face. Every name. You'll walk out of here whole, and rich, and safe, and the dead can stay restless. They're only the dead."

Isolde stepped between them.

She had been fading for miles — Marguerite had watched it happen, the queen spending herself like a miser finally broken open, guiding them through the pass, holding the veil thin enough to climb. What remained of her now was barely more than outline and intention. But intention, Marguerite had learned, was the strongest material in the world.

"Aldric," Isolde said.

He flinched. Actually flinched, a full-body recoil, as though struck. "You're dead."

"Yes. You saw to it. Poorly." She drifted closer, and her outline sharpened with every word, fed by something older than his hunger. "You broke the covenant to buy yourself years. Do you know what the covenant actually was, Aldric? It wasn't a wall. It was a *hand*. The living hold it out, and the dead take it, and everyone gets to rest. You cut off the hand and wondered why the body bled." She was very close to him now, close enough that the hunger in his eyes had nowhere to look. "You don't even remember her face. Your daughter. I've watched you reach for it for three hundred miles. It's gone. You paid it, and everything else, and the Spire never even asked you. You just kept taking until there was nothing left to be."

"Stop," Aldric said.

"You want to know the worst part?" Isolde's voice softened into something almost tender. "So do I. I signed the original terms. I knew the price would fall on the voiceless. We are not so different, you and I — we are only different in what we did when we finally saw the bill."

And she put her ghostly hands against his chest, and *unmade* him.

Not violently. That was the terrible grace of it. She simply began subtracting — year by stolen year, death by deferred death, feeding each one back into the weave above them, and the golden lattice drank it and burned brighter, and Aldric screamed, and it was not the scream of a king. It was the scream of a man discovering, all at once, the exact size of what he owed.

Marguerite turned back to the keystone.

*THE LAST PAYMENT,* said the Spire, and she understood, without knowing how she understood, that the final thread was the one she was standing inside of: her gift itself, the whispering in the marrow, the hearing of the dead. A carver who could hear no ghosts. A workshop full of silence. She hesitated for exactly one heartbeat.

Then she thought of Odile's smile, and the ten thousand tired faces at the rim of the world, and she opened herself completely.

The gift poured out of her like light from a shattered lamp, and the keystone blazed, and the weave above them closed — the last gap sealing with a sound like a door shutting softly in a house where everyone is finally asleep.

The silence that followed was enormous.

The dead did not vanish. They *settled* — the whole vast crowd of them sinking gently into the mountain, into the earth, into rest, the way snow settles, the way a child settles when carried at last to bed. Odile went last of those Marguerite could see, pausing at the edge of dissolution to blow her student a kiss, entirely unbothered, entirely herself, and then she was everywhere and nowhere, which is another way of saying at peace.

On the far side of the summit-bowl, King Aldric the Usurper collapsed onto the white stone as an ordinary, dying man. Old — truly old, all his debts collected at once. He lay there breathing in small, astonished sips, staring at the sky, and Marguerite, who could no longer hear the dead and never would again, still knew what he was seeing, because some knowledge lives deeper than memory: he was seeing his daughter's face, returned to him in the last minute it could matter.

He wept. Then he didn't. The Spire sang its single low note over him, patient as weather, and did not judge.

Marguerite swayed. Tomas caught her — she registered hands, steadiness, a voice saying her name in a tone that suggested the name mattered enormously to him, though to her it was merely a word someone was using. She looked up into a kind face belonging to a kind stranger who was crying without seeming to know it.

"Are we done?" she asked him. "Did we win?"

"We won," he said. His voice was wrecked. "Yes. We're done."

"Oh good." She smiled at him, this stranger with steady hands, and felt the strangest flutter of something — recognition with no object, homesickness with no home. "I feel like I should know you. Isn't that funny? I feel like I've known you forever, and I can't think why."

Below them, the army on the switchbacks had begun, soldier by soldier, to kneel.

And in the pocket of Tomas's coat, wrapped in cloth, a small carved bone bead sat warm as a held hand — though neither of them yet knew it was there, or what slept inside it, waiting for an August evening and a woman's willing hand.
