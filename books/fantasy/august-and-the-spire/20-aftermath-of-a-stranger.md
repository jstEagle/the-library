## 20. Aftermath of a Stranger

Tomas had imagined this moment a hundred times on the climb — the moment after. In most of those imaginings, Marguerite opened her eyes and knew him. In a few of the darker ones, she did not open her eyes at all.

He had never once imagined that she would open her eyes, look directly at him, and ask him to move because he was blocking her light.

"Sorry," he said, and stepped aside like a man caught loitering outside a shop he couldn't afford to enter.

She was sitting up in the narrow bed of the hospice at the Spire's foot, where the free-city volunteers had carried them both. Her hands went first to her wrists, as if checking for manacles that were no longer there. Then to her hair, which had come loose from its braid somewhere between the ford and the summit and now hung around her face in a way Tomas had privately thought of as *hers* for months. She looked at it as though it belonged to a stranger too.

"You're staring," she said. Not unkindly. The way one remarks on weather.

"I'm sorry," he said again, because it seemed to be the only sentence he owned.

"You keep saying that." She frowned, and the frown was familiar — the small crease between her brows that appeared whenever Odile's name came up, or when Tomas lied badly about liking chess. "Do I know you?"

The question landed the way he'd known it would land, and still knocked something loose in his chest. He had watched her feed her mother's face into the keystone. Her valley. The sound of Odile's voice. He had stood three feet away holding Veyra off with a borrowed sword and a wound in his side while she gave herself away piece by piece, and she had never once hesitated, and he had never once told her not to.

"No," he said. "Not anymore."

* * *

The healers called it a clean payment, which Tomas thought was an obscene way to describe it. The covenant had taken exactly what she'd offered and nothing more, they said — no fever, no fragmentation, no damage. She was whole. She simply wasn't *herself*, or rather, she was a self with all its furniture removed: a room swept bare, floors gleaming, waiting to be filled by someone new.

They told her she was Marguerite Quintana. They told her she had saved the world, more or less, though the volunteers said it shyly, the way people mention large things in small rooms. They told her the dead were resting now, everywhere, all at once — that the pressure that had sat on the living since Aldric's usurpation had lifted like a storm breaking.

Marguerite received all of this with a polite nod, the nod of a woman being read a menu in a language she almost spoke.

What she asked about, repeatedly, was bone.

"Do you have any?" she asked the head healer on the second morning. "I'd like to carve something."

"You've just woken from a magical ordeal—"

"It's not an ordeal if I don't remember it," she said reasonably. "And my hands feel wrong. Empty. Like they've been folded up in a drawer."

Tomas, who had been sitting in the corner pretending to read dispatches nobody was sending him anymore, went out into the corridor and laughed until his stitches hurt.

That was how he learned the shape of the thing he would have to live inside: she was still *her*. The humor was hers, dry and sideways. The stubbornness was hers. The way she thanked people precisely, as if gratitude were a craft requiring care. Everything essential had survived; only the story had been taken, and the story had contained him.

He could have left. The free cities had offered him anything he wanted — pardon, position, a commission to write the true account of the hunt so no one could ever sanitize it again. Isolde's testimony, preserved in the keystone's echo before it sealed, had cleared his name more thoroughly than any tribunal could. He was, officially, a hero of the restoration, a title he wore like a coat two sizes too large.

Instead he took a room at the hospice inn and told himself it was temporary. Just until she was steady. Just until she didn't need someone to sit in corners.

* * *

On the fourth day, Isolde came to him.

He felt it first as a coldness along the scar on his forearm — the queen had always announced herself that way, frost before voice. He was alone in the hospice garden, failing to sleep, when the air beside the bench thickened and she assembled out of it: translucent, silvered, and already fraying at the edges like cloth worn thin.

"You're fading," he said.

"I'm *finishing*," she corrected. "There's a difference, though I grant it's subtle from the living side." She settled onto the bench without quite touching it, the way she'd done for the entire journey, as if sitting were a courtesy she performed rather than a thing she needed. "How is she?"

"She asked me yesterday whether she'd ever been married. I said no. She looked relieved. Then she asked if she'd been happy, and I didn't know what to say, because I only knew her after—" He stopped. "After everything. And even then, mostly she was frightened, or grieving, or marching. But there were evenings. On the road. She used to hum while she carved. Badly. She hummed like a door hinge."

Isolde smiled, and for a moment the fraying edges stilled. "You loved her."

It was not a question, but he answered it anyway, because he was tired of guarding the sentence. "Yes."

"And she loved you. I watched it happen. It was infuriatingly slow — the two of you circling each other across half a kingdom like a pair of suspicious cats — but it happened. Which is why I'm here." She turned her hand over, and lying in her palm was a bead.

It was small, no bigger than a thumbnail, carved from pale bone — and Tomas recognized the work instantly, the fine spiral grooves, the tiny maker's mark near the drill hole: a spire with a star above it. Marguerite's signature. She must have carved it on the road, one of the quiet evenings, and given it to the ghost who traveled with them unseen.

"The covenant is sealed," Isolde said. "The dead rest. All of us — even the ones who clung hardest, even me. When I go through, I go through completely. No echoes, no hauntings, no little gifts drifting back." Her thumb moved over the bead. "But a gift can be given *before* the going-through. A loophole. I found it in the covenant's old terms — I signed those terms once, you know, and I have spent my death regretting every clause. It pleases me enormously to exploit one."

"What is it?"

"One memory. Hers. The covenant took what she offered willingly, but it could not take what she had already given away." Isolde held the bead out to him. "Inside is a single evening. The night before the ford, in the shrine at the waystation. She carved this bead and she talked to me, and partway through she stopped talking to me and started talking about you. About how you burned your commission. About how you flinched every time she looked at your wound but never once suggested she stop walking. About trust." The queen's voice, usually so crisp, went soft. "She said, *if I forget everything else, let me keep knowing what it feels like when someone chooses you twice.*"

Tomas could not speak. The garden was very quiet. Somewhere beyond the wall, the city bells rang the hour, and the sound seemed to come from far underwater.

"Why give it to me?" he managed finally. "It should be hers."

"It *is* hers. That's the point, inquisitor — do try to keep up, you were always slowest before breakfast." Isolde pressed the bead into his hand, and her touch was cold as river water and gone as quickly. "I'm not giving you a memory to keep. I'm giving you a memory to hold. Whether she opens it is her choice — hers alone, made freely, with no one watching and no debt attached. That's the only way it counts. Believe me. I know what it is to have choices made *for* you, however kindly."

She rose from the bench, or rather the idea of her rose, the silvered outline thinning toward transparency. At the edge of the garden she paused.

"Tomas. You stayed. Everyone who could have left, left, and you stayed, and you will stay again tomorrow, and the day after, asking nothing. That is either the stupidest thing I have ever witnessed or the finest, and I have decided—" the smile again, brief and bright as a struck match, "—it is both. Tell her the bead is from a friend. Don't tell her whose. Let her decide what it's worth before she learns who paid for it."

And then the frost went out of the air, and the garden was only a garden, and Tomas sat alone holding a piece of bone warm from a dead woman's hand.

* * *

He waited nine days, which he considered a heroic display of restraint and which Isolde, wherever she now rested, would probably have called typical.

In those nine days, Marguerite began rebuilding herself out of whatever materials lay at hand. She badgered the healers until someone produced a deer femur from the kitchen stores, and then she sat in the garden for hours with her knife, and the shavings that curled off the bone were so precisely, beautifully cut that the head healer came out just to watch. Her hands remembered. Her hands had never forgotten anything in their lives.

She also, on the sixth day, looked across the breakfast table at Tomas and said, "You're the one who sits in corners."

"I've been told that."

"You're the one who was here when I woke up. The healers won't say much about before, but they look at you like—" she searched for it, knife pausing over the bone, "—like you're a letter someone's afraid to deliver. Who are you? Really."

He set down his cup. He had rehearsed answers for this. *A guard. A former inquisitor. A man who helped you escape.* All true, all insufficient, all shaped to manage her rather than serve her.

"A friend," he said. "Of the late queen's, and now yours, if you'll have it. That's all I'll say unless you ask for more. And you shouldn't ask yet. Not because it's dangerous — it isn't, not anymore — but because some things are better found than handed."

She studied him for a long moment with eyes that had once known him completely and now catalogued him fresh: the gray at his temples he hadn't earned honestly, the sword calluses, the way he couldn't meet her gaze for longer than three seconds.

"Hm," she said. "You lie badly, whoever you are. But you don't seem to be lying now." She went back to her carving. "Sit where I can see you, next time. Corners make me nervous, and I don't know why, which is the worst kind of nervous."

So he sat in the open, in the garden light, and told her nothing, and it was the hardest and easiest work he had ever done.

* * *

On the ninth evening, he gave her the bead.

He had meant to be casual about it. He had planned a whole offhand delivery — *found this among your things, thought it might be yours* — rehearsed until the words lost their weight. Instead, standing in the garden at dusk with the first stars coming out over the Spire's distant silhouette, he simply held it out on his open palm and said, "This is from a friend. Someone who isn't here anymore. They wanted you to decide whether to open it. There's no obligation. None at all. If you'd rather throw it in the river, I'll walk you to the river myself."

Marguerite took it carefully, turning it in the last light. Her thumb found the maker's mark — the spire, the star — and stopped.

"I made this," she said slowly. "Didn't I. My hands know it. It's like meeting your own handwriting." She looked up. "Who was the friend?"

"That's theirs to tell, if the bead does."

"You're infuriating."

"So I've been told. Recently, in fact."

Something flickered across her face — not recognition, nothing so generous, but the ghost of a reflex, the sense of having made this exact joke with this exact person in some room now swept bare. She frowned at him, then at the bead, then tucked it into the pocket over her heart.

"Thank you," she said, precise as a craftsman. "Tell your friend — well. I suppose you can't." She hesitated. "If friends of yours keep leaving things for strangers, I may start to like your friends."

"They'd have liked that," Tomas said, and discovered his voice had gone rough, and pretended he had a cough.

* * *

She did not open it that night, nor the next. He knew because she kept it visible — on the sill of her window, in the sun, where the light ran along its spiral grooves — and he understood that she was doing deliberately what he was doing deliberately: learning the weight of a thing before deciding what it cost.

On the third evening she sought him out. He was mending a strap on his pack, a task he had stretched across four days, when she dropped onto the bench beside him close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, and held the bead up between two fingers.

"I've decided," she said. "But first — a question, and I want the truth, and I'll know if it isn't, because apparently I can always tell when you lie."

His heart performed something complicated. "Ask."

"If I open this, and it changes how I see you — if it makes you into someone I used to know instead of someone I'm just beginning to know — will you still be here the day after? Sitting in the open, where I can see you?"

"Yes," he said. No rehearsal needed. "However long that takes. However many days after."

She nodded, satisfied, the way she'd nod at a joint properly fitted. "Good. Because I've been thinking. Everyone keeps telling me what I *was* — the savior, the carver, the girl from the valley. It's like wearing someone else's coat. But this—" she tapped the bead, "—this is one evening. One. Small enough to try on. And whoever gave it trusted me with the choosing, which means they trusted me, full stop, and I find I want to know what that felt like. Even secondhand. Even borrowed."

She held it out between them, catching the dusk.

"So. Will you sit with me while I do it? Not telling. Not explaining. Just — present. Like furniture."

"Furniture," Tomas repeated gravely. "I can do furniture. I've had practice."

And Marguerite Quintana, who had saved the world and forgotten it, laughed — a short, startled, genuine laugh — and for one impossible moment the sound was so perfectly, so devastatingly *her* that he had to look away at the Spire and blink hard at the stars.

She closed her hand around the bead.

"Now," she said. "Tell me nothing. Starting now."

The bone warmed in her palm. Along its spiral grooves, faint as breath on glass, a light began to gather — and Tomas sat very still in the open, where she could see him, and waited to learn whether he was a stranger or a home.
