## 21. The Valley Remembers

The first frost of autumn came to Hollowmere on a morning so still that Marguerite could hear it — a faint crystalline ticking in the orchard, as though the valley were clearing its throat before speaking.

She stood at her workshop door with a cup of chicory going cold in her hands and listened to the silence.

It was not the silence she had grown up with. The old silence of Hollowmere had been a held breath, a hush pressed over the ears like wool — the sound of a valley doing its work of keeping things out. This new silence was different. It had texture. It had room in it. Somewhere down the lane a cart creaked; somewhere above, a crow argued with another crow about something only crows found important; and beneath all of it, where the dead used to press against the world like faces against glass, there was nothing at all.

Nothing, and it did not feel empty.

That was the part she was still learning. She had expected to grieve the quiet, the way one grieves a limb. Instead she kept catching herself standing in doorways like this one, listening to the absence, and finding it warm.

"You'll catch cold," said a voice behind her, "standing there like a heron."

Tomas came out onto the step with his own cup and his coat half-buttoned wrong, which after eight months he still managed most mornings. He had rebuilt the workshop's second room himself, badly, and then again with Odile's old notes open on his knee, better. His hands were a carpenter's hands now more than an inquisitor's, scarred across the knuckles from wood that fought back.

"I'm not a heron," Marguerite said.

"You're exactly a heron. All neck and judgment."

"Herons are dignified."

"Herons eat frogs whole," Tomas said, "and stand in swamps looking smug about it."

"That *is* dignified, when you think about it."

He laughed — he laughed easily these days, which had taken nearly as long as rebuilding the roof — and they stood together while the frost finished its ticking and the sun came up over the eastern ridge and turned the valley gold in long slow stripes.

Hollowmere had opened its borders in the spring. That sentence would have been unthinkable two years ago; the valley had spent three centuries perfecting the art of being nowhere, of being a wrinkle in maps, of answering travelers' questions with the bland courtesy of people who had nothing interesting to hide because nothing interesting existed here. Now there was a road sign at the southern pass. A real one, painted by the council, reading HOLLOWMERE — ALL WHO COME IN PEACE. Someone had added, in smaller letters beneath, in what Marguerite strongly suspected was old Marta's handwriting: *and if you don't come in peace, we know some very persistent ghosts.*

The ghosts were gone. But the joke stayed. Marguerite thought that was right, somehow. You kept the jokes.

* * *

The gifted had come slowly at first, then not slowly at all.

A girl from the river country who dreamed other people's dreams and woke exhausted from living too many lives. Twin brothers who could speak to bees, which sounded charming until you learned how much bees complained. An old soldier whose hands mended broken things — pots, fences, bones — without his quite meaning to, who arrived at the pass gate weeping and apologizing for both.

Marguerite taught them. That was the word the council used, *teach*, though what she actually did felt closer to what Odile had done for her: sat across a table, watched their hands shake, and told them the truth, which was that the gift was not a wound and not a crown but a craft, and crafts could be learned, and learning hurt, and that was normal.

"You're doing her voice," Tomas observed one evening, after a lesson. He meant Odile. He'd never met Odile; he'd helped bury what the inquisitors left of her. But Marguerite talked about her enough that Tomas had assembled the woman secondhand, the way you assemble a person from letters.

"I am not."

"'Now, breathe. The dead are patient. It's the living who rush.'" He folded his arms. "Word for word."

"That's just good teaching."

"It's haunting, is what it is. Benevolent haunting." He considered. "I'd have liked her."

"She'd have hated you for about a year," Marguerite said, "and then made you fix her roof."

"My roofs are excellent now."

"One of them is. The other leaks."

"The other is *characterful*."

She smiled into her cup. This was the shape their days had taken — bickering as a kind of weather, gentle and constant, rain on a roof that mostly didn't leak. She knew, in the way you know a room is missing a chair even when the room looks full, that there was a hole in her memory shaped like him. Isolde had told her so, at the end, in the fading hour when the queen had been more light than ghost: *You paid more than you know, bone-carver. There was a man. Be kind to him, whoever he turns out to be.*

So she was kind. And the strange thing — the thing she turned over at night like a stone with something glinting under it — was that kindness had begun, without her noticing the exact day, to feel like something else. Not memory. Something that didn't need memory. The way you can love a song you've forgotten hearing first.

* * *

On a grey Tuesday in late autumn, a wagon came up the south road carrying the last of the confiscated charms.

They had been held in the free city of Vael for two years — evidence, the magistrates called them, though evidence of what, no one could say, since the hunt was over and Aldric was ash and rumor. When the cities finally voted to return them, someone had the sense to ask where they should go, and every gifted hand in five cities pointed north, toward the valley, toward the bone-carver.

There were four hundred and eleven of them. Marguerite counted twice, because the number deserved accuracy: four hundred and eleven small objects, each one a voice stopped mid-sentence. Charms of bone and horn and river-stone, carved by hands like hers across the whole breadth of the realm. Some were crude. Some were beautiful. One, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of the crate, was Odile's — a charm of blackthorn wood and wren-bone that Marguerite recognized the way you recognize your own name spoken in a crowd.

She laid them all out on trestle tables in the workshop yard, and the gifted of Hollowmere came to help, and together they began the work of listening.

Not raising. Never that again. Just listening — holding each charm, feeling for the echo caught inside it, and writing down whatever remained. Names. Last words. A recipe for plum brandy. A child's counting rhyme. An apology, forty years overdue, addressed to a brother in a village that no longer existed. Marguerite copied each fragment into a great ledger, and the ledger went to the archive hall in Vael, and somewhere in the world each small voice was set down in ink instead of rattling in bone.

It took until midwinter. On the last day, when the tables were empty and the crate held nothing but wood shavings, the bee-twins' sister — a solemn girl named Petra who heard the thoughts of growing things — touched the empty crate and frowned.

"It's sad," she said.

"It's finished," Marguerite said.

"No." Petra patted the wood the way you'd pat a tired horse. "It's sad. It held voices for so long and now it doesn't. Things miss their work."

Marguerite looked at the girl for a long moment. Then she took the crate inside, sanded it smooth, and made it into a cradle, which she gave to the young couple at the mill who were expecting their first child in spring. The baby, when she came, was loud in the healthy, uncomplicated way of babies who have never once been haunted, and everyone agreed the cradle was lucky, and Marguerite did not correct them.

Some silences you fill. Some you furnish.

* * *

In February, the council asked her to take Odile's seat.

"There isn't a seat," Marguerite said. "Odile refused to sit on councils. She said committees were where good intentions went to be embalmed."

"Yes," said Elder Corwin, who had known Odile for fifty years and missed her with the specific patience of the very old, "and we've decided the valley has suffered enough, and it's time someone sat there anyway. Someone the gifted trust. Someone who—" he gestured vaguely at her, at the workshop, at the whole reorganized, reopened, cautiously hopeful enterprise of Hollowmere, "—does whatever it is you do."

"What I do is carve bones and argue with a carpenter."

"Then argue with us instead. We could use the practice."

She took the seat. She discovered she liked it less than carving and more than she'd expected, which she suspected was the honest description of most worthwhile work. Her first act was to establish a fund for travelers coming north with nothing — the hunt had left a generation of gifted people scattered and stripped, and charity, Marguerite had learned, was just memory wearing practical clothes. Her second act was to lose a procedural vote about goat grazing rights, which Elder Corwin assured her built character.

"Did you ever vote?" she asked him afterward, rubbing her eyes.

"Forty years. Lost hundreds of times." He gathered his papers with unhurried contentment. "The trick is losing to the right people."

* * *

Spring came up the valley like water filling a bowl.

The orchards bloomed. The mill couple's baby learned to yell. The bee-twins negotiated a labor agreement with their hives that everyone else pretended not to find alarming. And on a bright morning in April, Marguerite walked out past the shrine — rebuilt now, on its old foundations, plain and white-washed, with no names cut into it because the dead needed none anymore — and climbed the ridge where she and Odile used to sit when lessons ran long.

From up here the whole valley lay below like something carved and polished: fields, rooftops, the road winding south toward a world that had stopped hunting its own children. Smoke rose from chimneys. Somewhere a dog was having opinions. The wind moved through the grass in long silver strokes.

Marguerite sat down in the spot worn smooth by two generations of sitting, and let herself feel all of it.

This was the grief she'd never been allowed, back when the valley's peace depended on silence — grief for Odile, for the burned shrine, for the widow she'd charmed in chapter one of her own life and the hundred kindnesses done in secret. She had carried it through the capital, the road, the Spire. She had poured most of it into the keystone along with everything else. What remained now was not sharp. It was like the frost-morning silence: textured, roomy, warm at the center.

"You'd hate the committee," she told the wind. "Corwin keeps minutes. Actual minutes, Odile. He reads them aloud."

The wind said nothing, in the way of winds everywhere, and the nothing was companionable.

Down in the yard, faintly, she heard Tomas swearing at the characterful roof.

* * *

She found the bead in June.

She wasn't looking for it. That was the thing — she had genuinely stopped looking, had made a life so full of ledgers and students and leaking roofs and goat votes that the searching had quietly gone out of her, the way a fever goes. She was reorganizing Odile's old chest, preparing to donate the contents to the archive, when her fingers found a false bottom, and beneath it, wrapped in a scrap of cloth gone soft with age, a single carved bone bead.

She knew the carving style instantly. It was her own — her younger hand, her early work, the tight precise lines she'd used before Odile taught her to leave room for breath. But she had no memory of making it. Of course she had no memory. That was the entire architecture of the thing.

Isolde's gift. Or Isolde's question — the queen had never much distinguished between the two.

Marguerite sat on the workshop floor with the bead in her palm for a long time. It was warm. Bone always warmed faster than wood or stone, as if it remembered being alive, and this piece seemed to hold the warmth longer than it should, the way a hand holds warmth after a handshake.

She did not open it.

She wanted to — her thumb found the seam of it again and again, an itch under the skin of the day. But something in her, some instinct older than instruction, said *not yet*. Not because opening it might hurt. Because she had learned, this year of furnished silences, that some things keep. That a thing held patiently is not the same as a thing refused.

She put the bead in her pocket instead of the archive box. She carried it for weeks. Through the summer solstice festival, through Petra's graduation from student to colleague, through a July heat wave during which Tomas fixed the characterful roof at last and was insufferable about it for eleven days.

And then it was August.

* * *

The August evening came in gold and low, the way August evenings do when a valley has decided to be kind. Marguerite set two chairs outside the workshop door — the good chairs, the ones with cushions — and brought out the last of the plum brandy, distilled from a recipe recovered from charm number two hundred and six, which she felt gave the drinking a certain ceremonial weight.

Tomas settled into his chair with the sigh of a man whose roof does not leak.

"Big day tomorrow," he said. "The Vael delegation arrives. Corwin wants the yard swept."

"The yard is swept."

"He wants it swept *twice*. He wants it swept in a manner that communicates institutional dignity."

"I'll sweep it with gravitas."

"And the girl — Petra's new student, the dreamer — she's nervous. First time meeting city folk."

"She'll be fine. City folk are just folk with worse boots."

Tomas laughed, and the light went amber on the ridgeline, and Marguerite reached into her pocket without deciding to, the way you reach for a familiar stone.

The bead sat in her palm, warm as a held hand.

Tomorrow there would be delegations and swept yards and the long work of a valley learning to be a place instead of a hiding place. Tonight there was brandy and bickering and a man across from her whose face she knew the way you know a path walked daily — not from memory, but from use.

She turned the bead once between her fingers.

"Not yet," she murmured — though whether to the bead, or to herself, or to whatever patient ghost had carved it, she couldn't have said.

Across the little table, Tomas glanced over. "What's that?"

"Something I'm saving," Marguerite said, and closed her hand around it, and smiled.
