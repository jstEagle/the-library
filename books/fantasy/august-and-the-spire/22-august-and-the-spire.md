## 22. August and the Spire

The bead had sat in her pocket for a year before she could bring herself to hold it for longer than a moment.

It was small — smaller than a plum stone, carved from something pale and fine that she knew in her hands was bone even though her mind no longer carried the word *femur* or the memory of the first charm she'd ever cut. It was smooth from a ghost's careful turning. A queen's last act of kindness, pressed into the palm of a woman who would wake up with nothing.

Marguerite kept it the way other people kept coins they never meant to spend: touched often, used never.

Tonight, though, it was August, and August in Hollowmere smelled like cut hay and woodsmoke and the green rot of apples fallen too early off the tree behind her workshop. The valley had opened its borders in spring, and the road down from the pass now carried carts instead of soldiers, pilgrims instead of prisoners. She'd heard — from a trader, from a letter, from the ordinary gossip of an ordinary living world — that the free cities were teaching children to carve again. That somewhere in Vhalen there was a school with her name over the door, which embarrassed her so much she'd written to ask them to take it down, and they had replied with a polite letter explaining that they would not.

The evening light came through her window the color of honey left too long in the sun. Outside, someone's goat was complaining about something. Inside, Marguerite Quintana sat at her workbench with a dead queen's gift in her hand and finally, finally turned it over enough times to find the seam.

*You may open it or not,* Isolde had said — or so Tomas told her, because she herself remembered none of it. *One memory. Choose well.*

She had spent a year choosing badly, which is to say: not at all.

There were practical reasons. There always were. If she opened it, she might remember things that hurt. If she opened it, she might learn what she'd paid, and grief for a loss you couldn't feel was a strange, unproductive kind of grief — like mourning a room you'd never seen in a house you didn't own. And if she opened it and it was empty, if the bead held nothing but a queen's good intentions—

But those weren't the real reasons, and she knew it, sitting alone in the amber light with her thumb on the seam. The real reason was simpler and more cowardly. As long as the bead stayed closed, the woman she had been was still *possible*. Whole. Waiting. Open it, and whatever came out was all there would ever be.

A knock at the doorframe. Two knocks, apologetic, the second softer than the first, as though the knocker hoped the first hadn't been heard.

"Come in," she said, without turning around. "The door's open. It's always open. You know it's always open."

"I know," said Tomas. "I knock anyway. It seems rude to just appear."

"You appear every Thursday."

"Thursdays are different. Thursdays I'm expected." He came in carrying a basket — he always carried a basket; it was their running joke, or rather it was his joke and she had agreed to let it be theirs — filled this time with late plums and a loaf still warm enough to fog the cloth over it. "Bread from Marta. Plums from your own tree, technically, since your tree hangs over the wall and everyone in the village considers anything past the wall common property."

"That's theft."

"That's *agreed custom*. There's a difference. Theft has a trial afterward."

He set the basket down and did what he always did, which was sit across from her at the workbench without being asked, as though the chair had been assigned to him by some committee neither of them had attended. He was grayer at the temples than he'd been a year ago — she knew this only because she'd watched it happen, Thursday by Thursday, a change slow enough to be witnessed rather than remembered. That was the strange arithmetic of her life now: she had no past with this man, and yet she had more *observed* history with him than with anyone. Every Thursday, catalogued. The way he held his cup. The way he lost at chess and pretended not to mind and minded enormously.

"Good evening," he said.

"Good evening," she said. "You're staring at my hands."

"I'm staring at the bead."

Of course he was. He always noticed it — the one object in her workshop she never carved, never sold, never explained. She'd caught him looking at it perhaps forty times over the year, and each time he looked away quickly, like a man avoiding a scar on a lover's body.

"Do you know what it is?" she asked. She asked sometimes. It was almost a ritual between them, this question and its answer.

"No," he said, as he always said. "Isolde gave it to you before—" He stopped, the way he always stopped. "Before you woke up. She said you'd know when to open it. That's all I know. I promise you, that's all."

"I believe you."

"You say that like it costs you something."

"It does, a little." She smiled to soften it. "Trusting people doesn't come naturally to me. I've been told I used to be better at it."

Something crossed his face then — quick, gone, like a fish under ice. She saw it every few weeks, that flicker. Grief with nowhere to attach itself. She had learned not to ask about it, mostly because when she'd asked early on, he'd gone very quiet and said, *I think we should start with chess*, and had set up the board with hands that shook slightly, and she had understood that this was a man holding a door shut with his whole weight, and that she loved him too much — already, absurdly, against all sense — to lean on it.

They played chess. He lost. He pretended not to mind. She pretended not to notice him minding. The goat outside complained about the state of the world. The plums went around, and the bread, and the light went from honey to copper to the deep blue that Hollowmere did so well, the blue of a valley that had learned to be safe.

And then, because it was August, and because the bead had grown warm in her pocket through the whole long game as though it had opinions about timing, Marguerite said:

"I'm going to open it tonight."

Tomas's hand paused over the board — mid-move, a knight suspended above a doomed pawn. "Tonight?"

"Tonight. Now, probably. Before I lose my nerve, which historically takes about four minutes." She took the bead out and set it on the bench between them, next to the captured pieces. "I wanted you here for it. I don't know why. That's not true — I know exactly why. Whatever's in it, I don't want to be alone with it. You're the person I'm not alone with. Does that make sense?"

"Yes," he said quietly. "That makes sense."

"If it's bad — if remembering hurts — will you stay?"

"Marguerite." He said her name carefully, the way a man says a word in a language he learned long ago and never stopped speaking even after everyone else forgot it existed. "I have stayed through worse than a memory."

"What worse thing did you stay through?" she asked, genuinely curious, and watched the fish go under the ice again.

"Chess," he said. "Your chess. It's brutal. Ask anyone."

She laughed, and he laughed, and under the laughter there was that other sound, the one she couldn't name, the one like a man laughing while carrying something heavy up a long hill.

* * *

The seam gave easily. Of course it did — Isolde had made it to be opened, not kept. It parted under Marguerite's thumbnail with a sound like a held breath released, and the two halves of the bead fell open on the bench like a tiny mouth beginning to speak.

Light came out. Not much. A glow the color of the last minute of sunset, pooling in the groove of the bone, and within it — impressions, not images. She understood suddenly that this was not a picture to look at but a door to walk through, and that walking through it would cost her the bead itself, and that once she stepped in she could choose what to carry back out.

*One memory,* Isolde had said. *Choose well.*

Marguerite put her fingertips into the light.

The world folded.

She stood — or existed, or was simply *present* — inside a feeling the way you stand inside weather. And the feeling was this:

Being trusted.

Not trust as a fact. Trust as a *temperature*. The warmth of a hand letting go of yours at the top of a stair because it believes you'll find the next step yourself. The specific gravity of a secret placed in your palms like something breakable and alive. Someone turning their back to you in a dangerous place — not carelessly, but deliberately, as a gift, as a way of saying *I know who you are, and who you are is safe.*

She felt it whole. She felt it the way you feel sunlight through a window: knowing the sun is larger than the window, knowing the warmth is only a fraction of what's out there, and being glad of the fraction.

And threaded through it, faint as watermark, she caught edges of context — not scenes, not names, but shapes. A prison wagon swaying. A chessboard balanced on knees. A man burning a piece of paper that had his own name on it and watching the ash rise with an expression she somehow knew was the freest he'd ever been. Hands — his hands, she was nearly sure, though she could not have said how she knew — pressing a key into hers in a corridor that smelled of stone and fear, and the wordless sentence those hands spoke: *I won't watch them do this to you. Go.*

She could have pulled harder. She felt the rest of it standing there in the dark behind the warmth — a name, a face, a year of Thursdays, a wound taken at a river ford, a voice saying *begin again* — all of it available, all of it one deep breath away.

She also felt, with equal clarity, the price. Take it all, and she would be taking back everything she had paid to seal away — every grief, every debt, the whole terrible beautiful ledger of who she had been. And the woman she had become this year — the one who laughed at plums, who slept without listening for the dead, who had built a quiet life out of silence where ghosts used to press — that woman would be overwritten. Not destroyed. Just... set aside, like a coat worn through one long winter and finally hung up.

Marguerite stood in the warmth and chose.

She took the feeling. Only the feeling. The temperature of being trusted, the weight of a secret given freely, the memory of a hand letting go at the top of the stairs because the person below believed in her. She folded it into herself the way she'd once — she was suddenly sure of this, though she couldn't say why — folded charms into bone: gently, precisely, with love for the material.

Then she let the rest go.

The light dimmed. The bead halves cooled on the bench, and when she touched them again they were only bone — smooth, silent, finished. Somewhere very far away, or very close, she thought she heard a woman's voice, dry and regal and fond, say something that might have been *Well chosen* or might have been *Took you long enough*, and then there was only the goat, still complaining, and the blue hour, and Tomas watching her with an expression she had never seen on him before: hope held so carefully it barely dared to breathe.

"Well?" he said.

"Well," she said. Her eyes were wet. She wasn't entirely sure when that had happened. "It was worth the wait."

"What was in it? If you can— you don't have to tell me."

"I can tell you. That's the funny part. I can tell anyone. It's mine now." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand and laughed at herself. "It's a feeling. Being trusted. Someone trusted me with something enormous, and I could feel how much it cost them to do it, and they did it anyway. That's all. No name. No face. Just—" she pressed her hand flat over her sternum— "this. Warm. Like a coal that won't go out."

Tomas nodded slowly. He was smiling, and beneath the smile his eyes were bright and wrecked, and she thought: *he knows whose trust that was.* She thought: *he knows, and he will never say, because saying it would make it his, and he wants it to stay mine.*

"Whoever they were," she said, watching him, "they must have been extraordinary."

"They'd disagree," he said, and his voice cracked on the second word, and he covered it by reaching for a plum. "Extraordinary people don't lose at chess every Thursday."

"Maybe they lost on purpose."

"Nobody loses on purpose to you."

She laughed. Then, because the bead was spent and the night was warm and honesty was easier in August than in any other month, she said the thing she had been circling since spring:

"Tomas. Why do you come here? Every week. Bread, plums, chess, losing. You have a life in the city — you've told me about it, the school, the commission, all of it. You ride three days each way to lose at chess. Why?"

He was quiet for a long moment. The goat complained. Somewhere down in the village a dog answered it, taking the goat's side.

"Because," he said finally, "a long time ago — before I knew you, I mean, before I had the honor of knowing you — someone taught me that the most important thing a person can do is show up. Especially when showing up costs something. Especially when the person you're showing up for can't remember why you bother." He turned a captured knight over in his fingers, black paint worn silver at the ears. "So I show up. It's not a sacrifice. It's the best part of my week. It's the best part of my *year*."

"And someday," she said, "maybe you'll tell me who taught you that."

"Someday," he agreed. "When you're ready. When we both are."

"And until then?"

"Until then," he said, setting up the board, "we begin again. Your move."

* * *

Later — much later, the candles burned to stubs, the chess abandoned at a draw for the first time in recorded history — Marguerite walked him to the gate, because the moon was full and the road was safe and she wanted another ten minutes and was no longer the kind of person who pretended otherwise.

At the gate he paused. "One question," he said. "The feeling. The trust. Will you keep it? Or does it fade, like—"

"Like a dream?" She shook her head. "No. Charms hold. I'd know — I mean, I *do* know, everyone tells me I was famous for it." She smiled. "It holds. Whoever gave it to me built it to last."

"Good," he said softly. "That's — good."

He walked down the lane toward the inn, and she watched him go, and the coal behind her sternum glowed steady as a hearth-fire, and she thought: *I don't know your name. I don't know your face. But I know what you gave me, and I know you keep coming back, and I am going to spend the rest of my life earning it again.*

"Same time Thursday?" she called.

He turned around, walking backward, and the moonlight caught his smile. "Wouldn't miss it."

She stood at the gate until he was out of sight. Then she went back inside, to her workbench, to her tools and her pale clean bones and her quiet, and began — as she did every night now, joyfully, usefully, wonderfully — to carve.

Outside, the valley breathed. The dead slept. August held on, generous to the last, like a friend who knows the party's ending and stays anyway to help with the dishes.

And in the morning, she knew without knowing how, there would be plums.
