## 1. The Valley That Refuses Ink

There are three things every child in the valley learns before they learn their letters: how to read the weather off the mountains, how to keep a fence mended so the sheep don't wander, and how to say *nothing's wrong here* with a straight face.

I learned all three early. I learned a fourth thing too, though nobody taught it and I've never said it aloud: that the valley refuses ink. Not metaphorically. Literally. You can stand on Miller's Rise with a fresh quill and a good vellum sheet, sight down the length of the Aldwater to where it bends at the mill bridge, and draw what you see — and what you'll get is a map of somewhere else. A river with the wrong curve. Hills in the wrong places. Once, when I was eleven and foolish enough to try three times in an afternoon, a coastline.

We are two weeks' ride from any sea.

So I do what every sensible cartographer does with an impossible subject: I work around it. I survey by foot and chain and plumb line, I count paces like a miser counts coins, and I commit nothing to paper until I'm back inside my workshop with the shutters drawn, where the ink behaves itself for reasons I have never once been brave enough to investigate too closely. The valley gets its maps. The crown gets its toll-road charts and its tithe boundaries and its tidy little picture of a place with no secrets worth the parchment.

And I get to keep living here, which is more than most men with my particular affliction can say.

*Affliction.* My mother's word, delivered the way you'd name a wart or a weak heart. *It's not a gift, Dominic, gifts are given by someone who means well.* She'd caught me at fourteen, hunched over a scrap of hide on which I'd drawn our neighbor's lost ewe — drawn it from nothing, from no sighting, just a sudden itch behind my eyes and then the shape of a sheep standing under a specific crooked alder, and there the ewe was when we went looking, chewing away without a care in the world while old Marta Halversen wept over her like a returned soldier. Mother burned the hide in the bread oven and sat me down and told me, in the flat voice she saved for funerals and tax collectors, exactly what would happen to people like me if anyone ever found out.

That was twelve years ago. In those twelve years I have mapped drainage ditches, disputed hedgerows, the cellar of the new tithe barn, and one memorable stretch of road the crown insisted was a mile longer than God had made it. I am, by local reputation, a dull man with a steady hand and an unfortunate talent for being right about distances. Dull is armor. Dull is a long life. I recommend it.

On the morning everything cracked, I was doing the dullest thing in my repertoire: chaining out the boundary stones along the east pasture for Farmer Odell, who had gotten into one of his disputes with the widow Crane about whether a certain stone had walked four feet north since his grandfather's day.

"Stones walk," I told him, kneeling in the wet grass with my chalk and my ledger. "Frost heaves them. Sheep lean on them. It's four feet of argument over a rock, Odell."

"It's the principle," Odell said, which is what every man says when the principle costs him less than the alternative. He stood over me with his arms crossed, breathing through his nose like a bull deciding whether the red thing was worth the charge, and above us the August sky did its usual valley trick — blue as a painted ceiling, close and still, the kind of sky that makes you feel like a figure in someone else's miniature.

I set the stone's position down in the ledger, packed up my chain, and walked home along the Aldwater with my boots dusty and my mind agreeably empty. That was the whole of my ambition on that particular morning: an empty mind, a cool drink, and an afternoon spent fair-copying the Odell-Crane dispute into something the assize clerk could read without swearing.

The valley let me have the walk. It even let me have the drink. It was the afternoon it objected to.

* * *

My workshop sits behind my mother's house, a converted smokehouse that smells permanently of tallow and old paper, and I love it the way some men love a chapel. Every map I've ever made honestly lives there, rolled in oilcloth or pinned flat under river stones. Every map I've ever made dishonestly lives there too, in a locked chest under the floorboards, because I lack the imagination to destroy evidence and the courage to keep it anywhere else.

I unrolled a fresh sheet of vellum, weighted the corners, ground a little gum arabic into my ink — the good iron-gall, the batch that behaves — and began the fair copy. Boundary stone at the alder. Stone at the ford. Stone at the rise where the pasture breaks toward Crane's orchard. My hand moved in its usual way, which is to say like a plow horse: steady, unsurprised, going exactly where it is told.

Somewhere around the fourth stone, my hand stopped being told.

I noticed it first as a sound — a faint dry whisper, nib on vellum, continuing after I had lifted my fingers from the pen. I looked down. The pen lay across the inkwell where I'd set it down. And the line was still moving.

Not my line. My lines are ruled and patient, black and narrow. This line moved the way water moves under ice — quick, silver-dark, sure of itself — and it was drawing hills. Rolling country, steep-sided, with a river threading through the middle of it in a pattern I knew with sickening intimacy was *wrong*, because I have spent half my life learning every legal lie about this valley's geography and this was not one of them. The river forked. Our river does not fork. The hills closed around the fork like cupped hands, and into the space between them the ink poured little marks — dwellings, maybe, or thorns, I couldn't tell — dozens of them, ringed and ranked, deliberate as beads on an abacus.

I did the reasonable thing. I flung myself backward off the stool, which is how I came to be sitting on the floor of my own workshop with a bruised tailbone, watching a pen draw a map with nobody holding it.

"Stop," I said. To a pen. Because terror makes idiots of us all, and my particular idiot tradition is talking to objects.

The pen did not stop. It finished the hills, finished the forked river, and then — this is the part I still can't make sit quietly in my head — it drew a road. One road, winding up out of the cupped hills, running south and west across the vellum in a long confident stroke, and at the end of the road it wrote, in a hand that was almost mine but older, tighter, like writing practiced in front of a mirror:

*THE ROAD IS STILL OPEN.*

Then the nib lifted, hovered a moment like a heron deciding on a fish, and touched down once more below the words. A single mark. A small, branching, wicked-looking shape.

A thorn.

The pen rolled off the edge of the desk and clattered on the floorboards, and the room went ordinary again — tallow smell, dust motes, the muffled sound of Mother beating a rug next door as if the world were a place where rugs got beaten and pens stayed put. I sat on the floor for a long time. Long enough for the light through the shutter crack to travel the width of the room. Long enough to notice my hands shaking and be annoyed at them, because shaking hands are a luxury for men whose ink doesn't write back.

Here is what I knew, sitting there among my honest maps: whatever had just happened, it hadn't come from me. I want to be clear about that, because later — when other people started telling this story — everyone assumed the pen was mine doing, my gift gone rogue, my secret leaking out the end of a quill. But I know my own gift the way you know your own cough. When it takes me, there's an itch behind the eyes, a pressure, a sense of leaning toward something far away. There was none of that. This was the opposite of reaching. This was something *arriving*. Something that had traveled a very long way to put three words and a thorn on my vellum, in a valley where — I have always believed, I was raised to believe, my mother staked both our lives on believing — magic cannot follow.

Apparently it doesn't need to follow. Apparently it can send letters.

I should have burned the sheet. Any sensible man in my position — a man whose continued breathing depended on the crown never learning there was anything unusual about Dominic Kavanagh — would have fed it to the stove before the ink dried and spent the evening telling himself it was a fit, a fever, bad gum arabic.

Instead I sat on the floor and looked at the thorn, and felt something I hadn't felt since I was fourteen years old watching a ewe appear under a crooked alder: the vertiginous, shameful thrill of being *right* about the world. All those years of dullness, of chained paces and disputed stones, of being the valley's most boring man on purpose — and underneath it all, apparently, I was still a door. And doors don't get to choose what walks through them.

I copied the map. That's the confession I'll carry into whatever comes next: before I burned anything, I copied it. Twice. Once into my working ledger, small and disguised among drainage notes, and once onto a scrap of good vellum that went into the locked chest under the floorboards with all the other evidence of my slow-motion suicide. Then I fed the original to the stove and watched the hills curl and the forked river go brown and the words blister and vanish — except the words didn't quite vanish. Heat darkens iron-gall; it doesn't erase it. For a moment, as the sheet blackened, *THE ROAD IS STILL OPEN* stood out in ghost-grey against the char, legible, patient, like something that could afford to wait.

I raked the ashes fine and went inside to supper.

* * *

Mother had made barley soup, which she makes when she's worried, and was pretending it was just barley soup, which is how I knew she'd heard something. News travels fast in a valley. Faster than roads, faster than rivers, and considerably faster than sense.

"Odell says you were short with him," she said, ladling soup with the gravity of a woman arming a garrison.

"Odell wanted me to declare a rock insane."

"Mm." She sat, folded her hands, and studied me across the table with the look she'd worn for twelve years — the look of a woman perpetually listening for hooves. "You've got ink on your face."

I wiped at the wrong cheek. She reached over and scrubbed the right one with her thumb, rough as sandstone, and for a second neither of us said anything, and the silence between us was the old familiar architecture: a house built of things unsaid, load-bearing walls of *nothing's wrong here*, roofed tight against weather neither of us named.

"Dominic." She stirred her soup without eating it. "Anything happen today?"

And here is where I should tell you I lied well. I'd had twelve years' practice. But my mouth was full of the taste of woodsmoke and ghost-grey letters, and what came out was:

"Do you remember what you told me. About the valley. About why we're safe here."

Her spoon stopped.

"You said magic can't cross the boundary. You said that's why we came, why your mother came, why the gifted families settled here and kept their heads down and became farmers and fletchers and — cartographers." I kept my voice level, conversational, a man discussing rocks. "You said nothing on the outside can reach us in here."

"That's what I said." Her eyes had gone very still. People underestimate my mother; they take her smallness and her flour-dusted forearms for harmlessness, the way they take my dullness for emptiness. "Why?"

"Because today," I said, "something reached us."

I told her. Not all of it — not the copies in the ledger and the chest, because a man has to keep one wall of the house standing even as the rest burns — but the pen, the hills, the forked river, the words. She listened the way she listens to storms coming over the pass: motionless, counting the distance by the thunder.

When I finished, she was quiet for the length of three breaths. Then she stood, went to the window, and checked the latch, which she does when the world has stopped making sense and latches are the last sense available.

"A thorn," she repeated. "It drew a thorn."

"At the end of a road. Coming south and west."

"South and west," she said softly, "is the deep end of the valley."

"I know where south and west is, Mother."

"No." She turned around, and in the lamplight her face had aged ten years in the space of a sentence, and I understood suddenly that my mother had not spent twelve years merely hiding. She had spent them waiting. There is a difference, and the difference was standing in her kitchen holding a ladle. "You know where it is on a map. You don't know what's there. Nobody knows what's there. That's rather the point of it."

"The point of what?"

She looked at me for a long moment — measuring, I realize now, how much of the truth I could carry, or perhaps how much truth she could bear to hand to a son who had already started copying forbidden maps instead of burning them. Outside, the August dusk was settling over the valley, blue and harmless, the sheep bells tinkling somewhere up the slope, the whole world performing innocence.

"Eat your soup," she said finally. "And tomorrow, when Odell comes about his rock, you will tell him the rock hasn't moved, and you will smile, and you will be the dullest man in this valley. Do you understand me?"

"Mother—"

"*Do you understand me?*"

"I understand you," I said. And I did. What I didn't say — what I couldn't say, because I only admitted it to myself much later, lying awake in the dark with the itch behind my eyes and the ghost of a forked river behind them — was that the dullest man in the valley had spent that afternoon watching a door open in the bottom of his safe, ordinary life. And that the words on the burning page hadn't frightened me half so much as the feeling underneath them.

Because *the road is still open* wasn't a threat. Read it again, the way I read it a hundred times that night, the way I still read it now. It wasn't *beware*. It wasn't *stay away*.

It was an invitation. Addressed, by a hand almost exactly like my own, to me.

Three days later, the criers came to the mill bridge, and the valley learned that the crown had declared a hunt for the gifted — and I stood in the crowd with ash under my fingernails, understanding that whatever had written to me across the impossible distance of my safe little world, it had not written soon enough. Or perhaps, and this thought arrived cold and unwelcome as frost on a window:

it had written exactly soon enough, and the road it spoke of ran straight through me.
