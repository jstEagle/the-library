## 2. Proclamation at the Mill Bridge

The trouble with living in a valley that refuses ink, I've always maintained, is that it also refuses gossip. News arrives here the way water arrives in a drought — eventually, grudgingly, and usually smelling of somewhere else.

So when the criers came to Mill Bridge on the first market day of harvest, half of Alderbrook thought it was a tax notice and brought ledgers.

I brought bread. This turned out to be the correct instinct, though not for the reasons I assumed at the time. You want your hands full when the world changes; it gives you an excuse not to applaud.

There were three criers, which was two more than any proclamation in my lifetime had warranted. They stood in a row on the bridge's crown, in tabards of royal black and gold, and the middle one — a broad man with the voice of a landslide and the eyes of a man reading someone else's laundry list — unrolled a scroll with the ceremony of a surgeon opening a patient.

The millwheel turned behind them. It always turns. That was the whole joke of the location, I suppose, though I doubt the crown's heralds appreciated it: a proclamation about fire, delivered over moving water, in a town named for grinding grain into something softer.

"Hear ye," the landslide voice began, and the market went quiet the way a flock of birds goes quiet — all at once, and for different reasons.

I will set down what he said, as near as I can recall it, because I have a cartographer's memory for boundaries and this was a boundary. Everything after that morning sat on one side of it.

"By decree of the Crown, and in the name of the peace of the realm: it is henceforth declared that the practice, possession, or concealment of the Gift is an offense against the Crown and against the common good. All bearers of magic — called the Gifted, the marked, the touched — must present themselves for registration within forty days. Those who shelter them share their guilt. Those who surrender them shall be rewarded."

He paused there. I remember thinking, absurdly, that he was waiting for applause, and that no one in Alderbrook had ever applauded a tax notice either, so he was going to be disappointed.

"Those who do not surrender themselves," he continued, "shall be sought. And those who are found," and here his voice did something I did not expect — it softened, the way a man's voice softens when he's about to tell a widow her son is dead, "shall be burned."

The millwheel kept turning. Somewhere a child laughed, then stopped, having noticed that no one else was laughing.

*Burned.* Not imprisoned. Not exiled. Not even the euphemism of "processed according to law." Burned, said plainly, in a market square that smelled of apples and wet wool, to a crowd that included the midwife, the schoolmaster, and old Tam Wicker, who was eighty-one years old and mostly deaf and had to have it explained to him twice, loudly, by his granddaughter, who then looked around at her neighbors as though seeing which of them had heard.

I stood very still and ate my bread.

This is a skill of mine, eating bread while the world rearranges itself. I recommend it. The mouth is busy, the eyes can wander, and no one remembers afterward what the man with the bread did or didn't do. I watched the crowd the way I watch unfamiliar terrain — not looking at anything, cataloguing everything.

Marta Hale, the farrier's wife, standing with her arms folded, nodding slowly. Marta had always nodded at proclamations; it was a reflex of hers, like a dog sitting when told. But beside her, her husband was not nodding. He was looking at the criers with his jaw set, and I remembered — because my memory for boundaries is a curse as often as a craft — that the Hale's eldest boy had survived a fever last winter that had killed three other children in the same week, and that Hester Hale had been seen walking the boy's sickroom at night with her hands glowing faint as foxfire, and that the village had agreed, collectively, without ever saying a word, to have seen nothing of the kind.

I wondered what Marta was nodding at now.

The schoolmaster, Pell, had gone the color of porridge. Pell, who taught arithmetic to children and had once, after a bottle of plum wine at the harvest dance, confided to me that he could make a slate write its own sums if he hummed to it, and that he'd never told a soul, and wasn't it a funny sort of lonely, keeping a thing like that? I had agreed that it was. I had bought him another bottle. I had not mentioned that my own secret was considerably larger than his, because one does not trade confidences with a man who hums to slates; one simply stands downwind of him and hopes.

And near the front — this I will confess I looked for deliberately — stood Seren Vael, the huntress, back from the northern ranges a full month early, her bow slung across her back like an afterthought, her face doing nothing at all.

I should explain about Seren, or at least explain what I understood of her then, which was approximately one-tenth of what I understood later and one-hundredth of what I felt. She was the crown's tracker, quartered at the garrison by the ford, and she was the only person in Alderbrook who could out-walk me, out-ride me, and out-silence me, in that order of humiliation. She sang to her horses when she thought no one was listening. She sang badly. She knew she sang badly. She sang anyway, which told me more about her character than any conversation we ever had, and I include the conversations we had later, the ones where she told me things I still can't write down without my hands going unsteady.

That morning she stood in the crowd and listened to a decree that would, in time, put her own hands in irons, and her face did nothing, and I thought: *there is a woman who has already decided what she thinks and sees no reason to share it with a bridge.*

Then her eyes moved through the crowd — the way a tracker's eyes move, in sweeps, in quadrants — and passed over me, and stopped, and came back.

I took another bite of bread.

She held my gaze for perhaps two seconds. There was nothing in it I could read, which from her was normal, but there was something in it I could *feel*, which was not. It was the look of a woman filing something away. I have since learned that this is exactly what it was. At the time, I filed it away myself, under *problems for another day*, which is where I filed most things that year, and the drawer got very full.

* * *

The criers read the proclamation twice more, once in the square by the well and once at the crossroads by the tannery, and by the third reading the crowd had thinned to idlers and the idlers had thinned to silence, because the second and third readings gave people time to do arithmetic.

Forty days. That was the figure everyone carried home like a stone in the shoe. Forty days to decide what you were, what your neighbor was, and what your neighbor might decide *you* were. Forty days for the crown's men to arrive behind the criers — because they always arrive behind the criers; the proclamation is the soft hand, and what follows is the other kind.

I walked home the long way, along the millrace, because the long way is empty and the short way is full of people doing the same arithmetic.

Here is what I did not know that morning, and what I want to set down honestly, because a man who tells his own story gets to choose what to confess and I have decided to be greedy about it: I did not yet know that the proclamation was aimed, in part, at me. I knew my gift was a hanging offense in principle — it always had been; the valley was simply too far from anywhere for the principle to bother traveling. I knew the moving ink was a candle in a cellar. What I did not know was that somewhere in the capital, a compass had already begun to turn.

What I knew that morning was smaller and in its way worse. I knew that Alderbrook had just stopped being a place where everyone agreed to see nothing.

The valley had always had a covenant, unwritten, older than the mill. Magic couldn't cross the valley's rim — I'd tested that boundary my whole life, patiently, the way you'd test a scar — but inside it, small gifts survived like flowers under a stone. Hester Hale's foxfire hands. Pell's arithmetic slates. The brewer's daughter who could tell when a cask would spoil. Old Mother Ives, who was not, whatever the children said, a witch, but who did know when it would rain to the hour, and was never wrong, and had never once explained how. We all knew. We all arranged our knowing carefully, the way you arrange furniture in a small room, so that no one trips and no one has to look directly at it.

The proclamation picked up all that furniture and turned it over.

I reached my workshop as the light was going long and amber, and I stood in the doorway a while, looking at the shelves.

A cartographer's workshop is a confession that hasn't been read yet. Every map is a record of where you've been, and mine were records of rather more than that. The mundane work was there — the survey charts, the drainage plans, the boundary disputes I'd arbitrated with ink and patience. But beneath the floorboards under the drafting table, wrapped in oilcloth, was the rest of it. Twenty years of the gift. Maps that had drawn themselves. Maps of places that don't exist, sketched by my own hand while my hand was somewhere else entirely. Maps with margins full of ink that moved when no one was watching, that crept toward certain corners of the page like iron filings toward a lodestone, that sometimes, on bad nights, spelled things out.

I had told myself for years that they were harmless. That the valley's silence protected them. That no one would ever come looking, because no one had ever come looking, because the valley was the place the world forgot on purpose.

The proclamation had just informed me, in the landslide voice of a bored man on a bridge, that the world had remembered.

* * *

I burned them that night. All of them.

I want to be precise about what that cost, because it's easy to make it sound noble and it wasn't noble. It was surgery without anesthetic. It was amputating twenty years of the only part of myself I'd never had to hide.

I built the fire in the yard, behind the workshop, where the light wouldn't show through the shutters, and I fed the maps to it one at a time, because I am a coward even in arson and wanted to be able to stop.

The first ones went easily. The early maps, from when I was young and the gift was a novelty — a sketch of a coastline I'd never seen, drawn in my sleep; a city of white terraces that my ink had built on its own while I was mending a fence. They burned like any paper. Paper doesn't care what it means.

Then the others.

There was a map — I will describe it and then I will stop describing it — of a valley that was not this valley, ringed in hills that were not these hills, with a mark at its center that my ink had made and remade until the paper wore thin: a circle of thorns. I had never understood it. I had folded it small and kept it at the bottom of the chest, the way you keep a letter you can't answer. When it went into the fire, the flames went *green* for the length of a breath — one breath, no more — and then they were ordinary fire again, and I stood in my yard in the dark with my heart going like a millwheel and told myself I had imagined it.

I had not imagined it. I know that now. But a man can survive almost anything if he's allowed to misremember it for a few years.

The last map I burned was the one I'd been working on that spring: the survey of the north ranges, the honest, mundane, taxable work I did for the crown's survey office, except that in the lower margin, where I kept my private annotations, the ink had begun to move again. Little rivers of it, creeping. Little marks accumulating in one corner, in a pattern I didn't recognize and recognized anyway, the way you recognize a face in a crowd before you know whose it is.

I held that one over the fire longer than the rest. Partly because the survey office would want its chart, and I'd have to redraw it, and redrawing is a week of work. And partly because — I'll say it plainly, since no one's listening — I was afraid that burning it wouldn't work. That ink which moves on its own might have opinions about being burned. That I'd feed it to the flames and the flames would refuse, the way the valley refuses ink, and then I would be standing in my yard holding a piece of paper that had just told me *no*.

It burned. Paper is loyal to fire that way. But as it caught, the moving ink in the margin seemed to *run* — the marks scattering like ants whose stone has been lifted — and for a moment, before the fire took them, they arranged themselves into something I could almost read.

Almost. That is the gift and the torment of my whole life, summed in one word. Almost.

I stood there until the fire died down to red eyes and ash, and then I raked the ash into the cold ashes of the woodpile where it would look like nothing, because I am thorough even in cowardice, and I went inside and did not sleep.

Here is the arithmetic I did instead, lying in the dark, listening to the millrace:

The proclamation said *concealment* was an offense. Every person in Alderbrook who had ever politely not-seen Hester Hale's hands was now, by the letter of the law, guilty. Which meant the law had just made criminals of the entire valley, and the only currency left was denunciation. Marta Hale's nodding. Pell's porridge face. The brewer's daughter, who was seventeen. Forty days, and then men with the crown's authority would start asking questions, and neighbors who had spent a generation arranging their not-knowing would discover how much they'd actually known all along, and how much of it was worth a reward.

And somewhere in that arithmetic, unavoidable as a boundary line, sat me.

I had burned the maps. But a gift isn't paper. You can't feed it to a fire, and I'd known that since I was nine years old, since the first time the ink moved and I ran to my mother and she held my face in her hands and said, *then we never speak of it, and we never let it show, and we live anyway.* She'd been right for thirty years. The valley's silence had been right for a hundred.

The proclamation had ended the silence. And a gift that can't be burned can be *seen*, and I had spent my whole life making sure it couldn't be, and now there were forty days, and men coming behind the criers, and a huntress who had looked at me on a bridge for exactly two seconds too long.

I got up before dawn and redrew the north ranges survey, honest and mundane and taxable, every river in its place, every boundary true.

And in the lower margin, in the corner where the moving ink had gathered, I left the paper blank.

It did not stay blank.

By the time the light came up over the eastern rim, three small marks had appeared there, neat as stitches: a circle of thorns, a road running toward it, and beneath the road, in an ink-hand that was mine and was not mine, a single word.

I have read a great many maps, and I am not easily spooked by marginalia. But I will admit that when I looked down at that small, patient, self-written word, my first thought was not *what does it mean*.

My first thought was: *how long do I have?*

The word was: *coming.*
