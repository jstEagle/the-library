## 3. What the Compass Found

The trouble with living honestly in a valley that refuses ink is that you get very good at lying with your hands.

I'd spent three days after the proclamation pretending to be a boring man. It's harder than it sounds. Boring men don't burn papers at midnight; they don't bury ash under the turnip bed and plant something fast-growing over it, like grief or radishes. Boring men don't stand in their own workshop at dawn, running their thumb along a shelf of maps that no longer exist, feeling like a man who's amputated his own hand and keeps reaching for things.

My mother knew. Mothers always know. She stood in the doorway that morning with two cups of barley tea and the particular expression she wore when she'd decided not to ask questions — a kind of polite blindness she'd perfected over thirty years of marriage to my father, who had been many things, none of them simple.

"You've been up all night," she said.

"I've been up all night being unremarkable," I said. "It takes concentration."

She set the cups down on my drafting table, on top of the one map I hadn't burned — the valley survey, the honest one, the dull one, forty pages of elevations and stream widths and the precise angle at which the eastern ridge declines into farmland. My bread and butter. The work that kept me legitimate. She looked at it for a long moment, then at me.

"Dominic."

"Mm."

"If anyone asks, you are exactly what you appear to be."

"I am exactly what I appear to be," I said. "A cartographer of modest talent and immodest fees."

"Good." She handed me the tea. Her hands were steady, which frightened me more than shaking would have. My mother shook when she was afraid. When she was *resolved*, she went still as a millpond. "Then drink your tea and be boring where I can see you."

It might have worked. That's the thing that haunts me about that morning — it might actually have worked, if not for the compass.

* * *

The compass belonged to a man named Aldery Voss, royal cartographer second class, who arrived in our valley on the third day of the hunt with a warrant, four witch-hunters, and an instrument he called a truth-needle. I learned all this afterward, of course. At the time, I learned it the way everyone learns catastrophe: gradually, then all at once.

It began with Tam Wicker's boy hammering on my door at an hour when respectable people were still asleep.

"Master Kavanagh! Master Kavanagh, there's soldiers at the mill bridge!"

"There's always soldiers at the mill bridge," I said, pulling on my coat. "It's a bridge. Bridges attract soldiers the way jam attracts wasps."

"Not like this." The boy's face was gray as porridge. "They've got a crier. And a — a thing on a pole. A needle thing. It's *spinning*."

I want to tell you my blood went cold, that I felt the floor tilt beneath me, that some prophetic shiver ran up my spine. What actually happened was more banal and more terrible: I finished buttoning my coat, told the boy to run home and stay there, and walked out into the street wearing the face of a curious tradesman. Because that's what we do, isn't it? We put on the face first and feel the fear later, in private, where it can't cost us anything.

The mill bridge was already crowded. Our valley holds perhaps four hundred souls, and it seemed every one of them had found a reason to be near the bridge that morning — checking their traps, admiring the water, standing very casually in clusters of three and four with their heads together. Suspicion has a smell. It smells like wet wool and held breath.

The soldiers had formed a line across the bridge's throat. In front of them, a crier in crown colors read the proclamation again to a crowd that had heard it twice already — *all bearers of the gift must be surrendered to his Majesty's hunters, that the land may be cleansed* — and beside him, mounted on a pole like a standard of war, hung the compass.

I should describe it properly, because it deserves description. It was the size of a dinner plate, brass-cased, its glass clouded with age, and its needle was not iron but something pale and bone-colored that moved wrong — not swinging the way needles swing, but *leaning*, the way a dog leans toward its master's voice. As I watched from the edge of the crowd, it turned slowly, deliberately, past north, past east, and settled pointing down the bridge, toward the village, toward—

Toward my street.

Not at me. Not yet. The needle couldn't pick me out of a crowd, I'd learn later; it could only find the *work* of magic, the residue, the place where gift had soaked into wood and stone. And my workshop — my little shop with its drafting table and its forty honest maps and its hearth where I'd burned everything else — my workshop was saturated with twenty years of moving ink. If the needle leaned toward anything in this valley, it would lean toward that room the way a drunk leans toward the last lit window.

"Clear," said Voss, consulting it, and made a mark on a slate. "Next quarter by noon."

He was smaller than I expected, Aldery Voss. A neat, dry man with ink-stained fingers — a fellow craftsman, I thought absurdly, we could compare nibs — and eyes that never finished looking at anything. He consulted his needle the way I consulted my instruments: without doubt, without wonder. That frightened me more than cruelty would have. Cruelty hesitates sometimes. Competence doesn't.

I did the arithmetic standing right there in the crowd. Quarter by noon. My street was the third quarter. I had perhaps four hours to make my workshop innocent, and you cannot scrub twenty years out of floorboards before lunch.

So I did what any sensible traitor to himself would do. I went home and started lying to my own house.

* * *

My mother met me at the door, took one look at my face, and said, "How long?"

"Till noon. Maybe less." I was already moving through the workshop, opening shutters, airing the room — because stale rooms smell of secrets, don't ask me how I know. "Mother, listen. Whatever they ask—"

"I know what to say."

"You don't know what they'll ask."

"Dominic." She caught my hands, and hers were still steady, millpond-still. "Your father hid from the press-gangs for two winters. I lied to a tax assessor's face while your father's contraband sat under our bed and you slept in the cradle above it. I have been lying to authority since before you could walk. Let me do my work."

What do you say to that? I said nothing. I scrubbed. I carried my drafting table out and set it in the yard as if sunning it, stripped it bare, washed it with lye until the wood went pale. I scattered honest clutter — invoices, grain measurements, a commission letter from the wool factor dated last month. I swept the floor so hard I nearly went through it. And through all of it, the needle's lean hung in my mind like a splinter: *toward my street, toward my door, toward the room where the walls themselves had learned my secret.*

Because here's what I hadn't understood until that morning, scrubbing lye into boards like penance: magic doesn't leave when you burn the evidence. It soaks in. Twenty years of maps that drew themselves, of ink that moved at midnight, of a gift practiced daily in one small room — the room remembered. The needle wasn't hunting me. It was reading my house like I read terrain.

At half past eleven, my neighbor Marta Hale came over the fence with a basket of plums and an expression I mistook, God forgive me, for sympathy.

"Heard they're searching the quarters," she said. "Terrible business. Your mother holding up?"

"She's made of millpond," I said. "You know her."

"Mm." Marta set the basket down. She looked around my yard — at the naked table, the lye-pale boards visible through the open door, the too-thorough cleanliness of everything. And something crossed her face, quick as a fish under water. I saw it. I've kicked myself ten thousand times for seeing it and saying nothing. "That's a lot of washing," she said, "for a man with nothing to hide."

"Marta—"

"No, no." She smiled, and it reached her eyes, which somehow made it worse. "Nothing to hide. Of course. Good luck at noon, Dominic."

She took her plums and went back over the fence, and I stood in my yard knowing — *knowing*, the way you know weather coming off the ridge — that I had just been weighed and found worth selling. But here's the shameful part, the part I keep in the locked drawer of myself: I couldn't even hate her. Marta Hale had three sons and a husband who drank, and the crown was paying informants in silver, and fear makes arithmetic of us all. She'd simply done sums I hadn't. Betrayal by the familiar doesn't come with horns and a black cloak. It comes over the fence, in an apron, carrying fruit.

* * *

They came at noon precisely, because competence keeps schedules.

Four hunters in gray-and-crimson, and behind them Aldery Voss with his slate and his terrible needle, and behind *them* — this is the detail that still turns my stomach — a handful of neighbors, gathered at a respectful distance to watch, the way you watch a house fire. Marta Hale among them. She didn't look away when I saw her. That took a kind of courage too, I suppose. The courage of a woman who has decided not to be ashamed until it's convenient.

Voss didn't knock. He stood in my doorway holding the compass flat on his palm, and the bone-colored needle strained against its casing, leaning toward my workshop like a starving thing toward bread.

"Well," he said, almost gently. "That's the strongest reading in the valley. Stronger than the tanner's barn, stronger than the old shrine. Twenty years strong, I'd guess." He looked up at me, and those unfinished eyes went through me like a probe through soft ground. "You're the surveyor. Dominic Kavanagh."

"I am."

"Forty-one maps of this valley in the crown archive. All accurate. All *dull*." He pronounced *dull* like a diagnosis. "No anomalies. No unexplained features. Not one margin note in a strange hand. Do you know how rare that is? Every surveyor leaves fingerprints, Kavanagh. Every single one. You left none." He tilted the compass slightly, watching the needle strain. "Until now."

My mother came out of the house wiping flour from her hands, wearing her millpond face, and stepped between us with the ease of long practice.

"Can I help you, sirs? This is a private household."

"Warrant to search, madam, under the Gifted Hunt Act." Voss produced the paper without looking at it. "We'll be brief."

"Search, then." She folded her arms. "You'll find paper, ink, and a man who charges the wool factor double for rush work. If that's a crime, arrest him and save me the argument."

And she was *good*. I mean that. While the hunters went through my workshop with gloved hands, she stood in the yard answering Voss's questions with the flat, bored fluency of a woman discussing drainage — yes, he works alone, no, he keeps no apprentices, yes, the ink is ordinary oak-gall, you may test it, and indeed they tested it, and it *was* ordinary, because I'd swapped every bottle that morning. She gave them nothing. She built, brick by brick, a wall of tedium around me, and for one shining hour I believed in it.

Then Voss walked to the center of my workshop, held up the compass, and let it speak.

The needle didn't waver. It pointed straight down at the floorboards, straining, quivering, and Voss crouched and laid his palm flat against the wood, the way I'd lay a palm against stone to feel for water.

"Here," he said softly. "And here. And along this wall." He rose, brushing dust from his knee, and turned to my mother with something almost like courtesy. "Madam, does your son sleep badly?"

"What?"

"The readings cluster around the drafting table and the bed loft. Places a man spends his nights." He wiped his fingers on a cloth, delicately, as if the floor had soiled him. "Residue of practiced gift, madam. Years of it. This room has been *used*."

"That room has been used for cartography," my mother said, and her voice was still level, still millpond, but I heard the ice creak underneath. "By a licensed surveyor. With a license. Which you are welcome to check."

"Oh, I checked it this morning." Voss smiled — a small, dry, professional smile. "Excellent credentials. That's rather the point. A licensed surveyor with a spotless archive and a floor that screams." He turned to me. "Mr. Kavanagh. Where is your real work?"

"My real work is in the crown archive," I said. "Forty-one maps. You said so yourself."

"Yes. Dull ones." He began to walk the room slowly, trailing his fingertips along the wall like a blind man reading. "You see, I've done this in nine valleys, Mr. Kavanagh. And in eight of them, the gifted run. They flee, they hide, they leave cold trails and empty houses. But every so often—" he stopped at my drafting table, bare and lye-scrubbed, and rested one ink-stained finger on it "—every so often there's one who stayed. Who hid in plain sight. Who was so careful, for so long, that his caution itself becomes the loudest thing in the room." He looked up at me. "Forty-one perfect maps. Forty-one. No surveyor is that good. Only a man hiding something is that good."

And there it was. The trap I'd built myself, board by board, year by year: my competence had become my confession. The very perfection that kept me safe had marked me, because perfection, in a valley full of flawed and struggling craftsmen, is itself a kind of anomaly. I had been so busy hiding the magic that I'd forgotten to hide the excellence.

"I don't know what you want from me," I said, and it came out hoarse.

"The truth would be a start." Voss opened his hand, and one of the hunters stepped forward with a case — and inside it, nested in velvet, lay instruments I'd only heard whispered about. Rings of cold iron. A slender stylus of white metal. "Or we can proceed to testing, which is less pleasant for everyone, and which tends to leave even the innocent somewhat changed." He said it mildly, the way a physician discusses bleeding. "Your choice, Mr. Kavanagh. Truth, or iron."

My mother moved. I'll give her that — she moved like a woman planting herself in front of a charging ox. "He is my son and he is no—"

"Mother." I said it quietly. Because I'd seen the hunter's hand drift toward the iron rings, and I'd done the arithmetic again, the same arithmetic as ever: survival first, principle later, self-loathing at leisure. "Mother. It's all right."

It wasn't all right. Nothing about what happened next was all right. But I looked at Voss, and I thought: *he wants a finder. He said it himself — nine valleys, cold trails. They can't find the gifted. That's why they need me.* And I thought: *if I go quietly, if I bargain instead of burning, maybe I'm still holding the pen.*

"Captain," I said — he wasn't a captain, but flattery is free — "if I tell you what you want to know, will you search my mother's house anyway?"

Voss raised an eyebrow. "That depends what you tell me."

"Then let's not waste each other's afternoon." I picked up my satchel from its hook — my working satchel, the one with the good calipers — because a man walking into captivity should carry tools, even if the only tool he has left is himself. "I'm coming with you. Voluntarily. And my mother stays out of this."

"Dominic—" My mother's voice cracked at last, the millpond finally breaking, and that sound hurt worse than the iron rings would have.

"It's fine," I told her, and kissed her forehead, and whispered against it, so only she could hear: "Radishes grew well this year. Tell no one."

She laughed — one broken, wet syllable of laughter, our oldest code, *the turnip bed, the ashes, keep the lie alive* — and then the hunters closed around me, and Voss fell into step beside me with his needle stilled at last, satisfied, and we walked down my street past my neighbors' faces, past Marta Hale's carefully blank window, out toward the mill bridge where the whole thing had started.

I remember thinking, as the bridge boards rang under our boots: *home was safe for thirty-one years. Thirty-one years of dull, perfect maps.*

And then a needle found the one room where I'd been brilliant.

Behind us, my mother stood in her doorway, flour on her hands, watching her son walk away in custody — and I did not look back, because I already knew what I'd see, and some things a man cannot carry and still walk straight.

What I didn't know — couldn't know, then — was who waited at the far end of the bridge, checking the girth straps of a gray mare, humming a tune so confidently off-key that the horses seemed to tolerate it out of pity.

I was about to meet the huntress who would ruin my life in the best possible way.

But that, as they say, is another chapter's business.
