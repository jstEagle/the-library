## 4. Iron and Oath

They took me out of the valley in a wagon with iron bars, which struck me as redundant. I was a cartographer. The most dangerous thing about me was my handwriting.

The bars were for show, I understood that later. Everything the crown did on this hunt had a little theater in it — the black cloaks, the white horses, the compasses they carried like relics. A man in chains makes better gossip than a man in a coat, and gossip is how you teach a valley to be afraid.

I spent the first day of the ride memorizing the road. Not because I planned to escape. Because it's what I do. The world is a page and I can't stop reading it, even when the reading is being done to me.

My mother's face at the window stayed with me longer than I wanted it to. She'd lied to Captain Vess with her chin up and her hands folded, told him her son was nothing but ink-stained and dull, and she'd almost gotten away with it until old Marta Corrigan leaned over her fence and said, in a voice pitched to carry, "His maps move, Captain. I've seen them. My boy saw one walk off the table."

Marta. Forty years of borrowing sugar and returning it with interest. Forty years of her standing at my mother's hearth every midwinter. One sentence, and I was cargo.

I didn't hate her, which surprised me. What I felt was something colder and more useful: I understood her. Fear does that. It doesn't make people wicked; it makes them accurate. She'd looked at me and seen exactly what the criers had told her to see, and she'd done the arithmetic every neighbor in that valley was doing now — what is he worth to me alive versus silent?

* * *

Captain Vess received me in the tithe barn at Crossford, because apparently the crown believes interrogation goes better among the smell of old grain and rope. He sat behind a trestle table with a single candle, which I took as a compliment. Men who want you comfortable are men who've already decided what you are. Men who give you one candle are still deciding.

He was not what the ballads promise. No scarred cheek, no voice like gravel. He was a tidy, gray-templed man with kind eyes and the posture of a schoolmaster, and when he gestured me into the chair across from him, he did it the way you'd invite a colleague to sit.

"Dominic Kavanagh," he said, consulting a paper he plainly didn't need. "Cartographer. Commissioned work for the mill guild, the parish, two landholders. Sound reputation. Quiet man." He looked up. "Your gift moved ink."

"It's a nervous hand," I said. "Runs in the family."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment that I'd said a joke, filed away for later consideration.

"Master Kavanagh. I'm going to tell you how this evening goes, so we can both relax. There is a document in front of me with your name on it. Under crown law, a confirmed bearer of magic may be interrogated by any means the presiding officer deems necessary. I have never found those means necessary. I find that people talk, given time and respect." He folded his hands. "But there is also a woman named Seren Hale outside, who is the finest tracker I have employed in eleven years, and if you decline to talk, I will ask her to follow your mother home tonight, and simply watch. Watching is legal. Do you understand me?"

I understood him. That was the worst part. He wasn't threatening torture. He was threatening patience, applied to the people I loved, and there is no oath or principle that survives patience aimed at your mother.

"I understand," I said.

"Good. Then let's begin with something small." He turned the paper around. On it was a sketch — my sketch, taken from the workshop, the one where the ink had walked. "This map drew itself. Places you've never been. Tell me what it is."

And here is where I should tell you I was brave. Here is where some better man says *burn me, then*, and means it, and the story remembers him kindly.

What I actually thought, sitting in that chair with one candle between us, was: *if I die here, Mother watches them take the house anyway, and Marta Corrigan gets the fence dispute settled in her favor, and nobody ever tells my father's grave why.*

What I actually said was: "It's a map of somewhere real."

Vess went very still, in the way of a man who has been fishing all day and finally feels weight on the line.

"Go on."

"The gifted don't live in towers, Captain. They don't live anywhere the crown can find, because everything the crown can find has already been found. They live in the places that resist mapping. Valleys that fold. Woods that turn surveyors around. There are enclaves — whole settlements — tucked into the geography like coins under floorboards. Every mapmaker in the kingdom has hit those places and blamed their instruments." I heard my own voice go flat and professional, and hated it, because it sounded like a sales pitch, because it was one. "You've been hunting for a year and caught what — stragglers? Hedge-witches too poor or too stupid to run? You're not finding enclaves because you can't see them. I can."

The candle flame leaned, though there was no draft.

Vess studied me for a long moment. "You're offering to hunt your own kind."

"I'm offering to read a map," I said. "What you do with the reading is crown business."

Liar. Coward. Both true, and neither sufficient. Let me be precise about what happened in that barn, since precision is the only virtue I've got left: I chose survival, and I dressed the choice in technical language, and I watched a good man nod along believing he'd bought a tool when what he'd bought was a knife that would cut whichever way I could make it cut.

Because that was the calculation underneath the shame, the one I couldn't say aloud: *every enclave I lead them to is an enclave I can warn.* I would be inside the hunt. I would know the routes, the timetables, the man Vess trusted. And somewhere ahead of the hunters, always, there would be a night when my ink moved first and a family walked out the back of their lives carrying what they could hold.

It was a plan built on a foundation of myself, which is to say it was built on sand with delusions of stone. But it was a plan. It was the only shape my survival could take that didn't require me to become entirely the thing they wanted.

"Terms," Vess said at last.

"You don't burn anyone on my word alone. You verify. If I point and you find nothing, you release me and my mother keeps her house."

"And when I find something?"

"Then I keep working."

He nodded slowly. "You'll travel with the company. You'll be treated as an asset, not a prisoner — provided you remain useful. The moment I believe you're steering us wrong, Master Kavanagh, the arrangement changes, and it changes in ways you won't enjoy." He rose, straightened his coat, and for a moment the schoolmaster fell away and I saw the other thing underneath, the thing that had burned whatever he'd burned before Crossford. "One question before we conclude. Your map — the one that drew itself. Did it show you anything you recognized?"

"No," I said.

That was true, and it was the last wholly true thing I would say to him for a long while, and I want it noted that I noticed the difference.

"Then we're concluded." He unlocked the shackles himself. "There's stew, if you want it. We march at dawn."

* * *

Outside, the camp had gone quiet except for the horses. A woman sat on a rail fence near the picket line, tuning a fiddle she never seemed to play, and I knew her at once from the way the sentries gave her space without being told to — the way you give space to a drawn bow.

Seren Hale. The tracker. The threat made flesh that Vess had mentioned with such gentle menace.

She looked at me the way trackers look at things: completely, without hurry, taking inventory. Then she frowned slightly, as if I'd said something odd, though I hadn't spoken.

"You flinched," she said.

"When?"

"Just now. When the horse shook its head." She slid off the fence. "Why would a mapmaker flinch at a horse shaking its head?"

"Because it's a large animal near my head," I said. "Most people call that sense."

"Mm." She didn't smile. But she kept looking at me a moment longer than inventory required, and I had the distinct, unpleasant sensation of being read — not the way Vess read me, page by careful page, but the way weather reads a coastline. Finding the shape underneath. "Welcome to the hunt, Master Kavanagh. Try not to draw anything."

She walked off toward the fires, humming something tuneless and wrong, and I stood there in the cold with my wrists chafed and my soul freshly sold, doing the arithmetic I'd be doing for months afterward:

Every lie buys a life. Every life costs a lie. And somewhere down that road, the sums were going to come due all at once, and there would be no map for the place where they did.

Behind me, in the dark of the supply wagon, I heard it — faint, unmistakable, the dry whisper of a nib dragging across paper.

My ink was drawing again.

And it was drawing fast.
