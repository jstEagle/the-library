## 5. The Huntress Who Sang Off-Key

The horses hated the valley road, and I sang to them anyway.

That was the arrangement between me and every animal I'd ever handled: they endured my voice, and in exchange I kept them from bolting at shadows, which — on this particular road, in this particular season — were plentiful and mostly armed. The song was an old drover's tune my mother used to hum while she plucked geese, all about a girl who marries a river, and I sang it flat as a millstone, because that was how I'd always sung it, and because Captain Vess had once told me that if I ever learned to carry a tune she'd have no way of finding me in a crowd.

I took that as a compliment. Vess did not hand out many.

"Again," she said now, riding knee to knee with me at the head of the column, her voice pitched under the wind so only I could hear it. "You let him hear you coming, Seren, and he'll be dead before we crest that hill."

"He's bound wrist to wrist," I said. "And blindfolded. And sitting in a cart."

"And he put three of my best compasses into fits before we ever laid eyes on him." She said it without heat, which from Vess meant she'd been thinking about it for two days. "A man who can make iron lie doesn't need his hands or his eyes. He needs one unguarded moment."

I glanced back at the cart. Dominic Kavanagh sat exactly where the guards had dumped him, spine straight against the rail like a man posing for a portrait nobody had asked for, the black cloth knotted over his eyes. His wrists were raw where the irons had chewed them. He didn't shift. He didn't slump. He just sat there with his chin slightly lifted, as if he were listening to something past the edge of hearing — and then, when my gaze lingered half a second too long, he turned his head toward me. Toward me specifically, blindfolded, across forty feet of rutted road and six mounted men.

He flinched. A small one, barely a twitch of the shoulders, like a man who'd touched a door handle and gotten a spark off it.

Then he faced forward again and went back to being furniture.

"Huh," I said.

"What?"

"Nothing." I nudged Bracken forward. "Hill's ahead. Let's go ruin someone's morning."

* * *

Here is what the crown knew about Dominic Kavanagh before we ever reached him: that he was a surveyor of some local repute, thirty years old or near it, unmarried, the son of a widow who kept bees; that his maps of the Thornvale were considered the finest in three counties, though no one could say precisely why, since the valley was small and dull and mapped to death already; and that a royal compass, the kind we use to sniff out gift-work, had spun toward his workshop hard enough to crack its housing.

Here is what the crown did not know, because I hadn't told anyone: that when we dragged him out of his mother's house at dawn, he had looked at our warrant — not read it, *looked* at it, the way you look at a bill you've been expecting — and said, quietly, "Which neighbor?" And that when Vess named the man, Kavanagh had nodded once, like a physician confirming a diagnosis he'd already made, and walked to the cart on his own legs.

Prisoners begged. Prisoners wept, bargained, pissed themselves, swore vengeance. In four years of hunting I had never once seen a prisoner look *inconvenienced*, the way you're inconvenienced by rain.

Now, on the road east toward the first of the season's targets, he was inconvenienced again, and worse than inconvenienced: he was *nervous*. Not about us. About the road.

We crested the hill above Miller's Hollow an hour past noon, and I watched him through the gap in the cart's slats as the village came into view below — smoke from chimneys, geese in the lane, a shrine to some harvest saint with its little tin sun polished bright. Ordinary. Sleepy. The kind of place where nothing has happened for a hundred years and everyone likes it that way.

Kavanagh's whole body went rigid.

"Left fork," he said suddenly. His voice came out rough, like it hadn't been used yet today. "Take the left fork past the shrine. Not the right."

Vess reined in. "Why?"

"The right fork floods this time of year. Spring melt off the ridge. You'll lose the axle."

It was plausible. It was even true — I could see the marsh grass creeping up both verges of the right-hand road, the telltale yellow-green of ground that drinks more than it should. Any surveyor would know it. That was the reasonable explanation, and I gave it to myself twice, and both times it slid off something in my chest that wouldn't hold it.

Because he hadn't looked at the road. He'd been facing forward, blindfolded, when he said it. And the flood damage, if there was any, would be around the bend, out of sight of the hill.

"Check it," Vess said to me.

I rode down alone, singing to Bracken — flat, steady, the goose-girl and her river — and found the right fork exactly as promised: two hundred paces along, the culvert under the road had silted up and the water stood ankle-deep across the ruts, wide as a barn door. An axle would have gone through. A column would have lost an hour digging out.

An ordinary surveyor might have known that. From maps. From memory. From the spring before last.

But he'd said it *before* we rounded the bend. Before the road was visible at all.

I sat there a moment with the water dripping off Bracken's fetlocks, listening to the geese argue in the lane behind me, and felt the particular cold that comes down the back of the collar when a case stops being a case and starts being a question. Then I turned around and rode back up and reported what I'd seen, in the flattest voice I owned, and did not report the rest.

Not yet. I wanted to watch him longer first. Call it professional thoroughness. Call it whatever keeps a person warm at night.

* * *

Miller's Hollow held nothing. We turned the village inside out by sundown — every loft, every cellar, every root-cave in the hillside — and found lint, and cheese, and one extremely indignant ferret. If there had ever been gifted folk here, they'd smelled us coming and gone, and good luck to them.

Vess took it the way she took everything, which was silently and with a jaw like a drawn bow. She stood in the empty square while her hunters stacked their gear, and I saw her glance at the cart, at the blindfolded man sitting in it, and I saw the thought move behind her eyes like a fish under ice.

"You knew," she said. Not a question. She crossed to the cart and stood over him. "Before we came down the hill. You knew the village was empty."

"I know roads," Kavanagh said. "Roads are my trade."

"Roads don't tell you who's fled them."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "They really don't."

There was a silence with a knife in it. I sat my horse at the square's edge, close enough to hear, far enough to be scenery, and I watched the two of them — the captain with her grey braid and her patience like a loaded crossbow, and the prisoner with his ruined wrists and his maddening calm — and I noticed the thing I'd been circling all day finally land.

He wasn't calm. He was *holding* calm, the way you hold a full cup on a moving cart. And every few minutes, when the wind shifted or a door banged somewhere in the village, his head turned a fraction toward it — toward things he couldn't possibly have heard through wool and distance. Once, when a shutter banged loose on the far side of the square, he flinched before the sound finished traveling. I timed it against my own ears. He beat me by a breath.

You can explain a lot of things about a man. You cannot explain a man who hears tomorrow's weather in yesterday's wind.

"Captain." One of the outriders came jogging up, a boy called Pell with mud to his knees. "Found something at the treeline. North field."

What Pell had found was a thorn branch, snapped fresh, lying across the path out of the north woods. Except it wasn't a thorn branch, quite. The thorns were long as my finger and curved like hooks, and the wood of it was dark as old blood, and when Vess crouched to look at it, the cut end was still wet. Sap, you'd think. Except sap doesn't steam in evening air.

Nobody touched it. Nobody had to. We'd all seen warding-thorn before, or heard of it — the kind of hedge that grows where gifted folk want to be left alone, the kind that takes a hand off a man who reaches for it uninvited. One of ours had died to it in the spring, over in the hill country. Tam Osgar. Good lad. Sang worse than me, if you can believe it.

"There's an enclave in these woods," Vess said. Standing. Brushing off her knees. Her voice had changed — flattened, brightened, the voice she used when the hunt stopped being work and started being blood. "Close. Days, maybe less. Pell, mark the spot. We camp here tonight and—"

"They'll be gone by morning," Kavanagh said.

Everyone turned. He hadn't moved. His face was tilted toward the thorn branch he couldn't see, and for the first time since his arrest, the calm had cracked clean through, and underneath it was something I recognized, because I'd spent four years learning to hide the same thing in my own face.

Fear. But not of us.

"They'll be gone by morning," he said again, softer, "and if you ride after them in the dark, the thorn will take more than a branch."

"And how," Vess said, very quietly, "would you know that?"

Silence. The geese had gone to bed. Somewhere a dog was dreaming. And Dominic Kavanagh, blindfolded and bound in a cart in a village square, turned his head — not toward Vess, not toward the guards, but toward me, sitting my horse at the edge of the firelight, and said:

"Ask your tracker. She's the only one here who isn't certain I'm lying."

Every eye swung to me. I kept my face the way I keep it on bad days: pleasant, blank, a shut door with flowers painted on it. Inside, my heart was doing something undignified.

"Is that so," Vess said. It wasn't a question either. Nothing she said was ever a question; questions implied doubt, and doubt was for other people.

"He's guessing," I said. "They all guess. Give a prisoner enough rope-shaped words and he'll hang himself with them eventually." I swung down and started unsaddling Bracken, busying my hands. "I say we do what you were going to do anyway. Camp. Ride at first light. If they've run, they've run — but thorn hedges don't walk away, Captain. Whatever grew that will still be growing tomorrow."

It was good reasoning. It was even mostly honest. What it was not was the reason I said it, and the reason was standing in a cart with bloody wrists, watching me with his blindfolded face like he could see straight through the painted flowers on my door — and what lived behind mine, God help me, was starting to look uncomfortably like what lived behind his.

Vess held my gaze a moment longer. Then she nodded, once, and the camp began to build itself around her decision, tents and fires and picket lines, the great machine of the hunt folding itself down for the night.

* * *

I took the second watch, because I always took the second watch. First watch belongs to people with families to write to; third watch belongs to insomniacs and the guilty. Second watch is mine: the world quiet but not yet dead, the fires burned to orange hearts, the horses drowsing on their feet.

I walked the picket line singing to them, flat and low, the goose-girl and her river, and Bracken flicked one ear at me in the ancient equine gesture that means *your singing remains a crime, but proceed.*

That was when I heard the other singing.

It came from the cart. Thin, careful, barely more than breath — a lullaby, or the bones of one, some valley tune I half-knew, sung so softly it might have been meant for no one. Sung badly, actually, wandering off its notes like a drunk leaving a tavern. But sung *gently*, and aimed — I realized as I drifted closer, leading Bracken by the rein — not at himself.

At the horses nearest the cart. Two of Vess's greys, high-strung beasts that had screamed and fought their tethers since noon, ever since we'd come within sight of those north woods. They were standing hipshot now, heads low, breathing slow, asleep on their feet for the first time all day.

He couldn't see them. He was singing to them anyway, and they were listening, and the song was working, and every hair on my arms was standing up like the thorns in the north field.

"That's cheating," I said.

He stopped. Turned his head toward my voice. Up close, in the dark, with the blindfold pushed crooked, he looked younger and much more tired, and there was a smear of dried blood at his temple from the arrest that nobody had bothered to clean.

"Is it?" he said. "They were frightened."

"Of the woods."

"Of what's in the woods." A pause. "Animals know before we do. Always have."

"Do they." I leaned on the cart rail, casual as anything, and studied him. He studied me back, or aimed his face at me, which amounted to the same thing. "You know, most prisoners spend their first week begging. Or plotting. You've spent yours giving road advice and serenading cavalry mounts. Either you're the strangest man the crown ever arrested, or—"

"Or," he said, "you should decide for yourself what I am. You're the tracker. Track."

Something cold walked down my spine wearing my own boots. Because that was the second time today he'd pointed at me — at *me*, out of everyone — as if he knew. As if he'd picked the one hunter in his company whose curiosity outweighed her obedience, and decided, coolly, deliberately, to cultivate it.

Or as if he knew something else. Something about me that I hadn't told a living soul, that I kept locked in a box under my ribs next to the frost-dreams and the off-key singing and the way animals had always, always come to me first.

"Get some sleep, surveyor," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "Long ride tomorrow."

I led Bracken back down the picket line. Behind me, after a moment, the soft bad singing started up again, and the greys sighed, and the fire settled, and I stood in the dark with my heart going like a drum and understood, with the clarity that only comes at two in the morning, that I was in trouble.

Not the kind Vess could burn. The other kind. The kind that starts with a question you can't stop asking.

*What are you?*

And underneath it, quieter, in a voice I didn't recognize as mine:

*And what am I, that you looked straight at me?*

In the north woods, something rustled where nothing should have been walking. The thorn hedge, maybe. Growing.

By morning, the enclave would be gone — he'd said so, and I was beginning to suspect that when Dominic Kavanagh said a thing about roads and running, the world arranged itself to oblige him. By morning we'd ride north into empty woods and Vess's jaw would set another degree tighter, and the hunt would roll on to the next village, and the next.

Unless I told her what I'd seen tonight.

Unless I didn't.

I stood my watch until the sky went grey, singing flat to the horses, and made my choice the way I made all my choices lately: slowly, unwillingly, and with the distinct feeling of stepping off a map's known edge.
