## 6. First Blood on the Map

There is a particular quality to guilt that no one warns you about: it is patient. It doesn't arrive like a storm. It arrives like damp — seeping in through the mortar of an ordinary morning until the whole wall of you is cold and you can't remember when it started.

Mine began with a compass.

Captain Vess's royal cartographer — a narrow man named Pell with ink-stained cuffs and the eyes of someone who had never once been surprised by anything he'd seen from above — carried a compass of black horn that did not point north. It pointed at me. I knew this because I'd watched it do it three times now, each time we broke camp, its needle swinging past true north like a dog straining at a leash toward the thing it wanted to kill.

"Your instrument disagrees with my maps," I told him on the fourth morning, as cheerfully as a man can who hasn't slept. "Perhaps you should have it seen to."

"It isn't broken," Pell said, without looking up. "It's interested."

I decided then and there that I hated him, which was convenient, because hating him was considerably easier than hating myself.

* * *

The hunt had been in the field for nine days. Nine days of Vess riding at the column's head like a blade wearing a uniform, of Seren ranging ahead on her grey mare singing something that was either a folk ballad or a crime against music, of me sitting my horse with my hands bound loosely to the saddle horn — for show, mostly; Vess had learned quickly that I worked better unbound, and pride is a cheap leash — while my gift crawled out ahead of us through the earth like a blind worm tasting for bread.

That is the closest I can come to describing what it does. The gift doesn't see. It *tastes*. Roads taste of iron and old feet. Rivers taste of cold and forgetting. And places where the gifted have gathered — places where magic has soaked into the timber and the soil the way smoke soaks into a coat — those taste of honey gone slightly wrong. Sweet, but with something underneath it that makes your back teeth ache.

I had spent twenty-six years keeping that taste to myself inside a valley where magic couldn't follow, where my gift was a compass with nothing to point at, where I could pretend I was merely a man who drew very good pictures of very ordinary hills. Then the crown had put a sword at my back and said *taste*, and I had said yes, because I am, at bottom, a coward with excellent penmanship.

So on the ninth day, when the honey-and-rot taste rose up out of the ground beneath my horse's hooves, strong enough that I actually swayed in the saddle, I knew exactly what I had found.

An enclave. A real one. A family, most likely — enclaves this close to the valley were never large, just two or three households of the gifted tucked into some fold of land where the old wards still held, living quiet, planting quiet, dying quiet. Living the life I had lived, before the compass came for me.

And behind me rode forty hunters who would burn them out of it.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about treachery: it is not a single decision. It is a hundred small ones, each one reasonable, each one survivable, and you only recognize the shape of the whole when you're standing inside it.

I had told myself, in the beginning, that I would find nothing. That my gift would fail outside the valley — it always had before, hadn't it? That was the story I'd clung to through Captain Vess's interrogation, the bargain I'd struck with my own conscience: *you'll promise them anything, because the gift won't work anyway, and everyone lives.*

Then it had worked. Of course it had worked. The valley's silence had never been a cage around my talent; it had been a mercy extended to everything else.

So I had begun lying with my maps instead. Drawing the taste wrong. Bending roads that ran straight, souring sweet ground into barren, sending the column marching in confident circles through country that grew more suspicious of us with every mile. For six days it had worked beautifully. Vess had grown quieter, which frightened me more than shouting would have, but she had not yet grown suspicious.

Now I had perhaps an hour to ruin all of it.

* * *

The enclave sat in a hanging valley off the north road — I could taste its shape the way you can taste the shape of a fruit through the skin of your cheek. Three farmsteads, maybe four. A millstream. Old warding work in the hedgerows, the kind that hums against the gift like a struck bell. And people. I could taste the people. Two of them tasted of smallness, of milk and quick feet.

Children.

"There's nothing here," I announced to Vess, as the column crested the ridge that looked down into the hanging valley. My voice came out steady. I was almost proud of it. "The taste I caught yesterday was a dead trace. Old hearth-magic, years cold. See for yourself — no roads lead down there but sheep tracks."

Vess didn't answer immediately. She stood in her stirrups, looking down at the green fold of land with its honest little fields and its thread of millstream, and I watched her face do the thing it did when she was weighing a man's word against a needle's.

It was Seren who spoke first. She had ridden up beside me without my noticing, which was becoming a habit of hers I resented deeply, and she was frowning at the valley with her head tipped like a bird's.

"The smoke," she said. "Three chimneys, and it's morning-cook smoke, not banked fire. Someone's home down there, Captain."

"Chimneys lie," I said. "Half these farms are empty. People leave, Seren. They leave all the time." I heard my own voice climb half a note and hated it. "Ask your prisoner. He has no reason to lie."

"No," Vess agreed mildly. "None at all."

She turned her horse down the slope.

* * *

I want to be fair to myself here, because no one else will be. In the hour that followed I did not simply sit on my horse and watch. I tried. Gods know I tried, in every sly and cowardly way available to me.

When the vanguard reached the first hedgerow and the warding in it woke — I felt it wake, a shiver through the ground like a cat stretching — I called out that the hedge was diseased, that bramble blight had been through, that any man who touched it would carry spores back to the horses. Two hunters believed me. One even stepped back.

Seren dismounted, studied the hedge, and said, "This bramble's been cut and trained. Deliberately. Look at the spacing." She glanced up at me, and there was something in her face — not suspicion, worse than suspicion, *attention* — that made my stomach drop. "You said sheep tracks. Sheep tracks don't prune."

"I'm a mapmaker, not a shepherd."

"You're a liar with excellent penmanship," she said, almost pleasantly, and turned away before I could decide whether she'd meant it as an insult or a joke or both.

They found the first farmstead empty. Doors open, hearth warm, a pot of porridge still breathing steam. The family had run — they must have felt the hunt coming the way animals feel weather, or perhaps their own wards had whispered warning down the hedgerows. But they had run heavy, with children and livestock and whatever they could not bear to leave, and heavy things leave slow.

Vess read the ground like I read a chart. "Two hours ahead. Maybe three. They'll make for the high passes." She swung up into the saddle. "Master Kavanagh. Which way?"

And there it was. The question, laid out clean as a drawn line on vellum. Point wrong, and maybe they escaped — or maybe Vess stopped trusting my maps entirely, and then every enclave in the province burned because I'd spent my credibility on one family. Point right, and I delivered them.

I pointed left. Toward the river gorge. It was wrong — the family had gone right, up toward the passes, I could taste their trail like a smear of honey on the wind — but the gorge was slow going, and a lie with a delay in it was still a lie that bought time.

"They'd cross at the shallows below the falls," I said. "It's the only ford a loaded cart can manage."

Vess looked at me for a long moment. Then at Pell, whose black-horn compass lay open in his palm, its needle straining east. Toward the passes. Toward the truth.

"Interesting," Pell said. "The compass disagrees with the cartographer."

"The compass pointed at *me* a week ago," I snapped. "Has it occurred to anyone that your needle finds magic, not fugitives? There are wards all over this country, old ones, dead ones, they'll pull it every which way—"

"Enough." Vess's voice wasn't loud. It never needed to be. "We ride both ways. Pell takes ten men east. I take the rest to the gorge." Her eyes settled on me, gray and unhurried as deep water. "You ride with me, Master Kavanagh. If your ford is there, you've earned your supper. If it isn't—" She smiled, which she did rarely and badly. "Well. We'll discuss your supper."

* * *

The gorge was beautiful, which felt like a personal insult. Evening light came down the rock walls in long amber bars; the river ran shallow and loud over its stones; and nowhere along a mile of bank was there a ford that could take a loaded cart, because I had invented it, and invention does not survive contact with water.

Vess reined in at the gorge mouth and looked at the river for a long time. Behind her, thirty hunters waited. Ahead of us, somewhere over the eastern ridges, a family with two small children was climbing toward the passes with every minute I had bought them.

I was doing the arithmetic of my own survival — *she can't prove the lie, she needs me, she'll keep me* — when the horn sounded from the east. Three notes, rising. Contact.

Pell had found them.

I will not pretend I understood, in that moment, why my chest went the way it did. I had known them for less than an hour. I had never seen their faces. But my gift had tasted those children — milk and quick feet — and some part of me had already filed them under *mine to protect*, the way a mapmaker files a river under *blue*. And now the horn was telling me I had failed, and the failure had a sound, and the sound was three notes rising.

"To the east!" Vess wheeled her horse. "Kavanagh — ride with me and pray your needle's better than your conscience!"

* * *

We came up over the last ridge at full gallop, and I saw it all at once, the way you see a finished map: the fleeing cart slewed sideways in a lane between two drystone walls, the oxen screaming, a woman standing in front of it with her arms spread wide and light coming off her hands like heat off a road in August. The man — her husband, I guessed, or her brother — was hauling two children over the far wall, pushing them up into the bracken. And between the hunters and the family, growing out of the lane itself, out of the packed earth and the old warding-stones set there generations ago by people whose names were lost —

A thorn.

Not a bush. A thorn. It came up out of the ground like a spear made of shadow and iron, taller than a man, black branches unfolding, and where its barbs touched the evening air they left lines of silver light, like scratches on glass. Warding magic, ancient and desperate, fed by the woman's gift and the blood in the soil and whatever old covenant the enclave's founders had signed with the land itself.

"Hold!" Vess shouted. "HOLD — do not touch it!"

One hunter did not hold. I never learned his name. He was young, and afraid, and his horse bolted sideways into the thorn's reach, and one black barb caught him across the chest the way a hand catches a sleeve.

He came off the saddle already gray. By the time he hit the ground there was frost on his lips, though it was August-warm, and the silver lines where the barb had touched him were fading like breath off a window. The thorn drank him in three heartbeats and stood there afterward, swaying slightly, sated, terrible, patient.

The woman screamed — not at the hunters, at the thorn, as if calling back a dog — and the great black spear folded down into the earth and was only soil again, and she turned and ran for the wall, for her children, for the high dark passes.

"Let them go," Vess said quietly.

Every head turned. I confess mine turned fastest.

"Captain?" Pell, breathless, compass in hand.

"Look at the ground, Pell. Look at the walls. This place was warded before our grandmothers were born, and the warding just ate Hendry like a plum stone." She dismounted slowly, walked to the spot where the thorn had been, and crouched, pressing two fingers to the soil. When she raised them, I swear the dirt clung to them like it didn't want to let go. "Thirty men cannot fight what grows out of the ground itself. We were sent to find the gifted, not to feed their garden." She stood, and looked east, at the passes, at the small figures already vanishing into the bracken — too far now, too high, gone. "Mark the location. Both of them. The farm and the thorn."

"And the prisoners' trail?" Pell asked.

"Is cold, and climbing, and I don't send men into high passes at dusk after a demonstration like that." She turned, and her gaze found me across the lane with the ease of a needle finding north. "But tomorrow, Master Kavanagh, we will talk about fords. And about how a man who cannot find a river crossing managed, nine days ago, to lead us straight to the one true enclave in forty miles of empty country."

She walked away. Seren lingered a moment longer, her grey mare stamping, her eyes moving from the scorch of frost on the dead hunter's chest to me, and back again. She didn't say anything. She just looked at me the way she'd looked at the pruned bramble — with attention, with that bird-tipped head, filing something away.

Then she followed her captain down the lane, and I sat my horse alone in the amber light, next to a patch of ordinary earth that had eaten a man, and understood at last what I had become.

I had thought my gift was a weapon aimed at me. It wasn't. It was a weapon in my hands, and weapons don't care whose blood comes out — only that it does. Somewhere over those passes a woman was carrying her children into the dark because of me. A hunter lay cooling in a lane because of the wards I had led us against. And tomorrow Vess would start doing sums, and I was running out of lies to make them balance.

Behind me, faint on the wind, I heard Seren begin to sing to settle the horses — low, wandering, gloriously off-key — and I closed my eyes and thought: *she watches everything, she misses nothing, and sooner or later she is going to look at me and see exactly what I am.*

What I did not know — what I could not have mapped with every gift and every lie I possessed — was that when she finally saw me, she would be looking at a mirror.
