## 7. Thirteenth Moon Rising

I dream in silver.

That's the first wrong thing. I don't usually dream at all — or if I do, it's the ordinary kind: horses, rain, my mother's kitchen with its crooked shelf and the smell of scorched barley. Dreams you forget before your feet hit the floor. Useful dreams for a useful life.

This one is not that.

In this dream I am standing in a valley I have never seen, and yet I know its name the way you know your own name — not learned, just *there*, worn into me like a groove in a door handle. The hills rise on every side, steep and green, and around the whole bowl of them runs a ring of thorn. Not a hedge. A wall of it, black branches thick as a man's waist, and every thorn is sheathed in silver light, the way frost sheathes a fence post at dawn. It hums. That's the second wrong thing. Walls don't hum. But this one does, low and patient, like a hymn sung by something that has been singing so long it has forgotten there was ever a first note.

And inside the valley — quiet. Terribly quiet. The kind of quiet that isn't absence but *holding*, the way a fist holds a coin. Something is kept here. Or something is kept out. The dream won't tell me which, and I have the distinct impression it's laughing at me for asking.

I walk toward the thorn wall. My feet make no sound, which offends me even in sleep; I've spent eleven years learning to move quietly, and here the valley does it for free, like a child showing off. When I get close enough to touch the silvered thorns, they lean away from me. All of them at once. Like flowers turning from shade.

Like they know me.

"Who are you?" I ask, because apparently in dreams I'm brave enough to interrogate architecture.

The wall doesn't answer. But above the valley, where there should be twelve moons — I counted, don't ask me why, some part of me was counting the way you count exits in an unfamiliar room — there is a thirteenth, rising fat and pale over the eastern ridge, a moon that has no business existing, hanging there like a coin someone slipped into a purse.

It looks down at me.

It knows me too.

I wake with my heart going like a drumline and my breath fogging the air of a tent that should be warm, because it's late summer and I'd banked the fire myself three hours ago. Frost on the canvas. Frost on my bedroll. Frost —

On my fingers.

Silver-white, delicate as spun sugar, lacing across my knuckles and up past the second joint, and when I breathe on it, it doesn't melt. It *brightens*. Just slightly. Just enough.

I do what any sensible person would do.

I scream into my own shoulder, silently, the way you learn to when you share quarters with six other hunters, and then I sit very still for a long time while the camp sounds filter in — Vess's voice giving the dawn order, someone cursing at a pack mule, the prisoner coughing in his ropes by the supply cart.

The prisoner. Kavanagh. The mapmaker whose hands found things my compass couldn't.

Frost climbs off my fingers on its own, gathering itself into a small perfect snowflake above my palm, rotating slowly, and then it collapses into nothing with a sound like a whispered word.

Right, I think. Okay.

I was taught what to do about this. I was taught it in lectures and drills and one very memorable demonstration involving a gifted woman from Harrow Fen who could make plants grow with a touch, and how Captain Vess had explained, patiently, kindly even, that what grew unnaturally could be cut out naturally, and then showed us exactly what she meant. I was twelve. I didn't sleep for a week, and afterward I was better, cleaner, more certain. That was the point of the demonstration. It worked on me the way it worked on all of us.

Magic is rot. Magic is the crack in the world where something old and hungry leaks through. Magic is why the border wars never ended, why crops fail in the western reaches, why children are born wrong. We hunt it because someone has to, and better us — trained, disciplined, merciful by comparison — than the mobs that come after we're gone.

That's the catechism. I can recite it in my sleep.

Apparently I can also frost a tent in my sleep, which raises certain theological questions I am not equipped to answer before breakfast.

* * *

"You look terrible," Tam says, handing me a tin cup of something that claims to be tea. "Bad dreams?"

"Bad mule," I say. "Kicked my tent pole all night."

"Mules don't kick tents."

"This one's ambitious." I drink the tea. It tastes like boiled regret. Around us the camp is striking itself down with the practiced violence of soldiers who've done this four hundred times — canvas folding, fires drowned, the supply cart groaning as they load it. And at the edge of it all, sitting where they've staked him, the cartographer watches everything with those quiet gray eyes that miss less than they should.

Here's what I know about Dominic Kavanagh, three weeks into this arrangement: he flinches at the wrong things.

Not the obvious things. He watched us burn the Waymeet cottage without blinking — stood there with his bound hands and looked at the flames like a man reading a letter he'd already received. But when Corporal Hesk whittles at a stick during watch, Kavanagh goes rigid, eyes tracking the knife like it might turn on him. When we crossed the stream at Alder Ford, he stared at the water for a full minute, lips moving, counting something under his breath. And yesterday, when I sang to settle the horses — I sing badly, deliberately badly, because horses don't care about pitch and men who care about pitch are men who talk too much — he turned and looked at me like I'd struck him.

Not offended. Not annoyed. *Recognizing.* The way you look at a face in a crowd that you're sure you know until it turns and it's a stranger.

I told myself it was nothing. Prisoners go strange. Ropes and fear will do it.

But now I'm standing here with frost-ghosts still tingling in my fingertips, and I'm looking at the prisoner, and I'm thinking: *he knew the water at Alder Ford was shallow enough to cross at the narrows before anyone tested it. He knew the Waymeet cottage had a cellar before we found it. He knew.*

He knows things he shouldn't know, and I do things I shouldn't be able to do, and neither of us has said a word, and the camp keeps moving around us like weather.

"Seren." Vess's voice cuts across the yard, and I snap straight. She stands by her horse with a rolled map in one hand, and she's watching me the way she watches everything — like a falconer deciding whether the bird is worth the glove. "You're on point today. We're moving north along the ridge. Our guest says there's a crossing the maps don't show."

"Does he," I say.

"He insists it's the only way through before the rains." Vess unrolls the map just enough for me to see the ridge line, and there — inked in a hand too fine for a soldier — a trail threads up through the rocks that I would swear wasn't there when we made camp. Wasn't there last night. The ink sits dark and wet-looking against the old paper, like it grew there overnight.

Something cold walks down my spine that has nothing to do with frost.

"You'll ride ahead," Vess says. "If it's a trap, you're expendable." She smiles to show it's a joke. It isn't entirely.

"Yes, Captain."

I saddle up, and as I lead my horse past the cart, Kavanagh lifts his head. Up close he looks worse than I feel — rope-burned wrists, a week of bad rations, shadows under his eyes deep enough to lose a coin in. But his gaze snags on my hands. On my bare fingers, where the frost was.

For one heartbeat neither of us moves. Then he says, very quietly, so only I can hear:

"Cold morning."

"It's August," I say.

"It is," he agrees, and looks away, and there is something in the set of his mouth that is almost — almost — pity.

I mount up and ride north, and I don't look back at him, because if I look back at him I'll have to admit what I saw in his face, which is that he wasn't surprised. He looked at my hands the way the thorn wall leaned away from me in the dream. Like something recognized something.

*You were taught what to do about this,* says the catechism in my head, in Vess's kind, patient voice. *What grows unnaturally can be cut out naturally.*

Except the catechism assumed the rot would always be out there. In cottages and cellars and hidden enclaves. In other people's children. It never once occurred to me — and I'm angry at myself for this, furious, eleven years of training and I never once considered — that I might kneel at my own fire someday and find the frost already blooming in my palms.

The ridge trail is real. Of course it's real. His gift found it, or his guilt drew it, and either way the company follows the mapmaker's ink north like iron filings after a lodestone, and I ride point, and the thirteenth moon — full, impossible, hanging in a sky that should hold twelve — sits pale above the morning sun like a secret nobody else can see.

Nobody else remarks on it. Nobody else even glances up.

Which means either I'm dreaming still, or everyone else in this company sees twelve moons and a clean sky, and I am the only one standing in a different world.

I choose to ride, and keep my hands warm in my gloves, and say nothing.

But that night, when we make camp and the horses are settled and Kavanagh is staked by the cart as always, I take first watch near him — my idea, volunteered before Vess could assign it, and if she thinks it odd she doesn't say. The fire burns low. The impossible moon climbs. And I sit three paces from the enemy, watching the frost gather on the grass around my boots in a neat silver circle, and I think:

*He flinches at knives. He counts rivers. He looked at my hands like he already knew.*

And I think, with a dread so complete it feels almost like calm:

*I need to know what he knows. Before Vess does.*
