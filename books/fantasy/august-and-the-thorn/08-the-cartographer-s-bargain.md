## 8. The Cartographer's Bargain

The false map took me three nights to draw, and I hated every line of it.

Not because forgery was hard — forgery, for a man whose ink moved when he wasn't looking, was the easiest lying in the world. No. What I hated was that it had to be *good*. A bad lie gets people killed; a good one just gets them lost, and lost is survivable. So I sat in the lean-to behind Maren's smokehouse with a guttering candle and drew the Hollow Fen with loving precision: every reed bed, every drowned alder, every treacherous channel rendered in the confident hand of Dominic Kavanagh, royal cartographer attached to the Gifted Hunt.

And then, with the same hand, I moved a ford two miles north of where it actually stood.

It felt like signing my own death warrant in beautiful calligraphy. If Vess caught me — and Vess missed nothing, Vess counted steps and weighed silences — the best I could hope for was the fire they'd promised everyone else. The worst didn't bear mapping.

But the alternative was handing the hunt a true map to the enclaves, and I'd already watched one family run into the hills while my gift lit their hiding place like a signal fire. I'd already seen what a warding thorn did to Tam Weller's throat. My talent was a weapon whether I held it or not; the only question left was who got cut.

So: forgery. Three nights of it. On the third night, near dawn, an old woman stepped out of the smokehouse shadows and said, "You hold your pen like a man apologizing."

I did not scream, which I count among my finer achievements. I did knock over the candle, which I do not.

She righted it with one hand, unhurried, and looked at me the way you look at a horse you're deciding whether to buy. She was small and brown and wrinkled as a walnut, with eyes the color of pond ice, and she wore a traveling cloak gone green with moss along the shoulders, as if she'd been standing under trees for forty years without pause.

"You're the elder," I said. "Maren's grandmother's cousin. Ilsabet."

"Ilsabet," she agreed. "You're the traitor. Shall we trade insults all night, or shall we trade something useful?"

* * *

Here is what I knew about Ilsabet before that night: nothing, except that the enclave folk spoke her name the way sailors speak of weather — with respect, dread, and the certainty that arguing was pointless. Here is what I learned in the following hour, crouched over my own forged treason while she watched:

That she had known about me since before the hunters came. That the moving ink had been felt, in its small way, all across the valley — a tug on the gifted senses, faint but unmistakable, like a bell heard through a wall. "We thought it was a child coming into power somewhere," she said. "Then we thought it was a haunting. Then the crown arrived with compasses, and we understood it was neither. It was a mapmaker whose maps made themselves."

"And you let them take me anyway."

"We let them take you because a cartographer inside the hunt is worth ten sentries outside it." She smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. "Also because we could not have stopped them, and pretending otherwise would have gotten us all burned. Hope is not the same thing as strategy, boy. Learn the difference early."

I should have resented her for it. Instead I felt something dangerously close to relief — the first time since the mill bridge proclamation that anyone had told me the truth plainly, without a warrant or a flame behind it.

"The fen map," she said, nodding at my work. "The ford is wrong."

"The ford is wrong by design."

"Good. Make the marsh deeper too. And here—" her finger came down on a stretch of blank vellum, "—put a village. Small. Twelve roofs, a shrine, a well."

"There is no village there."

"There will be by the time they read it. The hunt believes maps, Dominic. They believe them more than they believe their own eyes. Give them a country that doesn't exist and they will march through the real one without seeing it." She straightened up, joints crackling like green wood. "That is your bargain, if you want it. False maps from you, in exchange for answers from me. You've been wondering about the valley. I can see it in you. You look at the mountains the way other men look at locked doors."

My mouth went dry. "What do you know about the seal?"

"Enough to know why your ink moves." She settled onto the floor across from me, cloak pooling around her, suddenly very still. "But answers are not free, even between conspirators. First show me you can lie beautifully. Finish the fen. Then we talk."

* * *

I finished the fen. I deepened the marsh, invented the village, added a false shrine with a false saint's day marked in red. When I handed it over, Ilsabet examined it for a long time, holding it close to the candle, tilting it against the light.

"Vess will check this against the scouts' reports," she said at last.

"The scouts haven't been into the deep fen. Nobody has. That's rather the point of fens."

"Mm. And if she sends someone?"

"Then someone drowns in a channel I drew two miles from where it actually is, and I'll carry that too." I heard my own voice come out flat and bitter and didn't much care. "Everything costs, elder. You taught me that yourself."

Something flickered across her face — not approval exactly, but recognition. The look of one gambler watching another make a bet she wouldn't have dared.

"Now," she said. "Ask."

I asked. I asked everything at once, questions I'd been swallowing since the night my ink sketched a realm I'd never seen: What is the valley? Why does magic die at its borders? Why does my gift — the only gift I've ever had — work only here, only inward? Why did my mother flinch when I once asked about my grandfather's birthplace? Why does the thorn hedge on the western ridge grow in patterns no natural hedge grows in?

Ilsabet listened to all of it without blinking. Then she said one word.

"Kavanagh."

"That's my name."

"It was also the name of the man who built the lock." She folded her hands. "Sit down properly, boy. This is a long story and you're going to want to be sitting."

* * *

This is what she told me, in a voice like dry leaves, while the candle burned down to a coin of wax:

That the valley was not always quiet. That before the quiet, there had been a war — not a war of crowns and borders, but a war of *kinds*, the gifted against something older than the gifted, something that fed on them. War-magic, she called it. Not a weapon but a hunger, born — or awakened — in the age when the first gifted banded together for safety and accidentally taught their power to eat.

"That thing consumed whole bloodlines," she said. "It grew fat on them. And the strongest of the gifted, the ones who survived, understood that they could not kill it. You cannot kill a hunger. You can only starve it, or cage it."

"So they caged it."

"They sealed it. Thirteen of them — thirteen mages, each giving a piece of themselves to the working, each bound to a moon. Twelve moons to hold the seal, and a thirteenth, hidden, to renew it. They built the valley itself as the lock. The quiet you feel at the border — the way magic gutters and dies beyond the ridges — that is not absence. That is the lock *working*. Everything magical that enters the valley is drawn down into the seal and swallowed. Fed to the prisoner, bit by bit, to keep it asleep instead of starving mad."

I sat with that. The valley where magic cannot follow. The valley that had kept me safe my whole life by being a prison — and I had never once wondered who the prison was for.

"One of the thirteen," I said slowly, "was a Kavanagh."

"One of the thirteen was the *first* Kavanagh. The mapmaker. The one who charted the seal from the inside, so that those who came after would know its shape." Her pond-ice eyes fixed on mine. "Your gift isn't an accident, Dominic. It's an inheritance. The blood remembers what it built. Your ink moves because the valley recognizes its locksmith's hand. You don't map the world, boy. You map the *lock*."

The candle guttered. Somewhere outside, a nightjar called, and the sound seemed to come from very far away, as if the whole valley were holding its breath around us.

"Why tell me this?" I managed. "Why now?"

"Because the crown is hunting the gifted, and every gifted soul they burn outside the valley weakens nothing — but every gifted soul they drive *into* the valley feeds the seal, and keeps it strong. The hunt is doing our work for us, horribly, by accident. But seals don't last forever. And when yours begins to fail—"

"How would I know? What would it look like?"

Ilsabet smiled again, and this time there was no comfort in it at all.

"It would look like ink that moves on its own," she said. "Like a map sketching realms the mapmaker's never seen. Like a lock beginning to dream of what it holds."

* * *

I walked back to the hunters' camp an hour before dawn, carrying a forged map against my chest and a cosmology behind my ribs, and I have never in my life felt so thoroughly like two men walking in step.

To Captain Vess, I would present the fen chart at first light: humble, precise, indispensable. To Ilsabet and the enclaves, I was now the hunt's cartographer in name and theirs in fact. Double agency, they'd have called it in some other story — though I suspect whoever coined the term never had to eat breakfast with the people he was betraying while betraying the other people too.

And underneath both loyalties, new and cold and enormous: the valley was a lock, and I was born of the locksmith, and the lock was dreaming.

Tentative hope, Ilsabet had given me — a way to protect my own kind from inside the machine that hunted them. Intoxicating, she'd warned me, and she was right; there was a giddy vertigo in deception done well, in watching Vess accept the fen map with a curt nod and order the column south, away from twelve real roofs and toward a village that existed only in my ink.

But that evening, as the camp struck tents and Seren sang her horses steady — off-key, unbothered, lovely in the way that only the entirely unaware can be — I noticed frost blooming on the ground where she'd walked. Just a patch. Just for a moment. Gone before the second verse.

And I remembered what Ilsabet had said about locks that dream, and I thought: *the seal isn't the only thing in this camp that's waking up.*

I just didn't yet know which of us it would demand first.
