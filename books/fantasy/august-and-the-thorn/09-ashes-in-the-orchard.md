## 9. Ashes in the Orchard

The orchard was burning when we arrived, which was the crown's doing, and the children were screaming, which was not.

Let me tell you how Captain Vess described the operation, because I have a gift for remembering the exact shape of a lie. "A supply cache," she said, standing at the head of the column with the dawn behind her and her face lit like a coin. "Rebel provisioning point. Possibly harboring a gifted individual." Singular. Individual. As if magic came in ones, like teeth you could pull and be done with.

I'd tracked the enclave for three days. That was my job — best tracker in the company, the one they pointed at sign like a divining rod. I found the crushed apple cores, the small footprints, the laundry line strung between two trees with shirts the size of my forearm drying in the wind. I said nothing about the laundry line. I told myself the captain would see it too, and that she would do the math, and that the math would stop her.

The math did not stop her.

"The orchard is full of children," I said. I remember my voice sounding very reasonable, the way it does when you're already losing. "There's a laundry line. Small shirts."

"Gifted children grow into gifted adults," Vess said, and gave the order to fire the western rows, because the wind was coming from the east and she wanted the smoke to drive them out the way she wanted them to come. She was good at this. That was the worst part. She was not a butcher fumbling with the knife; she was a surgeon, and she believed to the marrow that she was cutting out a disease.

I have burned a lot of things in my service to the crown. Barns. A chapel that sang back. A man's letters, once, while he watched, because Vess said it broke them faster than breaking their fingers. I had never burned a place where children slept, and I want to be honest about what that means: it means I had been lucky, not good.

The smoke came off the apple trees in long gray ropes. The apples themselves were popping in the heat — I could hear them, little wet explosions, sweet-smelling, the whole grove smelling like cider and char. And out of the smoke came the children, because that is what smoke is for, and Vess's hunters were waiting with iron manacles and rope and that terrible patient look they get, the look of men doing a job.

I want to say I thought it through. I didn't. What I did was this: I saw a girl, maybe eight, go down at the edge of the eastern rows with a hunter's hand in her hair, and I saw a smaller one — a boy, all elbows — turn around and run *back into the burning trees* after her, which is the stupidest bravest thing I have ever witnessed, and my feet decided things without consulting me.

"Kestrel, with me," I said, naming the hunter nearest, because an order sounds lawful if you give it in a confident voice, and then I did not wait for Kestrel, I just went, into the smoke, into the heat that peeled the breath off my lips.

The trees were roaring. I found the boy first, crouched behind a trunk with his arms over his head, and I hauled him up and he fought me, of course, because I was a stranger and a soldier and the whole world was on fire. Then I found the girl — the hunter who'd grabbed her had moved on, gods know why, maybe the smoke, maybe conscience, maybe just arithmetic — and I put her over my shoulder and the boy's wrist in my fist and I walked out of that orchard through the back rows where the fire hadn't reached, and I did not stop walking until the trees were behind me and the hill was in front of me and my lungs were full of apple-scented death.

Kestrel caught up at the top of the hill. He looked at the two children. He looked at me. He was a decent man, Kestrel, which is a dangerous thing to be in Vess's company, and I watched him do the same arithmetic I'd done at the laundry line.

"They ran from me," I said. "I lost them in the smoke."

He looked at the children again. The girl had her face pressed into my shoulder. The boy was staring at Kestrel with the flat, ancient hatred children reserve for the people who burned their home.

"Smoke's thick," Kestrel said, and turned around, and walked back down the hill, and that was that. One decent man's silence, purchased for nothing. I'd remember that too, later. I'd remember who owed whom.

* * *

Vess knew. Of course Vess knew. She didn't say anything at the time — the children were gone, the enclave scattered, the day counted as a success in her ledger — but that evening she came to my fire while I was cleaning my boots, and she sat down across from me without asking, which she never did.

"Two children unaccounted for," she said.

"Smoke was thick."

"You came out of it with soot on your hands and nothing in your arms."

"Empty arms, empty hands. You can check."

"I intend to." She said it mildly, the way you'd discuss the weather. She had gray eyes, Vess, the color of a blade left out in the rain, and she looked at me with them for a long, long moment, and then she reached over and turned my left hand palm-up, and she drew something on it with her thumbnail — a short stroke, a mark, quick and cold. It didn't hurt. It wasn't meant to hurt. It was meant to be *there*, and it was: a thin white line across my palm that hadn't existed a moment before, some hunter's trick, some oath-ink, I never learned what.

"You're my best tracker," she said. "That's the only reason you're sitting here. If you were my second-best, I'd have you in irons right now, and we both know it. So I'm going to watch you, Seren. The way you watch sign. And I'm very good at watching."

She stood, brushed off her coat, and left. I sat there looking at the mark on my hand. It didn't fade for three days.

Here is the thing I couldn't explain to her, and can't entirely explain to you: I didn't save those children out of conscience. Or not *only*. When I picked that girl up, something in my chest had turned over — a recognition, like hearing your own name called in a crowd. I'd felt it before, small times. The chapel that sang back: I'd hummed along before I could stop myself. The frost on my window that morning last month that had spelled, faintly, in fern-patterns, something almost like a word. I kept not thinking about it. I was very skilled at not thinking about it. I had been trained my whole life to hunt a thing, and the thing had been sitting inside me the whole time like a swallowed coal, and every day I didn't look at it was a day I chose the hunt over the truth.

The girl, over my shoulder, had whispered into my ear — I'll never forget it — "You're one of us. I can tell. You're warm."

I have never been so frightened by a compliment in my life.

* * *

The company made camp that night in a fold of hills north of the orchard, far enough that the glow had faded to a bruise on the horizon. I couldn't sleep. I took the first watch, then traded for a second watch I didn't need, and around midnight I found the prisoner's fire.

Dominic Kavanagh sat alone at the edge of the picket line, where they kept him — close enough to consult, far enough to run for, not that he'd ever run, he had the runner's build and none of the runner's appetite for it. They kept him in rope by day and trust by night, which told you something about how Vess valued him: as a tool, not a threat. His maps were spread out on a flat rock in front of him and he was copying one by firelight, the ink moving under his hand in that way of his that I had decided, months ago, not to find beautiful.

I sat down across the fire without asking. Fair's fair; Vess had done it to me.

He didn't look up. "If you're here to check my work, the river bends east, not west. The crown's maps are wrong. They've been wrong for forty years."

"I'm not here about the river."

"Nobody's ever here about the river." He set down the pen. Up close, in firelight, he looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep — tired like a house that had been lived in too long by someone too careful. "You're the one who went into the orchard."

Word traveled. Of course it did. "The one who came out empty-handed, you mean."

"That's what I heard." He looked at me then, and there was something in his face I couldn't read — not judgment. I'd have known judgment; I'd been wearing it on other people all week. It was closer to *recognition*, and I didn't like that, because recognition was exactly the thing I was hiding from.

"Two children got out," I said. "That's all you need to know."

"That's not all I know." He said it quietly. The fire popped. Somewhere behind us a horse shifted and sighed. "I know the enclave had a cellar under the eastern wall with a cold-latch on it — a ward that keeps milk and meat. I know the children were moved there two days ago because I told the elder the company was coming, and I know the cellar has a second exit into the stream cut, because I drew it, three years ago, before I understood what my maps were for."

I stared at him. The firelight moved on his face. "You're telling me this why?"

"Because you went into a burning orchard for children you'd been ordered to chain." He picked up the pen again, turned it over in his fingers. "Because you've been watching me for a month like you're waiting for me to say something, and I've been waiting to see if you were the one worth saying it to. And because your left hand is closed in your lap like it's holding something you don't want anyone to see."

I looked down. My fist was around the mark Vess had left, white line and all, as if I could hide it by holding it.

"It's nothing," I said.

"It's a warden's mark," he said. "Old hunter's craft. It shows the captain where you are, always. It's also — " and here he hesitated, and I watched him decide, in real time, to step off a cliff — "it's also iron, and iron itches, doesn't it, if you've got any gift in you at all. Like a nettle under the skin. Like a word you keep almost remembering."

The itch. Gods, the itch — I'd been telling myself it was rope-burn, insect bites, imagination. I uncurled my hand. The white line sat there, thin as a hair, and under my skin it itched like mad, and Dominic Kavanagh looked at it the way a man looks at a wound he predicted.

"You should sleep," I said, which was cowardice with a blanket on it.

"Probably." He didn't move. Neither did I.

* * *

We talked until the fire burned down to a red eye. Not about anything. That was the miracle of it — we talked about nothing with the whole truth pressing up under the floorboards, and both of us pretending not to hear it knock.

He told me about the valley where he grew up, how the maps there refused to hold still, how he'd spent his childhood thinking he was broken because his surveys never matched anyone else's. I told him about my mother, who sang to the horses, and how the horses would fall asleep mid-stride, and how I'd thought everyone's mother did that. He laughed at that — a real laugh, startled out of him, the first I'd heard — and the laugh did something to my chest that I filed under *later*, along with the frost and the coal and the girl's whisper, in the growing archive of things I would examine never.

"You know what's strange," he said, poking the fire. "Everyone in this company talks to me like I'm a compass. *Where's the next one, Kavanagh? Which way, Kavanagh?* You're the first person who's talked to me like I'm a person since they roped me."

"Maybe I'm softening you up. Interrogation technique."

"Is it working?"

"Absolutely. I know your mother sang to horses. You're done for."

He smiled down at the fire, and then the smile went somewhere else, quieter. "The orchard," he said. "The children. Thank you. I know what it cost."

"It cost nothing. Smoke was thick."

"Seren." My name in his mouth, the first time he'd used it. It landed somewhere it had no business landing. "The smoke wasn't thick."

I looked at him across the coals and he looked back, and for a moment — one long breath of a moment — we were both perfectly, dangerously honest without saying a single true thing. Two liars by a fire, each holding a coal in a closed fist, each watching the other's fist and saying nothing, because saying it would burn us both and the night was cold and the fire was warm and the company slept thirty feet away.

"I should take second watch," I said, and didn't move.

"Third watch too, if you want," he said. "I'll keep you company. I'm told I'm good company, for a compass."

* * *

I dreamed that night, in the gray hour before dawn, of a valley sealed in silver — a valley like a bowl turned upside down over a light, and under the light something turning over in its sleep, vast and patient, and a thorn the size of a church door, and a voice that was not a voice saying *soon, soon, soon*. I woke with frost on my fingers.

Not on the ground. Not on the grass. On my fingers — a rime of white, delicate as sugar, melting as I watched, in the middle of a summer night, with the fire still glowing ten feet away.

Across the dead coals, Dominic was awake. He'd seen. I knew he'd seen, because he didn't pretend to be asleep, and because his face, in the last of the embers, was the face of a man watching a door he'd been knocking on finally open from the other side.

"Seren," he said softly.

"Don't," I said.

He didn't. He just sat there with me while the frost melted off my hands, and neither of us said one true thing, and it was the truest hour I'd ever spent with anyone.

In the morning, Vess called the company to order and unrolled a new set of orders with a wax seal the size of my palm. "We move south," she said. "The prisoner has found us an enclave in the hill country. A large one." Her gray eyes found Dominic, then me, then rested on me a half-second too long. "And Seren — you'll walk beside the prisoner today. I want my best tracker close to my best compass."

Dominic's maps, I noticed, were folded under her arm. And the top one — I saw it only for a moment, as she turned — was drawn in ink that was still moving.

Not the way his ink usually moved, slow and searching, like a hand feeling in the dark.

Moving fast. Moving *away*. As if it were running from something.

As if it were running from her.
