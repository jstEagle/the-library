## 10. The False Map

There is an art to lying with a map, and I had spent three weeks learning it the way a man learns to swallow broken glass — carefully, and hating every moment.

The trick, for anyone curious, is not in the false things. False things are easy. Draw a river where no river runs, and any hunter worth her boots will find the absence within a day and start asking why the king's cartographer can't tell water from wishful thinking. No — the art is in the true things. You bury your lie inside a hundred accurate details. You get the elevation right. You get the stream crossings right, the old burned homesteads, the wolf dens, the places where the road floods in spring. You make the map so honest everywhere else that the one dishonest inch goes down like a dose of poison in honeyed wine.

So when Captain Vess unrolled my latest chart across the campaign table and ran her finger along the ridge line above Hollowmere, everything she touched was true except the thing that mattered: the valley fork that should have bent east toward the Thornhollow enclave instead bent west, into a maze of ravines I had spent two days inventing and one night praying no woodcutter would contradict.

"The gifted keep to high ground near water," she said. It wasn't a question. She'd said it eleven times now, and each time she said it a little more slowly, the way a woman counts coins she suspects are being shorted.

"Water, timber, and escape routes," I agreed. "Enclaves cluster where all three meet. Thornhollow's got a spring-fed millpond on its north side — see there." I tapped a spot I had drawn with loving precision and knew to be a bramble-choked sheep pasture. "That's their gate. If we come down from the west ridge at dawn, we'll have them boxed before they hear us."

Vess looked at me. She has a way of looking at a man like she's reading the fine print under his skin, and I have never once enjoyed it. Her face is all planes and patience, the kind of patience that has outlasted better liars than me.

"You've been very useful, Kavanagh," she said.

"I do my best."

"Mm." She rolled the map up with a snap. "West ridge. Dawn. Seren — you'll take point with the prisoner."

Seren, who had been sharpening her knife by the fire with the concentration of someone pretending not to listen, glanced up. Our eyes met for exactly as long as two people who share a secret nothing can afford. Then she went back to her blade.

"Yes, Captain," she said.

* * *

We broke camp before light, and I walked at the front of the column with a rope around my wrists and Seren beside me, and if you'd told me six months ago that the two facts of my life would be a leash and this woman, I'd have asked which one was worse. The answer changes daily.

The morning was doing that gray autumn thing where the fog sits in the hollows like something poured. Seren hummed as she walked — off-key, always off-key, some marching song about a miller's daughter that she murdered note by note with total serenity.

"You're flat," I said.

"I'm aware."

"It's almost impressive. Like watching someone lose a fight on purpose."

"My mother said the same thing." She adjusted the rope between us, giving me slack without being asked. "She said if I couldn't carry a tune, I should at least carry it confidently."

"And did you?"

"Confidently? Always. In tune?" She smiled at the fog. "The gods gave me other talents."

I wanted to ask what those were. I wanted to ask a great many things, because over these weeks something had grown between us that neither of us named — a habit of walking close, of finishing each other's sentences about terrain, of sharing food without asking. And underneath it, in both directions, I suspected, lay the same buried cargo: what she was hiding, what I was hiding, and how much either of us could afford to know.

Instead I said, "The ravines ahead are bad footing. Loose shale. If the captain wants speed, she'll want to reconsider the route."

It was the closest I could come to warning her. Every step west took us deeper into country I had invented, and every invented mile was a mile the real enclave didn't have to fear. That was the arithmetic I lived by now. I fed Vess phantom valleys and sleeping villages got to keep their sleep. It felt, on good days, like smuggling. On bad days — and today was shaping up to be a bad day — it felt like juggling knives over an open flame while the knife-juggler's guild watched from the front row.

Because Vess had stopped believing me. I could see it accumulating in her, day by day, like silt behind a dam. Three weeks of enclaves that weren't where I said. Three weeks of cold camps and empty hollows and hunters grumbling that the cartographer's gift had gone soft. She hadn't accused me. Accusation requires proof, and proof requires a test, and I had begun to feel, the way animals feel weather, that the test was coming.

The fog thickened as we climbed. Somewhere behind us, a horse complained about the shale.

"Dominic." Seren's voice had dropped, casual as anything, pitched only for me. "The captain had a letter last night. From the provost marshal."

My stomach performed a small, controlled collapse. "Letters travel slowly out here."

"This one traveled fast." She kept humming. "Sealed with black wax. I carried it to her tent myself. She read it twice and then she looked at you across the fire for a long time."

"What kind of look?"

"The kind I give a trail I think has been laid false."

I said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't be a confession or a lie, and I was so tired of rationing myself between the two.

Ahead, Vess raised a fist, and the column halted.

* * *

The place she chose to stop was a clearing I knew intimately, because I had invented it. On my map, this clearing held a shrine — one of the roadside shrines the gifted sometimes left, marked with a thorn-sigil, evidence of passage. I had drawn it to lend the route credibility. A shrine implies travelers; travelers imply a destination; a destination implies an enclave worth burning.

There was no shrine. There was grass, and fog, and a crow watching us from a dead pine with what I swear was editorial interest.

Vess dismounted and stood in the middle of the clearing, waiting until every hunter had assembled and every eye was on her. Then she turned to me.

"Kavanagh. Show me the shrine."

The world went very quiet, the way it does before a wave breaks.

"Captain?"

"Your map says there's a thorn-shrine here." She drew my chart from her coat and held it up, and there it was, my own careful ink, damning me in my own hand. "Three weeks, Kavanagh. Three weeks of empty hollows. Of enclaves moved overnight. Of trails gone cold the day after you mark them." She began to walk toward me, unhurried. "Either your gift is failing, or your loyalty is. So here is what happens now. You will show me the shrine. If it's there, we ride on, and I'll allow that the gifted are simply cleverer than I'd hoped. If it isn't—"

She let it hang. Behind her, a dozen hunters shifted, and hands found weapons with the easy boredom of men who had done this before.

"—then I'll know exactly what you are, and we'll conduct the rest of this hunt with you in irons, and when we finally do find an enclave, you'll watch what we do to it from the back of a wagon."

The fog pressed in. The crow said something rude.

And then — the gods are cruel, or they're comedians, and I've stopped being able to tell the difference — Seren spoke.

"Captain." She had dismounted too, and was crouched at the clearing's edge, parting the grass with two fingers. "There's blood here. Old. Days, maybe a week." She looked up, and her face was perfectly composed, and I understood in one lurching heartbeat that she was lying for me — lying with the same skill I used on maps, truth wrapped around falsehood. "And look — the grass is worn in a path. Something stood here. Was pulled down, maybe. They must have moved the shrine when they caught wind of us."

Vess crossed to her. Knelt. Studied the ground for a long, long moment.

"There's no path," she said quietly.

"There's wear," Seren said. "Faint. Rain's taken most of it."

Another silence, longer than the first. I stood with my rope-ends in my fists and my heart going like a millwheel in flood, and I understood that whatever happened in the next ten seconds would decide whether I lived to lie another day.

Then Vess stood. Brushed off her knees.

"Burn the clearing," she said to her hunters. "If they're watching, let them see we don't forget." And then, to me, without turning around: "You're bleeding luck, Kavanagh. Luck runs out. Remember that."

* * *

We rode on westward through the invented ravines, and I thought we had passed the test, and I was a fool, because Vess had not been testing the shrine at all.

Near midday we topped a ridge, and below us — real, actual, maddeningly real — lay a farmstead in a fold of hills that existed nowhere on any chart I'd made. Smoke rose from its chimney. Goats in the yard. A woman hanging washing on a line, white sheets snapping in the wind like surrender flags.

"That's not on your map," Vess said, beside me. Not angry. Worse. Pleasant.

"No," I admitted. My mind raced through the ravines of my own fabrication looking for a handhold. "Small holding. Off the survey routes. Half these hill farms aren't charted — the crown doesn't pay me to map goat pens."

"No," Vess agreed. "But the crown pays you to find the gifted. And that woman—" she pointed, and I saw it then, saw what her sharp eyes had seen and mine had missed — "has been hanging those sheets with her left hand only. Look at the right sleeve. Pinned empty at the elbow. And yet the wash-line is strung higher than any one-armed woman could reach, and the sheets are dry already though the fog only lifted an hour ago." She smiled without warmth. "Magic leaves habits, Kavanagh. Even small ones. Even kind ones. Shall we go and ask her whether she's charted?"

Everything in me screamed. This was not the plan. This was a stranger, a hill farmer, a woman drying laundry in the ordinary miracle of an ordinary afternoon, and Vess had found her not through my gift but through her own — the terrible, patient genius of a woman who had decided to stop trusting mine and simply hunt.

"If we go down there," I said, and heard my own voice come out thin, "and she's nothing — just a widow with a goat problem — then this hunt loses another day chasing smoke, and the provost marshal's next letter will be asking about you, Captain, not me."

For a moment I thought it might work. Vess weighed it; I watched her weigh it, the scales moving behind her eyes.

Then Seren said, quietly, "There's a warding-thorn hedge along her south fence, Captain. I can see the bloom from here. Blue-black. That's not hedgerow."

And I want to be honest, because this account is the only confession I'll ever get to make: in that moment I hated her. One clean hot second of hatred, before understanding crashed in behind it — because Seren wasn't betraying the woman. Seren was saving me. A confirmed find meant a credible cartographer; a credible cartographer meant freedom to keep feeding Vess fiction for months more. Seren had done the arithmetic faster and colder than I had, and had paid for it with a stranger's name written in thorn-bloom.

Vess smiled for real this time.

"Hunters," she said, "form line. Kavanagh — you'll walk in front. Let's see your gift earn its bread."

* * *

The woman's name was Maren Ostrey, though we learned that only afterward, from the deed nailed inside her door. When we came down the ridge she did not run. I'll grant her that forever: she stood in her yard with her empty sleeve pinned and her chin up, between us and her door, and she said, "Can I help you fine folk?" as if we were neighbors come to borrow salt.

Vess did the talking. Vess always does the talking. She asked about the wash-line, and the woman said her late husband had strung it high. She asked about the hedge, and the woman said thorns grow wild in the hills. She asked to see inside the house, and the woman stepped aside — stepped aside! — with the terrible dignity of someone who has already counted her options and found them all ending the same way.

Inside, they found what they found. A cot. A loom. Dried herbs. And beneath a floorboard by the hearth, because Vess's hunters tore the cottage apart board by board with methodical joy, a bundle of letters tied in blue string, written in a hand that shimmered faintly when you tilted it to the light — correspondence with an enclave, warm and domestic and utterly damning. *The children are well. Tell your sister the fever broke. We will expect you at the turn of the moon.*

Maren Ostrey did not cry. She looked at the letters for a long time, and then she looked at me — at me, standing in her doorway in chains I wasn't even wearing, wearing instead the free man's guilt — and she said, "You're the map-man. I've heard of you. The one who finds us."

And I said, "I'm sorry," which was the truest and most useless sentence of my life.

They bound her and put her in the wagon, and Vess posted two hunters to guard the farmstead until a detail could return to burn it, standard practice, and the white sheets stayed on the line behind us, snapping and snapping in the wind, all the way out of sight.

* * *

That night the camp was almost festive. A capture at last — real proof, a real prize, letters that would lead interrogators to an entire network. The hunters drank. Someone sang, worse than Seren, which I hadn't believed possible. Vess sent a rider south with a dispatch sealed in triumphant black wax.

I sat at the fire's edge with a bowl of barley I couldn't eat, doing the arithmetic that was becoming my whole life. One woman, traded for my freedom to protect hundreds. One farmstead, for every enclave I could steer the hunt away from. It was good arithmetic. It was the kind of arithmetic that lets a man sleep, if he's the kind of man who can sleep after teaching himself that people can be exchanged.

I couldn't sleep.

Seren found me at the picket lines, where I was pretending to check the horses' fetlocks in the dark. She didn't say anything at first. Just stood beside me in the cold, shoulder almost touching mine.

"She knew the risk," Seren said finally. "Anyone who keeps warding-thorns knows exactly what the world is now. She chose her letters. She chose her people. That's not the same as what—" She stopped herself.

"As what?" I said. "Say it."

"As what Vess does. Or as what you're doing, carrying her maps, finding her roads." Seren turned to look at me, firelight catching half her face. "Except you're not, are you. Your maps have been wrong for three weeks. Deliberately wrong. And today I watched you nearly break trying to save a woman you'd never met, and I watched you fail, and I watched you hate yourself for it." Her voice dropped. "So which is it, Dominic? Are you the hunt's map-man, or are you ours?"

The question hung between us like frost forming.

"I'm yours," I said, before wisdom could stop me. "God help me, I'm yours. Both meanings."

Something moved through her face then — relief and fear tangled together, the expression of a woman hearing a confession she'd been starving for and dreading in equal measure. She looked away first, toward the dark hills where Maren Ostrey's farm smoldered in memory.

"Then be careful," she whispered. "Because Vess tested you today, and you passed. But she passed you the way a fisherman passes a net through water — to see what it catches. And tomorrow, Dominic, tomorrow she'll pull tighter."

Across the camp, Vess stood alone at the far fire, reading the provost marshal's reply by its light. I couldn't see her face. But I saw her fold the letter away, and I saw her look — once, briefly, directly — at the two of us standing together in the dark.

And I understood, with the cold certainty of a man watching his own ink run, that the next trap would not be set for a stranger.

It would be set for us.
