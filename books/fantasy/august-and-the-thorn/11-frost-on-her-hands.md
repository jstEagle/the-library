## 11. Frost on Her Hands

I have been shot at, gored by a boar, and once bitten by a tax collector's dog, and I can tell you with authority that none of it hurt as much as the moment I looked down at my own hands and saw winter blooming there.

We had been chasing a skirmisher through the barley fields outside Marrowfen for the better part of an hour. He was one of the gifted — a boy, really, maybe seventeen, with the kind of talent that made doors open when he thought about them and locks forget they were ever locked. Vess wanted him alive. Vess always wanted them alive now; dead gifted tell you nothing about where the others are hiding.

So we ran him down through the standing grain, and when he turned at bay in a hollow with his hands up and his eyes wild, and one of our crossbowmen loosed anyway — because discipline is a rumor that travels slowly — I did what any tracker would do.

I threw myself between the bolt and the boy.

I didn't think about it. That's the part I keep returning to, turning over like a river stone. I didn't think. My body moved before my mind could remind me whose side I was on, and then the bolt was three feet from my chest and stopping — actually *stopping*, mid-air, hanging in the hot August light like a fly pinned to the day itself — and the air around me went cold enough to see my own breath.

Frost crawled out across the barley. Every stalk within ten feet of me silvered over, bowed, and shattered into glittering ruin. The boy stared at me. The crossbowman stared at me. And somewhere behind me, I heard Captain Vess stop walking.

The bolt dropped. The cold let go of the world all at once, like a hand opening, and the heat rushed back in so fast my ears popped.

"Well," I said, because I have never in my life known when to shut up. "That's new."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about discovering you are the thing you've spent your whole adult life hunting: it doesn't feel like revelation. It feels like coming home to find your furniture rearranged. Everything is where you left it and nothing is where you know it should be, and you stand in the doorway of your own life wondering who has been living here.

I'd had the dreams, of course. A valley sealed in silver, a moon that shouldn't exist counting turns above it, frost on my fingers when I woke. I'd told myself they were just dreams. I'd told myself the way the horses calmed under my hands was good horseman's sense. I'd told myself a great many things, the way you talk loudly past a door you don't want to open.

The frost on the barley had opened it for me.

Vess crossed the field toward me with her boots crunching on ruined grain, and her face was doing something I'd never seen it do. Captain Maren Vess has exactly two expressions: the one she uses for reports and the one she uses for funerals. This was neither. This was a woman recalculating.

"Seren." She said my name the way you'd test ice before stepping on it. "What was that."

"Draft," I said. "Cold air off the fen. It happens."

"In August."

"Freak weather. You've read the almanacs."

"I wrote half the almanacs," Vess said, "and none of them say *frost in a barley field around a huntress who just stopped a crossbow bolt with her bare—*"

"It missed," I said. "It fell short. You all saw it fall short."

Three hunters were watching us. The boy in the hollow was watching us. And Kavanagh — the prisoner-cartographer, the man I'd been watching for weeks with a suspicion I hadn't dared name — Kavanagh was watching me with an expression I couldn't read at all, which frightened me more than Vess did.

Because here's the thing I hadn't told anyone, not even myself, not properly: I had known. Some part of me had known since the dreams started, since the night I woke with frost melting on the windowsill of my tent and told myself the latch had been open. And some other part of me had been waiting, these last weeks, for Dominic Kavanagh to look at me the way he was looking at me now — not with suspicion, but with recognition. Like a man seeing his own handwriting on someone else's letter.

"Bind her," Vess said quietly. "Seren Aldous, you're detained under the Gifted Edict pending examination. Rennick, take her sword. Tam, fetch the irons."

Nobody moved. Not because they loved me — though Tam owed me his life twice over and Rennick had eaten my cooking often enough to owe me more than that. They didn't move because the order was wrong and everyone knew it was wrong, and soldiers hesitate at wrong orders the way horses hesitate at bad bridges.

Then Dominic stepped forward.

* * *

I want to be fair to the lie he told, because it was a beautiful piece of work, and I have since come to appreciate beautiful work even when it's done to save my neck. He said:

"Captain. If I may." He had that cartographer's voice, the reasonable one, the one that made you feel stupid for disagreeing. "You've seen this before. In the field, in the enclaves. Gifted talent doesn't erupt like that untrained — it builds. Weeks of small signs. Dreams, chills, animals going quiet around the bearer." He paused, letting Vess hear her own memory of every report he'd ever annotated for her. "If Huntress Aldous had been carrying a gift, I'd have mapped it weeks ago. My compass would have found her the way it finds everything else. It hasn't. What you saw was a warding echo — residual magic from the boy. They sometimes discharge into whoever's nearest when they're cornered. It burns out. Check her hands in an hour. There'll be nothing."

He said it all smoothly, reasonably, and the whole time his eyes never left mine, and what lived in his eyes was not calm at all. It was a shout. *Play along. Please. Play along.*

And God help me, I did. I held out my hands like a woman showing she carries nothing, and I let Rennick examine them, and I kept my face bored, and inside I was screaming in a language I didn't know I spoke.

Vess wasn't convinced. I could see she wasn't convinced. But conviction isn't the same as proof, and proof was what Vess ran on — proof, warrants, procedure, the whole iron scaffolding she'd built her soul out of. She looked at my empty hands. She looked at Kavanagh's compass, which sat in its case on his belt and pointed, as far as anyone could see, at nothing in particular.

"An hour," she said finally. "Then the irons regardless, until we reach a proper examiner. Rennick, watch her. And Kavanagh—" her voice dropped into something colder, "—you've become very helpful lately. I haven't decided whether I trust that."

"Nobody sensible trusts a mapmaker," Dominic said. "We sell the same territory twice."

It got a grim little laugh out of the men. It did not get one out of Vess. She turned and gave the order to move out, and the boy in the hollow — the boy I'd caught a bolt for — looked at me once, and mouthed something I didn't catch, and slipped away into the standing grain while every hunter's back was conveniently turned.

Conveniently. Yes. I noticed that too, later.

* * *

They gave me an hour and eleven minutes. I know because I spent all of it staring at my own fingers, willing them to stay warm, and counting.

The camp was striking tents for the march to Marrowfen village. Rennick stood six feet from me holding my confiscated sword and looking deeply uncomfortable about the entire arrangement. "Seren," he said, low. "Whatever it was. Whatever it is. Say the word and I'll swear I saw nothing."

"You saw frost in August, Rennick."

"I see a lot of things. Bad eyes run in my family." He shifted. "Vess will burn you. You know that. Doesn't matter if it 'burns out.' Once she writes your name in the ledger, the ledger doesn't have an eraser."

"I know."

"So don't be here in an hour."

I looked at him. Six years in the company together. He'd carried me a mile on his back after the boar. "You'll hang for helping me."

"I'll hang for *nothing*," he said, "if you're quick about it."

That was the moment I understood what liberation felt like, and I want to describe it honestly: it felt like terror wearing a festival mask. My whole life — the company, the road, the fires I knew, the songs I knew, the small sure architecture of being Vess's best tracker — all of it was ending, and underneath the grief of that was a horrible, singing relief, like a bone setting. Because I had known. All those weeks of watching Kavanagh flinch at things he shouldn't know, all those nights of frost-dreams and waking cold, I had *known* what I was, and I had stayed anyway, marching with the hunt, sleeping beside people whose orders ended in ash. Staying had been its own kind of lie, and lies rot you from the inside.

Rennick walked me toward the picket line to "check the horses," which was the oldest excuse in the company, and the horses — bless their enormous stupid hearts — were already saddled, already bridled, already standing in a neat row facing away from camp, which no horse does naturally.

"He talked to them," Rennick muttered. "The mapmaker. Horses like him. Don't ask me how."

I mounted. I took one look back at the camp — at Tam striking the big tent, at the cook-fire smoke going up straight and white into the August sky, at Vess standing at the field desk writing, already writing, my name going into the ledger even now — and I turned my horse south, and I did not look back again, because looking back is how people get caught.

Kavanagh was waiting at the tree line. Of course he was. He had two horses and the flat, resigned expression of a man who has just made a decision he intends to regret thoroughly.

"You're coming with me?" I said. "You're a prisoner. You're their prisoner. This is the worst escape plan I've ever heard, and I once watched a man try to escape through a chimney."

"Their prisoner was a fiction I maintained for their benefit," he said. "Also, if I stay, Vess asks me questions about you, and I'm apparently a worse liar than I thought, because she almost didn't buy it." He handed me the second horse's reins. "Also also, you caught a crossbow bolt out of the air with winter. I have approximately four hundred questions and nowhere safe to ask them."

"And if I'd rather ride alone?"

"Then I'll follow you at a respectful distance and be extremely annoying about it."

Despite everything — despite the frost still ghosting in my fingertips, despite the ledger, despite leaving Rennick standing in a paddock holding my sword and my whole former life — I laughed. It came out cracked and startled, like a bird flushed from cover, but it was a laugh.

"That," Dominic said, swinging up into his saddle, "is the first time I've heard you do that properly. You usually sing instead. Off-key."

"You've been listening to me sing?"

"Everyone listens to you sing. It's impossible not to. It's like listening to a wagon with a loose axle go over a bridge."

"Charming. Get moving, mapmaker."

* * *

We rode hard until the light went long and copper, and then harder after, putting hills and streams and a burned-out mill between us and the sound of pursuit horns. We didn't talk much. There was too much to say and none of it fit in a saddle, so we let the horses do the conversing, and the horses agreed unanimously on south.

We made camp in a hollow of old beeches where a shepherd's hut had fallen in on itself generations ago. Dominic built the fire small and shielded, the way you build a fire when you've learned what firelight costs, and I sat against the hut's broken wall with my hands held out to the warmth, and watched the frost come.

It came gently, this time. Little ferns of white feathering up from my knuckles, curling, melting in the firelight, rising again. Like breathing. Like something in me that had been holding its breath for twenty-six years finally exhaling.

"It doesn't hurt," I said. "That's the strange part. You'd think it would hurt."

"Does it feel like anything?"

"Cold water. But friendly about it." I flexed my fingers and the frost bloomed and died, bloomed and died. "My mother used to say I was a winter child. Born in a snowstorm, wouldn't stop crying until they put me near the window. I thought it was just a story mothers tell." I looked at him. "How long have you known? About me. Truly."

Dominic fed a stick to the fire. He took his time about it, which was answer enough, but he gave the rest too. "Since the thorn fields. Week three. Your horse spooked at a warding line and you grabbed its bridle and the warding went dark where you touched it. Wards don't go dark. They burn or they hold. Yours just... went quiet. Like it recognized you."

"And you said nothing."

"What was I going to say? *Good news, huntress, you're the thing we're hunting.*" He glanced at me sideways. "Besides. You'd have reported yourself. You were still reporting everything then. You were the most honest person in that company and it was making me sick watching you do it."

"And now?"

"Now you're a fugitive sitting next to a liar, so we match."

I should have been afraid. Sitting in the dark with a man I'd helped capture, hunted by my own captain, carrying a death sentence in my own skin — by every measure I owned, this was the worst night of my life. But the terror and the liberation had gotten tangled somewhere on the road, braided together like the frost-ferns on my fingers, and what I mostly felt was *light*. Unburdened. The particular lightness of a person who has finally stopped pretending, even if what she's stopped pretending to be might get her burned.

"Vess won't stop," I said. "You understand that. An hour and eleven minutes was generous. By tomorrow she'll have decided I fooled her, and by tomorrow night she'll have decided you helped me fool her, and by the day after she'll have the whole company hunting two traitors instead of one boy with clever doors. She'll use my habits. She knows every habit I have. She taught me half of them."

"I know," Dominic said. "Which is why we're not going anywhere she'd predict. We're going somewhere that isn't on any chart."

"There are places not on charts?"

He smiled, and in the firelight it was the first honest smile I'd ever seen him wear — all the careful cartographer sanded off it, and something younger and more frightened and more determined underneath. "Seren," he said, "charts are how I lie to people. The truth is considerably larger."

I was still deciding whether that was romantic or infuriating when the frost on my fingers flared — sudden, sharp, all at once, cold racing up both arms like a struck bell — and both of us went still.

Not my doing. I knew it wasn't my doing the way you know a knock at the door isn't the wind.

Far off to the north, back along the road we'd ridden, a horn sounded. Then another. Then a third, lower, longer — the signal Vess used only twice a year, the one that meant *all companies converge*.

"She's not following our trail," I whispered.

"No," Dominic said softly, staring north into the dark, and his face had gone the color of paper. "She's not following anything. Seren — that horn isn't pointed at us. It's pointed home. At the valley."

The frost on my hands answered the horn, note for note, like something in me that had been waiting all my life to be called.
