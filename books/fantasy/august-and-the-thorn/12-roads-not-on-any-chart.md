## 12. Roads Not on Any Chart

### Dominic

The first thing you learn about exile is that it smells like wet wool.

The second thing is that Seren Vane cannot cook. She'd built a fire with the efficiency of a woman who had done it a hundred times under worse conditions, skewered two river trout she'd caught with her bare hands — *bare hands*, like some kind of feral saint — and then produced a seasoning pouch containing nothing but salt and what I sincerely hoped was dried onion.

"This is terrible," I said, chewing.

"You're welcome."

"It's genuinely the worst fish I've ever eaten, and I once ate a fish out of the millpond on a dare."

"Then your palate was ruined by privilege." She took a bite of her own cooking without flinching. "This is what food tastes like when nobody's coddling you."

"This is what food tastes like when someone hates you personally."

She smiled around her next bite, and I felt that smile somewhere behind my ribs, which was inconvenient, because we were fugitives, and fugitives have no business feeling things behind their ribs.

Three days out of Ashford Hollow. Three days since her frost had bloomed across a skirmish like a signature on a confession, since I'd lied to Captain Vess with my heart hammering so loud I was sure he could hear it, since we'd run into the hills with nothing but my satchel of maps, her sword, and the mutual understanding that we were each other's only alibi now.

She'd barely spoken the first day. Grief does that — hers for the company she'd served since she was sixteen, mine for a life I'd already lost back at the workshop. But grief, I've found, is a poor traveling companion. It doesn't carry its share of the weight. So by the second morning I'd started talking, mostly to fill the silence, and by the second evening she'd started answering, mostly to tell me I was wrong about everything.

It was going well, all things considered.

"Show me," she said suddenly.

"Show you what?"

"The thing you do. The gift." She said the word carefully, the way you'd handle a blade you didn't trust yet. "You look at the landscape and then you look at nothing, and then you draw something true. I've watched you do it four times since Ashford. You think I haven't noticed."

I set down the terrible fish. "It's not dramatic. There's no lightning. Mostly it looks like a man squinting at dirt."

"I've killed men for less interesting reasons. Show me anyway."

So I took out my journal, opened to a fresh page, and tried to explain the thing I'd never explained to anyone, because explaining it meant admitting it existed.

"Every place has a shape underneath its shape," I said. "The valley you can see — hills, rivers, roads — that's just the top layer. Underneath there's another one. Older. The way water remembers the channel it cut before the river moved." I uncapped my ink. "Most people walk on the top layer their whole lives. I can feel the bottom one. Like a second pulse."

"And the ink?"

"The ink listens." I held the page flat against a stone. "Watch."

For a long moment, nothing. Then the black line I'd drawn — just an idle contour, a hillside — shivered. Crawled. Bent itself into a new shape: not the hillside we sat on but a hollow half a mile east, a fold in the land with a spring at its throat, and a path down to it that no surveyor had ever recorded because no surveyor had ever known to look.

Seren went very still. Frost prickled along her knuckles before she noticed and clenched her fist to stop it.

"That's the spring," she said quietly. "There's a spring there. I found it two years ago on patrol. It's not on any chart."

"No. It's on the other chart. The one underneath."

She stared at the moving ink the way I imagined she'd once stared at her own frosted fingers — with the particular horror of a person discovering the world is bigger than the version they were trained to defend. Then, because she was Seren, she got practical.

"Can it find people?"

"Yes." I met her eyes. "That's how Vess used me. That's what I did."

The silence stretched. Somewhere downslope, a nightjar called its three-note question.

"But you drew us a route around the last checkpoint," she said slowly, working it through. "And the ford at Marlow. And—" She stopped. Recalculated. I could almost hear the arithmetic. "You've been steering us away from every patrol. Not toward them."

"I've been steering us away from everyone," I said. "Gifted or otherwise. Right now I only want the map to find one thing, and it's the road that keeps us alive."

She looked at me for a long moment, and whatever she saw there seemed to satisfy some private audit. "All right," she said. "Then tomorrow I teach you to fight, because if your map ever fails, you'll need to know how to keep breathing without it."

"I know how to fight."

"You know how to flail with a knife and apologize. Different discipline."

### Seren

He fought the way he drew maps: precisely, earnestly, and entirely wrong.

"Elbow in," I said, for the ninth time. "You're carving the air. The air has feelings too, apparently, and you don't want to hurt them."

"I'm *trying*—"

"You're thinking. Thinking is for before and after. During, there's only the body." I knocked his practice stick aside — a branch, really; we couldn't afford steel sounds — and tapped his sternum with two fingers. "Dead."

"This seems unfair. You've been doing this since childhood."

"So did you with your maps. Nobody wept for my opponents." I reset his stance, adjusting his shoulders, and tried very hard to notice only the mechanics of him. The trouble with Dominic Kavanagh was that he paid attention the way I wished more people did — completely, without flinching — and being attended to like that was a drug I hadn't known I was addicted to until someone administered it daily.

We drilled until noon, then walked, then drilled again in a beech clearing where the light came down green as bottle glass. He improved. Slowly, grudgingly, the way a stubborn mule improves, but he improved. By the fourth evening he could fall properly, which is ninety percent of surviving, and by the fifth he landed a touch on my shoulder and looked so astonished I nearly let him have a second one.

"There," I said. "You're marginally less likely to die."

"High praise."

"I save my praise for people who deserve to live long enough to hear it twice."

But it wasn't the drilling that undid me. It was the nights.

He'd spread his charts on a flat rock and show me the world as his gift saw it — the under-shape, the old channels, the ghost-rivers beneath the real ones. And I'd sit across the fire with frost melting off my fingers into the dirt and realize I could almost see them too. Not the way he did. More like... pressure. A hum in my teeth when we crossed a place where the deep layer ran strong. I never told him. I told myself it was because he had enough to carry, but that was a lie with good posture. The truth was that saying it aloud would make it real, and I had spent my whole adult life making it real for other people, at the end of a rope or a torch, and I could not bear to be the thing I'd hunted.

One night he caught me humming. Off-key, obviously. It steadied the horses back in the company; it steadied me now, though there were no horses, just firelight and his terrible singing voice joining in like a cat being stepped on.

"We are going to die of that song," he said.

"If we die, we die harmonizing badly. There are worse ends."

"Name one."

"Burning. Which was the plan, last month, for both of us, so I'd say we're ahead."

He laughed — really laughed, head back, and I catalogued the sound somewhere permanent. That was the danger of exile nobody warns you about: it strips everything down to essentials, and then you find out what your essentials are. Mine, it turned out, were salt, sleep, and this ridiculous cartographer who talked to his own ink.

### Dominic

On the sixth night she taught me to throw a knife, and on the seventh night I taught her to read the under-shape, and on the eighth night neither of us slept much, and none of those sentences mean what a discreet narrator might want them to mean. Mostly.

Actually, some of them mean exactly that. We were fugitives, not saints.

It happened after the checkpoint at Grey Ford. We'd crept past within a hundred yards of a patrol fire — her hand flat on my chest, both of us pressed into a drainage culvert smelling of ancient frog — and when we finally came up gasping on the far bank, giddy with borrowed breath, she looked at me and I looked at her and the whole calculus of *we shouldn't* quietly collapsed under the weight of *we might not get a later*.

Afterward we lay in the dark under a hawthorn, her head on my shoulder, and I traced the under-shape of the sky out of habit — stars have layers too, though I'd never told anyone that — and said, "I need to tell you something. About the bargain."

"With the elder. I know."

"You *know*?"

"Dominic. You disappear every time we camp near standing stones. You come back smelling of other people's woodsmoke and carrying answers you pretend you always had." She shrugged against my shoulder. "I'm a tracker. I track."

"Why didn't you say anything?"

"Because whatever you traded them, you traded it to keep people alive. I watched you do it for months from the inside of the hunt. I know the shape of your sins." A pause. "Mine are heavier. Ask me sometime about the family at Wellbrook. Ask me why I stopped singing on the road home after."

I didn't ask. Not then. Some doors you leave shut until the person on the other side opens them, and I understood doors better than most.

Instead I said, "When this is over — if there's an over — I want to map somewhere no one's ever commissioned. Just a place, for no reason. Would you come?"

"To watch you squint at dirt?"

"It's more dignified than it sounds."

"Yes," she said, into my shoulder. "Obviously yes."

I fell asleep believing it, which should have warned me. Happiness in wartime is always a loan, and the interest is brutal.

### Seren

The crow found us on the eleventh morning.

Not a real crow — a paper one, folded from a page torn out of a ledger, wings char-black, sitting on our cold fire ring like it had been waiting for breakfast. Dominic went white the moment he saw it.

"That's enclave work," he said. "Message-fold. They only use it when—" He snatched it up and unfolded it, and I watched his face do something I'd only seen once before, on the day the hunt reached the orchard.

"What is it?"

His voice came out flat, which from Dominic meant the opposite of calm. "It's Tam. The elder's grandson. One line." He turned the paper so I could read it, though I already knew, the way you know a wound before you look at it.

*They took grandfather. Vess has him. Come home or don't.*

Below it, in different ink, smaller, hurried: *the twelfth moon sets in nine days.*

Dominic stood very still, the way he did when the under-shape shouted at him. When he finally spoke, it was barely above the fire's crackle.

"They're marching on the valley, Seren. Whatever the elder told them under questioning — and he'll have told them enough, he'll have told them *exactly* enough to make it holy — Vess is marching on the seal." He looked up at me, and I saw it settle over him: not fear, exactly. Direction. "Nine days. We have to get ahead of an army."

I picked up my sword. My hands, I noticed, were frosting over, and for the first time since Ashford I didn't clench them to stop it.

"Then we'd better start running," I said, "and you'd better sing to keep us steady."

"I sing terribly."

"I know," I said. "Harmonize anyway."
