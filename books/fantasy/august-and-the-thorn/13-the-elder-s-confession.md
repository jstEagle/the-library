## 13. The Elder's Confession

The elder's house smelled of beeswax and old paper, which should have made me feel at home and instead made me feel like a forgery sitting in a genuine article's parlor.

Her name was Maren Ostrow, though everyone in the enclave called her Grandmother Thistle, on account of a hedge she'd grown around the settlement that drew blood from anyone who entered with ill intent. I had walked through that hedge an hour ago with Seren at my side, both of us carrying the particular exhaustion of people who have been running for eleven days, and the thorns had parted for me like a crowd making way for a funeral.

I hadn't decided yet whether that was flattering or ominous.

"You're the mapmaker," she said, pouring tea into cups so thin I could see light through them. "The Kavanagh boy."

"Man," I said. "Technically."

"The Kavanagh *man*, then." She sat across from me, small and folded like a letter someone meant to send but never did. Her eyes were the color of river ice — pretty until you remembered what river ice does to the unwary. "You've been selling my people to the crown in exchange for your own skin, and then selling the crown false maps in exchange for answers. Do you know what we call that, in the enclave?"

"Tentative hope?" I offered.

"We call it *walking two roads*. And the trouble with walking two roads, boy, is that eventually you come to a fork, and legs only bend one way." She sipped her tea. "So. Which road brought you here?"

I looked at Seren. She was standing by the window with her arms crossed, doing her best impression of a woman who wasn't listening to every word, which was undermined by the fact that she was absolutely listening to every word. Frost had bloomed once on her fingertips three days ago during a skirmish, and since then she flinched whenever the temperature dropped, as if winter itself might report her to Captain Vess.

"The second road," I said. "The one where I stop lying."

Maren set down her cup. "Good. Because I'm tired, you're marked, and the girl by the window is going to frost my glass if she doesn't breathe slower. Ask your questions."

* * *

Here is what I had pieced together before that night, from fragments: that the valley where I was born — the valley where magic could not follow, where my gift had never worked beyond its borders, where I had hidden behind mundane survey work for thirty years — was not simply a place. That it was a seal. That my bloodline had something to do with it. The elder of the first enclave I'd bargained with, a hedgewitch named Colm, had told me exactly that much before Vess's men burned his village and took him.

What I learned from Maren Ostrow over the next four hours rearranged everything I thought I knew about home, history, and the modest size of my own problems.

"Tell me about the valley," I said. "All of it. Not the version they teach children."

Maren laughed, and it was not a kind sound. "They don't teach children any version. That's the point. Silence is the first mortar in any wall." She rose, went to a cabinet, and returned with a book. Not a book — a ledger, leather gone black with age, its spine stitched with wire instead of thread. She laid it on the table between us but kept her hand flat on the cover, as if it might bolt.

"Before there was a crown," she said, "there were the Gifted Wars. Not wars *about* the gifted, mind you. Wars *fed by* them. There was a power in those days — the histories call it the Red Harvest, because that's what it did, it harvested. It ate magic. It drank the gifted dry and left husks, and it grew stronger with every gifted life it consumed, and the kings and warlords of that age fed it deliberately, because it won battles. You could point it at an enemy city and it would empty that city of every witch, seer, hedge-healer, storm-caller, and child who ever floated a feather. And then it would turn on the army that held its leash, because that is the nature of such things. They are never leashed. They are only pointed."

My mouth had gone dry. I reached for my tea and found my hand unsteady enough that I pretended I hadn't.

"The Red Harvest killed millions," Maren continued. "Gifted and mundane alike, eventually, because a fire that eats fuel doesn't care whose woodpile it started in. And when it was finally — *temporarily* — stopped, twelve bloodlines came together to do the stopping. Twelve families of the strongest gift-workers alive. They couldn't kill it. Nothing kills it. So they buried it."

"In the valley," I said.

"In the valley." She looked at me steadily. "Under it, rather. Your ancestors didn't settle the Quiet Valley, Dominic. They built it. Every hill, every stream, every stubborn field that refuses good wheat — all of it raised as a lid. A seal woven out of twelve bloodlines' worth of magic, anchored in stone and root and oath. The valley doesn't refuse ink because it's shy. It refuses ink because it refuses *everything* — scrying, divination, far-speech, every gift that might reach down and knock on the door of the thing sleeping underneath."

I thought of my whole life. My gift that worked everywhere except home. The maps that came out blank no matter how carefully I surveyed. I had spent thirty years believing I was broken in one specific, localized way.

"I'm one of the twelve bloodlines," I said. It wasn't a question. I already knew; Colm had told me. But saying it aloud in this room, with this book under her hand, made it heavier.

"Kavanagh. Yes. The twelfth family, the last to swear, the ones who gave the most." Maren's voice gentled, which frightened me more than the ice-eyes had. "Do you know why your gift works everywhere except inside the valley? Why you can map the unmappable abroad but can't draw your own kitchen?"

"No."

"Because the seal isn't a wall around you. It's a debt *in* you. Your family paid their portion of the binding into their own blood. The valley doesn't reject your gift, boy. Your gift *is* the valley. Part of it. The part that walks around on two legs and makes jokes at inappropriate moments." She tapped the ledger. "Every Kavanagh born since the sealing carries a splinter of the lock. That's why the crown's compasses spin near you. That's why your ink moved on its own and sketched a realm you'd never seen — the seal is thinning, and the splinters are starting to remember what they're holding shut."

Seren had turned from the window. She said nothing, but she had come closer without seeming to move, the way she always did, quiet as snowfall.

"And if it breaks," I said. "If the seal fails. What happens?"

Maren opened the ledger.

* * *

The pages were dense with script in at least four hands, some of it centuries old, some of it fresh — Maren's own entries, I realized, dated within the last year. She turned to a page near the end and rotated the book toward me.

It was a census. Names, ages, villages. Hundreds of names. And beside each name, in red ink, a single word: *taken.*

"What am I looking at?"

"The harvest," she said. "The new one. Every gifted person the crown's hunt has captured in the last two years — every one burned, hanged, or 'processed,' as the writs say — has been taken somewhere. Not to a common grave. To a site in the northern mountains. We have three survivors' accounts, all matching. There is a pit there, old and deep, and the crown has been filling it."

"With bodies."

"With *fuel*." Maren's knuckles whitened on the ledger's edge. "Boy, do you understand what the hunt actually is? Vess believes she's cleansing the realm of corruption. The king believes he's securing his throne against witches. But someone told the crown where to dig, and someone convinced the crown that dead gifted souls are treasure. The Red Harvest doesn't need to be summoned. It needs to be *fed*. Two years of pyres, and that pit is nearly full, and when it is full—"

She stopped. I watched her decide whether to finish the sentence, and watched her decide that I needed to hear it.

"—it will wake up. Under a mountain, in a kingdom ruled by men who think it's a weapon they own. And the first thing it will do is finish the meal it started eight hundred years ago. And the second thing it will do is come home. To the valley. To the seal. To the one door it remembers being shut in its face."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere outside, the thorn hedge rustled in a wind I couldn't feel.

"The hunt," I said slowly, "is gathering fuel for it."

"The hunt *is* the fuel-gathering. Every pyre Vess lights is a spoonful in the pit's mouth. Your captain thinks she's saving the realm." Maren smiled, and it was the saddest thing I'd seen in weeks, which was saying something given the fortnight I'd had. "She's setting the table."

I thought of First Blood on the Map — the family fleeing into the hills, the hunter dying on a warding thorn, the guilt I'd been carrying like a stone in my boot ever since. I thought of the orchard, and the children Seren pulled from the smoke. I thought of the stranger I'd sacrificed to pass Vess's trap route, whose name I still didn't know, whose face I saw every time I closed my eyes.

Every one of them. Spoonfuls.

"I led them to enclaves," I said. My voice came out flat, which was the only way I could get it out at all. "Before I turned. People I found with my gift went onto wagons, and the wagons went north, and I—"

"You didn't know," Seren said. First words she'd spoken since we sat down.

"It doesn't matter whether I knew."

"It matters," Maren said, "more than you can possibly imagine. Which brings us to the fork in the road I mentioned." She closed the ledger and put both hands on it, and looked at me the way surveyors look at ground they're about to build on. "The seal is thinning. The thirteenth moon approaches — you've seen the signs, or you wouldn't be here. When it completes its turn, the seal will be at its weakest in eight hundred years. If the pit fills before then, the Harvest wakes strong, and nothing on this earth stops it. But if the seal holds through the turn — if it is *renewed* — the valley sleeps another age, and the pit, full or not, stays shut behind it."

"Renewed how?"

"That," she said, "is the question your family was designed to answer. The renewal requires the twelve bloodlines. Eleven are scattered, hidden, diminished — some may be extinct. But the twelfth, the Kavanagh line, the line that gave the most—" she spread her hands, "—is sitting in my parlor drinking my tea."

I set down my cup very carefully, because I suddenly did not trust my hands with fragile objects.

"What does renewal cost?"

Maren held my gaze. Behind her, Seren had gone very still, the stillness of a hunter who has heard something move in brush she cannot see into.

"Everything costs," the elder said. "That's the first law of magic and the last lesson of kings. What the renewal costs, I don't fully know — the rite is written in your blood, not mine. But I know what the alternative costs, and I've seen its receipts." She tapped the ledger once more. "So. You wanted the truth, mapmaker. Here is the shape of your map now: an ancient hunger waking in the north, a crown digging its grave with righteous enthusiasm, a seal failing under your feet, and a rite only your blood can perform, at a price no one living can name."

I sat with that. Outside, the wind picked up, and somewhere in the hedge a thorn sang against another thorn like a bow across a string.

"You said the rite is written in my blood," I said finally. "Meaning what, exactly? Metaphorically, or—"

"Or literally? Both. The Kavanaghs bound themselves into the seal bodily, boy. Your blood is a key. Possibly the key. Which means there are people — in the crown, in the pits, in places older than either — who would very much like to know where you are." Maren rose and began trimming a candle whose flame hadn't wavered, a gesture I recognized as the fidget of a woman buying time before bad news. "And I regret to tell you that someone already knows."

The candle hissed.

"How long," Seren asked quietly, "have they known?"

"Since the mill bridge," Maren said. "Since the day the criers read the proclamation and a compass spun toward a cartographer's shop. You thought that was accident or informer. It was neither. The hunt was *sent* for you, specifically, by someone who understands what your bloodline is — someone who wants the key found before the moon turns." She looked at me with those river-ice eyes, and for the first time all night I saw fear in them, and it was fear *for* me, which was somehow worse than fear of anything. "You were never conscripted, Dominic Kavanagh. You were collected."

* * *

We left before dawn. Maren gave us the ledger's newest pages copied fair, a pouch of dried plums, and one piece of advice delivered at the door while the thorn hedge bowed us through.

"The moon turns in nineteen days," she said. "Vess is marching on this valley — the elder Colm broke under questioning and gave her just enough truth to make the hunt a crusade. She'll tell her soldiers they're going to break a seal and win a war forever. She'll believe she's a hero." Maren gripped my wrist, hard, her small fingers surprisingly strong. "You know what's actually buried here. So does the girl. Nineteen days, mapmaker. Go home ahead of the army. And whatever the rite asks of you when you stand at the thorn—"

She paused. Wind moved through the hedge, and the thorns whispered among themselves like a congregation deciding whether to sing.

"—decide beforehand," she finished, "what you're willing to pay. Because it will not ask twice, and it will not ask politely."

We walked east into the grey beginning of morning, toward the valley, toward the army behind us and the seal ahead, and I caught myself doing sums I didn't want to do: nineteen days, one bloodline, twelve locks, one price unnamed.

Behind us, faint as a struck bell, the thorn hedge began to sing.
