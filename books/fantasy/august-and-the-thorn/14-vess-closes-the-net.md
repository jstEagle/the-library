## 14. Vess Closes the Net

I have always been a creature of habit. Vess used to say it was my best quality — that a tracker who could be predicted was a tracker who could be relied upon. I'd laughed at the time. It seemed like praise.

It was not praise. It was a file, and she'd been keeping it on me for years.

We learned this the hard way, three days after leaving the elder's hollow, when we stopped at a river crossing and Dominic looked at me with that particular expression he got — the one like a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like — and said, "You're going to want to sleep with your back to stone, aren't you."

"I sleep fine anywhere."

"You slept against the standing stone at Harrow Ford. You slept against the chimney at the tanner's ruin. You've slept against every vertical surface between here and the border, Seren, and you do it in that order — east side, always the east side." He crouched by the water, watching me. "You're building her a trail of breadcrumbs."

"I'm building us *shelter*."

"You're building her a map of you." He said it gently, which was worse. "She trained you. She knows how you think because she taught you how to think. Every habit you keep is a door she can walk through."

I wanted to argue. I was very good at arguing; it was one of the few gifts I'd ever owned openly. But I thought about Vess kneeling beside my bedroll at the orchard fire, adjusting my pack straps without asking, the way she'd once told me — laughing, over cheap wine — *"If I ever hunt you, girl, I won't need hounds. I'll just set out everything you hate and wait where you don't look."*

"Fine," I said. "Tonight we sleep in the open. Middle of the fen. No stone."

Dominic smiled, and for a moment the fugitive's grayness lifted off him and he was just a tired man pleased with his cleverness, and I loved him so stupidly much that I almost forgot what was coming.

Almost.

* * *

The marshes south of the Elder's country were called the Whispering Fens, which is the kind of name cartographers give places where people go missing. Dominic had mapped them once, years ago, from the valley's edge — the only place his gift reached — and he talked as we waded, narrating the drowned world like a tour guide for the damned.

"Peat bog ahead, then a channel that looks solid but will take a horse to the ribs. After that, the old causeway. The crown built it before the valley sealed and never finished it."

"How do you know all this if your gift dies at the valley line?"

"Because I walked it. Before the hunt, before any of this, I spent two summers surveying for a wool merchant who wanted a road that didn't exist." He paused, thigh-deep in black water, and gave me a crooked look. "Also because I've been dreaming about these fens since the thorn started talking to me. Which I mention only so you know I'm aware of how it sounds."

"It sounds like the beginning of a ballad where everyone drowns."

"Most good ballads start that way."

We moved through the fen for two days, sleeping on hummocks, eating smoked eel and a bread that fought back. And it worked, mostly. We saw no riders, no smoke, no sign of the hunt. I began — foolishly, I see now — to relax.

On the third evening, Dominic stopped so suddenly I walked into his back.

"What?"

He didn't answer. He was looking at the ground. At the mud. At the small, neat pile of stones someone had stacked at the edge of the causeway — three flat stones, one atop another, deliberate as a signature.

"That wasn't there yesterday," he said.

"No."

"Seren. Who stacks stones like that?"

And I knew. Of course I knew. I'd seen her do it a hundred times, at crossroads, at burned homesteads, at the edge of every site she'd cleared. Vess marked her passages the way other captains planted banners — quietly, precisely, a habit from her surveying days, before the hunt, when she'd been a mapmaker too.

*Gods. She'd been a mapmaker.* And I'd led a mapmaker into a marsh, along the only dry route through it, following habits she'd installed in me like stitches.

"She knows exactly where we are," I said. "She's known for days. She's not chasing us. She's herding us."

"To where?"

I looked down the causeway, at the long spine of stone running out into the darkening fen, and felt the answer settle in my stomach like swallowed ice. "To somewhere with only one way out."

* * *

They came at dusk, which was tactically sound and dramatically predictable, and I have never decided which annoyed me more.

Torches first — a dozen points of flame bobbing along the causeway behind us, cutting off retreat. Then the flanking lines, wading through the shallows on either side with the slow, spread-fingered patience of people who had done this many times and enjoyed it in a way they'd never admit. And at the center, on the causeway itself, walking unhurried, Captain Vess.

She'd changed since the orchard. Harder around the mouth. There was a new scar at her hairline and a new stillness in her shoulders, and when her eyes found mine across two hundred feet of black water and dying light, there was no anger in them at all. That frightened me more than anger would have.

"Huntress!" she called, and her voice carried flat and clear across the fen. "You're standing in my net. Come in, and the cartographer lives. Stay, and I take you both, and I burn something of yours for every hour you've cost me."

"She's bluffing," Dominic murmured. "Partly."

"Which part?"

"The part where she lets either of us live."

I drew my knife. Old habit. Useless weapon. But my hands wanted to hold something, and the alternative was holding nothing.

"We run the causeway," I said. "North end. If the water's shallow enough past the beacon tower—"

"The water isn't shallow anywhere past the beacon tower. I told you, I *mapped*—"

"Then we fight."

"Seren—"

"They'll take the elder's hollow next," I said, and heard my own voice come out strange and flat. "That's why she herded us here instead of taking us on the road. She didn't want us warning them. She wanted us *here*, pinned, while her riders—" My throat closed. I made myself finish. "While her riders rode north."

Dominic went pale in the torchlight. "How many days' ride?"

"Two. Maybe less, if she pushed them hard before doubling back to pen us."

"So whatever we do here," he said slowly, "the hollow falls."

"Unless we break out tonight and outrun the news."

"And if we fail, we die in a swamp for nothing."

"Yes," I said. "That's the shape of it."

He looked at me for a long moment. Torchlight moved on his face. Then he did the thing I would remember for the rest of my life, however long or short that turned out to be: he smiled, took my hand, and said, "Well. I've always wanted to die in a place I've surveyed. Very tidy. Let's make it untidy."

* * *

What followed was not a battle. Battles have lines and drums and some pretense of order. What followed was two people and forty hunters in waist-deep water in the dark, and the dark belonged to us.

I had grown up in hill country where the fog came off the valleys like breath, and I knew how to be invisible in it. Dominic knew the fen's bones — every channel, every sinkhole, every shelf of firm ground — better than the men hunting us knew their own boots. So we did not run the causeway. We left it, dropped into the water, and became rumors.

I took the eastern flank. Three hunters went down in the reeds before anyone understood they were going down — a knee to the temple, a blade-flat across the throat, a shove into water deep enough to swallow a scream. Not killing blows. I told myself they weren't killing blows. Some of them even were.

Dominic, to my everlasting astonishment, held the western side alone. He couldn't fight — I'd spent weeks trying to teach him and produced a man who swung a sword like a woman beating a rug — but he didn't need to. He needed only to *know things*. He stood in the shallows calling out positions in a low, carrying voice — *"Two at the dead alder, one behind you, left, LEFT"* — steering me through the dark like a lighthouse with a grudge, and the hunters, hearing a voice that seemed to come from everywhere, began to believe the fen itself had turned against them.

"There," he shouted. "The tall one, reeds, ten paces — Seren, *duck*—"

I ducked. A crossbow bolt passed through the space my head had been occupying and buried itself in a hummock with a sound like a kiss.

"Thank you!"

"Don't thank me, *move*—"

We broke through the northern line at full dark, soaked to the bone, bleeding in unimportant places, and ran. Behind us the torches scattered and multiplied, shouts overlapping, hunters firing at shadows and each other. Ahead of us lay miles of open fen and, beyond it, the high ground and the road north.

We ran until my lungs burned and my legs shook, and then we ran more, because grief is an excellent fuel and I had just filled the tank.

It was only at dawn, collapsed in the ruins of a shepherd's hut on the fen's far edge, that the arithmetic caught up with me.

"The hollow," I said.

Dominic didn't answer. He was wringing out his coat, and his hands had gone very still.

"Dominic. The hollow."

"I know."

"Brannoc is eighty years old. Maren has a baby. The warding-thorns are strong but there are maybe thirty of them against a company of—"

"*I know.*" His voice cracked on it. He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "I've been doing the sums since midnight, Seren. Two days' hard ride. They'll reach it tomorrow night. We can't get ahead of them. Even if we killed ourselves on the road, we'd arrive hours behind the riders, and we'd arrive *known* — Vess will have our descriptions on every tongue between here and the valley."

"Then we follow. We follow and we—"

"And we what? Watch?" He lowered his hand, and his eyes were red-rimmed and furious and terrified, and I realized with a jolt that the fury wasn't at me. It was at himself. "This is what I am to them now. Did you understand that? I sat in a cage and offered my gift to a witch-hunter, and every person I've failed since is a debt with my name on it. The family in the hills. The stranger on the trap route. And now Brannoc, who trusted me, who gave me the truth about the seal, who—"

"He knew the risk."

"He *knew the risk*," Dominic repeated softly. "Yes. That's what we say about people we feed to the fire, isn't it? It's what Vess says. I've been telling myself Brannoc knew the risk, and I sound exactly like her."

I crossed the ruined room and sat down beside him. The dawn light came through the broken roof in gray bars. Somewhere outside, a curlew cried its idiot heart out over the fen.

"Listen to me," I said. "You are not Vess. Do you know how I know? Because she has never once, in all the years I served her, lost sleep over a single soul she burned. You can't stop being the man who made bad bargains. But you can decide what you do next. That's the whole difference. That's the entire war."

He looked at me. "And what do we do next?"

I thought about it. I thought about Brannoc's voice saying *the seal is blood, boy, your blood*, and about the vision the thorn had shown him — the one he wouldn't describe, the one that put a grave in his eyes whenever he looked at me. I thought about Vess riding north with the elder bound in a wagon, asking questions, and Brannoc — proud, stubborn, ancient Brannoc — giving her answers, because under torture everyone gives answers eventually, and the truth in Brannoc's keeping was worth more than any enclave.

"She'll question him," I said. "Vess. And Brannoc will hold out for a while, because he's a granite-hearted old bastard, but he'll give her something. Enough. Enough to make the hunt into a crusade. Enough to point her at the valley."

Dominic had gone very quiet. "You think she'll march on the valley itself."

"I think she's been marching toward it her whole life and only just got the address."

"Then we have to get home first. Warn the enclaves. Get ahead of her." He was already standing, already moving, grief converting itself to motion the way I was learning grief did, if you refused to let it sit. "Seren — the seal. If Vess reaches the thorn with an army and a prisoner who knows its name—"

"Then we don't sleep," I said, "and we don't eat, and we don't mourn, and we walk until our feet are stumps. And when we get there, we make sure that when she comes for the valley—"

I stopped. Because the sentence I'd been about to finish was *we'll be ready*, and I suddenly understood, with the clarity that comes at the end of sleepless nights, that readiness was not the same as survival. That somewhere ahead of us stood a thorn and a moon and a price, and that Dominic knew the price and hadn't told me, and that I would let him keep that secret a little longer because I was a coward about exactly one thing in this world.

"—she finds the valley closed to her," I finished. "However it has to close."

Dominic looked at me a moment too long. Whatever lived behind his eyes, he folded it away, and nodded, and shouldered his pack.

"One more thing," he said at the door of the ruined hut, as the sun finally cleared the fen and lit the long road north. "When we warn the enclaves — when we tell them the hunt is coming for all of them, for the valley, for everything — some of them will fight. You know that. Hill folk with thorn-magic and nothing left to lose."

"I know."

"And some of them will die fighting, and some of those deaths will be ours to carry, because we're the ones bringing the news." He held out his hand to me. "So I need to hear you say it. That this is a war now. Not a flight. A war."

Outside, the curlew cried again. I took his hand.

"It's a war," I said. "And Vess started it the day she taught me to track. Time she learned what she taught."

We went north, two traitors on one road, with the sun at our backs and the whole weight of the coming season pressing down on us like weather — and behind us, far behind us, in a fen turning gold with morning, I knew that Captain Vess was already standing over an old man in chains, asking her questions in that soft, patient voice, and that every answer he gave her was a drumbeat marching toward home.
