## 15. What the Thorn Remembers

The ink had been moving for three days.

Not the way ink moves when water touches it, or when a careless elbow smears a finished chart. This was purposeful. I'd wake at dawn in whatever ditch or barn we'd claimed for the night and find my travel journal open, the pen I'd capped myself lying uncapped beside it, and a line of black crawling across the page like something learning to walk. It drew rivers I didn't know. Hills with a hunched, deliberate shape. And always, at the center of everything it drew, a small dense knot of hatching — as if the map were trying to say *here*, over and over, in the only voice it had.

Seren noticed on the second night. She watched the line creep for a long moment in the firelight, her frost-scarred hands folded in her lap, and then she said, very quietly: "It's drawing you home."

"It's drawing *somewhere*," I said, because I was a coward about certain words even then.

I'm getting ahead of myself. Let me start where it actually started, which was the morning we crossed the old survey line my father had scratched into an oak when I was nine years old — *D.K., boundary of the knowable* — and stepped out of every map I had ever trusted.

* * *

The valley doesn't appear on charts for the same reason a face doesn't appear on a mirror's back. It isn't hiding. It simply isn't *for* them. Every cartographer who ever tried to render it from outside produced either blank vellum or nonsense — coastlines that spiraled inward, mountain ranges arranged like a closed fist. My gift was the exception that proved the rule, though I spent thirty years pretending it wasn't a gift at all, just a trick of good survey work. Within the valley, magic couldn't follow me. Beyond its edge, apparently, nothing else could keep up either.

But now the ink moved, and the moving ink knew the way, and so Seren and I went where it pointed: north along a ridge no crown surveyor had ever named, down through birch country gone silver with early autumn, following a line of black that sometimes crawled ahead of us on the page like a dog scenting home.

"You could stop," Seren said on the third morning. She said it lightly, the way she offered most dangerous things. "We could go east instead. There's supposed to be a ferry town past the fells. No seal, no thorn, no ancestral doom. You could draw maps of ordinary places and I could learn to sing on key."

"You already sing on key," I said. "You just refuse to."

"Flatterer." But she was watching me with those level grey eyes that had unnerved Captain Vess himself once, according to camp rumor, and she wasn't smiling. "Dominic. The elder's story keeps me up at night, and it wasn't even mine. Your ancestors chained a god in your backyard and you're walking toward the chain."

"Not a god. A war."

"A war that eats people." She crouched to turn her boot over, shaking out a stone, and when she straightened she wouldn't look at me. "The hunt burned the orchard because they thought they were cleansing it. Vess marches on your valley because she thinks she's going to win a war. Nobody in this whole wretched country does evil on purpose. That's what frightens me. Everyone's so *reasonable*."

She was right, of course. She usually was, which was one of the many things I hadn't yet told her I loved about her, because I was rationing my confessions like a miser. I'd told her about the bargain with the enclave elder — the false maps, the stranger whose capture had bought our credibility. She'd told me about the children from the orchard, and about the first time her hands frosted in her sleep, and about the hunts before the orchard, the ones where she'd obeyed orders. We had traded our worst selves like prisoners trading rations, and somehow come out richer, but there were still vaults in both of us. Mine contained a sentence I couldn't shape yet. Hers, I suspected, contained the same one.

"The ink wants me there," I said finally. "And I want to know why. That's all. Curiosity. A cartographer's disease."

"Liar," she said fondly, and shouldered her pack, and we walked on.

* * *

We found the first marker at noon.

It was a stone, waist-high, furred with lichen, and carved into its face was a spiral — tight, precise, the same spiral I had been doodling unconsciously in margins since childhood. My father had scolded me for it. My schoolmaster had too. *Waste of ink, boy.* I knelt and put my palm flat against the carving, and the lichen was warm.

"Dominic," Seren said, in the voice she used for warning shots.

"I know."

"Do you? Because you've got the look you get when the ink moves. The one where you pretend to be calm and your hand shakes."

"My hand is not—" It was. I stood, wiped my palm on my coat, and made myself look at her. "My family came from here. Before the valley, before the migration south. The elder said my blood forged the seal. I thought that meant ancestors. Generations back. Abstract people."

"And now?"

"Now I think the abstract people left instructions."

There were more stones. They appeared as we climbed — each one spiral-carved, each one warmer than the air justified, spaced like fence posts along a path that no longer pretended to be anything natural. The trees changed around us. The birches gave way to something older, black-barked and leafless in the wrong season, and between their bare trunks I began to see threads. Silver filaments, spider-fine, strung from tree to tree at head height, sagging slightly under their own weight, catching the light like wire.

"Wards," Seren breathed. She raised one hand toward a thread and stopped herself an inch short. Frost bloomed on her fingertips anyway, unasked. "They're humming. Can you hear them?"

I could. Not sound exactly — pressure. The feeling in your ears when a storm is still hours off but already deciding. And beneath it, faintly, something else. A rhythm. Slow, vast, patient as geology. I had heard it my whole life without knowing: in the silence of the valley, in the reason ink behaved itself there, in the flatness my gift always hit like a wall. The valley wasn't empty of magic. It was a held breath.

"We're close," I said. "Past the last ridge there should be—"

The ink answered for me. In my journal, riding open in the crook of my arm, the black line surged, filled the page corner to corner, and then drained away entirely — leaving a single image behind, drawn with a precision my own hand has never managed: a thorn. A great black thorn the length of a man, driven point-down into the earth, silver threads radiating from it in every direction like roots made of light.

Seren looked at the page. Then at me. Then she did the bravest thing I saw her do in all our months together, which is saying a great deal: she took my hand, frost and all, and kept walking.

* * *

The hollow at the heart of the valley was smaller than I expected. Places of enormous weight usually are; the world doesn't waste scenery on what matters. A ring of the black trees, dead or sleeping, I couldn't tell. Silver threads converging overhead into a single bright knot, like a roof of captured starlight. And in the center, rising from a mound of dark soil, the thorn.

It was the size the ink had promised — black, seamless, curved like a raptor's talon — and it was *alive*. I don't mean metaphorically. As we approached I watched the surface move, slowly, the way a horse's hide twitches when flies land somewhere it can't reach. The soil around its base was cracked in a ring, and through the cracks seeped a light that wasn't light, a pressure-colored glow, the held breath of the whole valley exhaled thin.

The rhythm was deafening now. Or would have been, if it were sound. As it was I felt it in my teeth, my sternum, the backs of my eyes.

"Don't touch it," Seren said.

"I wasn't going to touch it."

"You're walking toward it with your hand out."

I looked down. My hand was out. "That's not me doing it," I said, which was true, and which was also the least comforting sentence I have ever spoken aloud.

Here is what I understood, standing at the rim of that cracked ring of earth, in the last moment of my life before everything: the seal was failing. Not breaking — failing, the way a dam fails, slowly and invisibly and then all at once. The threads above us had gone slack in places. The glow through the cracks pulsed with the rhythm, and the rhythm was quickening, and somewhere beneath my feet a war older than kingdoms was turning over in its sleep, dreaming of the taste of gifted blood, and the crown's army was marching toward this exact spot carrying two hundred gifted captives like wine to a feast.

And the thorn wanted me. My blood had forged it. Which meant, though I didn't have the words for it yet, my blood might be the only thing that could mend it — or the only thing that could finish breaking it. Both doors stood open. Both were mine.

"Dominic." Seren's voice, very quiet. "Whatever it shows you — remember that visions lie. Especially kind ones."

I turned to tell her I loved her. I had decided, in that final second, that I was done being a miser.

Then I touched the thorn.

* * *

I have tried, in the years since, to describe what the thorn showed me, and I have failed every time, because it wasn't pictures. It was memory with the volume turned up until memory became the room you were standing in.

I was a woman named Aldith Kavanagh, kneeling on this exact spot four hundred years ago, driving the first silver thread into the earth with a needle of her own sharpened bone while the war-magic screamed beneath her — screamed in voices of everyone it had eaten, and some of those voices belonged to her children. I was her brother Tomas, holding the thorn — grown, not made, grown from a cutting of the world's own cruelty — and driving it down, down, feeling it drink from his hands and wanting it to, because he had watched the war-magic wear his daughter's face and ask him, in her voice, to please let it out. I was thirteen generations of Kavanaghs after that, each one walking to this hollow once in their life to press blood against the thorn and renew the vow, each one hearing the same instruction passed down like an inheritance nobody wanted: *when it wakes, one of ours must feed it what it cannot take by force.*

What it cannot take by force. For four hundred years, the family had argued about what that meant. Gold. Years of life. Names. The thorn had never needed to collect, and so the debt had never been read.

Now I stood inside the newest Kavanagh, and the thorn read the debt aloud.

It showed me the war. Not the ancient one — the next one. Vess's army breaching the valley at moonset, the gifted captives screaming, the seal cracking like river ice, and the war-magic rising out of the hollow wearing the faces of everyone it had ever eaten, offering itself to the crown as a weapon, and the crown accepting, because crowns are reasonable. I saw years of it. Valleys burning in countries I'd only ever drawn. Gifted children harvested like the orchard. And threading through all of it, everywhere, always, I saw Seren — fighting, fleeing, standing between the war-magic and strangers, her frost spreading across battlefields in beautiful desperate flowers — and every version ended the same way. Her body in the snow. Her face turned toward me. Her mouth shaping my name with no breath left to say it.

Every future the thorn owned, she died. That was the price of the war continuing. Not defeat — continuation. The war-magic fed on the gifted, and Seren was the brightest thing in it, and it would find her, in every future, the way water finds the lowest ground.

Unless the seal held. Unless it was remade. And the thorn, patient as geology, showed me the exchange rate: it could mend itself, drink the waking war back down, hold for another four hundred years — but it required payment of equal weight to what it guarded. A memory of equal weight to the pull of a war.

I didn't understand that arithmetic yet. Some part of me understood it perfectly, and that part went cold and quiet and started counting.

The thorn released me.

I came back to myself on my knees in the dark soil, and Seren had my shoulders, and she was saying my name in the tone you use for pulling someone out of deep water, and there was frost on her hands and tears freezing in her lashes and I realized distantly that I was the one who had pulled her down into the ring, that I'd been gripping her wrists hard enough to bruise, that some part of me had been trying to anchor myself to her while four hundred years of ancestors poured through my skull.

"How long?" I rasped.

"Minutes. Dominic, your nose is bleeding. What did it—"

"It showed me the war." I wiped my face and my hand came away red and I laughed, which frightened her, I could see it frighten her, and I couldn't stop. "All of it. Ours and the next one. Do you want to know the punchline, Seren? The seal can be mended. One Kavanagh, one payment, and the valley holds another four centuries. The hunt could march in tomorrow and find nothing but a hillside."

"That's—" Hope lit her face, and I had to watch it, knowing what I knew, and it was the cruelest thing the thorn did to me, worse than the visions. It gave me a way to save everything except the one thing I'd walk into that hollow to protect. "That's wonderful. Dominic, that's *wonderful*, we can warn them, we can—"

"Yes," I said. "We can."

I didn't tell her the price. I want that recorded plainly, wherever such things are recorded: I looked at the woman I loved, radiant with hope I had no right to permit, and I lied to her by omission, and I told myself it was mercy.

The thorn had shown me the arithmetic. A memory of equal weight to the pull of a war. And there was only one thing in my life that weighed that much.

Behind us, far to the south, so faint it might have been weather, the first horns of Vess's army sounded against the hills — and the thirteenth moon, unseen in the afternoon sky, began its last climb.
