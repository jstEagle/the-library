## 16. The Interrogation of the Elder

They brought Maren in at dusk, and I will say this for Captain Vess: she has an eye for theater.

The tent was the big one, the command pavilion with the crown stitched on every panel, and inside it she had arranged everything like a stage. A single brazier. A table with a ewer of water and one cup — one cup, that was the message, *you drink when I permit it*. Two chairs, and hers was taller. Behind her, two of her veterans stood with their arms folded in that way soldiers have of looking bored while being anything but.

I was made to stand at the back. My hands were bound — loosely, insultingly loosely, as if Vess wanted me to know she didn't consider my wrists a threat worth iron — and a guard held a rope that went nowhere in particular. Just rope. Just the idea of rope.

Maren looked smaller than I remembered. In the elder's hollow under the hills she had seemed carved from the same stone, all angles and certainty, her voice the kind that made you sit up straight even when you disagreed with every word. Now she shuffled between the guards like a bundle of sticks someone had tied together badly. Her left eye was swollen shut from the marshes, where they'd taken her, where Dominic and I had run and she had turned back to buy us time with whatever the gifted keep instead of courage.

She saw me standing there and something moved behind her ruined face. Not surprise. Worse than surprise — recognition. She'd known I would be here. She'd known what I was before I did, maybe, and now she watched me watch her be dragged toward a chair, and she did not look away, and neither could I.

"Sit her down," Vess said.

Maren sat. It took her a moment, joints protesting, and when she finally settled into the low stool — they'd given her a stool, lower than the captain's chair, everything calibrated — she arranged her hands in her lap with terrible composure, like a woman preparing to wait out a long sermon.

"Water," Vess said, and poured it herself. She handed the cup across with both hands, almost tender. "You're old. Old women dehydrate. I want you talking for days."

"I want," Maren said, and her voice was gravel but it carried, "to go home."

"You can't." Vess sat back. "Home is a problem we're going to solve together, you and I. So let's begin simply. You're the elder of an enclave of the Gifted. How many of you are there?"

Maren drank. Slowly. Making the captain wait. I found myself almost smiling despite everything — there was a whole language of insolence available to the powerless, and Maren spoke it fluently.

"Fewer than you've burned," she said.

Vess didn't flinch. "How many?"

"Count your smoke."

The veteran on the left shifted his weight. Vess raised one finger without looking at him, and he went still again. She leaned forward, elbows on knees, and studied Maren the way I'd seen her study tracks — patiently, reading what others missed.

"You misunderstand your position," Vess said. "You think this is a negotiation. It isn't. You think your silence protects anyone. It doesn't — I already know where the enclaves are; your cartographer friend drew me maps for weeks. What your silence does is decide *how* this goes. Gently or otherwise. And I should tell you, grandmother, I have burned gentler people than you for less interesting answers."

"They told me about you," Maren said. "In the enclaves. The huntress who sings off-key."

My stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

"The children used to sing it," Maren went on, conversational, awful. "*Seren, Seren, sings so fine, if she finds you, don't you hide.* They thought it was funny. A hunter who can't hold a tune. We thought it meant you were harmless." Her good eye came up and found me across the tent. "We were wrong about a lot of things."

"Maren—" I started.

"Quiet," Vess said, without heat, without turning around. That was somehow worse than shouting. "You'll speak when spoken to, tracker. Or have you forgotten which side of this table you're on?"

I hadn't forgotten. That was the whole rot of it. I knew exactly which side I was on, because I'd spent years on it, singing my flat little songs over the heads of people I was hunting, telling myself the order mattered more than the arithmetic. And then frost had bloomed out of my own fingers in front of forty witnesses, and the arithmetic had come for me too, and now I stood roped at the back of a tent watching an old woman decide how much truth to feed the thing that had eaten my life.

Because that's what I understood, watching Maren's good eye move — she wasn't resisting. Not really. Resistance was for people who wanted to win. Maren was *choosing*. Choosing what Vess got to know, and in what shape, and I realized with a cold slide of horror that she was going to give her something. The only question was whether it would be poison or bread.

"So," Vess said. "The valley. My scouts report the mist won't let them past the ridge line. My maps show roads that aren't there and lose roads that are. My prisoners—" a small gesture that included me, generously, as if I were furniture she owned, "—tell me it's just superstition. Old wards. Tell me why a valley needs wards, grandmother. Wards keep things out. What is your valley keeping *in*?"

And there it was. The question. Dominic had tried to tell me on the road, in his careful cartographer's way, drawing lines in ash: *the valley isn't hiding from the world, Seren, the world is being kept safe from what's under the valley.* I hadn't fully believed him. Belief is heavy, and I was carrying enough.

Maren was quiet for a long time. The brazier popped. Somewhere outside, horses complained about the flies.

Then she laughed. Low, dry, genuinely amused, and I saw Vess's jaw tighten because laughter was not in her script.

"You think it's a prison," Maren said. "You've been marching toward it for months thinking you're storming a jail. Breaking the lock. Freeing the land from old superstition." She shook her head. "Captain. Captain. Let me tell you what you're actually carrying in your saddlebags."

"Enlighten me."

"A war." Maren leaned forward, and for a moment the stick-bundle straightened into something older and harder, and her voice filled the tent the way it used to fill the hollow. "Before your crown, before your kingdom, before your precious maps — there was a war fought with magic the way you fight with fire, except fire has the decency to burn out. This magic didn't. It *fed*. On us. On the Gifted. It drank talent like wine and left husks, and it grew, and the ones who survived it did the only thing left. They buried it. Sealed it under stone and thorn and silence, and they built a valley on top of the grave and swore their bloodlines to tend it forever. Your valley isn't a refuge, Captain. It's a lid."

Vess was very still. I'd served under her long enough to read her stillness — most officers fidget when they think, but Vess went motionless, the way water goes still before it freezes.

"A lid," she repeated.

"And you," Maren said, "are holding the crowbar."

Silence. The brazier popped again. One of the veterans glanced at the other.

"That's a story," Vess said at last. "A good one. Old woman's cosmology. Why would I believe it?"

"Believe it or don't." Maren shrugged, and the shrug cost her something — I saw her wince where the guards had handled her. "It changes nothing either way. The seal holds or it doesn't. But here's what your belief decides, Captain: whether you march three hundred soldiers onto a grave out of ignorance, or out of ambition. Because I can see you're already doing the sums. I can see it in your face. War-magic that never burns out. An army that drinks it never loses. Your crown has been fighting border wars since before you were born, and losing half of them, and here you sit, one valley away from winning all of them forever."

"Don't presume to know my—"

"You took the bait the moment I said *war*," Maren said, merciless. "Your pulse is in your throat. Don't be ashamed. It's a good trap. It was set for kings and it caught a captain, and it will catch the king too, because it's not even a trap, it's just the truth wearing its best clothes. The magic down there is real. It is powerful beyond anything your crown has ever touched. And it can be taken. All of those things are true." She paused, and her good eye glittered. "What's also true is that the last people who took it fed their own children to it within a generation, because that's what it asks. But you don't need to hear that part yet. That part comes later. That part always comes later."

I wanted to scream. I wanted to cross the tent and shake her — *why are you telling him this, why are you handing him the sword* — but I couldn't move, couldn't speak, and worse, worse than all of it, some cold part of me understood exactly what she was doing, and understood that it might even work.

Because Vess was not a fool. That was the tragedy of her. A fool would have laughed and called it witch-talk. Vess sat there turning it over, and I watched her do the officer's arithmetic, the same arithmetic she'd taught me on my first campaign: *every asset is a weapon, every weapon serves the crown, and mercy is just logistics you haven't priced yet.*

"If what you say is true," Vess said slowly, "then the hunt has been a waste of time. Burning hedge-witches and root-singers while the actual prize sits under one valley."

"Now you understand," Maren said softly. "You weren't hunting us, Captain. You were *thinning* us. Every Gifted you burned was one less voice that could have warned the rest. One less pair of hands that could have tended the seal. You've been doing the war-magic's work for it, these five years, and calling it piety." She smiled, and it was the saddest thing I had ever seen. "It doesn't need to corrupt you. It just needs you busy."

"Enough." Vess stood. For a moment she just looked down at Maren, and I swear on my mother's grave I saw doubt cross her face — real doubt, the first I'd ever seen there — before discipline closed over it like ice over water. "Guards. She goes in the wagon. Bound, gagged if she talks to anyone. If she's lying, she hangs at the valley. If she's telling the truth—" Vess picked up her gauntlets from the table. "Then the crown owes her a pension."

"And if it's true," Maren said to her back, "and you open it anyway?"

Vess stopped at the tent flap.

"Then I'll have won the crown's wars," she said. "That's the job."

She swept out. The veterans followed. The guard tugged my rope, and I dug my heels in for one stupid second, looking at Maren sitting alone by the brazier with her cup of water, and she looked back at me and mouthed something. Three words. I couldn't catch them. I've turned them over a thousand times since, trying every shape they might have been, and I still don't know, and I think that might be the cruelest part.

* * *

They put me in a supply wagon, which was either carelessness or contempt, and I lay in the dark between sacks of oats and listened to the camp break itself down around me.

Orders traveled. I heard them in fragments through the canvas: the vanguard moving at first light, the baggage train rerouted, riders sent south to the garrisons. And once, close to my wagon, Vess's voice, level and carrying, giving the one order I'll hear until I die:

"—no burning at the valley. Nothing burns. I want the ground intact. Whatever's under it, the crown takes it *whole*."

Whole. Not destroyed. Harvested.

I pressed my palms against my eyes until I saw stars. This was the horror of it, the thing I couldn't stop chewing: nothing Maren had said was a lie. That was the genius of the trap she'd sprung, or fallen into, or both — every word true, and every word a blade. The war-magic was real. The crown's wars were endless. The prize was real. And so Vess had marched out of that tent not as a witch-hunter anymore but as something infinitely more dangerous: a believer with a mission statement. Five years of burning had been ugly. Ugly things can be stopped by shame. But this — this was *noble* now. End the border wars. Save a generation of soldiers. Win the peace the crown had promised since before any of us were born. Who wouldn't march for that? I would have marched for that, a year ago. I had marched for less.

That's how it works. That's what none of the enclave stories ever said plainly, because the elders were too proud: evil doesn't need to recruit you. It just needs to hand you a flag and point you at something. The war-magic under the valley didn't need to whisper to Vess in dreams. It had waited a thousand years for a woman with good boots and a bad arithmetic to come dig it up herself, believing all the way down that she was saving the world.

And we had told her how.

Dominic's face rose behind my eyelids — wherever he was tonight, ahead of us on the road or behind us in the dark, running home to warn them. He'd know by now. Word travels faster than armies when the word is *they're coming*. He'd be standing in the hollow with Maren's people, drawing lines in ash again, except this time the lines would be walls, and the walls would have to hold against three hundred soldiers who thought they were liberators.

Three hundred soldiers who thought they were liberators, and one of them was me, until the frost came out of my fingers, and the difference between me and them was one bad night and a lie from a man who loved me.

I lay back against the oats and did the only arithmetic left to me. Two days' march to the ridge line. One day to breach the mist, if it let them — and it wouldn't, it couldn't, unless—

Unless. There was always an unless. The seal needed tending, Maren had said once, in another life. Tended by bloodlines sworn to it. Bloodlines like Dominic's. And the crown's army was coming with wagons and ladders and faith, and somewhere in that equation was a crack, and everyone in this story was going to spend the next chapter of it trying to be the one who decided where the crack opened.

Near dawn, the wagon lurched into motion. Through a tear in the canvas I watched the twelfth moon sink fat and orange behind the hills — the last night of the old count, the elders used to say, though I'd never asked what it counted down to. I knew now. God help me, I knew now.

Two days to the valley. One moon left.

Behind me, in the prisoner's cart, I heard Maren begin, very quietly, to sing. Some old thing in a language I didn't know, worn smooth as river stone. The guards shouted at her to stop. She didn't. And off-key, flat as a dropped plate, I sang along — because it was the only weapon I had left, and because somewhere ahead of us a man was waiting, and because the song, whatever it meant, was older than the crown and it was not on Vess's side.

The army marched east, toward the morning, toward the lid.

And I rode with it, counting moons.
