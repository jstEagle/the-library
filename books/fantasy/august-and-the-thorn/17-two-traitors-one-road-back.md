## 17. Two Traitors, One Road Back

### Dominic

The army moved like a wound through the country — a red line on no map I'd ever drawn, and I could have drawn it in my sleep by then, because we were riding just ahead of it.

Three days ahead, then two, then one and a half, which is not a measurement any sane cartographer would admit to. Vess had stopped being cautious somewhere around the elder's capture and started being *fast*, and fast was worse. Cautious men ask questions. Fast men burn things while you're still composing the answer.

"We can't outrun them," Seren said, hunched over her horse's neck as we came down out of the marsh fens into country that finally remembered it was summer. "We can only arrive first."

"Arrive where?"

"Everywhere." She said it without looking at me, the way she said most true things. "You know the enclaves. You've been drawing their false maps for months. So draw me a route — every hearth that will light a beacon when they see the army coming, every family that needs to be gone before the vanguard crests their hill."

I looked at her. She'd lost weight since the marshes; we both had. There was marsh-grass still tangled in her braid and she hadn't bothered to remove it, and somehow that — the tangle, the not-bothering — made my chest hurt more than the pursuit did.

"You want me to map my own betrayals," I said. "In reverse."

"I want you to map your *amends*," she said. "There's a difference. It's mostly direction."

So I did what I do. I closed my eyes, let the gift rise up through me like water finding its level, and watched the ink move across the page in my mind's eye — north to the hazel-hollow where the Tallow family kept bees that hummed warnings, east to the stone circle where old Maren taught children letters that rearranged themselves, west along the river to the ferryman who was three people wearing one coat. The road unspooled ahead of us, and for once I wasn't ashamed of what it showed.

We rode through the night. We rode through the next day. And somewhere past the second dawn, with the army's dust visible behind us like a second horizon, Seren pulled up her horse and said, "Stop. We need to talk before we get there."

"Talk while riding?"

"No." She dismounted. Walked to the stream bank. Sat down on a flat stone with the particular finality of someone preparing to say something terrible. "Sit down, Dominic. I'm going to tell you what I've done, and I'd rather not fall off a horse doing it."

*

Here is what she told me, sitting on that stone with her hands flat on her knees like a child at lessons:

She told me about the first hunt, four years ago, when she was nineteen and so proud of her tracking ribbon that she wore it to bed. A gifted woman in a mill town, accused of souring milk. Seren found her hiding in a root cellar. The woman had magic enough to kill her — Seren knew it even then, felt it like heat off a forge — and instead the woman had simply held up her hands and said, *you're one of us, girl, can't you feel it?* And Seren had reported her anyway. Had stood watch while they took her away. Had gotten a commendation.

She told me about the twins in the fen country, age eleven, who could speak to eels. She told me about the old man whose shadow moved independently, whom she tracked for six weeks because he was clever, and how when she finally cornered him he'd looked almost relieved, and how she'd thought about that relief for two years without understanding it until she started growing frost on her own fingers.

She told me about the orchard. Not the burning — I'd seen that, we'd both stood in that ash — but the part after, the part I hadn't known. That this wasn't the first time she'd disobeyed an order. That there had been a boy in Corrow's Ford, two years earlier, and she'd smuggled him out in a grain cart, and Vess had never found out, and Seren had carried the secret like a stone in her boot ever since, walking on it every day.

"And now you know," she finished. Her voice didn't shake, which somehow made it worse. "Everything. Every door I kicked in, every person I handed over, every night I slept fine afterward because I was *good at my job*. You wanted honesty as sanctuary. Well. Here's the sanctuary. It smells like smoke."

I sat with it. I sat with all of it, and I'll be honest — some animal part of me flinched. Some part of me that remembered the thorn-warding that killed Aldous the hunter, that remembered families running into hills because of maps I drew, wanted to point at her and say *you*, the way she might point at me.

But here's the arithmetic nobody teaches you: guilt isn't a currency you can trade. Her sins don't cancel mine. They just sit beside them, two piles of ash, and you either build on the ground between them or you don't.

"The woman in the mill cellar," I said. "What was her name?"

Seren blinked. "Marta. Why?"

"Because if you're going to carry her, carry her name, not just the weight." I looked at the stream, because looking at her was suddenly difficult. "And because I have something to confess too, and I'd rather do it while you're still deciding whether to hate me."

"Dominic—"

"The bargain with the elder." I made myself say it plainly, no varnish, the way you pull a splinter. "It wasn't charity. Yes, I traded false maps for answers about the seal, but understand what I actually offered. I offered to keep feeding the hunt *just enough* real finds to keep Vess trusting me. I planned it. I sat down and calculated how many gifted strangers I could spend to protect the many, and I picked the number, Seren. Three. I decided three people's freedom was an acceptable price, and one of them — the cooper from Harrow Fen — I never even learned his name before they took him."

The stream went on being a stream. Birds did bird things. The world declined to end on schedule.

"That's why you passed the trap route," she said quietly.

"That's why I passed the trap route."

"And you're telling me this now because—"

"Because you gave me Marta's name and the twins and the boy in the grain cart, and it would be cowardice to take your confession and hoard mine like ammunition." I finally looked at her. "You asked me once why I helped the enclaves. The ugly truth is I don't fully know. Part survival, part guilt, part — I don't know, some stubborn belief that a map should protect people instead of hunting them. But I know it wasn't clean. Nothing I've done since the hunters put irons on me has been clean. If you're choosing me, choose *this* version. The one who does the math and hates himself and keeps doing it anyway."

Seren was quiet for a long moment. Then she reached over and took my hand — the ink-stained one, the guilty one — and turned it palm up, and studied it like a page.

"Marta used to bake," she said. "Before. She'd leave loaves on windowsills for people who couldn't afford bread, and nobody knew it was her, because the loaves just... appeared. That's what I think about. Not the cellar. The loaves." She closed my fingers back over my palm. "You're not the worst thing you've done, Dominic Kavanagh. Neither am I. Now get up. Maren's stone circle won't warn itself."

And that was it. No absolution, no ceremony. Just two traitors on one road, each carrying the other's worst knowledge like a lantern — heavy, but at least it lit something.

I have loved her since the fire in the orchard ashes, if I'm honest, maybe since the off-key singing. But I didn't know I trusted her until that stream bank. Love is a feeling. Trust is a decision you make with full information, and I made it there, sitting in the mud, holding the hand of a woman who had hunted my kind and chosen to stop.

### Seren

He fell asleep mid-sentence that night, which was fair, because I'd fallen asleep mid-sentence the night before, and mid-sentence the night before that. Exhaustion makes honest people of us all.

We'd warned the hazel-hollow — the Tallow family and their bees were already gone when we arrived, which meant someone else had gotten word out first, which meant the network was alive, which meant hope, actual hope, the kind I'd spent four years professionally stamping out. Old Maren at the stone circle had refused to leave — "I am eighty-one years old, girl, let the crown come collect me, I'll make it expensive" — but she'd taken our warning and given us bread and, more usefully, given Dominic a sealed letter for the ferryman, and the ferryman turned out to indeed be three people in one coat, and by the third evening of riding we'd done what we set out to do.

Every enclave warned. Every hearth that would listen, listening. Behind us, Vess's army found empty houses and cold ovens and, in one memorable case, a barn full of bees that had been instructed to be unhelpful.

Which left us, on the fourth night, alone at last. Truly alone — no pursuit close enough to matter by morning, no errand left, nothing between us and the valley road except the valley road itself, and everything we hadn't said yet.

Dominic was asleep. I lay beside him watching the fire and thinking about the strange arithmetic of confession — how you spend a secret and expect to feel lighter, and sometimes you do, and sometimes you just feel *spent*.

He stirred. "You're staring at me."

"You snore in iambic pentameter. It's very literary."

"I do not snore." He rolled onto his side. Firelight caught the ink stains on his fingers, permanent now, I suspected, dyed into the skin like a stain on the soul made visible. "You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you look at me like you're memorizing me for later. You did it in the orchard too. Right before you said something brave."

I hadn't known I did it. But he was right, and I had something brave queued up, so apparently my face had been keeping minutes of the meeting without me.

"When I was nineteen," I said, "the night after I reported Marta, I sang to the horses. Off-key, like always. And one of them — a gray mare named Juniper — she leaned her head against my chest while I sang, and I remember thinking, *she doesn't care that I'm bad at this. She just wants the voice.*" I pulled my knees up. "I've been thinking about that mare a lot lately. Because that's what my gift is, I think. Not frost, not the dreams. The voice. Things lean toward me. Animals, wards, the thorn-wards in the marsh that let us pass when they should have torn us apart. I sing off-key and the world leans in anyway."

Dominic was very still. "You think that's why the wards spared us."

"I think that's why *everything* spares us, half the time, and neither of us has wanted to say it out loud because saying it makes it a plan, and plans can fail."

"Seren." He sat up. In the firelight his face had that look — the cartographer's look, the one where the terrain shifts under him and he starts redrawing. "If the seal cracks — when it cracks — Vess thinks the war-magic is a weapon. The elder says it feeds on the gifted. But if things *lean toward you* — if the magic itself recognizes you—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "No. I'm not doing this. I'm not turning you into a variable."

"You were going to say I might be able to hold it. Or close it. Or something."

"I was going to say I don't know, and I refuse to find out by using you."

"Well." I reached over and poked him in the sternum, which is a technique I learned from him, actually, back when he was my prisoner and insufferable about it. "Then here's my counter-confession, cartographer, and it's the last one: I'm not going to promise to stand behind you at that seal. I'm not going to promise to run, or hide, or be sensible. I'm going to be there, next to you, whatever the thorn asks for. That's not a variable. That's a fact. Map around it."

For a long moment he just looked at me. And I watched him do the math — I could practically see the numbers behind his eyes, the risk ledgers, the terrible gift of his that always knew where things were, including, presumably, the exact location of every way this could go wrong.

Then he laughed. Quietly, tiredly, but really laughed, for the first time since the marshes.

"You know what my mother used to say?" he said. "'A map is just a love letter to a place.' I thought it was nonsense when I was twelve." He took my hand. "It wasn't nonsense. I just hadn't met you yet."

"That was smooth. Did you practice that while I was asleep?"

"Three drafts. The first two were terrible."

I kissed him then, firelight and ink-stains and all of it, the whole ruined honest architecture of us, and if the world ended the next day I at least intended to arrive at the ending having said every true thing I owned.

We broke camp before dawn. The valley road ran south and slightly downhill, and by midmorning we could see it — the green line of the ridgeline, the haze above it that wasn't quite haze, the place where my dreams had always lived before I knew they were memories.

Dominic reined in at the top of the rise and went pale.

"What?" I said. "What is it?"

He didn't answer right away. He pulled out the little traveling journal he always carried, opened it flat, and I saw the ink moving — not smearing, *moving*, crawling across the page like something alive and urgent, sketching lines he wasn't drawing.

"It's showing me the valley," he said. His voice had gone strange. "Except — Seren, these aren't the valley's borders. These are the *seal's*. And look—" He turned the page toward me, and I saw it: twelve marks arranged in a ring, like moons, like hours, and the twelfth mark was nearly closed. Nearly a circle. "The twelfth moon sets tomorrow night. The thirteenth turn comes after. And the ink says—" He swallowed. "The ink says Vess will reach the valley before we do."

Behind us, far to the north, a smudge of dust rose over the ridge we'd crossed yesterday. Ahead of us, home. Between us and it, one night, one army, and a circle almost closed.

I turned my horse south. "Then we ride faster than the truth," I said, "for once in our lives."

And we rode.
