## 19. Thirteenth Turn

The moon was almost whole when we reached the seal.

I want to say I remember the climb with clarity — every switchback, every handhold, every prayer I didn't know how to say. But memory is a dishonest cartographer. It draws what it wants to keep and leaves the rest blank. What I actually remember is this: Dominic's hand in mine, cold as river stone; the thorn-hedge rising out of the dark like a wall built by something that hated walls; and above us, twelve moons wheeling toward their impossible thirteenth sister, who hung low and silver and wrong over the valley's heart.

"Almost there," Dominic said, for the third time.

"You've said that since the marshes."

"I'm a mapmaker. We repeat ourselves when we're frightened."

He smiled when he said it, but his eyes were on the hedge, and there was something in his face I'd learned to read on the road — the look he got when his ink moved without permission, when the world told him things he hadn't asked to know. He knew this place. Not from maps. From blood.

The battle behind us had gone quiet in the way battles do when they're only pretending to be finished. Vess's forces had breached the valley at moonset, and for six hours we'd fought — or rather, they'd fought; I'd done what I could with frost and fury while Dominic turned his charts into weapons, folding roads shut, unfolding chasms where none should be. The man had drawn a gorge into existence between two hillsides and then apologized to it. That's Dominic. Even his miracles have manners.

But Vess had come through anyway. Vess always came through. That was the thing about Captain Ilse Vess that no one understood until it was too late: she wasn't cruel, and she wasn't mad, and she wasn't even ambitious, not really. She was *certain*. And certainty, I've learned, is more dangerous than any of those. Cruelty can be reasoned with. Madness can be outrun. Certainty just keeps walking.

"She'll follow us here," Dominic said now, watching the treeline below the hedge. "She knows where we're going. She's always known."

"Then we don't have much time."

"No." He looked at me then, and there it was again — that expression I'd been catching all week, the one I'd filed away as exhaustion or fear. "Seren. If something happens—"

"If something happens, you'll say something maudlin and I'll tell you off for it, and then we'll both be embarrassed. Save it."

"Seren—"

"The moon's turning, Dom. Whatever you have to do, do it fast, and whatever you have to say—" My voice did something traitorous and cracked. "Say it after."

* * *

The seal-thorn stood at the valley's center like a tree that had given up pretending to be a tree. It was black iron and living wood at once, branches twisted into knots no rope could tie, and where the light of the thirteen moons touched it, the bark gleamed wet and silver, as if the whole thing were sweating moonlight. The air around it hummed. Not a sound exactly — more a pressure, the way a held note hums in your teeth.

Dominic approached it the way you approach a sleeping animal. Slowly. Palms open. Like an apology.

I kept watch at the hedge-line, because someone had to, and because watching him do this — watching him stand before the thing his ancestors had made, the thing his blood was made to answer — felt too intimate to witness directly. Some doors you shouldn't watch people open.

Below us, torchlight moved through the orchards. The enclave folk had fallen back to the inner hills. Somewhere down there, the elder was either dead or caged, and I couldn't let myself think about which, because if I thought about it I would go back down into that fire, and someone had to stay up here and guard the one person in the world who could close the door Vess wanted opened.

That was my job now. Not hunting the gifted. Guarding them.

Funny how a life turns. You spend years learning to track people by the way they break twigs, and then one day you're using everything you know to keep a soft-spoken mapmaker alive, and you realize you never once tracked anyone you loved. Love doesn't leave tracks. Love walks on water and leaves nothing behind, and you spend your whole life following footprints wondering why yours never show.

Frost prickled along my knuckles. I let it. On the road, Dominic had taught me not to fight it — *it's not a dog to be broken, Seren, it's weather; you don't train weather, you dress for it* — and so I dressed for it, breathing slow, letting the cold pool in my palms like water I might need later.

I would need it later. I didn't know that yet. Or maybe some part of me did, the part that had dreamed of a sealed valley in silver, the part that woke with frost on its fingers and dread in its chest. Dreams are just maps of places you haven't been yet. Nobody tells you that until you arrive.

"Torchlight," I said. "West slope. Moving fast."

"How fast?"

"Vess-fast."

Dominic closed his eyes. His palm hovered an inch from the thorn's black bark. "Then I need to start now."

"What do you need?"

"Time." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, thin as wire. "And for you to not watch."

"Too late. I've been watching you since the mill bridge."

"That was you? At the bridge?" He opened his eyes, and for one absurd moment, with an army climbing the hill toward us and the end of the world humming three feet away, he looked genuinely delighted. "You sang. Off-key. I thought a cat was dying."

"It was a dirge for your dignity."

"I love you," he said.

Not *I love you too*. Not a reply. A first strike, thrown now, at the worst possible moment, because he was a coward about everything except the truth. The frost in my hands went hot, which frost has no business doing.

"Don't," I said. "Don't you dare say that standing in front of a door you're about to walk through alone."

"I'm not walking through it alone. I'm just the one holding the key." He pressed his palm flat against the thorn.

The hum changed pitch.

* * *

Here is what I saw, and I report it exactly, because after tonight no one will believe it and I need at least one honest witness, even if the witness is only paper:

When Dominic's blood met the seal, the thorn *woke*. Silver light ran up its branches like dawn running up a mountain range, and the air split open with a sound I felt in my sternum before I heard it — a deep, grinding groan, stone against stone, a door that had been shut for a thousand years remembering how to be shut harder. The thirteen moons overhead seemed to lean in. All that light bent toward the thorn, and through it I glimpsed — just for a heartbeat — what the seal held back.

A war. Not a memory of war. War itself, coiled and patient, feeding on the gifted the way a fire feeds on green wood, waiting for enough fuel to be gathered. Waiting for a crown to build it a pyre out of everyone like me.

And then Vess stepped out of the trees.

She was alone. Of course she was. She'd left her soldiers at the hedge line — she'd learned on the march that gifted ground eats armies, but it makes room for one woman with a sword and a certainty. Her armor was scorched. There was ash in her cropped hair. She walked toward us across the silver-lit clearing with the unhurried gait of someone who has already won and is simply arriving at the location of her victory.

"Seren." She stopped ten paces off. Her voice was almost gentle. It always was, right before the worst of it. "Step away from him."

"He's saving your world, Captain."

"He's *closing* it." She drew her sword — slowly, ceremonially, because Vess believed even violence deserved proper form. "You've seen what's behind that seal. So have I, now. The elder showed me. Do you know what I saw? Power enough to end every war for a thousand years. Power enough to make sure no village ever burns again, no family ever flees into hills, no child ever—" Her jaw tightened. "They burned children, Seren. In that orchard. Because we were ordered to. I have carried that order in my gut for a year. And you want me to believe the answer is to lock that power away forever and keep burning whoever the crown points at next?"

"Yes," I said. "Because power like that doesn't get spent. It spends *you*. That's the whole trick of it. That's what the elder tried to tell you."

"The elder lied."

"The elder confessed. There's a difference. Lying takes talent. Confession just takes losing everything." I stepped between her and Dominic, and the frost came up my arms unbidden, silvering my sleeves, and I watched her see it — really see it, the gift she'd spent her life hunting, wearing the face of her best tracker. "You trained me, Captain. Every trick I know, you gave me. So you know I won't bluff. You know I don't miss. Step aside."

Something moved behind her eyes. Grief, maybe. Or the ghost of grief, in a woman who'd amputated hers years ago.

"I taught you to track liars," she said. "You're standing in front of one."

Behind me, the thorn screamed — a high, splintering note, and the silver light guttered. Dominic cried out, and I heard him say, half-delirious, "*It wants more, it wants something heavier, it's not enough, my memories aren't enough—*" and I understood, with the cold clarity that comes once in a life, what was happening.

The seal was cracking. The moon was completing its turn. And the price was being paid right now, in front of me, and I could feel — I don't know how, but I could *feel*, the way you feel weather coming in a scar — what it was taking from him. What it was going to take.

Him. Us. Everything we were to each other.

No.

"Last chance, Seren," Vess said, and raised her sword.

"Captain," I said, "you taught me one thing worth keeping. You said: *the best fighters lose well.*"

I turned my gift on her.

I had never done it deliberately — the frost had always been weather, accident, defense. But Dominic had taught me to aim it, and grief is the finest whetstone there is. The cold left me in a single exhaled breath and hit Vess like a wave hitting a seawall. Ice sheathed her blade arm to the shoulder. Frost climbed her breastplate in blooming white ferns. She staggered — she actually staggered, Captain Ilse Vess, who had never in her life been moved by anything she didn't choose to be moved by —

— and drove forward through it.

That was the thing about her certainty. It didn't feel cold. Cold was *mine*. Hers burned. She came through my winter like it was a curtain, sword first, and I was too slow, too spent, too busy pouring everything I had into the ice, and the blade took me under the ribs.

The world tilted. The clearing went sideways. I heard someone scream and understood distantly that it was me, and then that it wasn't — it was Dominic, tearing his hand free of the thorn, the seal still unfinished, still *cracking*, silver light bleeding out of it like light from a wound.

Vess stood over me, sword dripping, and her face — gods, her face. Not triumph. Horror, arriving late, the way it always does. "Seren—"

"You lose well," I told her, and laughed, and blood came with it, warm and shocking in the cold air. "See? Best lesson you ever gave me."

Behind her, the thorn's light flared blinding. Dominic was shouting words I couldn't parse — old words, ancestral words, his blood singing through them — and the thirteenth moon reached the top of its arc, and the seal *cracked*.

Not broke. Cracked. A seam of darkness opened down the thorn's trunk, thin as a knife cut, and through it I felt the war lean close — vast, hungry, *interested* — and then Dominic threw himself at the gap with both hands and both bloodlines and everything he had left, and the crack began, slowly, agonizingly, to close.

Vess took one step toward him. Two. Her frozen arm shattered the ice and rose, sword leveling at his back.

I had maybe one frost left in me. Maybe less than one.

I spent it all.

The last of my gift left me in a single lance of cold that caught Vess's sword arm at the elbow and locked it solid, and she roared — actually roared, the certainty finally cracking under something truer — and spun toward me, and I smiled at her, because that's the last weapon of the dying and I've always been frugal.

"Seren." Dominic was at my side now, on his knees, hands pressing the wound, doing the useless mathematics of pressure and blood. His face was wet. When had he started crying? "Stay. Stay with me. Look at me. Seren—"

"Did it hold?" My voice sounded far away. Everything did. The moons were very bright. Thirteen of them. Who designs a sky with thirteen moons? An optimist. Only an optimist.

"It held. It's sealed. Seren, it's sealed, you have to stay—"

"Good." I got a hand up — it shook terribly — and found his collar, and pulled him down until his forehead touched mine. "Listen to me, mapmaker. Whatever it took from you. Whatever it's still taking. I'll remember for both of us. That's the deal. That's my bargain, and I drive a harder bargain than you do, and I never once lost a negotiation in my life, so don't you dare argue—"

"I'm not arguing," he whispered. "I never win arguments with you."

"Correct. Write that down. Put it on a map."

His laugh broke in the middle. Around us the silver light was fading, the thorn going quiet, the thirteenth moon sliding past its zenith, and somewhere below us the battle was ending or beginning or simply stopping mattering, and Vess was on her knees in the frost with her sword arm dead and her certainty dead with it, staring at us like a woman reading a map of a country she'd destroyed.

"Seren," Dominic said. "Seren, stay."

"Always," I said. "That was always the plan."

The moons blurred. The cold took me gently, the way snow takes a field — not violently, just completely. The last thing I saw was his face, memorizing it, hoarding it, because I had made him a promise: *whatever it took, I'll remember for both of us.*

I intended to keep it.

I intended—

* * *

I woke to birdsong and the smell of bread.

There will be those who say no one comes back from a wound like mine. Those people have never been healed by a valley full of the gifted, working through the night, spending gifts they'd hidden for generations on a huntress who used to hunt them. I owe them everything. I intend to spend my whole life owing it.

What matters is this: I woke. Three days later, in a bed in the elder's house, bandaged, aching, alive. And the first thing I did — before bread, before water, before I could even sit up — was reach for my memories of Dominic the way you reach for a lamp in a dark room.

They were all there. The mill bridge. The campfires. The marshes. His terrible jokes. The way he said *I love you* like a man confessing to a crime.

All there. Every one.

I lay back and laughed until my stitches hurt, and then I cried until they hurt worse, and then I got up, and I went to find him.

He was in the garden.

He was kneeling among the bean rows with a trowel in his hand, helping old Marta stake the vines, and when he saw me he stood, and smiled, polite and pleasant and empty as a cleared room, and said:

"Hello. You must be the one who was hurt. Are you well? Marta says you saved us all."

The beans swayed. The birds went on singing. Somewhere in my chest, a promise I'd made to a dying woman — to myself, to him — settled its weight on my shoulders like a yoke.

*Whatever it took, I'll remember for both of us.*

He remembered nothing. Nothing at all.

But I remembered everything. And I had made a bargain, and I drive a hard bargain, and I have never once lost a negotiation in my life.

So I wiped my face, and I picked up a trowel, and I knelt beside him in the dirt, and I said:

"Tell me, stranger. How do you feel about long stories?"
