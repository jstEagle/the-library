## 21. A Stranger at My Door

They tell you, in the hunt, how to knock on a door you mean to breach. Three raps, flat of the palm, no rhythm — a rhythm invites an answer, and an answer is a variable. I had knocked on a hundred doors that way. Doors with families behind them. Doors with children hiding in cellars. Doors that opened onto screaming.

I knocked on Dominic Kavanagh's door the same way, because my hands had forgotten any other grammar.

It was the wrong way to knock on the door of a man who used to make me laugh by drawing maps of places that didn't exist and insisting they did.

The door opened, and there he was. Alive. Whole. Unscarred, which seemed like an obscenity given what the rest of us carried. He looked at me the way a man looks at weather — noting it, adjusting to it, entirely unmoved by it.

"Can I help you?" he said.

His voice was the same. That was the worst part. The same low, careful voice that had once said *Seren* like the word was a place he wanted to be. Now it said *can I help you*, and meant: *you are a stranger, and strangers get directions, not welcome.*

"I—" My throat did something complicated. "I heard you were alive."

"I am." He tilted his head, polite, faintly puzzled. "Have we met? You look as though you're deciding whether to arrest me."

That almost got a laugh out of me, which would have been its own kind of tragedy, because he'd said something nearly identical the first time we'd truly spoken, on the road, when we were both liars pretending to be colleagues. He didn't know that. He couldn't. I had watched him feed every memory of me into the thorn like a man paying off a debt with his own blood, coin by coin, while I stood there with frost burning through my palms and could do nothing but watch him forget me in real time.

"You could say we've met," I managed.

"Then I'm sorry," he said, and he sounded genuinely sorry, which was somehow worse than indifference. "I'm told I've lost... some things. A year, mostly. People. There's a gap where certain faces should be." He touched his temple, embarrassed. "The healer says the memories may come back. She says it gently, the way people say things they don't believe."

Behind him, the workshop smelled of ink and paper and woodsmoke, exactly as it always had. His mother's chair sat empty by the hearth. On the table, half-finished, lay a map of the valley — our valley, the one that refused ink, drawn now in steady confident lines as though it had never held a seal or a war or a woman's dying breath.

He followed my eyes to it. "You know cartography?" he asked, brightening slightly, grasping for common ground the way drowning men grasp for driftwood.

"No," I said. "But I knew someone who did."

"He sounds useful," said the stranger wearing my love's face, and stepped aside to let me in out of the rain, because courtesy lived deeper in him than memory, and that small unearned kindness undid me more thoroughly than cruelty ever could have.

* * *

I stayed three days before I admitted I was staying.

There were practical reasons. My wounds from the siege were still knitting — the thorn's backlash had left silver scarring across both palms, and Vess's parting blow had cracked two ribs that healed crooked and ached when it rained. The valley had no other healers; its gifted folk had scattered after the resealing, gone to ground, and who could blame them. The crown's army had come and been swallowed, and now the outside world whispered about the Quiet Valley the way sailors whisper about reefs.

Also, there was no practical reason strong enough. I'll say that plainly. I stayed because leaving felt like a second death, and I had already attended enough funerals that season — the elder's, Vess's, the version of Dominic who had known my name.

He was courteous. That word will haunt me. He made up the spare room without being asked, remembered where the linens were kept though he couldn't remember why he knew that. Muscle memory, the healer called it. The body keeps what the mind loses. His hands still mixed ink perfectly. His fingers still found the seam of a map's border without looking. Once, passing me the bread at dinner, his knuckles brushed mine and he flinched — a small involuntary thing, quickly smoothed over — and apologized for it.

"It's all right," I said.

"I don't know why I did that," he said, troubled.

"I do," I said, and then lied: "Old habit. Soldiers flinch."

He accepted that. He accepted everything I told him about himself with the grave attentiveness of a scholar taking dictation. Yes, we'd traveled together. Yes, we'd escaped the witch-hunters. Yes, you fought beside your neighbors when the army came. He never asked the question I dreaded — *were we more than comrades?* — and I never answered it unprompted, and between those two mercies we built a silence the size of a cathedral.

At night I lay in the spare room listening to him move below — the scratch of pen, the creak of the floorboard by the window he always leaned on — and I told myself stories.

That's what grief does when it has no grave to visit. It becomes a storyteller. I narrated him back to himself in the dark. *Once there was a man who drew false maps to save lives, and lied so well to a captain that she marched her whole company in circles for a month.* Once there was a man who caught me singing off-key to frightened horses and didn't mock me, just hummed along worse, until the horses calmed and I was laughing. Once there was a man who looked at frost blooming from my terrified hands and said, *that's the most beautiful dangerous thing I've ever seen,* and meant both words equally.

The stories kept me company. They were poor substitutes. A story of bread is not bread.

* * *

On the ninth day, I caught him talking to the map.

I'd come down early, ribs aching, meaning to build up the fire before he woke. But he was already at the table, barefoot, hair uncombed, bent over the valley chart with his lips moving. Not reading aloud. Conversing. Pausing. Listening.

"Dominic?"

He straightened too fast, guilty. "Nothing. Thinking through survey lines."

"At dawn. To the paper."

"My mother talked to her work," he said, after a moment. "I'm told I do it too. Apparently it runs in the family, along with everything else I can't account for." He rubbed his eyes. "Sometimes I look at this valley and I feel — " He stopped. Started again. "You'll think I'm mad."

"I once fought a captain with ice I pulled out of my own bones," I said. "Madness and I have an arrangement."

That startled a real smile out of him — brief, lopsided, *his* smile, arriving like a bird blown off course into the wrong season. Then it faded under the weight of whatever he'd been about to confess.

"I feel like the map is waiting for me to remember something," he said quietly. "Not facts. The healer's facts are useless to me — names, dates, they slide off. This is different. It's more like..." He searched. "Like standing in a house you grew up in, after years away. You don't recall the rooms. But your feet know where the stairs are."

"And the valley is the house."

"The valley is the house," he agreed. "And there's a room in it I'm not allowed to see. Every time I try to look directly at — at whatever happened at the end, at the seal, at the thirteenth moon —" His hand drifted toward his temple again, that unconscious gesture. "Pain. Like a door locked from the inside. By me, I think. Or for me."

I kept my voice level. "Maybe some doors are locked for good reason."

"Maybe." He looked at me then, really looked, and for one vertiginous moment I thought — *he sees me, he knows me, the body remembers what the mind lost* — but it passed, and he was only being polite again. "You were there, weren't you. At the end. You don't talk about it either."

"No," I said. "I don't."

"We're a pair of locked doors, then." He said it lightly, but his eyes stayed on my scarred palms a moment longer than courtesy required. "Do those hurt? In the rain, I mean. You flex them when it storms."

*I flexed them the night you forgot me,* I didn't say. *I flex them every time you call me 'guest.'*

"A little," I said.

* * *

Winter came down off the hills and settled over the valley like a debt collector. I stayed. Of course I stayed. Leaving was unthinkable; staying was unbearable; I had learned from the best that these are often the same road walked in opposite directions.

I earned my keep the only way I knew how. I split wood badly and mended fences worse. I taught him, when he asked, how to hold a knife — he'd apparently forgotten that too, along with everything else, though his body remembered the footwork faster than his mind remembered anything, another betrayal by muscle memory, another ghost walking around in him without its name.

"You're a good teacher," he said one evening, breathing hard after a drill, snow in his hair.

"I had a patient student once."

"Was he any good?"

"He got better," I said. "Right up until the end."

He laughed, and the sound went through me like a blade finding the old crack in my ribs. Because it was *his* laugh. Memory hadn't taken that. Memory had taken me and left the laugh, the way a fire takes the house and leaves the chimney standing.

Some nights I sang. Off-key, always off-key, in the kitchen while he worked. I did it deliberately at first — a lure, a test, watching for the flicker of recognition. Nothing came. But I kept doing it anyway, past hope, past strategy, because the horses had liked it and because somewhere in the sealed room behind his eyes, maybe, something stirred.

One night he set down his pen mid-verse.

"That song," he said slowly. "Where's it from?"

"I don't know. Made it up, probably."

"No." He frowned at the table, at nothing. "No, I know this song. I don't know *how* — " His hand rose toward his temple and stopped halfway, trembling. "It hurts to reach for it."

"Then don't," I said quickly. "It's just a song."

"It's not just a song." He looked up at me, and there was something raw in his face, something straining against the lock. "Seren. Why do I know this song?"

My name. He'd said my name — not as a label, but the way you say a word you've been chewing for months without tasting. And I understood, suddenly, with terrible clarity, that I could not keep doing this. Could not live in his house as a ghost telling stories to a stranger, feeding him crumbs of himself and calling it charity. Either I told him the truth or I left, and I had already learned what leaving cost.

So I sat down across from him, and I took a breath, and I began.

"You wrote it," I said. "For me. After the orchard burned. You said I sang to steady the horses and it was the worst singing you'd ever heard, and then you wrote me a better song, and I refused to learn it on principle." My voice cracked; I let it. "We were on the run. We'd both lost everything. And somewhere between the marshes and the mountains, we stopped running from each other too."

He had gone very still. Ink dried forgotten on his pen.

"There was a seal," I went on, quieter. "Under the valley. Your blood forged it, generations back. It was breaking, and an army was coming, and the only way to close it was a memory heavy enough to balance what the war-magic weighed." I turned my scarred palms up between us. "You chose. You fed it every memory of me. Every single one. And it worked, and the valley closed, and you woke up polite."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, snow slid off the roof.

"I don't remember," he said at last, and his voice was wrecked and careful. "I believe you. God help me, I believe you — the way you say it, the way you look at me, like I'm a letter you keep rereading hoping the words changed." He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "But believing isn't remembering. I could believe every word and still be a stranger to you tomorrow. Is that what you want? Someone who loves you out of obligation to a story?"

"You already asked me that once," I whispered. "Almost word for word. The night before the siege. I gave the same answer, too."

"What was it?"

"I said: love the man you are now. The other one I can carry alone."

He lowered his hands. Looked at me for a long time — and I watched something move behind his eyes, some animal testing the bars of its cage, some door straining at a lock that even the thorn might not have made perfect.

"Seren," he said again, tasting it. "Tell me more. About him. About us. All of it — even the parts that hurt. *Especially* the parts that hurt."

"Why?"

"Because my feet know where the stairs are," he said, "and I want to find the room."

Outside, far off in the frozen hills, something answered him — faint, impossible, out of season. The sound of a single thorn-bud cracking open in the dead of winter, weeks before August, weeks before it should.

Neither of us moved. Neither of us breathed.

Then Dominic turned toward the window, toward the sound, and I saw his hand rise — not to his temple this time, but flat against the glass, as if greeting someone on the other side.
