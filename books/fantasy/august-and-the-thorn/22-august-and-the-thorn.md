## 22. August and the Thorn

### Dominic

A year is a long time to be a stranger in your own life.

I know this now the way I know the shape of my own hands: intimately, and with a certain professional resentment. A cartographer learns that a map is only ever a record of what someone chose to notice. My memory is a map like that. Whole territories have been left blank, and whoever drew it — me, apparently, or something wearing my blood — chose not to label the emptiness.

The valley in August smells of cut hay and river stone and, this year, of thorn.

That last one is new. The great bramble at the heart of the valley — the one I'm told I bled on, the one everyone else treats with the wary reverence you'd give a sleeping bear — has put out buds. White ones, tight as knuckles, weeks early. The elder's granddaughter swears the thorn hasn't bloomed since before her grandmother's grandmother, which is either wonderful or terrible depending on who you ask, and nobody will ask me, because I am the man who doesn't remember why he did any of it.

They're kind to me, the valley folk. That's the worst part. Kindness with a careful edge, the way you'd be kind to a soldier home from a war no one wants to describe. They tell me small things. That I liked my bread burnt on one side. That I hummed while I worked. That there was a woman.

They never say more than *a woman*, and I've stopped asking, because every time I ask, Seren goes quiet in a way that makes the whole room quiet, and I'd rather have noise than that particular silence.

Seren. She stayed. That much even my blank map knows. She lives in the cottage next to mine — my cottage, which she keeps saying is *ours*, a word I accept the way you accept weather. She mends fences and trains the valley's young people in things she won't name and sings while she works, always off-key, always unbothered by it. I find the singing unbearable and I would fight anyone who asked her to stop.

This morning she knocked on my door before dawn, which she does not do, because we have an arrangement as delicate as any treaty: she exists near me, and I exist near her, and neither of us names the thing between us because naming it might break it.

"Dominic." Her voice through the wood. "You need to see the thorn."

"It's barely light."

"It's blooming."

I was dressed before she finished the sentence. I didn't examine why. Some urgencies live deeper than memory.

* * *

### Seren

He came out barefoot, boots in hand, hair flattened on one side, looking exactly like the man I fell in love with and nothing like him at all, and I have gotten very good at holding both of those truths without dropping either.

"The buds," he said, when we reached the clearing. "They're white."

"They're white," I agreed.

"Thorns don't bloom white."

"This one does."

He crouched at the edge of the bramble — close enough to touch, far enough to be polite, a distance he keeps from everything now, including me — and studied it the way he studies everything, head tilted, eyes moving like he's drafting lines only he can see. The buds were everywhere. Thousands of them. In the grey pre-dawn they glowed faintly, the way snow holds light after sunset.

"When will they open?" he asked.

"No one knows. The old records say mid-autumn."

"And it's August."

"It's August," I said, and heard how my voice caught on the word, and hated myself for it, because he noticed. He notices everything except the one thing I need him to notice.

He stood. Brushed off his knees. And then, quietly, in the tone of a man asking about crop rotation: "Did I love you?"

The question hit me the way Vess's blade once hit my shoulder — clean, deep, meant. A year I had waited for him to ask it, rehearsing answers in the dark. And now that it came, all my rehearsals deserted me.

"Why do you ask that?" I managed.

"Because." He was still looking at the thorn. "When I look at these flowers, my chest hurts. Not grief exactly. Grief implies you know what you lost. This is more like—" He frowned, hunting the word. "Like a map with a burn mark. You can see where something used to be. You just can't read it anymore."

I could have told him everything. I have imagined telling him a thousand times — the mill bridge, the proclamation, the fire in the orchard, the marshes, the seal, his face as the memories left him and mine as I watched. I have composed the speech so many times it has worn smooth as river glass.

Instead I said, "Ask me again in autumn. When the blooms open."

He looked at me then, really looked, and whatever he saw in my face made him nod slowly, like a man agreeing to terms he suspects are unfair but can't prove.

"All right," he said. "Autumn."

And then the thorn bloomed early anyway.

* * *

### Dominic

It happened fast. One moment the buds were fists; the next, the nearest of them opened with a sound like a held breath released, and the scent rolled over us — honey and rain and something older underneath, something like iron left out in a storm — and I was somewhere else entirely.

Not somewhere. *Some-when.* I was standing on the mill bridge with ink-stained fingers and a commission letter I hadn't yet burned, watching a woman argue with a crown crier about the wording of a proclamation, and she was wrong about every clause and magnificent about all of them, and I thought — I distinctly remember thinking — *I would follow that voice into anything.*

Then the bridge was gone and I was on my knees in the grass with Seren's hands on my shoulders and my own tears running hot down my face, and I did not understand a single thing that was happening except that I was crying, hard, the way you cry at funerals for people you loved and cannot picture.

"Dominic. Dominic, breathe. What did you see?"

"A bridge," I gasped. "The mill bridge. There was a proclamation. There was a woman arguing with the crier about— about the legal definition of 'bearer,' she was quoting contract law at him, who *does* that—"

"You do," Seren said softly. "You did."

I looked up at her. Her face was wet too, though I hadn't seen her cry, and her hands were shaking against my shoulders, and behind her the whole bramble had begun to open, bloom after bloom after bloom, white and silent as falling snow going upward.

"What was her name?" I asked.

Seren laughed — a broken, beautiful sound. "Yours, if you want it. It was me, Dominic. That was us. That was the day we met."

* * *

### Seren

He cried for a long time. I held onto him and let him, because I learned in the marshes that some things you cannot fix, you can only witness.

When he finally spoke again, his voice was wrecked and steady at once, which is very him, whichever version of him is currently in residence.

"I don't remember it," he said. "I want to be clear about that. I saw it, the way you see a map of a place you've never been. But I don't *remember* it. And I'm—" His jaw worked. "I'm furious. At myself, presumably, for whatever I did. Because clearly I did something. You don't pay a year of yourself to buy silence unless the debt was enormous."

"You paid it to save everyone," I said. "Including me. Especially me."

"That sounds like me." He wiped his face with his sleeve, leaving a streak of grass stain across his cheekbone, and almost smiled. "The especially-me part. I was always sentimental about maps and insufferable about you."

"You don't know that."

"I know it the way I know north," he said simply. "I can't show my work. But I know it."

We sat in the wet grass while the thorn bloomed around us, and I told him. Not everything — not yet, not the seal, not the war-magic, not the way he fed our whole history into the bramble petal by petal while I screamed at him to stop. But the small things. The bridge. The proclamation. How he'd burned his maps rather than let the hunt use them. How he'd lied to Captain Vess with a straight face and to me with a crooked one. How off-key my singing was and how he'd pretended, badly, that it wasn't.

He listened the way he draws — completely, greedily, missing nothing.

"Tell me more," he said, when I ran out. "All of it. Even the parts that make me look bad."

"Especially those?"

"*Especially* those. If I sacrificed a year of myself, I want to know what kind of man earned it back."

So I told him about the false maps, and the stranger we lost to Vess's trap, and how he'd carried that guilt like a stone in his boot for months. I watched his face take each blow and hold. And when I got to the part where he'd chosen the world over me — when I described the seal closing and his eyes going empty and courteous — he reached over and took my hand, the first time he'd touched me in a year, and said:

"That was the stupidest brave thing I've ever done, and I don't remember doing it, and I already regret it more than I can measure."

"The thorn took the memories," I said. "It didn't take the man who made them. He's still in there. I've spent a year watching for him."

"Has he shown up?"

"He fixed my fence in April without being asked. He hums while he works. He burns his bread on one side." My voice went unsteady. "He shows up constantly. He just doesn't know it's him."

* * *

### Dominic

Here is what I understood, sitting in a field of impossible white blossoms with a woman's hand in mine and a year of my life gone to pay for a war no one will write down:

Memory is not the same as love. I had assumed they were the same thing — that losing one meant losing the other. But the thorn had taken my recollections and left the orientation intact. I was still pointed at her. Whatever instrument in me had found her across a crowded bridge, through a war, through a sacrifice I couldn't recall making — it still worked. It had been working all along, in the fence-mending and the bread-burning and the way I woke some mornings certain I'd dreamed of singing.

"I'm not going to get it back, am I," I said. "The year."

"Maybe not all of it." Seren nodded at the bramble. "It gave one back today. As interest, the elder called it. Maybe it's a lender, not a thief. Maybe it gives things back slow."

"Then I'll be here when it does."

She turned to look at me fully, and I saw hope in her face the way you see frost on a window — arrived overnight, fragile, transforming everything it touched.

"You'll stay," she said. "Knowing you don't remember. Knowing you might never."

"Seren." I tried her name out loud, properly, for the first time — not as a neighbor's word but as a weight, a coordinate, a place on the map. It fit my mouth like it had been waiting there. "I don't remember loving you. But I walked out here barefoot before dawn because you said the word *thorn*, and my chest hurts when I look at these flowers, and I know — the way I know north — that you are the truest thing on any map I've ever drawn. That's not memory. That's just me. Apparently I come this way."

She laughed and cried at the same time, which I suspect she'd done before, in a year I didn't have, and I resolved to be present for every such occasion going forward.

"Tell me the story again," I said. "From the bridge. Slowly. I want every clause."

"You hate clauses."

"I married my life to a woman who argues in clauses. I must have developed a taste for them."

* * *

### Seren

So I told him. All of it, this time — the seal, the war, the choice, everything — because the thorn had cracked its own door open, and I was done keeping my truth locked in a room he couldn't enter.

And as the sun came up over the Quiet Valley, gold on ten thousand white blooms, he listened with ink-stained fingers laced through mine, and when I reached the end — the empty eyes, the courteous stranger at the door — he squeezed my hand and said, "That man sounds like an idiot. Fortunately, I intend to replace him."

The thorn bloomed around us all through August, weeks early, petals opening one by one like a ledger being repaid.

And late that evening, as we walked back toward the cottages — *our* cottages, I decided I'd stop correcting him — the last bud of the day unfurled, and the wind carried its scent over us both, and Dominic stopped dead in the lane.

"What is it?" I asked.

His eyes had gone wide and bright, the way they did when his gift moved ink without permission.

"There's another one coming back," he whispered. "I can feel it. Not the bridge. Something else. Something with rain in it, and your voice, and—" He pressed a hand to his chest and laughed, astonished, tearful, alive. "Seren. I think it's giving us the orchard fire. I think it's giving us the night we finally told the truth."

I took his hand, and we stood together in the blooming dark, two people at the beginning of a map neither of us had drawn yet — and waited, side by side, for the past to come home one memory at a time.
