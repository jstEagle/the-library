## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The candle had burned down to its last knuckle of wax when Oscar finally admitted the arithmetic was lying to them.

Not the ordinary kind of lie — not a transposed figure or a smudged column, though the Guild of Impossible Things produced those in abundance. This was the other kind, the kind that lived in the margins and breathed. The entry before them read, in Master Veyren's own hand: *One dream, harvested from a sleeping fisherman, sold to the widow Corvane for the price of her grief.* Below it, in Oscar's careful script: *Paid in full.*

Except it wasn't. Grief did not come to a price. Grief came to you, night after night, wearing different shoes, and it never once asked what you owed.

Oscar set down the pen and pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes until the world went the color of embers. Around them, the counting room of the Guild settled into its nightly sounds — the tick of the great clock that ran backward on Thursdays, the sigh of shelves packed with ledgers so old their spines had gone soft as bread, the distant clatter of something in the reliquary that was either a caged bird or a very small storm. No one who worked past midnight ever checked which.

Fourteen years, seven months, and eleven days. That was what remained of Oscar's indenture. They knew it the way other people knew their own heartbeat, because they had written it out themselves, once a year, in the front of their personal ledger — the small one, the forbidden one, the one they kept sewn into the lining of their coat where Veyren's fingers would never think to look. *Debt remaining:* and then the number, and then, beneath it, in letters smaller than shame: *Freedom remaining.*

The Guild did not call it freedom. The Guild called it *discharge*, as if a person were a wound that would eventually close over. Oscar had signed their name at thirteen, when their mother's fever debts had come due and the only institution in the city willing to buy a child's future was the one that dealt in impossible things. Their mother had wept at the signing. Oscar had mostly felt the cold of the ink drying on their finger where they'd pressed it to the page, and the strange sensation — one they would spend years trying to name — that the ledger had pressed back.

*The paper remembers,* the old scribes liked to say, tapping their temples. *Ink is just memory learning to hold still.*

Oscar had believed it was poetry, once. Now they knew better. Now they had seen a ledger refuse an entry. Now they had watched a debt scroll itself off a page and vanish like breath from glass, and they no longer thought of the books as books. They thought of them as animals. Patient ones. Hungry ones.

They picked up the pen again.

The account before them was one of the small ones — the Guild kept its horrors in the large folios, chained shut, but even the small ones had teeth. A tinker in the Lowmarket had purchased three minutes of yesterday, wanting to return to the moment before his daughter left for the coast, and the Guild had sold it to him at the standard rate plus interest. But yesterday, it turned out, did not like being revisited. Each time the tinker stepped into his three minutes, the moment frayed a little more at the edges, and now the daughter's face had gone smooth as an egg in his memory, and he had come to the Guild demanding restitution for a face.

Oscar wrote: *Claim denied. Memory is sold as-is. See clause nine.*

Clause nine was Oscar's own invention, drafted two years ago when the complaints had grown too frequent, and Veyren had approved it with a flick of his pale hand and said, *Good. You have a talent for making cruelty sound like weather.* Oscar had gone home that night and been sick into the canal, and then washed their mouth with cheap gin, and then written the day's tally in their hidden ledger with a hand that shook only a little.

That was the trick of surviving the Guild, if survival was the word for it. You learned to shake only a little.

* * *

The bell above the counting room door rang at half past two, which was wrong, because no one visited the counting room at half past two, and also because the bell rang in a minor key, which it only did when someone crossed the threshold carrying something the building wanted.

Oscar looked up. The door stood open onto the corridor, and the corridor stood empty, and the lamps along its length guttered in sequence, one after another, like a row of men turning away.

"Hello," Oscar said, to no one, because saying *hello* to no one was another thing the job taught you.

The lamps righted themselves. The bell forgot its grievance. And Oscar, whose heart was hammering in a way they refused to dignify by acknowledging, returned to the tinker's account and discovered that while they had been looking away, a new line had appeared at the bottom of the page in handwriting that was almost — *almost* — their own:

*Balance outstanding: one face.*

They sat very still for a long moment. Then they took out their knife — a scribe's knife, blunt-nosed, for scraping errors — and scraped the line away, and the vellum accepted the scraping the way stone accepts rain, without complaint and without forgetting.

"You're tired," Oscar told themselves aloud. Talking to oneself was permitted in the counting room; talking to the ledgers was not, though everyone did it, because everyone had, at least once, been certain a ledger was listening.

Outside, far off beyond the shuttered windows, thunder cleared its throat.

* * *

Storms came early this year. Everyone said so — the fishwives, the lamplighters, the old cartographer Pellam who haunted the map room and smelled of tar and dying. *The sky's got an account open too,* Pellam had wheezed last week, leaning on his cane outside Oscar's cell of a sleeping room. *And I've seen the collection notice.* He'd laughed until the laughing became coughing, and Oscar had held his elbow and felt, through the threadbare sleeve, how little of him was left to hold.

Oscar thought of Pellam now as they worked through the last folio of the night: the *Register of Obligations, Minor Arcana,* volumes twelve through nineteen. The Guild's debts were sorted by strangeness the way other houses sorted by date. Volume twelve held the ordinary miracles — love potions that worked too well, bargains with rivers, a man who had sold his shadow and wanted it back with interest. By volume seventeen the entries stopped making sense in any language Oscar knew, and by nineteen the ink itself seemed embarrassed, pooling in the corners of each letter as though trying to hide.

It was volume nineteen that held the entry Oscar hated most, the one they had copied out forty times in their head before they could sleep some nights:

*Received: one promise, unfulfilled, origin unknown. Held in trust against a claimant who has not yet come. Interest accrues in years.*

No name. No date of maturity. Just that patient, terrible phrase — *has not yet come* — sitting in the middle of the page like a chair left waiting at a table.

Oscar had asked Veyren about it once, four years ago, in the master's study with its windows of green glass and its smell of cold iron and lilies. Veyren had been feeding his moths — white ones, huge as sparrows, that lived in a cage of silver wire and ate nothing anyone could see. He had not looked up.

"Some accounts," he said, "are not ours to understand. Only to keep." And then, with the mildness that was worse than any threat: "Do keep it well, Reyes. Some debts are older than the people who owe them."

Oscar had bowed and left, and climbed the stairs to their room, and lain awake listening to the moths' wings against the wire, a sound like a book being opened and closed, opened and closed.

* * *

Dawn found the counting room gray and the fog pressing at the windows like a face looking in. Oscar blew out the candle, squared the folios, and carried the night's work to the Iron Cabinet — the great black chest in the corner that locked with three keys and, rumor insisted, one appetite. Their key was brass, worn smooth, warm from their pocket. The second key hung on Veyren's neck. The third had not been seen in living memory, and the Cabinet opened anyway, every time, which was the sort of fact the Guild expected you not to think about.

Oscar thought about it constantly.

On the way out, they passed the Duty Board, where the day's assignments were chalked in the quartermaster's brisk hand. Sefa must have come through already; the chalk was fresh, and there was a smear of it on the frame where she always leaned her hip. Oscar scanned the list out of habit — *Ilara Vance, borderlands, overdue; Pellam, map room, frail; Reyes, counting room, nights indefinite* — and felt the familiar small death of reading *indefinite* applied to themselves.

Indefinite. As if fourteen years were a rounding error. As if a life were a sum that could be carried forward forever.

They were still standing there, hating the word, when the knock came.

Not at the counting room door. At the *guild* door — the great one, the river-facing one, oak banded in iron, older than the city charter and twice as stubborn. Oscar heard it through three corridors and two closed doors, which should not have been possible, and heard it again, and understood that whoever knocked was not knocking politely. They were knocking the way the drowned knock.

The building woke around them. Lamps flared in empty rooms. Somewhere overhead, Veyren's moths beat against their cage in a sudden frantic applause, and Oscar heard the master's voice through the floor, one word, calm as a blade laid flat on a table:

"Now."

Oscar did not run toward the door. Scribes did not run; running implied urgency, and urgency implied that events happened *to* the Guild rather than being entered into its books. But they walked quickly, quickly, past Sefa emerging from the quartermaster's office with her sleeves rolled and her eyes already bright with the particular joy she took in catastrophes, past the reliquary where the small storm rattled its bars, down the main stair where the portraits of dead masters watched with painted patience —

— and through the antechamber, where Veyren already stood, robed, composed, his white moths settled on his shoulders like epaulettes, staring at the great door as though it were a page he intended to read.

The knocking stopped.

In the silence that followed, Oscar heard the rain, and under the rain, fainter, stranger, a voice — high, thin, exhausted, a child's voice — speaking words in a language that made no sound Oscar's ears could catch and yet landed somewhere behind their sternum like a coin dropped down a well.

Every ledger in the building turned its own page.

Oscar felt it happen — felt it in the soles of their feet, a whisper of moving paper, hundreds of volumes shifting at once in the dark of the archive like a herd of animals rising. Beside them, Sefa swore softly. Ahead of them, Veyren smiled, and it was the smile of a man watching a debt he had waited a lifetime to collect come walking up to his door.

"Well," the master said. "Open it."
