## 2. A Knock Before Dawn

The storm arrived the way debts do — slowly at first, then all at once, and never when it was convenient.

Oscar heard it building all evening while they worked the third-floor counting room, a low grumbling in the east like a creditor clearing his throat. By midnight the rain had come down the mountainside in earnest, hammering the slate roof of the Guild of Impossible Things as though it, too, had a grievance to file. By the hour before dawn, the gutters were roaring and the lightning was close enough to turn the frost-ferns on the windows into brief, silver ledgers of their own — columns of light, entered and erased.

Oscar did not sleep well in storms. They never had. Sleep required the mind to set down its burdens, and Oscar had never once in twenty-six years succeeded in setting anything down. Instead they lay in their narrow room above the archive, listening to the rain catalogue the roof tile by tile, and told themselves, as they did every night, that there were only four years and two hundred and eleven days left on their indenture. Four years. Two hundred and eleven days. After that, the Guild's claim on their hands, their eyes, their hours would lapse, and they would walk out the front gate a free person with nothing at all, which was a kind of wealth no ledger could touch.

The knock came at the hour when the night is thinnest, when even the rain seems to hesitate.

Three strikes. Slow. Deliberate. The kind of knock a person gives when they have knocked on many doors already and been refused, and have stopped expecting anything but the sound of their own knuckles.

Oscar lay still and listened. The Guild had a bell for visitors — a bronze thing shaped like a coiled serpent, rung by a cord that dangled from the gatehouse — and no one with any sense used the door itself after dark. The door was for deliveries. The door was for things that did not wish to be seen arriving.

Three strikes again. Softer this time. Almost apologetic.

Oscar rose, pulled a robe over their nightshirt, and took the lamp from its hook. The flame shivered as they carried it down the back stair, past the archive doors with their iron bands, past the notice board where the month's requisitions fluttered like moths pinned in place. The air smelled of wet stone and lamp oil and, faintly, of the ink that saturated everything in the building down to its bones. Twenty years in the Guild and Oscar could no longer smell ink as a separate thing. It was simply the smell of the world, the way salt was the smell of the sea.

The bolt was cold under their fingers. They hesitated — a habit, that hesitation, drilled into them by a decade of Master Veyren's lectures on prudence — and then, for reasons they would spend many nights afterward trying to account for, they did not hesitate after all. They lifted the bar and pulled the door open into the dark.

The child stood at the threshold like something the storm had decided, at the last moment, to set down.

It was small — seven years old, perhaps eight, though it was hard to say, because the child was so caked in mud from crown to boot that its actual edges were a matter of conjecture. Its hair was plastered flat. Its clothes had once been good ones; Oscar could see the ghost of embroidery at the collar, the kind of stitching that cost more per thread than Oscar earned in a week. Now the embroidery was brown with road-muck, and one shoe was missing entirely, and the child's bare foot on the wet flagstones was blue-white with cold.

Its eyes were what stopped Oscar's breath.

Not their color, though the color was strange enough — a pale, rain-washed grey, the grey of the sky just before it decides to snow. It was their *attention*. The child looked at Oscar the way no one had ever looked at Oscar before: not past them, not through them, not at the Guild's livery or the lamp in their hand, but at them, precisely, completely, as though Oscar were a sentence the child had been reading for a very long time and had finally, at this door, in this storm, finished.

"You're the one who writes," the child said.

The words were in the common tongue, and they were ordinary words, and they went through Oscar like a blade through wet paper. Because Oscar had never seen this child. Because the gate had been barred. Because the nearest village was six miles down the mountain and no seven-year-old walked six miles through a storm like this and arrived looking less lost than the person answering the door.

"I—" Oscar's voice came out wrong, too high. They lowered it. "It's the middle of the night. Where are your parents? Where did you come from?"

The child tilted its head. Rain ran off its chin.

"You're the one who writes," it said again, in exactly the same tone, with exactly the same weight, as though repetition were not emphasis but *correction* — as though Oscar's questions had been noise, and this was the signal.

And then it spoke again, and the words were not in the common tongue at all.

Oscar would spend years trying to describe what those words were like, and would never quite succeed. They were not harsh, though they had edges. They were not musical, though they had rhythm. They sounded the way the archive smelled — old, layered, dense with things that had been set down and never taken back. Each syllable seemed to arrive from farther away than the child's mouth, as if the words had been traveling a long time and the child was merely the last station on their road.

The lamp flame bent sideways. There was no wind.

Behind Oscar, down the corridor, in the dark of the counting room, something answered.

It was a small sound. Oscar almost missed it under the rain — a dry, whispering rustle, like a hand turning a page. But no hand was turning a page. Oscar knew the sound their own archive made at night, the settling of paper, the creak of the presses; this was not that. This was the sound of many pages turning at once, in many rooms, in a building where every soul was asleep.

The child stopped speaking. It looked past Oscar's shoulder, into the dark interior of the Guild, and its mud-streaked face did something complicated — fear and recognition fighting for the same ground.

"It heard me," the child said quietly. In the common tongue again. As though the First Tongue — and Oscar would learn, later, to call it that, though tonight it was only *the words that made the paper move* — as though that language were a door best closed quickly. "It always hears me. That's why I ran."

"Ran from what?" Oscar asked. "From whom?"

The child looked up at them, and the rain-washed grey eyes were suddenly, terribly tired. Old. The tiredness of a thing that had been awake far longer than its body suggested.

"From everyone who wants to know what I'm *for*," it said.

* * *

Oscar did the thing they would later understand was the first true decision of their life, though at the time it felt like nothing at all — like a clerk initialing a page without reading it. They stepped back from the threshold and let the child in.

The warmth of the hall hit the small body like a wall. The child swayed, and Oscar caught its arm — thin, shaking, cold as the flagstones — and steered it toward the kitchen hearth, where the night-coal was banked but not dead. They did not take the child to the dormitory. They did not ring for the porter. They did not, though some deep clerkish instinct whispered that they ought, make any entry whatsoever in the night-book that hung by the gate, recording all arrivals, all departures, all comings and goings in the Guild of Impossible Things.

Later, Oscar would ask themselves why. The honest answer, the one they would finally write down in a private notebook they kept behind a loose brick, was this: because the child had said *you're the one who writes*, and some part of Oscar — the part that had spent twenty years being written *about*, entered, assessed, debited — had recognized, in that instant, a fellow creature that did not wish to be recorded. Not yet. Not by them.

They got the fire up. They found a blanket in the linen press that smelled of cedar, and a heel of bread, and set water to warm. The child ate the way storm survivors eat — fast, mechanically, as though eating were a task assigned rather than a pleasure — and then sat with its bare feet nearly in the coals and let the color come back into them, and watched Oscar with that same finished-sentence attention.

"What's your name?" Oscar asked.

The child considered this for a long moment. Outside, the thunder had moved off down the valley, grumbling its accounts to the hills.

"August," it said at last. "But that's the name they gave me. The other one I can't say here. It would—" The child frowned, searching. "It would make too much of itself. Words are heavier where you live."

"Where I live," Oscar repeated. "You mean the Guild."

"I mean *here*." August gestured with a crumb-dusted hand at the kitchen, the walls, the whole weight of the building around them. "Everything here is written down. I can feel it. It's like walking into a room where everyone is talking at once and all of them are talking about *accounts*." The child's nose wrinkled. "You have so many ledgers. Do you know that? Hundreds. Thousands. They're all awake right now. They've been awake since I knocked."

Oscar's hands went still on the kettle.

"They're paper," they said. "Paper and ink. They don't wake."

August looked at them with something close to pity.

"Everything that's written down is a little bit alive," the child said, in the mild tone of someone correcting a child's arithmetic. "That's what writing *is*. You put a piece of a thing into the marks, and the marks remember it, and remembering is the beginning of being. Your Guild knows this. That's why it writes so much. It's not recording the world." August leaned forward, and the firelight moved in its grey eyes. "It's *making* one."

Oscar laughed. It came out as a single dry note, without any humor in it at all.

"You've been listening to the archivists," they said. "They talk like that when they've had too much of the counting wine. Paper is paper, child. Ink is ink. The Guild writes things down because writing is how the powerful hold what they have, and that is all it has ever been."

"Then why," said August, "did every ledger in this building turn to the page with *your* handwriting on it, the moment I knocked?"

The kettle began, very softly, to sing.

Oscar did not answer. They took the kettle off the hook with hands that were, they noted distantly, entirely steady, which seemed a betrayal of everything going on inside them. They poured water over the tea leaves and watched the steam climb, and they thought about the sound in the dark — the many pages, the many rooms — and about the fact that the night-book by the gate hung open to no particular page, and that Oscar had not written in it, and that some animal part of them had known, at the time, that it must not.

"Who told you to come here?" they asked instead. "Someone sent you. Someone dressed you in good clothes and pointed you up a mountain in a storm. Who?"

"No one sent me." August wrapped the blanket tighter. "I dreamed this door. I've dreamed it since before I can remember. A door with a serpent on the bell-cord, and rain, and a person with tired hands who opens it." The child looked at Oscar's hands — at the ink-stains in the knuckle-creases that no scrubbing had ever fully lifted — and something in its small mud-caked face went soft and sad. "You have the tired hands."

"I'm a scribe," Oscar said. "All scribes have tired hands. It's not a prophecy. It's a occupational condition."

August smiled. It was the first time, and the smile transformed the whole small face, cracked the mud, showed the child underneath — a child, actually a child, someone who ought to be asleep in a bed somewhere with a mother in the next room.

"You argue like the ledgers," August said. "Everything has to balance."

"Everything *does* balance. That's the entire discipline."

"No," said August, and the smile faded, and the old tiredness came back into the grey eyes like a tide coming in. "That's what you were taught. But some things don't balance. Some things are owed that can't be paid. That's why I'm here. That's what I *am*." The child held out one small hand, palm up, as though asking Oscar to read it like a page. "I'm the part that doesn't balance."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, a beam settled with a groan. And beneath both sounds — so faint that Oscar would convince themselves by morning they had imagined it — came that dry whisper again, rising up through the floors from the archive below, from the counting room above, from the record-rooms and the charter-vault and the long iron-shelved galleries where the Guild kept its centuries: the sound of pages turning. Turning. Turning. As if every book in the building were looking for something, or looking at something.

Or looking at someone.

Oscar set down the kettle very carefully, the way one sets down a thing that has suddenly become dangerous.

"August," they said. "What happens if the Guild decides you belong in its ledgers?"

The child did not flinch. That was the thing Oscar remembered longest, in all the nights that followed — that the child did not flinch. It only looked into the fire, and its bare feet curled away from the coals, and it said, in the voice of someone reciting a lesson learned too young and too well:

"Then I'll be in them forever. And whatever's written down about you —" the grey eyes lifted, and found Oscar's, and held them — "that's what you are. Isn't it? You of all people know. You're the one who writes."

* * *

Dawn came grey and rinsed-out, the storm spent, the mountain steaming where the sun first touched it. The bell for the morning shift had not yet rung when Oscar heard footsteps on the back stair — measured, unhurried, the footsteps of a man who had never in his life needed to hurry because the world arranged itself around his pace.

Oscar stood up from the hearth-side chair so fast that August, curled asleep in the blanket, woke with a start.

Master Veyren filled the kitchen doorway the way winter fills a valley. He was not a large man, but he was a *complete* one, in the way a contract is complete — every clause in place, nothing left open, nothing to be argued. His grey eyes went from Oscar, to the child, to the abandoned bread, to the blanket, and Oscar watched him read the scene the way he read everything: instantly, totally, and with an interest that had no warmth in it at all.

"Reyes," he said. His voice was mild. It was always mild. "There's mud on my kitchen floor."

"Master Veyren, I can explain—"

"You were going to." Veyren stepped into the room, and his gaze settled on August, and something happened in his face — something very small, very deep, like a coin dropping into a well that is deeper than any well has a right to be. "Weren't you."

August, still half-asleep, looked up at him. And Oscar saw the child's whole body go still in a way that had nothing to do with sleepiness — the stillness of a rabbit that has caught a new scent on the wind.

"You're the master of the writing house," August said.

"I am." Veyren smiled. It was a good smile. It was, Oscar thought with a cold turning in their stomach, an excellent smile, practiced, calibrated, the smile of a man greeting an asset he had been promised. "And you, little one, must be very tired, and very hungry, and very far from home. You'll want for nothing here. I give you my word."

August looked at Oscar.

It was only a glance — a flick of the grey eyes, half a second, there and gone. But Oscar had spent twenty years reading small marks in long documents, and they read that glance the way they read everything: completely. It said: *this is the one I ran from.* It said: *you opened the door.* It said: *what happens to me now?*

Veyren turned back to Oscar, and the smile remained, and the well behind it gave back no sound.

"Reyes," he said mildly. "Clear your morning schedule. From today, you'll take a new commission. Everything the child says — every word, every syllable, in whatever tongue it chooses — you'll set down exactly as spoken. You'll transcribe for it, and for it alone, and you'll bring the pages to me each evening, uncorrected." He paused at the doorway, and the morning light behind him made his shadow stretch long across the kitchen floor, all the way to the hearth, all the way to where the child sat wrapped in a borrowed blanket.

"And Reyes," Master Veyren added, without turning around, in the tone of a man remarking on the weather. "The night-book by the gate is blank for yesterday's arrivals. I do so dislike blank lines in my records. See that it doesn't happen again."

His footsteps faded up the stair. The bell rang for the morning shift. And in the silence after, Oscar became aware — slowly, then all at once, with a sensation like the floor tilting beneath them — that every ledger in the Guild of Impossible Things had gone quiet at once.

As if they had all turned, together, to a new page.

And were waiting to see what would be written on it.
