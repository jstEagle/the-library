## 3. The Master's Interest

The storm had not finished with the night when Master Veyren came down to the entry hall.

Oscar heard him before they saw him — the particular cadence of his footsteps on the guild stairs, unhurried, each one placed like a word in a sentence he had already written. Veyren did not hurry for storms or children or gods. He descended as he did everything: at the pace of a man who believed the world would wait for him, and was usually right.

Oscar stood where they had stood all night, ink-stained fingers still curled around the ledger they'd used to shield themselves from the child's voice. The book felt warm against their chest in a way no book should feel warm. Behind them, huddled in the puddle of mud and rainwater she'd brought in with her, the girl watched the stairs.

She had stopped speaking. That was somehow worse than the speaking.

* * *

Veyren reached the bottom step and paused there, letting the lamplight find his face the way he liked it found — half shadowed, half gold, a man perpetually caught between wisdom and mystery by his own careful design. He was not a tall man. He did not need to be. He wore his authority the way other men wore coats tailored to them: fitted, expensive, impossible to imagine on anyone else.

His eyes went first to Oscar. Then to the child. Then back to Oscar, and something in that second look made Oscar's stomach drop through the floorboards into the cellar of old debts beneath the guild.

"You woke me," Veyren said. It was not an accusation. It was worse. It was an observation, delivered with the mild tone of a man noting that it had rained.

"The door was knocking, Master." Oscar heard their own voice come out steady, which surprised them. "I answered it."

"At the third bell of morning." Veyren stepped off the last stair onto the flagstones. His slippers were dry. Of course they were dry; he had been awake, then, before the knock. Or he had slept in his clothes, as he sometimes did during the last week of any month, when the ledgers demanded balancing and the moon did whatever the moon did. "In this weather. And you opened it."

"She was alone, Master."

"Everything is alone when it arrives." Veyren crossed the hall toward the child with the measured tread of a man approaching a horse he intended to buy. He stopped three paces away — close enough to look, far enough to be looked at — and crouched slightly, though his knees never quite bent the way ordinary men's knees bent. He studied her the way Oscar had seen him study rare acquisitions: a first-edition atlas, a vial of ink ground from meteoric glass, a debtors' contract signed in blood because the debtor had nothing else.

"And what are you?" he asked the girl softly.

The girl said nothing. She looked at Veyren the way a wild thing looks at a snare it has only just noticed — not yet caught, but understanding, suddenly and completely, the concept of *caught*.

"She speaks a language no one knows," Oscar said. The words left them before they could weigh them, and immediately they regretted the weight of them. Too much offered. Too freely given. "Or — I couldn't parse it. I tried. It isn't any dialect in the reference stacks. It isn't Old Imperial, it isn't High Cant, it isn't—"

"I didn't ask you to parse it." Veyren did not raise his voice. He never raised his voice. Raising one's voice was for men whose authority had to be carried by volume rather than weight. "I asked you to open the door. Which you did. Everything after that is consequence, and consequences belong to me."

He turned back to the child. Up close, Oscar could see what the storm had done to her — the mud dried in pale tracks down her cheeks like the ghosts of tears, the way her small hands were fisted in the wet hem of her shift, the shivering she was trying so hard to disguise as stillness. She couldn't have been more than seven. Eight at the outside. Her eyes were the color of deep water over stone, and they moved from Veyren's face to Oscar's and back again, cataloguing, weighing, deciding.

Deciding what, Oscar would wonder about for years.

"Child," Veyren said, and his voice changed — softened, warmed, took on the honeyed grain he used for donors and dignitaries and anyone whose belief he needed more than their money. "You've come a long way in terrible weather. You must be hungry. Frightened. Alone in a world that doesn't speak your tongue." He extended one hand, palm up, an offering without anything in it. "But you're here now. And the Guild of Impossible Things takes in every impossible thing that knocks."

For a moment — just a moment — the girl's fists loosened.

And then Veyren's other hand, hanging loose at his side, closed around the ledger Oscar hadn't noticed him carrying. His own ledger. Bound in grey calfskin, its spine cracked from use, its pages soft with handling. Oscar knew that book. Everyone in the guild knew that book. It was the master's private record, the one kept locked in the cabinet behind his desk, the one no scribe but Oscar was permitted even to touch, and only under supervision, and only to make fair copies.

Veyren opened it to a marked page and held it out — not to the child, but beside her, angled so she could see it.

On the page, in Veyren's small, precise hand, was a single line:

*Arrived in storm. Third bell. Speaking.*

Nothing else. No name. No description. No date, even — though the date was there, tucked into the margin in numerals so small they might have been decoration.

The girl stared at the page. And Oscar watched — would always watch, in memory, replaying it in the dark hours afterward — watched the child's whole body go rigid, watched her breath catch in a way that had nothing to do with cold, watched her look at those four words as if she understood them perfectly.

As if she had read them.

"That's enough," Veyren said quietly, and closed the book.

* * *

By the time the sun came up — a thin, watery, apologetic sun, wringing itself out over the rooftops — the machinery of the guild had already begun to turn around the girl.

Oscar was ordered to draw a bath. Then to find dry clothes. Then to sit with her while she ate, and write down everything she ate, and how much, and whether she ate it in the manner of a child or in the manner of something older wearing a child's shape. (She ate like a child, thank every merciful power — bread torn, not cut; honey scooped with fingers; milk drunk in desperate gulps followed by a small, embarrassed belch that she covered with both hands.)

Then Veyren arrived in the breakfast room, and the machinery turned again.

"You will transcribe her," he said.

Oscar looked up from the slate they'd been using to tally bread and honey. "Transcribe her, Master?"

"Everything she says. Every sound. Every syllable. From this moment forward." Veyren walked the length of the room as he spoke, trailing one finger along the shelf of dictionaries and grammars that lined the wall — the guild's proud collection of dead languages, each one embalmed in its own handsome binding. "When she sleeps, you will note that she slept, and whether she spoke in her sleep, and if she spoke in her sleep, what. When she eats, you will note her words. When she weeps — and she will weep, they always weep at first — you will note that too. Every utterance, Oscar. Verbatim. In order. With your phonetic best guess rendered above the line and your interpretive gloss below it, per the standard method."

"Master." Oscar set down the chalk. Their mouth had gone dry in a way that had nothing to do with the bread. "With respect — she's a child. Not a text."

Veyren stopped walking. He turned, and the look he gave Oscar was long and level and utterly without anger, which was how Oscar knew they were in danger.

"A child is a text," Veyren said. "A child is nothing *but* text. She is a message that hasn't finished arriving. Someone wrote her, Oscar — someone sent her — and our task, our sacred and tedious task, is to read her correctly. Do you understand? Misread her, and we learn nothing. Read her truly—" he smiled, and the smile did not reach anywhere near his eyes, "—and we may be the first people in recorded history to understand what the world is trying to tell us."

"The world talks to everyone, Master. Mostly it says *mind your own affairs*."

Something flickered across Veyren's face — amusement, perhaps, or the memory of amusement — and was gone. "Your wit is noted. Your transcription begins now." He produced, from inside his coat, a notebook. New. Expensive. Its pages were heavy rag paper, the kind that took ink without bleeding, the kind the guild reserved for documents meant to outlast their authors. He set it on the table in front of Oscar with a small, deliberate click. "You will keep this book on your person at all times. You will sleep with it within reach. If the child speaks in your presence and you fail to record it, I will know. If you record it falsely, I will know that too. Do you understand me?"

"Yes, Master."

"Do you *understand* me, Oscar?" And there it was — the repetition, the gentle pressure, the question that wasn't really a question but a hand closing around the back of the neck. "This is important work. Perhaps the most important work this guild has undertaken in three hundred years. I am entrusting it to you because you are precise, because you are discreet, and because you owe this house eleven years, two months, and some odd days of your life. I would hate to see precision become carelessness, or discretion become silence, or debt become... renegotiated."

There it was. Oscar felt it settle over them like a wet cloak: the debt, always the debt, invoked not as cruelty but as arithmetic. Eleven years, two months, and some odd days. Veyren knew the number to the day. He probably knew it to the hour. Somewhere in his grey calfskin ledger there was a line with Oscar's name on it and a figure at the end that represented everything they were worth, and Veyren consulted that figure the way a farmer consults the sky.

"I understand, Master," Oscar said.

"Good." Veyren straightened his cuffs. At the doorway he paused, and did not turn around. "One more thing. The child is not to leave the building. She is not to speak with anyone but you and me. She is not to see the other scribes, the quartermaster, the hunters — no one. As far as the rest of the guild is concerned, we took in a stray during the storm and she is convalescing. Am I clear?"

"Perfectly, Master."

"And Oscar." Now he turned, and the morning light caught his face full, and for one unguarded instant Oscar saw something underneath the stewardship — something quick and bright and hungry, a flame behind frosted glass. "Thank you. Truly. You've brought me something extraordinary."

*Not us*, Oscar thought. *Me. You've brought* me *something.*

They said none of it. They said, "Yes, Master," and watched him go, and listened to his footsteps climb the stairs at their unhurried pace, each one placed like a word in a sentence already written.

* * *

The room Veyren gave the girl was on the third floor, at the end of the east corridor — a guest cell normally reserved for visiting assessors, which meant it had a bed that didn't scream, a window that latched, and a lock on the *outside* of the door. Oscar noticed the lock. They were certain the girl noticed it too, because when Oscar showed her in, she walked to that door and put her palm flat against the wood beside the lock plate, and held it there for a long moment, as if listening to the mechanism breathe.

Then she turned around and looked at Oscar, and said something in that language.

It was short. Three syllables, maybe four. Her voice had recovered from the storm — it was clear now, almost musical, with a strange doubled quality, as though two speakers were saying the same word a half-beat apart.

Oscar took out the new notebook. Opened it. Dipped the pen.

"What do I call you?" they asked. "Do you have a name?"

The girl tilted her head. Then she pointed — deliberately, gravely, the way a queen points at a map — at herself, and said a single word.

Oscar wrote it down phonetically as best they could: *Aw-gust*. Though the middle sound wasn't quite a *g*, and the ending wasn't quite a *st*; it was softer, rounder, a sound that seemed to arrive slightly before it was spoken, like thunder before lightning. Underneath, in the interpretive gloss line, Oscar wrote: *name? self? "August"?*

The girl — August — watched them write. And when the pen touched the paper, she leaned closer, and her eyes went wide, and she said another word, urgent now, pointing at the page, at the ink, at the letters taking shape under Oscar's hand.

Oscar looked at what they'd written. Looked at the word *August* sitting there in their own handwriting.

The ink was still wet. And it was moving.

Not much. A hair's breadth. The letters settling against each other like birds rearranging on a wire, shifting their spacing, tightening their formation — and when they stilled, the word on the page did not quite say *August* anymore. It said something else. Something Oscar's eye slid off of twice before it held: a word that looked, in the fading light, like it had always meant *the one who counts*.

Oscar's hand shook. A drop of ink fell and blotted the margin.

August watched the blot spread, and sighed — a small, old, weary sigh, the sigh of someone watching a translation fail — and sat down on the assessor's bed, and pulled her knees up, and looked at the locked door.

Oscar closed the notebook. Held it against their chest, where it lay warm as a sleeping animal.

Below them, through the floorboards, they could hear the guild waking: Sefa quarreling with a delivery man about the count of a candle crate; the apprentices' bells; somewhere, a hunter's boots crossing the yard. An ordinary morning. An ordinary house.

Except for the lock on the outside of the door. Except for the book against their ribs, keeping its own counsel. Except for the child on the bed who had looked at a ledger the way other people look at home.

Eleven years, two months, and some odd days, Oscar thought. That was the price of the rest of their life.

And Master Veyren, who knew that number to the hour, had just thanked them — warmly, sincerely — for bringing him the most extraordinary thing the guild had ever owned.

Oscar uncapped the ink, opened the notebook to a fresh page, and began at the top, in their smallest, most careful hand:

*Day one. Subject arrived speaking. Transcription follows.*

The pen scratched. Outside, the storm's last clouds tore apart, and the thirteenth moon — invisible in daylight, waiting, patient as a debt — hung unseen over the city, counting down.
