## 4. Words Older Than Walls

August knew the smell of the world before the Veil the way other children knew their mothers' voices — not because anyone had told them, but because it lived somewhere behind their ribs, folded small and warm like a moth in winter.

Wet stone. That was the first thing. Wet stone and something green growing through it, patient as forgiveness. In the memory-fragments that came to August not as pictures but as *knowings*, there had been a city built of that wet stone, and the green had been allowed to grow through it on purpose, because the First Speakers had believed that anything alive should be permitted its own road home.

August sat cross-legged on the narrow cot the Guild had given them — a cot that smelled of cedar shavings and old fear — and pressed their palms flat against their knees, and tried very hard not to remember out loud.

That was the rule they had made for themselves on the first night. *Do not remember out loud.* Because when August remembered out loud, things happened.

The candle on the stool across the room had been behaving strangely all evening. It leaned toward August whenever they breathed deep, the way flowers lean toward light, except August was not light. August was something else. The flame bent like a listener bending toward a whisper, and wax ran down one side of the candle in a long, slow tear.

*Stop it,* August told the candle silently. *I didn't ask you.*

The candle did not stop. Nothing ever stopped, once it started listening. That was the trouble with the words inside August — they were not really words at all, not the way the grey-robed people here used words, little sounds that bounced off things and fell dead on the floor. The words inside August were older than walls. Older than the idea of walls. They were the sounds the world had made before anyone decided the world should have edges, and when August spoke them, the edges got confused about what they were supposed to be doing.

August had learned this the hard way, in another town, in another storm, before the mud and before this door with its iron hinges shaped like sleeping birds. They had been cold, and they had said the word for *warmth* — the true word, the first one — and the hearth in the inn had roared up so bright and so hungry that the tapestry caught fire and the innkeeper's cat had screamed in a voice like a person's, and everyone had looked at August the way people look at a door that has begun to open by itself.

So: do not remember out loud. Do not speak unless spoken to. Do not let the edges get confused.

But the Guild of Impossible Things did not seem to understand rules. The Guild seemed to understand only *studying*, and studying, August was learning, was a kind of hunger that wore spectacles.

* * *

They came for August every morning after the bell with the cracked voice rang. Not the same ones every time — the Guild turned its scholars over like pages — but always the same ritual. A chair placed in the center of the room, facing a desk. A lamp adjusted. A slate, sometimes, though the chalk never survived what August had to say; it crumbled, or it wrote things no one had drawn, or once it wrote a single character over and over until the slate went black and the scholar carrying it dropped it and left the room and did not come back.

"Say it again," they would ask. "Slower."

And August would look at them with the flat, careful face they had practiced in puddles and window glass, and say nothing, because *slower* was not the problem. The problem was that the words did not live in August's mouth. They lived deeper, in the place where the moth slept behind the ribs, and pulling one out was like pulling a thread from the hem of the world. You could not pull just one. You pulled, and the whole hem began to travel.

Yesterday a woman with ink-stained cuffs had asked August to say the word for *door*. Just that. Just the ordinary word, she'd promised, the plain word any child would use.

August had tried. August had reached for the plain word — *dorren*, the market-word, the word with no teeth in it — but the old word had been sitting on top of it, the way a big dog sits on a small one, and what came out instead was the First Tongue's name for door, which is not a name for a door at all but a name for *the agreement between two spaces that they will remain separate*, and the lamp flame had stood straight up like a soldier, and the door of the room had swung open two inches on its own, politely, as if asking whether it was wanted.

The woman had written for a long time after that. Her pen scratched like a mouse in a wall. And August had watched her write and felt something worse than fear.

Fear would have been simple. What August felt was loneliness wearing fear's coat.

Because the woman was writing down *sounds*. August could see it — her marks were short, practical, arranged in rows like beads. She was trying to catch the First Tongue the way you catch a bird by breaking its wings and pinning it to a board. But the First Tongue could not be pinned. It was not made of sounds. It was made of *meanings that remembered being true*, and every scholar who transcribed it wrong was, in some small way, telling the world a lie about itself, and the world was beginning to notice.

August could feel it noticing. At night, lying on the cedar-smelling cot, August could feel the edges of things — the edges of the room, the edges of the Guild, the great soft-grey edge far away that the grey-robed people called the Veil without understanding what they were naming — all of it shifting, restless, like a sleeper turning toward a voice calling from another room.

*Soon,* the edges seemed to say. *Someone will say it properly soon.*

August pulled the blanket up to their chin and did not answer.

* * *

There were fragments. That was the honest truth of it, the truth August kept even from themselves most days: they did not remember the other side of the Veil. They remembered *pieces* of it, the way you remember pieces of a song your mother sang before you could talk — the melody without the verses, the warmth without the kitchen.

A tower of white reed-glass that hummed when the wind came off the water, and the humming was a language too, and everyone in the city could read it.

A market where nothing was bought or sold, where people simply brought what they had made and set it down and took what they needed, and the taking was a kind of speaking, and the leaving was a kind of listening.

A garden grown entirely of instruments. You walked through it and it played you.

And underneath everything, under all the fragments like stones under moss: hands. Many hands, pressed together, palm to palm, in a circle wide enough to hold a horizon. August did not know whose hands. August only knew that the circle had been singing, and that the singing had a shape, and the shape was a door, and the door was closing, and the last thing the hands had sung was not a farewell.

It had been an instruction.

*Remember us correctly,* the hands had sung, or something in the marrow-deep way fragments work, that is what they had sung. *However long it takes. However far the forgetting travels. Remember us correctly, and we are not lost.*

August had woken from that fragment more times than they could count, in ditches and haylofts and once, memorably, in a ferryman's boat, with the fragment still ringing in their teeth and tears on their face they had no memory of crying.

Remember us correctly.

And now August sat in a guild full of people who wanted, more than anything in the world, to remember *incorrectly*. To take the First Tongue apart and use its bones for hinges. To open the edge — August had heard them whisper it in the corridors, thinking the mud-streaked child understood nothing — to open the Veil and see what was on the other side, as if the other side were a country and not a congregation, as if what waited there were a treasure and not a crowd of exiled singers pressing their faces against the far side of the sky, waiting to be remembered right.

*They think I'm a key,* August thought, watching the candle lean. *Keys don't get asked what they want.*

* * *

It was the scribe who broke the pattern.

August noticed scribes the way August noticed weather — necessary, everywhere, mostly ignored. This one had appeared on the second day, a thin figure with ink permanently shadowing the first knuckle of their right hand, who sat at the desk in the corner and wrote and wrote and wrote while the scholars did their asking. August had catalogued them and dismissed them: *another one who writes sounds.*

But on the fourth evening — the evening the chalk died, the evening the slate went black — August had glanced over, expecting the usual bead-row transcription, and had seen something else.

The scribe's page was moving.

Not much. Not obviously. If you looked straight at it, it held still, the way stars hold still when you look straight at them. But August had learned young never to look straight at anything important, and out of the corner of their eye, the scribe's handwriting was *shifting* — letters leaning into new shapes, strokes rearranging themselves like birds settling on a wire into a different order — and what the page was becoming was not a record of sound at all.

It was becoming meaning.

August's breath caught. The candle flared. The scribe looked up.

For a moment — a long moment, three heartbeats, four — the scribe and the child looked at each other across the lamplit room, and August searched the thin face for the hunger the others wore, the collector's hunger, the pin-the-bird hunger.

It wasn't there. What was there instead was something August recognized with a jolt so sharp it hurt: the look of someone standing at the edge of an enormous understanding and trying not to fall in. The look of someone whose hands were shaking, and who was writing anyway.

The scribe mouthed something. No sound. Just the shape of it, careful, half-question:

*Is this right?*

And then, before August could decide how to answer — before August even knew whether they *wanted* to answer, whether answering was safe, whether anything was safe — the door opened and Master Veyren came in smelling of cedar and cold authority, and the scribe's hand covered the page, and the moment folded itself away like a letter slipped back into its envelope.

But August held onto it. August was good at holding onto things; it was practically the family trade.

* * *

That night, unable to sleep, August lay on the cot and conducted an inventory, the way the ferryman had taught them to inventory a boat before trusting it to water. One scribe. Thin. Ink-shadowed knuckles. Eyes that went wide instead of narrow when the old words moved. A page that listened.

Against that: a Guild that studied children like weather. A master whose interest had a weight to it, a gravitational pull, the way a well has gravity if you stand too close to its lip. Scholars who said *again, slower* and meant *again, smaller, again, breakable*.

And against all of it, faint but persistent as the reed-glass tower's hum: the memory of hands in a circle, singing *remember us correctly*, and the sudden, terrifying thought that maybe — *maybe* — remembering correctly required exactly one thing the Guild did not have and one thing the scribe might.

Not scholarship. Not authority. Not spectacles or seals or ledgers.

Someone willing to listen without pinning.

August turned over. The moonlight through the shutters lay across the floor in bars, and the bars looked, in the way of moonlight, almost like the slats of a gate — and beyond the gate, in the way of imagination, almost like a garden of instruments waiting to be played.

*If I teach them,* August thought, and the thought was dangerous and warm and terrible, like a coal carried in a cupped hand, *if I teach them even one word truly — the true way, the meaning-way — then someone in this whole grey building will finally know what I am saying.*

*And then I won't be alone in the language anymore.*

*And then they'll know what the Veil is for. And what I'm for. And maybe those aren't the same thing, and maybe they are, and maybe—*

The candle, long since snuffed, gave one soft pop in the dark, as if agreeing.

August pressed their face into the pillow and, for the first time since the storm had delivered them to this door with its sleeping-bird hinges, let a fragment rise all the way to the surface — carefully, silently, a diver coming up slow so as not to burst — and held it in the lantern-room of their mind and examined it by moonlight.

It was the smallest fragment. The oldest one. Older than the tower, older than the market, older than the garden. It was not a picture at all, only a sensation: the feeling of being *understood* — completely, instantly, without translation — the way water understands rain.

*I want that,* August admitted to the dark. *I want it so much it frightens me. And wanting things is how they catch you.*

Down the corridor, a door opened and closed. Soft footsteps passed August's room and paused — just for a breath, just long enough — outside the door.

August held perfectly still. Through the gap beneath the door, lamplight pooled and trembled, and a shadow crossed it, thin, pausing, the shadow of someone holding a page they could not stop reading.

Then the footsteps moved on, and the light went with them, and August lay in the dark with their heart going like a bird in a cupped hand, and made the second rule of their captivity, quietly, in the old tongue, where no one could hear it and nothing could misunderstand:

*Tomorrow, I will show the scribe one true word. Only one. And we will see what kind of listener they are.*

Outside the shuttered window, far above the Guild's grey roofs, the sky was clear for the first night since the storm — and August, drifting at last toward sleep, did not yet know enough of this world's arithmetic to count its moons, and so did not see the small pale stranger rising in the east, one turn ahead of schedule, keeping a secret the Guild had paid centuries to keep.
