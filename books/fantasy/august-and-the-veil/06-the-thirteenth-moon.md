## 6. The Thirteenth Moon

Master Veyren kept the almanac where he kept all his true possessions: beneath the floor of his own humility.

The chamber was small, windowless, and lined with cedar that had been oiled so many decades it had gone the color of dark honey. It sat behind his private oratory, past the shelf of devotional texts he read aloud to visiting benefactors — *On the Stewardship of Wonders*, *The Patient Hand*, *Duties of the Keeper* — books written by men who understood that power, properly worn, should look like a burden. Veyren had authored two of them himself. He was rather proud of the third chapter of *The Patient Hand*. *A keeper does not seize the miracle; he prepares a place for it.* He had written that sentence at twenty-six, before he fully believed it, and had spent the thirty years since making himself into the kind of man who could.

He knelt now on the cold flagstones and pressed the carved rosette in the corner. The floor panel sighed open on hinges that had been greased with tallow and patience. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth against damp, lay the things the Guild of Impossible Things could not afford to have known about: three ledgers of original accounts, a sealed letter from the founder bearing a wax impression no living seal matched, and the almanac.

He lifted it out with both hands, the way one lifts an infant.

It was not a beautiful book. That was part of its genius. Bound in scuffed calfskin, its spine cracked, its brass clasps green with age, it looked like any of ten thousand farmer's almanacs sold at autumn fairs for a copper apiece — planting tables, weather lore, saints' days, the phases of the moon rendered in crude woodcuts. Any inspector who found it would yawn and move along. Only when you knew what to look for did the book confess itself.

Veyren carried it up the narrow stair to his study, where a single lamp burned behind green glass, and set it on the desk beside the ordinary almanac — the public one, the one whose twin hung in the guildhall kitchen so the cooks could plan around market days and tides.

He opened the false one first. The moon table there was correct, correct, always correct: twelve moons, waxing and waning in their tidy procession, new moon to full and back again, month upon month until the end of arithmetic. Every astronomer in every tower in every city would swear to it. The sky itself swore to it. Twelve moons. It had been twelve moons since before the Veil, since before the kingdoms, since men first thought to carve a notch in bone and call it counting.

Then he opened the true one.

The woodcut for the coming season showed thirteen circles.

The thirteenth moon was drawn smaller than its sisters, tucked into the lower margin of the page like a mistake, like something the woodcutter had added in a moment of honesty and been ashamed of. Its face was blank — no craters, no features, just a pale disc with a single line beneath it, which scholars of the founder's circle had spent generations arguing over. Veyren had settled the argument for himself long ago. The line was not a decoration. It was a horizon. The thirteenth moon was drawn rising.

He ran his thumb over the page, feeling the grain of paper that had been made, according to the colophon hidden in the binding, from reeds grown on the far side of the Veil. Paper from a country that officially did not exist. Ink mixed with ash from a fire that officially had never burned. The Guild's founding generation had built their greatest lie out of true materials. It was a lesson Veyren had taken to heart in everything he did.

*Thirteen turns,* the old marginalia said, in the founder's own cramped hand. *And on the thirteenth turn, the door remembers it is a door.*

Below that, another hand, later, more careful: *We sealed it because we were afraid. Let whoever finds this be braver.*

Veyren had read those lines perhaps four thousand times. He had stopped hearing them as words years ago. They were more like a heartbeat now, or a pulse in a wrist he held — proof that the thing he served was alive.

He took out his own notebook, plain and unremarkable, and began to calculate.

This was the work he loved best, and he permitted himself to love it only here, in this room, alone, where love could do no harm. The mathematics of the thirteenth moon were exquisite. It did not orbit as the others orbited; it *accumulated*. Each year it drifted a fraction — a fraction no naked eye could catch, a fraction the public almanac's rounding absorbed and erased — but across centuries the fraction compounded, and the moon that did not exist crept toward the sky that could not deny it. In the year of the founder's death it had been forty-one years distant from conjunction. His successor had inherited a gap of thirty-three. Veyren's own master, old Aldous the Lame, had died with the interval down to nineteen, muttering on his deathbed that God had been cruel to let him come so close and no closer.

Veyren had inherited nine.

That had been eleven years ago.

He wrote the figures now in his small, patient hand. Nine years of drift consumed. Two remaining after last winter's reckoning. And the correction — the mechanism, the engine, the great buried purpose of the Guild — which could close the final gap in a single night, if it was fed what it needed.

If it was fed a voice.

His pen paused above the page. Outside, the storm that had battered the guildhouse since dusk threw rain against the shutters like a creditor throwing stones. Somewhere below, the night scribes would be working late by lamplight, balancing accounts of impossible things — a debt of seven years' sleep owed to a sleeping knight, a widow's claim on her husband's ghost, the standard nonsense that kept the Guild respectable and solvent and utterly underestimated. Good. Let them count their small impossibilities. None of them knew they worked inside the largest one ever constructed.

None of them except Sefa, who suspected too much and could be trusted precisely because she wanted things. Wanting people were reliable people. And none of them except—

The child.

Veyren set down his pen and laced his fingers, and allowed himself, briefly, the luxury of thinking about the child without the discipline of purpose.

It had come through the storm tonight. Mud to the knees, hair plastered flat, standing at the guild door as though the door had called it — and perhaps it had; the threshold stones were older than the building and had never been entirely inert. The gatekeeper had nearly turned it away. A mud-soaked beggar child in a flood year was not, on its face, an impossible thing; the world produced those in abundance, and the Guild had never pretended to handle grief.

But then the child had spoken.

The porter's account — Veyren had taken it within the hour, sitting the man down with brandy and kindness, extracting every syllable — was that the words had been *wrong*. Not gibberish. Gibberish has a shapelessness to it, a chaos. These words had had too much shape, as though the child were speaking a language so old that the air itself had forgotten how to carry it, and each syllable landed slightly out of time with the world, like footsteps on stairs that aren't there. The lamps in the entry hall had guttered in sequence, left to right, like soldiers presenting arms.

And the ledgers.

Veyren's mouth went dry even now, remembering the porter's report of it. The ledger rack in the entry hall — the great public rack where debtors' books were shelved between visits — had *hummed*. Not fallen, not stirred. Hummed, low and resonant, forty volumes vibrating in sympathy like the strings of an instrument touched by a note too deep to hear. When the child stepped back from the desk, the humming stopped. When it leaned forward, it began again.

The porter thought it was the storm. The porter was a good man and a dull one, and Veyren had thanked him warmly, paid him a bonus against the wet season, and sent him home.

Then he had gone down to the entry hall himself, alone, and stood among the silent ledgers, and felt — there was no other word for it — felt the room *listening* for something it had lost.

He had given the child to Oscar Reyes within the hour.

Thinking of Reyes, Veyren experienced the complicated sensation he had learned to think of as stewardship. The scribe was competent beyond their station, careful, quiet, possessed of a memory that bordered on the unnatural and a hand that could forge a bishop's signature so perfectly that the bishop himself would doubt. Veyren had purchased Reyes's indenture eight years ago from a debtor's prison in Marrowgate, and in eight years the scribe had never once asked why the Guild needed a forger of such caliber. That restraint was worth more than talent. Talent was common. Restraint was priceless.

Reyes would transcribe the child. Reyes would parse the language, or enough of it. And whatever Reyes found would flow upward to Veyren, drop by drop, as water finds a cistern — because Reyes's debt was seventeen years long, and hope is the finest leash ever braided.

There would come a moment, Veyren knew, when the scribe would begin to ask questions. They always did; it was the flaw in the otherwise perfect temperament of scrupulous people. Scrupulous people believed the truth was a place, somewhere reachable, and that arriving there would set them free. Veyren had learned to leave little signposts along the route — partial truths, well-lit corridors — so that when the questions came, the answers were already waiting, pre-approved, and the questioner would feel clever rather than watched.

He was already composing the first signpost. Something about the child being a refugee from the borderlands, a speaker of a dying tongue, precious and fragile, requiring protection. Protection was a word that opened doors in generous people. He would deploy it within the week.

* * *

Near midnight, he went down to the vault.

The descent took eleven minutes if one did not hurry, and Veyren never hurried. The stair spiraled down through strata of the guildhouse's history — foundations laid over temple stones, temple stones laid over something older still, a ring of dressed granite that archaeologists would have wept over and which the Guild's founding charter described, in a single dry clause, as *the base, previously existing*. Previously existing. As though the thing below had simply been waiting for tenants.

Lamps burned at intervals, fed by an oil blend Sefa compounded herself; ordinary tallow smoked too much for the depth. The air grew cooler, then strangely warm, then cool again, passing through temperatures like passing through memories. By the ninth turn of the stair, Veyren could hear it: the sound he had heard perhaps six hundred times and which had never once become ordinary.

A ringing. Faint, vast, continuous — less a sound than the memory of one, the way your ears ring after a bell has stopped. The engine, idling.

The vault opened around him like the inside of a thought.

The orrery filled the cavern: rings within rings within rings, brass and silver and a dark metal no smith in the city could name, mounted on arms tall as trees, turning with a slowness that made motion feel like a form of patience. Twelve great spheres rode the outer rings — the twelve honest moons, each engraved with its proper name and phase-table. And at the center, on a pedestal of black glass, a thirteenth sphere no larger than a heart, polished to a mirror, waiting.

Around the walls, cut into the granite in characters older than any alphabet taught in any school, ran the inscription the founder's circle had copied from the temple stones and completed from the almanac's margins. Veyren had spent thirty years learning to read it. It was not a spell. Spells were for people who thought the world could be commanded. It was a *grammar* — a description of how the Veil was written, and therefore of how it might be edited.

The Veil was not a wall. That was the first lesson, and the hardest. The Veil was a sentence — a prohibition spoken so long ago and by so many voices together that it had hardened into physics. *Let there be a difference between us and them.* Spoken in the First Tongue, sustained by consent renewed in every generation without anyone knowing they were giving it, woven into oaths and borders and bedtime stories and the word *no*. You could not break such a sentence by force. Force was a foreign vocabulary; the sentence did not recognize it.

But a sentence could be *answered*. Answered in its own tongue, by a voice it recognized. And the engine — the beautiful, buried, patient machine — was nothing more than an amplifier, a throat, a means of making one voice large enough to be heard by the foundation of the world.

For three hundred years the engine had lacked only two things: the correct alignment of the moon that did not exist, and a voice in the First Tongue to key it.

Veyren stood beneath the turning rings and looked up at the thirteenth sphere, and felt what he always felt, and had long ago stopped apologizing to himself for it. Not ambition — he had trained that word out of his self-understanding decades ago, the way a surgeon trains out a tremor. What he felt was *responsibility*. The weight of a thing entrusted. Generations of keepers had carried this purpose without flinching, had died with the interval unfinished, and Veyren would not be the keeper who faltered at the threshold of completion.

Because the world beyond the Veil was dying. That was the second lesson, the one the almanac's margins recorded in hands that shook: the exiles, the First Speakers, the ones sealed out — their country was a wound that had never closed, and wounds left untended do not heal; they spread. Already the Veil thinned in places it should not thin. Already things crossed that were not supposed to cross, and the crossings were getting less accidental. Every year the border towns reported more dreams, more lights, more livestock born wrong. The choice was not between the Veil standing and falling. The choice was between the Veil opening *on purpose, in daylight, by treaty* — or tearing, in the dark, in blood, whenever the accumulated lie finally failed.

He was not opening a door. He was performing a controlled demolition of a condemned building, with all the care that phrase implied.

So he told himself, in this room, beneath the turning rings, and the telling had long ago worn smooth as a river stone.

Two years. Perhaps less, if the child proved what the ledgers' humming suggested. The engine needed a voice, and voices could be studied, strengthened, prepared — the way one prepares a choirboy, or a bride. There were phrases in the inscription that had to be spoken correctly, in sequence, by the keyed voice. There were fasts and anointments prescribed in the founder's notes. There was, noted once in a margin and never repeated, a cost — the ink there browned as though the writer had hesitated — but every great transaction has fees, and it was not a keeper's place to flinch at the price tag of salvation.

Veyren climbed the eleven-minute stair, restored the almanac beneath the floor of his humility, and knelt for a while in his oratory among the devotional books, hands folded, wearing the face he wore for God, whom he had never met and did not require.

* * *

He was awake past the second bell when the summons came — a soft triple knock at his study door, the agreed signal from the night watch. He opened the door on a junior scribe, gray-faced, holding a lamp that shook.

"Master. The child. It's speaking again — in its sleep, they think, but—" The boy swallowed. "The ledgers are doing it again. All of them. And Master Reyes is already down there, writing."

Veyren dressed without hurry. He chose the heavy robe, the one that made him look like what people needed a master to look like. At the door he paused, and the junior scribe flinched from whatever passed across his face — not anger, nothing so cheap. Recognition. The look of a man who has waited thirty years at a station and finally, down the line, sees the lantern swing.

"Fetch me the transcription when Reyes finishes," Veyren said gently. "And wake no one else. A wonder is a delicate thing. Too many eyes bruise it."

The boy fled. Veyren stood a moment in the dark of his study, listening to the storm spend itself against the shutters, and beneath the wind, faint and rhythmic, he could almost hear it — the hum rising through the floors, through the stone, through the bones of the building, the whole guildhouse become a sounding board for a voice older than walls.

Soon, he thought. On the thirteenth turn, the door remembers it is a door.

And I will be the one holding the handle.
