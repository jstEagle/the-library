## 7. Bargains in the Stairwell

The stairwell behind the counting room smelled of tallow, cold stone, and the particular sourness of old ink gone bad — a smell Oscar knew better than their own heartbeat. It was the smell of the Guild's underbelly, where the ledgers that didn't want to be found were kept, and where Sefa held court like a spider who had grown fond of her web.

Oscar descended the steps slowly, one hand trailing the wall. The stone was slick with condensation this time of year; the temple's bones wept in the wet season, and the Guild had never bothered to fix what the masons called *the building's honest grief*. Below, a single lamp burned behind the quartermaster's counter, and beneath it Sefa sat with her spectacles pushed up into iron-gray hair, weighing something small and silver on a scale that had no business being accurate to the grain.

She didn't look up. "You walk loud for a scribe."

"I walk like someone who doesn't want to be here."

"Then you're learning." She set down the weight, brushed her fingers across the little silver object — a seal matrix, Oscar realized, worn nearly smooth — and finally raised her eyes. They were pale as dishwater and missed nothing. "Sit. You've got the face of a man about to ask for something he can't afford."

"I'm not a man."

"You've got the face of a person about to ask for something they can't afford. Better?" A thin smile. "Sit anyway."

Oscar sat on the stool across the counter, which creaked with the particular indignity of furniture that had heard too many bargains. The lamp between them guttered, steadied. Somewhere above, muffled through three floors of archive, the guild bell rang the hour of Vespers, and Oscar counted it without meaning to — another hour of the indenture spent, another coin from a purse that emptied slower than hope.

Sefa waited. That was her gift, everyone knew it. Master Veyren could charm a room, could make even ruin sound like stewardship, but Sefa could outlast a glacier. She weighed things: ink, silver, secrets, silence. Everything had a mass, and everything had a price, and she had never once in twenty years been caught paying more than something was worth.

"The child," Oscar said at last.

"Mm." Sefa picked up her pen and made a small mark in a ledger open before her — not writing anything, just marking time, letting Oscar watch her control even the rhythm of the conversation. "What about the child?"

"What do you know about the Veil?"

The pen stopped.

For a long moment the only sound was the lamp hissing and, far below them — so far it might have been imagination — a low, rhythmic thrumming, like a heart buried in the temple's foundations. Oscar had heard it before, always in the deep places, always faintly, and had always told themselves it was water in the cisterns. Tonight they couldn't quite manage the lie.

"That," Sefa said quietly, "is an expensive question."

"I have money."

"You have debt." She said it without cruelty, the way one reads aloud a number already known to both parties. "Fourteen years, two months, eleven days remaining on your indenture. I balance your account every quarter. You are worth exactly what the Guild paid for you and somewhat less than what you cost." She folded her hands. "So no. You don't have money. You have something better, if you're brave enough to spend it."

Oscar's mouth went dry. "Which is?"

"Access." Sefa leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the fine network of wrinkles around her eyes, each one earned, each one a record of a bargain witnessed. "You transcribe for the Master now. Every word the child says goes through your pen before it goes through anyone else's ears. Do you understand what that makes you? Not a servant. A *gate*. And gates, scribe, are the only currency in this building that appreciates."

The thrumming came again, a little louder, or perhaps Oscar was only listening harder now. They thought of August asleep upstairs in the guest cell that wasn't quite a cell, guarded by locks that hadn't yet been installed because Veyren wanted the child comfortable, cooperative, *unafraid*. They thought of the words August spoke that no living scholar could parse, and of the way the ink behaved when Oscar set them down — how it seemed less like transcription and more like *translation*, as though the language were writing itself through Oscar's hand and merely tolerating the alphabet.

They thought, too, of the forged entry. Three nights ago, small and terrible: a line added to the requisition log, shifting two vials of binding-salts from the stores into the Master's private inventory, where no one audited. A test. A sin. A door opened in themselves that Oscar had sworn was locked.

Guilt sat in their chest now like a swallowed stone. But resolve was growing around it, the way pearl grows around grit — layer by layer, until the wound becomes something valuable.

"I can't give you the child's words," Oscar said. "Not directly. If the Master learned—"

"He'd erase you." Sefa said it flatly, without drama. "Not kill. Erase. There's a difference, and the difference is a ledger. Your name comes out of every book in this building, and then there is no you to find, no debt to collect, no record that a person named Oscar Reyes ever signed an indenture. You'd be a rumor with a body." She smiled thinly. "I've seen it done once. The man kept walking around for years afterward. Kept asking people who he was. Very sad. Very tidy."

*Erase.* The word went through Oscar like cold water through cloth. They had spent their whole indenture dreaming of freedom, and here was its dark twin: freedom not as release but as annihilation, the debt dissolved by dissolving the debtor.

"And what would you want," Oscar asked carefully, "for telling me about the Veil?"

"Three entries." Sefa held up fingers, gnarled as tree roots. "First: whatever the child says about doors, thresholds, crossings. Verbatim, in your own hand, delivered here. Second: notice of any new orders regarding the child — schedules, movements, visitors. Third—" she paused, and her dishwater eyes did something almost gentle, "—a favor to be named later. Unspecified. Payable when I call it."

"No." The word came out faster than Oscar intended. "Not the third. An open favor to a quartermaster is just debt wearing a different coat. I'm trying to get *out* of debt."

"Everyone is." Sefa resumed her weighing, unbothered. "And everyone ends up owing someone. The only choice you ever get is *whom*. Choose me. I am cheap compared to the alternative." She nodded upward, toward the floors above, toward the office where Master Veyren kept his almanacs and his soft voice and his plans. "That man will own you forever, scribe, and he'll make you grateful for it. I only want to own you briefly."

The lamp flickered. In its stutter, the shadows on the vaulted ceiling seemed to move wrong — too fluid, too deliberate — and Oscar thought again of the ink rearranging itself on the page, meaning assembling itself out of strokes that should have meant nothing. The world had felt thinner since August arrived. As though the Veil itself were listening.

"One entry," Oscar countered. "Thresholds and crossings, verbatim. Nothing else. No open favors."

"Two entries and the favor, but the favor is capped." Sefa's smile sharpened. "Whatever I ask, it cannot be a crime against the child. I don't deal in children. Old rule. Older than me."

It was, Oscar would think later, the strangest mercy the Guild had ever shown them — hidden inside a bargain, wrapped in usury, priced like everything else. But tonight they only felt the scales tipping, the stone of guilt shifting against the growing pearl of resolve.

"Agreed," they said. "Capped favor. Two entries. Now talk."

Sefa blew out a breath, and for the first time since Oscar had known her, she looked tired. She reached beneath the counter and drew up a book — not a ledger, older than any ledger, bound in hide that might have been human and might have been goat and had stopped caring about the difference. Its pages were the color of old teeth.

"Before the kingdoms," she said, "there was one tongue. Not the first language men invented — the language that was here *before men*, the one the world speaks to itself when it thinks no one's listening. The First Tongue. And the people who spoke it natively — the First Speakers — they didn't live on our side of anything. There was no side. There was one world, whole, and it was loud."

She turned the ancient book open to a page dense with diagrams: concentric rings, a circle scored through with a single line, figures like moths pressed between worlds.

"Then came the war. Not our war — a war *in* the language itself. A schism among the Speakers over how reality ought to be addressed. Polite forms versus imperative, if you can believe such a thing caused an apocalypse. It did. When words can unmake, disagreement becomes catastrophe. So the survivors did the only merciful terrible thing available." Her finger traced the circle bisected by the line. "They spoke a Separation. A sentence so large it took nine voices nine days to finish, and when it was done, the world was two, and the seam between them was the Veil. Our ancestors weren't the heroes of that story, scribe. We were the ones who got walled *in*. Or walled *out*. Depends which side of the sentence you were born on."

"The child," Oscar said. Their voice sounded strange to themselves. "August speaks it."

"Natively." Sefa closed the book with a soft, final sound. "Which means the child isn't speaking a language. The child is speaking a *place*. Every word is a piece of the other side, carried across. And every piece carried across weakens the mortar." She looked up. "Now ask me why the Guild has been buying First Tongue speakers for four hundred years, and why we keep them in archives instead of universities, and why the Master smiles like a man reading a will he wrote himself."

Oscar's blood had gone slow and heavy. "Why?"

"Wrong question. Right question is: what does the Guild *own* that needs a native speaker to activate?" Sefa began putting away her scale, her weights, her terrible old book, each item returned to its place with the tenderness of ritual. "Because the Guild never collects knowledge for its own sake. Knowledge is inventory. Inventory exists to be *used*. And somewhere in this temple there is a thing that has been waiting four centuries for a voice it was built to hear."

The thrumming rose once more beneath them — and this time Oscar was certain. It wasn't water. It had a rhythm. It had *patience*. Something under the temple was keeping time, and had been keeping it for longer than the Guild had kept books.

"Our bargain stands," Sefa said briskly, all weariness shuttered away again. "Two entries. Thresholds and crossings. Bring me the first within the week, and bring it written in your left hand — I want handwriting that can't be traced to the transcription copies."

Oscar stood. Their legs felt borrowed. At the foot of the stairs they paused, one hand on the weeping stone.

"Sefa. The sound. Under the temple. What is it?"

The quartermaster adjusted her spectacles, and behind the glass her eyes were unreadable as closed books.

"Count the moons sometime," she said. "Carefully. All of them."

* * *

Oscar climbed back up through the archive levels with Sefa's words lodged in them like a splinter working toward the heart. The stacks rose on either side, black shelving vanishing into darkness overhead, ten thousand ledgers sleeping in their thousands of years of accumulated debt. The Guild recorded everything. Debts, births, purchases, prophecies, sins. Somewhere in these shelves was a book with Oscar's name in it, and fourteen years of their life measured out in columns, and somewhere else — older, deeper — perhaps a book with a child's name in it, dated before the child existed.

They passed the transcription room and stopped.

Through the open door, lamplight fell across the desk where August's words waited in Oscar's own hand, forty pages of a language that wrote meaning instead of sound. And there, on the topmost page, Oscar saw it fresh, the way you see a familiar room when someone has moved a chair:

Among the careful rows of translated glyphs, one phrase recurred. August said it in sleep. August said it at meals, to the bread, to the rain against the glass. August had said it the first night, mud-soaked and shivering at the guild door, and Oscar had transcribed it faithfully without understanding:

*The door remembers who opened it.*

Oscar stood in the doorway a long time, the guilt-stone and the resolve-pearl turning slowly together in their chest. Then they went to find paper, and ink, and their left hand, and began — stroke by careful stroke — to pay a debt to a woman who weighed everything, while beneath the floor something patient counted moons toward a number that had not yet arrived.

Above the archive, through the high slit windows, the sky was clearing after the storm. And there, faint as a watermark, hung a moon that Oscar had never been taught to see — a sliver of pale light where the almanacs swore there should be none.

Thirteen, whispered the ink on the page, in a language older than walls.

The count has begun.
