## 8. What August Remembers

The dream always began with water.

Not rain — August knew rain now, had learned its many voices in the weeks since the Guild took them in: the soft gossip of it on slate roofs, the hard drumming on the courtyard cobbles, the way it hissed in the gutters like something whispering secrets it didn't want found. This was not that. This was a sea, slow and vast and warm as breath, moving beneath a city built on its back.

August stood on a bridge of white stone — no, not white. The stone was every color at once, the way an oyster shell is, shifting when you tilted your head. The bridge arched over canals where lights drifted like lazy fish: lanterns without fire, glowing softly green-gold, bobbing along on currents that sang. That was the thing about the city in the dream. Everything sang. Not with mouths or throats but with *being* — the walls hummed, the water hummed, the air itself was thick with a music that wasn't sound so much as meaning, meaning poured out into the open for anyone to drink.

August knew the words for it. That was the terrible part, the part that made waking hurt. In the dream, August understood every syllable of the city's song, because the city spoke the First Tongue, and the First Tongue was not a language you learned. It was a language you *were*.

*Vess-ah-nu-il,* the bridges said. *I hold you up because I choose to.*

*Keth-mara-soth,* said the drifting lights. *We remember what you forget.*

And the sea beneath everything, deep and patient, said only one word, over and over, the way a mother says a child's name:

*Home. Home. Home.*

In the dream, August walked the bridge toward a tower made of glass that had been blown by no wind and shaped by no hand — glass grown like coral, branching, full of small rooms where people sat writing. Not with ink. They wrote with their fingers in the air, and the letters hung there glowing, and other people came and read them and wept or laughed, and then the letters dissolved into the general song.

August had never seen this place. August was almost certain of that. Seven years old, mud-stained, plucked off a road in a storm by a guild that smelled of dust and lamp oil and old paper — what life could August possibly have lived that held a city like this?

And yet.

Memory is a strange country. It has borders that don't show on any map, and sometimes you cross one without knowing, and find yourself standing in a field you have never planted but somehow recognize every furrow. When August woke each morning in the narrow bed in the Guild's east attic, with its blanket that smelled of cedar chest and its window that looked out on a courtyard where scribes hurried with their heads down like people afraid of the sky, the dream did not fade the way dreams were supposed to. It stayed. It sat behind August's teeth all day, humming.

---

There were other fragments too, smaller ones, that came not in sleep but in odd waking moments, like sparks struck from a flint.

The taste of fruit that didn't grow anywhere in this world — sweet and smoky at once, like a plum that had dreamed of being bread. August had bitten into an apple from the Guild kitchen's stores two days after arriving and had nearly cried, not from disappointment exactly, but from the *wrongness*, the way a song played one note flat makes your whole body flinch.

The feeling of being carried. Not by arms — by *wind*. A great warm wind that held you the way water holds a leaf, and voices all around saying *faster, faster, they are coming,* and the wind saying nothing at all but simply obeying, because in the First Tongue, to name a thing truly is to be obeyed by it.

And once — only once, on the fourth night, half-asleep — the sound of a door closing. An enormous door, stone on stone, the kind of sound you feel in your ribs. And behind the door, muffled but unmistakable, singing. Hundreds of voices. Thousands. Singing the same word over and over, and the word was not *home*. The word was *remember us*.

August had woken then, heart slamming, and lain in the dark listening to the Guild's small sounds — the settling of beams, a mouse in the wall, Master Veyren's boots crossing the hall below at an hour when honest men were asleep — and had thought, with a clarity that felt older than seven years old:

*I am on the wrong side of that door.*

---

They studied August here. August understood that much, even if the studying wore gentle faces.

There was the round woman with ink-stained fingers who brought meals and asked bright, harmless questions — *do you like honey on your bread, do you sleep well, what a lovely coat, is it wool?* — while her eyes flicked constantly to the slate she kept on her lap, on which she wrote down everything August answered, and several things August didn't.

There was the tall man who measured things. He measured August's height against a marked doorframe; he measured the space between pupils; he held a cold glass lens before one eye and then the other and murmured numbers to an assistant. Once he asked August to sing. August sang the only song worth singing, three notes of the First Tongue, just *keth-mara-soth*, we remember what you forget — and the tall man dropped his lens. It cracked on the floorboards and he stared at August the way people stare at a fire that has jumped the hearth.

And there was Master Veyren himself, who came in the evenings, smelling of candle smoke and something colder underneath, like the air off a frozen lake. Veyren never measured anything. Veyren only listened, with his head tipped slightly, his gray eyes patient as a spider's, and afterward he would smile and say, *Tomorrow, perhaps a little more,* and leave, and August would feel the room feel emptier than before he'd entered it.

But there was one person who did not study August. Or rather — who studied, but kept apologizing for it with their whole body.

The scribe. Oscar.

Oscar came with paper and pens and a bottle of ink the color of a night sky, and sat across the little table, and asked August to speak. But Oscar asked differently than the others. The others asked the way you dig at a locked box with a knife. Oscar asked the way you sit beside a bird that has flown into a window, and wait, and let it decide when it is ready to move.

Oscar also had a habit that fascinated August beyond all reason: whenever August spoke the First Tongue, Oscar wrote it down — and then, thinking no one watched, Oscar would mouth the sounds silently, shaping them, tasting them. Over and over. *Keth.* *Mara.* *Soth.* Like someone learning a prayer in secret, ashamed of praying.

On the sixth evening, August decided.

When Oscar dipped the pen and looked up with those careful, careful eyes, August leaned across the table and said — slowly, gently, the way you hand someone something breakable —

"*Il-vessa. Nu-keth il. Soth-mara.*"

*I see you. You carry remembering. Do not be afraid of the song.*

Oscar's pen stopped. Ink gathered at the nib in a fat black bead and fell onto the page, blooming like a small dark flower. Oscar's lips moved, repeating the shapes without voice, and August watched something happen in the scribe's face — a door opening in a house long shut.

"You're telling me—" Oscar began, and stopped, and tried again. "That first part. *Il-vessa.* What does it—"

But August never got to answer. Boots in the corridor. The round woman with her slate, come to fetch the transcription. The moment folded itself away like a letter.

Still. That night, lying in the cedar-smelling dark, August turned the moment over and over like a smooth stone found on a path. *Hope* was a dangerous thing — August knew this the way the body knows falling. Hope made you look at doors. Hope made you count the people between you and them. Hope made the attic bed feel less like a nest and more like a cage with a view.

But hope was also the only thing that tasted right.

---

It was that same night, or near enough — the ninth night, though August had lost exact count — that the dream changed.

The city rose as always: the singing water, the shell-colored bridges, the coral-glass tower full of writers. August walked the main bridge toward the tower, and the lanterns drifted close, murmuring *we remember what you forget*, and the sea said *home, home, home* beneath everything.

But this time, someone was waiting on the bridge.

A figure stood at the arch's crown, silhouetted against the tower's glow, and even before August could make out details, the knowing arrived the way it always did in dreams — complete, instant, unearned. August knew this person. Had known them forever. The way you know your own hands.

The figure turned.

It was Oscar.

Not the Oscar of the Guild attic — this one wore different clothes, robes the color of deep water, and their face was younger and older at once, the way faces in dreams are. But the eyes were the same. Those careful, apologetic, hungry eyes. Eyes that read everything and signed nothing.

"August," the dream-Oscar said, and the name in the First Tongue sounded different here — not a name but a *title*, ringing like a bell through the bridge stones. "*Il-soth-august-mara.* Remembered-one, keeper-of-the-song."

"I don't understand," August said, which was a strange thing to say in a dream where understanding poured from every wall.

"You will." Dream-Oscar knelt, bringing their face level with August's, and took both of August's hands in theirs. Their grip was warm and real and trembling slightly, and August thought: *dreams shouldn't tremble.* "Listen to me. There isn't much time. The Veil is thin here — thinner than they know, thinner than even I knew, until—" The dream-Oscar pressed their lips together, and August saw grief move across the borrowed face like weather. "Until I made a mistake. A long time ago. Longer than you've been alive. Longer than this body has been alive."

"What mistake?"

"I promised something," dream-Oscar said. "To people on the other side of the door. I promised them they would not be forgotten. That someone would come who remembered them *rightly* — not as monsters, not as myths, but as what they are." The grip tightened. "And then the door closed, and I was on this side, and I forgot. Forgetting is what this side does best. But promises made in the First Tongue don't die. They sleep. And they wait for a mouth."

"A mouth," August repeated.

"You." Dream-Oscar's eyes were wet now, and the lanterns had drifted near, circling them both, whispering *we remember, we remember.* "You are my promise, August. Grown into a child. Given feet and a voice. They sent you through because I swore it, and sworn words travel farther than armies."

"But I don't remember how to get back. I don't remember the way through the Veil, or the words, or—"

"You don't need to remember alone." Dream-Oscar smiled, and it was Oscar's smile exactly, the one that kept apologizing for existing. "That is why I am telling you this now, on this side of your sleeping. Find me. The me with ink on their fingers and fear in their throat. The me who tastes the words when they think no one sees. That one still carries the promise in their blood, even if the mind has lost it. Teach them. One word at a time if you must. And when the thirteenth moon rises—"

The bridge shuddered.

The singing faltered — every voice at once, the water, the walls, the lanterns, the sea — as if the whole city had drawn a single horrified breath. Far off, past the tower, past the shining districts, August saw it: a crack of darkness splitting the horizon, and through the crack, a sound like a door of stone grinding toward its frame.

"No," dream-Oscar breathed. "No, it's early, it isn't time—" They gripped August's shoulders now, urgent, the careful composure cracking clean through. "Listen. Listen! When the moon comes — the moon that shouldn't exist — they will try to use you. To force the door. But a door forced breaks, and what's behind it will not come gently. You must not let them *force* it. Do you hear me? It must be *opened*. Opened by consent, by truth, by—"

The grinding grew louder. The lanterns scattered like startled fish. The beautiful city dimmed at its edges, guttering like candles in wind, and dream-Oscar pulled August close, and the last thing August felt was the warmth of those hands and heard the last thing they said, shouted now over the dying song:

"—and August — whatever they tell you — whatever they *write about you* — remember that ledgers lie, but the tongue does not. Find the one with my face. Tell them: *the debt was never yours to pay—*"

The door slammed.

August woke sitting upright in the narrow bed, both hands outstretched in the dark, palms burning as if they'd actually held someone. Cedar smell. Mouse in the wall. Rain beginning again against the roof, soft and ordinary.

For a long time August didn't move. The dream clung like wet cloth. The city's song still echoed somewhere behind the sternum, fading note by note, and August caught themself humming it aloud before stopping, frightened — the round woman collected everything on her slate, even humming, probably.

*Find the one with my face.*

Outside, far below, a door opened and closed. Boots crossed the courtyard — quick, purposeful, unfamiliar strides, accompanied by the jingle of harness and the low murmur of the gatekeeper's greeting. Someone new arriving at the Guild in the grey hour before dawn.

August slid from the bed to the window, breath fogging the cold glass, and looked down.

Below, shaking rain from a traveling cloak in the courtyard's lamplight, stood a stranger — tall, weathered, armed, with a hunter's easy stance and a face that made the gatekeeper bow. And beside the stranger, holding an umbrella neither of them used, wrapped in a nightrobe despite the hour, smiling his patient spider's smile up at the window — up, August realized with a lurch, directly at *August's* window —

stood Master Veyren.

He was looking at the arrival the way a man looks at a key he has waited years to fit a lock. And as the stranger handed over sealed papers, papers stamped with the borderlands' mark, August's own words from the dream came back to them unbidden, whispered in their own voice in the dark room:

*A door forced breaks.*

Down in the courtyard, Veyren laughed at something the stranger said — a warm, carrying laugh, a host's laugh — and led the newcomer inside out of the rain.

August stood at the window a long while after the courtyard emptied, watching the rain stitch silver threads through the lamplight, one hand pressed flat against the cold glass.

*Hope is a dangerous thing.* But it had already taken root, and dangerous things, once rooted, do not ask permission to grow. Somewhere in this building of dust and ledgers was a person wearing a promise's face. And tomorrow — tomorrow August would find a way to say one more word to them. Just one more.

*Soth-mara. Do not be afraid of the song.*

Because the moon that shouldn't exist was coming. August could feel it already, faint as a splinter under the skin, pulling at the tide behind their ribs.

And the door was starting to grind.
