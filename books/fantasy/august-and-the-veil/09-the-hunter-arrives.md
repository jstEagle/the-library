## 9. The Hunter Arrives

The ravens came back before she did.

Oscar noticed them first from the scriptorium window — a wheel of black shapes turning over the eastern road, where the borderlands gave way to the guild's cultivated fields and the fields to the long causeway that crossed the marsh. Ravens meant the hunting parties were returning. Ravens in that particular circling pattern, low and patient, meant the party had come home whole. The birds followed Ilara Vance the way debt followed Oscar: not out of affection, but because they knew where the bodies were.

Oscar's pen stopped mid-word. A blot of ink swelled on the page like a bruise, and they watched it spread with the peculiar detachment of someone watching their own heart do something foolish.

*She's early,* Oscar thought. *Three weeks early. Which means either the border went quiet, or something at the border made it quiet.*

Neither possibility was comforting. Both possibilities meant Veyren had sent for her.

The Guild of Impossible Things did not employ hunters for its impossible things. Impossible things, as a rule, could not be caught. The hunters existed for the other category — the possible things the guild wanted anyway. Stolen manuscripts. Runaway apprentices. Debts that had grown legs. And now, apparently, mud-soaked children who spoke in a language older than the walls that held them.

Oscar blotted the page, capped the inkwell, and told themselves the quickening in their chest was only dread.

It was a lie, and they were a scribe, and lies were their trade, so at least it was a well-made one.

* * *

They heard her before they saw her, which was also true of storms and of certain kinds of grief. Boots on the entry hall flagstones — not the careful tread of guild clerks, who had learned to walk softly around their own ambitions, but the flat, unhurried stride of someone who had spent six months walking through country where softness got you killed. Then the smell of her arrived ahead of her body: woodsmoke, wet leather, pine tar, and beneath it all something that was simply *Ilara*, a scent Oscar's memory had been quietly preserving against their will for two years, like a pressed flower in a book they'd sworn never to open again.

Oscar was carrying a stack of transcription folios down the west stair when she came through the archway, and there was no time to decide what face to wear, so they wore the only one available: the blank, deferential mask of a guild scribe.

Ilara Vance stopped three steps into the hall.

She had changed. That was the first thing — not the new scar splitting her left eyebrow, not the grey coming in early at her temples, though those were changes too. It was the way she held herself. Two years ago she had moved like water finding its level, loose and laughing, always half-leaning toward whatever amused her. Now she stood like a drawn bow. Like someone who had learned that every posture was a position, and every position could be defended or lost.

Her eyes found Oscar across the hall with the terrible efficiency of a hunter's eyes, and for one unguarded half-second — less than a heartbeat, barely enough time to be denied later — something moved behind them. Something old. Something that looked, absurdly, like relief.

Then it was gone, shuttered behind duty, and she inclined her head the precise degree required between a hunter and a guild scribe.

"Reyes." Her voice was lower than Oscar remembered. Rougher. Border-country voice, wind-scoured. "Still balancing the impossible books?"

"Someone has to," Oscar said. "The impossible doesn't reconcile itself."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, more the ghost of where a smile used to live, like a hearth long cold but still holding the shape of the fire. "I'd have thought the guild would've worked you to bone meal by now."

"They tried. Bone meal is very good at arithmetic."

The joke landed somewhere between them and lay there, neither of them picking it up. Around them the entry hall went about its business — porters hauling crates, an under-clerk hurrying past with his arms full of candle requisitions, two novices whispering by the notice board — but the space where Oscar and Ilara stood had gone strangely quiet, the way a room does when two people are having a conversation that is mostly happening underneath the conversation.

"You look well," Ilara said, and it came out wrong, stiff, like a phrase translated from another language. She seemed to hear it herself. Her jaw tightened.

"You look like the border," Oscar said, which was truer and therefore safer.

"That's one word for it." She shifted the strap of her pack — a heavy thing, scarred leather, a hunter's pack — and Oscar understood suddenly, with a lurch, why she was standing in the entry hall instead of reporting to the master's office. Why she was here, in the traffic of the main floor, where any passing clerk might see her speaking too long to a scribe. She was waiting to be summoned. And she was filling the waiting with him.

"Veyren sent for you," Oscar said. Not a question.

"Raven reached us at Coldmere Ford. Three weeks' ride compressed into nineteen days." Her eyes moved over Oscar's face with that same efficient sweep, cataloguing — the shadows under their eyes, the ink stain on their knuckle, the way they held the folios slightly too tight. Hunters read people the way scribes read pages. "He didn't say what for. Just that I was to come at once and report to no one but him."

"He'll tell you it's a guarding detail."

"Do you know that, or are you guessing?"

"I transcribe his correspondence." Oscar kept their voice level, bored, the voice of a man discussing weather. "There's a child. Came to the gate during the storm, some weeks back. Speaks a language nobody recognizes. The master has taken an interest."

"An interest." Ilara repeated the words slowly, tasting them, and her expression did something complicated. Anyone who had served the guild longer than a season knew what *the master has taken an interest* meant. It meant the thing was already dead; it just hadn't been informed yet. "And you're telling me this out of clerical thoroughness."

"I'm telling you because you asked."

"I haven't asked anything yet."

"No," Oscar agreed. "You're working up to it."

For a moment — just a moment — the drawn-bow stance softened, and Ilara Vance looked at Oscar Reyes the way she had looked at them across a campfire in the Ashvale hills, two years and a lifetime ago, when the world had still contained rooms where the two of them could be nobody in particular. *You always did answer questions I hadn't asked,* she'd said once, laughing, her cold feet hooked under Oscar's warmer ones. *It's going to get you killed.* Or maybe she'd said *it's why I love you*. Memory was a bad archive. Things got misfiled. Things got forged.

"Oscar." She said their name quietly, and the hall's noise rushed back in around it. "Whatever this is. Whatever he wants guarded. If it's what I think—"

"Master Veyren."

Veyren's voice preceded him the way cold precedes frost — not loud, never loud, but arriving everywhere at once. He came down the grand stair with his hands folded behind his back, silver-haired and serene, dressed in the plain dark wool he affected precisely because everyone knew he could afford cloth woven with actual moonlight. His gaze touched Oscar without pausing, the way a man's gaze touches furniture, and settled on Ilara with warm, proprietary approval.

"Hunter Vance. Nineteen days from Coldmere. I said at once, and the guild got better than it asked for." He smiled. Veyren's smiles were famous for making people feel chosen. "Come. Walk with me. Your charge is waiting, and I imagine you have questions."

"Master." Ilara bowed. When she rose, her eyes cut to Oscar — one last glance, quick as a knife thrown backward — and then she turned and followed Veyren toward the east wing, toward the room where August lived behind a door with a new lock, and Oscar stood alone in the entry hall holding a stack of folios that had gone very heavy in their arms.

*If it's what I think,* she had started to say.

What did Ilara Vance think? What had the raven from Coldmere actually said? Oscar climbed the west stair slowly, mind churning, and arrived at an answer they didn't like: it didn't matter what she thought. What mattered was that Veyren had sent for *her*. Out of every hunter on the guild's rolls — and there were forty, hardened, loyal, interchangeable — he had sent for the one person alive who could walk straight through Oscar's defenses by saying their name in a certain tone of voice.

That wasn't coincidence. Veyren did not deal in coincidence. He dealt in levers, and he had just gone looking for Oscar's, and he had found it sleeping in the borderlands wearing a hunter's badge.

* * *

Oscar saw them together that evening, through the narrow window of the transcription gallery that overlooked August's dayroom.

The room had been prepared, in the guild's way, to look like kindness. Good light. A fire. Books, toys, a table set with honey cakes that no one had explained to August were bait. The child sat cross-legged on the rug, small and watchful, hands folded in their lap, and even from above — even through glass — Oscar could see the posture of a creature deciding whether the hand reaching for it held food or a snare.

Ilara crouched in front of the child. Not looming. Crouching, the way you'd approach a frightened animal or a suspicious child, which amounted to the same discipline. She said something — Oscar couldn't hear it through the glass — and offered her hand, palm up, empty.

August looked at the hand for a long time. Then, with the grave deliberation of a judge accepting evidence, the child took it.

Oscar exhaled a breath they hadn't known they were holding, and hated themselves a little for it. Because it was good — it was genuinely good — that August had someone near them who knew how to be gentle without being false. And it was catastrophic, because that same someone would report everything. Every word August spoke. Every habit. Every hour the child spent staring at the transcription gallery window, which August did sometimes, head tilted, as if they could see Oscar through the glass and through the language both, as if they knew exactly who sat up here writing down their soul in borrowed ink.

Ilara would guard August. Ilara would also guard August *from* people, and Oscar was rapidly becoming one of the people August needed guarding from.

Below, Ilara glanced up — hunters always knew when they were watched — and found Oscar's face at the window. Neither of them moved. Glass between them, thirty feet of air, two years of silence, and the whole weight of the guild's architecture pressing down on the moment. Then Ilara turned back to the child, and Oscar stepped away from the window, and the gallery felt colder than the corridor outside.

* * *

She found them at midnight, which was how Oscar knew it was serious. Ilara Vance had never in her life observed curfew, but she had always observed the difference between public and private, and midnight was private's territory.

A soft knock — two, then one, an old rhythm, theirs once — and Oscar opened the scriptorium door to find her leaning against the frame with a bottle of plum brandy from some border distillery and an expression carefully arranged into casualness, which on Ilara was more alarming than anger.

"The guards change at the half-hour," she said. "Your corridor's clear till then."

"You checked my corridor."

"I check everything. It's the job." She held up the bottle. "Five minutes. For old times' sake, which is a stupid phrase, but I couldn't think of a better one on the road."

Oscar should have said no. Every prudent instinct, every lesson the last weeks had burned into them, said *close the door, scribe, say your ledger is full.* Instead they stepped aside, because two years was a long time, and because some part of them wanted to know if the woman inside the hunter was still in there, and because — they admitted it only in the privacy of their own skull — because Veyren had sent for her, and Oscar needed to know what she knew.

They drank from tin cups among the shelves. The brandy tasted like smoke and stone fruit and distance. For a while neither spoke, and the silence was almost — *almost* — the old kind.

"She talks in her sleep," Ilara said finally. "The child. Words I don't know, but they have a shape. Like a prayer, or a list." She turned the cup in her fingers. "You've heard it. In your transcriptions."

"Some."

"What is it? What does the guild think it is?"

Careful, Oscar told themselves. Careful. "The master believes it may be a dialect from before the Concordance. Possibly pre-kingdom. He wants it documented."

"And what do *you* believe?"

Oscar looked at her. Firelight from the banked lamp moved on her face, catching the new scar, the tired eyes, the mouth that had once known how to find his in the dark without looking. Two years ago he had watched her choose the guild over him — not cruelly, never cruelly; Ilara did nothing cruelly except perhaps to herself — had watched her sign the hunter's oath with steady hands while Oscar stood in the doorway of the signing hall holding words they'd rehearsed for a week and never said. *Come with me instead,* those words had been. They had died unspoken, and their ghost had haunted every ledger since.

"I believe," Oscar said slowly, "that the master's interests and the child's interests are not the same thing. And I believe you're going to be asked to stand between them."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I can afford."

Ilara set down her cup. When she spoke again her voice had dropped, gone rough at the edges. "Nineteen days on the road, Oscar, I had nothing to do but think, and most of what I thought about was you, which annoyed me considerably. And I kept coming back to one thing. You wrote me. After I took the oath. One letter, and then nothing."

"I wrote—"

"One letter." She held up a hand. "And I've thought about that letter for two years, and I want to know why a scribe — a *guild* scribe, indentured, careful, the most cautious man I ever slept beside — would write me four pages and sign none of them. No name. No mark. Nothing. I thought it was bitterness. Then I thought it was fear. Now—" she leaned forward, and her eyes were the hunter's eyes again, sharp and sorry all at once, "—now I'm standing in a guild full of locked doors and a child who speaks the end of the world, and I'm thinking maybe you signed nothing because you already knew what it costs to put your name on things."

The lamp flame shivered. Somewhere in the stacks, a page turned with no hand to turn it.

"Midnight's over, Hunter Vance," Oscar said quietly. "Your corridor won't stay clear forever."

Ilara held their gaze a moment longer — searching, wounded, unconvinced — then rose, took the bottle, and paused at the door with her hand on the frame.

"For what it's worth," she said, not turning around, "the letter was the best thing anyone ever gave me. I still have it. I've read it so many times the creases are going soft." A pause. "Whoever you were when you wrote it — I'd like to meet them again someday. Before this place finishes making you into somebody else."

Then she was gone, boots fading down the corridor, and Oscar stood alone among the listening shelves with two cups and a heart behaving like a badly kept account.

Because that was the trouble. That was the whole trouble, laid bare in five minutes and a cup of plum brandy: Ilara Vance had been sent here as a lock, and she was the one key in the world that fit Oscar, and Veyren knew it, and sooner or later — probably sooner — Oscar was going to have to choose whether to let her turn.

And below, faint through the floor, so faint it might have been the building settling, a child's voice was singing in its sleep in a language older than walls — and the ledgers on the shelf were humming along.
