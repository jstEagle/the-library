## 10. Ledger Fire

The trouble with a secret is that it breathes.

Oscar had learned this in the third year of their indenture, when they had hidden a stolen plum in the toe of their boot and spent the whole day aware of it — the small warm weight of it, the way it seemed to pulse against their ankle like a second heart. A secret was the same. It had weight. It had warmth. It insisted on being noticed even when no one else could see it.

For six days now, Oscar had carried two secrets through the halls of the Guild of Impossible Things: that August's language wrote meaning instead of sound, and that Oscar had begun — carefully, guiltily, in the margins of their own private notebook — to learn it.

The first secret sat in the transcription folio under lock and key, as ordered. The second lived closer to the skin, folded into the lining of Oscar's coat, written in a cipher of their own devising that was really no cipher at all but simply the First Tongue itself, which no one else in the building could read and therefore needed no disguising. There was an elegance to that. The safest hiding place for a forbidden thing was inside another forbidden thing, the way a smuggler hides one jewel inside a larger stone.

The transcription itself had grown strange over those six days. Oscar transcribed each session faithfully — every syllable August spoke rendered into Guild-standard glyphs, the phonetic notation Master Veyren demanded, precise as anatomy. But the pages did not stay put. By lamplight at night, reviewing the day's work, Oscar would find that certain passages had shifted. Not the words themselves; the *weight* of them. A line recording August saying *the water remembers the shape of the cup* would read, by morning, with an urgency it hadn't possessed the night before, as though the ink had spent the hours dark deciding what it meant and had grown more certain in its opinion.

Oscar had taken to reading the folio aloud in a whisper, just to see if the words behaved differently when given breath.

They did. But Oscar was not yet ready to write down what they did.

* * *

On the seventh day, the fog came off the canal in thick grey ropes and wound itself around the guildhouse like a patient animal, and the lamps in the archive burned all morning because the windows gave back nothing but white. It was the kind of day when paper felt damp and ink dried slow, when the whole building smelled of tallow and wet stone and the faint mineral tang of the vellum vault below. Oscar was at their desk in the transcription room, three floors up from the vault, copying out the previous evening's session while August sat across the table under Ilara Vance's watchful eye, drawing shapes in spilled salt because Veyren had decreed that idle hands made idle tongues.

Ilara had been assigned to them both for four days now. She stood by the door with her hunter's stillness, arms crossed, hood down, and she had not once looked directly at Oscar longer than duty required. That was its own kind of eloquence. Four days of careful not-looking, and every hour of it landed somewhere between Oscar's shoulder blades like a hand they couldn't shrug off.

August looked up from the salt. The child's eyes were the grey-green of canal ice, and they had that quality — Oscar had stopped trying to deny it — of seeing slightly more of the room than a room should allow.

"You're holding your pen wrong," August said in Guild-common, then repeated something in the First Tongue, soft, three syllables that tasted like cold iron and riverwater.

"What does that mean?" Oscar asked, as they were supposed to ask, pen poised over the folio.

"Loosen your grip," August said, which was not a translation, and smiled the small closed smile that meant *I know you know*, and went back to the salt.

And that was when the page caught fire.

There was no warning. No smoke curling up from a lamp, no spark from the hearth across the room — the hearth wasn't even lit; the fog-day chill had kept everyone away from it. One moment the folio lay open before Oscar, forty leaves of careful transcription, and the next the ninth leaf simply *bloomed*. Flame stood up out of the paper the way a figure stands up out of a chair, unhurried, deliberate, as though it had been sitting there all along waiting for the conversation to reach it.

Oscar recoiled so hard the bench legs shrieked against the floor. "The folio—"

Ilara moved. Later, Oscar would try to reconstruct exactly how she crossed the room — she was simply *there*, one heartbeat at the door and the next with her hands full of burning book, and she did the thing hunters are trained to do with burning things, which is to smother rather than beat, folding the folio shut and pressing it flat against the stone sill of the window until the flame starved. The smell was appalling. Burning ink has a sweetness to it, a burnt-sugar rot, and beneath it the sharper stink of scorched vellum, and beneath *that* something else entirely — something that did not belong to any ordinary fire. Ozone, almost. The air after lightning. The smell of the world holding its breath.

When Ilara opened the folio again, nine leaves were ash-edged and the tenth — the page that had burned — was gone entire. Not charred. *Gone.* Where the leaf had been sewn into the binding there remained only a clean stub, cut as neatly as a razor cuts, and the binding thread dangled unbroken, as though the page had never been.

"The hearth," Ilara said flatly. "It wasn't lit."

"No."

"And you weren't smoking. You don't smoke." She looked at Oscar then, fully, for the first time in four days, and her eyes were doing arithmetic. "What's on the missing page?"

Oscar's mouth had gone dry enough to crack. They made themselves look at the transcription record — they kept a running index, every entry numbered, every entry summarized in a single line. Entry thirty-one. Evening session, sixth day. August speaking of doors.

Of the door in the temple cellar.

Of the door that August said was *already open a finger's width, and getting wider, and the ones who built it are still here, wearing robes, carrying ledgers.*

"I don't remember," Oscar said, and heard the lie come out smooth and terrible, and hated how good they had gotten at it in only seven days.

August, very quietly, tipped the salt dish over and let the shapes run together.

* * *

Master Veyren arrived within the quarter hour, which told Oscar he had been close by already. He came into the transcription room the way weather comes into a valley — not quickly, but inevitably, filling every corner, displacing the air. He was a tall man built along the lines of a steeple, narrow and ascending, with grey hair cropped close and eyes the pale gold of old varnish. He wore his stewardship the way other men wore armor: comfortably, and always.

He stood over the ruined folio for a long time without touching it. Around him the room arranged itself into attention — Ilara straightening, Oscar rising, August going still as a held note.

"Sabotage," Veyren said at last. He said it the way a physician names a disease: not with alarm, but with the satisfaction of a diagnosis confirmed.

"Master," Ilara began, "the circumstances suggest—"

"They suggest precisely what I say they suggest." He turned the folio's stubbed binding toward her with one long finger. "A controlled destruction of a single page. Selective. Targeted. An accident burns what is nearest; this burned what was *important*. Someone in this house does not want the child's words preserved." His gaze traveled the room and settled, briefly, on the salt scattered across the table, and something moved behind the varnish of his eyes that Oscar could not name and did not enjoy trying to.

"The child cannot have done it," Ilara said. "He was drawing in salt."

"The child is the most valuable object in this building, Hunter Vance. Valuable objects attract hands. Our task is to make sure none of those hands belong to us." He straightened. New measures came off him in a list, clipped and clean: the transcription folio to be kept henceforth in the iron cabinet in his own study, not the archive. Sessions to be conducted only in his presence or Ilara's — never both absent. All scribes assigned to the August account to surrender their personal notebooks for inspection at week's end. And the archive stacks, all nine floors of them, sealed after dusk with the new wax — the guild-seal wax, the kind that screamed.

That last one landed in Oscar's stomach like a swallowed stone. Their private notebook was in the lining of their coat, and it was full of the First Tongue, and week's end was three days away.

"Master," Oscar heard themselves say, "if someone in the house is sabotaging the account, wouldn't inspection of the scribes be better done quietly? Announced inspections only announce where the guilty should tidy."

A pause. Veyren regarded them with that patient, ascending attention, and for a moment Oscar felt like a marginal note being considered for erasure.

"You argue like a man protecting something," Veyren said mildly.

"I argue like a scribe who wants the record complete," Oscar said. "The record is the work. Everything else is furniture."

Something flickered at the corner of Veyren's mouth — not quite a smile; approval, perhaps, which was worse. "Then you will not mind the inspection." He gathered the ruined folio under his arm. "Hunter Vance, double the corridor watch tonight. Scribe Reyes—" he paused at the door, half-turned, backlit by the fog-white window, "—you will re-transcribe entries twenty-five through thirty-one from memory. Tonight. I want to know whether your memory is as good as your arguments."

He left. The door clicked. The room exhaled.

August looked up at Oscar across the dead salt-shapes, and the child's face was carefully blank in the way that children's faces are blank when they have understood everything and decided to say nothing. But as Ilara herded them toward the door, August passed close — close enough that Oscar felt the child's breath, cool as well-water — and murmured, in Guild-common, pitched for no one else:

"It wasn't me. And it wasn't you." A beat. "It was the page. Some words don't want to be written down twice."

* * *

Oscar re-transcribed the entries that night because refusal was impossible and delay was suspicious, and because — this was the truth they admitted only to the lamp — they wanted to see what would happen. If the words burned once, would they burn again? Was the fire in the page, or in the meaning?

They worked in their own narrow cell at the top of the guildhouse, under a single tallow lamp, with the fog pressing at the shuttered window like a face. From memory, entry by entry. Twenty-five: August naming colors that had no common equivalents, *the red of a door before it opens*. Twenty-six: a song fragment, nonsense in Guild glyph-work. Twenty-seven through thirty: the sessions where August had begun, unprompted, to speak of the temple. Of stone under stone. Of a turning thing.

Thirty-one: the door, a finger's width open.

Oscar wrote it all out in fair copy, hand steady, heart anything but, and when they reached the final line of thirty-one — August's voice rendered in careful phonetics, *al-tem-ma, al-tem-ma, the hinge is oiled with years* — they set the pen down and waited.

The lamp flame leaned. Just leaned, the way flames lean when a draft finds a room, except there was no draft, and it leaned *toward the page*, straining at its wick like a dog at a rope, and the ink on the fresh-written line darkened, darkened, drinking light rather than reflecting it, and the air above the folio developed the faintest shimmer, the way a road shimmers in summer heat, and—

Oscar blew out the lamp.

Sat in the dark with their heart slamming and the fog breathing at the window, and understood, with the cold clarity that comes only in absolute blackness, two things at once.

First: the fire had not been sabotage. The words themselves refused captivity. Which meant the Guild's whole apparatus — the transcription, the folios, the iron cabinet, Veyren's careful custody — was an attempt to imprison something that could not be imprisoned, and sooner or later the Guild would conclude that if the words could not be kept on paper, they would have to be kept some other way. In a person. In a cage. In a child who could never be allowed to stop speaking them, or never be allowed to speak again.

Second, arriving a half-heartbeat behind the first and far more quietly, the way the worst realizations do: someone else knew. Not about the fire — about the *language*. Because the measures Veyren announced had not been the measures of a man reacting to arson. They had been the measures of a man protecting a schedule. Double watch. Sealed stacks. Inspection of notebooks. Those were precautions against *escape*, not theft — against a scribe learning too much, against knowledge walking out of the building on two legs.

Veyren was not afraid of losing the transcription.

Veyren was afraid of losing *them*.

Oscar sat in the dark a long while, listening to the fog drip from the eaves, feeling the notebook against their ribs like a second heart, warmer now than it had ever been. Three days until inspection. Nine floors of sealed stacks between them and the vault. And somewhere below, behind iron, a door that a child swore was already open a finger's width, oiled with years, waiting.

They struck a light and pulled the notebook from its lining and turned to the last page they'd written, and found — though they had left it plain and dry, no lamp near it, no flame anywhere near it — that the final word had changed overnight.

Where Oscar had written, in the First Tongue, the phrase August used for *door*, the ink now read the phrase for *threshold* — and beneath it, in a hand that was almost Oscar's own but not quite, a single additional word.

The word for *soon*.
