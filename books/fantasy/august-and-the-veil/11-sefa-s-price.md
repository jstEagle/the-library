## 11. Sefa's Price

Sefa had counted the guild's spoons for eleven years, and she could tell you to the ounce how much silver walked out of this building in a thief's pocket on any given night.

She knew because she let it happen.

Not the silver — the silver was bait, a small sin permitted so that larger sins might go unnoticed by men like Veyren, who watched everything and understood nothing that did not glitter. What Sefa moved through her storerooms was quieter than silver. Candle-ends. Rope. Blank paper from the sealed southern shipments. Once, an entire crate of surgical needles marked as spoiled and burned, though no fire had ever been lit. The Guild of Impossible Things believed itself unrobbed because its quartermaster was a gray woman with ink-stained cuffs and a limp she exaggerated on inspection days. Sefa had spent eleven years teaching them to look through her, and they had been excellent students.

Tonight she stood in the deep cellar beneath the counting room, among barrels of tallow and bolts of salted linen, waiting for Oscar Reyes to find the note she had left folded inside their ledger.

The note had said: *Third barrel from the left wall. Bring nothing. Tell no one.*

The stair door opened at half past the midnight bell — punctual, cautious, one hinge-squeak suppressed by a hand that knew which hinges sang. Sefa allowed herself the smallest satisfaction. A scribe who mapped the building's noises was a scribe who could be taught.

"You came alone," she said, not turning from the barrel she was ostensibly inspecting. "Good. I wasn't certain you would."

"Quartermaster." Oscar's voice was careful, the voice of someone standing on ice and listening for the crack. In the lamplight their face looked hollowed, cheekbones sharp as folded paper. They had not been sleeping. Good. Sleep made people stupid; worry made them honest. "Your note said—"

"My note said bring nothing, and you brought your ledger anyway." She turned then, letting her eyes flick to the book clutched against their chest like a shield or a child. "I suppose I should be flattered. Or insulted. You thought I meant to trap you, and you brought the evidence of your own crimes to the trap."

"I thought if you meant to trap me," Oscar said, "you'd have done it somewhere with more witnesses."

Sefa smiled. It was an expression she rationed carefully, like lamp oil. "Eleven years, scribe, and you finally say something worth hearing. Yes. If I wanted you in chains, we'd be in chains already. Sit. The barrels are clean enough. Everything down here is clean; that's rather the point of it."

Oscar sat on an upturned crate, spine straight, ledger on their knees. Waiting. That was the trouble with scribes — they waited to be told things, as though the world were a dictation. Sefa had learned long ago that the world was instead a set of accounts, and most people died without ever reading their own column.

"Do you know what happens to the wax?" she asked.

Oscar blinked. "The—"

"The candle wax. From the scriptorium. Every week I render it down and sell it back to the chandlers, who sell it to us again at markup. Do you know why I'm telling you this?"

"No."

"Because it is the only dishonest thing I've done in eleven years that Master Veyren knows about, and he permits it, because it saves him four crowns a year and costs him nothing but a blind eye. That is how Veyren understands the world, scribe. Everything is permitted if the arithmetic favors him." She crouched, unhurried, joints complaining, and lifted the third barrel's lid. Beneath the tallow, packed in straw, lay a bundle of oilcloth. She unwrapped it slowly, letting the sound of it fill the cellar — the whisper of cloth, the click of something hard within.

Inside lay a page. Old. Vellum gone the color of weak tea, edges soft as cloth. Ink that had not faded so much as *settled*, the way sediment settles in still water.

Oscar went very still. Sefa watched them read without touching — the way their lips parted, the way their breath changed pitch. They recognized the hand. Of course they did. They had been transcribing its cousin for weeks now, the words that came out of the mud-child's mouth in shapes no living scholar could parse.

"Where did you get this?" Oscar whispered.

"The same place the child got its language," Sefa said. "From under the temple."

* * *

She let the silence do its work. Silence was a tool like any other; she kept hers honed.

It was Oscar who broke first, because Oscar always broke first — that was written into them as clearly as any debt schedule. "That's impossible. The First Tongue isn't written anywhere. August's speech predates every script in the archive. I've checked. I checked *everything*—"

"You checked what the archive lets you check." Sefa turned the vellum toward the lamp. "Look at the margin. Lower left."

Oscar leaned in. Their finger hovered above the small mark there — a circle quartered by two lines, with a dot at the center — and Sefa saw the moment recognition landed, saw it land badly, like a dropped weight.

"That's the guild seal," Oscar said. "The founding seal. It hasn't been used since—"

"Since the charter was signed. Two hundred and forty-one years ago." Sefa rewrapped the page with the tenderness she reserved for things that frightened her. "This fragment came out of the ground beneath the Temple of the Turning Year, forty years ago, when the masons sank the new cistern. I was a junior quartermaster then, cataloguing salvage. Nine pages total. Eight went up to the masters, sealed, and were never spoken of again. This ninth page I kept. Because I am, as everyone knows, a woman who counts."

"And the language on it—"

"Is the child's language. Word for word. I've listened at doors, scribe. I hear more than spoons." She rose, knees cracking, and began to walk the aisle between barrels, her limp vanishing now that no one official watched. "Here is what your eight sealed pages never told you, and here is why I am telling *you*, of all people, in a cellar that smells of tallow and dead men's boots.

"The thirteenth moon is not a mystery. It is not an omen. It is not the wrath of heaven or the whim of the sky." She stopped, turned, and looked at Oscar the way she looked at a shipment she was deciding whether to accept. "It is a machine. Built by hands. Our hands. The founders of this guild dug beneath the temple before the temple had a name, and they built an engine — an orrery, the old records call it, though orrery is too gentle a word — and they built it to turn the heavens themselves. To hang a moon in the sky that no god had ordered, on a cycle no almanac admits, ticking down toward a single night when the Veil between this world and the other one goes thin as wet paper."

Oscar's face had gone the color of the vellum. "You're mad."

"I'm a quartermaster. Madness doesn't balance." Sefa's voice stayed level, almost bored, because levelness was armor and she had worn it so long she sometimes forgot there was skin beneath. "You've seen the ledgers. Don't tell me you haven't wondered. Two centuries of requisitions for brass and quicksilver and glass ground to tolerances no lensmaker will admit to. Shipments marked *temple maintenance* that weigh nine hundred pounds. A standing order, renewed every generation, for *one child's worth of acoustic channeling apparatus*. You balance those books, Oscar Reyes. You know exactly what has been bought, and you have never once dared ask what for."

"The moon-engine." Oscar said it flatly, testing the weight of it. "In chapter and verse, the archivists call the thirteenth moon a natural anomaly. There are treatises—"

"Treatises are what powerful men write so they don't have to confess." Sefa spat, delicately, into the gutter drain. "I've read the requisitions, and requisitions don't lie. Men lie. Treatises lie. But no one forges a purchase order for ninety pounds of singing-glass unless someone intends to make something sing."

The lamp flame bent in a draft from the stairwell. Somewhere above them, the guild slept on — hunters in their bunks, Veyren in his tower with his hidden almanac and his cold patient arithmetic, and the child, August, locked in the blue room with guards at the door and ink-stained visitors twice a day. Sefa had listened at that door too. She had heard the child's voice through the wood, and it had done something to her chest she did not care to examine, some old splinter of feeling working loose. She had buried three children of her own body and one of her heart, and none of them had spoken languages older than kingdoms, but all of them had known what it was to be owned.

"So," Oscar said slowly. "Veyren means to open the Veil. With the child. On the night of thirteen moons."

"Veyren means whatever Veyren means; he tells the masters a different sermon each decade — renewal, conquest, communion, whichever the times make fashionable. But yes. The engine needs a key, and the key is a voice, and the voice arrived at our door soaked in mud and speaking the First Tongue, and Veyren smiled for the first time in six years, and you noticed, didn't you? You noticed the smile." She stepped closer. "You notice things, scribe. It's why I chose you."

"*Chose* me." Oscar laughed, short and bitter. "I'm indentured, quartermaster. I'm owned. Whatever you think you're choosing, it belongs to Veyren first."

"Yes," Sefa said. "There it is. The debt. Fourteen years remaining, is it? Fourteen years of your life entered in a book, transferable, collectible, sellable. I've seen the entry. Page nine hundred and twelve of the Grand Register. *Reyes, O., scribe, term fourteen years, collateral: labor entire.*" She recited it without looking away. "Now I'm going to offer you something, and you're going to want to spit at me, and I want you to listen all the way to the end before you decide which of us is betraying whom."

* * *

She took the oilcloth bundle and set it in Oscar's hands. Let them hold the weight of it while she spoke.

"In forty days — thirty-nine, now; I count better than the almanac does — the thirteenth moon reaches full. The engine turns. And either the Veil opens, or it doesn't, and every version of that night ends with the child spent like coin. You know it. I've watched you know it for three weeks, watched you carry it around behind your eyes like a stone you can't put down. So here is my price, scribe, and my salvation, offered together because I've never in my life managed to separate them.

"Help me steal the engine."

Oscar stared. "Steal it. Steal a machine the size of a cathedral floor, buried under the temple, guarded by—"

"Not the brass and glass. Anyone who thinks the engine is its parts deserves the failure they'll get." Sefa tapped the wrapped vellum in Oscar's grip. "*This* is the engine. The pages. The founding documents — the eight sealed leaves and the keystone page that keys the whole mechanism to a voice. Without its founding text, the orrery is furniture. Expensive furniture. Veyren's grand design dies in a cellar full of scrap metal, and the thirteenth moon becomes what it should have been all along: a strange light in the sky that harms no one."

"And the child?"

"The child walks out of this guild before the final night, and no one follows, because no one remembers why they were following." Sefa held up one finger. "But understand the cost, because I promised you honesty and I intend to keep it even now. To take the keystone page from the masters' vault, we need someone inside the transcription chain — someone whose hand is trusted, whose forgeries pass, whose access is routine enough to be invisible. There is exactly one such person in this building, and they are sitting on my tallow barrel holding my evidence."

The silence stretched. Above them, faintly, the guild clock tolled the quarter hour, each stroke falling through the floors like a stone down a well.

"You're asking me to forge," Oscar said. "Again. For you."

"I'm asking you to forge *once more, properly* — not to shave a debt or slip a rival a bad figure, but to unmake a crime two hundred and forty years in the committing." Sefa crouched to meet their eyes, and let the armor slip, just a hair's width, just enough. "And I am telling you what nobody else in this guild will ever tell you: that after it's done, I will burn your debt page myself. Page nine hundred and twelve. I have the vault combination. I have had it for eleven years. When the engine is stolen and the child is free, Oscar Reyes will owe this guild nothing — not a coin, not a year, not a word. You will walk out of here owning yourself for the first time since you signed."

Something moved across Oscar's face — hope, that dangerous animal, sniffing at the door. Sefa watched it warily. Hope was the most expensive thing in her inventory and the least reliable.

"And what do you get?" Oscar asked quietly. "Out of all this. The great quartermaster, who counts everything. What's your column in this ledger?"

Sefa straightened. She had prepared an answer — something about justice, about the wrongness of spending children, about the world beyond the Veil deserving its refugees back. All true. None of it the truth.

"A debt of my own," she said instead. "One I've carried forty years, since I catalogued those nine pages and read the eighth one and understood what the founders intended, and said nothing, and took my wage, and grew old. I am owed a chance to correct an entry, scribe. That's all any of this is. An old woman balancing her books before the account closes."

Oscar looked down at the vellum in their hands. Looked up. And Sefa saw the moment the scales tipped — saw it in the jaw first, the way resolve sets in a face like mortar setting.

"If I do this," Oscar said, "if I forge the keystone duplicate and help you lift it — I want one thing beyond the debt."

"Name it."

"The eighth page. The one you read forty years ago. I want to see what made an honest woman willing to become a thief."

Sefa held their gaze for a long moment. Then she reached into her bodice, into the inner pocket where she kept the only thing she had ever stolen that mattered, and drew out a second fold of oilcloth, smaller, softer with handling.

She held it out — and paused.

"Tomorrow," she said. "Midnight. Same barrel. Read it, and then give me your answer." Her hand closed around the page again, and she turned toward the stairs, limping once more, the gray quartermaster restored like a mask settling into place. "Sleep on it, scribe. Though I suspect—" she glanced back, lamplight catching the ink stains on her cuffs, "—that neither of us will sleep at all."

Behind her, in the dark of the cellar, Oscar Reyes sat very still among the barrels, holding the child's language in both hands, and did not follow her up the stairs for a long, long time.
