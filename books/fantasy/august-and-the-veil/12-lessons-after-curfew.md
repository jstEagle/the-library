## 12. Lessons After Curfew

The guildhouse slept the way old buildings do — in creaks and settling bones, in the slow exhalation of pipes that had carried water since before anyone now living was born. Oscar lay awake beneath their narrow blanket on the third floor of the scribes' wing, listening to the rain begin again against the leaded glass, and counted.

Not debts tonight. Not years. They counted footsteps.

Veyren's boots had crossed the hall below at half past eleven, unhurried, the tread of a man who owned everything he walked upon. The night wardens had made their rounds at midnight, lantern-light sliding along the corridor seams like a blade passed under a door. And now, at last, silence — the particular, weighted silence of a house pretending to sleep while it listened for itself.

Oscar rose. They dressed without lighting a lamp; ten years in this building had taught their hands the geography of dark. Sash, coat, the transcription folio hidden inside the false back of their writing case — the one forgery they'd never been asked to make, a compartment built into the case by some earlier scribe with secrets of his own, its maker long dead and its existence known to no one living but Oscar.

The corridor floorboards knew which ones to avoid. Third from the stair, skip. The long plank outside the washroom, step on the nail-head, not the middle. Down past the locked cabinet of confiscated manuscripts, past the window where rain traced silver veins down the glass, to the landing where the archive stairs began their spiral into the older parts of the house.

August was already there.

The child sat cross-legged on the cold stone with their back against the banister, knees drawn up, mud-brown hair still carrying the shape of the pillow they'd left. In the blue-dark they looked smaller than by day — a wisp of a thing, seven years old or eight, with eyes that caught what little light there was and held it the way still water holds stars.

"You came," August whispered.

"I said I would."

"Adults say things." The child's voice was flat with an experience no child should own. "They say them like coins they can take back."

Oscar crouched, bringing their face level with the boy's. "I don't take words back. It's a professional failing. If I could unsay even one entry in my life, I'd be a happier person."

Something flickered at the corner of August's mouth. Not quite a smile. The first sketch of one, an artist's underdrawing.

"Where are we going?" the child asked.

"Somewhere the walls are too old to have ears."

* * *

The sub-cellar had been a chapel once, before the Guild bought the building and repurposed God. The vaulting still arched overhead in ribbed stone, and the lancet windows were bricked up but not erased — you could see, if you knew how to look, the ghost-arches of them in the mortar, the way you can see a river's course in a dry valley. Someone had shelved the space with ledgers nobody had opened in a century: inventories of properties the Guild no longer held, accounts of members struck from the rolls, the great sedimentary layers of other people's forgotten obligations.

Oscar lit a single candle stub, cupping the flame until it steadied, and set it on an overturned crate between two stacks of dead paper.

"No one comes here?" August asked, hugging their knees.

"The Guild forgets things. That's the one mercy of an institution built on memory — it has far more than it can hold." Oscar spread their coat on the flagstones. "Sit. Stone steals heat through wool faster than you'd believe."

August sat. For a moment neither spoke. The candle breathed. Somewhere above, the house groaned through a change of wind.

"Master Veyren says you're to write down everything I say," August said at last. "He said it in front of me like I wasn't there. Like I was weather."

"He does that."

"What happens to the pages? After you write them?"

Oscar weighed the truth against the hour. "They go to his private study. Under lock."

"And then?"

"I don't know." That much was honest. "But I'm going to find out. Which is why we're here." They opened the folio, took out a pen, and then — deliberately, watching the child's face — did not dip it in ink. "Tonight, nothing gets written. Tonight I want to learn."

"Learn what?"

"How your language works. Not the sounds. The *meaning*. When you spoke on the first night, in the courtyard — I transcribed it phonetically, every syllable, and the page rearranged itself before morning. The ink moved. Words I wrote as sound became words written as sense." Oscar turned the folio toward the candlelight, showing the page: the transcription of August's first storm-born utterance, and beneath it, in ink that had crawled overnight into new positions, a line of characters none of them had written. "This is your sentence. Or something like it. I want you to tell me if it's true."

August leaned close. Their breath fogged faintly in the cellar cold. When they saw the characters, they went very still — the stillness of a child hearing their mother's voice from a stranger's mouth.

"That says *the door remembers being open*," August whispered.

"Is that what you said?"

"It's what I *meant*." The child looked up, and there was something ancient moving behind the young eyes, something that made the candle flame lean though no draft stirred. "That's the whole thing about the First Tongue. You can't say what you don't mean. There's no lying in it. No — no *costume* for words. Every word is what it is, all the way down."

Oscar felt the scholar's hunger rise in them, that old vertiginous thrill, and pushed it down hard. "No lying at all?"

"Not in speaking." August hesitated. "But—" The child's face did something complicated. "There are words for doors. Opening-doors and closing-doors and doors that are both. Maybe a lie is a door that's both."

Oscar stared at the page. *The door remembers being open.* A language without falsehood — and yet the Guild wanted it transcribed, catalogued, *owned*. What do you do with a tongue that cannot deceive? You put it in chains. You build an engine around it. You make it say only what you allow.

"Teach me," Oscar said quietly. "One word. Just one true word, so I can feel what it weighs."

August studied them for a long moment — the way, Oscar thought, a locksmith studies a lock before deciding whether to pick it or break it. Then the child reached out and touched the page, one small finger resting beside the crawling ink.

"*Sethra*," August said.

The word went through Oscar like cold water through cloth. Not heard — *understood*, instantly and totally, the way you understand falling. It meant more than any single word should: it meant *to hold a thing gently enough that it stays itself*. Holding without gripping. Keeping without owning.

"Oh," Oscar breathed.

"Yeah," August said. "*Oh.*"

* * *

On the third night, August brought a question of their own.

They met again in the dead chapel, and after an hour of the First Tongue — Oscar's mouth clumsy around vowels that seemed to require the whole chest, the throat, the *intention* entire — the child sat back on their heels and asked, in the flat careful tone of someone rehearsing:

"How do you lie?"

Oscar set down the pen. "Why do you ask?"

"Because everyone here does it. All day. The wardens lie to each other about whose turn it is to stoke the boilers. The cook lies about the meat. Master Veyren—" August's jaw tightened. "Master Veyren told me the tests wouldn't hurt. They hurt. He smiled when he said it wouldn't hurt, and I didn't know until after that smiling is something people do when they're hiding a knife behind a door." The child wrapped thin arms around their knees. "I can't hear the knives anymore. In the First Tongue I could always hear them. Here I'm deaf. So teach me. Teach me how the knives sound."

The candle guttered. Oscar found, to their surprise, that their throat had gone tight.

What kind of world was this, that a child had to be taught deception as a survival skill — and what kind of person was Oscar, that their first instinct was pride in the curriculum?

"All right," they said. "First lesson. Lies aren't mostly words. Words are the smallest part. A lie is a *shape* you make with your whole self." They held up one hand. "Your face. Most people watch the mouth, but the mouth is easy. The eyes are harder. When you lie, don't look away — looking away is what guilty people do, and everyone knows it. Look *at* them. Look at them slightly too long, because honest people hold eye contact without thinking about holding it."

August nodded, grave as a novitiate.

"Second. Your hands. Fold them or keep them still. Liars' hands flutter — they try to escape." Oscar demonstrated, letting their own fingers twitch theatrically, and was rewarded with the ghost of a laugh. "Third, and most important: give them a truth to stand on. A lie with nothing true underneath collapses. But a lie built on a true thing — a real detail, a real feeling — will bear weight. If Veyren asks whether you slept well, don't just say yes. Say yes, and add something true: *the rain kept me awake until late.* Let him check the rain. Let him find it falling. Then he'll believe the rest."

"The rest being the lie."

"The rest being the lie." Oscar paused. "August. Why does a child need this?"

The question hung in the candlelight. August looked down at their own hands — small hands, chapped from the storm-night, nails bitten to the quick.

"Because I think," the child said slowly, "that someday soon, someone is going to ask me if I know how to open the Veil. And I think the right answer is no. And I think I'll need them to believe it."

Oscar's blood went cold and hot at once. "Has someone asked you already?"

"Not yet." August lifted their eyes. "But the moon came early this month. I counted. Everyone else thinks the almanac is wrong. The almanac isn't wrong."

* * *

By the sixth night they had a rhythm, a liturgy of two. Oscar would slip down after the wardens' midnight round; August would follow twenty minutes later, having learned — from Oscar — to time the warden's limp, the uneven gait of old Corvus's bad knee, so that the sound of his passage marked safe corridors like bells marking hours.

They learned each other in the intervals between lessons.

Oscar learned that August was afraid of deep water but not of heights, that the child remembered a city of white terraces built into a cliffside above a sea the color of hammered tin, that August's earliest memory was of being sung to sleep by many voices in unison — a chorus, the child said, that was also somehow one voice, the way a chord is many notes and one sound.

And August learned Oscar. Learned that Oscar hummed when concentrating and stopped abruptly when caught at it. Learned that Oscar's debt to the Guild was fourteen years old and had nine remaining, incurred — though Oscar never said how — in a single catastrophic week when they were nineteen. Learned that Oscar flinched, very slightly, whenever anyone said the word *free*, the way a scarred dog flinches at a raised hand.

"Why nine years?" August asked one night, unpicking the First Tongue's word for *river* — a word that changed meaning depending on whether the speaker had ever been drowned. "Why not run? People run from debts."

"Where would I go? My name is in every ledger in every guildhouse from here to the coast. I'd be property wherever I stood." Oscar turned the pen over in their fingers. "Besides. Running is a kind of lie. You have to spend the rest of your life maintaining it."

"You're teaching me to lie."

"Yes."

"So you think some lies are worth maintaining."

Oscar opened their mouth to answer and found, annoyingly, that the child had outflanked them. "Some," they admitted. "The ones that keep people alive."

August nodded slowly, filing this away in whatever vast and terrible library the child carried behind those eyes. Then: "Teach me another word. A true one."

"*Sethra* again. It's the only one I've mastered."

"No. A new one." August scooted closer to the candle, and the light moved across the child's face like a hand smoothing a bedsheet. "This one means—"

Footsteps.

Not Corvus's limping tread. Not the wardens'. These steps were light, quick, certain — descending the archive stairs two at a time with the unhesitating stride of someone who knew exactly where they were going, because they had been given orders about exactly where to look.

Oscar pinched out the candle. Darkness fell like a dropped curtain. In the black, they felt August's small hand find theirs and grip it — *sethra*, thought Oscar absurdly, in the dark, *holding gently enough that the thing stays itself* — and together they pressed back into the gap between two ledger-stacks, breathing shallow, as the cellar door at the top of the stairs swung open and lamplight spilled down the steps in a long gold blade.

A silhouette filled the doorway. Tall. Cloaked in the grey of the border service, travel-worn, with a hunter's crossbow slung across her back and rain still beaded on her shoulders.

Ilara Vance stood at the head of the stairs, her lantern raised, her eyes sweeping the dark chapel where a century of forgotten debts sat sleeping on their shelves.

She had come home early. She had come home armed. And she was looking, Oscar realized with a heart that had stopped entirely, directly at the overturned crate where the candle stub still smoked its thin, betraying thread.
