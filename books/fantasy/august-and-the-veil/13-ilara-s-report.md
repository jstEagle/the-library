## 13. Ilara's Report

The report room smelled of tallow and old paper and the particular staleness of a building that had been arguing with the weather for three hundred years. Rain worked at the shutters in long, patient fingers. Somewhere below, the archive breathed its dust through the floorboards, and Ilara Vance sat at a scarred oak desk with a blank report form before her and a full inkwell she had not yet touched.

She had been back from the borderlands for eleven days. Long enough for the road to leave her boots. Not long enough for the borderlands to leave her.

*Incident Report — Guild of Impossible Things, Office of the Hunter-Commissioner,* said the header, in that infuriatingly serene copperplate. *Subject: containment status of asset designated AUGUST.*

*Asset.* She turned the word over the way she'd turn a coin she suspected of being shaved. Eleven days ago it would have sat in her mouth without complaint. Now it tasted like something left too long in a dead man's pocket.

She picked up the pen. Set it down. Picked it up again.

The trouble was not that she didn't know what to write. The trouble was that she knew exactly what to write, and exactly which parts of it were true.

* * *

She began the way all reports began: with the facts that could be verified by anyone standing in the right corridor at the right hour.

*Asset secured within the east wing under continuous watch. No unauthorized contact recorded. Asset's speech remains unparseable by guild staff; transcription assigned to scribe O. Reyes per Master Veyren's directive of the ninth.*

There. That much was stone. That much would survive any audit, any cross-examination, any of Master Veyren's soft, terrible questions asked in his soft, terrible voice while he poured tea no one drank.

She wrote on.

*On the fourth night of watch, asset exhibited disturbed sleep. Vocalizations during sleep do not correspond to any known dialect pattern. Recommend continued phonographic record.*

Also true. Also stone. What she did not write was that she had stood outside the child's door at the third bell and heard August weeping in a language that made the candle flames lean toward the sound like men leaning toward a window where music played. She did not write that when the crying stopped, the silence afterward had felt less like an absence and more like a held breath — the whole east wing holding it, walls and wainscoting and the portraits of dead masters, all waiting to see if the child would speak again.

She did not write that she had gone in.

That was the first omission, and it was already sitting on the page like a stain only she could see. She had gone in because the night-watchman swore he'd seen the door open from the inside, and a hunter who ignores a breached containment is a hunter writing her own dismissal. So she had gone in with her hand on her knife and found — nothing. A child asleep in a narrow bed, mud from the first night still dried in the seams of the blanket because no one had thought to replace it. A window latched from within. And on the sill, in a fine grey powder that might have been ash or might have been frost, a line of marks that were not letters and were not scratches and were not anything Ilara had a word for, except that looking at them made her think, absurdly, of her mother's hands kneading bread.

She had reported the breach. She had not reported the marks. In her written account the sill was bare, the window merely rattled by wind, the watchman mistaken.

Why? She could have given the Commissioner a dozen reasons and none of them would have been the true one. The true one was this: some instinct older than her training had looked at those marks and told her that the Guild must not see them yet. Not until she understood what they were. Not until she understood what the Guild would *do* about them.

Ilara Vance had served the Guild of Impossible Things for nine years, and in nine years she had learned one law above all others: the Guild did not contain impossible things. It *farmed* them.

* * *

The rain thickened. A runner passed the door, sandals slapping wet stone, and did not knock. Good. She had chosen this room — the small report annex off the west stair, the one with the draught under the door and the reputation among the clerks as cold and dull — precisely because no one came here unless summoned.

She thought about Oscar.

Not deliberately. Deliberately she thought about Oscar as little as possible, the way a soldier avoids pressing on a wound to learn its depth. But the report required her to write the name — *scribe O. Reyes* — and the moment the pen formed the *R*, eleven years fell on her like a dropped banner.

She had met Oscar Reyes in the map room, of all places, the spring they were both nineteen. Ilara had been sent to collect a survey of the Saltmarsh approaches; Oscar had been shelving folios with that particular scribe's economy of motion, every gesture costing exactly as much effort as it needed and not a grain more. They had argued about the cartography of the Veil — Ilara insisting the border fortifications mattered, Oscar insisting, with a small fierce light in their eyes, that you could not fortify against something you refused to understand. Ilara had called them naive. Oscar had called her well-trained, and both words had landed like kisses.

For two years after that, there had been a bench behind the dye-works where the river smell covered everything, and stolen hours, and a shared conviction that the world was larger than the Guild admitted. Then Ilara had taken her commission. Then Oscar had signed their indenture — *signed it*, Ilara still could not say the word without hearing the scratch of that pen, selling nine years of their life to pay a debt that had never been fully explained to her — and something between them had quietly closed, the way a door closes in a house where someone has died. Not slammed. Just closed, and then everyone agreed to walk softly past it.

Now she was assigned to guard the child, and Oscar was assigned to transcribe the child, and Master Veyren had arranged these assignments himself, with the mild pleasure of a man setting two pieces on a board.

*Transcription proceeds apace,* she wrote. *Scribe Reyes maintains regular hours. No irregularities observed.*

The lie went down onto the page smooth as oil, and she hated it, and she wrote it anyway.

Because there *were* irregularities. She had eyes, and nine years of training in using them. She had seen the transcription room twice from the corridor — once legitimately, once by arranging her patrol route past it more often than any sane patrol required — and both times she had seen Oscar bent over the page with an expression she recognized from the old days, from the map-room arguments, from the bench behind the dye-works: the expression of a person discovering that the world was larger than they'd been told. Wonder, worn openly, unguardedly, the way only Oscar wore it.

And she had seen the ledgers react. Everyone pretended they hadn't. The second night, when August had spoken a word that cracked like green wood, every inkwell in the east wing had shivered — she had watched the surface of the ink tremble in the pot on the watch-desk, rings spreading from nothing, as if something vast had exhaled beneath the building. The watchman had blamed the settling of the foundations. Foundations settle downward. This had rippled *upward*.

She had also seen, three nights ago, the state of Oscar's hands. Ink to the second knuckle, yes — that was ordinary. But there had been a tremor in the right hand that was not fatigue. She knew Oscar's tiredness the way she knew her own sword-arm's; this was different. This was the tremor of a person carrying something heavy in a place no one could see.

Someone else knows, she had thought, watching that tremor. Someone besides me.

* * *

She set the pen down and let herself do the arithmetic she had been refusing for eleven days.

Fact: the Guild had taken custody of a child found speaking a language no living scholar could parse, and within a single day the Master himself had assumed personal oversight — Veyren, who had not personally overseen an acquisition since the Saltmarsh reliquary, eight years ago.

Fact: the east wing, where August was housed, stood directly above the sealed sub-vaults. Ilara had checked the architectural folios on her second day back, casually, the way one checks the weather. The east wing's foundations went down farther than any cellar had business going. There were floors under the archive that appeared on no plan she could find, and the plans that should have shown them were missing from the folio — cleanly excised, stubs of cut paper remaining like pulled teeth.

Fact: Master Veyren had asked her, on her third evening back, over tea she hadn't drunk, how the borderlands fared. And when she had answered — truthfully, that the Veil-country was quiet, quieter than it had been in a decade, the thin places thinned to almost nothing — he had smiled the way a farmer smiles at a field before the harvest, and said, *"Quiet everywhere, then. How convenient."*

How convenient. She had turned that phrase over for nine nights. It was nothing. It was a pleasantry. It was also, she now suspected, the most honest sentence the man had spoken in her presence in nine years.

The Guild of Impossible Things collected impossible things. That was its trade, its pride, its name. But Ilara had begun to wonder — in the small cold annex, in the rain, with a half-written report before her — whether the Guild had ever been content to *collect*. Whether somewhere beneath the east wing, in those floors that appeared on no plan, something was being *built*.

And whether a child who spoke the First Tongue was not the Guild's prisoner at all, but its instrument. Its key. Its — she reached for the word the old surveyors used for the mechanism that raised a drawbridge —

— its *counterweight*.

Her hand had gone cold around the pen. She noticed it distantly, the way one notices weather.

* * *

She forced herself back to the report. Duty was a discipline like any other; you did not wait to feel it, you performed it and let feeling catch up.

*Assessment:* she wrote, and paused, and then wrote the assessment that would keep everyone safe for another week, including — especially — the people she was not naming. *Asset poses no immediate threat. Containment adequate. No escalation of measures recommended at this time.*

No escalation of measures. She read the sentence back and understood what she had done. Because she had also learned, in nine years, how the Guild read reports: it read them for permission. A report that said *no escalation recommended* bought time. A report that said *asset exhibits anomalous phenomena, recommend intensified study* bought chains, and worse than chains — whatever the Guild did to things it wanted to understand completely. She had escorted such acquisitions before. She remembered the reliquary from the Saltmarsh, and the reliquary's keeper, and the particular silence of the wagon on the last mile into the city, when even the horses seemed to know.

So the report was incomplete. She knew it the way she knew her own heartbeat: *this document is a lie by subtraction.* Every fact on it true; the shape of the truth absent. If Veyren audited her — if he took the report up in his long white fingers and read it with those patient, hooded eyes — he might see the shape of the hole. He was very good at holes. He had made a life of them.

But an incomplete report was not yet treason. An incomplete report was a hunter's judgment call: *insufficient evidence, further observation required.* Every clause defensible. Every omission deniable. She was not protecting a crime. She was protecting — what? A child's sleep. A line of marks on a windowsill. A tremor in a familiar hand.

A love she would not name, because naming it would make it a lever, and levers were Veyren's native craft.

She signed the bottom of the page. *I. Vance, Hunter-Commissioner's Office, Border Service (seconded).* Her signature came out steady, which surprised her. Hands always knew more than hearts about what they could bear.

* * *

She was blotting the page when the runner came back — the same one, out of breath, rain in his hair, with a summons that had clearly been composed in haste:

*Hunter Vance to attend the Master in the transcription gallery. Immediately. There has been an incident with the papers.*

An incident with the papers.

She rose, buckled her sword-belt out of habit rather than need, and stood a moment in the doorway with the unfinished report in her satchel, listening to the rain and to the building beneath the rain — the deep, slow breathing of the archive, and beneath that, faint as a pulse through a wrist, something else. A hum. Rhythmic. Almost patient.

It might have been the foundations settling.

It might have been counting.

Ilara adjusted her collar, put on the face the Guild paid her to wear, and went down through the wet stone corridors toward the transcription gallery — telling herself, with the last of her honesty, that she would look at Oscar first, before anyone else, before Veyren's eyes could find theirs. She would read the tremor in that familiar hand. And whatever had happened to the papers, whoever had burned them and why, she would decide in that first glance — silently, instantly, the way she decided everything now —

which side of the ledger she was standing on.
