## 14. The Room Beneath

The key Sefa had pressed into my palm three nights ago was made of bone.

I had told myself it was ivory. I had told myself a great many things in the years since I first signed my name into the Guild of Impossible Things — that the ledgers were only ledgers, that the masters were only men, that the debt I carried was an honest debt with honest interest and an honest end. But the key was warm in a way bone is warm, and it fit no lock I had ever seen, and when I held it too long I began to dream of tides.

So on the night of the waning quarter moon, when the archive had gone quiet as a held breath and even the lamps seemed to lean away from their wicks, I took the stair beneath the scriptorium that was not supposed to exist.

* * *

Everyone at the guild knew there were cellars. Everyone knew the cellars held paper — forty generations of it, vellum and rag pulp and papyrus older than the kingdom itself, stacked in bales that sweated ink-smell through the summer months. What no one spoke of was the thirteenth stair. There is a superstition among scribes, passed from master to apprentice like a disease: never count the steps down. Count them coming up, if you must count at all, because what lives below counts you first.

I had laughed at that superstition for eleven years. That night, standing at the top of the descent with a candle cupped against my chest like something newborn, I counted thirteen steps before my foot found stone where stone should have ended.

The fourteenth step rang hollow.

I stood there a long while, listening to the echo travel downward and not come back. The air rising past me smelled of cold metal and old rain and — faintly, unmistakably — of August. Not the child himself; not mud or milk or the woodsmoke sweetness that clung to his collar. It was the smell of his voice, if a voice can be said to smell of anything. Ozone and river-stones. The same scent that had risen off the transcription pages when the ink began to move.

The key grew warmer against my palm.

I went down.

* * *

The stair became a spiral, and the spiral became a throat. The walls changed under my fingertips as I descended — dressed granite giving way to blocks so old their chisel-marks had been worn to silk, then to something that was not stone at all but a kind of fused glass, dark and faintly veined, warm as skin. My candlelight slid across it without catching. I had studied every material catalogued in the Guild's *Compendium of Substances*, all nine hundred entries, and this was none of them. This was older than cataloguing. This was perhaps older than the idea of naming things.

There were words in the glass.

I stopped at the first landing and raised the light, and my breath left me in a thin white ribbon. Carved into the walls — no, grown into them, the way frost grows into a window — were lines of script that flowed and shifted when I looked directly at them and went still when I looked away. I knew that behavior. I had spent six weeks watching it happen in a child's transcription, hunched over my desk at hours when honest scribes slept, telling myself the ink was settling, that lamplight played tricks, that I was tired.

It was the First Tongue. It covered the walls the way water covers a well-shaft — everywhere, patient, waiting to be drunk.

And here is the thing I will carry to whatever grave is allowed me: I could read some of it now. Not fluently. Not the way August spoke it, whole sentences falling out of him like birds startled from a hedge. But the night lessons had taken root, and phrases surfaced in me unbidden, translating themselves behind my eyes before I could decide whether I wanted them translated.

*We were many,* said one line. *We are fewer.*

*Do not write us wrong,* said another, and beneath it, smaller, almost pleading: *we remember everything written about us.*

My hand shook. Wax dripped hot down my knuckle and I did not feel it.

* * *

The last flight opened without warning into a space that should not have fit inside the temple's footprint.

I knew the geometry of the building above. I had copied its floor plans twice, once as an exercise and once for the surveyors, and I knew that below the scriptorium there was room for perhaps two cellar levels before one struck bedrock and the old foundation stones of the pre-Guild shrine. What I stood in now was a cavern tall enough to lose a cathedral in, ribbed with pillars that rose out of darkness below and vanished into darkness above, and filling it — filling *all* of it —

was the engine.

I have searched my vocabulary for a better word and found none, so let me use the worst one honestly: it was beautiful, and it was a machine, and those two facts have never since been separable in my mind.

It was an orrery. I recognized the principle from the brass models apprentices play with — nested rings, spheres within spheres, the dance of heavens rendered in miniature. But this one was thirty fathoms across, and its rings were not brass. They were bands of that same dark glass, each one wide as a city street, suspended in nothing, turning around a central sphere that hung in the air like a captive moon. And upon those rings, etched in lines of light that moved like mercury, were the orbits of moons.

Twelve of them. Our twelve. I found ours easily — the pale familiar one, third ring from the center, marked with symbols I half-knew. Then the others, each in its place, each turning in its long slow obedience.

And beyond the twelfth, on the outermost ring, where the model-makers always leave blank metal:

A thirteenth orbit. Empty. Waiting. Its track was scored deep into the glass, polished by centuries of turning, kept clear, kept ready — the way a cradle is kept ready. The way a trap is kept baited.

The whole vast mechanism turned in absolute silence. That was the worst of it, at first. Something that size should have groaned like a ship, should have filled the cavern with the grind of ages. Instead it moved as silently as the actual sky, and I understood dimly that it was not driving anything. It was *listening*. Every ring, every band, every hair-fine line of light was tuned — tuned the way a luthier tunes strings — to a single instrument.

To a voice.

* * *

I found the workstations at the cavern's edge, and that was where the vertigo truly took me.

Not the scale of the thing above — though my body kept insisting the floor should tilt, that a machine so large must fall, that the temple overhead must be a toy balanced on a drum. No. It was the desks. The ledgers. Row upon row of them along the curved wall, hundreds of volumes, spines cracked with age, and every single one of them open or recently closed, dusted, maintained. *Current.* I lifted the nearest and its pages fell open to an entry dated four days ago. Four days. In a vault no one was supposed to know existed.

The entry was a table. Down its left margin ran a column of dates stretching back decades. Down its right ran measurements — pitch, resonance, harmonic intervals recorded in notation I didn't recognize until I saw, tucked in the corner of the page, a small annotation in Master Veyren's precise, bloodless hand: *Subject A-1, vocal sample, age 3. Subject A-2, age 5. Subject A-3—*

Subjects. Plural. Numbered.

I turned pages with hands that no longer felt like mine. Decades of entries. Children's names reduced to letters and numbers, their babble and their first words and their childhood songs transcribed by someone — by many someones, over generations — and fed into tables, and the tables fed into the engine, tuning it, refining it, narrowing its ear toward one specific frequency that had not yet existed.

Until August.

I found the earliest volume then, shoved at the end of the row, its spine rotted soft. The paper inside was the color of old teeth. The hand that had written it was archaic, formal, the Guild-script of three hundred years past, and I read the opening line by candlelight in a cavern beneath the world while a machine the size of a sky listened for a child:

*Let it be set down, in this the founding year of our most noble endeavor, that the Veil shall be opened in our time or our children's time or our children's children's time, however many generations the working requires. The moon we shall make. The voice we shall find. Patience is the first virtue of the impossible.*

The moon we shall make.

I read it again. And again. The words did not soften with repetition; they sharpened, they cut deeper each pass, until I had to close the book and press my palm flat against its cover just to feel something solid. Every story I had ever been told about the thirteenth moon — the rare celestial visitor, the omen, the wonder that came once in a millennium — assembled itself in my memory and then, piece by piece, quietly dismantled. Not a wonder. A *project*. An artifact wound up beneath the temple and loosed into the sky, ticking toward conjunction with the patience of people who measured time in centuries because they intended to own all of them.

And the voice we shall find. Not *send for*. Not *raise*. Find. As though a child were a missing component, a keystone rolled under a cabinet three hundred years ago, waited upon.

I thought of August asleep upstairs in the east room, curled around his own knees, murmuring in a language older than kingdoms. I thought of how Veyren looked at him — not with cruelty, which I would have known how to fight, but with the particular tenderness of a man regarding a tool he has waited his whole life to hold. I had told myself I was imagining it. Possessiveness, I'd called it in my own head, the way one calls a storm weather.

It was not possessiveness. It was *completion*. The master had been born into the last chapter of a book begun before his ancestors' ancestors, and August was its final word, and I — I was merely the scribe hired to copy it out fair.

My candle guttered. In the sudden stutter of shadow, the orrery's rings seemed to turn faster, and I heard — or dreamed I heard, though I have never since been sure, and never want to be sure — a sound rise out of the silent machinery. Faint. Distant. A thread of sound woven through the silence like silver through grey cloth.

A child's voice. Humming.

The engine was listening to him even now. Even through fathoms of stone. Even in his sleep. Somewhere above me August breathed and dreamed, and down here in the dark the great patient rings shivered with delight, adjusting themselves by fractions of degrees, drawing closer to the note they had waited three hundred years to hear.

I pressed the bone key hard against my sternum, as if I could keep it from hearing me, and understood with total, sickening clarity that it already had. The nearest ring had tilted — barely, a degree, less — toward the workstation where I stood. Toward *me*. The lines of light along its rim pulsed once, softly, like a pulse taking measure.

It knew I was here. Perhaps it had known since the fourteenth step. Perhaps it had known since the night a mud-soaked child knocked on the guild door and the ledgers in the receiving hall had rustled on their shelves like leaves in wind, and I had told myself that was the draft.

*Patience is the first virtue of the impossible.*

I put the founding ledger back exactly where I had found it. I straightened the table entries. I walked back up the spiral with my candle shielded and my breath held, counting the stairs as I climbed — fourteen, thirteen, twelve — and the superstition finally made sense to me, and I hated it, and I obeyed it anyway.

At the top, in the honest dark of the ordinary cellar, I stood a long time doing sums. Eleven years remained on my indenture. The thirteenth moon would rise in fifty-three days. Those numbers had lived side by side in my head for weeks without touching, the way numbers do, innocent as neighbors.

They touched now. Fifty-three days. Eleven years. One child. One machine. One choice, already forming somewhere in me below thought, the way the vault lay below the temple — waiting for me to descend far enough to find it.

I blew out my candle. And in the black, very softly, in a language I had promised myself I was only learning to protect him, I practiced the First Tongue's word for *freedom* — and somewhere far beneath my feet, I swear on every ledger I have ever kept, something turned to listen.

What else, I wondered, walking back through the sleeping archive, had the guild written about August — and where? What page, what charter, what founding document held his name in ink older than his bones?

In the morning, I resolved, I would request access to the restricted charters. Whatever pretext I needed. Whatever forgery it cost.

Because if they had built a moon for him, then somewhere in this house of paper, they had written him down — and I needed to know, before he did, exactly what they had made him sign.
