## 16. A Debt Called Love

The summons came by way of a boy who did not look at Oscar's face.

That was how it worked in the Guild of Impossible Things. Messages were carried by children too young to be blamed for what they repeated, and the message itself was never written down, because Master Veyren understood better than anyone alive that ink was testimony and testimony could be seized. So the boy stood in the doorway of the transcription room, mud drying on his boots like old candle wax, and said only: "Master wants you at the ninth bell. The observatory stair. Come alone."

Then he turned and left, and Oscar sat alone among the pages that had once rearranged themselves under their pen — pages they had since locked in a false-bottom drawer Sefa didn't know about, beneath three years of deliberately boring correspondence about grain tariffs — and felt the familiar cold walk up their spine, vertebra by vertebra, like a hand counting them.

Ninth bell. The observatory stair. Alone.

Oscar looked at the window. Beyond the glass, Greyhollow lay under its usual bruise-colored dusk, chimney smoke standing straight up in the windless cold. And above the rooftops, faint as a watermark, hung the thirteenth moon — still a sliver, still wrong, still growing fatter each night in a sky that had no room for it. Twelve nights now. Sefa had counted them on her knuckles the way she counted everything, and her face had been gray when she finished.

Twelve nights until the moon reached fullness, until the engine beneath the temple woke, until August —

Oscar closed the drawer on that thought the way one closes a drawer on something living.

* * *

The observatory stair smelled of iron and old starlight, if starlight could be said to go stale. It was a spiral of black granite worn hollow in its center by two centuries of climbing feet, and it rose through the heart of the guildhouse like a throat. Oscar climbed it slowly, one hand on the rail, listening to their own breath echo back at them from above, as though someone were climbing down to meet them.

Veyren was waiting in the orrery chamber at the top, but not at the great brass model of the heavens where he usually received petitioners. He was standing at the window instead, his back to the door, hands folded behind him, watching the thirteenth moon with the expression of a man regarding a field he had planted long ago and which was, at last, coming ripe.

"You're punctual," he said, without turning. "You always were. Even as an apprentice. I used to tell the other masters: this one's ink dries before it hits the page."

"Master." Oscar bowed, because the body remembered its manners even when the mind was screaming. "You asked—"

"I asked nothing." He turned then, and his face in the lamplight was mild, reasonable, the face of a patient teacher. That was the thing about Veyren that no one who met him casually ever believed when Oscar tried to describe it later: there was no cruelty in him. There was only certainty so complete it had polished away everything else, the way water polishes stone. "I offered. There's a difference, and you of all people appreciate precision."

He gestured, and Oscar saw what he had prepared.

On the table between the window and the great orrery lay a single ledger. Not one of the working ledgers, not the debt-books with their columns of years owed and years served. This one was old — older than Oscar's indenture, older than Oscar — bound in green leather gone soft as skin, its corners rounded by handling. Oscar knew it without being told. Everyone in the guild knew it, the way everyone knows the shape of the thing that owns them.

*The Founding Charter's companion volume. The Master's Ledger. Every indenture ever signed into the Guild of Impossible Things, from the first name to the last.*

To the last.

"Open it," Veyren said gently.

"I don't have authority to—"

"*Open it*, Oscar."

Oscar opened it.

The pages fell to the entry near the end almost on their own, the book knowing what was wanted of it the way all the guild's books seemed to know things they shouldn't. And there it was, in ink that had faded from brown-black to the color of dried blood:

**Reyes, O. — scribe, third class. Term: fourteen years. Served to date: nine years, seven months, eleven days.**

Four years, four months, nineteen days remaining.

Oscar had those numbers carved somewhere behind their ribs. They checked them the way other people checked a wound, daily, involuntarily, hoping against arithmetic. Four years, four months, nineteen days of copying other people's impossible accounts, sleeping in a cell the width of a coffin, eating guild bread that tasted like punishment, asking permission to sneeze — and then freedom, at thirty-one, with half a life still unspent.

Veyren let them look at it for a long moment. Then he drew a small knife from his sleeve — a scribe's knife, bone-handled, for trimming quills — and laid it beside the open ledger.

"Do you know," he said, conversationally, "what happens to an entry in this book when it is struck?"

"It's... annulled. The debt is discharged. It's written about in the histories. A struck line is the highest mercy the charter allows. It's happened twice in two hundred years."

"Three times," Veyren corrected softly. "But the third is not yet history. Watch."

He set the point of the knife against the first stroke of *Reyes* — and did not cut. He only touched it. And the ink moved.

It flinched, actually flinched, drawing away from the blade like a worm from salt, and Oscar felt it in their own chest, a sympathetic contraction, because of course they felt it; they had felt the ledger's attention since the day they signed it at fifteen, their father's debts transferred onto their shoulders along with their father's funeral costs. The guild's books were not records. They were *tethers*. And Veyren held the end of every one.

"One gesture," Veyren said. "One line struck, and the book forgets you owe us anything. Four years vanish. You walk out of this guildhouse tomorrow morning owning your own name, your own hands, your own hours. No master. No permission. No cell." He smiled, and it was a kind smile, which made it worse. "You could go south, if you like. You always wanted the south. You wrote about it in your apprentice essays — badly, but with feeling. The sea. Real oranges. Sunlight that doesn't come filtered through our lovely perpetual Greyhollow cloud."

Oscar's mouth had gone dry. "In exchange for what."

"In exchange for nothing you haven't already agreed to do." Veyren walked past them to the window, to the moon. "The child will be brought to the vault on the final night regardless. That has been decided since before either of us was born — the charter is quite explicit, and I've shown you the relevant page. All I require of you is that the child comes *willingly*. Calmly. Trusting the hand that leads them." He turned his head just enough that Oscar caught the profile, the mild eyes. "And whose hand does August trust, Oscar? Whose voice do they search for in a crowded room? Whose lessons do they beg for after curfew — yes, I know about the curfew lessons, don't waste my time with your face, I invented that face."

The cold went through Oscar completely then, front to back, like a needle threaded through the whole length of them.

"The child trusts you," Veyren said. "That is not an accusation. It is the single most valuable asset this guild currently possesses, and I am prepared to pay for assets. Four years and four months, struck from the book. Or—" he tapped the ledger, gently, almost affectionately— "you can refuse, and serve out your term honestly, and watch from the transcription room while someone else walks August down the observatory stair, someone less gentle, because if it isn't you it will be Ilara, and Ilara has never in her life been gentle with anything she was ordered to guard."

"That's coercion."

"That's *commerce*," Veyren said, with real warmth. "Everything is commerce. Debts, names, moons, love — especially love. Do you think the child's trust in you is free? It was purchased with nine years of your loneliness. You were lonely here, so you were kind to the one creature lonelier than yourself, and now that kindness has a market price, and I am offering to buy it at rates no honest merchant would match." He sighed, and for one moment the polish slipped and something ancient and tired showed through, something that had watched two hundred years of ledgers fill and close. "I do not enjoy this part. I want you to know that. In another life I would have been a gardener. But the Veil is failing, Oscar — you've seen the almanac, you've seen the moon — and when it fails *unmanaged*, it doesn't fall neatly. It tears. Everything on the far side comes through at once, hungry and uncounted, and the world ends not in fire but in *bookkeeping*: more mouths than the ledger of the world can hold. What we do in twelve nights is not cruelty. It is triage. And I need my best scribe's steady hand to see it done mercifully."

Oscar looked down at the ledger. At their own name, small and brown and patient. Nine years, seven months, eleven days of their life, recorded in someone else's handwriting, in a book they would never be permitted to read beyond their own line.

They thought of August's face in the lamplight during the curfew lessons — the fierce concentration, the way the child's whole body leaned toward knowledge like a plant toward light, the terrible question they'd asked only last night, in the First Tongue and then again, carefully, in common speech: *"If I open it, do I stop?"* And how Oscar had lied — the second skill they'd taught the child — and said *of course not*, and watched August choose, with visible effort, to believe them.

They thought of Ilara's face at the canal gate, months ago, tearing her commission in half. Of Ilara's report she knew was incomplete. Love, priced and paid, everywhere, in every direction, and every price hidden until the bill came due.

"May I have until tomorrow?" Oscar heard themself ask. Their voice sounded very far away, like someone else's pen scratching in another room.

Veyren considered. "Until the tenth bell tomorrow. Not further — the moon won't wait on your feelings." He slid the knife across the table toward Oscar, hilt first, an offer and a threat in one motion. "Hold it, if you like. Feel how easy it would be."

Oscar did not take it. They looked at it instead, lying on the green leather beside their name, and understood suddenly — with the clarity that always came a moment too late to be useful — that this was the true test. Not the delivery of August. The knife. Whether they would reach for their own erasure with their own hand.

"No," Oscar said quietly. "I'll give you my answer tomorrow. But I don't need the knife to know what my name weighs."

Something flickered behind Veyren's eyes — disappointment, or perhaps respect, or perhaps the gardener estimating a plant that would need pruning. "Tomorrow, then. Ninth bell, this chamber." He returned to the window. "Oh — and Oscar. The child ate poorly tonight. Refused supper. If you happen to pass the east rooms on your way down, a visit wouldn't be improper. Comfort them." The mild eyes found theirs in the window's reflection. "Whatever you decide, they should be calm on the final night. Begin practicing that now."

* * *

Oscar did not go to the east rooms.

They went down instead, past their own floor, past Sefa's quartermaster office with its lamp still burning (he knocked once, twice, received no answer, and did not wait for one), down and down the service stairs to the cold stone throat of the undercroft, where the pipes sang and the dark smelled of river water and tallow. They needed darkness. Darkness was where thinking happened honestly; lamplight only dressed thoughts up in respectable clothes.

Four years, four months, and nineteen days, struck free. For a child delivered calm and trusting to a machine designed to use their voice as a key and, if Sefa was right, their life as fuel.

*It's commerce,* Veyren had said. *Especially love.*

He was right, that was the obscene thing. He was right about all of it. Oscar's kindness to August *had* been bought with nine years of loneliness, and now it had a market price, and the market was open, and the seller was a fifteen-year-old debtor who had never truly escaped the day the ledger first closed around their name.

They pressed their forehead against the cold stone of the undercroft wall and breathed, and the pipes sang their one note, and somewhere above them the thirteenth moon dragged its borrowed light across the sky.

There was a version of this where Oscar took the bargain. They could feel that version waiting, patient as the ledger, reasonable as Veyren. Take it. Strike the line. Live. Go south. Eat an orange. Let the world's ending be managed by professionals. Wasn't that, in the end, exactly what the guild had trained them for nine years to believe — that some accounts were too large for any one person to balance, and the only dignity available to the small was accuracy?

And underneath that, quieter, the other voice — the one that had started speaking only recently, in August's accent, in the First Tongue's strange singing cadence:

*A ledger that owns your name can strike it. But a person who gives a child to the engine to buy their own name back — that person keeps the name, and loses everything it might have meant.*

Oscar straightened. Their breath fogged in the undercroft dark.

Twelve nights. One answer due at ninth bell tomorrow. And one page locked in a false-bottom drawer — a page of the First Tongue in their own hand, the phrase August had made them copy over and over until the shapes stopped being marks and started being meaning — that Veyren did not know existed.

Sefa had told them what the engine read. Veyren thought it read August's voice.

It read names. Anyone's name, spoken in the First Tongue.

Oscar climbed back up through the dark, slowly, one hand on the rail, counting the steps the way they counted everything now — and at the top of the stair, framed in the observatory chamber's doorway, lamplight behind her, arms crossed, commission-less and unmistakable, stood Ilara Vance.

"We need to talk," she said. "About what he offered you. Because he offered me the same thing an hour ago — and I want to know which of us he thinks will say yes."
