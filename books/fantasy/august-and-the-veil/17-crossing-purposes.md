## 17. Crossing Purposes

*Oscar*

The lamp in the archive alcove had burned down to a thumb's width of oil when Ilara Vance stepped out of the dark between the shelves.

She did not announce herself. She never had. In the borderlands she had learned to move like weather — you felt the pressure change before you understood what was coming — and now the whole archive seemed to hold its breath around her: the dust settling differently, the candle flames leaning away as if in deference or dread. She stood at the mouth of the alcove in her hunter's grey, rain still beaded on her shoulders from the walk across the courtyard, and she looked at Oscar the way she used to look at a trail that had gone cold.

"You've been avoiding me," she said.

"I've been working." Oscar did not look up from the ledger. Their pen kept moving. It was a small mercy of their trade that the hand could lie even when the voice could not; the nib traced its patient columns of figures while every other part of them strained toward her like a compass needle. "The Guild doesn't pay me to stand about being sociable."

"The Guild pays you to be accurate." She came closer. Her boots made no sound on the stone, which was somehow worse than if they had thundered. "And your accuracy has developed holes."

* * *

*Ilara*

Ilara had rehearsed this conversation eleven times on the road back from the borderlands, and twelve more times in the barracks washroom, watching her own face in the spotted mirror until it stopped looking like hers. Every version ended badly. In some she accused him outright. In one — the one she liked least, because it was the truest — she simply asked him to run, and went with him.

What she had not rehearsed was how he would look. Thinner. The lamplight found new hollows under his cheekbones, and his hands — those quick, clever hands she had once kissed, once mapped like territory, once believed were hers to know — trembled faintly above the ledger, though the writing never wavered. He had gotten better at hiding. That was the thing about loving a scribe: they learned to forge everything, eventually, including themselves.

"Show me the transcription," she said. "August's words. All of it."

"Why?"

"Because Master Veyren ordered a full accounting, and I counted the pages in the binding case this morning. There are nine missing." She let that sit between them like a blade laid flat on a table. "Nine pages of a language that can unmake the world, and no one in this guild can tell me where they went. Except perhaps the person who transcribed every word."

Oscar's pen stopped.

It was such a small thing, that stopping. But Ilara had spent six years learning to read the silences between people's words, out on the borders where a paused footstep meant ambush and a held breath meant the thing behind you had noticed you noticing. And Oscar's silence now was not the silence of innocence. It was the silence of someone counting.

"You think I took them," Oscar said quietly.

"I think someone took them. I think whoever took them knew exactly which pages mattered. And I think—" She stopped herself. This was where the rehearsal always failed her, where duty and devotion crossed like swords and neither won. "I think I'm asking you before I ask anyone else. That should mean something."

"It means everything," Oscar said. "That's what frightens me."

* * *

*Oscar*

The nine pages were beneath the floor tile under the third shelf of the alchemy section, wrapped in oilcloth, and they weighed on Oscar's mind like nine stones in a river crossing. They had been there for six days. Six days of carrying them through corridors past hunters, past Sefa's knowing eyes, past Veyren's study where the master sat late with his hidden almanac and his cold, patient arithmetic. Six days of lying by omission to the Guild, which was easy, and to Ilara, which was not.

Because Ilara had come back changed. Not softer — the borderlands did not soften anyone — but *settled*, the way a blade settles into its sheath after years of rattling. She wore her commission like skin now. And yet when she stood close enough that Oscar could smell woodsmoke and wet wool and the particular bitter herb she chewed against fatigue, something in Oscar's chest performed its old treachery, its old arithmetic: *one life plus one life, carried together, weighs less than either alone.*

They had been lovers for two winters, years ago, before her oath, before his debt swallowed the last of his freedom. They had parted in this same archive, and she had said, *When my term ends, find me,* and he had said, *I will,* and both of them had known that indentures do not end, they only change owners. Now here she was, sworn to guard the child he was stealing, asking questions whose honest answers would put a rope around both their necks.

"Ask me anything else," Oscar said. "Ask me where I sleep. Ask me what I eat. Ask me whether I believe the Guild's account of the Veil." They finally lifted their eyes to hers, and the look cost them something they would not be able to reclaim later. "Just don't ask me to hand you the truth in pieces small enough to file."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Oscar agreed. "It's a confession wearing an answer's clothes. You taught me to spot those, on the ridge above Coldharbor. You said: *when someone won't say yes or no, they're telling you the question is bigger than they're allowed to answer.*"

Ilara's jaw tightened. Rain dripped from her hair onto the stone, each drop loud as a struck coin. "You remember everything," she said. "That was always the trouble with you."

"And you remember nothing convenient. That was always the trouble with you."

For a moment — one suspended, unbearable moment — the hunter's mask slipped, and underneath it was just Ilara, twenty-three years old again, laughing at him in the snow because he'd tried to write a love letter in formal guildhand and produced something that read like a tax assessment. Underneath the grey coat and the oath and the six hard years, her eyes were still the color of deep water over slate, and they were afraid. Not of him. Of the space between them, which had grown teeth.

"Nine pages," she said softly. "If I report this—"

"Then I hang. Or worse. Veyren doesn't hang people anymore; he files them." Oscar closed the ledger with deliberate calm. "You know what happens to inconvenient records in this guild, Ilara. You've seen the furnace room."

"Don't." Her voice cracked along its length. "Don't make me picture it."

"Then don't report it."

* * *

*Ilara*

There it was. The fork in the road, laid bare, no fog to soften it.

*Don't report it.* Three words, and every one of them treason. If she turned and walked to Veyren's study right now, she would be doing her duty, and her duty was the only clean thing she owned. It had carried her through the borderlands when the fever took half her company. It had given shape to the years after Oscar, when loving anything had seemed like a luxury purchased with other people's blood. Duty did not leave you. Duty did not forge entries in ledgers or vanish into the night with stolen pages. Duty stayed.

But duty had also brought her back to this archive, to this alcove, to this man — because Veyren had assigned her to the child, and the child orbited Oscar like a moon, and she had told herself it was surveillance. She had told herself a great many things on the ride south.

"If I don't report it," she said slowly, "and it surfaces anyway — and it will surface, Oscar, things like this always surface — then I'm complicit. My name goes in whatever book they keep for people who chose wrong."

"There is no book for people who choose right," Oscar said. "I've checked. I balance it every day. There are only books for people who owe."

Something in her chest turned over. Because that was the wound of him, the whole wound of him, laid open without anesthesia: Oscar Reyes, who had sold seven years of their life to the Guild of Impossible Things for a debt inherited from a dead father, who tallied their remaining freedom nightly the way other men counted coins, who spoke about liberty the way starving people speak about bread — casually, bitterly, so as not to weep.

"How much is left?" she asked. "Your term."

"Four years. Two months. Eleven days." No hesitation. He had the number ready, the way a condemned man has the date of his execution ready. "Not that the number means anything anymore. I've seen what's in the vault, Ilara. I've seen what this guild built under the temple, and why. Four years is a courtesy they may not honor."

"What's in the vault?" she asked, and heard her own voice go flat and hunter-cold, because that was the question she should have asked first and had been circling for days like a bird over deep water.

Oscar studied her for a long moment. The lamp guttered. Somewhere far off in the stacks, a page turned itself — or sounded as if it did; the whole building had been behaving strangely since the child arrived, ink listening, paper remembering.

"You'd have to report that," Oscar said. "Whatever I show you, you'd have to carry it up to Veyren, because that's what your oath requires. So I'm going to ask you the only question that matters, and I want you to answer it honestly, because you've never lied to me and I need — I need one person in this building who hasn't."

"Ask."

"If I showed you proof that the thirteenth moon isn't a wonder. That it's a machine. That the Guild built it, and built *him* — built August, bought him before he was born, wrote his name in the founding charter a hundred years ago — if I showed you all of that, written in Veyren's own hand across three centuries of hidden almanacs..." Oscar's voice did not shake, but only because it was holding itself together with both hands. "Would you report it? Would you deliver it to the man who made it? Or would you finally, after six years, decide what you actually serve?"

* * *

*Ilara*

The silence stretched until it hurt.

She thought of the child then — August, with his mud-colored eyes and his terrible patience, the way he watched her patrol the corridor outside his room each night with an expression no child should own: the expression of livestock assessing a fence. She thought of the report she had filed last week, the one with the careful omissions, the one where she had written *subject sleeps soundly* when she had in fact heard him crying in the First Tongue at three in the morning, a sound like water running backward, and had stood outside his door with her hand raised to knock and had not knocked, because knocking would have made it real, would have made her responsible.

She had already chosen, she realized. Somewhere between the borderlands and this alcove, between the crying child and the careful lies in her own reports, she had already chosen. She had simply not yet had the courage to sign it.

"If you showed me that," she said, "and it was true, then my oath is to the Charter, and the Charter says the Guild exists to protect the Veil. Not to open it. Not to own children. Not to build moons." Her voice came out low and steady, and she was grateful for it, because inside she was anything but steady; inside she was nineteen again, taking her oath with her hand on the Charter, believing every word. "So no. I wouldn't report it to Veyren. I'd report it to the Charter. And I'd arrest anyone standing between the two."

"Including me."

"Including you." She held his gaze. "Especially you. You're the one standing closest."

Something moved across Oscar's face — relief and grief in equal measure, twins born at the same instant. "That's the most romantic thing you've ever said to me," they whispered.

"Don't." She stepped closer despite herself, close enough to see the oil-stain on their collar, the ink under their fingernails, the exhaustion carved into every line of them. "Don't make this a joke. I am standing on the edge of the only clean thing I have left, and you're making jokes."

"I'm not joking." Oscar's voice dropped to almost nothing. "I'm telling you that you just promised to arrest me, and it's the first time in six days I've believed anyone in this building might actually keep a promise. Do you understand what that makes you? It makes you the last honest thing I have left."

Her hand moved before she authorized it — rose and caught the front of his shirt, not to strike, not quite to hold, but to anchor herself to something solid in a world gone fluid. He did not pull away. His breath was warm and fast. The lamp flame leaned toward them like a curious animal.

"The nine pages," she said. "Where are they?"

"Under the third shelf, alchemy section. Wrapped in oilcloth. Where they've been since before you got back." A pause. "I wanted you to find them yourself. I couldn't decide if that was trust or cowardice."

"It was both. Everything worth doing is both." She released his shirt, smoothed the wrinkle her fist had made — an absurd, domestic gesture, absurd and devastating — and stepped back into her hunter's posture, spine straight, face composed. "I haven't decided anything tonight. Understand that. I walked in here a hunter and I'm walking out undecided, and undecided is the most dangerous thing I can be."

"I know."

"If Veyren moves the child before the final night—"

"He won't. He needs the thirteenth moon. We have until it rises." Oscar hesitated, and then, as if paying out the last rope of a bridge: "Ilara. When it rises — whatever you decide — decide *before* that night. Don't let him make the decision for you. That's what he does. He arranges the world so that doing nothing serves him best."

She turned to go. At the mouth of the alcove she stopped, her back to him, rain-damp and grey and trembling somewhere deep where it didn't show.

"The letter you wrote me," she said, "the winter we parted. The one in formal guildhand that read like a tax assessment."

"You remember that?"

"I've had six years to translate it." Her voice was very quiet. "Line forty-one. *All debts honestly accounted remain payable.* You weren't talking about money."

"No," Oscar said. "I wasn't."

She left. Her footsteps made no sound on the stone, but Oscar heard her anyway, the way you hear a door closing in another room — not with the ears, but with the sudden draft of a future changing shape.

Alone, they knelt, pried up the floor tile, and unwrapped the oilcloth. Nine pages. The First Tongue shimmered on them in the lamplight, rearranging itself, meaning breathing through the ink like blood through gills. On the topmost page, in letters older than kingdoms, one phrase repeated — the same phrase August murmured in his sleep, the same phrase the engine beneath the temple was tuned to receive:

*The door remembers who opened it.*

Oscar stared at it for a long time. Then, with the finest penmanship of their life, they began to copy it — not to hide it, not to destroy it, but to give it to Ilara in the morning, along with everything else.

Outside, through the high slit window, the sky over the temple was clear for the first time in weeks. And there, faint as a fingernail paring, where no moon had any business being —

the thirteenth moon had begun to rise early.
