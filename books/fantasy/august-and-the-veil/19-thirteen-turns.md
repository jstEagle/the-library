## 19. Thirteen Turns

The moons came to August in the dark, one by one, like coins being counted out of a purse that would soon be empty.

They had learned to sleep with their eyes half-open in the Guild of Impossible Things. The bed they had been given was softer than any bed they had ever known — stuffed with goose-down, smelling faintly of lavender and old money — but softness was its own kind of trap. Soft things held you. Soft things made you forget you were a guest, a specimen, a *specification*, as Master Veyren liked to say when he thought no one else could hear him through his office door.

So August lay awake on the third night of the waning moon and counted.

It was something the First Tongue had taught them before they could walk: that all things kept time, and all things could be read. The river outside the temple walls kept time in its stuttering over the weir. The candle in the corridor kept time in the way its flame leaned toward the draft under the door, always east, always toward the borderlands where the Veil grew thin. And above it all, past the smoked glass of the dormitory window, the sky kept time in silver.

One moon hung there now, fat and pale and slightly wrong, tilted at an angle no honest moon should tilt. But August knew — the way they knew the taste of rain before it fell — that it was not alone. It had twelve sisters hidden behind the world's shoulder, waiting their turn, and the Guild had built the arithmetic that would call them out one after another until the sky was crowded with thirteen faces and the Veil between worlds wore thin as wet paper.

Thirteen turns. That was what Sefa had whispered in the stairwell, not knowing August was curled on the landing above, listening the way small people learn to listen. Thirteen turns of the false moon, and then the engine beneath the temple would drink a voice and open a door.

August pressed their palm flat against the cold window glass and began, quietly, in the language older than kingdoms, to count down.

*Thirteen.*

* * *

In the morning, Oscar came with bread and ink-stained fingers and the particular careful smile that meant Oscar had spent the night worrying and did not want August to know it.

"You didn't sleep," Oscar said. It was not a question. Oscar had a scribe's eye for evidence — the shadows under August's eyes, the blanket folded but unslept-in, the faint fog of breath still ghosting the window.

"The sky was talking," August said.

Oscar sat down on the edge of the bed, careful, always careful, as though August were a page that might tear. "What did it say?"

August considered lying. Oscar had been teaching them to lie — strange lessons, held after curfew, in the gap between the archive shelves where the dust hung like held breath. *When someone asks what you know,* Oscar had said, *give them a smaller truth. A truth with the dangerous edges shaved off.* August was getting better at it. They had lied to Ilara twice now, and once to Veyren himself, and the lies had come out smooth as river stones.

But this was Oscar. And besides, August was tired of carrying the counting alone.

"It said *thirteen*," August said. "And then it said *twelve*. And every night it will say one less, until it says *none*."

Oscar went very still. Stillness, August had learned, was what fear looked like on grown-ups who had practiced being afraid quietly for years. "You know about the thirteenth moon."

"I know about all of them." August pulled their knees up under their chin. They were small for eight; everyone said so; Veyren said it too, in his warm voice, *such a little vessel for such a large purpose*, and August had only recently understood that *vessel* was a word for things that got filled up and used. "I counted them the first night I came here. Before I ever saw the temple. They were already in my head, waiting. Like words I'd forgotten."

"August." Oscar's voice had dropped low, the way it did when the corridors might have ears. "What do the moons *do*?"

"They take turns," August said. "And when the last one rises, the door opens. That's what the engine is for. That's what *I'm* for."

The words came out flat and simple, the way true things do when they have been sitting in your chest long enough. Oscar's face did something complicated — a whole weather system passing over it in the space of a heartbeat — and then Oscar reached out and took August's hand, which was also complicated, because Oscar's hands trembled even when the rest of them was steady.

"No one is using you for anything," Oscar said. "Not while I'm—"

But August shook their head, because they were tired of smaller truths too, tonight, from either direction.

"You don't understand yet," they said gently, because Oscar was the one who needed gentling now. "I've remembered more. In dreams. I remember the city beyond the Veil — the singing streets, the towers that hummed. And I remember why the First Speakers were shut out. Not because they were monsters. Because we were *loud*. Because our voices moved the world, and the world couldn't hold that much moving." They looked down at their own small hand inside Oscar's larger one. "The door doesn't open by itself, Oscar. Doors need keys. And keys get turned until their teeth wear smooth, and then they get thrown away."

Oscar's grip tightened. Outside, the false moon was setting into the smoke of the city, pale as a fingernail paring.

"That's not going to happen," Oscar said.

August smiled at them — the smile they had practiced, the one that said *yes, of course* — and gave Oscar the smaller truth, and let them believe it, because believing it was the only thing keeping Oscar upright.

But alone, afterward, with the bread going stale on the sill, August let themselves know the bigger one.

* * *

Here is what August knew, and had known since before they had words for knowing:

They had been born on the far side of a promise.

The memories came in fragments, most of them, like shards of a bowl that had been dropped a long time ago and never swept up. A tower of woven light. A woman whose face changed depending on how you looked at her — sometimes young, sometimes older than stone — who had held August's face in hands like cool water and said, *You will go through, little hinge, and you will be the last word of a very long sentence.*

He had worn many faces in the dreams. Sometimes the water-woman's. Sometimes a face August did not know. And once — only once, and it had frightened them so badly they had woken screaming — a face like Oscar's. Older, graver, eyes that had seen centuries, but the same stubborn set of the mouth, the same ink-shadowed fingers. That dream-face had stood at the edge of the Veil with its hand raised, and it had spoken the First Tongue fluently, beautifully, the way no mortal throat should be able to, and it had said:

*I will not let them spend you.*

August had told Oscar about the dream once, carefully, watching for the flinch. Oscar had gone quiet for a long time and then said only, "Dreams are strange country," in a voice that suggested the dream had walked through them too and left footprints.

Now, sitting cross-legged on the dormitory floor with the morning sun making a bright trapezoid on the boards, August laid the fragments out the way Oscar laid out ledger entries, and looked at the sum total without flinching.

Fragment: the thirteenth moon was built. Not born. Built — a mechanism of rings and counterweights somewhere beneath the temple, humming a note too low for ordinary ears, a note August could feel in their back teeth whenever they descended past the second cellar stair.

Fragment: the engine needed a key, and the key was a voice, and the voice was theirs, because they had been made — grown? chosen? *written*? — on the other side of the Veil for exactly this. The water-woman's hands. *Little hinge.*

Fragment: keys are used once.

Fragment: Master Veyren called them *vessel* and smiled like a man counting coins.

Fragment: Oscar's face, in a dream, saying *I will not let them spend you* — which meant that some part of the future, or some part of the deep past folded forward, already knew what August had only just finished understanding this morning.

That they were meant to die opening the door.

There. It was done. The thought, which had circled them for weeks like a hawk circling a field mouse, finally landed, and settled its weight, and was, in its terrible way, almost a relief. Fear of a thing unnamed is worse than the thing. August had crossed the Veil as an infant; they had walked out of the wilderness alone; they had knocked on the Guild's door in a storm because the storm itself seemed to be listening to them. They were not made for comfort. Perhaps they were not made for lasting.

But oh — and here was the part that hurt worse than dying, the part they could not shave the edges off of — oh, they had *liked* being alive here. The bread with honey that Oscar smuggled up. The way the archive smelled of leather and lamp oil. The secret lessons after curfew, Oscar's tongue stumbling through the First Tongue's rounded vowels, August laughing until the dust motes scattered. Ilara's gruff kindness, the way she pretended not to notice when August took extra from the supper table. Even the city itself, gray and smoky and loud, full of people who shouted and bargained and sang off-key — a world so gloriously *imperfect* compared to the humming perfection beyond the Veil.

They did not want to stop tasting things. That was the whole of it. That was the terrible, childish, unheroic truth. Thirteen turns, and then no more honey, no more laughter, no more Oscar.

August put their forehead against their knees and breathed, and let the grief move through them like weather, because the water-woman had taught them that too: *feel it all the way through, little hinge, or it will feel you.*

* * *

In the afternoon, they went looking for proof.

Proof was an Oscar-word. August had learned it from watching them work — the way Oscar never trusted a number until it appeared in two separate ledgers, never trusted a story until it left a paper trail. So August became, for one afternoon, a small scribe of their own life.

The vault stairs were guarded during daylight hours, but the guards were men, and men had bladders, and August had learned the rhythm of their rounds. When the corridor emptied, they slipped down past the second cellar, past the third, to the iron door that breathed its low subterranean note against their teeth. They did not go in. They only pressed their ear to the metal and listened to the engine dreaming, and confirmed the first entry: *it is real, it is waiting, it knows my name.*

Then the library. This was easier; children in archives were furniture, invisible, and August had spent weeks learning the geography of the stacks the way other children learned the geography of playgrounds. The founding charter was kept in the reading room, chained to its lectern like a dog that might bite. August had seen it before — had felt sick seeing it, though they hadn't known why until later — and now they climbed onto a stool and read it again with new eyes, hunting the second entry.

And there it was, in the middle of the charter's dry legal language, in ink darker than the rest, as if it had been written by a different hand or a different century:

*...and the key shall come, a child speaking the First Tongue, born under no lawful moon, and the Guild shall hold the child in trust until the thirteenth turn, when the debt of exile comes due and must be paid in full.*

*Paid in full.*

August read it three times. The words did not change. Words in ledgers, they had learned, rarely did — that was the whole horror of them. Somewhere, centuries ago, someone had written down what August was for, the way you write down the price of nails, and every Guild master since had simply carried out the transaction.

They were not a child who had been captured by the plan. They were a child the plan had ordered in advance, like a coat cut to measure before the wearer was born.

August slid off the stool. Their legs were steady. That surprised them a little. They had expected, from all the stories, that this kind of knowledge would knock you down. Instead it just stood beside you, patient as a shadow, and walked where you walked.

* * *

They found Oscar at dusk in the transcription room, hunched over the pages of August's own recorded speech, and closed the door behind them, and turned the little brass lock that Oscar had shown them, and said:

"I know what happens on the last night."

Oscar's pen stopped mid-stroke. A bead of ink swelled at the nib and fell, blooming black on the blotter like a small drowned star.

"August—"

"I read the charter. I heard Sefa. I count the moons every night, Oscar, I've counted them since before I could talk." August crossed the room and stood close, close enough to see the lamplight trembling in Oscar's eyes. "On the thirteenth turn, Veyren binds me to the engine, and my voice opens the door, and the door opens all the way, and I don't come back from that. Keys don't. I'm not afraid of being wrong about it. I checked. I checked everything twice, like you taught me." They swallowed. "I'm telling you because you deserve a truth with no edges shaved off. You've given me so many."

For a long moment Oscar did not move, and August watched — with the strange floating calm that had settled over them all day — as the scribe's face broke and reassembled itself, broke and reassembled, like ice on a thawing river.

"We'll find another way," Oscar said hoarsely. "There's always another way. Ledgers have margins. Every account has—"

"Oscar." August took the pen gently from their fingers and set it down. "Listen to me. I've accepted it. That's not the same as wanting it. But I need you to hear me say it plainly, because I think—" and here their voice did waver, just once, just a little, "—because I think you're going to try to trade yourself for me, and I can't—"

"Don't," Oscar whispered.

"—and I *can't* carry that too. If it has to be someone, let it be me. I was made for the door. You weren't. You were made for—" August searched for the word, and found it, and gave it in the First Tongue first, then translated: "*keeping*. You keep things. Accounts, promises, people. So keep this one thing for me: don't break yourself trying to unmake what I am. Promise me."

Oscar was crying now, silently, the way scribes cry, without disturbing the page. And August, who had faced the memory of exiled gods and the fact of their own ending with a steady heart, found that this — Oscar's tears — was the thing that nearly undid them.

They climbed onto the chair beside Oscar and put their arms around the scribe's shoulders, small arms around a shaking frame, and held on.

Outside the smoked glass, the false moon rose, tilted and wrong, beginning its twelfth-to-last turn. And far below them both, under cellars and foundations and the buried bones of the temple, the engine felt the child's grief through six floors of stone, and adjusted its rings by a fraction of a degree, and waited — patient as ink, patient as debt — for the voice it had been promised.

August felt it listening. And in the quietest corner of their heart, where they kept the things they told no one, they added a thirteenth fragment to the collection:

*Someone wearing Oscar's face once promised not to spend me. I intend to find out whether that promise belongs to the past — or to a future that hasn't happened yet. And if it hasn't happened yet, then maybe, maybe, it isn't fixed. Maybe futures are just drafts.*

*And if they're drafts —*

Down in the transcription room, August drew back from the embrace, wiped Oscar's cheeks with their sleeve, and said, in a voice that had stopped sounding like a child's:

"Now help me steal the ending. I want to write a different one."
