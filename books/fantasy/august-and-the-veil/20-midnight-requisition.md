## 20. Midnight Requisition

The vault had a heartbeat.

Oscar had felt it the first time they descended into the Room Beneath — a slow, tidal pulse that came up through the soles of their boots and settled somewhere behind the sternum, as though the orrery below were a great sleeping animal and every visitor a small warmth it registered in its dreams. Tonight, standing at the top of the spiral stair with Sefa's stolen ring of keys cold against their palm, Oscar felt that heartbeat quicken. Or perhaps it was only their own.

*You have done this a thousand times,* they told themselves. *You have forged a bishop's seal to settle a debt. You have copied a dead man's hand so faithfully his widow wept over the letter. You are the finest forger the Guild has ever owned.*

*Owned.* The word sat in them like a swallowed stone.

Below, through the iron grate in the floor, the moon-engine turned in its slow centuries. Even from here Oscar could hear the whisper of its rings — brass on oiled bearings, a sound like someone reciting numbers in their sleep. Thirteen concentric wheels, each keyed to a moon that existed or ought to exist, and at the center, waiting like an empty chair at a table set for one, the cradle where August was meant to stand.

Oscar pressed their ear to the door of the archive antechamber and listened. Rain worked at the temple roof above, a patient percussion. Somewhere two floors up, a night clerk coughed in his sleep. The Guild slept the way a knife sleeps: lightly, and with intent.

They unlocked the door.

* * *

The keystone page lived in the Vellum Sanctum, the innermost room of the archive, where documents too dangerous for shelves were kept flat in drawers lined with ash-wood and salt. To reach it, Oscar had to pass the long gallery of founding charters, past the glass case where August's name waited in ink older than the child's bones — *AUGUST, GIVEN, THIRTEENTH TURN* — and Oscar made themselves look at it as they passed. Made themselves read it slowly, the way one reads a threat.

*This is what they did to you before you were born. This is what I am going to unmake.*

Their breath fogged the glass. They wiped it clean with their sleeve, because a scribe leaves no trace, because that was the first law of the trade and tonight it would keep them alive.

The Sanctum door required three keys and a spoken word. The word was a guild phrase in Old Common, something about harvests and honest weights, and Oscar spoke it quietly, feeling absurd, feeling like a thief in a story — which, they supposed, was exactly what they were now. The lock turned with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

Inside, the air was dry as old bread and smelled of salt, ash, and the faint copper tang of preservation spells laid down by archivists three generations dead. Moonlight came through a single high slit of window, thin as a blade, and lay across the drawer fronts in a pale stripe. Oscar did not light a lamp. Their eyes had been trained for this — twenty years of reading by whatever light the Guild begrudged them — and besides, lamplight in the Sanctum would be visible under the door, and Ilara Vance missed nothing. Not anymore. Not since the fire.

*Page eleven burned itself to nothing in front of nine witnesses,* Oscar reminded themselves. *And she looked at me across that smoke and knew. She didn't say it. But she knew.*

He pulled the third drawer from the left.

There it lay under a sheet of waxed linen: the keystone page. It was smaller than Oscar had imagined — no larger than a hymnal leaf — and deceptively plain, a rectangle of vellum gone the color of weak tea, covered edge to edge in the First Tongue. The characters did not sit still. Even in starlight they moved, the way water moves when you look at it sideways: loops becoming locks, locks becoming doors, doors becoming the suggestion of a mouth about to speak. This was the page that told the engine which voice to listen for. Which name to open upon.

August's name was not written on it. That was the horror and the genius of the thing. The page was blank of names entirely — a lock waiting to be told what it guarded. Whoever held this page, and knew how to speak to it, held the thirteenth moon in the palm of their hand.

Veyren kept it here, unguarded by anything but ritual and fear, because he believed no one else could read it. Because he believed the Guild's scribes were tools, and tools do not learn the language of their masters' machines.

Veyren had never watched Oscar transcribe August at midnight. Had never seen the ink lean toward meaning like a plant toward sun. Had never understood that a person who spends their life copying other people's words eventually becomes a vessel that any language can pour itself into.

Oscar lifted the page with both hands, fingertips at the edges only, and felt it hum against their skin — faint, insistent, like a bird's heart. It *knew* it was being taken. Perhaps pages like this always know.

"Now," Oscar whispered to it, "you and I are going to make a lie together."

* * *

They had prepared the forgery over six nights, working in fifteen-minute increments between the rounds of the night clerks, and those six nights had been the strangest devotion of their life. Forging an ordinary document meant imitating a hand. Forging this meant imitating a *language that wrote itself* — and so Oscar had done the only thing possible. They had learned to let go.

Each practice attempt began the same way: a sheet of matching vellum, aged with tea and smoke and buried for two days in the ash-bin beneath the scriptorium stove; a stylus of bone; and August's voice in their memory, teaching them the shapes after curfew in the dark of the map room, August's small finger tracing loops in the dust while Oscar repeated them aloud until the syllables stopped tasting foreign.

*It isn't drawing,* August had said, exasperated, the way children are exasperated by slow adults. *It's remembering. You already know it. It's just very old in you.*

And the strange, terrible truth — the truth Oscar had not yet confessed even to themselves in full — was that August was right. When Oscar let the stylus move without governing it, when they thought of rain and debt and Ilara's face and the sound the Veil made in dreams, the characters came out of their hand almost whole. Imperfect. Flickering where the true page flowed. But legible to the engine, Sefa promised. Legible enough.

*A lock doesn't need a perfect key,* Sefa had said, counting out her price in whispers. *It needs a key that turns.*

Now, in the Sanctum, with the original humming against their palms, Oscar laid the forgery beside it and compared them by starlight, line by line, the way they had compared ten thousand documents in ten thousand years of servitude. The vellum matched. The aging matched — they had even reproduced a foxing stain along the lower right corner, drip by patient drip of tannin. The script—

The script flickered. There. Along the fourth line, where the original's characters turned like slow fish, the forgery's characters stuttered, hesitated, became merely *drawn*. A dead thing imitating a living one. Any archivist with a trained eye might see it. Might.

Oscar closed their eyes. Breathed. And did the thing they had failed to do in six nights of practice, the thing they had been afraid of without admitting the fear: they stopped trying to copy, and started trying to *mean*.

*I am doing this so a child does not die,* they thought, pouring the thought down their arm, into the bone stylus they'd brought for exactly this repair. *I am doing this because every ledger in this building is a cage and I have been ink in its veins for twenty years. I am doing this because the Veil was built on a lie and I will not build another one. Mean it. Mean every stroke.*

The stylus moved. The characters woke. Where they had flickered, they began to turn — slowly at first, then with the easy motion of the original, loop becoming lock becoming door. Oscar felt the correction leave their body like blood leaving a wound: not just effort but certainty, years of certainty, all of it spent in four lines of borrowed script.

When they finished, the two pages lay side by side and Oscar could not tell them apart.

Not *almost*. Not *close enough*. Could not tell them apart — and Oscar had once detected a forgery by the weight of a single period. They laughed, or something near laughter, a small broken sound in the salt-smelling dark, and had to press a hand over their own mouth. Exhilaration went through them like lightning through a mast, root to crown. Twenty years of copying other people's truths, and here, at last, was a lie worthy of them. The finest work of their life, and no one would ever see it. No one could ever know. That was the shape of mastery nobody warned you about: your best work must vanish completely, or it kills you.

They slid the forgery into the drawer, squared it under the waxed linen, and closed the drawer with the tenderness of a gravedigger.

The original went inside their shirt, against their skin, where its hum beat faintly against their breastbone like a second, smaller heart. Point of no return. They had crossed it already, of course — weeks ago, the first forged entry, the first midnight lesson, the first time they'd looked at August and thought *mine to protect* instead of *theirs to use*. But there are crossings you make in the dark and only feel the water afterward, and this was one. From this moment, every path behind them was burning. If the forgery held until the thirteenth turn, they won everything. If it failed — if one character stuttered, if one clerk opened that drawer a day early —

Then Oscar Reyes would die in the Room Beneath, and August would open the Veil anyway, and everything would have been for nothing except the manner of the failing.

Oscar stood a moment longer in the starlight, letting their eyes memorize the room — the drawers, the stripe of moon, the dust undisturbed except where it must be — and then did the last, hardest thing. They took out their knife and cut a thread from their own cuff and left it, coiled small, in the seam of the drawer's lining.

Sefa's instruction. *Leave a flaw. A perfect theft is invisible, and an invisible theft is blamed on ghosts, and ghosts get hunted for months. Leave a flaw, and everyone will find it within a day, and everyone will believe that finding it means the danger is past.* The quartermaster understood ledgers the way sharks understand water. Oscar hated how much they were learning from her.

They locked the Sanctum. Locked the antechamber. Wiped the fog of their breathing from the charter case as they passed August's name, and this time did not let themselves read it.

* * *

The stairwell was where it went wrong.

Oscar was three steps from the ground floor, moving at the pace of a tired clerk, when the lamp at the landing came alight — not gradually, the way lamps do, but all at once, flaring like a struck match. Someone had warded it. Someone had warded it *tonight*, after the fire, after the tightening, and Oscar's stomach dropped through twenty years of floors.

Ilara Vance stepped out of the shadow of the arch, commission papers in one hand, hunting-knife at her hip, and her eyes found Oscar on the stairs the way her arrows found things: instantly, and without mercy, and — once, long ago, in a summer neither of them mentioned anymore — without any wish to hurt.

"Scribe." Her voice was level. "You're awake late."

"Balancing," Oscar said, and heard with relief that their voice did the weary thing perfectly, the small bitter laugh, the shrug of a man owned by his columns. "Master Veyren wants the transcription reconciled against the incident reports before the turn. As if ink keeps regular hours."

Something moved behind Ilara's eyes — the old argument between what she felt and what she'd sworn, playing itself out in the space of a breath. She looked at Oscar's hands. At the shirt buttoned to the throat. At the single bead of sweat, despite the cold, at Oscar's temple.

"The fire," she said. "Page eleven."

"What of it?"

"It didn't burn like paper." She came one step closer, and the lamp threw her shadow up the wall behind her, tall and winged. "Paper burns fast and dirty. That page burned clean, from the center out, like something leaving it. Like the words evacuated." Her jaw tightened. "I've seen border-witchery do that. I've seen *First Tongue* do that."

The keystone page pulsed against Oscar's chest, once, like a heart that had overheard its own name.

"You've been in the borderlands too long," Oscar said softly. "Come inside. Have tea. Remember that the world is mostly paperwork."

For a long moment Ilara said nothing. The rain filled the silence, patient as an archivist. Then she reached out — and Oscar's whole body braced for the search, for the hands at their collar, for the end of everything — but she only straightened Oscar's collar where the stairs had rumpled it. A gesture from another lifetime. Her fingers lingered half a heartbeat longer than duty allowed.

"Go balance your columns, Reyes," she said, turning away. "And Oscar —" she paused at the arch, not looking back, her voice dropped to something barely louder than the rain — "whatever you're carrying under that shirt. Whatever it is. Be *very* sure."

She was gone up the stair, lamp guttering behind her, and Oscar stood alone in the dark with their heart slamming and the stolen page beating against it, two rhythms refusing to synchronize.

*She knows.* Not everything. Not yet. But Ilara Vance had always been able to read Oscar the way Oscar read ink — and sooner or later, she would file what she'd just seen.

Oscar climbed the last steps, walked the long corridor to their cell, bolted the door, and only then let themselves take the page out and hold it up to the window, where the thirteenth moon — nearly full now, wrong and beautiful, hanging where no calendar had ever promised it — lit the living characters until they seemed to swim like fish in dark water.

"Turn," Oscar whispered to it, in the First Tongue, the word August had taught them, the word for *open gently*.

The page shivered. Turned.

Somewhere far below, in the Room Beneath, the great rings of the moon-engine changed their whisper — a half-tone, a held breath — and Oscar understood, with exhilaration and terror in equal measure, that the engine had just heard its new key for the very first time.

And that it had liked the sound.

* * *

Dawn was still an hour off when the knocking started — three blows, spaced evenly, from the corridor side of Oscar's bolted door. Not a clerk's knock. Not a hunter's either; hunters knocked like they broke things.

This knock was small. Patient. In a rhythm Oscar knew, because they had invented it together, two conspirators in a dark map room: *shave-and-a-cut, ledger-man, open up.*

August.

Oscar crossed the cell and put their eye to the seam. The corridor was empty — but the keystone page had gone suddenly, violently warm against their skin, warm as fever, warm as a hand, and outside the narrow window over the water cistern, where no moon should be, the thirteenth moon's light had begun, very softly, to bend toward the temple.

Toward the vault. Toward the drawer where the forgery lay sleeping.

Toward the lie that had less than a day left to hold.
