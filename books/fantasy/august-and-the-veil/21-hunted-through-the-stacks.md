## 21. Hunted Through the Stacks

* * *

Ilara Vance found the lie the way she found everything: by standing still longer than anyone else was willing to.

The vault of the Guild of Impossible Things held forty thousand ledgers, and she had been walking their aisles since the fourth bell of the morning, running her thumb along spines, letting her eyes do what hunters' eyes did — refusing to skip. The others swept in patterns. Patterns were for people who expected to find things. Ilara swept like a woman reading a confession, and so it was she who stopped at the shelf marked *Requisitions: Keystone Instruments*, and it was she who pulled the slim black volume with the brass corner-caps, and it was she who felt — before she saw it — that the page was wrong.

Paper had a weight to it. Ink had a weight. A forged page was always a hair too eager, its lines drawn with the faint desperation of a hand trying very hard to be ordinary. She had known Oscar Reyes for six years; she had watched those hands write letters by candlelight, had fallen asleep to the scratch of them. She knew how they moved when they meant it, and how they moved when they were afraid.

She turned to the keystone requisition entry and read it twice.

Then she closed the ledger, held it against her chest for one long breath, and thought: *You magnificent, doomed fool.*

She could have put it back. She could have said nothing, let the sweep pass, let the discrepancy surface days from now when it would be too late to matter. That was the temptation, and it arrived wearing a face she had kissed in a boathouse on the Aldmere canal, in another life, before the borderlands taught her what oaths cost.

But Veyren was already suspicious. And if Ilara concealed a forgery and it surfaced later, she would not merely fail August and Oscar — she would be removed, replaced by someone with no reason at all to hesitate at the canal gate.

So she carried the ledger to the master's study, laid it open on his desk, and said, "The keystone page has been copied. Badly enough to be found. Well enough to have been used."

Master Veyren did not touch the book. He looked at it the way a man looks at a wound in his own hand — with a kind of distant, offended wonder.

"Who?" he asked.

"The handwriting is masked. But there are only four people with vault access after curfew." She kept her voice level, her eyes on the middle distance, giving him nothing of what she knew. "I can narrow it."

"No need." Veyren rose, and the lamplight made his shadow climb the wall behind him until it seemed to wear the room like a robe. "Ring the bell. All of it. Every hunter in the house. Seal the doors, lock the stairwells, and bring me the scribe."

He paused at the window, watching rain stitch silver threads down the glass, and added, almost gently:

"And Vance — do not find them first. Find them *only*. Whatever you feel about the scribe, remember what they've stolen. They haven't taken a page. They've taken the thirteenth moon."

* * *

Oscar heard the bell before they understood it.

It began as a single deep note somewhere below the archive — the great bronze alarm that hung in the guild's oldest stairwell, cast, according to legend, from the melted seals of a bankrupt kingdom. One note. Then another. Then a third, faster, and the sound stopped being a sound and became a weather system, rolling up through the floors, rattling the ink bottles on Oscar's desk, setting every ledger in the archive humming faintly on its shelf like a hive waking.

August looked up from the floor, where they had been drawing shapes in spilled salt — First Tongue characters, small and patient as insect tracks.

"That's a bad bell," the child said.

"That is a catastrophically bad bell." Oscar was already moving, sweeping the salt drawing into their palm and crushing it, snuffing the lamp with a pinch of wet fingers. Their heart had gone strange and light, the way it always did in the first second of disaster — a terrible clarity, the world suddenly all edges. *They found the forgery.* Of course they had. Of course. He had made it perfect, and perfect was the problem; a perfect duplicate drew the eye precisely because everything around it was imperfect. He should have left a blot. He should have left a flaw. Somewhere in the last three years he had forgotten that the guild's whole architecture ran on flaws — debts, errors, small corruptions — and honesty, real honesty, was the one thing no one here would believe.

"August. Listen to me. We're going to walk now, calmly, toward the west stacks—"

The door at the end of the scriptorium corridor banged open.

Oscar did the arithmetic in half a heartbeat: two hunters, lanterns, thirty feet of corridor, one child. No. Not that way.

They went the other way instead — through the binding room, past the great press with its screws and stink of hot glue, and into the stacks.

The archive's hidden geography was the one inheritance Oscar had never sold. Every scribe learned the official map: numbered aisles, catalogued shelves, the tidy grid of the guild's memory. But Oscar had spent seven years balancing impossible accounts, and impossible accounts required impossible storage — acquisitions the guild owned but denied, books seized but never entered, whole collections that existed only in the margins of other books' inventories. Behind the official shelves lay older shelves, unnumbered, built into the bones of the building itself: the ghost library, where the guild kept everything it wished it didn't know.

Oscar pressed a particular carved panel — a moth, wings folded, worn nearly smooth by generations of guilty hands — and the shelving swung inward on a hinge greased with tallow.

"In," they whispered. "Quickly. Don't touch anything with your bare skin."

"Why?"

"Because some of these books bite."

August slipped through, and Oscar followed, easing the panel shut just as the scriptorium door burst open behind them and lantern-light slashed across the room they had left.

They stood in darkness that smelled of dust and vellum and something older underneath — stone sweat, the cold breath of the temple's foundations. Through the gap between shelves, Oscar watched two hunters move through the binding room, methodical, lanterns raised. One of them paused at the salt. Bent. Touched the crushed traces with one finger.

"Tracks," the hunter said. "Fresh."

"Sound the aisle bells. Sweep pattern nine. Nobody goes alone."

Their boots receded. Oscar let out a breath they'd been holding since the first bell stroke, and in the dark, August's small hand found theirs — not grabbing, just resting there, the way you rest a hand on a table to confirm it's solid.

"You've done this before," August whispered.

"Never," Oscar whispered back. "But I've read about it extensively."

And despite everything — the bells still tolling overhead, the net already closing — he felt the child smile in the dark.

* * *

Sefa heard the alarm from the quartermaster's cage and did not stop counting.

That was the discipline of her life: whatever fell, whatever burned, whatever came through the door with knives or writs or holy fury, the count continued. She finished the bolt-fabric tally, blotted it, squared the page, locked the strongbox, and only then lifted her head and listened properly.

Three-bell pattern. Vault breach protocol. And beneath it, fainter, the particular shuffle-and-stamp of hunters being issued crossbows at midday, which meant someone upstairs believed the thief was dangerous, which meant someone upstairs believed the thief was *close*.

Sefa smiled the way a moneylender smiles at a debtor's misfortune — professionally, without warmth, and with an exact calculation of profit.

She had told Oscar about the engine beneath the temple because she wanted it stolen. That had been the whole architecture of her generosity: not kindness, not conscience, but leverage. A stolen keystone page meant a hunt; a hunt meant chaos; chaos meant the vault's secondary mechanisms — the ones even Veyren didn't know she knew about — would be briefly, beautifully unwatched. She had planned to slip down during the panic herself.

What she had not planned was how fast the panic would come, or how thoroughly the guild would seal itself. Already she could hear the bar dropping across the main doors, the chain going up over the stairwell gate. Veyren wasn't hunting a thief tonight. He was building a box.

Which meant Oscar Reyes and the child were inside the box with him.

Sefa considered this. She owed Oscar nothing — less than nothing; the forged entries in her own ledger were a hanging offense if ever audited. But Sefa had survived thirty years in the guild by understanding one truth above all others: *when the powerful build a box, they always intend to be the ones holding the lid.* And Veyren holding the lid, with the engine primed and the thirteenth moon six nights out, was a future in which Sefa's own careful thefts became footnotes in someone else's triumph.

No. If the box closed, everyone inside it lost. Including her.

She took the ring of grey keys from its hook — the keys nobody officially acknowledged, that opened the service ways, the old drainage galleries, the passages the temple's builders had made for reasons the guild had never bothered to learn — and stepped out of her cage, locking it behind her out of pure habit.

"Hold the count," she murmured to the empty room, and went down into the walls.

* * *

The ghost library went deeper than any map admitted.

Oscar moved through it by touch and memory, one hand on August's shoulder, the other trailing along shelf-edges that grew older as they descended — planed oak, then rougher pine, then shelves cut directly into living rock, holding books whose titles were stamped in languages the catalog refused to name. Above them the bells went on and on, muffled now to a heartbeat rhythm, and between the tolls they could hear the hunters: boots on stone, voices calling aisles, the dry ratcheting sound of crossbow cranks.

"They're getting closer," August said. Not frightened. Reporting. The child had a way of stating terrible facts the way other children reported the weather.

"Above us, yes. We're going under them. There's a stair behind the sealed collection — the one the charter calls the *Registry of Withheld Things* — and it comes out near the canal gate."

"The gate Ilara watches."

Oscar's stomach dropped. "You remember that."

"I remember everything you say when you think I'm asleep." August's voice was very quiet in the dark. "You talk about her like a word you lost."

Oscar had no answer for that, so they gave the only honest one available: silence, and a tighter grip on the child's shoulder, and forward motion.

The stair was where seven years of marginalia said it would be — behind a door disguised as a shelf-back, its handle a fake book spine labeled in faded gilt: *Debts Forgiven*. Oscar almost laughed. Only the guild would file a doorway under absolution.

They were halfway down when the light found them.

It came from below — a lantern swinging up the stairwell, and with it voices, two hunters climbing from the canal side, sealing the lower routes. Oscar froze, pulling August back against the wall into a recess that might once have held a statue of some saint the guild had decided against. The lantern-light climbed the steps toward them, throwing their shadows huge and wavering across the rock.

Ten steps away. Eight. Oscar's hand found a loose piece of shelf-iron on the ground — a bracket, heavy, cold — and their mind went to a place they hated: the arithmetic of violence, weight versus distance, whether they could strike hard enough to drop one hunter before the other shouted.

Then August stepped out of the recess.

"Wait," the child said — and spoke a single syllable of the First Tongue.

It was not loud. It was barely a sound at all. But the stairwell *answered*. The lantern-flame bent sideways as if in wind that wasn't there. The words of the hunters died in their mouths — Oscar saw one of them open his lips and produce nothing, his face flooding with animal confusion. And every book on the ancient shelves around them shivered at once, a soft rustling like ten thousand pages turning to look.

The hunters stood in the ringing dark, pale, disarmed by something they couldn't name and wouldn't report — because who would believe it?

"Up," August said, in plain trade-tongue now, pointing upward with the serene authority of a child directing adults. "We go up. They go down."

And the hunters — veterans, killers, men who had dragged fugitives out of burning buildings — turned and went down the stairs, quickly, without deciding to.

Oscar stared. "What did you say to them?"

"I didn't say it to *them*." August took Oscar's hand again. Their palm was warm, human, trembling slightly now that it was over. "I said it to the stair. I asked it to be a door going the wrong way. It agreed." A pause. "Most old things agree, if you ask politely. It's only people who argue."

Oscar filed that away in the part of the mind where terror and scholarship had begun to share a desk, and they climbed.

* * *

Ilara swept the upper archive with a squad of six and found exactly what she intended to find: nothing.

It was a delicate craft, finding nothing well. Too fast and Veyren's other hunters would circle back behind her; too slow and someone would notice the scribe's trail going cold wherever she happened to be standing. So she searched thoroughly and wrongly — checked the scriptorium twice, combed the reading rooms, sent two men to the roof though no roof in the guild's history had ever sheltered anyone. Each wrong search bought Oscar minutes. She spent them like a miser spending gold she'd sworn to give away.

Her report, when she called it in at the relay point by the astronomy tower, was precise and useless: *"No sign of the scribe or the child in the north ranges. Recommend sealing the canal gate."*

She recommended sealing the canal gate because she knew — with the sick certainty of a woman who had loved a liar and learned to read the lies — that Oscar would make for the water. Recommending it was cover. Getting there first was the plan.

What she had not planned for was the third figure moving through the lower corridors, keys in hand, whistling an old border song under her breath.

Sefa passed within ten feet of her at the junction by the cistern stairs, and both women pretended, with flawless professional courtesy, not to see each other. Ilara understood immediately what the quartermaster's presence meant — the grey keys, the downward direction, the whistling — and understood, too, that reporting it would seal every route including the one she needed open.

Some conspiracies are never spoken aloud. They simply happen, in parallel, between people who would deny each other to their graves.

Ilara adjusted her patrol route toward the canal gate and began, quietly, to rehearse the many faces of a woman who has caught no one.

* * *

The last stretch of the ghost library ended at a wall of chained folios, and behind it — Oscar pressed the moth-panel a final time, shoulders screaming, August hauling on the edge beside them — the service gallery opened up: a brick throat of a passage, breathing cold air that tasted of river.

They had made it perhaps fifty paces when the bells changed.

The three-note alarm cut off mid-stroke, and a new pattern began — two notes, pause, two notes, urgent as a fist on a door. Oscar knew the code from the guard rosters they transcribed. *Target sighted.*

Not them. Someone else had been seen.

They pressed into an alcove as boots thundered overhead, and through a rusted grate in the ceiling Oscar glimpsed lanterns crossing the gallery above, converging — away from the canal, toward the east wing. Toward the sealed collections. Toward—

Toward the place where Sefa had gone down.

"Oh no," Oscar breathed. "Oh, you *idiot*, Sefa—"

"Is she the one who helped us?" August asked.

"She's the one who helped herself. We were incidental." But Oscar was already moving faster, dragging the child through the dark, because whatever game the quartermaster was playing, its collapse would bring the whole hunt down on top of them. "Come on. The gate. Before they remember the water exists."

The passage sloped. The air grew wetter. And then, ahead, framed at the end of the gallery like a painting hung in a tomb: the canal gate — iron-barred, moonlit, the black water sliding beyond it, and a lantern set on the quay stones outside, and a silhouette standing beside the lantern with a hunter's stillness.

Ilara.

Oscar stopped so abruptly that August bumped into their back. Every escape they had rehearsed assumed pursuit from behind. None of it accounted for the person in front — for six years of letters and laughter and one catastrophic silence, standing between them and the river with a crossbow slung across her shoulder.

August peered around Oscar's hip at the waiting figure, then up at Oscar's face, reading it the way the child read everything — completely, mercilessly, with love.

"That's her," August said softly. "Isn't it."

Behind them, far off in the dark of the gallery, the two-note signal sounded again — closer now, and multiplying.
