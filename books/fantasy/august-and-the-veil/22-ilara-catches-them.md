## 22. Ilara Catches Them

The rain had stopped an hour before dawn, but the city hadn't noticed. Water still fell from every eave and gutter in long silver threads, pooling in the cobbles of Tanner's Row until the whole street became a shallow mirror, and Ilara Vance walked through that mirror with her boots splashing and her hood down because she wanted the cold on her face. She wanted to feel something other than the thing coiled in her chest, which had no name she was willing to give it.

She had known for two days. She had known since the moment she stood in the scorched archive and looked at the ash of the burned transcription page and then at the duplicate keystone entry in the engine's requisition ledger — a forgery so perfect it had taken her three hours of comparison against the guild's master exemplars to find the flaw. One hairline hesitation in the downstroke of the *th*. A scribe's hand pausing at the exact moment his conscience did.

She knew Oscar Reyes's hand the way she knew her own heartbeat. She had watched it for six years across candlelit tables, across shared ledgers, across the narrow geography of a bed too small for two people who both dreamed of running. She could find that hand in ten thousand pages. She could find it blind.

And so she had done what duty demanded: nothing. She had filed no report. She had told no hunters. She had walked the archive corridors with her lantern held high and her eyes aimed carefully at nothing, and she had let the hours bleed away like a man letting blood from his own arm to buy time for someone else's escape.

Now the time was spent. The gate was ahead.

The Canal Gate was the oldest of the city's nine water-doors, a black arch of riverstone through which the Sere flowed out beneath the wall and into the marsh country beyond. In daylight it was a place of barges and fishwives and shouting. At this hour it was silent except for the water, which moved under the arch with a sound like slow breathing, and the lamplight lay on it in broken gold coins that drifted east toward the border and never came back.

Ilara stopped in the shadow of the customs house and waited, because she already knew they were coming. She had known since midnight, when the watch rotation she herself had written put the gate guard at his supper and left forty minutes of emptiness along the quay. She had written that rotation three days ago. She had told herself it was coincidence. She had never once believed it.

They came out of the tanner's alley at half past four, and even now, even expecting them, the sight of it struck her somewhere beneath the ribs.

Oscar first, moving low, one arm around the child. August walked close against the scribe's side, so close their shadows merged into a single shape on the wet stones, and the boy — girl? Ilara still didn't know; the child seemed to change depending on how you looked, the way the First Tongue changed depending on who read it — the child's bare feet made almost no sound on the cobbles. Mud from the storm still streaked the small face, dried now into pale continents across brown skin, and the eyes above it were open and unblinking and fixed on the gate as though the child had been walking toward this exact arch since before the city had a name.

*Since before the Veil*, said a voice in Ilara's head that sounded uncomfortably like the cartographer's dying whisper, the report she had read and sealed and told no one about. *The thin places run like veins. The last one is inside the temple.* No. Not inside. *Out* — the gate led out, and beyond the wall the marsh began, and beyond the marsh—

She stepped out of the shadow.

"Stop there."

It came out harder than she intended, or exactly as hard as she intended; she honestly couldn't tell anymore. Her voice cracked off the arch of the gate and came back to her doubled, and she saw Oscar flinch — saw the whole body seize, the arm tighten around August, the free hand dart up not in surrender but instinctively, protectively, over the child's ear.

Then Oscar turned, and the lantern light found their face, and Ilara felt six years collapse into a single breath.

They looked terrible. They looked luminous. There was ink on their jaw — of course there was, there had always been ink on their jaw, some smudge or fleck she used to wipe away with her thumb without either of them remarking on it — and their eyes were red-rimmed and sleepless and absolutely, terrifyingly calm. It was the calm that frightened her. She had loved a weary person, a resigned person, a person who counted down years like coins in a jar. This person standing before her had spent all the coins. This person had nothing left to lose and had discovered, apparently, that it made them lighter.

"Ilara." Just her name. No plea in it. That was worst of all — that Oscar hadn't even begun to plead.

"Don't," she said. "Don't say anything clever. I've rehearsed this arrest eleven times in my head and every version ends with me saying something clever back, and I don't have it in me tonight."

August shifted behind Oscar's arm. The child looked up at Ilara with those unblinking eyes, and Ilara felt the look go through her like a hook through cloth — felt, absurdly, *understood*, as if the child were reading her the way the ledgers read August, finding the meaning underneath the sound. Finding the word under the words.

"You're the one from the dream," August said.

The voice was small and clear and it carried strangely in the wet air, the way a bell carries farther than its size should allow. Ilara's hand went to her sword hilt without her permission, an old reflex, and the child tilted their head at the gesture with something that was almost pity.

"What dream?" Ilara heard herself ask. Fool. Weak. She was supposed to be arresting them, not conducting conversations.

"The promise dream," August said simply, as if everyone had it, as if it were weather. "You were standing in a doorway wearing gray, and you wouldn't come in, and you wouldn't leave. And you said—" the child frowned, searching, and when the words came they came in a cadence that was not quite the common tongue, vowels rounded and old, "—*you said, I will stand where I am, and the door will stay a door.*"

The water breathed under the arch. Somewhere far off in the sleeping city a dog barked twice and gave up.

Ilara realized her hand had fallen away from her sword. She realized she was shaking, very slightly, in a way she hadn't shaken since the borderlands, since the night the raiders came over the ice and she had held the line alone for an hour and afterward couldn't stop trembling and Oscar had held her until dawn without saying a single word, because Oscar had always understood that some things needed witness and not comfort.

"That wasn't a dream," she said quietly. "That was a memory. From before."

"Before what?" Oscar asked. And there it was — the scholar's hunger, the thing that had made them the Guild's best scribe and the worst possible person to own, because you could chain a curious mind's body but the curiosity itself would keep digging.

"Before I took the commission," Ilara said. "Before Veyren pinned the gray on me and told me the child was cargo and the cargo was holy and my love for you was a liability he was choosing, generously, to overlook." She laughed, and hated the sound. "I thought I could be both. Duty by day and— and whatever we were by night. I thought the two halves of me could live in the same house without burning it down."

"And now?"

"Now I've read your handwriting in a forged ledger, Oscar. Now I know you stole the keystone page. Now I know that in—" she checked, bitterly, the internal clock she'd been keeping, "—in perhaps twenty minutes, the night watch finds the empty cell, and by sunrise there are thirty hunters on the marsh roads, and every one of them is better rested than I am." She stepped closer. The lamp caught them both, and for a moment they might have been anyone, any two people saying goodbye at any gate in any century. "So I'm asking you, and I need the truth because everything else I have is already spent: can you get them across the Veil? Do you actually know what you're doing? Or are you just running, and taking a child with you?"

Oscar held her gaze. Behind them, August watched with the patience of stone, of moonstone, of something older than patience.

"I know what I'm doing," Oscar said. "Not all of it. Not enough of it. But I know the first true thing I've learned in fifteen years of writing down other people's lies: the Veil isn't a wall, Ilara. It's a sentence. And sentences can be rewritten." A pause. The ghost of the old crooked smile, the one that used to mean trouble and delight in equal measure. "I'm going to rewrite it."

"With the child's voice."

"With whoever's voice is willing," Oscar said, and glanced down at August, and the glance between them contained entire chapters Ilara would never read. "That's the part Veyren never understood. He thinks the key is a person. It's not. It's a speaker. Anyone who learns to say it."

*Anyone,* Ilara thought, and felt the ground tilt faintly under her certainties. *Anyone at all.*

She should take them in. The thought arrived on schedule, punctual as a debt collector, dressed in all its proper uniforms: the oath, the commission, the thirty years of service ahead of her, the pension, the honor, the gray cloak that Veyren had draped on her shoulders with his soft priest's hands while telling her she was the finest instrument the Guild had ever produced. Instruments didn't choose what they played. That was the whole point of instruments.

She looked at August instead.

Up close, in the honest light, the child was just a child — small, mud-streaked, exhausted, one shoe missing entirely, the little toes curled against the cold cobbles. A child who had crossed a world to reach a door, carrying nothing but a language. A child whose name was written in a charter dated before their birth. A child the Guild had purchased before they existed, the way you purchase a field, the way you purchase a future, the way the powerful purchase everything that hasn't happened yet.

"No," Ilara said aloud.

Oscar blinked. "No?"

"No, I'm not taking you in." She reached up to the clasp at her throat. Her fingers were steady now, steadier than they'd been all week, all year, all six years of living as two people in one body. "Stand there. Both of you. Watch this. I want witnesses."

The commission was heavy vellum, sealed in lead and gray wax, lettered in Veyren's own immaculate hand: *Let it be known that Ilara Vance, Hunter of the Third Chair, is charged with the keeping and conveyance of the asset designated AUGUST, per the will of the Guild of Impossible Things, in perpetuity—*

In perpetuity. As if perpetuity belonged to them. As if anything did.

She tore it in half.

The sound was small — wet vellum doesn't tear cleanly, it resists, it gives with a soft fibrous gasp — but in the silence of the quay it rang like a snapped bowstring. She tore it again, lengthwise, and again, crosswise, and let the pieces fall from her fingers into the black water, where they spread apart, whitening, drifting, and slid one by one under the arch and out toward the marsh and the sea and the places where the Guild's writ ran out.

The lead seal went last. She dropped it from the bridge rail and watched it flash once in the lamplight and vanish, and the ripple it left widened and widened until it touched both banks.

"There," she said. Her voice cracked on it, and she let it crack. "Desertion. Witnessed. Write it in your ledger, scribe — it's the only entry about me I'll ever ask you for."

Oscar was staring at her as if she'd torn the sky. "Ilara — they'll hunt you too. You know what happens to hunters who break commission. They don't imprison us. We *know too much* to imprison."

"I count on it." She smiled, and it hurt, and it was real. "Better a hunted woman than a hollow one. I've been hollow since the day you left my rooms with your ledgers under your arm and your eyes already somewhere else. I'm done being furniture, Oscar. Veyren can have my cloak back. He can have my chair. He cannot have my hands."

August stepped forward then — out from behind Oscar's arm, into the space between the two adults, into the lamplight — and did the last thing Ilara expected. The child took her hand.

Small fingers. Cold. Certain.

"*Vesh-kel-annu,*" August said softly, and though Ilara had never studied the First Tongue, had never wanted to, had feared it the way soldiers fear the songs of the countries they're sent to burn — she understood it anyway, the meaning arriving in her chest ahead of any translation, the way the ledgers understood, the way everything old understood:

*The door stays a door. And you may stand in it whenever you wish.*

Ilara knelt. It wasn't a decision; her knees simply went, the way they go in temples. She brought her face level with the child's and found the eyes looking back at her without fear, without judgment, with the terrible gentle clarity of someone who has seen the beginning of the world and the end of stories and has decided, against all evidence, to be kind anyway.

"When you get to the marsh," she said, low and fast, the hunter reasserting herself one final time because it was the only way she knew how to love — "don't take the ferry road. Veyren will seal it by noon. Take the drover's path north of the drowned church; the mud's knee-deep but the hunters won't think of it for a day. And when you reach the thin place—" she hesitated, then committed, giving away the last secret she owned, "—the cartographer's map is wrong about the eastern vein. It's moved. It moves. Listen for singing. Where the singing is loudest, that's the door."

"How do you know that?" Oscar whispered.

"Because I've stood at three of them," Ilara said, "on patrol, alone, at night, and never reported a single one. Because I've been listening to that singing for six years and telling myself it was wind." She rose. Her knees ached. Everything ached, and it was the good ache, the ache of a cast coming off a healed bone. "Go. Before the watch turns. And Oscar—"

Oscar paused at the threshold of the arch, the child's hand in theirs, the east graying faintly at the rim of the world.

"The th," Ilara said. "In the forged entry. You hesitated on the downstroke. Fifteen years of forging and your conscience still shows up in your penmanship." She smiled, and meant it, and let it break her. "Don't ever fix that. It's the best thing about your hand."

For a heartbeat neither of them moved, and the whole city held its breath around them — the water, the walls, the drowning lamps, the gray light rising like a tide. Then Oscar pressed two fingers to their own lips and then to the air between them, the old farewell, the one that meant *no words, then; words would ruin it* — and turned, and walked with August under the black arch, and the water took their footsteps and gave back nothing, and the gold coins of lamplight broke around them and closed again behind them as if no one had ever passed.

Ilara Vance stood alone at the Canal Gate until the sun came up.

She did not run. Running was for the guilty, and besides, they would find her here; she wanted them to find her here, standing in the doorway, refusing to come in and refusing to leave, exactly as the dream had promised. When the first hunter's whistle shrilled from the direction of the archive — when the bells began, ragged and urgent, calling the Guild awake to its emptied cell and its stolen page — she unbuckled her sword belt and laid it on the bridge rail, neat as a returned library book, and folded her gray cloak over it with the commission's torn scraps still damp in the pocket.

Let them write it down, she thought, as the boots came hammering along the quay. Let them enter it in their impossible books. *Hunter Vance, found at the Canal Gate, commission destroyed, accomplice escaped.* Let Veyren read it in his beautiful voice to his hungry congregation. Let them make her a lesson, a warning, a cautionary verse in the Guild's long psalm of ownership.

Because she had seen the child's face in the lamplight. She had heard the sentence the child spoke, and understood it without learning it, and that understanding had done something to her that no sermon and no oath and no fear could undo. The Veil was a sentence. Sentences could be rewritten. And somewhere out in the marsh, knee-deep in the drover's mud, a scribe with a conscience in his penmanship was walking toward the rewriting with a child who remembered the beginning of the world —

— and Ilara Vance, deserter, traitor, door-keeper, smiled at the rising sun like a woman who had finally, finally paid off a debt.

The hunters rounded the customs house at a dead run and stopped dead at the sight of her: unarmed, unhooded, unhurried, waiting.

"Well," she said, spreading her empty hands. "Took you long enough."

Far to the east, past the wall, past the marsh, past the edge of every map the Guild owned, the thirteenth moon was setting full and fat and pale as a peeled egg — twelve turns remaining — and the singing, very faintly, had begun to change key.
