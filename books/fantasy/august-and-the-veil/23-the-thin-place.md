## 23. The Thin Place

The map had lied about the distance and told the truth about everything else.

Oscar had counted the miles as they walked — three days out of the border town of Hallowmere, past fields gone fallow in the way fields do when farmers stop believing they'll be farmed again, up a road that narrowed to a track, then to a suggestion, then to nothing at all. The cartographer's ink had promised a ridge by the second nightfall, a stand of white birches like candles left burning for someone who never came home, and beyond them, the place where the world wore thin.

All of it was there. But the map had drawn the Veil as a line, and lines are what people draw when they cannot bear to draw the truth.

The truth was that the Veil had no edge you could see. It had an edge you could *feel* — a change in the air like stepping from a warm room into water, a pressure behind the eyes, a hush that was not silence but the held breath of something enormous waiting to exhale.

Oscar stopped at the treeline and set down the pack. Their shoulders ached from it; three days of bread ends, cheese gone waxy, the keystone page wrapped in oilcloth against their ribs where it lay like a second heart. August stood beside them, small and mud-brown and utterly still, staring at the birches as if counting them.

"How many?" Oscar asked.

"Forty," August said. Then, after a moment: "No. Forty-one." The child's voice had changed since Hallowmere. It had been changing all along, shedding the flatness of fear the way a snake sheds skin, and underneath it was something older, something that made the syllables land strangely, like stones dropped into very deep water. "One is lying."

"One tree?"

"One tree is lying." August said it without drama, the way another child might report that one of the sheep had gotten into the turnips. "It wants us to think there are forty so we feel safe. Things near the Veil learn to lie. It's the first thing they learn."

Oscar looked at the forty-first birch. It stood slightly apart from the others, its bark peeling in long curls, and now that August had said it, Oscar could see what they had missed: the tree cast its shadow the wrong way. The sun sat low in the west, and every other shadow stretched east across the frost-silvered grass. This one stretched west, toward the light, patient as a finger pointing home.

"We don't touch it," Oscar said.

"We were never going to touch it." August almost smiled. Almost. "You're learning."

They walked around the lying tree at a respectful distance, the way one walks around a sleeping dog of uncertain temper, and the ground began to rise beneath them, gentle at first and then not gentle at all, until they were climbing through heather the color of old bruises and the wind came off the high moor carrying a smell Oscar had no name for. Not rain. Not stone. Something like the smell of a room just before a storm arrives, or just after a stranger has left it — the sense that the air had recently been occupied.

And then they crested the ridge, and Oscar forgot how to breathe.

* * *

August had seen the edge before. That was the terrible thing about memory; it never warned you which parts would come back whole.

From the ridge, the land fell away into a shallow valley, and the valley was full of light — not sunlight, though the sun was setting behind them, but a pale silver luminescence rising out of the ground itself, out of the grass, out of the cracks between the stones, as if the earth below this place were made of moonlight packed like snow. It pooled in the hollows. It clung to the heather. And through the middle of it ran the Veil.

Not a wall. Not a curtain. August had tried, in the long nights of lessons after curfew, to explain it to Oscar, and Oscar had tried to write it down, and the ink had always refused, rearranging itself into shapes that meant *threshold*, that meant *skin*, that meant *the pause between two notes of music*. Now, seeing it with their own eyes, Oscar understood why the language of ledgers had failed. The Veil was a standing shimmer, taller than any cathedral spire, wider than the eye could follow as it curved away north and south along the contour of the world, and it moved. Slowly. Like heat-haze over summer roads. Like the surface of a pond into which a stone had been dropped a thousand years ago and the ripples had never quite finished.

It did not separate two places. That was the horror and the beauty of it. It separated two *weathers*. On this side, autumn dusk, cold wind, the smell of coming frost. Through the Veil, visible as things seen through deep water, the same hills continued — but lit by a sun that hung in a sky the color of green glass, and grass grew there in colors that had no names in any ledger Oscar had ever kept, and shapes moved among the grasses, distant and slow, too far to resolve into anything but intention.

"They're still there," Oscar whispered. They hadn't meant to speak aloud. "After all this time. They're still—"

"Singing," August said.

And then Oscar heard it.

At first it seemed like wind. The moor wind had been singing all day, worrying the heather, whistling through the birches behind them. But this was different. This came *through* the Veil, attenuated, warped, the way a voice sounds heard through a wall — and it was voices, many voices, layered voices, men's and women's and voices that belonged to neither category and some that belonged to no category Oscar could imagine, rising and falling in a pattern that was almost melody and almost grief and almost, terribly, a lullaby.

Oscar had spent eleven years transcribing impossible things. They had written down confessions from mirrors, contracts with rivers, the last words of a man who had been dead for two hundred years and had come back specifically to dispute an invoice. Nothing had prepared them for the sound of a whole exiled world singing to the door that kept it shut.

"What are they singing?" Their own voice sounded small and cracked. "Do you know it?"

August didn't answer right away. The child had gone very pale under the dirt, and their hands — small hands, chapped from three days of walking, hands Oscar had bandaged on the second night when the scree had torn them open — were clenched at their sides so tightly the knuckles had gone white.

"I know it," August said finally. "I sang it before I could talk. Everyone does. It's the first thing they teach you on the other side, before your own name." A breath. "It's a song about remembering. It says: *remember us correctly.*"

The wind took the words and carried them toward the shimmering wall, and Oscar would have sworn — later, in the telling, they swore it absolutely — that the singing changed. Rose. Leaned toward them the way flowers lean toward light.

"It knows you're here," Oscar said.

"It knows *both* of us are here." August's voice had gone strange. Flat again, the way it had been that first night at the guild door, and Oscar knew enough now to know what that flatness meant. It meant the child was afraid. "That's the problem, Oscar. That's the whole problem."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the ridge, because the map's final instruction had been explicit and the cartographer had died writing it: *Approach only at full dark. The Veil sleeps shallow at night. Do not approach at noon, do not approach at dawn, and above all do not approach when the thirteenth moon rises, for then it does not sleep at all.*

Three nights remained before the thirteenth moon. Three nights, and then Veyren's engine would drag August to the vault floor, and the orrery would turn, and the Veil would be opened whether anyone on either side of it wanted it opened or not.

So they waited for dark, sharing the last of the bread, and Oscar watched August watch the Veil, and the singing went on and on, patient as tide.

"You should sleep," Oscar said.

"I'm afraid to sleep here." August pulled their knees up to their chest. "My dreams leak. Here, I think they'd leak *out*."

"Then stay awake with me. We'll take turns."

For a while the child said nothing. The silver light from the valley climbed the slope and laid itself across August's face, and in that light the child looked older than ten years, older than a hundred, a face that was a doorway with something vast standing just behind it.

"Oscar. Can I tell you something I haven't told you?"

"You can tell me anything."

"That's what the dream-person said too." August's eyes stayed fixed on the shimmer. "In the dream. The one with your face. You said *you can tell me anything*, exactly like that, and then—" The child stopped. Started again, smaller. "And then in the dream, you wrote my name in a book, and the book closed, and I couldn't remember myself anymore. I woke up on this side of the Veil with mud in my mouth and no idea how I got there, and the only word I still knew was my own name, and even that felt borrowed."

Oscar's chest tightened. They thought of the founding charter in the vault, the name *August* inscribed in ink older than the child's bones, dated decades before the birth. They thought of Veyren's soft voice saying *the Guild does not find prophecies, scribe. We make our arrangements early.*

"August," they said carefully, "I have never written your name in a book. I've written it in *my* book, the transcription ledger, and I burned those pages myself, and I would burn every page in the world before I let a book close on you."

"But you *would* write it. If they ordered you. If Master Veyren put his seal in front of you and said *scribe, record*—" August turned, and their eyes in the silver light were the color of the sky beyond the Veil, green glass over deep water. "Wouldn't you? You always obey. That's what debt is. That's what you told me debt is. It's obedience with a number attached."

The words landed like a slap, precisely because they were true. Oscar looked at their own hands — ink-stained, calloused in the shape of a pen, hands that had forged and copied and balanced accounts for eleven years, hands that had stolen a keystone page four nights ago and forged its twin so perfectly that Sefa herself had grunted approval — and could not find an honest answer anywhere in them.

"Yes," Oscar said at last. Because August deserved truth more than comfort, and because they were sitting at the edge of a world's exile and lies felt obscene here. "If you'd asked me a month ago, yes. I would have obeyed. I obeyed for eleven years. I obeyed the day I first saw you, when Veyren said *transcribe everything*, and I did, and my pen listened to your language better than I did." They swallowed. "But that was before I learned that the Guild built the moon. Before the charter. Before I understood what they intend for you on the thirteenth night. I'm not that person anymore, August. I'm not sure I ever was. I just didn't have anywhere to put the not-obeying until you gave me somewhere."

August studied them for a long time. Somewhere in the valley below, the singing swelled and softened, swelled and softened, a heart the size of a world beating against a wall.

"The dream-you said something else," the child said quietly. "At the end. Right before the book closed. They said: *I'm sorry. It's the only way to keep you safe.*" A pause. "Was that you? Is some part of you already planning to—"

"No." It came out harder than Oscar intended, hard enough that August flinched, and Oscar gentled it: "No. Whatever that was — a memory, a prophecy, the Veil showing you fears the way the lying tree shows you shadows — it wasn't me. I am not going to close any book on you. I am going to open one. There's a difference, and I will spend whatever it costs to show you the difference."

The child held their gaze a moment longer. Then, slowly, the tension went out of those small shoulders, and August leaned sideways until their head rested against Oscar's arm, trusting and heavy, the way only exhausted children lean.

"Tell me a story," August murmured. "A boring one. About numbers. Your voice does the thing where the numbers make the fear get quiet."

So Oscar told them a story about balancing the quarterly accounts of the Guild of Impossible Things — about the year the Guild's mirror-division claimed a loss of seventeen reflections and Oscar had tracked each one down like a detective, finding six in a pawnshop, four in a widow's locket, and seven in a well that had developed opinions. August laughed twice, small exhausted laughs, and somewhere in the middle of the seventh reflection the child's breathing slowed and deepened, and they slept after all, dreams and all, because sometimes the body decides fear can wait.

Oscar sat awake beside them, watching the Veil breathe its slow silver breathing, and listened to the singing, and tried to hear, underneath the melody, what the song was actually asking.

*Remember us correctly.*

And underneath that, fainter, woven through like a thread of darker silk:

*One of you promised.*

* * *

Oscar slept in turns, as promised, and woke in the black hour before dawn to find August gone from the bedroll.

Panic arrived before thought — panic, and the image of the vault, the orrery, Veyren's hands — and Oscar was up and moving before their boots were fully on, stumbling downslope through the heather, following the silver glow, calling the child's name in a whisper that wanted to be a shout.

He found August — *they* found August, Oscar corrected themselves, they had decided on *they* for themselves years ago and the habit of precision mattered now more than ever — standing twenty paces from the Veil.

Twenty paces. Close enough that the shimmer's light played over the child like water-light, close enough that the singing needed no wind to carry it, close enough that Oscar could see individual threads within the great moving curtain, filaments of pale fire braiding and unbraiding, and behind them, nearer than yesterday, the shapes in the other-world grasses had stopped moving. All of them. Every distant silhouette stood facing the Veil, facing August, still as herons.

"August." Oscar kept their voice low, the way you speak near a deer, near a cliff-edge, near anything that might startle and fall. "Come back from there."

"I'm not doing anything." The child didn't turn. "I just wanted to hear it properly. Without the wind arguing."

"What is it saying?"

August was quiet for so long that Oscar crossed half the distance without deciding to, boots silent on the frost-stiffened grass, and stopped at the child's shoulder. Up close, the Veil was worse and better than from the ridge — worse because it was beautiful, better because beauty, Oscar realized, had been the thing they feared. No one runs from a wall. People run from doors.

"It's saying my name," August whispered. "All of it. Not the short name. The whole one, the one I had before. The one I lost." Their voice fractured. "Oscar, they remember me. I thought — I thought maybe they'd forgotten, maybe I was sent away because I was broken or wrong or — but they're singing my name. They've been singing it every night since I was born. Maybe every night since the charter was written. Waiting for me to come home."

Something in Oscar's chest broke and reformed around the ache. "Then we'll get you home," they said. "Somehow. Some way that doesn't cost your life to buy it. That's the whole point of what we're doing—"

"No." August's hand found Oscar's sleeve and gripped it, and the grip was trembling. "You don't understand. Listen. *Listen.*"

And Oscar listened, really listened, letting the sound bypass the scholar and the scribe and the debtor and all the armored clerks of their soul, and heard what lived inside the lullaby.

Grief. Grief so old it had become geology. And under the grief, hope — hope so fierce it had curdled into something frightening, the way hunger becomes frightening, the way a besieged city's joy at seeing sails on the horizon becomes frightening when you realize the sails might be rescue or might be raiders. The singers were not singing *welcome home, little key*. They were singing *open, open, open*, and beneath *open* ran a counter-melody of *please*, and beneath *please* ran something that had no translation into any tongue Oscar owned:

*We have waited so long. We will not wait politely forever.*

"They're not monsters," August said. Tears cut clean tracks through the dust on the child's face. "I knew that. I remembered that. But they're not *safe* either. They're starving, Oscar. Not for food. For *through*. Eleven hundred years on the wrong side of a door, and the door is me, and they love me, and that's the most frightening thing I've ever known, because things that love you and starve at the same time — they don't mean to break you. They just do. The way the lying tree doesn't mean to be a lie. It's just what survival taught it."

Oscar crouched down to the child's level, there at the edge of the world, with the exiled singing washing over them both like a tide that had been rising for eleven centuries.

"August," they said. "Look at me."

Green-glass eyes came around, wet and ancient and ten years old.

"When we go through — if we go through, if there's any way to do this that isn't Veyren's way — what happens to you? What does the door do to the key?"

August's lower lip trembled. The child looked back at the Veil, at the still-waiting shapes in the other grass, and when they spoke, their voice was the smallest Oscar had ever heard it, smaller than the first night at the guild door, smaller than the cellar, smaller than all the dark places they had hidden together.

"I don't know," August said. "That's why I'm afraid. Everyone over there remembers me perfectly. And I can't remember myself at all. What if opening the door means becoming the door?" A shudder ran through the small body. "What if I stand in the thin place and the thin place stands in me, and after that there's no August, there's just — an opening, forever, with everyone walking through me and calling it homecoming?"

The singing rose. The filaments of the Veil brightened, stirred, reached — Oscar could have sworn they reached — and in the grey pre-dawn the whole valley held its breath, and the shapes beyond the shimmer leaned closer, closer, close enough now that Oscar could see faces.

Faces that were not monstrous. Faces that were tired, and hopeful, and hungry, and *familiar* —

One of them raised a hand. Not in menace. In greeting. In the particular desperate greeting of someone who has been waving at a ship for a very long time.

And it spoke — the sound passing through the Veil like light through water, arriving bent but whole, in a voice like a bell rung underwater, in words that Oscar's months of secret lessons somehow assembled into meaning even as terror tried to scatter them:

"*Little door. Little key. Come and see who waits. Come and see what we became while you were gone.*"

Beside Oscar, August went rigid.

"Oscar," the child breathed. "That one. The one speaking. I know that one." The tears came faster now, and the child's hand crushed Oscar's sleeve. "That's the voice from my dream. That's the one who told me to run. That's the one who said —"

The figure stepped forward, right up to the inner surface of the Veil, close enough that its features resolved at last through the shimmer — and August made a sound Oscar had never heard a human child make, a sound from before language, from before the Veil, from the first morning of the world.

"— said they were wearing your face."
