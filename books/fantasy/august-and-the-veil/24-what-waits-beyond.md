## 24. What Waits Beyond

The rift did not look like a door.

August had expected a door, because doors were what people made when they wanted to go somewhere — wood or iron or stone, with hinges and handles and the honest promise of an edge you could touch. The Veil was not that. The Veil was a place where the world forgot to finish itself, where the air went thin as old paper and the light came through wrong, bent, like sunlight through water in a cup held by a trembling hand.

They stood at its edge now, Oscar beside them, close enough that August could hear the scribe breathing — quick, shallow breaths, the breath of someone trying very hard to be brave for someone smaller.

*You don't have to come closer,* Oscar had said, back at the canal gate, back before Ilara tore her commission in half and the city fell away behind them like a coat shrugged off. *We can just look. Looking is enough.*

But looking was never enough. That was the whole trouble with August, the thing the Guild had never understood even while writing their name into a charter older than their bones. Looking was how August was made. Looking was what they were *for.*

So August stepped forward, and the Veil leaned toward them like a curious animal, and it opened.

Not all at once. It opened the way frost opens on a window when you breathe on it — a bloom of clear space spreading from the center outward, edges feathered and trembling, and through that clearing August saw the other side.

They had been told about monsters.

Everyone was told about monsters. The priests in the market squares drew them on festival banners: shapes with too many joints and mouths where eyes should be, hunger given legs, the war-hungry realm beyond the Veil waiting with its teeth out for the taste of mortal kingdoms. August had grown up on those banners the way other children grew up on lullabies. They had dreamed them. They had woken screaming from them more than once, though they could never say why the monsters felt less like nightmares than like *misremembered things*, like songs sung off-key until the melody died.

What waited beyond the rift was not singing off-key.

It was singing true.

---

The sound reached August first, before any shape resolved. It came through the thin place the way warmth comes through a wall from a hearth in another room — muffled, but unmistakably alive. A low, layered sound, many voices braided into one, rising and falling in a rhythm that August's body knew before their mind caught up. Their feet shifted. Their hands rose slightly, palms out, the way they did when they spoke the First Tongue without meaning to.

Because that was what the singing was. The First Tongue, sung instead of spoken, worn smooth by centuries of use the way river stones are worn smooth by water. Not the sharp, bright syllables August carried in their blood — those had edges still, edges that could cut the world open. These voices had rounded theirs down. Or maybe the world over there had done it for them, grinding everything soft against everything else, until all that remained was this: a sound like grief and patience having a conversation.

"August." Oscar's hand hovered near their shoulder, not quite touching. Scribes learned early not to touch things that might be documents. "Do you see—"

"I see," August said, and their voice came out strange to them, doubled almost, as if the First Tongue were already leaning into their mouth.

Beyond the rift, the land began to resolve.

It was not fire. That was the first thing. All the banners showed fire, rivers of it, cities of it, the war-hungry realm burning forever because that was easier to paint than truth. But the land beyond the Veil was gray and gentle and *tired*. Hills rolled away under a sky the color of pewter, and the hills were terraced — farmed, August realized, with a jolt that went all the way down to their heels. Someone had built walls of stacked stone along those slopes, low walls, the kind you build to keep soil from washing away, which is the work of people who intend to stay. There were orchards. Bare-branched, winter-sleeping, but pruned with care, each cut deliberate, each tree shaped by years of patient hands.

And there were houses. Small ones, stone and timber, smoke rising from a few chimneys in thin gray threads. A village. A poor, huddled, stubborn village pressed up against the far side of the Veil like children pressing their ears to a door.

People moved among the houses.

August's breath stopped.

They were not monsters. They were *people* — tall, some of them, taller than any folk August had seen in the market towns, and narrow, with skin in shades August had no names for: deep umber shot through with faint silver, pale gray gone almost blue at the knuckles, one old woman whose skin held a shimmer like mother-of-pearl when she turned to lift a basket. Their eyes were large. Their hands were careful. One of them, a young man with his sleeves rolled up, was arguing gently with a child about something, gesturing at a row of winter squash, and the child was stomping a foot, and the man was laughing — laughing, in the war-hungry realm, in the land of teeth and fire, laughing at a stubborn child who didn't want to carry squash.

"They're farming," August whispered. "Oscar. They're *farming.*"

Oscar said nothing. When August glanced up, the scribe's face had gone the color of the parchment they worked on, and their eyes were wet, and their lips were moving silently — transcribing, August realized. Even now. Even here. Some part of Oscar Reyes would be taking notes until the day the ledgers closed over them forever.

Then the nearest figure looked up.

She had been hanging laundry — actual laundry, shirts and shifts on a line strung between two posts, white cloth snapping in a wind August couldn't feel — and her head turned toward the rift with the slow, wary precision of a deer that has heard a stick break. Her eyes found August through the feathered edge of the opening.

Her eyes found August, and she *recognized* them.

August knew it the way they knew the First Tongue: not learned, but remembered. The woman's whole body changed. The basket slipped from her hip and she didn't catch it; she was already moving, dropping the clothespins, running — not away, *toward*, calling out behind her in that rounded, river-smoothed speech, and others turned, and then they were all turning, all of them, every face in the little gray village swinging toward the tear in the world like flowers finding a sun that hadn't risen in a very long time.

"August." Oscar's hand landed on their shoulder at last, gripping hard. "Step back."

"They're coming here."

"That's why we step *back*—"

But August didn't step back. August stepped forward, right up to the trembling edge, close enough that the feathered frost of the Veil brushed their cheeks like cold silk, and the woman on the other side stopped three paces from her side of the opening and fell to her knees.

That was the moment the myth broke.

Monsters did not kneel. Monsters did not press both hands flat against the ground and bow their heads and speak — and she was speaking now, voice carrying through the thin place clear as a bell rung underwater, words in the First Tongue so worn and softened that August had to lean into them, translate them through the ache of recognition:

*It's you. Oh, it's you, the small one, the key-child, the one the stars promised. We felt you being born. We felt you on the other side like a lamp lit in a dark house, and we have kept watch every night since, every single night, waiting to see if you would ever come to the window.*

More of them arrived. They came out of the houses and down from the terraces and up from wherever the dead were mourned, dozens of them, scores, and they gathered behind the kneeling woman in a loose crescent, and none of them crossed toward the rift. None of them reached through. They knelt, or stood with heads bowed, or simply stared with expressions August had spent eight years learning to read on human faces and needed no translation at all now:

Hope. Terrible, exhausted, half-ashamed hope. The hope of people who have hoped so long that hope has become a wound.

"Why are they kneeling?" August asked. Their own voice cracked in the middle. "Oscar, why are they kneeling? I'm a child. I'm nobody. I'm—" *a purchased prophecy, a name in a charter dated before my birth, a key the Guild forged a moon to turn* — but they couldn't say any of it out loud, not here, not in front of these faces.

"You know why," Oscar said quietly.

And August did know. Knew it in the marrow-place where the First Tongue lived. The kneeling woman lifted her head, and tears ran freely down her silver-shot face, and she spoke again, and this time she spoke slowly, deliberately, rounding each word with such care that it was clearly a message meant to cross the divide intact:

*Little key. Little door-child. We have been called monsters so long that our grandchildren draw themselves with teeth. Listen — listen, please, before anyone teaches you what we are. We were not exiled for making war. We were exiled for refusing one. Do you understand? There was a council, at the end, when the worlds were young enough to still be argued over. Our speakers stood in that council and said: let there be a border, let there be a Veil, let the two peoples live apart if apart is what keeps the peace. And the ones who wanted conquest — on both sides, little one, on *both* sides — they wrote the histories. They wrote us as the hunger beyond the wall, because a people who choose peace make a very convenient monster. Fear keeps borders strong better than stone ever did.*

She bowed her head again, forehead nearly touching the gray earth.

*We do not ask to come through. Hear us. We do not ask for gates or bridges or armies. We ask only this: when your world tells stories about us, tell them true. Remember us right. That is all. That has always been all. A people can survive exile. What kills us slowly, year by year, generation by generation, is being remembered wrong.*

---

The wind moved through the rift, or something like wind — a current of air that smelled of woodsmoke and turned earth and rain on stone, the smell of ordinary life, and it carried the singing with it, the great braided song rising again from the village behind the kneelers. August stood at the threshold with tears freezing on their lashes and understood, finally, what they had been hearing in their dreams all their life.

It wasn't a war song. It had never been a war song.

It was a lullaby. Hundreds of years old, sung across a Veil to no one, sung to children who might someday exist on the other side, sung to a key-child not yet born — *sleep, little border, sleep, little door, the ones beyond love you, whatever they've told you* — and August had heard it in the womb and in dreams and in the spaces between the Guild's bells, and had never once known what it was saying until now.

"They're begging," August said. The words came out small. "They're begging me not to remember them wrong."

"Yes," Oscar said. The scribe's voice was rough as unsanded wood. "And do you know what the Guild's charter calls them? I read it to you. *The hungry host. The unmaking tide. The enemy sealed in mercy.*" A bitter breath. "Mercy. As if the wall were a kindness done *to* them."

"But the engine—"

"The engine doesn't open a door onto an army, August. Look at them. There is no army. There's a *village*. There are squash gardens and laundry lines and a man laughing at a stubborn child." Oscar's hand tightened on August's shoulder. "Veyren needs them to be monsters. A treaty doesn't need a key. A rescue doesn't need a sacrifice. Only a war does. He has to open that door believing something terrible waits behind it, or everything he's built — the moon, the engine, you — becomes murder instead of salvation."

Across the rift, the kneeling woman raised her head once more. Behind her, the crescent of the First Speakers stirred, and one by one they began to do something that undid August completely.

They showed their hands.

Every one of them, kneeling or standing, turned their palms outward toward the rift — empty, open, held high the way you hold them when you want to be searched, when you want to prove you carry nothing. An entire village of the feared and monstrous, standing in the gray light with empty hands raised toward a child, asking nothing, offering everything, begging only to be remembered right.

The old woman with the pearl-shimmer skin began, softly, to sing the lullaby alone. And one by one the others joined, until the whole thin place rang with it, and the Veil itself seemed to lean in and listen, its feathered edges going still as a held breath.

August's hands rose without their permission. Palms out. Empty. Matching them across the divide, mirror to mirror, and the moment their hands opened, the rift *sang back* — a note from the Veil itself, deep and glad, like a string plucked in the floor of the world.

And in that ringing moment, August saw what they had not seen before, what the singing had hidden: at the edge of the village, half-buried in the hillside, stood a structure of pale stone. Old. Older than the houses. Its doorway was tall and narrow and utterly dark, and carved above the lintel, worn nearly smooth, was a symbol August knew better than their own name — the same mark that spiraled through every page of the Guild's founding charter, the same mark branded into the keystone page Oscar had stolen from the vault.

The First Speakers followed August's gaze. The singing faltered. And the kneeling woman's face changed — hope draining into something older and sadder, something like apology — as she spoke the last words August would carry out of the thin place that day:

*Yes, little key. You see it truly. Your Guild did not build their engine from nothing. They built it from the plans of the door we sealed ourselves — the door we swore never to open. They have been reading our oldest shame for three hundred years, and they think it is a weapon. Come back tomorrow. Come back, and we will show you what the door was really for — before the thirteenth moon rises, and men who remember us wrong try to walk through it.*

Behind August, far back along the ridge, a bell began to ring.

Not a temple bell. Not a Guild bell. A hunting horn — long, low, and getting longer, getting lower, answered a moment later by another from the east, and another from the south, closing like fingers around a fist.

Oscar pulled August back from the rift. The feathered frost began to close.

"They found the cartographer's map missing," the scribe breathed. "August — they're in the hills already. We have to move *now*."

Through the shrinking gap, the kneeling woman pressed her empty hands together and held them to her heart, and mouthed words the closing Veil almost stole: *Tomorrow. Remember us right. Tomorrow.*

The rift sealed with a sound like a book shutting.

And August, running into the darkening hills with Oscar's hand locked around theirs, carried the lullaby with them — and beneath it, growing louder with every stride, the memory of a pale stone door, and the terrible new question it opened:

If the First Speakers had sealed that door themselves, swearing never to open it —

what, exactly, had they been so afraid of letting out?
