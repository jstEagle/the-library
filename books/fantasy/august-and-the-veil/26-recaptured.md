## 26. Recaptured

The border town was called Latch, and it had earned its name honestly. Every road into it narrowed, every gate swung inward, and every window in the low stone houses had been built with an eye toward watching the street. Oscar had known this, in the abstract, the way a scribe knows the weight of a page before it is written. Knowing it in the body was another thing entirely.

They came down off the moor road at dusk, August's small hand tucked in Oscar's, the Veil's edge a pale bruise of light on the horizon behind them — too close, and not close enough. They had meant to pass through Latch before the gates closed for the night, buy bread, buy silence, be gone by the hour when honest towns light their lamps and dishonest ones light their lanterns for other purposes.

Oscar had counted the days since the flight from the archive. Nine. Nine days of hedgerows and hollows, of August sleeping against their shoulder, of the First Tongue murmured half-dreaming into the dark — words that made the frost bloom in spirals on the ground, words that made the horses of passing farmers turn their heads as though hearing their names called from a great distance.

Nine days of freedom, and Oscar had begun, dangerously, to believe in it.

"You're walking faster," August said. "Your feet are arguing."

"My feet are always arguing. It's how I think."

"What are they saying now?"

*Turn back. Take the moor. The town is a mouth.* But the bread was gone, and August's boots had split at the seams, and the child's lips had gone pale at the edges in a way Oscar did not like. Hunger made liars of careful people. Hunger had made a forger of Oscar Reyes once already.

"They're saying," Oscar said, "that we eat tonight, and then we never come near a town again until the moon turns."

August looked up at them with that grave, unchildlike attention that had stopped unnerving Oscar somewhere around the fourth day and had become, instead, something like the sound of a familiar bell. "The moon turns anyway," August said. "That's the trouble with moons."

They came through the south gate of Latch as the lamps were being lit, and the lamplighter nodded to them, and a woman selling chestnuts called out the price without looking up, and everything was so ordinary that Oscar's shoulders finally came down from around their ears.

That was the mistake. Oscar would replay it for years, afterward — the exact moment the shoulders dropped, the exact breath in which vigilance was traded for the smell of roasting chestnuts and woodsmoke and the ordinary miracle of a town being a town.

They were three stalls from the baker's when the chestnut woman's voice stopped mid-word.

Not because of anything Oscar did. Because of the bell.

It was not a loud bell. It was a small, sweet-toned thing hanging above the south gate, the kind of bell that announced a merchant's cart or a returning son. But it rang three times in a rhythm Oscar's body understood before their mind did — three, pause, three — and down the length of the street, doors that had been opening for the evening began, one by one, to close.

"Oscar." August's hand went tight. "The lamps. Look at the lamps."

The lamplighter had stopped. He stood beneath his half-lit lamp with his pole still raised, and he was looking at them — not with curiosity, not with the idle appraisal townsfolk give strangers, but with the fixed, unhappy attention of a man doing a thing he has been paid to do and does not want to do.

"Keep walking," Oscar murmured. "Toward the north gate. Don't run. Running is a confession."

They walked. The chestnut woman drew her brazier back from the edge of her stall. A dog began to bark somewhere and was silenced. The street narrowed ahead of them the way Latch's streets were built to narrow, funneling toward the north gate like a throat, and Oscar's feet were arguing again, loudly now, and losing.

They were fifty paces from the gate when it opened — not outward, as gates open for travelers, but inward, and the men who came through it did not come like travelers. They came like water finding a channel: unhurried, total, filling the street from wall to wall.

Guild hunters. Oscar knew them at once, not by the grey travel-cloaks or the short staves they carried, but by the way they moved — the way Sefa moved, the way everyone who had spent years in the archive's service moved, with the economy of people accustomed to handling things that must not be damaged.

*Things that must not be damaged.*

"August," Oscar said, and knelt, and took the child's face in both hands. "Listen to me. The lesson from last night. The first lie."

August's eyes were enormous. "My name is Pond. I live with my aunt in the marsh country. I've never seen a book."

"Good. Good. And if they ask who I am—"

"Nobody. A nobody who found me on the road." The child's voice only shook a little. "Oscar. Your feet. What are they saying?"

Oscar's feet, at that moment, were saying nothing at all, because the hunters had reached them, and the street had gone quiet in that particular way streets go quiet when everyone watching is pretending not to watch.

The hunter in front — a broad, grey-bearded man with a guild signet burned into the leather of his bracer — looked down at them with something that was almost courtesy.

"Evening," he said. "You'll come with us."

"My nephew and I are traveling to his aunt in the marsh country," Oscar said, and heard their own voice do the thing Veyren had once praised it for: go flat, go dull, go forgettable. "We've no business with the Guild."

"No," the hunter agreed. "You haven't. He does." He did not look at August as he said it. He looked at the space just above the child's head, where the air, Oscar realized with a lurch, was *moving* — faintly, subtly, the way air moves above a candle. The Veil's proximity had marked August like a brand. All the hedgerows in the world could not have hidden that shimmer from people trained to see it.

"His name is Pond," Oscar said.

"Is it." The hunter crouched, unhurried, and held out his hand to August the way one offers a hand to a horse. "Pond, then. You'll come with us, Pond. Your aunt will be found. Everyone's aunt is always found."

And August — who had learned to lie, who had learned it in a dark archive after curfew with Oscar's hand on their shoulder — August looked at the hunter and said, in a small, marsh-country voice, "I want my aunt."

It was perfect. It was flawless. And it did not matter at all, because the shimmer above the child's head brightened as they spoke, as though the First Tongue could not help itself, as though every word the child said in any language carried the older language underneath it like a river under ice.

The hunter's expression did not change, but his hand closed gently, terribly, on August's shoulder.

"Oscar!" August's marsh-country voice cracked and became only August, only a child. "Oscar—"

"I'm here." Oscar was on their feet, was reaching, was saying it in the voice they had used for nine days on the road, the voice that had no flatness in it at all. "I'm here, I'm—"

The staves came down across their arms. Not brutally — the Guild did not do brutal where witnesses could see — but with the impersonal efficiency of men moving furniture. Oscar felt their knees hit cobblestone, felt the cold of the street through their trousers, saw the world tilt and fill with boots and the undersides of lamps and, for one last sliver of a second, August's face turned back toward them over the hunter's shoulder — not crying, not screaming, but looking at Oscar with that grave attention, and mouthing something.

Oscar read the child's lips the way they had learned to read the ink that listened.

*Count the moons.*

Then the north gate swallowed them both, in opposite directions, and the street of Latch went back to being an ordinary street, and the chestnut woman, Oscar would learn later, told everyone she had seen nothing at all.

* * *

They put Oscar in the back of a wagon, because a person being returned to the Guild under writ of recovery was property, and property traveled in wagons, and property was chained — but lightly, at the wrists, with a padlock that Oscar examined with a professional eye even as despair sat on their chest like a stone.

A good lock. Not the best. The Guild bought its locks in lots of fifty from the same Ironmongers' Cooperative that supplied half the province, and Oscar had copied the key pattern twice in their indenture, once as an exercise and once because Veyren had wanted a duplicate made and could not be seen making it.

The knowledge sat in Oscar's mind like a coin at the bottom of a well. It did not help. It was simply there, the way all useless knowledge was there, because Oscar Reyes had never in their life been able to stop learning things.

The wagon rolled south through the night. The hunters rode alongside, six of them, and they did not speak to Oscar, and Oscar did not speak to them. Somewhere ahead of them, in another wagon or on another road entirely, August was being carried the same direction, and Oscar held onto that — the same direction, the same destination, the same master — the way a drowning sailor holds onto driftwood that is also, admittedly, pulling them under.

Veyren had them back. That was the arithmetic of it, and Oscar had always been good at arithmetic. Nine days of freedom, purchased at the cost of the finest forgery of their life, and the sum total was: Veyren had them back, and had August back, and the thirteenth moon was — Oscar counted, hating the fluency of it — eleven nights away.

Eleven nights to do what? They had no keystone page. It had been taken from them at the border town, along with the cartographer's map, along with everything, and even if they had kept it, what then? Chains and locks and eleven nights and a vault full of hunters, and Ilara somewhere in that number — Ilara, who had torn her commission in half at the canal gate and let them go, and who would now be standing in Veyren's study explaining that torn commission, or not explaining it, and what did one do with a hunter who had chosen love over duty except take the choice away from her by catching the fugitives herself?

Oscar lay against the wagon's slats and watched the stars wheel and thought, with a clarity that felt like illness: *I taught him to lie, and it saved him for nothing. I taught myself to hope, and it cost us both.*

The despair was not dramatic. That was the worst of it. It did not howl. It settled in like a debt coming due, familiar, patient, almost comfortable — the old arithmetic of the indenture, the ledger's cold logic: *you were always going to be brought back. Everything you are is an entry in someone else's book. You only ever borrowed the idea that you were a person.*

The wagon jolted through a ford. Cold water sound. The hunters muttered. Somewhere an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

And it was there, in the flat dark of the wagon bed, that Oscar did the thing that had kept them alive through seven years of Veyren's service, the thing that was the whole of who they were beneath the weariness and the fear: they began, without deciding to, to take inventory.

*Lock: Ironmongers' Cooperative, pattern C, pin-tumbler, five pins. I can feel the pins through the shackle if I work it. Chain: forty links, welded, but the anchor ring on the wagon bed is riveted, and rivets can be sheared by anyone with a lever and time, and I have eleven nights, which is either nothing or everything. Hunters: six. Names unknown, except—*

Except.

Oscar went still.

The hunter riding closest to the wagon, the one who had not once looked at them in four hours, whose horse kept the pace of the wagon with the unvarying precision of long practice — that hunter had, in the first hour, when the others were checking their tack and the road was empty, done a small thing. A tiny thing. She had reached back and adjusted the wagon's tail-flap where it gaped, and in doing so had pulled it closed over Oscar's chained hands, out of the rain that had begun to fall.

A hunter did not do that. A hunter kept the property wet if the property was supposed to be uncomfortable, and dry if the property was supposed to be comfortable, but a hunter did not do it *unasked, unobserved, and unrecorded* — and above all, a hunter did not do it with the particular economy of motion, the particular angle of the wrist, that Oscar had watched across a quartermaster's counter for seven years.

Oscar sat up slowly, chains whispering, and looked at the rider's back. Grey cloak. Guild signet. The set of the shoulders — broad, unhurried, carrying their weight the way Sefa carried hers, like a woman who had decided long ago exactly how much of herself she was willing to spend on any given day and had never once exceeded the budget.

"Sefa," Oscar said. Just the name. Barely a breath.

The rider did not turn. Did not flinch. The horse kept its pace.

But after a moment — after the wagon had jolted through the ruts of a crossroad and the rain had thickened and the other riders had drawn their hoods down — the rider's voice came back over her shoulder, low and dry and exactly, exactly the voice of the woman who had once sold Oscar the truth about the Veil for three forged entries and had never once, in seven years, given anything away for free:

"You're leaking, scribe. Your left shackle. Mind you don't leak all over the wagon bed — the Guild charges for damaged property, and you've got nothing left to charge."

Oscar's heart slammed once, hard, against the stone of despair, and the stone — not cracking, not yet, but shifting — let a little light through the seam.

"Sefa." Their voice shook. They let it. "You're one of them. You're—"

"I'm a quartermaster," Sefa said. "I go where the inventory goes." A pause. The rain drummed. "You stole from the Guild, scribe. Finest forgery of your life, I hear. Veyren's had the whole archive turned inside out looking for the original, and do you know what he found?"

Oscar said nothing.

"He found," Sefa continued, in the tone of a woman reading from a ledger she had memorized, "that the keystone page he's been guarding for two hundred years has been in his own vault the entire time, exactly where it should be, in perfect condition — and that it is, as of nine days ago, the most carefully preserved piece of blank paper in the province."

The wagon lurched. Oscar gripped the anchor ring with chained hands and laughed — a broken, disbelieving sound that the rain swallowed — because of course. Of course. The forgery had been perfect. It had been so perfect that Veyren had not yet discovered the theft, because the duplicate *was* the keystone page, as far as every eye in the Guild could tell, and the original was sewn into the lining of a coat that was, at this moment, in a lost-property bin in the town of Latch.

"You knew," Oscar whispered. "When you pulled the flap closed. You knew what I'd done."

"I count everything," Sefa said. "It's my one flaw." She rode a few paces in silence, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped to almost nothing, pitched under the rain, meant for Oscar alone. "Eleven nights, scribe. The engine turns on the thirteenth moon, and Veyren means to turn it with the boy's voice, and the boy's voice means the boy's life — you know that now, don't you? You've read the charter."

"I've read the charter."

"Then hear the inventory." And Sefa turned, at last, just her head, just enough for the lamplight of a waystation ahead to catch her face — calm, lined, utterly without mercy and utterly without cruelty, the face of a woman about to read out the terms of a debt. "Six hunters on this road. Two are mine — bought, not bought, don't ask, it's in the ledger and the ledger is clean. The vault below the temple has one door with one lock, and I have held the key to that lock for eleven years, and in eleven years no one has ever once counted the keys."

The wagon rolled toward the waystation's light. Ahead, south, the towers of the Guild of Impossible Things stood against the storm-dark like a row of upright books on a shelf, and somewhere among them August was awake or asleep, counting moons, and somewhere above them all the thirteenth moon was turning in its impossible orbit, patient as a debt.

"So count with me, scribe," Sefa said. "One door. One lock. One key nobody's counting. And eleven nights." Her eyes, in the half-light, were the grey of a ledger's margin. "The question — the only question — is whether you've got one more forgery in you, or whether the finest work of your life is going to be the last."

The wagon passed under the waystation's lamp, and the light crossed her face and left it, and Oscar, chained in the dark, in the rain, in the cold arithmetic of their own ruin, felt the stone on their chest shift one inch further — and through the seam, faint but unmistakable, the light of a plan.
