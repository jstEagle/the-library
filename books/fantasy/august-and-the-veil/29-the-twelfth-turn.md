## 29. The Twelfth Turn

The vault breathed.

Oscar had felt it from the first stair — a slow exhalation of cold air rising through the temple's bones, carrying the smell of bronze and old starlight. Now, standing at the lip of the great chamber beneath the sanctuary, they understood that the breath was not the building's. It was the engine's. The orrery turned above them like a second sky inverted, thirteen arms sweeping their patient circles, twelve moons of pale glass riding the arms and one socket empty and waiting, and every rotation pushed air through channels cut into the stone so that the whole room inhaled and exhaled with the rhythm of a thing asleep.

A thing asleep, but not for long.

Oscar pressed themselves into the shadow between two pillars and counted guards. Two at the far door, halberds grounded, bored. One circling the gallery above, boots ringing on iron grating. None near the orrery's base, because no one was fool enough to stand beneath it — the guild's own superstition, Veyren's sermons about holy ground, working in Oscar's favor tonight. Sefa's poison had done its quiet work an hour ago: the lamps along the eastern corridor had guttered, the watch-bell had rung false alarm, and the guards had flowed toward the trouble like water finding a crack.

Which left the vault, for a few stolen minutes, to the scribes and the sleeping god.

And to August.

The child sat at the base of the orrery with their knees drawn up, small against the vast brass root of the machine, lit by the slow wheeling of the twelve moons overhead. They were not bound — not yet; that indignity was reserved for tomorrow night, the thirteenth turn, when the empty socket would fill and Veyren would want his congregation to see the key unbound and willing. Tonight August was only guarded by distance and locked doors, and neither had stopped Sefa's keys, and neither would stop Oscar's.

"August," Oscar whispered, and the word fell into the breathing dark like a coin down a well.

The child's head came up. In the moon-glass light their eyes were enormous, and for one terrible heartbeat Oscar saw no recognition in them at all — only the flat wariness of something that had been studied too long by too many faces. Then the wariness broke, and August was on their feet and crossing the space between them at a dead run, and Oscar dropped to their knees and caught them, and the impact knocked the breath out of them both.

"You came." August's voice was muffled against Oscar's shoulder, furious and small. "You said you'd come and I thought — I thought maybe you were dead, because Sefa got out but nobody knew about you, and Ilara wouldn't look at me, she just stood outside the door like a—"

"I know. I know. I'm here."

"They chained my ankle to the bed. It left a mark." August pulled back and shoved up the hem of their rough gray shift to show a raw red ring above one thin ankle, as if proof were needed, as if Oscar could have doubted anything done to this child. "It doesn't hurt anymore. I made it not hurt. But I wanted you to see it anyway."

"I see it." Oscar did see it, and felt it settle somewhere behind their ribs alongside all the other debts they carried — the forged entries, the stolen keystone page folded against their heart, Ilara's torn commission, Sefa bleeding somewhere in the dark on their behalf. "We're getting you out of here. Tonight. There's a way through the cisterns, Sefa marked it, we can be past the border shrine before—"

"No."

Oscar stopped. The word was quiet, but it landed with the weight of the First Tongue behind it, and Oscar had spent enough nights learning that language to hear the difference now — the way August's ordinary words were children's blocks, and this was a stone.

"August—"

"No." The child stepped back, out of the circle of Oscar's arms, and planted their feet on the vault floor as though the floor might argue. Above them the orrery wheeled, and one of the glass moons passed directly over August's head and threw a small cold halo there, and Oscar thought: *even the machine crowns him.* "I'm not going through your door. And I'm not going through theirs." A small hand rose and pointed at the empty socket turning overhead. "I'm not opening the Veil. Not for him. Not for anyone. Not ever."

* * *

August had counted the moons.

That was the first thing, the thing that lived under everything else like a stone under a stream. Twelve turns of the sky since the thirteenth moon had first shown its face over the temple spire, thin and wrong and hungry-looking, and August had counted each one from whatever window they were allowed — the scriptorium window with its view of the bell tower, the infirmary window with its view of nothing, the narrow slit in the cell where they'd spent three days after the fire in the transcription pages. Twelve moons. One left.

Sefa had told them what happened on the last one. Not gently, because Sefa didn't do gentle, but honestly, which August was learning was rarer and worth more. *The engine needs a voice keyed to it,* she'd said, cleaning her nails with a knife in the lamplight of the storeroom where she'd been caught smuggling him bread. *Veyren built it around yours. On the thirteenth turn, he puts you under the empty socket and you sing the Veil open, and then everyone in this city gets to find out what's on the other side whether they asked to or not.*

"And me?" August had asked. "What happens to me after?"

Sefa had looked at him for a long moment, and August had watched her decide — watched her weigh the truth against kindness and choose truth, because that was the only gift Sefa knew how to give. *Engines burn fuel, little moon.*

So August had known, for eleven days now, exactly what the thirteenth turn meant. And they had done what they always did with terrible knowledge: they had held it very still inside themselves, the way you hold a bird so it won't bruise, and they had waited for Oscar.

Now Oscar was here, kneeling in the machine's shadow smelling of ink and canal water, saying *we're getting you out*, and August loved them so much it hurt, and the answer was still no.

"Don't you understand?" August said. "If I run, he finds me. He always finds me. My name is in his book — the old book, the charter — he wrote me down before I was born, he wrote what I'm *for*. As long as my name is in his ledger, I belong to the ledger. Running is just being stolen property." They had learned these words from listening to the scribes argue, and they deployed them now like weapons taken off a fallen soldier. "But if I open the Veil — even once, even a crack — the engine gets what it wants. And engines don't stop wanting. You taught me that. You said a debt never sleeps, it only waits."

"That's different. That's money."

"It's the same shape." August's chin came up. "You always say things are the same shape underneath. You showed me with the ledgers. Debts and promises and names — same shape. So." They pointed again at the empty socket, wheeling closer, wheeling away, patient as winter. "If I never speak the opening words, the engine never turns all the way. If I never turn all the way, the Veil holds. That's how I win. I just have to be silent forever, and die quiet, and it's fine, because I already decided, and deciding makes it not scary anymore. Mostly."

The lie was good. August had been practicing lying — Oscar had taught them, strange lessons after curfew, *say it plain, say it boring, liars decorate* — and this lie was decorated with *it's fine* and *mostly* and delivered in a flat small voice, and it was still the worst lie August had ever told, and they could see Oscar see straight through it, because Oscar's face did the thing it did, the crumpling-at-the-corners thing, the thing that meant someone was about to be kind, which was the most dangerous thing any face could do.

"Come here," Oscar said.

"I'm standing right here."

"Come *here*." And August went, because some debts you pay gladly, and let Oscar fold them in again, and this time August let their forehead rest against Oscar's collarbone and stopped pretending, and the shaking started, the shaking they'd been holding off for eleven days, the shaking that was all the terror of the cell and the chain and the counting of moons finally coming due.

"I don't want to die quiet," August whispered into the wool of Oscar's coat. "I decided it but I don't want it. Is that allowed? Can you decide a thing and also not want it?"

"Yes," Oscar said, and their voice cracked in the middle of the word, and August held onto that crack like a handhold on a cliff. "Yes, that's allowed. That's the most human thing there is."

* * *

Above them the twelfth moon completed its circuit and began another, and Oscar held the child and did the arithmetic of despair.

One night left. Tomorrow dusk, the thirteenth moon would rise — Veyren had announced it from the pulpit, had preached it into the hungry faith of the crowd, *renewal, beloved, renewal* — and the guild would bring August down these stairs in white robes, and either the child opened the Veil or the child died refusing, and Oscar had run every road out of that fork and found each one bricked shut. Flight: the hunters, the seals on the gates, the name in the charter that let Veyren track August like a hound tracks blood. Bargain: Veyren had already offered freedom priced at betrayal, and Oscar had already refused it, and masters did not make the offer twice. Force: two of them, one of them eight years old, against a fortress full of believers.

And underneath all of it, the keystone page burned against Oscar's chest where they'd hidden it — the stolen heart of the engine, the sheet of First Tongue notation that Sefa had bled for, that Ilara had torn her commission over, that Oscar had forged a twin of so perfect that Veyren himself had handled the forgery and smiled. They had the engine's heart. They simply had no body to put it in, no way to use it that didn't end with August under the empty socket singing the world's oldest wound open.

Unless.

The thought arrived the way dangerous thoughts always arrived — quietly, sideways, dressed as something small. Oscar almost didn't catch it. Almost let it pass, because catching it would mean looking at it, and looking at it would mean knowing.

*The engine reads voices,* Veyren had said, that day in his study, wine-dark and certain. *It was tuned across centuries to one voice alone. The key is the child's throat and nothing else.*

But Oscar had transcribed August for months. Had sat night after night with ink that rearranged itself, learning the language that wrote meaning instead of sound. And in those months they had noticed — had filed away without understanding, the way scribes file everything — that the First Tongue did not care who spoke it. It cared whether it was spoken *truly*. The ink listened to accuracy, not anatomy. When Oscar had mispronounced, the page corrected them. When Oscar had spoken a phrase perfectly, the page had *answered* — once, only once, a single character lifting off the parchment and hanging in the air like a mote of gold before settling back down, and Oscar had told themselves it was a trick of lamplight because the alternative was unthinkable.

Unthinkable then. Unavoidable now.

"August." Oscar drew back and took the child's face in both hands. "Listen to me. I need to ask you something, and I need the true answer, not the brave one. When the engine listens — when it's listening for your voice — what is it actually listening *for*? The sound? Or the meaning?"

August blinked. The question moved visibly through them, the way questions did — Oscar could watch this child think, could see the machinery behind the eyes, and had never once found it anything less than astonishing. "The meaning," August said slowly. "The First Tongue isn't sounds. Sounds are just... clothes. The words are the meaning wearing clothes. Grandmother said—" A flicker of grief, there and mastered; the grandmother belonged to the life before the Veil, the life August remembered in fragments. "Grandmother said a word spoken falsely does nothing. A word spoken truly does everything. It doesn't matter whose mouth. She said that's why they were exiled. Because their words were too true for the walls people wanted."

The vault breathed. The moons wheeled. Somewhere above, faint through fathoms of stone, the temple bells rang the third hour, and Oscar heard it as a countdown.

"Then listen to me," Oscar said, "and listen carefully, because I'm only going to say this once and then we're going to spend every hour until dusk making it true." They gripped August's shoulders. "You are not going to open the Veil. You are not going to die quiet. And you are not going to be silent forever, because silence forever is just a slower version of what Veyren wants, and I refuse — do you hear me, I *refuse* — to let that man win by making us smaller. We are not going to hide from the thirteenth turn. We're going to take it."

"How?" Small, fierce, disbelieving. "How?"

"Tomorrow night, when he brings you to the socket — you go. You walk down those stairs on your own feet, in his white robes, and you let him think he's won. And while he's gloating, while the whole gilded congregation is on its knees, I'm going to be standing somewhere in this room with the keystone page and every phrase you've taught me, and I'm going to speak the opening myself."

August stared. "But it'll kill you. Sefa said the engine burns—"

"Sefa said it burns *fuel*. Fuel is whatever feeds it." Oscar was thinking aloud now, fast, the plan assembling itself out of ledgers and linguistics and sheer refusal, half certainty and half prayer. "Your name has fed it for centuries — it's written into the charter, it's soaked into the mechanism. But names aren't the only true things. Words are true too. Maybe truer. If I speak the opening *completely*, *truly*, with the whole language behind it — if I give it a truth bigger than the one it was built on — maybe it takes the words and lets the speaker keep breathing. Maybe it doesn't. But August—" and here Oscar's voice did break again, and this time they let it, because the child deserved to hear the cost named plainly, "—if the choice is your silence forever or my one loud night, I know which one I'm choosing. I've known since the knock before dawn. You are not a key. You are a person who deserves windows and doors and a long life and someone to teach you to swear properly, and I will burn every ledger in this city before I let a machine eat you."

For a long moment August said nothing. The orrery turned. A glass moon crossed the child's face and made them briefly luminous, briefly older than eight years, briefly something the First Speakers beyond the Veil might have recognized as their own.

"You'd really do that," August said at last. Not a question. An audit. Checking the entry against the column.

"I'd really do that."

"Okay." And then, with the terrifying practicality of children: "Then we have until dusk tomorrow, and I have to teach you the rest of the phrase, and the middle part is the hard part because it has a sound your mouth doesn't know yet. We should start now. Sit down. No — *sit*, scribes are always standing like the floor owes them money."

Oscar laughed — a broken, astonished sound that echoed off the pillars and made one of the distant guards shift his weight — and sat down on the cold vault floor beside the child, beneath the wheeling moons, and August began to teach.

They worked through the fourth hour, the fifth. The phrase that opened the Veil was nine phrases long, and August gave them one at a time, demanding perfection, rapping Oscar's knuckles with a small finger when the meaning wobbled. *Say it plain. Say it true. Liars decorate.* All of Oscar's own lessons handed back to them, sharpened. And with each phrase the vault seemed to lean closer, the engine's breathing deepening, the glass moons brightening by fractions, as though the machine could feel its key being copied and did not yet know what to make of it.

It was nearly dawn when August paused, mid-lesson, and looked up at the empty socket passing overhead, and said, very quietly:

"When you speak it tomorrow — when the engine takes your words — ask it for doors. Not a wall. Not a gate. Doors. Grandmother said the Veil was never supposed to be a wall. Walls are what frightened people build. Ask it for doors, and mean it, and maybe..." The child shrugged, suddenly eight years old again, suddenly tired. "Maybe the truest word wins."

Oscar looked at the empty socket — one more turn, one more night, and it would be full — and felt no fear, or rather felt fear the way you feel cold on a morning you've decided to walk into anyway.

"One more lesson," Oscar said. "The last one. Then you sleep, and I go, and tomorrow we finish this."

"What's the lesson?"

Oscar reached into their coat and drew out the keystone page, unfolded it in the moon-glass light, and laid it on the stone between them — the engine's stolen heart, dense with notation, humming faintly against the floor.

"Teach me to read this," Oscar said, "before the thirteenth moon rises — because I think there's something written in it that Veyren never understood, and I think it's the reason the engine really works. And I think it changes everything."
