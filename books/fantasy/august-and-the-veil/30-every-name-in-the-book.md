## 30. Every Name in the Book

The cell had no name for its hours. Oscar learned to measure time by other things: the drip of water somewhere behind the eastern wall, the changing color of the light through the barred slot high above — grey, then white, then a bruised amber that meant evening in the world above — and the sound of boots on stone, which came at intervals too irregular to be a schedule and too regular to be anything but deliberate.

They had taken everything. The satchel with the keystone page sewn into its lining. The cartographer's map, which they had burned rather than surrender, feeding it page by page into a tavern stove while hunters hammered on the door. Even the stub of pencil Oscar kept behind their ear out of habit older than thought.

Everything except what was in their head.

That was the irony Oscar turned over in the dark like a coin worn smooth. They had spent their life putting things down — ink on vellum, names in ledgers, debts in columns — because what was written could be owned, and what was owned could be traded, and what could be traded kept a person alive. And now the only thing of value left to them was something no one could take, because it had never been written down at all.

*Veth-ka-lumen. Sera-veth-ka.*

The phrase August had taught them, syllable by syllable, in the vault's shadow, in the reed-bed dark before the border town. Oscar did not know yet what all of it meant. They knew enough. They knew it opened doors.

They sat against the cold wall and said it silently, again and again, the way a starving man counts coins he cannot spend.

* * *

They came for him on the third day — or what Oscar judged was the third day, though the amber light had come twice, which made it either two or four, and the uncertainty sat in his stomach like a swallowed stone.

Not Veyren. Not yet. Two guards whose faces Oscar did not know, and behind them, walking with her hands clasped behind her back as if she were inspecting a granary, Sefa.

"Quartermaster," Oscar said. The word came out flat. In the border town she had been among the guards who took him; whether she had engineered that capture or merely failed to prevent it, he still did not know, and the not-knowing was its own kind of wound.

"Scrivener." She dismissed the guards with a flick of two fingers. They withdrew up the stair, and their boot-sounds faded, and then there was only the drip of water and the two of them and the small square of amber light between them like a coin neither could pick up.

"You're alive," Sefa said. "I'll confess I gave even odds."

"Even odds." Oscar laughed, and it came out wrong, more breath than sound. "You stood in the yard when they put these chains on me."

"I stood in the yard," Sefa agreed. "And I also made certain the wagon went to the guild cells instead of the deep archive, where Master Veyren sends people he doesn't intend to see again. You're welcome, by the way. You can thank me properly once you've stopped looking at me like that."

"Like what?"

"Like I'm a ledger entry you haven't finished auditing."

Oscar looked away. The wall was damp and furred with a pale moss that grew, impossibly, without light. Everything in this place grew without light. He thought of the vault beneath the temple, the great brass rings of the orrery turning in the dark for centuries, fed by nothing anyone could see.

"Why are you here, Sefa?"

"Because you stole my keystone page." She crouched down to his level, her knees cracking, and her face in the amber light was old and tired and sharp as a scale-weight. "Or a copy of it. Or something. Veyren has been in the vault for two days straight, and he is *furious*, and Master Veyren furious is a weather system, and I have spent forty years learning to read weather. So I need to know what you actually took, scrivener, because if it's what I think it is, we are all standing much closer to the edge than either of us planned."

"I took the page that keys the engine," Oscar said. "The true one. What Veyren has is my forgery."

Sefa closed her eyes. For a moment she looked every year of her age, and ten besides. "Of course you did. Of course. The finest forgery of your life, you told me once, would be the one that saves it." She opened her eyes. "Does it work? The forgery. Has he tried it?"

"He hasn't needed to try it. The thirteenth moon hasn't risen." Oscar shifted; the chains clinked, a small bright sound in all that dark. "Two nights. Maybe three. Then he puts August in front of the orrery, turns the rings, speaks the words from the keystone page, and either the Veil opens or it doesn't."

"And if it doesn't—"

"Then he learns the forgery is false, and he comes down here and asks me very politely for the real page, and I tell him, because by then August will have taught me—" Oscar stopped.

Sefa waited. She was good at waiting; it was half her trade.

"Taught you what?" she said softly.

Oscar hadn't meant to say it. But the cell was dark and he was tired and Sefa had, whatever else she was, whatever treacheries she carried folded inside her loyalty like letters inside an envelope, come down into the dark to sit with him. "The First Tongue," he said. "Some of it. Enough."

The silence that followed had weight. Somewhere above them, the guild went about its evening — a door, a voice, the far bell that marked the change of watch — and all of it sounded very small, like sounds heard through water.

"You can speak it," Sefa said at last. "The language that predates kingdoms. The language the Veil was written in. A scribe. An indentured scribe with four years left on his debt." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "Do you understand what that makes you?"

"Tired," Oscar said. "It makes me very tired."

"It makes you a key," Sefa said. "And keys, scrivener, are the only things in this world worth stealing."

* * *

After she left — after the boots faded and the amber light died and the slot above went the color of ash — Oscar lay back against the moss-furred wall and tried to sleep, and could not, and so did what he had always done when he could not sleep: he worked the problem the way he used to work a ledger, line by line, looking for the column that didn't balance.

The engine read voices. That was what everyone believed. It was what Veyren believed — Oscar had seen it in the master's face in the vault, that night of vertigo and brass, the certainty shining off him like oil on water: *the child's voice will turn the rings.* It was why August mattered. It was why August had been purchased before birth, named in a charter older than his own bones, raised like a rare instrument in a case. One voice. One key. One boy, irreplaceable, and therefore ownable.

But Oscar had stolen the keystone page. Had held it under a shuttered lamp in the dead hours and copied every stroke of it, and copying it had done what copying always did to Oscar — slowed the world down until he could see the joints in it, the places where meaning was nailed to sound.

The keystone page was not a transcription. That was the first thing. Every other document in the guild's archive recorded the First Tongue the way a deaf man might record music: marks standing in for noises, noises standing in for meanings, three removes from the thing itself. But the keystone page was written *in* the First Tongue — truly in it, the way August spoke it, the way the ink had rearranged itself under Oscar's pen that first week, meaning flowing directly into shape without passing through sound at all.

And the shapes on the keystone page — Oscar saw them now, in the dark, with the terrible clarity of a thing understood too late to be convenient — the shapes were not instructions.

They were a signature.

He sat up. The chains dragged across the floor, and he barely heard them.

Every document in the Guild of Impossible Things bore a signature. Every debt, every indenture, every charter. Oscar had forged hundreds of them; he knew signatures the way a locksmith knows locks, from the inside. And a signature did not care who *spoke* it. A signature cared who *was*. When Oscar forged Master Veyren's hand, the forgery did not work because Oscar imitated the sound of Veyren's voice saying *I approve this expenditure.* It worked because the shape of the name on the page *stood for* Veyren — stood in his place, carried his authority, was, in the eyes of any ledger, Veyren himself.

Names were how the guild owned things. Names were how ledgers bound people to debts, children to charters, prophecies to purchase-orders. Oscar had known this his whole working life the way a fish knows water, and never once, until this moment, in this cell, had he thought to ask what else names might do.

The engine didn't listen for a voice. The engine listened for a *name* — spoken in the First Tongue, where name and voice and self were the same stroke of the same pen. August could open it because August's name was written in the founding charter, because August *was* a key the way a signature is a key, stamped into the world before he drew breath.

But the keystone page —

Oscar pressed his palms against his eyes until sparks bloomed in the dark.

The keystone page was signed. Of course it was signed. Everything the guild ever made was signed. And the signature on the keystone page was not August's. It couldn't be; the page predated August by generations. It was the signature of whoever had built the engine, whoever had written the opening-phrase into being — and that signature was a name in the First Tongue, a name that could turn the rings, a name that could key the machine—

A name that belonged to someone who had been dead for three hundred years.

No. Oscar lowered his hands. No, that wasn't the shape of it. Think. Think like a ledger. If the engine required the builder's own name, the builder's death would have bricked the door shut forever, and Veyren's whole design — the thirteenth moon, the purchased child, the centuries of patient stewardship — would be pointless. The engine must accept *any* name spoken in the First Tongue. Any name at all. Because that was what names in that language *were*: not labels, but assertions. *I am the one who opens this.* Speak your name in the First Tongue before the orrery, and the engine did not ask whether you were the right person.

It asked whether you were willing to be.

Anyone. Anyone who could speak it. Not a voice. Not a bloodline. Not a purchased prophecy.

A person.

Oscar began, very quietly, to laugh — the awful, wheezing laugh of a man who has found the error in the ledger on the last page of the audit, the single transposed figure that changes everything, and understands that the error has been sitting there since the first entry, invisible, because everyone including the auditor wanted the books to balance.

All those years. All those ledgers. The guild had built a god-machine keyed to a child, had bought a soul before it was born, had hunted Oscar across two hundred miles and chained him in a cell — and the whole vast mechanism, the moon and the orrery and the charter and the sermon, all of it rested on a misunderstanding of grammar. On reading a signature as a password instead of a promise.

Veyren thought the engine asked *whose voice are you?*

It asked *who are you?*

And Oscar Reyes — debtor, forger, scribe, coward, thief, lover, liar — had spent four nights in a reed bed learning to say his own name in a language older than kingdoms.

* * *

Sleep came finally, near dawn, and with it a dream that was not entirely a dream. He stood in the vault again, but the vault was flooded with shallow silver water, and the great rings of the orrery turned slowly overhead like moons seen through ice, and August stood waist-deep in the silver, looking up at him with an expression Oscar had never seen on the child's face before, and could not, in waking life, have named.

"You understand now," August said. In the dream the child's voice was the First Tongue entire, and Oscar understood every word, and the understanding felt like standing in warm rain.

"I think so," Oscar said. "The engine reads names."

"The engine reads *truths*," August said. "A name is only the smallest truth. There are larger ones." The child lifted a hand, and the silver water rose with it, sheeting off in ribbons. "When the night comes, you will have to say more than who you are. You will have to say what you want. The Veil listens for wanting. It always has. That is why it was built — to keep our wanting apart from yours."

"What did your people want?" Oscar asked. "Beyond the Veil?"

August's face did something complicated — grief and humor sharing a single small space, the way they do in children and in the very old. "To be remembered correctly," the child said. "It is not so much to ask. Everyone fails at it."

Oscar woke with the taste of silver water in his mouth and the guard's boots already on the stair.

* * *

They brought him bread and water and, unexpectedly, paper.

One sheet. One pencil stub, sharpened with a knife someone had cared about. The guard — young, nervous, unwilling to meet Oscar's eyes — set them on the floor inside the bars and retreated without a word.

Oscar stared at them for a long time. Then he understood: Veyren wanted him to write. Wanted a confession, perhaps, or the location of the true keystone page, or simply wanted to see what a scribe did when handed tools in a cage. The master had always believed that everything could be read out of what a person produced. Give Oscar ink and he would betray himself in his own handwriting.

Oscar picked up the pencil. Turned the sheet over. Found the grain of it, the way he'd been taught at nine years old, kneeling at a bench in the guild scriptorium with a switch waiting for the first crooked letter.

Then, in the smallest, finest hand he possessed — a hand no one had ever seen, a hand reserved for things that must never be traced — he wrote out the First Tongue phrase August had taught him. Syllable by syllable. Sound-shapes first, the way a foreigner writes down a song. And beneath each sound-shape, the true form, the meaning-form, the strokes that were not pronunciation but assertion.

*Veth-ka-lumen.* I am the door's intention.

*Sera-veth-ka.* I am the one who chooses.

He looked at what he had written for a long time. It was the first thing he had ever written in that language, and the page seemed to hold it differently than pages hold ink — the way a cupped hand holds water, aware of the weight.

Anyone could key the engine. That was the clarity, terrible and clean as a blade laid flat on a table. Which meant August did not have to die. Which meant the choice did not belong to Veyren, or to the charter, or to the thirteenth moon climbing toward its fullness over the temple roof.

It belonged to whoever was willing to stand before the rings and say *I am* in a language that could not lie.

Oscar's hand was shaking. He noticed it with distant interest, the way one notices weather. Four years of debt. Thirty years of silence, of writing other people's words in other people's hands, of being a signature-space in someone else's ledger. And now, in two nights, he would stand in the vault and speak — aloud, in the oldest tongue there was — and every record in the Guild of Impossible Things would learn, in that instant, exactly who had been keeping their books.

The boots came again. The guard took the paper — Oscar let it go; it said nothing a stranger could read — and the pencil, and the bread crust he had saved, and left him the water.

Oscar drank, and sat back, and watched the slot of light climb the far wall toward noon.

Two nights. And somewhere above him, in a tower room with a locked case, a child was counting moons, and somewhere below him, in a vault full of turning brass, a machine was waiting to be told a truth — and Oscar had just realized, with the calm of a man past fear, that he already knew most of the sentence.

But not all of it. And the missing part — the part August had refused to teach him in the reed bed, turning his face away, saying *not that word, not yet, you are not ready to carry it* — that part was going to cost something. Oscar could feel its absence the way a ledger feels a missing page: a thinness, a wrongness, a place where the binding creaked.

He had two nights to learn the rest of his own name.

He only hoped August was ready to teach it — because the last word of the phrase, the one the child would not say, was a word Oscar had begun, in his dreams of silver water, to suspect was not a word at all.

It was a price.
