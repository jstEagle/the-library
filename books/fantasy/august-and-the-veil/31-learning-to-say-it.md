## 31. Learning to Say It

The last lesson began the way all our lessons had begun: with a candle, a stolen hour, and the sound of my own voice in a room too small to hold it.

August counted the moons first. That was the ritual now, every night since the vault, since the orrery with its brass arms reaching for a sky that only existed in Veyren's almanac. August would stand at the narrow window of the scriptorium loft — the one place in the Guild of Impossible Things where no one looked, because no one imagined a scribe and a child would hide in plain sight among the ledgers — and count.

"Twelve," August said. "Twelve turns left."

I did not answer. There was nothing honest I could say that would not be a lie dressed up as comfort, and August had learned from me how to hear the difference. I had taught this child to lie, after all. *Say you are tired when you are afraid. Say you are cold when you want to weep. A lie is a small door, Oscar had told me, and doors can be closed.* August had been an apt pupil in the way children are apt at everything — completely, terrifyingly, without mercy.

Now the pupil turned teacher, and what remained to teach was the one thing I had begged not to learn.

* * *

We sat on the floor between the shelves of debt-ledgers, backs against a case of confiscated maps, the candle guttering in a dish of melted tallow August had requisitioned with a look of such perfect innocence that even Sefa would have applauded. The child's hair was still damp from the washbasin; it curled at the ends like question marks. In the candlelight the mud-stains of that first storm-night were long gone, but I sometimes saw them anyway, ghosted onto August's skin — the night the ledgers had shivered at a child's voice, the night my life had stopped being an account and started being a story.

"You're afraid," August said. Not accusing. Observing, the way August observed everything, with the terrible clarity of someone who remembered a world before walls.

"I'm a scribe," I said. "Fear is just ink that hasn't found its page yet."

"That's not true." August's mouth twitched. "You taught me that lie yourself. Lesson four."

I laughed despite myself — a small, cracked sound in the dark. "Lesson four. Yes."

"But you *are* afraid." August scooted closer, knees knocking mine. "Good. It should be frightening. Oren says—" and there August stopped, the way August always stopped at that name, the First Speaker who wore my face in dreams, who had made a promise across the Veil before either of us was born. August's fingers worked at the fraying edge of a sleeve. "Oren says the phrase is not a spell. It's a *greeting*. You don't force a door open with it. You knock so loudly the door has to decide what it is."

"And what will it decide?"

"That depends on who's knocking." August looked up at me, and in those eyes — eyes the color of river water over stones, eyes that had seen a city beyond the Veil and refugees begging not to be remembered wrong — there was something older than the face around them. "That's why it has to be you. Not me. Me, the engine hears a key turning in a lock. You—" August touched my chest, right over the sternum, where a lifetime of swallowed words had calcified into something like bone. "You, the engine hears a person choosing."

* * *

We began, as always, with breath.

"The First Tongue isn't sounds," August said, in the cadence of every lesson before, but tonight there was a gravity under it, a stone rolling downhill gathering speed. "You learned that from the ink. It's *meaning wearing sound like a coat*. So before you say the words, you have to mean them. All of them. At once. Can you do that?"

"I've spent my life meaning things I wasn't allowed to say," I said. "I can manage three words."

"Nine words." August held up small fingers, counting. "And they aren't words the way ledger-words are words. They're more like... hands. Each one is a hand doing something. The first opens. The second names. The third—"

"Bind," I said. "I know the shape of it. I've watched you write it in the margins for weeks."

"You've watched. Watching isn't knowing." August took both my hands then, turned them palm-up, and laid something in them: a strip of parchment, thin as onion skin, covered in the transcription — my own handwriting, but rearranged, the ink having crawled overnight into configurations no human hand could make. The meaning-script. The language that wrote truth instead of sound. "Read it. Not aloud. Read it until your chest hurts."

I read.

And my chest hurt.

Because the strip didn't say *open the Veil*. It said: *Let what was divided know itself again. Let the wall remember it was once a handshake. Let the two skies recognize each other as one sky, seen from two sides of a wound.*

It said: *I am not afraid of what is true.*

It said — and here my throat closed entirely — *whoever speaks this speaks for both worlds, and gives their name to both, and keeps neither.*

"August." My voice came out rough as unsanded wood. "This last line. *Keeps neither.* What does that mean? Exactly. Precisely. The way I would need it to mean."

August was quiet for a long moment. The candle flame leaned, straightened, leaned again, as if listening.

"It means the speaker stops belonging to one side," August said finally. "Oren told me. The First Speakers tried it once, at the beginning, and the ones who spoke it — they became the border themselves. They're the thin places now. The fords and the hollow hills and the places where the mist doesn't know which side it's on." Small shoulders rose and fell. "They didn't die, exactly. They just stopped being *only here*. Or only there."

"And you'd be asking me to become a place."

"I'm asking you to become a *door*." August's chin lifted, stubborn, and for a heartbeat I saw not a child but something ancient wearing a child's fury like borrowed armor. "Doors get walked through, Oscar. Doors get leaned on and kicked and propped open with bricks and slammed in anger. Doors are *useful*. Isn't that better than being a wall? Walls only get climbed or torn down."

I wanted to argue. I searched for the argument the way I searched a ledger for a false entry — methodically, line by line — and found none. Every objection circled back to the same account: my name, my debt, my small hoarded years of freedom, weighed against two worlds and a child who had never been given a single year that belonged to them.

"Show me," I said.

* * *

August taught the way water teaches stone: patiently, inevitably, by returning.

"*Vaelen,*" August said, and the syllable moved through the loft like weather. The candle flame bent toward the child. Somewhere below us, in the archive, I heard — or dreamed I heard — pages stirring in their sleep. "*Vaelen* means *open*, but it also means *the moment before opening*, the held breath. Say it."

"*Vaelen.*"

The word came out of my mouth wrong and right at once — wrong because my tongue stumbled on the vowel, which was less a sound than a widening, and right because the ink in the open transcription pot beside us shivered, rose in a fine black thread, and wrote the word in the air in meaning-script before dissolving.

August grinned, fierce and sudden. "Again."

I said it again. And again. Until the widening lived in my throat even in silence, until I understood that *vaelen* was not pronounced but *performed* — that the mouth had to become the thing it described, lips parting like a gate, breath passing through like a traveler.

Then the second word. "*Sovari.*" To name. To call a thing by its whole self, shadow and light and debt and dream. August made me practice on objects first — the candle (*sovari*, and the flame stood taller), the shelf (*sovari*, and I felt the old oak's whole life flicker past: seed, sapling, axe, joiner's plane, decades of holding other people's burdens upright). Then on ourselves.

"*Sovari Oscar.*"

The naming went through me like cold water through cloth. For one vertiginous instant I was every version of myself at once — the indentured scribe, the forger, the liar, the lover Ilara had once known, the child who had first been sold into the Guild's keeping, the old man I might have become if the thirteenth moon had never risen — all of them present, all of them named, none of them denied. I gasped. August caught my elbow.

"That's the cost," August said softly. "You have to hold all of you. The engine reads names, and a name is everything, not the tidy part you show people." A pause, and then, quieter, with the terrible gentleness children reserve for adults they love: "Even the parts you're ashamed of. Even the forged entry. Even Ilara."

My eyes stung. "You've learned cruelty very well."

"I've learned honesty very well. You taught me that too. Lesson nine." August squeezed my arm. "Again. *Sovari Oscar.*"

I said it until the shaking stopped. I said it until holding my whole name felt less like drowning and more like standing in a river that knew me.

* * *

The third word we saved for the deepest part of the night, when the Guild slept and even the mice in the walls seemed to hold their breath.

"*Thalassein*," August whispered. To bind. But not the binding of chains — the binding of book to spine, of family to table, of promise to keeper. The binding that holds by belonging rather than by force. "This is the word Veyren doesn't understand. He thinks the Veil is a lock and the engine is a key and you are the hand that turns it. He's wrong. The Veil is a *torn agreement*. The First Speakers made it in grief, in war, in haste — a wall built out of a wound. *Thalassein* doesn't rebuild the wall. It finishes the sentence the wall interrupted."

"What was the sentence?"

August looked at the window, at the twelve remaining slivers of moonlight the almanac promised, and recited it like a psalm learned in a language of exile:

"*Two skies, one sky. Two peoples, one people. Divided for safety, divided for sorrow — let the division end in choice, not in conquest. Let neither side swallow the other. Let the door stand between them forever, and let the door be loved.*"

Silence. The candle burned steady now, as if it had made peace with the dark.

"That's the treaty," I breathed. "That's what the phrase *is*. It's not an opening. It's a signing."

"Now you understand why it costs." August's voice was very small. "A signature binds the signer. Whoever speaks it becomes the seal. Becomes the guarantee. If either world breaks faith, the breaker breaks *you*." The child's hands folded in their lap, small and grave as a merchant closing accounts. "Oren couldn't come do it himself. None of the First Speakers can — they're already the border, already spent. They've waited centuries for someone on this side willing to pay. They hoped it would be someone who chose it. Not a bought child. Not a bound instrument." Those river-water eyes lifted to mine. "Someone free."

"I'm not free," I said. "I'm owned down to my handwriting. Veyren holds my debt like a knife at—"

"No." August shook their head, and the certainty in it stopped my breath. "That's what you keep getting wrong, Oscar. That's what the ledgers taught you to believe. A debt is a story about you. It isn't you. Tomorrow night, when you speak *thalassein*, every ledger in this building is going to discover that it never owned anything at all. It just wrote your name next to numbers and pretended the numbers were real." A ghost of a smile. "You'll be the first person in Guild history to be freed by grammar."

I laughed — really laughed, helpless and wet-eyed, the sound bouncing off the confiscated maps of worlds no cartographer had survived visiting. August laughed with me, and for a while we were just two conspirators on a cold floor, giddy with terror, sharing stolen tallow-light.

* * *

Near dawn, August grew quiet. I knew the signs now: the stillness, the inward gaze, the way the child's breathing slowed as though listening to something far away. The memories came then, sometimes — the city beyond the Veil, the towers of woven light, the exiled speakers singing to be remembered rightly. I sat with August through them as I had learned to, saying nothing, offering only my shoulder, which August leaned against like a small warm weight anchoring a boat.

"When you say it tomorrow," August murmured, half-dreaming, "I'll be standing beside you. Bound to the orrery, probably. Veyren will have his ceremony and his sermon and his hungry faithful crowd, and he'll think the engine is answering *me*." A pause. "But I'll be saying it with you. Under my breath. Both of us. Two voices, one phrase. That's how it was always meant to be spoken, Oren says. Never alone. Alone, it's a sacrifice. Together, it's a *vow*."

"August." I turned to look at the child fully. "If this goes wrong—"

"It won't go wrong." August's eyes opened, clear and calm and unbearably ancient. "It will go *expensively*. There's a difference. Wrong means it fails. Expensive means it works and you pay. We're going to pay, Oscar. Both of us. You'll lose your name. I'll lose..." The small hands flexed. "I don't know what I'll lose. Maybe the being-a-key part of me. Maybe the remembering. Maybe I'll wake up the morning after and be just a child who talks funny, with no city in my head and no Oren in my dreams." The chin trembled once and steadied. "I've decided that's acceptable. Belonging somewhere real is worth more than remembering somewhere lost."

I gathered the child against my side and held on, and above us the false ceiling of the scriptorium loft hid us from a Guild that had planned this night for centuries, and below us the vault hummed with its brass orrery, patient as a trap, and outside the window the twelfth-to-last moon slid westward like a coin rolling toward a slot.

"One more time," August whispered against my shoulder. "All nine words. Slowly. Mean every one."

I closed my eyes. I opened my mouth. I let the first word widen my lips like a gate, and the second fill my chest with my whole unhidden name, and I reached for the third—

—and the bell began to ring.

Not the hour-bell. Not the alarm. The *summons* — the great bronze voice in the temple tower that had not sounded in living memory, rolling across the sleeping compound note by bronze note, waking lamps in window after window after window, and beneath its tolling I heard the thing that turned my blood to ice water:

Voices. Hundreds of them. Rising from the courtyard below, chanting in ragged, reverent unison — the guild faithful, summoned in the dead of night, crying out a word they could not possibly know.

"*Vaelen. Vaelen. Vaelen.*"

August went rigid against me. "He's starting early," the child breathed. "Oscar — the moon. Look at the moon."

I looked. Through the frost-furred glass, low over the eastern wall, the thirteenth moon was rising — full, impossible, silver as a blade — a full night ahead of the almanac's promise.

Veyren had lied about the calendar.

There was no twelfth turn left.
