## 32. The Night of Thirteen Moons

**Veyren**

Master Veyren had dressed for the occasion in the vestments of the guild's first century — heavy charcoal wool gone silver at the seams, embroidered along the hem with the names of every master who had served before him. There were forty-one names. He had spent the better part of an hour running his thumb across each one, feeling the raised thread like a rosary, and he had saved the forty-second space for last. It sat empty above the hem's end, a small rectangle of unworked wool, waiting.

Tonight it would be filled.

He descended the temple stair slowly, because a man about to make history should be seen to move with gravity, and because his knees had begun to complain these last two winters, and because the vault below was cold enough to make haste painful. Torchlight climbed the walls ahead of him, thrown up from the orrery chamber by a hundred braziers his acolytes had lit at dusk. The light made the stairwell breathe — gold and shadow, gold and shadow — and Veyren found the rhythm of it steadying. Like a pulse. Like the world's own pulse, waiting to be quickened.

The thirteenth moon was already visible through the temple's high oculus when he reached the vault floor: a pale coin of light riding low over the eastern hills, too large, too near, the way it had grown nearer every month since the almanac's final page had told him what the guild's founders had buried beneath his feet. He had read that page eleven years ago, the night he was raised to mastership, and he had spent eleven years preparing for the hour when the coin would rise full and thirteen-fold reflected in the orrery's rings.

The hour was now.

The chamber opened around him like the inside of a bell. The orrery dominated it — that vast engine of concentric brass rings, wide as a millwheel, tall as three men, each ring etched with the First Tongue in characters that no living scholar had been able to read until a mud-soaked child had walked through the guild door and spoken them as easily as breath. The rings hung motionless now, but Veyren knew they were not idle. They were listening. They had been listening for eleven years, for a hundred, for however long the founders had tuned them to a voice not yet born.

And in the center of the rings, bound to a chair of white oak with cords soaked in salt and silver-wort, sat August.

The child looked small. That was the thing Veyren had not expected, after all his reading, all his careful study of the transcripts Oscar Reyes had made — he had expected the key to the Veil to seem *larger* than a person, and instead the child looked like any child pulled from a river, wet-eyed and shivering, wrists chafed raw where the cords bit. August's hair had dried in wild tufts. One shoe was missing. The guild's physicians had fed the child broth and a mild sleeping draught that had not worked, because August's eyes were wide open and fixed on Veyren with an expression he chose not to examine.

"You understand," Veyren said, and his voice carried in the vault's great hollow the way a stone carries down a well, "that this is not a cruelty. I want you to know that. Whatever comes after — whatever the histories say of this night — I want you to know that I never intended cruelty."

August said nothing. The child had not spoken since the hunters brought them back from the border town, and the silence had a texture Veyren did not like. It was not the silence of fear. It was the silence of someone counting.

"The world beyond the Veil has been exiled for nine hundred years," Veyren continued, circling the orrery's base, one hand trailing along the brass. "Exiled by frightened men who built a wall instead of a bridge. And we have paid for their fear ever since — in wars that leak through the cracks, in the thin places where the Veil frays and things come through that should not, in the slow rot of a world half-alive. The founders of our guild saw it. They built this engine. They waited. And the First Tongue came back to us in the shape of a child, because the world is generous, August, even when men are not."

He stopped before the chair. Up close, the child smelled of salt water and lamp oil and, faintly, of ink — the transcription room's ink, which clung to everyone who worked near it. Veyren crouched, slowly, so as not to startle, until his eyes were level with the child's.

"You are not dying tonight," he said softly. "Whatever Reyes told you. Whatever the ledgers implied. The engine does not require a life. It requires a voice, sustained, at the moment of alignment. The moon will hold the door. The rings will hold the words. You will be tired, and you will be frightened, and then it will be done, and you will be the child who ended nine hundred years of exile. There are worse fates. There are *far* worse fates."

August's lips moved. No sound came. But Veyren had spent months with the transcripts; he had learned to read the shape of the First Tongue on human lips, and the shape the child's mouth made now was one he knew from page after page of Oscar Reyes's careful hand.

*You are lying.*

Veyren rose. He straightened his vestments. Above them, through the oculus, the thirteenth moon climbed another hand's width toward the zenith, and all around the vault, the orrery's rings began — very softly, like a struck glass heard from another room — to hum.

* * *

**August**

The cords were tight, but August had learned something in the months of lessons after curfew, something Oscar had taught with a crooked smile and a voice dropped to almost nothing: *a lie is just a door you build where there wasn't one.* And so August had built a door out of silence. Had let the sleeping draught win, or seem to. Had let the wrists go limp in the hunters' grip. Had let the body be carried down and bound and pitied, all the while counting — the braziers, the guards, the rings, the turns of the moon overhead, visible through the oculus in slices as the child's head lolled against the chair's back.

Twelve moons had come and gone since the first night. August had counted every one. And tonight the thirteenth hung above the temple like an open eye, and August knew — had known since the twelfth, since the terrible arithmetic had finished itself in the dark of the cell — what the engine wanted. Not a voice sustained. Veyren believed that, or needed to believe it, and the difference did not matter. August had heard the rings humming in dreams for weeks now, had heard what they hummed: a word with a hole in it, a word shaped like a person, a word that would only be complete if the person speaking it poured everything into the speaking and did not stop when the pouring hurt.

The founders had built the engine to be keyed by a life. August was sure of it the way one is sure of hunger.

But August was also sure of something else, something that had kept the child's spine straight through the broth and the cords and Veyren's soft, terrible kindness: Oscar had promised. *I will find another way.* And Oscar Reyes was a person who kept promises made in the dark of a cell, August knew this, because August had heard the promise made in the First Tongue, where lies cannot live. The First Tongue had no word for a promise that was not true. If Oscar had spoken it falsely, the words themselves would have curdled on the air, and they had not curdled. They had rung.

So August sat in the chair and let the cords bite and watched the moon climb, and did the only work left to do: held the opening phrase whole in the mind, every syllable, the way one holds a breath — not to use it, but to keep it from being used wrongly. If the rings were going to take a voice tonight, they would have to take it fighting.

The humming deepened. One of the great rings trembled, then another, and the characters etched in the brass began to glow like coals under ash. August felt the words in their own chest stir in answer, felt the First Tongue rise toward the surface of the throat like water finding a crack — and clamped down on it, jaw tight, teeth shut, a child holding a flood behind a door made only of will.

The moon touched the zenith's edge.

The first ring began to turn.

* * *

**Oscar**

They had taken Oscar's scribe's knife, belt, and boots, and locked them in the transcription room — not a cell, because Veyren still, in his careful soul, wanted the record kept, and there was no one else who could keep it. *Write what happens,* the master had said through the door, and his voice had been almost gentle. *History will want a witness, Reyes. You have always been a good witness.* And then the lock had turned, and the footsteps had gone away down the stair, and Oscar had been left alone with the archives, the lamps, and the sound — faint, rising, unbearable — of the orrery beginning to sing.

Oscar had tried the door eleven times. The twelfth time, they stopped trying and did what they had been trained to do for fifteen years: they looked at the room.

The transcription room was the most secure space in the guild after the vault, because it held the guild's most dangerous possession — not the engine, not the charter, but the *record*. Every word August had ever spoken in the guild's presence lived here, in forty-one bound volumes of Oscar's hand. And the room was built to protect them from fire, from water, from theft — which meant, Oscar realized with a lurch that turned their stomach to cold iron, that the room was built to be *sealed*. Stone walls a forearm thick. Shutters of lead-cored oak. And a ventilation system — because sealed rooms killed their scribes — a ventilation system of narrow copper ducts that ran up through the walls, past the vault stair, venting at last into the temple's bell tower.

The ducts were nine inches wide.

Oscar was not a large person. Fifteen years of ledger-work and stolen bread had made them narrow as a door-crack, and they had crawled through worse — through the archive's sub-stacks during the fire of the eastern wing, through a culvert in the border town with Ilara's hand pushing them from behind, laughing, both of them younger and unsworn and free. They thought of Ilara now and their chest seized, because Ilara was down there, somewhere, in the vault or above it, sworn to a duty that had already eaten her commission and her sleep and perhaps her heart, and Oscar could not think of her and also fit through a nine-inch duct, so they stopped thinking and began.

They stripped the lamp of its oil and greased the duct's mouth. They pulled off the grate with the scribe's awl they had hidden in their sleeve cuff — *always keep an awl, Reyes; a scribe's tools are the last things they search* — and they went in headfirst, into the dark and the copper smell and the rising song of the engine, which came down the duct like water and filled their skull until their teeth ached.

The duct was hot. The duct was too narrow at the second bend, and Oscar lay there in the dark with their cheek against copper and their lungs full of the First Tongue's resonance and thought, quite calmly, *I will die in a wall, and no ledger will even record the manner of it* — and then, because they had spent fifteen years learning to read what was written rather than what was meant, they heard the song clearly for the first time.

The rings were not humming August's phrase. They were humming the *frame* of it — the opening, the structure, the grammar — with a hole in the middle. A word-shaped absence. And the absence was pulling, the way the moon pulls the sea, and Oscar could feel it pulling through the copper, through their ribs, through the name they had written at the bottom of their first forged entry eleven years ago — *Oscar Reyes, scribe, second class* — pulling at the name itself, as if the engine did not want a voice at all.

As if it wanted a name.

Oscar's whole body went cold in the hot duct. *The engine reads names, not voices.* They had known it, had seen it in the keystone page, had told August in the cell — but knowing it and *hearing* it were different things, and the engine's hunger came up the duct now like a hand around their throat, and it was not reaching for August. It was reaching for whoever spoke. It would take August because August was there, because August was the key they had cut, but the lock itself — the lock would take *any* name, any soul willing to say the phrase and let the phrase say them.

They crawled. The third bend. The fourth. Light ahead — the bell tower's vent, a coin of moonlight — and Oscar hauled themselves toward it with their arms burning, and below them, through the temple's bones, the first ring completed its turn with a sound like a cathedral clearing its throat, and a child's voice, thin and defiant and *fighting*, rose into the First Tongue.

August was holding the flood. August could not hold it forever.

Oscar broke through the vent into the bell tower's night air, gasping, moonlit, thirteen moons' worth of light pouring over them from a sky that had waited nine hundred years for this, and began to run down the tower stair.

* * *

**Ilara**

Ilara Vance stood at the vault's upper gallery with her hand on her sword and her heart going like a war drum, and she did not draw, because there was nothing to draw against. The enemy was a machine. The enemy was a moon. The enemy was an old man in century-old wool reciting the guild's founding creed in a voice like warm honey, and forty acolytes on their knees around the orrery, faces upturned, weeping with a joy that made her sick.

She had filed her report three days ago. She had left out the canal gate, the torn commission, the cartographer's map she had found in Oscar's cell and returned, silently, to its hiding place beneath the floorboard. She had told herself it was strategy — keep the hunters looking outward, at the border roads, while the real danger stood in the vault wearing vestments. She had told herself many things. Tonight, watching the rings turn and the acolytes weep and the child in the white oak chair strain against the cords with their whole small body, Ilara admitted the truth she had been carrying since the borderlands: she had not protected Oscar out of strategy. She had protected them out of love, and love had made her a poor hunter, and she was glad of it, and she was ashamed of it, and both things were true in the same breath the way the two worlds were true on either side of the Veil.

The second ring turned. The humming climbed into a register she felt in her fillings, her bones, the scar on her shoulder from the border war. One of the acolytes fainted and was not caught. Nobody moved to catch him. The faith in the room had passed beyond bodies.

And then Ilara saw, at the far edge of the gallery, half in shadow by the stair — Sefa. The quartermaster stood with a ledger under one arm and her other hand inside her coat, and her face was the face of a woman doing arithmetic, and her eyes were fixed not on the orrery but on the *base* of it, on the heart-chamber Veyren had shown no one, and Ilara had spent fifteen years reading soldiers and smugglers and she read Sefa now as easily as a broadsheet: *she has something in her hand, and she is waiting for her moment.*

Their eyes met. Sefa did not look away. Very slowly, deliberately, the quartermaster raised one finger to her lips.

Above them, through the oculus, the thirteenth moon slid into perfect alignment with the orrery's axis, and the light that fell through the temple's bones was not silver anymore but white, white as a page, white as an unwritten ledger, and every ring of the engine was turning now, and August's voice cracked on a syllable that Ilara did not know but felt like a knife laid flat against her sternum.

And from the tower stair behind her — from the direction of the sealed transcription room that no living person should have been able to leave — came the sound of running feet.

* * *

**Veyren**

The third ring turned, and Master Veyren lifted his arms, and the acolytes rose with him like a congregation at the elevation, and he began the invocation his predecessors had written two hundred years before his birth — the words of renewal, of the wall coming down, of the world made whole.

He did not see the bell tower door open. He did not see a figure without boots come down the vault stair two steps at a time, copper-greased and ink-stained and lit from behind by thirteen moons' worth of impossible light. He did not see Sefa's hand come out of her coat, or Ilara's turn from the gallery rail, or anything at all, because at that moment the orrery's innermost ring — the one the founders had sealed, the one no document in the archive described — began to turn on its own, and the word-shaped hole in the engine's song opened wide as a door, and the whole vault leaned toward the child in the white oak chair like a congregation leaning toward its god.

And the child stopped fighting.

August went still in the cords. Looked — not at Veyren, not at the rings, but past all of it, toward the stair, toward the running feet — and smiled. A small, mud-stained, missing-one-shoe smile, the smile of a child who has counted the turns and found one more than the arithmetic allowed.

The vault door banged open.

The song held its breath.

And a voice that no ledger had ever recorded, in a tongue older than every wall in the world, began to speak.
