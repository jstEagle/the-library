## 34. Two Doors, One Choice

The Veil did not tear so much as hesitate.

That was the first thing Oscar understood, standing in the vault with the orrery screaming light around them: the boundary between worlds was not a wall at all. It was a held breath. And it had been holding for a thousand years, and now, under the thirteenth moon, it was exhaling.

The chamber had become a place without edges. The great brass rings of the moon-engine turned in slow, grinding orbits overhead, each one etched with names — thousands of them, tens of thousands, generations of scribes who had never known what their signatures fed. Light poured through the rift above the central dais like water through a cracked dam, and where it fell, the stone forgot it was stone. It rippled. It remembered being sky.

August stood bound at the center of it all.

Not with rope — Veyren was too clever for rope. The child was bound in ledger-lines, ribbons of vellum that wound from wrist to wrist to throat, each strip covered in writing, each word a small ownership. *Property of the Guild of Impossible Things. Asset. Instrument. Key.* August's eyes were open, and they were not afraid, and somehow that was worse than fear would have been.

"Oscar." Veyren's voice cut through the engine's song, and it had lost nothing of its warmth. That was the horror of Master Veyren; he could be warm while the world came apart. He stood at the base of the dais, robes scorched at the hem where Sefa's poison had eaten through the engine's heart and failed to kill it entirely, his hands raised as though in blessing. "Oscar, listen to me. There is still a right way to end this night."

Behind him, Ilara Vance stood with her crossbow drawn, the bolt leveled at a point six inches from Oscar's heart. Her hands were steady. Her eyes were not. Blood ran from her hairline where a falling ring-bracket had caught her during the fight, tracing a line down her jaw like a signature she hadn't chosen.

And on the floor between them all, half-curled against the dais steps, Sefa bled. She had driven her knife into the engine's heart-chamber and the heart-chamber had answered, a whipcrack of light that had opened her side from hip to ribs. She was pressing both palms against the wound and laughing — actually laughing, low and ragged — because of course she was. Sefa had always known the price of everything. She'd simply gambled it would be someone else who paid.

"You're bleeding out," Oscar said to her, and their voice sounded strange to them, unpracticed, as though they hadn't used it for anything but transcription in years.

"I'm aware," Sefa said through her teeth. "I keep excellent accounts. Do the math later."

"The math," Veyren said gently, "is very simple."

He turned, and Oscar followed his gaze upward, into the half-open Veil.

Through the rift, the other realm showed itself in slivers. Not fire. Not the hellscape the Guild's sermons promised. Something stranger: a country of tall pale grass under a sun that hung too low and too red, and shapes moving in the grass — the First Speakers, the exiled ones, some of them watching the rift with an expression Oscar could only call *hope*, and others already turning away, shielding faces, as if they had learned long ago that hope was the cruelest thing the other side could send.

"They are waiting," Veyren said. "They have been waiting since before your guild existed, before my name, before yours. And when the door opens fully — when August speaks the last phrase and the thirteenth moon pulls the Veil apart like wet paper — there will be war. You know this. Everyone knows this. It is the oldest story we have." He stepped closer, and Ilara's bolt tracked him without her seeming to notice, which told Oscar everything about how much control she still had. "Unless."

"Unless," Oscar repeated.

"Unless the door is closed *tonight*. Sealed. Permanently." His voice dropped into the register he used for donors and dignitaries, the one that made lies sound like gifts. "You hold the key now, scribe. The engine answers you — I watched it answer you. Speak the closing phrase. Bind the Veil shut forever, and the war never comes. No border raids. No burning towns. No children dragged across in the night. A thousand years of peace, bought with one sentence." He smiled, and it was almost tender. "And I will burn every page of your debt myself. Tonight. Before witnesses. You will walk out of here owing nothing to anyone."

The words landed in Oscar's chest like coins dropped down a well.

They had wanted those words for eleven years. They had lain awake counting the days left on their indenture the way other people counted coins, and here was Veyren offering the whole sum at once, freely, *kindly*, and all it cost was sealing a people on the far side of a dying sky forever. All it cost was telling the universe a lie and making the lie load-bearing.

Because that was the truth of it, and Oscar knew it now, knew it in the ink-stained marrow of them: the First Speakers were not invaders. They were refugees. August had shown them — through the rift, in fragments, in the language older than walls — a city of exiles who begged only not to be remembered wrong. To seal the Veil with the closing phrase was to write *monster* over every one of them in letters no one could ever edit. It was the finest forgery imaginable. It would be believed forever.

It was exactly the kind of entry Oscar had spent a lifetime being paid to make.

"You're asking me to falsify the record," Oscar said quietly.

"I'm asking you to save the world."

"You're asking me to falsify the record," Oscar said again, louder, and the engine hummed around the words as if tasting them. "You've been doing it for three hundred years, Master. The thirteenth moon. The charter. August's name written before August was born. Every impossible thing this Guild has ever accomplished was a lie somebody wrote down carefully enough." They looked up at the turning rings, at the names. "I just never thought you'd ask me to forge the sky."

Veyren's warmth flickered — one heartbeat, two — and then returned, but changed, colder underneath. "Ilara," he said.

"Don't," Ilara said.

"She is about to doom—"

"*Don't.*" The word cracked out of her like a bowstring. The bolt wavered, found Oscar's heart again, steadied. Her voice, when it came, was lower. "Oscar. Look at me."

Oscar looked at her. Eleven years since the borderlands, since the night they'd parted with a promise neither of them had kept, and here she was at the end of the world with blood on her face and a weapon in her hands and duty wearing her like a coat two sizes too small.

"If you close it," she said, "the killing stops. Whatever they are — whatever they want — the killing *stops*, Osc. I've stood on the border. I've seen what comes through when the Veil thins. I've carried the bodies." Her jaw tightened. "And if you open it, and I'm wrong about them, then everything I've ever done is for nothing, and everyone I've ever buried died for a door."

"And if you're right about them," Oscar said, "then everyone they buried died for a wall."

Silence, except for the engine, except for the slow creak of brass, except for Sefa's breathing going thin and wet at the edges.

"That's not an answer," Ilara whispered.

"No," Oscar agreed. "It's a question. It's been the question the whole time. And the Guild's answer has always been to write the question out of the book."

* * *

From the dais, August watched them argue, and understood almost none of the words, and understood all of it.

The First Tongue did not have a word for *politics*. It had a word for *the space between two truths where people decide what will hurt less*. That was what filled the vault now. The man in fine robes wanted the door closed because closed doors kept him important. The woman with the weapon wanted it closed because closed doors kept her grief from meaning nothing. And Oscar —

August had taught Oscar the phrase that opened the Veil. Had given it over syllable by syllable in the dark of the archive, knowing what it cost, feeling the language take root in another person for the first time in a thousand years. And now Oscar stood holding it the way one holds a live coal, and August felt something they had no word for either, in any tongue: the terror of having handed someone a key and realizing you cannot follow them through the lock.

The vellum bindings bit their wrists. The engine drank at their voice even in silence, pulling at the First Tongue in their chest like tide pulling at sand. If Oscar spoke the closing phrase, the bindings would release — the engine needed the key alive and willing — and August would live, and the grass-country would go dark behind a sealed sky, and the old ones singing in the tall grass would sing themselves quiet over a few more centuries and then stop.

If Oscar spoke the true binding — the treaty-phrase, the one August had only half-taught because August had only half-learned it, from a memory of a memory, from a promise made by someone wearing Oscar's face in a dream — then the Veil would become something new. Neither wall nor gate. A border. With doors. With terms. With all the endless, exhausting, ordinary work of two peoples learning to knock.

It might fail. It probably would fail. Treaties were fragile things; August had seen a hundred of them rot in the memory-fragments, seen the words of agreement curdle back into war within a generation. The closing phrase was certain. Certain and wrong.

*Choose the certain wrong thing,* August thought at Oscar, with all the force the First Tongue could put behind a thought. *Or choose the uncertain right one. But choose it honestly. Please. Whatever else you do tonight — don't let it be a forgery.*

Oscar turned and looked at them.

And August saw that they had heard. Not the thought — the look. The way Oscar's eyes went soft and terrified and resolved all at once, the way a scribe's eyes look when they finally see what the page has been trying to tell them.

"August," Oscar said aloud, and the name rang strangely in the engine-light, and the vellum bindings shivered. "When I taught you to lie — do you remember what I told you first?"

August's mouth twitched. In the middle of the ending of the world. *A good lie is mostly true.*

"So here is the truth," Oscar said, turning back to face Veyren, facing Ilara, facing the rift and the red sun and the watching grass. "The whole truth, no forgery in it. The Veil should never have been built. Your Guild built it, Veyren — your predecessors, your charter, your engine, your thirteenth moon. Everything on the other side is there because we put it there, and everything we were told about them is a story we wrote to make ourselves the heroes." Their voice rose, and the engine rose with it, the rings accelerating, light climbing. "I spent my whole life writing down other people's versions of things. I'm done. I'm done being the hand that holds the pen for liars."

"Then you choose war," Veyren said, and all the warmth was gone now, burned away, and what remained was the cold machinery beneath, the thing that had moved moons. "Ilara. Shoot."

Ilara didn't shoot.

Her arms trembled — the first time Oscar had ever seen them tremble — and the bolt's point drifted, a finger-width, two, and then she did something worse than shooting or not shooting. She lowered the crossbow entirely, and she said, in a voice scraped raw:

"Tell me the truth one more time. Just you. Just to me. And then do whatever you're going to do."

Oscar held her gaze. "They're refugees, Ilara. They're scared, and they're starving, and they've been locked out of the sky for a thousand years by men like him. Some of them are angry. Some of them are dangerous. And all of them deserve better than a wall built on a lie." A breath. "That's the truth. All of it. Even the parts that frighten me."

Ilara closed her eyes. When she opened them, she stepped aside. Out of the line. Out of the order. Out of the story Veyren had written for her.

"Master Vance—" Veyren began.

"My commission says guard the asset," she said flatly. "There is no asset. There's a kid. And there's a scribe doing the bravest thing I've ever watched anyone do. Find someone else to shoot them. I'm done being your pen."

* * *

Sefa laughed again from the floor, wetter now, weaker. "Oh, that's beautiful. That's going in my memoirs." She coughed. "Veyren. Sweetheart. Check your heart-chamber."

Veyren froze.

Slowly, he turned toward the engine's core — the great crystal heart at the mechanism's center, the one Sefa's knife had found, the one everyone assumed her poison had failed to kill. It was glowing. But the glow was wrong: mottled, stuttering, eating itself. Her poison hadn't failed. It had simply been patient. It had waited, as Sefa did everything, for the moment of maximum inconvenience.

"What did you do?" Veyren breathed.

"Made sure that whatever happens next," Sefa said, grinning through the blood, "happens *fast*. Engine's dying either way, Master. So is the moon-cycle. So is your window. Seal it or speak it or stand there sermonizing — but the door is only going to be open for about a minute more, and after that it's closed for a century, and I won't be here to see which kind of coward you turn out to be." Her grin faded into something almost gentle. "Neither will I. Worth it. Best purchase I ever made."

The engine groaned. Above them, the outermost ring shuddered, slowed, and stopped. Then the next. The rift overhead flickered like a guttering lamp, and through it, faintly, impossibly, they all heard it — singing. Thin and high and desperate, from the tall grass of the other world. Voices begging, in a language only two people in the room could understand, not to be remembered wrong.

Oscar walked to the dais.

Veyren lunged — actually lunged, dignity abandoned, grabbing for Oscar's arm — and Ilara caught him, not with the crossbow but with her body, hauling him back, the two of them grappling like ordinary mortals at the foot of the machine while the extraordinary thing happened above them.

Oscar took August's bound wrists in their ink-stained hands. The vellum writhed against their fingers, whispering its little legal horrors — *property, asset, instrument* — and Oscar spoke, not the opening phrase, not yet, but the counter-word, the scribe's word, the one that means *amended*: a single syllable of the First Tongue that August had given them in the archive, that meant *this record is corrected*.

The bindings fell away like burnt paper.

August looked up at them, and for a moment neither of them said anything at all, because there was nothing left to teach and nothing left to learn, and the rift was dying above them, and the song was fading, and there was time for exactly one sentence.

"Together?" August asked, in the First Tongue, in the old words, in the language that predated kingdoms and debts and lies.

"Together," Oscar answered — and reached for the true binding, the treaty-phrase, the uncertain right thing, the hardest sentence ever set down in a living tongue.

The last ring began to fall.

Below them, Veyren screamed something — a name, maybe, or an order, or a prayer; no one would ever agree which — and Ilara held on, and Sefa pressed her hands to her wound and watched the door of the world hang balanced between wall and gate, and Oscar drew the breath that would cost them everything, and spoke.
