## 35. The Unbinding

The Veil stood half-open before me, and it was the most beautiful and most terrible thing I had ever seen.

It did not look like a wall torn down. It looked like a curtain of night that someone had gathered in one fist and pulled aside — a seam of darkness stretched taut from the vault floor to the orrery's crown, humming with a sound I felt in my teeth before I heard it with my ears. Through the gap, light moved. Not sunlight. Not moonlight. A slow, tidal gold, like the last hour of dusk stretched into forever, and shapes within it that might have been towers or trees or the shoulders of something vast turning over in its sleep.

August knelt at the base of the engine, wrists bound in guild-iron, and looked up at me with eyes that held no fear anymore. Only a question.

*Which words will you say?*

I could feel both phrases resting on my tongue like two stones in my mouth. August had taught them to me over weeks of stolen lessons, syllable by syllable, my clumsy scribe's accent softened by their patient corrections. The first was a sealing — a lie dressed as mercy. *There is nothing beyond. There was never anything beyond. Sleep.* It would close the Veil and fill the other side with a forgetting so complete that even the First Speakers would forget themselves. Peace, purchased with erasure. The same currency the Guild had traded in for three hundred years.

The second was the true binding. And it would cost everything I had.

"Oscar." Master Veyren's voice came to me across the vault, stripped of its sermon-warmth now, raw underneath. He stood near the orrery's rim with his robes scorched where Sefa's poison had eaten through the engine's heart and spat fire back at him. His hands were raised, palms out, the gesture of a man approaching a frightened animal. "Oscar Reyes. Listen to me. You are a scribe. You know what happens to words spoken carelessly."

"I know exactly what happens to words," I said. My voice sounded strange to me — not because it shook, but because it didn't. "They get written down. They get owned. They get forged and filed and used against the people who spoke them. I have spent eleven years learning that lesson, Master. You taught it to me yourself."

"The Veil has held for a thousand years—"

"The Veil has *lied* for a thousand years." The words came out of me with a force that surprised us both. Somewhere behind me, Sefa made a small sound — she was slumped against the orrery's base, one hand pressed to her side where a shard of the poisoned keystone had caught her when the engine convulsed. Alive. Breathing. I made myself look away from her; there would be time to bleed later, all of us together. "You built a thirteenth moon out of brass and ambition and called it stewardship. You bought a child before he was born and called it prophecy. Every wall in this world is a lie someone decided was load-bearing, and you are asking me to add another stone."

"I am asking you to save the world."

"No," said August.

The child's voice carried through the vault the way water carries through stone — not loud, but everywhere at once. Even bound, even kneeling, August spoke the way the First Tongue demanded: as if the words had existed first and the mouth merely agreed to carry them.

"You are asking him to choose which world is real," August said. "And only one of us gets to be wrong."

* * *

Ilara's crossbow was trained on my chest.

I could see her without turning fully — a pale line of her in my peripheral vision, standing on the stair that led up out of the vault, her commission still hanging in tatters from her belt where she'd torn it at the canal gate days ago. She should not have been here. She had chosen not to be here. But the Guild had re-sworn her, or she had re-sworn herself, and now the bolt point followed my heartbeat with the steadiness of a woman who had learned my body's rhythms in another life, under different stars.

"Oscar." Her voice was low, meant for me alone beneath the hum of the opening Veil. "Whatever you're going to do. Do it now. I can't hold this position and mean it much longer."

"You could lower it."

"I could do a great many things." Her jaw tightened. "Lowering it isn't one of them yet. Say your words, scribe."

There it was again — that word. *Scribe.* Eleven years it had been my whole name, my cell number, my debt, my cage. And I understood, standing in the light of a world that was not supposed to exist, that it was also the answer. Not the chains of it. The craft.

Because that was what the true binding was. Not a spell. Not a prayer. A *document.*

August had told me once, in the dark of the archive stacks, breath fogging between us: *The First Tongue doesn't describe things. It agrees to them.* A wall was an agreement that nothing crossed. A gate was an agreement that some things crossed and others didn't, and someone got to decide who. Both were contracts written by one side and imposed on the other. Both were lies of a kind — the lie of the wall being that the other side was empty, the lie of the gate being that passage was a gift rather than a right.

But a treaty. A treaty was an agreement both parties signed while awake.

I stepped forward, toward the seam of night and gold, and heard Ilara's breath catch, heard the crossbow's string creak as her aim adjusted. Veyren shouted something — my name, my debt, threats braided with pleas — but his voice was already growing distant, the way sounds grow distant underwater, because the Veil's opening was widening around me like a pupil dilating, and the light from beyond was touching my face, and it was warm.

Warm. After all the years of cold ledgers and colder stone, the first thing the other world gave me was warmth.

* * *

I began to speak.

Not the sealing. Not yet. First I spoke the language itself — the way August had taught me, the way the dying cartographer's map had whispered it in its thin brown lines, the way the ink in my transcription had rearranged itself those many nights ago, meaning flowing beneath sound like current beneath ice. The First Tongue did not come out of my throat so much as through it. I felt it pass through me the way light passes through glass, altering nothing, arriving changed anyway.

*I see you,* I said to the ones beyond the Veil, the exiled First Speakers, the refugees begging not to be remembered wrong. *We were told you were monsters. We wrote you as monsters. The writing was ours and the lie was ours and I am here to unwrite it.*

The gold light shivered. Something on the far side pressed closer — not threateningly, the way a crowd presses closer to hear.

*You are not empty,* I said, and each word landed in the vault like a struck bell. *You were never empty. The wall was built on the claim that you were nothing, and every year the claim was renewed, and every year the forgetting grew deeper, until your names survived only in one child's memory and one scribe's ink.*

Behind me, Veyren's voice cracked upward into something almost human: "Stop. Stop — you don't know what you're unmaking—"

"Yes I do," I said, in plain Low Speech, without turning around. "For the first time in my life, I know *exactly* what I'm unmaking."

Then I returned to the First Tongue, and I spoke the binding.

*Let there be neither wall nor gate. Let there be a border — a line drawn in honesty, visible to both sides, crossed by consent, guarded by neither fear nor hunger but by memory. Let what is written here be written in living language, so that it cannot be forged, cannot be filed, cannot be owned. Let every door in it open both ways. Let every door remember who opened it, and why.*

The words left me and did not dissipate. That was the difference — the thing no ledger had ever taught me, the thing the Guild had spent centuries trying to capture and compress into mechanisms and moons. Spoken words in our tongue die in the air. But the First Tongue *persisted.* Each phrase hung in the vault after I finished it, glowing faintly at the edge of vision, and then it moved — drifting toward the seam of the Veil like sparks rising, settling into the darkness the way words settle onto a page.

The Veil began to write itself.

I watched it happen and I wept without deciding to. The great dark curtain that had divided two worlds for a thousand years took my sentences into its substance and rearranged itself around their meaning. Where the seam had been a wound, it became a margin. Where the gap had been a breach, it became a doorway — and then more doorways, unfolding along the border's whole invisible length, doors of varying sizes, some tall as cathedral arches, some small as the wicket-gate in a garden wall, each one framed in that slow tidal gold. Not one gate to be fought over. Not one wall to be defended. A thousand thresholds, each one an agreement, each one bearing, if you looked closely, a faint script along its frame that I recognized as my own handwriting, translated into a language older than handwriting.

A treaty written in living language. And living language, unlike dead ink, does not sit still on the page. It breathes. It revises. It holds its signatories accountable.

The vault filled with light.

* * *

Veyren was screaming.

I turned at last and saw him at the rim of the orrery, and the sight of him broke something in me that I suspect will never fully mend — not pity, exactly, and not satisfaction either, but the terrible clarity of watching a man meet the document he spent his life forging. Because the treaty was reading *him.* The living script along the nearest doorframe had extended itself, gently, inevitably, the way ivy finds a wall, and it was winding through the air toward the master of the Guild of Impossible Things, and it was asking him, in the only language that cannot be lied in, to sign.

To sign was to be remembered truly. All of it. The ambition and the almanac and the child purchased before birth. To refuse was to remain outside the agreement — and outside the agreement, I understood now, there was no longer any ground to stand on. The old Veil was gone. The old order of walls and secrets and owned names had been unwritten. A man could not live in a sentence that had been erased.

"Reyes." He was looking at me, and his eyes were the eyes I had followed through eleven years of indenture — the eyes that had taught me letters, that had praised my finest forgery, that had offered me freedom priced at a child's life. "Reyes, please. I built this. Centuries of work. My predecessors, my masters, the whole long chain of us — we kept the world safe, we *kept* it, and you would hand it to strangers for the sake of a pretty idea—"

"They're not strangers," I said. "They're neighbors. They always were. You just never let them introduce themselves."

The script reached him.

What happened next happened quickly, and I will not pretend I saw all of it clearly — grief blurs the edges of even the moments we most need sharp. Veyren raised his hands, whether to sign or to strike I never learned, and the living words closed around him like water closing over a dropped stone, and the nearest door — the great one, the first one, the threshold directly before the orrery — opened wide, and the light beyond it pulled.

He fell backward into the gold. Or walked. Or was taken. From where I stood, bound August at my feet and bleeding Sefa at my side and Ilara frozen on the stairs with her crossbow lowered at last, too late, forever too late and exactly on time — from where I stood, it looked like a man stepping into the margin of his own unfinished page.

The door did not close behind him. That was the treaty's mercy and its judgment in a single stroke: the door stayed open. Whatever waited for Veyren on the far side of it, he would meet it honestly, remembered truly, with nowhere left to forge himself smaller than he was.

His scream lasted longer than he did.

* * *

Silence, afterward. The good kind — the silence of a room after a bell stops ringing, when the air still remembers the shape of the sound.

I went to August first. The guild-iron bonds had gone slack — the treaty does not recognize ownership, and shackles are, in the end, only a very blunt form of handwriting — and the child stood, rubbing their wrists, looking up at the thousand doors with an expression I had never seen on them before. Not wonder. Wonder they had plenty of. This was something quieter. Recognition. Like a traveler seeing, after decades, the coastline of home.

"It worked," August said, in Low Speech, deliberately, the way one shows off a borrowed coat. Then, softer, in the First Tongue: *You said it true. All of it. Even the hard parts.*

"The hard parts were the only parts worth saying."

Across the vault, Sefa laughed — a wet, pained laugh, one hand still pressed to her side, the other already, characteristically, reaching for the ledger she kept inside her coat. "Someone," she rasped, "is going to have to write all this down. Formally. With witnesses. Treaties have protocols, scribe, even living ones, and I'll be damned if I let the Guild's successors botch the filing."

"You're bleeding."

"I'm *bleeding and correct.* Note the distinction. It matters in negotiations." Her grin cost her something; I saw her wince through it. "Get over here and put pressure on this before I draft you into service as a bandage."

I tore a strip from my sleeve — the sleeve with the guild sigil stitched at the cuff, eleven years of indenture woven into the thread — and knelt beside her and pressed it to the wound, and she hissed, and swore at me with real affection, and above us the doors of the new border glowed on in their patient gold, holding their shape, holding their words, holding.

Ilara came down the stairs slowly. She stopped a few feet away, crossbow slung, face unreadable in the way I knew meant it was reading everything at once. The light from the nearest door caught the tear in her commission — the ragged halves she'd knotted back together at her belt, because even desertion, for Ilara, had to be documented.

"So," she said.

"So," I said.

"That was the First Tongue."

"That was the First Tongue."

"You spoke it better than I speak my oath." Her voice did something complicated on the last word. She looked away, toward the doors, toward the gold, toward the shapes moving there — the First Speakers coming to the thresholds now, hesitant as deer at a forest edge, pressing their palms flat against frames that warmed at their touch, reading the treaty that named them truly. *Neighbors. Remembered. Welcome by consent.* One of them — tall, silver-voiced, wearing robes the color of dusk — looked through the open door directly at me, and inclined their head, and I understood that somewhere in the living script my own words were recorded, including the confession that the monster-story had been ours.

"We'll need translators," Ilara said finally. "Border houses. Hundreds of them, if the doors keep doing—" she gestured at the thousand thresholds, "—that. Someone will have to stand in the doorways and make sure nobody's words get... owned."

The word landed between us like a key finding its lock.

She was offering me a future. Not freedom — the treaty had already given me that, though I didn't yet know its full price. She was offering me a *place.* A doorway. Herself on one side of it, maybe, if the years were kind and the guilt negotiable.

"Sefa needs a physician," I said, because I could not yet say yes, and could not bear to say no, and because Ilara deserved better than either. "And August needs sleep. And someone should tell the town below that the sky has an extra moon that belongs to everyone now."

Ilara nodded slowly. "One thing at a time, scribe."

"Not a scribe," I said. "Not anymore. The treaty took the title with the rest of it — you'll see. No ledger can find me now. No record owns me. I am, as of tonight, the only free person in either world, and it cost me my name to buy it."

"Then we'll give you a new one," said August, tugging at my ruined sleeve, pointing up at the nearest door and the faint script along its frame. "Look. It's already started."

And I looked, and saw, glowing in the living language along the border's first threshold, a single word where my signature should have been. Not *Oscar.* Not *Reyes* — that surname was already fading from every ledger in the world, dissolving like sugar in rain, and I could feel it going, the strange lightness of debts lifting and history thinning all at once. The word on the door was older than both. It was the First Tongue's word for the thing I had done and would keep doing, the thing the treaty itself was made of:

*Teller-of-truths-across-the-line.*

Below us, in the town, the bells had begun to ring — all of them, unscheduled, unhurried, the whole city waking to a sky with thirteen moons and a horizon full of open doors. And in the gold light of the nearest threshold, the tall First Speaker beckoned, and August took my hand, and somewhere behind me Ilara exhaled like a woman setting down a weight she had carried for years.

The border was open. The treaty was alive. And the first person to step through the first door was about to ask me, in a language I had only just learned, a question I had no ledger prepared to answer.
