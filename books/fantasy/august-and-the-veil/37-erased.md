## 37. Erased

The treaty did not end so much as settle, the way dust settles after a door is slammed in a far room.

Oscar stood at the center of the vault with the orrery still turning above them — thirteen moons of brass and bone-light wheeling through their impossible arithmetic — and felt the words they had spoken continue to move through the world like water through sand. The First Tongue did not stop when the mouth stopped. It went on. It was going on now, threading itself into every ledger in every archive in every city where the Guild had ever kept an account.

And it was taking their name with it.

They felt the first loss as a small coldness behind the sternum, the way one feels the exact moment a candle goes out in another room — not the darkness itself but the knowledge of it. Somewhere below them, in the deep stacks, a page was going blank. Oscar could not see it, but they knew it the way a scribe knows paper: by weight, by absence, by the particular silence of ink lifting from vellum and forgetting what it had been written to say.

*Reyes,* said the treaty, in the language before kingdoms. *Gone.*

The orrery shuddered. One of its thirteen moons — the false one, the engineered one, the moon the Guild had hung in the sky like a hook baited with a child — dimmed and folded inward on itself, brass becoming geometry becoming nothing. The mechanism beneath the temple, centuries in the building, exhaled once and went still. In the sudden quiet Oscar heard Sefa cough somewhere near the shattered keystone housing, heard Ilara's boots on the stair, heard August breathing beside them — quick, alive, unbound.

"Is it done?" August asked. Their voice was hoarse. They had been screaming, earlier; everyone had been screaming, though the screams had all been different kinds. Veyren's had been the worst, because his had sounded like prayer.

"It's done," Oscar said. "The Veil is a border now. There are doors."

"And you?"

Oscar opened their mouth to answer and found, to their astonishment, that they did not know.

---

The ledger had been with them for eleven years. It was a small thing, grey leather gone soft at the corners, its spine cracked in the shape of the shelf it had lived on. Every debt Oscar Reyes owed the Guild of Impossible Things was recorded in it in Master Veyren's own hand: the cost of the fever that had nearly killed them at nine years old, the price of the roof over their head, the interest that compounded like ivy, patient and green and strangling. Eleven years Oscar had balanced those accounts, page after page, subtracting years of their life in neat columns, watching the number of remaining indentured days shrink with the slow cruelty of a tide going out.

Now they sat on the vault steps with that ledger open across their knees, and the pages were blank.

Not torn. Not burned. Blank — and worse than blank, because blank pages have edges, have history, have the ghost-ridges of pen pressure pressed into the paper. These pages were smooth. They were smooth the way a river stone is smooth, as if no pen had ever touched them, as if the paper itself had been born yesterday and had never known a name. Oscar ran their thumb across the first leaf and felt nothing. No indentation. No memory in the fiber.

Their debt was gone. Which meant, by the oldest law of the Guild — older than Veyren, older than the charter, older perhaps than the Veil itself — that they owed nothing. That they belonged to no one. That they were free.

They should have wept. They had imagined this moment for eleven years, had rehearsed it in the dark of the scriptorium with the kind of shameful precision that only the indebted permit themselves: the walking out, the door, the air. And here it was, handed to them not by a warden's mercy but by a language older than ownership, and all Oscar could feel was the smoothness of the paper under their thumb and a grief so strange it had no shape yet.

"Your surname," Sefa said from the doorway. She was leaning against the frame with her arms crossed, and there was ash in her hair and blood drying at her temple where the engine's housing had caught her, and she was looking at Oscar the way she looked at accounts that refused to balance. "Say it."

"Reyes," Oscar said. "Oscar Reyes."

Sefa waited. Then, slowly, she frowned — not at Oscar, but inward, the frown of a woman consulting a ledger inside her own head and finding the column empty. "Huh," she said. "That's odd. I've known you six years. I know your handwriting better than my own. I know you take your tea bitter enough to strip varnish." She tapped her temple. "But I can't — there was a second name. There was always a second name. It went with yours like — " She snapped her fingers twice, hunting for the word, and the word wasn't there. "Like ink goes with a pen. And it's just gone. Like a tooth. You keep running your tongue over the gap."

Oscar looked down at the ledger again. At the smooth pages. At the place on the flyleaf where, in Veyren's hand, *Property of the Guild of Impossible Things: O. R——* had been written eleven years ago, the surname abbreviated because even then the Guild had considered it barely worth the ink.

The flyleaf was smooth too.

"I think," Oscar said quietly, "the treaty took everything. Not just the debt. Everything the ledgers held about me. Because I spoke it, and the treaty reads names, and my name was — " They stopped. Started again. "My name was mostly debt. That's what I was, on paper. Eleven years of owing. So it took the paper."

"And left the person," Sefa said. She pushed off the doorframe and came to sit beside them on the step, which she had never done in six years, and did not look at them while she did it, which was how Sefa said things that mattered. "You realize what you are now? You're the only free thing in this building. Maybe in this city. The Guild can't bill you. Can't bind you. Can't even *spell* you." A short, harsh laugh. "I spent twenty years trying to steal my way out of my own contract, and you talked yourself loose in one sentence."

"It wasn't one sentence."

"No," Sefa agreed. "It was thirty-one years of them. But it came out as one." She stood, brushed off her apron, and paused at the door. "Veyren's people will want to rebuild. There'll be forms. There'll be new ledgers." Her eyes flicked to the blank book in Oscar's lap, and something moved across her face that might have been envy, or blessing, or both wearing the same coat. "Don't sign anything."

Then she was gone, and Oscar sat alone on the steps of the dead engine with a blank ledger and no name worth writing.

---

They surfaced into a guild transformed.

The great hall, where Veyren had preached holy renewal to a crowd hungry for the thirteenth moon, smelled of smoke and wet ash. Clerks moved through it in stunned processions, carrying armfuls of records that had stopped being records overnight. Oscar passed the debt registry and saw a junior clerk standing before an entire wall of drawers, pulling one open, staring, closing it, pulling the next. Every drawer, apparently. Every drawer in the registry held something erased — not only Oscar. The treaty had been thorough in ways no one had intended. Debts forgiven. Contracts dissolved. Names lifted from obligations like stones lifted from a field, leaving the soil turned and strange and ready for planting.

"They're calling it the Unwriting," the clerk whispered as Oscar passed, and then blinked, and frowned, and looked at Oscar with the expression of someone trying to remember why a stranger seemed familiar. "Have we — do I know you?"

"You don't," Oscar said, and it was true, and it was the loneliest true thing they had ever said aloud.

In the scriptorium, the transcription of August's words lay where it had always lain, chained to the reading desk because Veyren had never trusted anything he owned to stay where he put it. The chain was still locked. The pages inside were white as snowfall. Months of work — every syllable Oscar had coaxed from the child, every rearrangement of living ink, every night-lesson transcribed by candlelight — smoothed away into paper that had never been written on. Oscar unlocked the chain anyway, out of habit, out of tenderness for the work, and held the blank leaves up to the window light.

For a moment — half a heartbeat, no more — they thought they saw something move beneath the surface of the paper. Not ink. Not quite. A shimmer, like fish under ice, like meaning swimming somewhere deeper than the fibers could reach. The First Tongue did not live on pages. It had only ever been *lodging* there. And perhaps, Oscar thought, lowering the leaves, some part of it remained lodged in them too — in the shapes their hands remembered making, in words that rose to their lips in a language no record could now attribute to anyone.

They set the transcription down and walked out of the scriptorium without looking back.

---

Ilara found them at dusk on the temple roof, where the sky was doing its ordinary miracle of turning over, the thirteenth moon already a fading rumor among the stars — visible now only as a slightly wrong patch of dark, a hole shaped like a moon that the eye kept trying to fill in.

She didn't speak at first. She came and stood at the parapet beside them, close enough that their sleeves touched, and together they watched the city below, where in the border districts — the thin places, the old thin places — lights were appearing that had no business existing. Doors standing open where walls had been. Figures moving in thresholds, tall and strange and careful, the First Speakers coming through into a world that had spent centuries telling stories about how monstrous they were, and finding, door by door, that the stories had been wrong.

"They won't remember me either," Ilara said at last. "Will they. The ledgers. My commission — the report I filed on you, the transfer orders, the — " Her jaw tightened. "There was a file. On us. On — " She made a frustrated sound. "I can see the shape of it. I can feel where it was. Like a scar that doesn't hurt anymore but you can still find it with your fingers."

"The treaty took what the ledgers held," Oscar said. "What you hold is yours. What I hold is mine." They hesitated. "Do you want me to tell you what was in it?"

Ilara was quiet for a long time. Below them, a First Speaker crossed a courtyard in the border district, moving with the unhurried grace of someone who has finally been allowed to walk in daylight, and a child ran alongside them, laughing, in two languages.

"No," Ilara said finally. "No. I want — " She turned, and in the last light her face was the face Oscar had loved across a canal gate with a torn commission in her hands, and older now by one terrible choice. "I want to write it again. New. Whatever it is. Not restored. *New.*" She reached out and took Oscar's hand — the scribe's hand, the ink-stained hand, the hand that had spoken a world into honesty — and pressed it once, hard, and let go. "But not tonight. Tonight I have to stand in front of the Council and explain why the Guild's greatest asset is a blank wall of paper, and I'd rather do it knowing there's something after. Something unwritten." A ghost of her old crooked smile. "You'd know about that."

She left. Oscar stayed until the stars came all the way out and the wrong-shaped darkness where the thirteenth moon had been began, very slowly, to seem less like a wound and more like a window.

---

They slept that night in the scriptorium, on the cot they had slept on for eleven years, and dreamed of nothing at all — the first truly blank sleep of their adult life, no debts circling, no columns to balance, no countdown ticking beneath the dreams. Just rest, wide and white as the pages in the grey ledger.

In the morning, August was waiting for them at the guild doors.

The child looked different. Lighter, somehow, as if the founding charter's claim on them had been a weight worn so long its absence registered as a change in gravity. Their name — August, whole and intact, because the treaty had taken Oscar's name and left the child theirs, returned it to them like property recovered from a thief — was their own again, in every record and in no record at all, belonging to no one but the small fierce person who carried it.

"They're leaving today," August said. "The First Speakers. Going back through the doors, to the border town, where the Veil is thinnest. Some of them remember me. From before." They scuffed a boot against the threshold, suddenly shy, suddenly eight years old. "They asked if I would come. To teach. The children there — ours and theirs — they'll need both tongues. Someone who speaks the way I do." They looked up. "They asked if anyone would come with me."

Outside the guild doors, the morning was bright and rinsed-clean, the storm-washed streets shining, the city smelling of rain and bread and beginnings. Somewhere in the depths of the building behind them, clerks were already sharpening pens and ruling fresh lines in fresh ledgers, because the world does not stop needing records, and the Guild of Impossible Things does not stop being the Guild merely because its greatest impossibility has come true. There would be new contracts. New debts. New names to weigh and own.

But not Oscar's. Never again Oscar's. They were a person no ledger could hold now, no line could bind, no column could count — free in the most absolute and frightening sense, with nothing owed and nothing owned and nothing, anywhere in the world, written down to prove they had ever existed at all.

Except, perhaps, in a new book. With a blank first page.

"Wait for me," Oscar told August. "One hour. There's something I have to buy from the stationer on Candle Lane. A ledger." They smiled, and it felt strange on their face, unpracticed, like a word in a language they were only beginning to learn. "An empty one."

August grinned back, and ran out into the light, toward the border town and the doors and the children waiting in two tongues — and Oscar turned back into the guild one last time, to walk out through it as no one at all, which was, they were beginning to understand, another way of saying *everyone*.
