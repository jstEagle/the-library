## 38. The Border Town

The town had no name yet either.

That was the first thing August had learned to love about it. Every place on the far side of the Veil had been named so long ago that the names had worn smooth as river stones — *Vessia*, *Caldun*, the Nine Terraces of Orm — names people said without tasting them anymore. But this town, strung along the border like beads on a wet thread, was too new for smoothness. People still argued about what to call it. The First Speakers wanted *Sennavar*, which meant *the place where we waited*. The townsfolk from the mortal side wanted something plainer, something like *Crossing* or *Ford*. The children, who were the only ones whose opinions mattered in the end, had voted with chalk: they wrote both names on the well, one above the other, and let the rain decide which faded first.

Neither had faded. That was how August knew the treaty was holding.

They stood now at the window of the schoolhouse — it was not really a schoolhouse yet, only a granary with the grain moved out and benches carried in — and watched the morning come up over the border house. Mist lay along the Veil the way flour lies on bread, thick and white and smelling faintly of iron when the wind turned. Through the mist, if you knew where to look, you could see the doors. Not the old doors, the ones from before, the ones that had been gates and then walls and then wounds. These were new doors, made in the year since the Unbinding, built by carpenters from both sides who argued about hinges the way priests argue about heaven. There were seven of them along the border's length. Each one opened onto the other world and each one could be closed, but none of them locked, because locking had been the whole problem once, and everyone agreed — uneasily, honestly, the way the treaty asked them to agree — that there would be no more locks.

August pressed their palm flat against the cool glass and breathed on it until a small fog bloomed, and in the fog they drew the first sign of the First Tongue: the open hand, the sign that meant *I am here, and I mean no taking.*

It was the first sign they had ever taught anyone. It felt right to begin every morning with it, even when the only one listening was a window.

Behind them, the benches began to fill.

* * *

There were eleven children this season, which was more than last season and fewer than Sefa had promised, because Sefa promised everything optimistically and delivered everything exactly. Six came from the mortal side — farmers' children mostly, and two daughters of a ferryman who had lost his route when the river changed course after the Unbinding, as rivers do when two worlds agree to share a watershed. Five came from beyond the Veil: children of the returned, born in exile or carried through it small, who spoke the First Tongue the way their grandparents had spoken it, softly, in cellars, in case someone was listening who should not be.

No one listened who should not be. Not anymore. But fear outlives its reasons the way scars outlive their wounds, and August understood that better than anyone teaching here.

"Sit," August said, in both tongues at once, letting the words braid together the way the treaty had braided the worlds. "Sit, and show me your hands."

This was the lesson. It was always the lesson, though the children did not know it yet. In the First Tongue, meaning lived in the hands as much as the mouth — the open palm, the closed fist, the two fingers crossed at the second knuckle that meant *a debt freely owed*. You could not speak it lying down, the elders said, and you could not speak it while hiding your hands, and this, August suspected, was why the language had never been used to build an empire. It was very hard to conquer anyone with your palms turned up.

The ferryman's daughters held out their hands first, brown and chapped from rope-work. Then the others. Eleven pairs of hands, held out into the cold morning air of the granary-school, waiting.

"Good," August said. "Now. Yesterday we learned the word for *river*. Today we learn the word for *border*."

An older boy — Tam, the ferryman's eldest, who was twelve and thought he was too old for lessons — shifted on his bench. "Isn't it the same word?"

"No." August smiled. They had learned, these months, to love this question, because it was the question the whole world was asking. "Show me."

Tam frowned, held up his hands, and made the sign for *river*: both palms flowing past each other, never touching. Then he hesitated, and made it again, slower, and stopped halfway, his palms hovering an inch apart, not touching, not apart.

The granary went quiet.

"Yes," August said softly. "That's it. That's the word. It isn't the same word as *river*, but it lives next door to it. *Border* is what happens when two rivers agree to run side by side without becoming one water." They walked the length of the benches as they signed it, slow, so every child could steal the shape of it with their eyes. "Before, there was a wall where the border is. A wall is this—" they crossed their wrists, binding them, a hard ugly knot of a sign, and the children flinched a little, even the ones too young to remember the wall, because some signs hurt in the bones. "And a door is this—"

They made the sign. Open hand, turning, opening outward. The sign from the window. The first sign.

"And a border," August finished, "is a row of doors that have promised each other to stay honest."

One of the exiled children, a small girl named Issa with ash-pale hair, raised her hand — raised her actual hand, in the mortal fashion she'd learned from the ferryman's daughters, and August's chest went tight and warm at the sight of it, two customs learning to share one classroom.

"What's on the other side of the doors?" Issa asked. "Really. Not what the grown-ups say."

August crouched beside her bench. Up close, Issa smelled of woodsmoke and the particular sweetness of children who had crossed the Veil young and remembered it as a dream. "What do the grown-ups say?"

"They say it's empty. They say everyone left is already here." Issa's chin lifted, defiant, but her hands, folded in her lap, told the truth her mouth wouldn't: the fingers of her right hand were curled around her left wrist, the sign for *someone is missing*.

August looked at those hands for a long moment.

"Some are," they said at last. "Most are. But the First Tongue doesn't have a word for *everyone*, Issa. Did you know that? It only has words for *all who answer*. And every season, when the mist comes off the border in the morning, someone answers. Someone walks through one of the seven doors, blinking, carrying everything they own in a bundle, asking if it's true — if the war is truly over, if the Veil is truly a border now, if they can truly go home." They touched Issa's folded hands gently. "So the answer is: most of us are here. And the doors stay unlocked for the rest."

Issa nodded slowly. Her fingers loosened, just slightly, around her wrist.

Outside, the mist was lifting off the border house, and August could see the morning watch coming down the path — two figures, one from each world, walking the line together the way the treaty required. One was a broad woman with a surveyor's chain coiled over her shoulder; the other was tall and gray-feathered, one of the returned, a former warden of the old gates who had spent forty years guarding a wall and now spent his mornings knocking politely on doors to make sure they still swung free.

Both worlds, walking the same line. August watched them until the mist took them, and thought, not for the first time, about the person who had made all of it possible, and who could not be thanked, because thanking requires a name.

* * *

At midday they ate together, the way the treaty had accidentally taught everyone to eat.

It had happened in the first weeks, when food from the two worlds would not keep in the other's air — mortal bread went stale within hours beyond the Veil, and the pale fruit of the returned rotted overnight on mortal shelves. So at every meal, the tables had to be set twice, half on one side of the border line that ran through the long common hall, half on the other, and families ate facing each other across three feet of invisible seam, passing dishes back and forth through the doors. It was inefficient. It was absurd. The children loved it more than anything else in the world, because it meant every meal was also a game of catch.

August sat, as always, exactly on the line, on the one bench that had been built straddling it, because August belonged to neither world entirely and to both completely, and someone had to sit in the middle or the middle would be empty.

Today the dish being passed was stew, mortal barley and Veil-side herbs, which was allowed because herbs, unlike fruit, were diplomatic. Tam ladled it across the seam into August's bowl with the exaggerated care of a boy showing off.

"You're sitting on the border again," he said.

"I live here."

"Nobody lives *on* a border."

"The word says otherwise." August signed *border* — the two palms hovering, not touching, not apart — and then, wickedly, sat down upon their own hands, and Tam laughed so hard he nearly dropped the ladle through the seam, which would have been a diplomatic incident, or at least a wet one.

But later, when the bowls were cleared and the little ones lay stacked like puppies on the benches for their afternoon sleep, Issa crept back to August's bench on the line and sat down beside them, careful, the way you sit next to someone at a funeral.

"I had the dream again," she said.

August went still.

All the returned children had it, the elders said — the dream of the crossing, of the night the Veil became a border, of the voice that spoke the treaty into being. Most of them dreamed it as light, or as singing, or as the sensation of a great held breath finally let go. But Issa dreamed it with details, and the details were wrong, or rather, they were right in ways no one else's dreams were.

"There was a room," Issa whispered, "with circles inside circles, turning. And a man tied in front of them, shouting words that made my teeth ache. And someone else — I couldn't see their face — standing between him and the circles. And they said the words. The opening words. But different. Changed." She looked up, ash-pale hair falling across her eyes. "In the dream, everyone knows who they are. Everyone bows to them. But when I try to look, there's just — nothing. Like a page with the writing scraped off. Why would I dream about someone with no name?"

Because names can be spent, August thought. Because a name can be the price of a treaty, paid in full, from every ledger, forever. Because I owe my life, and the shape of every morning, and the unlocked doors, and the word for *border*, to a scribe who is now no one, who cannot be found, who cannot be thanked, who maybe cannot even be remembered —

"Because some people pay for peace with the only coin that can't be counted," August said aloud, "and the dreaming part of us knows it, even when the waking part forgets."

"That's sad."

"It's the truest kind of story there is." August put an arm around the girl's thin shoulders. "But listen. Dreams scrape things off pages too. Sometimes they remember what the ledgers forgot. If the dream ever shows you a face — even a corner of one, even just the hands — you tell me. Promise me."

Issa promised, in both tongues, hands and mouth together, and ran off to sleep among the puppy-stack of children, and August sat alone on the border line watching the afternoon mist begin to rise off the Veil, gold-edged now, beautiful, harmless, and thought about scraped pages.

* * *

Evening brought the market, which was really just the space between the third and fourth doors where traders from both worlds had begun, without anyone's permission, to spread their blankets. The treaty had rules for trade — tariffs, inspectors, a shared currency that was actually two currencies stitched together at the middle, which everyone hated and everyone used — but the blanket-market obeyed none of them and thrived anyway, and the council had decided, wisely, to pretend not to notice.

August walked it as they did every evening, not to buy anything but to hear the language living. This was the work beneath the work. A treaty written in the First Tongue was not a document; it was a conversation that had to be kept going, and every bargain struck in the border-speech, every curse muttered in it, every child's counting-song that mixed the two grammars like two streams — each one was a clause being ratified anew.

Tonight the market smelled of roasting chestnuts and wet wool and the green bite of Veil-side pepper-root. A mortal potter was arguing cheerfully with a returned weaver about whether a pattern was stolen or convergent, and doing it in border-speech, badly, with enormous enthusiasm, his hands flying through signs he only half knew while the weaver corrected him with the patience of a mountain. Two old women — one from each side, inseparable for a year now, the town's most famous friendship — sat on a crate playing a game that was half chess and half something older, arguing moves in a private dialect of gestures and insults.

And near the fourth door, a small crowd had gathered around a newcomer.

August felt it before they saw it — a hush running through the market the way weather runs through wheat, heads turning toward the door, the particular silence of people witnessing something that happens only a few times a year. Someone was coming through. Someone was answering.

The figure that stepped through the fourth door was gaunt and travel-worn, wrapped in a coat that had been fine once, decades ago, and carried a bundle that clearly held everything owned. An elder of the returned — Mother Vesh, who remembered the old country — pushed through the crowd and made the open-hand sign, slow and formal, the welcome of the First Tongue: *I am here, and I mean no taking.*

The newcomer stared at the sign. Stared at the unlocked door behind them. Stared at the crowd — mortal faces and Veil faces mixed together like seeds in one palm — and then their legs simply stopped working, and they sat down in the road, in the dust, and wept.

"It's true," they kept saying, in the old high dialect, the one August had only heard from the oldest of the returned. "It's true, it's true, they said it was true and I didn't believe—"

Mother Vesh knelt and gathered the traveler in, and the crowd closed around them the way a body closes around a wound, gently, to hold it together, and August stood at the edge of it with their heart hammering and their eyes burning, because this — this exact moment, this stranger on the ground believing at last — was the thing the whole treaty was *for*. Everything else was hinges and tariffs. This was the point.

Later, when the traveler had been fed and installed in the guest room above the border house, August sat with them while Mother Vesh slept, and listened to the old stories, and answered the old questions. Yes, the war was over. No, no one was coming to make them leave. Yes, the children here learned both tongues. Yes, there was work, if work was wanted; there was always work, teaching, building, mending the seven doors that the weather and the skeptics wore down in equal measure.

"There was a name," the traveler said, near midnight, in the pause where the fire settled. "When we were exiled. The ones who stayed behind the Veil longest, the deep ones — they passed a name down, whisper to whisper, generation to generation. They said: *when the border opens, find this name, for this name paid for it.*" The traveler's ancient eyes fixed on August, bright with fever and hope. "Do you know it? Is it written somewhere? We kept it safe all this time. We want to say it properly. To honor it."

August looked into the fire for a long time.

The fire did not answer. Fires never did, though sometimes August suspected they wanted to.

"The name was spent," they said quietly. "Every ledger, every record, every memory. That was the price, and it was paid gladly, and the payment *was* the honor. If you speak a name that no longer exists, you don't honor it — you unmake the gift." They reached out and took the traveler's weathered hand. "Honor it this way instead: learn the border-word. Teach it to the children. Keep the doors unlocked. That's the name now. It's spelled in hinges."

The traveler wept a little more, and slept, and August banked the fire and stepped out into the blue cold of the border night.

Above the town, the moons were rising — twelve of them, faithful and ordinary, keeping their old rounds. The thirteenth was gone now, unmade or transformed; the scholars argued about which. August liked to think it hadn't been destroyed but *translated* — that somewhere in the sky where it used to hang, there was now simply an openness, a clear patch of night, a place where the sky had agreed to be honest.

Below, lamplight burned in the windows of the border house, where the day-watch was ending and the night-watch beginning, two figures overlapping at the threshold, exchanging the countersigns.

And coming up the road from the mortal side, alone, footsore, wrapped in a traveling cloak with the hood up against the mist, came a figure August did not know — and yet somehow, impossibly, in the marrow-deep way that the First Tongue knows things, *did* know. The walk was wrong for a stranger. Too tired, too determined, too much like someone who had been walking for months toward a place they'd only ever seen in a dream.

The figure stopped at the edge of the lamplight. Pushed back the hood.

A woman. Gray threading her dark hair. A scar August didn't recognize, and a face August had never seen awake but had dreamed, once, in a room of turning circles — not the nameless one, never the nameless one, but the one who had stood beside them, who had aimed and not fired, who had torn her commission in half at a canal gate.

The woman looked up at the border house, at the unlocked doors, at the town with no name, and August saw her lips move, shaping a question to herself that the mist almost stole away:

*"Is this where they keep the unnamed scribe?"*

August stood very still on the border line, heart loud as a drum, and did not breathe.
