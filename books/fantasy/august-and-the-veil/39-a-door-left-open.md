## 39. A Door Left Open

### Ilara

The border house had been a tollhouse once, back when the road mattered more than the river. Now its stone walls were scrubbed white, its shutters painted the blue of deep water, and over its door hung no sign at all — only an iron ring, polished bright by the hands of everyone who had ever paused beneath it to steady themselves before knocking.

Ilara stood in the road a long time before she knocked.

Snow came down in the slow, deliberate way of late winter, flakes fat and unhurried, settling on her shoulders as though the season itself were asking her to stay. She had traveled three days from the garrison at Vael's Crossing. She had told her commanding officer — *former* commanding officer, she reminded herself; there was no command anymore, only the Border Compact and its patient, impossible work — that she was inspecting way stations. It was even true, in the way most lies were true these days. The world after the Unbinding was full of truths that had to be carried carefully, like bowls filled to the brim.

She looked at the door. The door did not look back, which she appreciated.

*You could still turn around,* said the part of her that had turned around for eleven years. *You could file your report, keep your commission, keep your distance, keep everything you've kept.*

But she had torn her own commission in half at the canal gate with August's small hand in hers and Oscar's eyes full of rain, and she had learned something in the seasons since: some things, once torn, cannot be mended into what they were. They can only be made into something new. The Compact called it treaty-work. She suspected it had older names.

Inside the border house, someone was speaking.

Even through the door she recognized the cadence before the language — that particular rhythm Oscar used when they were translating, a kind of walking pace for the voice, each phrase given room to breathe. Then came the other voice: low, resonant, layered like water over stone. A First Speaker. Ilara had heard perhaps forty of them now, returned across the doors in the Veil — the Veil-that-was-no-longer-a-Veil, though old habits of naming died hard — and every one of them sounded to her like a song remembered from childhood by someone who had never been a child here.

The voices stopped. Footsteps. The latch lifted.

And then it was not a door between her and the past but simply a doorway, warm light spilling out across the snow, and standing in it —

Not Oscar. Not yet. A First Speaker, tall and grey-robed, her skin marked with the pale script of the old country. She studied Ilara with eyes the color of river ice and said, in accented but fluent Common:

"You are the one who let them go."

"I am."

"She talks about you." The woman's mouth curved, not unkindly. "When she thinks no one is listening. Come in out of the snow, hunter. The floor is cold enough without you making it worse."

### Oscar

Oscar knew the footsteps before the knock.

That was the trouble — or the gift, depending on the hour — of having spent years listening to people walk through the Guild's corridors: you learned the grammar of footfalls. Veyren's had been precise, clipped, a man punctuating his own authority. Sefa's had been quick and flat, a quartermaster's economy. And Ilara's — Ilara's had always been heavier on the left, from the old wound at Redford Bridge, a rhythm Oscar had once fallen asleep to, counting.

They were grinding ink when the knock came, and their hand did not shake, which surprised them. Their heart, however, performed a maneuver they had no ledger-entry for.

"The hunter from Vael's Crossing," said Mareneth, the First Speaker who ran the house's evening lessons, appearing in the doorway of the translation room with the inscrutable serenity Oscar had come to think of as characteristic of her entire people. Or perhaps just of her. "At the door. In the snow. Standing very straight, like a woman reporting for duty to a fort that no longer exists."

"Did you let her in?"

"I asked if she was the one who let them go. She said yes. I decided that was a good enough credential." Mareneth tilted her head, script-pale cheek catching the lamplight. "Was I wrong?"

Oscar set down the pestle. Looked at the ink, black and waiting, the color that had rearranged itself into meaning two years ago in a guild archive while a mud-soaked child spoke the shape of the world. Looked at the window, where snow was beginning to silver the glass.

"No," they said. "I don't think anyone's been right about me in a long time. You might be the first."

They went down the stairs slowly, because there was no hurry anymore — that was the strangest inheritance of the whole affair, that time had become a thing one could move through at a walking pace — and found Ilara in the entry hall, shaking snow from her cloak, looking exactly and nothing like the woman who had cornered them at the canal gate with a drawn blade and tears freezing on her lashes.

"You're taller," Oscar said, because eleven years of eloquence deserted them entirely in favor of that.

"You're nameless," Ilara said. "I hear it's very fashionable."

"It is, actually. Terribly difficult to sign documents, though." They hesitated at the bottom stair. The space between them was four steps and eleven years and one half-torn commission, and Oscar had crossed wider gaps with less fear. "Mareneth gave you tea?"

"Mareneth gave me tea and an interrogation disguised as hospitality."

"That sounds like her. She's getting better at the disguise. Give her another month."

Something happened to Ilara's face then — not quite a smile, but the tectonic movement beneath one, the ground shifting where a smile would eventually stand. "Hello, Oscar."

"Hello, Ilara."

* * *

### Ilara

They walked, because sitting felt too much like an ambush and standing felt too much like a trial, so they walked — out along the river path behind the house, where the Veil's old line ran visible as a seam of brighter air, shimmering faintly even in snowfall, the way heat shimmers above summer roads. Except now there were gaps in it. Doors. Three of them within sight of the border house, tall rectangles of gently stirring light, and through them, sometimes, figures moving in a country that had been a rumor for a thousand years.

Ilara had escorted refugees through those doors. She had stood guard over negotiations conducted in a language that predated kingdoms, watching words literally reshape the air. She had thought herself prepared for anything the new age contained.

She was not prepared for how quiet Oscar had become. Not timid-quiet. *Settled*-quiet. Like a house after the storm has passed and everyone has finally stopped bracing.

"The Compact sent me to inspect the way stations," she said. "That's the official version."

"And the unofficial?"

"The unofficial version is that I've written and destroyed four letters to this address." Snow gathered in her hair; she didn't bother with it. "The first one said everything. The second apologized for the first. The third was just logistics, because apparently I can only be honest when it's about supply routes. The fourth—" she exhaled, watched her breath bloom and vanish, "—the fourth was blank. I decided a blank page was either cowardice or trust, and I've been trying to decide which ever since."

Oscar walked beside her with their hands clasped behind their back, the old scribe's posture, and said nothing for ten full paces. Ilara counted. Counting was how she prayed.

"When you tore your commission," Oscar finally said, "at the canal gate — do you know what I thought? I thought: *she will hate me for this.* That I'd stolen the one clean thing in your life, your duty, and left you holding halves."

"You didn't steal it. I tore it."

"After I made it worthless."

"After you made it *true*." Ilara stopped walking and turned to face them, and the nearest door in the Veil threw its soft light across them both, so that it felt less like a conversation than a ceremony neither had agreed to attend. "Do you know what my reports said, all those months? Before the end? Half-truths. Omissions. I filed a report about the fire in the transcription wing that conveniently forgot a certain scribe had been seen near the archives. I lied in official ink to protect you, and I told myself it was duty bending, just a little, just this once. You weren't the only one whose ledgers stopped balancing, Oscar. Mine broke first. I just hid the wreckage better."

The snow fell between them. Through the nearest door, faintly, came singing — the evening song of the other side, the one August had taught the children here, the one that meant, loosely, *we are still here, we are still us.*

"Ilara." Oscar's voice had gone careful, the way it went careful around words in the First Tongue, words that could unmake things. "What are you here for? Truly. Because if you're here for forgiveness, I'll give it — gladly, tonight, before the snow stops. But if you're here for something harder than that—"

"I'm here because I inspected eleven way stations in three days and every single one of them was fine, and I realized somewhere around the eighth that I wasn't inspecting stations at all. I was working up the courage." Her breath clouded. "I'm here because I chose duty over you once, at the canal gate, and then I chose you over duty in the same breath, and I have spent every day since learning which choice was the real one. And I think — I *think* — they were both real. And I think the only way to find out is to stop choosing and just... stand here. On the border. With you."

* * *

### Oscar

Later — much later, after the lamps in the border house had burned down to amber and the students' dormers had gone dark, after Mareneth had looked at them both over the supper table with an expression of magnificent feigned indifference and announced she would be checking the eastern doors and would be gone approximately three hours — later, they sat in the translation room among the dictionaries and the half-finished treaty folios, and Oscar showed Ilara the work.

"This is the section we're stuck on," they said, turning a page covered in two scripts side by side, the First Tongue's living characters still faintly shifting, restless as fish under ice. "The Compact wants a word for *border*. The First Speakers won't accept *wall*, obviously. But they also won't accept *door*, because a door implies someone controls who passes. They keep insisting there's a third thing. A word for a boundary that exists only because both sides agree to remember it."

"And does the word exist?"

"Oh, it exists." Oscar laughed, tired and wondering. "It's just that it's also the word for *promise*, and the word for *debt*, and the word for *home you leave the light on in*. It's one sound. Try saying *all of that* in Common."

Ilara leaned closer to look, and her shoulder settled against theirs — lightly, tentatively, the contact of someone testing whether ice holds. Oscar did not move away. The lamp guttered and steadied.

"August asked about you," Oscar said quietly. "Last month. They're twelve now, did you know. Tall as my elbow. Teaching the little ones to count in both tongues, bossing everyone mercilessly. They asked—" Oscar's throat tightened unexpectedly, the way it still did around certain names, certain nights, the vault, the thirteenth moon rising like a coin flipped by a god. "They asked if the hunter who let them go was happy. I didn't know what to tell them."

"What did you tell them?"

"I told them the truth. That I didn't know. That happiness was a complicated account." Oscar turned to look at her, close enough now that the lamplight put gold in the grey of her eyes. "I'd like to amend the entry."

Ilara searched their face the way she had once searched treeline and rooftops for threats — thorough, professional, missing nothing. Whatever she found there, it took her a long moment to decide to trust it.

"The fourth letter was blank," she said. "Because everything I wanted to say, I only know how to say in actions. So here is my action: I requested transfer to the Compact permanently. Border liaison. There's housing in the village. It's ugly. It has a stove that smokes. It is four miles from this house."

"Four miles," Oscar repeated.

"Thereabouts. Walking distance, in decent weather. In bad weather—" the almost-smile finally arrived, crooked and wary and achingly familiar, "—in bad weather it's still walking distance. If a person were determined."

Outside, through the frost-furred window, the doors in the Veil glowed their patient glow, and the evening song drifted across the boundary that was no longer a wall, sung by two peoples who had spent a thousand years being strangers and were now, haltingly, term by term, clause by clause, becoming neighbors.

Oscar reached over and closed the dictionary.

"I was a scribe for fifteen years," they said. "I know how entries work. You make them carefully, because ink is forever. But I erased myself out of every ledger in existence, Ilara, so I get to write however I want now." They took her hand — scarred knuckles, callused palm, the hand that had held a blade at the canal gate and then torn the commission with it. "New book. First entry. No name required."

Ilara's fingers curled around theirs.

"What's the first line?" she asked.

Through the wall, faintly, from the children's dormer where August slept, came a murmur in the First Tongue — a dream, maybe, or a blessing, or simply the oldest language in the world saying its oldest word, which was not *power* and not *kingdom* but something closer to *stay*.

Oscar listened. Then smiled.

"*Stay*," they translated. "The first line is *stay*."

And outside, the thirteenth moon — the false moon, the guild's great crime, now merely a moon like any other, keeping its honest rounds — rose thin and silver over the border house, over the open doors, over the two people sitting close together in a room full of unfinished sentences, as if it too were curious to see what they would write next.
