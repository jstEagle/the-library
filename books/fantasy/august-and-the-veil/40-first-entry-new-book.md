## 40. First Entry, New Book

The ledger arrived on a morning that smelled of rain and woodsmoke, which is to say it arrived on an ordinary morning, which is to say it arrived on a miracle.

It came wrapped in oilcloth and tied with plain twine, left on the step of the border house by a courier who did not ask for a signature. There was no signature to ask for. That was the whole of it now — the whole strange shape of my life. I had been a man made of entries: *Oscar Reyes, scribe, indentured, debt of eleven years, four months, six days.* And then the treaty had taken its toll, and every record that held my name had let it go like a hand opening, and what remained was a person who could not be owned because no page remembered him.

I carried the parcel inside past the kitchen, where the smell of barley porridge and burnt honey said that August had been awake for hours, and up the stairs to the small room I kept for work. The window there faced east, toward the Veil — toward what had been the Veil, toward what was now something better and stranger and harder to name. On clear mornings you could see it from the hill road: a shimmer in the air like heat above a forge, running north and south as far as the eye could follow, stitched through with doors.

Doors. That was the word we had all agreed on, though the thing itself was more like a standing agreement than a door. You knocked. Sometimes it answered.

I set the parcel on the desk and did not open it right away. This is a habit I learned in the Guild of Impossible Things, where I spent nine years learning that the most dangerous objects are the ones that arrive quietly. I made tea instead. I watched the light come up over the borderlands, gilding the wet grass, touching the roofs of the town below where people were already moving about their ordinary business — hauling water, arguing about carts, living inside a peace that was less than a year old and still soft as new skin.

Then I untied the twine.

The ledger was exactly what I had asked Sefa to find, and exactly what she had sworn did not exist anymore, and exactly what appeared on my step anyway, because Sefa had never in her life met a promise she couldn't bend into a favor. Blank pages, heavy stock, sewn binding, no guild stamp, no watermark, no history. A book that belonged to no one. In the old days such a book would have frightened me more than any cursed artifact in the vaults, because a blank ledger is only potential, and potential is where all the trouble starts.

I ran my thumb along the edge of the pages. They whispered.

*Well,* I thought. *Here we are.*

---

You should understand what the First Tongue costs before you understand why I sat for a long time with an unopened book in a cold room, letting my tea go lukewarm.

The language predates kingdoms. It predates the idea of kingdoms. When the first speakers crossed out of the world beyond the Veil — refugees, exiles, the ones the old stories turned into monsters because it was easier than turning them into people — they brought the tongue with them, and the tongue was not like ours. Our words sit on things. Their words *do* things. To say a sentence in the First Tongue is to make it slightly more true than it was before, and truth, once made, does not politely return to being a possibility. Every phrase I speak in that language takes something from me. Not blood, not years — something less dramatic and more permanent. It takes certainty. It takes the comfortable fiction that I am one fixed person having one fixed life. Each time I bind a door or mend a rift or write a line of the treaty, I feel myself become a little more porous, a little more like the space between two worlds rather than a citizen of either.

August says this is not a cost but a widening. August is twelve years old and has been dead twice, in the sense of having passed through thresholds that do not ordinarily permit return, and so I try to take the child's word for metaphysics. But some mornings, when the light comes through the window and I can see straight through my own shadow to the floorboards beneath it, I think: *this is what it cost, and I would pay it again, and again, and I am not sure what that makes me.*

It makes me a scribe. It has always made me a scribe. That is the joke the universe told at my expense and then, eventually, at my honor. Nine years balancing impossible accounts — counting moons that didn't exist, tallying debts measured in favors and fears — and it turns out all of it was apprenticeship. Veyren taught me precision. The ledgers taught me patience. Even the forgery — the keystone page, the finest lie of my life, the one that started the avalanche — even that taught me something, though it took me a long time to learn what: that a copy can be more honest than an original, if the original was written in bad faith. The Guild's founding charter named a child who had not yet been born. My forgery at least had the decency to be a lie on purpose.

I opened the ledger to the first page.

---

The paper was good paper. Sefa had outdone herself, wherever she was — and she was somewhere; she always is, usually three steps ahead of whoever thinks they're chasing her, currently, I believe, advising the border council on matters of inventory while quietly skimming a percentage that everyone pretends not to notice because the alternative is doing the accounting ourselves. She had included, tucked inside the back cover, a note in her cramped merchant's hand: *No charge. Consider us square. Don't ever consider us square again, it's bad for business. — S.*

Below the note, in different ink, added later, someone else had written: *Tell the scribe the porridge was acceptable. — A.*

August signs everything with a single initial now. The child tells me it is not anonymity but efficiency. I suspect it is inheritance — my habit, picked up during our nights of secret lessons, when a girl who spoke the oldest language in creation learned from a tired scribe how to lie, how to hide, how to survive in a world that wanted to own her. I taught her forgery and misdirection and the hundred small dishonesties that keep a person free. She taught me the words that unmake walls. Between us we have never decided who got the worse education.

I dipped my pen.

The ink was ordinary ink — oak gall, iron, a little gum arabic, mixed by my own hand in a chipped bowl. I had considered, briefly, using the luminous stuff the Guild kept for its most important documents, the ink that listened, the ink that rearranged itself into meaning. But the treaty did not need ink that listened. The treaty needed ink that would hold still. There is a difference between a living document and a haunted one, and I have been party to both.

Outside, the morning bell rang in the town below, and under it, fainter, almost beneath hearing, the other sound — the sound the border makes now, the low harmonic hum of the Veil-that-is-not-a-Veil holding its shape, the sound of two worlds keeping a promise. Some nights it sounds like singing. Some nights it sounds like breathing. On the worst nights, the rare ones, when a door sticks or a misunderstanding swells, it sounds like an argument in another room, and I get up and put on my coat and go and mediate, because that is the work now. Peace is not a wall you build once. Peace is a ledger you keep.

I began to write.

The First Tongue has no alphabet in the way we understand one — August writes it in spirals, in lines that curve back on themselves like the rings of a tree, and each ring holds not a sound but an intention. But it can be rendered. We learned that together, the child and I, hunched over candle stubs after curfew in the Guild archive while hunters walked the stacks above us: that the language of making can be caught in the language of marking, if the scribe is willing to mean every stroke. That was the secret no one at the Guild ever understood, not Veyren with his almanacs, not the engine with its orrery of brass moons. The First Tongue cannot be transcribed by someone who doesn't mean it. The Guild had the words for centuries, carved into the vault walls, printed in forbidden folios, and they were dead letters, inert, because the men who copied them wanted power and the language only answers want of a different kind.

I wrote the first line of the treaty.

Not the whole treaty — the whole treaty exists now in a dozen copies, in both tongues, hung in every hall of negotiation from the border house to the parliament in the capital, and it is long, and much of it is boring, and that is precisely why it works. Treaties should be boring. Miracles should be boring. Anything truly durable has the texture of ordinary life. No — this line was the preamble, the sentence beneath all the other sentences, the one we had agreed would open every copy, the one that had to be rewritten by hand each time because printing it flattened something essential:

*I speak for neither side, and therefore for both.*

In the First Tongue it took the better part of an hour. The spirals had to be exact. The intention had to be exact-er. Halfway through, my hand began to shake — not from fear but from the peculiar vertigo of meaning something completely, without reservation, which is an experience most people have perhaps five times in a life and which a scribe working in the First Tongue must manufacture daily, like a baker kneading dough. *Neither side and therefore both.* I thought of the First Speakers in their settlement beyond the shimmer, teaching their children the names of mortal birds. I thought of the mortal farmers on this side who had, just last month, signed the grazing accord with the exiles' herdsmen, grumbling the whole time about foreign customs, which is to say integrating beautifully. I thought of Ilara.

Ilara comes on Thursdays. She arrives at dusk, official business mostly invented, stays for supper, argues with me about the placement of the northern doors until one of us laughs, and leaves before full dark, riding back along the border road with her commission — the new commission, the legitimate one, border warden of the middle reach — folded in her saddlebag next to the halves of the old one, which she has never thrown away. Torn paper keeps, if you press it flat. So do torn people. We are mending at roughly the same rate, she and I, which is to say slowly, honestly, with visible seams. Last Thursday she stayed past dark for the first time. She stood in the doorway looking at the shimmer on the horizon and said, "You know I still report on you," and I said, "I know," and she said, "This week's report says the translator drinks too much tea and talks to his books," and I said, "That's accurate," and she almost smiled, which from Ilara is a sonnet.

I finished the line. The last spiral closed. The ink dried with that faint golden undertone it gets when the language has fully settled into the page — not glowing, nothing so vulgar, just warm, like paper held near a candle at a safe distance.

Then the signature.

Or rather, then the place where a signature would go, if signatures were still possible, if ledgers still had the power to hold me, if any record anywhere in either world contained the letters of my name. The treaty had seen to that. When I spoke the binding — when I stood in the vault with the thirteenth moon burning overhead and August freed at my feet and Veyren's voice cracking with fury and faith, and I said the true words, the words that made the Veil neither wall nor gate but a border with doors — the engine had read me, as it read names, and it had found mine everywhere. In the Guild rolls. In the debt books. In the parish registers of the town where I was born to parents I barely remember. In the charter. In the almanac. In the memory of every clerk who had ever written *Reyes* and felt the small satisfaction of a name properly entered.

And it took them. All of them. Gently, the way snow takes footprints.

My mother, if she lives, does not know my surname. The Guild's archives contain nine years of my handwriting and no record of whose hand it was. Sometimes clerks at the border house squint at me and say, "Have we met?" and I say, "Probably," and they shake their heads and move on, troubled by a familiarity they cannot source. August remembers me — memory is not a ledger, thank whatever gods preside over mercy — but August cannot tell anyone else my name, because the child reaches for it and finds the same smooth absence, a shape with the center gone. Once, early on, this made the child cry. Now August calls me *Scribe*, capitalized, the way you'd title a saint, and I have stopped flinching at it.

There are worse fates than being unownable. I have seen the owned ones.

I set the pen down at the base of the finished line, where a name belongs, and I wrote instead a single mark: a small open circle, a door seen head-on, the symbol we use on the treaty seals. Then, beneath it, in plain common script, so that no future reader would mistake the omission for damage:

*Signed by the hand that owes nothing.*

---

Footsteps on the stairs — light, quick, the particular rhythm of someone who has been told to knock and considers knocking a formality reserved for strangers. August came in carrying two bowls of porridge, burnt honey and all, and stood beside my chair reading the page upside down, which the child can do in both tongues, a skill I have given up resenting.

"You started it," August said.

"Someone had to."

"Veyren used to say a ledger was a weapon." The child set a bowl on the corner of my desk, on top of Sefa's note, with the supreme indifference to paperwork that only the very young and the formerly prophetic can manage. "He'd hate this one. It doesn't threaten anybody."

"That's why he's not writing it."

August looked at the empty circle where my name wasn't. The child does this sometimes — looks at the absence directly, testing it, the way you test a healed bone. "Does it ever hurt?" August asked. "Being nowhere?"

I considered lying, out of kindness. I have learned from the best that lies can be mercies. But this was a ledger question, and ledgers deserve the truth.

"Every day," I said. "A little. Like weather. And every day it's also the freest I've ever been. Both things. The treaty runs on both things — that's the whole design. Neither side and therefore both." I tapped the drying spirals. "A wall picks one. A gate picks one. A border holds the tension between them and calls it peace."

August nodded slowly, the way the child nods when filing something away for later, which at twelve is most things. Then: "The delegation from the far settlements comes tomorrow. The ones who've never crossed a door. Mara says they're frightened."

"Mara is right."

"She wants you to greet them. In the First Tongue. She says hearing it spoken kindly changes something in people." August paused, and the morning light caught the fine scars on the child's hands — engine-marks, orrery-marks, the price of having been a key. "Does it? Change something?"

"It changed me," I said. "But I was already halfway changed. Ask me again after tomorrow."

The child grinned — quick, crooked, alive — and took the empty bowl and went back downstairs, calling something to Mara in the kitchen, and I heard the two voices braid together, one in the common tongue and one in the spiraled language older than walls, and neither one stumbled.

I looked at the page a while longer. The first entry of a new book. Somewhere in the Guild's ruined archive, in a vault flooded to the third stair, a charter is dissolving in the dark, its purchased prophecy finally expired, its claims on a child washed clean. Somewhere on the border road, a rider with torn papers pressed flat in her bag is checking the northern doors and finding them, today, quiet. Somewhere beyond the shimmer, people who were monsters for three hundred years are planting spring crops and arguing about fence lines, which is the most human thing anyone can do.

And here, in a small room above a kitchen that smells of burnt honey, a book with no owner holds its first line, signed by no one, owed by no one, binding no one — and holding anyway. Holding everything.

Peace, I have learned, is not the moment the fighting stops. That moment is just the door opening. Peace is the long work of walking through it, every morning, both sides, and writing down what you find.

I blotted the page, closed the cover, and reached for a fresh sheet.

There was more to enter. There always would be.
