## 1. The Charm Seller's Hour

The night market of Vell woke at the hour when honest people went to bed and everyone else came out to trade in things that had no business being traded.

Casper Blackwood had a stall between a woman who sold bottled fog (genuine fog, she insisted, harvested from the moors, though Casper suspected it was just water with opinions) and a man whose entire inventory was keys that opened nothing but were very beautiful about it. Casper's own wares were arranged on a square of black velvet: small glass vials on silver chains, each one holding a curl of light like smoke that had decided to be useful.

Memories. Stolen, refined, and sold as charms. The only magic left in Vell that worked, because it wasn't technically magic — it was just memory, and memory belonged to no god the king had outlawed.

"Charm seller," said a voice at the edge of the stall, soft as moth wings. "They said you'd be here. They said you're the one who does it properly."

Casper looked up into the face of a widow. You could always tell widows in Vell; they wore their grief the way other people wore good coats, carefully, so it wouldn't get rained on. This one was perhaps sixty, with flour still dusting her shawl — she'd come straight from baking, which meant she hadn't slept, which meant tonight was an anniversary of some kind.

"I do several things properly," Casper said. "But I suspect you mean the charms."

"My husband." She set both hands flat on the stall's edge, steadying herself against her own sentence. "Tomas. He died in the spring. Shipwright. And there was one day — one day I would give my teeth for. The day we took the boat out, our little boat, before he built ships for other men. He laughed so hard he fell in the harbor. He *fell in*, the great fool, in all his clothes, and he surfaced laughing with a fish in his shirt."

Casper felt the familiar ache behind the ribs, the one that never quite resolved into anything nameable. Twelve years now they'd been selling this exact moment back to people, and twelve years the ache had stayed exactly where it was, like furniture too heavy to move.

"You want his happiest hour," Casper said. "Woven into a charm."

"If you have it."

"I don't keep stock of specific hours, mistress. Every hour is different. What I have—" Casper lifted three vials from the velvet, letting them catch the lantern light, "—are hours of joy, unclaimed. A fisherman's dawn. A wedding feast. A child's first snow. Any of them will hold your grief gently. None of them will be Tomas."

The widow's jaw set. "I don't want gentle. I want *him*."

And there it was. Casper sighed inwardly, the sigh of a merchant watching profit walk out the door on principle, and did the thing they always did, the thing that kept them poor and kept them, in some stubborn corner of themselves, clean.

"Then let me ask you something first," Casper said. "When you remember him falling in the harbor — what color is the sky?"

She blinked. "What?"

"The sky. That day. What color?"

"Blue," she said slowly. "Blue with — there were clouds, white ones, fat ones, over the headland—"

"And the fish?"

"A mackerel. Little silver devil. It thrashed right down his shirt and he danced around the deck like a puppet with cut strings, and I laughed until I couldn't breathe, and then *he* laughed because I was laughing, and—" She stopped. Her hand had drifted up without her noticing, pressed flat over her heart.

"That," Casper said quietly, "is why I asked. You already have the hour, mistress. Whole. Perfect. Nobody can steal it from you and nobody can sell it back. What you've lost isn't the memory. It's the *reaching* for it. Grief sits on top of memories like a stone on a door, and after a while you forget there was ever a door at all."

The widow stared at them. Around the stall, the night market churned on — the fog-seller hawking her weather, a pickpocket drifting past whom Casper marked out of long habit, two lovers arguing over candied plums in the low, musical way that meant the argument was the best part of their evening.

"So what do you sell?" the widow asked finally. "If not the hour itself?"

"The key." Casper took down a plain vial, unremarkable among the wares, holding only a thread of pale gold light. "This is a stranger's joy — a farmer's, if you must know, the morning his first crop came in. It won't give you Tomas's day. But wear it against your skin, and when you sleep, it will go ahead of you like a lamp-lighter. It'll find the doors grief has shut and open them one by one. By week's end you'll be able to walk into that harbor whenever you like. Free of charge, forever. The charm is just the locksmith."

The widow was quiet a long moment. Then she laughed — a wet, cracked laugh, but a laugh. "You talk like a priest."

"Priests are outlawed in Vell, mistress. I'm insulted."

"How much for the locksmith?"

"For you? Six coppers." Which was half what it was worth, and they both knew it, and neither of them mentioned it, because that was how kindness survived in Vell — disguised as bad commerce.

She counted the coins out one by one, each placed in Casper's palm like something being entrusted. Then she paused, vial already at her throat, and looked at Casper with the terrible directness of the grieving.

"Where do you get them?" she asked. "The joys. The ones you sell."

Casper smiled the smile they'd practiced for twelve years, the one that reached everything except the eyes. "People leave them lying about. Careless folk."

It was even true.

* * *

An hour later, Casper found their restock walking toward them on two legs and drinking its wages.

He was a soldier — city garrison, by the cut of the coat, though the coat had seen better decades — and he was drunk in the particular way of men who drank to stop remembering, which made them, in Casper's professional opinion, the saddest kind of customer and the easiest kind of mark. He sat down heavily on the steps of the shuttered fountain at the market's heart, right beneath the old shrine niche where some saint or god had once stood before the king's chisels got to it. Just an empty alcove now. Everything in Vell was an empty alcove now, if you knew where to look.

Casper drifted over, hawking a tray of roasted chestnuts they'd bought purely as camouflage. (They hated chestnuts. Twelve years of hating chestnuts.)

"Nut, soldier? On the house."

The soldier squinted up. His eyes were wet and unfocused. "House? Whose house?"

"Mine. Generous establishment. Very charitable." Casper sat beside him, close enough that the theft could pass for a friendly clap on the shoulder. "Bad night?"

"They're saying the war's coming north." The soldier stared into his cup. "My brother's out past the salt marshes. Farm country. If the usurper's columns come through—" He stopped, jaw working. "We used to steal apples off his own trees, when we were boys. He'd chase me with a rake. Never caught me once. Not once in ten years."

"There it is," Casper murmured.

"What?"

"Nothing. Eat your nut."

The theft itself was gentle work, if you were gentle about it, and Casper was — it was the one rule they'd kept through twelve years of morally flexible commerce: *take only what's freely spilling.* Drunk men leaked memory like cracked barrels. Casper didn't need to pry anything open; they only needed to cup a hand near the overflow and let the brightest drop fall. The soldier's happiest hour — the orchard, the rake, the running — pooled warm against Casper's palm, weightless as sun-heated water, and slid up their sleeve into a waiting vial.

The soldier wouldn't miss it. That was the lie Casper told themselves every time, and every time it was mostly true, and the sliver of *not*-true was the tax they paid on existing at all.

"Sleep it off," Casper told the soldier, standing. "Go see your brother before the war gets ideas. Take apples. Steal them, even. It's good practice."

The soldier mumbled something that might have been gratitude or might have been a recipe. Casper left him to the fountain and the stars.

Walking back through the market, vials chiming softly against each other under their cloak, Casper did the accounting they did every night, the private ledger no fence ever saw. Three charms sold. One stolen. Rent due in nine days. Chestnuts: regrettable.

And underneath the ledger, as always, the blank page. Twelve years of nights like this one, and Casper Blackwood could remember none of them clearly — could remember *nothing* clearly, nothing before waking one grey morning in a rented room with a talent for theft, a purse of coin, and a hole where a life should have been. No childhood. No face of a mother. No reason for any of it. They had become the finest restorer of lost hours in Vell, and they could not restore their own, because their own had not been lost.

They had been *paid away*. Though Casper didn't know that yet. They only knew the shape of the absence, the way you know a missing tooth.

They were passing the shuttered fountain again, the empty saint-niche overhead, when the strangest thing happened.

A child's voice, thin and exhausted, drifted from the shadow of the shrine wall — a girl, maybe seven, kneeling in the dark where her mother couldn't see her, hands pressed together in the old forbidden shape.

"*Please,*" the child whispered. "Please, anybody. Mama says nobody's listening anymore. But if anybody *is* — please let Grandma get better. Please. I'll be good forever."

Casper stopped. Stood in the market noise with a tray of cold chestnuts and a pocket full of stolen joy, and watched.

And in the empty alcove above the girl — in the bare stone socket where a god had been chiseled out fifteen years ago — a candle stub that someone had abandoned, long dead, guttered.

And lit.

Small. Blue-white. Impossible.

The girl gasped. Casper's heart performed a maneuver it had not performed in twelve years, which was to stop entirely.

Because the flame wasn't just light. Casper *felt* it — felt it the way they felt memories, a tug somewhere deep beneath the blank page, threads pulling taut inside their own chest, threads they had never known were there, all of them suddenly singing toward that impossible little fire.

The candle flickered. The girl laughed, high and terrified and delighted.

And across the night market, one by one, the lanterns began to tremble.
