## 2. Prayers Answered

Casper Blackwood had three rules for living in Vell, and the first was: never be interesting.

The second was: never be interesting before dawn. The third, learned the hard way in a cellar off Tanner's Row with a witch-finder's boot an inch from their ribs, was: when in doubt, be boring. Boring was armor. Boring was a gray cloak and a yawn and a face that shopkeepers forgot the moment you left it.

So at the hour when the night market folded itself away and the honest city had not yet unfolded, Casper was the most boring creature in Vell: a thin figure in a gray cloak, counting a thin purse, walking home through streets that smelled of tallow and yesterday's rain.

They had done well tonight, by Casper's standards, which were modest and slightly dishonest. The widow Maren had wept over the charm — her husband's happiest hour, bottled: a summer kitchen, bread rising, his laugh with flour on it — and had paid triple because grief makes generous arithmetic. Casper had taken the coin, given her the small glass bead warm as a held hand, and felt the familiar thing they always felt, which was not quite guilt and not quite peace and lived somewhere between the ribs like a swallowed coal.

*You give people back what you lost,* Wren-less, nameless, twelve years ago, someone had told them once. Casper didn't remember who. That was rather the point of them.

They were passing Candlewick Lane when they heard the child.

The shrine at the corner of Candlewick and Sallow had been shuttered for twelve years — since the Edict of Quiet, since the old gods stopped answering and the new king declared it a mercy. Its little alcove was bricked up now, its saint's face chiseled smooth, and the city had learned to walk past without looking, the way you learn to walk past a grave that isn't yours.

The child had not learned. The child was perhaps six, in a nightshirt gone gray at the hem, kneeling on the cold cobbles with her hands pressed to the bricked-up stone, and she was praying in the fierce, businesslike whisper of children who have been told the gods are deaf and have decided not to believe it.

"*Please,*" she was saying. "*Please, I did everything right. I said the words Gran taught me. Please, I'll be good forever, please just make Mama's fever break—*"

Casper stopped. They told themselves it was only caution — a child alone at this hour would draw the watch, and the watch would draw questions — but their feet had stopped on their own, the way feet do when the ground hums.

"*Please,*" the child said, smaller now. "*Please. Anyone. I'm not picky.*"

Casper's mouth did something complicated. *I'm not picky.* Twelve years of selling other people's answered prayers in bead form, and here was a child negotiating with a bricked wall, offering eternal goodness like a coin she didn't have.

"Little one," Casper began, in their best boring voice, "the shrines are closed. Come along, I'll see you home—"

The candle in the alcove lit itself.

Not the bricked-up shrine — there was a votive niche above it, forgotten, its candle long since burned to a stub. The stub caught. A small, ordinary flame, except that it had no wick left to catch *on*, and it burned the color of honey and gold and something older, and the light fell down the wall like a hand smoothing a wrinkle out of cloth.

The child went still. Casper went stiller.

Then the flame *bowed*.

It leaned toward the girl — there was no wind, Casper would swear on their own stolen name there was no wind — the way a candle leans when someone leans over it to hear better. And the girl's face, tear-streaked and furious and six years old, filled with such naked, blazing hope that Casper had to look away, because it was like looking into a charm they'd never had the skill to make.

"*Thank you,*" the girl whispered. "*Thank you thank you thank you.*"

She ran. Bare feet slapping cobbles, home to her feverish mother and a prayer half-answered — because the gods, Casper thought, feeling the old cold turn over in their chest, the gods had never been able to do more than half. The rest was always mortal work. That had been the whole arrangement.

That had been the *covenant*.

The word arrived in Casper's head uninvited, and with it came a sensation Casper had no honest name for. It was like the moment in a theft when the memory comes loose — that faint *give*, a thread slipping off a spool — except it was happening inside Casper's own chest, in the sealed and bricked-up room where their past used to live. Something on the other side of that wall had heard the child. Something on *this* side had stirred.

Twelve threads, they thought, absurdly. Twelve years, twelve threads, all of them cut, and tonight one of them had *twitched*.

* * *

By the time Casper reached their rooms above the chandler's on Sallow Street, the city was already wrong.

It was a subtle wrongness, the kind only a professional would catch. The night market had not gone to sleep; its sellers stood in their stalls like people who had heard their names called from another room. Old Tam the eel-seller was facing the river, hat in his hands. The fortune-teller who sold Casper their paper charm-wrappings was laughing and crying at once, shuffling a deck she hadn't touched in years. And everywhere, everywhere, candles. Windows that had been dark for a decade glowed. Someone had set a stub in a cracked bowl on the churchyard wall, and it burned tall and gold and *attentive*.

The old gods were stirring. Not answering — not yet, not fully, the covenant was a broken bridge and one candle was not a crossing — but turning over in their sleep like great animals smelling rain.

Casper stood in their doorway and felt every hair on their arms rise, and felt, beneath that, the deeper thing: the tug. Threads pulling at the sealed place inside them, patient and vast, like the sea testing a door it had built the house against.

*They know I'm here,* Casper thought, and the thought arrived with total, terrible calm. *They always knew. They're just awake enough now to miss me.*

They went inside. They barred the door. They did not sleep.

* * *

The witch-finders came at the gray hour, and they came in numbers Vell hadn't seen since the Edict.

Casper heard them before seeing them: the specific rhythm of boots that believes itself righteous. From the attic window, between the chimney pots, they watched the sweep begin. Gray-and-scarlet coats, iron lanterns that burned cold white — no flame in them, nothing the gods could whisper through. A sergeant with a voice like a dropped slate calling the streets to stand in their doorways and be *counted*.

"By order of King Aldric," the sergeant called, and the words came out worn smooth from repetition, "all god-touched persons are forfeit. Any who witnessed signs are bound to declare them. Any who *concealed* signs—" he let the pause do the work; it was a well-trained pause — "will be presumed touched themselves."

Down the lane, the eel-seller Tam was being questioned. Casper watched him gesture toward the river, toward the candles, his hat wringing in his hands, and watched a finder in a gray mask write it all down in a book that looked, from this distance, unpleasantly thick.

Casper did the arithmetic of survival. Back door, cellar, the chandler's tunnel to the tannery — they'd bought that escape years ago and never needed it. They could be boring somewhere else by noon. Rule one, rule three, gray cloak, gray life.

They were halfway to the trapdoor when the tug came again.

It was not gentle this time. It was the child's candle, it was the bowed flame, it was a thread pulled taut across twelve severed years, and it ran from the sealed room in Casper's chest out through the city to every burning stub, and what it carried was not words. It was older than words. It was the feeling of being *known* — the specific, unbearable intimacy of a god looking at you the way the candle had leaned toward the child, close, listening, *glad*.

And beneath the knowing, faint as a name under water, something that was almost a voice said: *oath-keeper*.

Casper stood with one hand on the trapdoor and shook. Not from cold. From the other thing — the thing they had spent twelve years not naming, which was fear. Not of the witch-finders; finders were weather, weather could be waited out. This was the fear of being *found*, truly found, by the only parties who knew what was buried in them and had both the means and, apparently, the patience to dig.

The king wanted the gods dead. The gods wanted their oath-keeper. And Casper Blackwood, the most boring creature in Vell, stood between them holding a trapdoor and a purse of a widow's grief-money, with no memory at all of which side they'd originally been on.

Below, in the street, a child's voice — the same child, fever-bright-eyed, dragging her exhausted mother by the hand — shouted up at the gray-and-scarlet coats with the magnificent tact of the six-year-old:

"She's better! My mama's better! The *candle* did it!"

The masked finder turned toward her. The iron lantern swung.

Casper was down the trapdoor and into the dark before the scream started — not the child's; the finder's, when every candle in Candlewick Lane flared gold at once, all together, like a city full of held breath finally let go.

Above the tannery tunnel, in the dark, Casper pressed a hand to their own chest, to the sealed and bricked-up place, and felt it *knocking back*.

They had two choices, as always: run north and stay boring, or follow the tug toward whatever had loved them enough to wait twelve years.

Somewhere behind them, boots on the trapdoor. Ahead, the smell of the sea.

"Damn it," Casper said, to no one, to the dark, to the thread — and ran toward the water.
