## 3. What the Wolf Remembers

The god was dying, and dying gods leave a smell.

Wren had followed Fenmarrow's scent for nine days now — not his body, which lay curled in the hollow of a mountain like an old dog too tired to rise, but the thread of him that still clung to the world. It smelled of pine sap and cold rivers and, faintly, of blood that had been spilled gladly rather than cruelly. The thread ran south, into the lowlands, into Vell itself, which was the last place any self-respecting god-thread ought to go.

"Stupid," she told the thread, and the thread, being a thread, did not argue.

She came down out of the hills on the ninth evening with her hood up and her bow wrapped in oilcloth and her stomach making the kind of noises that would embarrass a much larger animal. Vell rose ahead of her: a city of slate roofs and crooked chimneys, all its shrines shuttered, all its bells silent, the way it had been for twelve years since the Edict of Silence. Magic was outlawed here. Gods were outlawed here. Wren, who was made almost entirely of both, found this inhospitable in the extreme.

*Find the anchor,* Fenmarrow had rasped at her, his great ribs rising and falling like bellows with a hole in them. *The one who holds the covenant's knot. I marked them once — you marked them too, little huntress, though your memory is younger than mine.* He had laughed then, a sound like rocks rolling downhill, and coughed up something luminous and sad. *They will not know their own face. Be patient with them. And Wren—* his yellow eye had caught hers, ancient and amused and unbearably fond, *—be kind. You were never very good at kind.*

"I am excellent at kind," she had said, and he had laughed harder, which could not have been good for a dying god, and she had left before he could say anything else embarrassing.

Now she stood at the edge of Vell's night market, and the thread of her god pulled taut as a bowstring, pointing directly at—

A stall. A small, shabby stall hung with glass charms that caught the lamplight like trapped weather. Behind it sat a person with ink-stained fingers and a face Wren knew better than her own hands.

Casper Blackwood.

Wren's heart did something complicated and undignified.

* * *

She watched first. Hunting taught patience; loving Casper Blackwood had taught more.

They were selling a charm to a widow — Wren could smell grief on the woman from twenty paces, old grief, worn smooth like a river stone. Casper held the little glass pendant up to the light and spoke softly, and whatever they said made the widow laugh through her tears, which is a rare magic all by itself, outlawed or otherwise. The widow paid. She walked away holding the charm against her collarbone like something rescued from a fire.

And Casper — Casper smiled after her, a small private smile, and then let it fall away the moment no one was watching, the way people do when they have practiced being alone so long it has become a posture.

*Oh, you idiot,* Wren thought, with enormous tenderness. *You beautiful, stubborn idiot.*

Fenmarrow's thread quivered. Somewhere under the city, under twelve years of silence and law, something old stirred in its sleep, and every hair on Wren's arms stood up to greet it.

She stepped out of the shadows and walked to the stall.

* * *

Casper looked up when her shadow fell across the charms, and Wren watched it happen: the polite merchant's nod, the flicker of appraisal — *customer, trouble, or witch-finder?* — and then the moment their eyes met, when all three categories failed at once and something behind Casper's face went quietly, terribly wrong.

"You," Casper said.

"Me," Wren agreed.

"No. I mean—" Casper set down the charm they'd been polishing. Their hands were steady, which Wren noted with approval; twelve years ago they had been a fidgeter. "I know you."

"Do you?" said Wren, who had rehearsed this conversation for nine days and four hundred miles and was already off script.

"Yes. No." Casper pressed two fingers to their temple as if checking for a leak. "That's the problem. I don't *know* you. But my chest is doing a thing. Like when you hear a song from childhood and you can't name it but your mouth knows all the words anyway."

"What are the words?"

"That's just it, there aren't any, there's just—" Casper gestured helplessly at her, at the whole of her, hood and oilcloth bundle and wolf-grey eyes. "You're standing in front of me and some part of me is *relieved*. Who does that? Who looks at a stranger with a weapon-shaped bundle and feels relieved?"

"A sensible person," said Wren. "The bundle is mostly blankets."

"It is not mostly blankets."

"It is sixty percent blankets."

Something cracked in Casper's careful merchant face — the ghost of a laugh, quickly strangled. Wren felt it land in her own chest like an arrow finding the old familiar target. *There you are.*

"Casper Blackwood," she said.

Casper went very still. "I didn't tell you my name."

"I know it. I've known it since—" She stopped. Recalculated. Fenmarrow had said *be patient*, and she had promised, in the way she promised things, which was badly. "You sell memories," she said instead. "Charms. Stolen hours in glass."

The wariness came back full force. "That's slander."

"It's true."

"It's *slanderous truth*, which is worse." Casper glanced down the market lane, quick and professional. "Look, whoever you are — and I'm going to need a name eventually, because 'sixty percent blankets' isn't going to hold up — you should walk away. Whatever you think I am, associating with it is a fine way to end up in a witch-finder's cart. They're sweeping again. Since the candles started lighting themselves."

"I know about the candles."

"Everyone knows about the candles. A child prayed at a dead shrine and the wick caught, and now half the city can't sleep and the other half is informing on their neighbors." Casper's voice dropped. "It's nothing. Old magic misfiring. There's no covenant anymore. There hasn't been for twelve years."

"There has," Wren said quietly. "It's been held. By one person. Whether they wanted the job or not."

The silence between them stretched. Around them the night market went on — a fishmonger arguing about eels, a child stealing an apple with tremendous incompetence — oblivious to the fact that the whole world had narrowed to a stall of glass charms and two people who used to know each other completely.

"Casper," Wren said, and then, because she had promised to be patient and was already failing, "you laughed once until you snorted wine out your nose at a funeral. Your own fault; you wrote the eulogy rhyme. You sleep on your left side with your hand tucked under your cheek like a child, even though you claim to be a hardened criminal of the memory trade. You hate coriander. You once told me — swore, actually, with your hand over your heart — that if you ever got rich you'd buy a house with a door so heavy it took two people to open, just so no one could ever leave without you noticing."

Casper's mouth opened. Closed. Opened again.

"I don't—" Their voice came out thin. "I don't remember any of that. I don't remember *anyone* knowing any of that. I checked, years ago. I went through what's left in here—" they tapped their temple, "—and it's like a house where someone took the furniture out of half the rooms and you can see the clean rectangles on the walls where things used to be. Twelve years of clean rectangles. So either you're lying, or you're the cruelest person alive, or—"

"Or I loved you," Wren said, "and you paid for something with the part of yourself that knew it."

The fishmonger won the argument about eels. Somewhere a shutter banged. Casper stared at her, and Wren watched them do the arithmetic — the same arithmetic she'd done on the mountain, the same terrible sum — and arrive at the answer they'd been avoiding for twelve years.

"That's not possible," Casper whispered. "Nobody pays *memories*. Not all at once. Not like that. It would kill—"

"It doesn't kill. I'm looking at proof." She leaned closer, and lowered her voice to the register she used for frightened animals and truths. "My god is dying, Casper. Fenmarrow. The wolf. He sent me to find the anchor of the covenant — the one soul the old oath still knots itself around. Nine days I tracked his thread, and it led me here, to a stall of stolen hours in a city that outlawed gods." She let that sit. "You want to know who you are? So do I. But I know where the answer lives, and it isn't in your head. It's north. In the stone you knelt at before you ever sold a single charm."

Casper's hand had drifted, without their seeming to notice, to the glass charms — touching them one by one, the way a person touches the clean rectangles on a wall.

"If I say yes," they said at last, "what happens to the stall?"

"You're worried about the *stall*."

"The widow comes back Thursday. She's owed a charm of her husband dancing badly at their wedding. If I simply vanish, she'll think—" Casper stopped. Swallowed. "Everyone I've ever given something back to. They'll think they dreamed me."

"They'll think you kept your word longer than most," Wren said. "Which you did."

Casper looked at her for a long moment. Then, absurdly, horribly, wonderfully, they began to laugh — the real one, the one Wren had crossed mountains to find, helpless and snorting and entirely undignified, and Wren realized with a lurch that she was laughing too, that nine days of pine sap and cold rivers had been worth it for this alone.

"I don't know you," Casper managed, wiping their eyes. "I want that on the record. I don't know you, and my chest is doing the thing, and I hate it."

"Noted," said Wren. "For the record: I know you, my chest is also doing the thing, and I've missed it."

Before Casper could answer, the market changed.

It was subtle at first — the fishmonger going quiet mid-sentence, the apple-thieving child freezing like a startled hare. Then the sound reached them: boots. Many boots, moving in unison, and the particular creak of iron cages on cart axles, and voices calling the street to curfew in the flat official tone of people who enjoyed their work.

Lanterns flared at the mouth of the lane. Grey cloaks. Silver pins shaped like closed eyes.

"Witch-finders," Casper breathed. "Here? This hour? They don't sweep the market, there's nothing here but—"

But Wren was already watching the lead finder, a tall figure whose lantern burned a color lanterns should not burn — a hungry, violet white — and who had stopped at the edge of the lane and turned their head, slowly, like a compass needle, until it pointed directly at the stall.

Directly at Casper.

"Get your blankets," Wren said, unrolling her bow. "We're leaving north tonight."

*"North?"* said Casper. *"Tonight?"*

The violet lantern swung toward them, and the boots began to run.

"*Yes,*" said Wren, and took Casper's hand — and felt, even now, even with hunters closing and a dying god waiting, the exact shape of a hand she had never forgotten — "*now.*"
