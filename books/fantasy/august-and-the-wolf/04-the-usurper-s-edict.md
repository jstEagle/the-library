## 4. The Usurper's Edict

King Aldric had never cared for the sound of his own voice until he learned what it could do to a crowd.

He stood on the balcony of the Ironhold with morning fog burning off below him and ten thousand faces tilted up like sunflowers that had given up on the sun. He let the silence stretch. Silence was a tool; his tutors in statecraft had taught him that much before they died of natural causes, which was to say, before he arranged for their causes to be natural.

"Twelve years ago," Aldric said, and the acoustics carried it to the last row of the square, "the gods went quiet. You were told this was mystery. I tell you now: it was mercy."

A ripple through the crowd. Good. He had rehearsed the pause.

"The old gods took your prayers and gave you nothing but dependence. They fed on your kneeling. And when they grew bored of you—" he spread his hands, a man wounded by truth, "—they abandoned you. But I have not abandoned you."

He told them about the candle in the shrine district. He told them about the well in Lowmarsh that had begun whispering back. He did not tell them that his witch-finders had drowned a woman testing that well, because crowds did not need arithmetic, only rhythm.

"So hear my edict." The rhythm quickened. "Any soul touched by the old powers—any charm-seller, any hedge-witch, any whisperer at wells—is forfeit. Their goods are the crown's. Their bodies are the fire's. And any who shelter them share their crime."

Below him, ten thousand people made the small collective sound of a city deciding not to look at its neighbors.

Aldric smiled for them, warm as banked coals, and thought: *And when I find the anchor—the one thing still knotted to your dead gods—I will pull until the whole covenant comes undone, and whatever divinity spills out of the cut will be mine.*

He turned from the rail. Behind him, in the shadow of the doorway, Serah Vole waited with her ledger and her patient, terrible smile.

"We've found something," she said. "In Vell. A charm-seller whose wares don't smell of theft."

"They all smell of theft."

"Not this one. This one smells of *oath*."

* * *

Casper knew Brannick was dying before Casper reached the top of the stairs, because Brannick's shop smelled wrong—no pipe smoke, no mildew, just the copper-and-lily scent of a man spending his last hours arguing with them.

"You look terrible," Casper said, shouldering the door shut behind them.

"I look *honest*, which is worse." Brannick lay propped among his cushions like a king in exile, which, in the economy of Vell's fences, he essentially was. His skin had gone the gray of old pewter. "Sit down, thief. I haven't the breath to chase you around the room, so sit where I can see you."

Casper sat. Twelve years of buying and selling through this man, twelve years of haggling that was mostly theater over prices they both already knew, and never once—not once—had Brannick asked where Casper had come from. Fences survived by not asking. It was practically scripture.

Which is why Casper's stomach dropped when Brannick said, "I'm going to ask you a question."

"Don't."

"I'm dying. Asking is the one luxury left to me." He coughed, wet and long, and when he surfaced he looked almost amused. "Do you know why I never asked? Because the first day you walked in here, selling me a memory of a wedding you claimed to have lifted off some bride, I tasted it. Old habit. Fences taste. And it wasn't stolen, Casper. Stolen memories have rough edges. Fingerprints. Yours had been *paid*. Given up freely, whole and clean, like a coin from your own purse. Nobody pays away their own past by accident."

The room felt suddenly very cold. "You're rambling. Dying men ramble."

"Dying men also settle accounts." He fumbled beneath his cushion and produced a letter, sealed with plain wax, and held it out with a hand that shook like a leaf deciding whether to fall. "There was another fence before me. Maren. She handled the covenant brokers, back when there was such a trade. When she went into the ground she left me one item for safekeeping, to be delivered if ever the gods woke again." His eyes glittered. "Well. A candle lit itself in the shrine district Tuesday. So."

Casper took the letter without breaking the seal. The paper was heavy, expensive, older than its wax. "What is it?"

"A bill," Brannick said, "of sale."

Casper broke the seal. Inside was a single sheet in a fine, formal hand—not Maren's, someone far older—and it read:

*Received, in payment for the reforging of the Covenant between the mortal realm and the divine: one oath-keeper's memories entire, freely surrendered. Term of escrow: until the gods answer again.*

Beneath it, a signature. Not a name. A small, careful drawing of a key.

Casper read it four times. The letters refused to rearrange themselves into anything more sensible.

"That's not possible," Casper said finally. "I would remember—"

"No," Brannick said gently. "That's rather the entire point, isn't it?"

The silence between them filled up with rain starting against the window. Casper became aware, distantly, of their own hands—steady, always steady, a thief's hands—and hated them for it.

"I brokered it," Casper said. It wasn't a question, but Brannick nodded anyway. "The covenant. The first one. And then I paid for the second one with—" They gestured vaguely at their own head, at the twelve-year fog behind their eyes that they had always assumed was damage, or mercy, or simply the way thieves were built. "With myself."

"With yourself," Brannick agreed. "Freely surrendered. Which means, thief, that somewhere under all that fog is a person who keeps their word even when the price is everything. I've known you twelve years. I always wondered where the honesty came from, buried in a profession like yours." He smiled, gray and fond. "Now I know. You're not a thief who lost something. You're a *keeper* who misplaced himself."

Outside, boots. Many boots, moving in the synchronized cadence of witch-finders working the street door to door. Brannick heard them too; his smile didn't waver, but his hand found Casper's wrist and gripped with surprising strength.

"Back stair. Through the tannery. Go."

"What about you?"

"What about me?" He laughed, which cost him visibly. "I'm a dying fence in a city full of edicts. My inventory is honest, my conscience is clean, and my god, if he exists, owes me nothing—which makes us the best of friends. Go, Casper. And when you find out who you were—" he squeezed once, hard, "—try to be someone I'd still have liked."

Casper went down the back stair two steps at a time, the letter pressed against their chest like a coal, and did not look back at the sound of the front door giving way.

* * *

Wren found them at the tannery gate, which was either excellent tracking or terrible luck, and Casper had stopped being able to tell the difference where she was concerned.

"You're limping," she said.

"You're bleeding."

"It's hardly a wound. It's a *souvenir*." She fell into step beside them anyway, matching their pace through the reeking alleys, and Casper noticed she'd positioned herself on the side facing the street—between Casper and the world, the way she always did, casual as breathing. "Vole's people hit Brannick's shop. I watched from the roof. I couldn't help without burning us both, so I memorized which ones hesitated. That matters later."

"You watched," Casper said flatly. "Brannick might be dead and you *watched*."

"Yes." No flinch, no apology. "Because if I'd moved, we'd both be in a wagon headed for Aldric's fires, and the covenant would stay broken forever, and everyone Brannick ever fenced honestly for would burn too. Sentiment is a luxury, Casper. I gave mine up years ago." A beat. "Mostly."

They came out onto the salt-quay road where the wind came off the water smelling of kelp and distance. Wren stopped, and Casper stopped, and for a moment they just stood there in the gray light like two figures someone had forgotten to finish painting.

"There's a ship," Wren said. "North, on tomorrow's tide. Fenmarrow's old routes, past the free cities, up to the ice-edges where Aldric's writ doesn't run. The pack has a holdfast there. Cold, ugly, safe." She said it all quickly, the way people recite things they've rehearsed. "You could sell charms to fishermen. Nobody asks questions past the ice-line. You could be nobody again. It's what you're good at."

"And you?" Casper asked. "Would you be nobody with me?"

Something crossed her face—there and gone, quick as a fish under ice. "My god is dying, Casper. Dying gods get one last hunt. Mine is finding the anchor and keeping it alive until the Godstone can be reforged. That's the whole of me. There isn't room on that ship for both jobs."

"So this is goodbye dressed as an offer."

"This is me trying to save your life," Wren said, and her voice cracked on the second word, just slightly, just enough, "which I apparently care about more than is *professionally advisable*, so yes. Goodbye dressed as an offer. Take it. Please take it."

Casper looked north, where the offer was. Then they took out Brannick's letter and held it up between them, the wind worrying the paper's edge.

"Do you know what this says?"

"I know what it *is*. I could smell the covenant on it from across the street."

"It says I paid for the first reforging with every memory I owned. Freely. It says whoever I was kept their word at a price that would kill most people twice." Casper folded it away, carefully, the way one handles a live coal. "It says the person you keep looking at like you know them—the person who *does* know me, apparently better than I do—that person was real. Was *good*. And I have spent twelve years being a fog with nimble fingers, Wren, and I am so tired of guessing at my own shape."

"Casper—"

"If I go north, I'll be safe and empty for the rest of my life, and the fog wins." They started walking south along the quay, toward the coast road, toward the free cities, toward the shattered heart of everything. "I'm done running from the truth. I'm going to run *at* it."

Wren stood frozen for exactly three heartbeats. Casper counted. Then she swore—a rich, inventive oath involving several of Fenmarrow's less dignified anatomical features—and caught up in four strides.

"For the record," she said, falling into place on the seaward side, hand resting near her knife, "this is the worst decision anyone has ever made, and I have personally watched a man wrestle a river-hag for a hat."

"Noted."

"And if you die remembering, I will be *furious* with you."

"Also noted."

They walked south together as the light failed, two small figures on a long gray road, and behind them Vell's bells began to ring the alarm for a king's soldiers marching on the free cities—and ahead of them, though neither could see it yet, the salt road unspooled toward drowned chapels, witch-finders, and a debt that had been waiting twelve patient years to be collected.
