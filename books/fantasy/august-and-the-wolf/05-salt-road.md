## 5. Salt Road

The sea had a way of making everything smaller, Wren thought. Grief, duty, the end of the world—all of it shrank to the size of a footprint in wet sand, and the tide came along and took even that.

She walked the salt road with her hood up and Casper half a step behind her, which was where they had been for three days now. Half a step behind, always. Close enough to grab if the witch-finders came over a dune; far enough that their breath didn't warm her neck. She had learned to measure distances like that in Fenmarrow's service. Everything was either within reach of your teeth or it wasn't.

"You're doing it again," Casper said.

"Doing what."

"The thing where you walk like the ground owes you money."

Wren glanced back. Casper's hair was stiff with salt spray, their coat patched at both elbows, and they were smiling—that crooked, sideways smile she remembered from a lifetime ago, before the Godstone, before the debt. It was unfair, really. Twelve years of stolen memory and they still smiled exactly the same.

"The ground *does* owe me money," she said. "I saved a village from a marsh-wight once and they paid me in turnips."

"Turnips."

"Good ones."

Casper laughed, and the sound went through her like a hook through the gill of a fish. She turned forward again and walked faster.

Behind them, far down the coast, Vell's watchtowers still burned against the morning haze. The witch-finders had swept the night market two days after the candles relit themselves, and Serah Vole's gray riders had ridden out of the city gates before dawn, moving north along the coast road like a slow spill of ink. Wren had seen them from the cliffs. She had counted forty banners and then stopped counting, because numbers that large made her want to do something stupid.

Fenmarrow had told her, in one of his fading dream-voices: *Go north along the salt road. Keep the anchor breathing.* He hadn't said anything about keeping the anchor fed, but Wren had bought bread and hard cheese in a fishing village with coins she'd won at dice, and Casper had eaten like someone who'd forgotten food could be good, which, knowing what she knew about the last twelve years, they probably had.

The chapel appeared in the late afternoon, or rather its ruins did—a stone bell tower leaning out of the dunes at an angle that suggested it had lost an argument with the sea, and below it, half-swallowed by the tide line, the roofless body of the chapel itself. The water came right into the nave now. Old gods' country, this coast had been, before the covenant frayed. The sea remembered.

"In there?" Casper said, eyeing the water lapping between broken pews.

"It's sheltered. The dunes block the view from the road. And drowned holy ground confuses trackers." Wren shrugged. "Also I'm tired and you're limping."

"I am not limping."

"You've been limping since the clam flats."

"That's called *walking thoughtfully*."

* * *

They made camp in the corner of the nave where the floor was still dry, on a shelf of stone above the high-tide mark. Wren built a small fire of driftwood because the smoke would smell of salt and read as nothing to anyone. Casper sat with their boots off, flexing their foot with great dignity, while Wren cleaned her knives.

The light did something strange in there. The sun came low across the water and filled the ruined chapel with gold, and the tide-pools left in the hollows of the floor caught it and held it, so that the whole nave seemed lit from beneath. Casper leaned over one of the pools, watching the small fish dart between the stones.

"Do you ever miss it?" Casper asked suddenly. "Hunting? For him?"

"Fenmarrow?"

"Mm. You talk about him like he's already a story you're telling."

Wren kept her eyes on her whetstone. "He is dying. There's a difference between a story and a eulogy."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be alive until we reach the Godstone. That's the whole of your job."

"And yours?"

She looked up. Casper was watching her across the firelight, and there was something in their face—not the market-charmer, not the thief. Something older and more careful. "Yours is to get me there," they said. "That's all. You don't have to die for it too."

"I'm not planning to die for it."

"No. You're planning to live for it. That's worse."

The tide was coming in. She could hear it filling the outer nave, sliding green and black among the pews, and the pools on the floor trembled as the water beneath them rose. In the largest pool—the baptismal font, maybe, or what remained of it—the light shifted, and instead of sunset-gold it went silver-gray, wolf-colored, and a voice came up through the water like a voice through deep snow.

*Little huntress.*

Wren was on her feet before the second word finished, knife drawn, heart slamming—and then she saw the shape in the pool, the great dark suggestion of shoulders and antlered shadow, and she knelt so fast her knees cracked on stone.

"My lord."

*You walk too slowly,* Fenmarrow said. His voice was thinner than it had been in the spring. Once it had shaken trees. Now it barely shook the water. *The gray riders have hounds that smell oath-magic. They will reach this chapel by moonrise.*

"Then we go now—"

*No. Listen.* The water shivered. *When the anchor stands before the Godstone, the covenant will ask a price. You know what price.*

Wren's throat closed. "There has to be another way."

*There is always another way. It costs more, and it fails.* A pause, long as a winter. *I tell you this because I am dying, little huntress, and dying things may say true things. When the time comes, you must let them choose.*

"They don't remember enough to choose! They don't know what they're giving!"

*Then give them the remembering,* he said, *and trust what they were.* The silver light in the pool dimmed, guttered. *Moonrise, Wren. And Wren—* the voice was almost gone now, almost human, almost fond— *you were never only my hound. Do not let my death make you into one.*

The pool went dark and ordinary, holding nothing but sky.

For a moment Wren stayed kneeling. Then she became aware of Casper standing very close, having heard every word, wearing the expression of someone who has just been told they are a candle burning toward a wick.

"So," Casper said lightly. "Moonrise."

"Moonrise."

"And the price is—" they waved a hand vaguely at the pool, at the sea, at the entire concept— "everything."

"Your memories. What's left of them. The twelve years are gone already; the covenant would take the rest." Wren stood, sheathed her knife, busied herself kicking sand over the fire. "We'll find another way. He's wrong. Gods are dramatic."

"Casper." Their own name in their own mouth, flat. "Do I look like someone who has a lot left to lose?"

"Yes," Wren said, before she could stop herself.

Casper blinked. "What?"

"You have—" She gestured at them, at the crooked smile, the salt-stiff hair, the thoughtful limp. "*This*. You built this out of nothing. Out of less than nothing. You spent twelve years with a hole where your life should be and you made charms that gave widows back their dead husbands' laughter. That's not nothing. That's the opposite of nothing, and if you pour it into a rock—"

"If I pour it into a rock, nobody else burns." Casper's voice was quiet. "I saw the towns, Wren. I saw what Aldric does to people who pray."

"I know what he—"

"*I know you know.*" They were close now, close enough that she could see the firelight still reflected in their eyes though the fire was dead. "So don't tell me to run north and be safe. Don't you dare."

The tide breathed in the outer nave. Somewhere overhead, in the leaning tower, a gull complained about the state of the world.

"Wren," Casper said, softer. "Why do you actually know my laugh?"

And because the truth was the only currency she had left, she told it. "Because I knew you before. Before the Godstone. Before you paid yourself away. You used to sing off-key around the pack fires and steal the last of the honey-bread and dare Fenmarrow himself to race you across the ice, and when you laughed, the whole valley laughed, because you laughed like the world was a joke you were in on." Her voice cracked and she hated it. "I have spent twelve years missing someone who didn't know my name. So yes. You have things to lose. I'm looking at them."

The silence after that was enormous, tidal.

Then Casper kissed her.

It was clumsy and salt-flavored and entirely too brief, because that was when the dog barked.

Not a real dog—one of the finder-hounds, its cry carrying flat and wrong across the dunes, followed by the creak of leather and the ring of steel on the road above. Wren broke away, knife already out, and pulled Casper down behind the fallen bell, which lay half-buried in the nave floor like a sleeping bronze god.

Gray cloaks on the dune ridge. Four of them, five, lantern-light swinging. And at their center, dismounting with the unhurried grace of a woman who has never once been late to anything, a figure Wren recognized from a hundred wanted-notices burned into her memory: gray coat, gray eyes, a smile like a paper cut.

Serah Vole looked down into the drowned chapel and said, pleasantly, to the dark:

"We know you're cold. Come up, and keep your memories."

Beside Wren, in the dark under the bell, Casper's hand found hers and gripped it.

"Well," Casper whispered. "Any other ways?"

Wren watched the lanterns begin to pick their way down through the pews, and felt the old wolf-shape rise in her blood, and grinned.

"As it happens," she said, "the sea owes me a favor."
