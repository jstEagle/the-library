## 6. The Debt Collector

They took Casper in the hour before dawn, which Casper considered professionally rude. Everyone knew the pre-dawn hours belonged to thieves and charm-sellers and other honest workers. Arresting someone then was like pickpocketing a pickpocket—there ought to be rules.

There had been no violence, which was somehow worse. Casper had woken in the loft above the dyer's shop on Tanner's Row to find three witch-finders already inside, sitting patiently on crates, as though they'd been waiting for the kettle to boil. One of them held Casper's entire stock of charms in a canvas sack, turning it over with the idle curiosity of a man inspecting fruit.

The woman in charge stood by the window with her back to the gray light. She was small and neat and gray all over—gray coat, gray gloves, gray hair pinned with a severity that suggested the pins had been threatened first. When she turned, her face was pleasant in the way of a well-kept ledger: everything in its column, nothing wasted.

"Casper Blackwood," she said. "Memory-thief. Seller of charms. Twelve years in Vell under three false names, and I do apologize—I've forgotten the third."

"Everyone does," Casper said. "It was very forgettable. That was the point."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. A ledger entry, perhaps, filed under *later*.

"I am Serah Vole," she said. "First Finder of the King's Commission for the Suppression of Idolatry. You will come with me."

"And if I say no?"

Vole glanced at the sack of charms. "Then I will open every one of these, one by one, and read them aloud until you beg me to stop. Do you know what happens when a widow hears her husband's voice come out of a stranger's mouth in a cellar full of armed men? It is not gentle, Mr. Blackwood. Grief is not gentle when it's handled roughly."

Casper looked at her for a long moment. "You've done it before."

"Twice." She said it without pride or shame, the way one reports rainfall. "You'll come with me, then."

Casper came.

* * *

They did not take Casper to a cell. They took Casper to a counting-house—a real one, abandoned since the banks fled Vell after the edict—and set them in a chair behind a clerk's desk, which struck Casper as either a joke or a courtesy, and they could not decide which was more unsettling.

Vole sat opposite. The two other finders withdrew to the door and became furniture.

"Do you know what we do with god-touched persons?" Vole asked.

"Burn them," Casper said. "I've seen the bonfires. Very festive. Terrible marshmallows."

"We burn what they carry," Vole corrected. "Not always the person. The King's edict is precise, whatever the mobs believe. We are not butchers, Mr. Blackwood. We are *auditors*." She folded her gray hands. "And you are the most interesting account I have ever been given."

"I'm flattered."

"You should be frightened." She leaned forward slightly. "Three nights ago, the old gods answered prayers in this city. Every finder in Vell felt it—the way you feel a floor shift underfoot. And in the middle of that... *stirring*, there was a place where the threads pulled tightest. A knot. My instruments—" she produced a small brass compass whose needle spun lazily, drunk, "—pointed here. To Tanner's Row. To a loft above a dyer's shop. To *you*."

Casper kept their face still. It was a skill older than their stolen memories; apparently some things survived erasure. "Compasses point north. Have you tried holding it the right way up?"

"The needle points at you," Vole said, unmoved. "So. Before I write my report, I am going to ask you plainly, because I have found plainness cheaper than torture in the long run: *what are you?*"

And here was the thing about being empty, Casper thought. Being empty meant there was nothing to protect. No buried childhood, no mother's face, no name-day or first kiss worth dying over. Twelve years ago someone had scooped Casper out and left a shape behind, and a shape could not be tortured, because a shape had nothing inside it to hurt.

But a shape could *bargain*.

"I'll tell you what I am," Casper said slowly, "if we discuss terms first."

Vole's eyebrows rose a fraction. "Terms."

"I'm a merchant," Casper said. "Everything I have is for sale. Including answers. You want to know what I am? I can show you better than tell you. But showing costs."

"You are proposing to sell *memories* to a witch-finder."

"I'm proposing to rent you one," Casper said. "There's a difference. Rent means I keep the deed."

For the first time, Serah Vole laughed—one short, dry note, like a coin dropped on stone. "Twelve years I have hunted your kind. Priests who wept. Prophets who raved. Children who lied badly. And now, finally, a *shopkeeper*." She sat back. "Very well, merchant. Name your price."

"Walk out of here afterward," Casper said. "Alone. Unmarked. Unfollowed."

"Ah." Something in her face closed, gently, like a ledger shutting. "That last part I cannot promise honestly, so I won't pretend to. I can promise not to kill you. I can promise you'll walk out under your own power. What happens *after* the door is above my pay grade, and below my honesty." She tilted her head. "Take it or leave it. Those are the market rates."

Casper weighed it. There was no weight to weigh. That was the whole trouble with being a shape.

"Done," Casper said. "Give me your hands."

* * *

Casper had done this a thousand times from the selling side: cup a stranger's face or hands, breathe, and *pull*—draw a memory up out of the deep water like a fisherman drawing nets, feel it thrash, wrap it, offer it. Giving a memory was easy. Taking one was easy.

What Casper had never done was go looking in their own depths. There didn't seem much point fishing a pond you knew was drained.

Except the pond wasn't drained. Casper had always known that, somewhere below the level of admitting it. There was water down there. Cold, black, and something moving in it.

Casper reached in—and the thing in the dark reached *back*.

The counting-house vanished.

* * *

Stone. White stone under bare knees, worn smooth by centuries of pilgrims, warm as skin though the wind off the mountains was knife-cold. The Godstone rose ahead, whole and blazing, so tall its crown was lost in cloud, humming like a struck bell that never finished ringing.

A younger Casper knelt before it—younger by twelve years, a stranger's face wearing Casper's own eyes, thin and terrified and absolutely certain. Around the stone stood others: robed figures, crowned figures, and shapes that were neither, tall presences like heat-shimmer given opinions. One of them was a wolf the size of a cathedral, eyes like green lanterns, watching Casper with an expression that was not pity and was worse than pity. It was *trust*.

A voice—not heard through ears, arriving instead the way cold arrives through a wall—said: *Who speaks for the mortal side of the covenant?*

And young Casper, kneeling, voice cracking, said: "I do. I'll hold it. Whatever it costs."

*Whatever it costs,* the presence repeated, and there was something almost gentle in it, the way there is almost gentleness in an executioner sharpening his blade. *Do you understand what you pledge?*

"No," young Casper admitted. "But I understand why. That has to be enough."

The wolf-god made a sound then, low, and if gods could grieve, that was the sound. *It will not be enough,* Fenmarrow said. *But it will have to do.*

And then the cost arrived, and it did not arrive like a blow. It arrived like tide. It came up soft and total and patient, and it took the mother's face first, and the name-day, and the first kiss, and the last kiss, and the city where Casper had been born, and the reason, the *reason*, the whole bright burning reason Casper had knelt on that stone at all—

* * *

"—Blackwood. *Blackwood.*"

Casper surfaced gasping, hands gripping the clerk's desk hard enough to splinter it. Their face was wet. Their whole body shook. Somewhere far away, Vole's gray hands were clamped over theirs, and her gray face was very close, and her eyes—for just a moment—were not a ledger at all. They were horrified.

"What did you see?" she asked quietly.

"A debt collector," Casper whispered. "An old one. With excellent manners." They wiped their face with a shaking sleeve and laughed, wet and cracked. "I knelt before the Godstone, Serah Vole. Before it broke. I swore the covenant would hold, and I paid for the swearing with—" they gestured vaguely at their own skull, "—all of this. Everything. I'm not a thief who lost his past. I'm a *payment* that learned to walk around and open a shop."

Vole sat back slowly. For a long moment she said nothing. The compass on the desk between them had stopped spinning. Its needle pointed at Casper's chest, steady as a nail.

"That's why the threads pull toward you," she said at last. "You're not god-touched. You're god-*bound*. You're the knot itself." She looked at Casper with something that in any other face might have been called respect. "Do you know what the King will do when he learns this?"

"Claim me. Cut me open. Use whatever's left of the knot to climb into heaven and lock the door behind him." Casper shrugged. "I've read the edict. He's not subtle."

"He is worse than subtle. He is *sincere*." Vole rose, straightened her gray coat, and signaled the finders at the door. "Our terms stand. You'll walk out alone, under your own power, alive. I gave my word and I keep my accounts balanced."

"But?" Casper said.

"But I am also required to mark you." From her pocket she drew a small stamp of black iron, shaped like a seal, and heated it for a moment against the desk lamp until the sigil on its face glowed dull red. "King's law. All released persons suspected of god-traffic bear the audit-mark. It cannot be removed. It cannot be hidden—it burns through glamours, through disguises, through lies. Anyone carrying the King's instruments will see it and follow." She met Casper's eyes, and her voice dropped, and for one unguarded instant Serah Vole sounded tired, and old, and human. "I would tell you to run somewhere he will never look. But we both know there is only one place you're going, and we both know he knows it too."

"The Godstone," Casper said.

"The Godstone." She pressed the stamp briefly to the inside of Casper's wrist. It hurt less than expected—a hot pinch, a whisper of smoke—and when she lifted it, a small gray sigil sat in the skin like a watermark, faint as breath on glass. Invisible unless you knew to look. Unless you carried the King's instruments.

"There is a back stair," Vole added, gathering her sack of confiscated charms and setting it, deliberately, on the desk in front of Casper. "The guards on it change shifts in four minutes. I have never once noticed how poorly guarded that stair is, and I have been finding things for twenty years." She put on her hat. At the door she paused, gray and neat and unreadable.

"For what it's worth, merchant," she said, "I hope you settle your debt. And I hope, for both our sakes, that when the King comes to collect his mark—he finds nothing there but ash and arithmetic."

Then she was gone, and the four minutes were burning, and Casper Blackwood sat alone in a dead counting-house with a sack of other people's happiness, a brand on their wrist, and—for the first time in twelve years—an address.

North. To the shattered stone. To the wolf who had grieved for them before they even knew they were lost.

Casper picked up the sack, found the back stair exactly as unguarded as promised, and ran.
