## 7. Ashes of the Free Cities

### Casper

The free cities announced themselves the way a dying man announces himself: by smell.

Smoke first, then salt, then the sweet-rot stink of burned grain. Casper crested the last hill above the coast road with Wren at their shoulder and felt their stomach drop through the soles of their boots. Out along the lowlands, the outlying towns of Harrowmere were burning—not all at once, not in the tidy way of a sack, but piecemeal, like someone setting fire to a manuscript page by page to watch which words screamed loudest.

"Vanguard," Wren said. Her voice had gone flat and quiet, the way it did when she was very angry or very sad, and Casper had learned in three weeks of travel that with Wren those were often the same thing. "Aldric doesn't waste time on sieges anymore. He burns the fields and lets winter do the rest."

"How many towns?"

"Enough." She shaded her eyes. Somewhere below, a bell was ringing wrong—three strokes, silence, three strokes—the old alarm for *gods walking*, except no gods were walking here. Just men with torches and a king's warrant.

Casper had spent twelve years perfecting the art of being nobody. It was a craft, really, and they had been proud of it once: the slouched shoulders, the forgettable face, the way shopkeepers' eyes slid off them like rain off waxed canvas. Invisibility had kept them alive through every purge, every witch-finder sweep, every night the candles went out in Vell.

Standing on that hill, looking down at smoke rising from a hundred chimneys that would never again smell of bread, Casper understood for the first time that invisibility had a price, and other people had been paying it.

"We could go around," they said, and hated how quickly they said it.

Wren looked at them. She didn't say anything. That was worse than an argument.

"I mean it," Casper said. "We're two people and half a god between us. What are we going to do, put out fires?"

"No," Wren agreed. "But we can walk through."

* * *

They walked through.

Harrowmere town itself still stood—Aldric's vanguard wanted the granaries intact—but its streets were choked with the overflow, refugees packed into the market square like sheep before a storm, all of them looking south toward the smoke as if staring hard enough might unburn what had burned.

Casper moved among them out of old habit, hood up, hands in pockets, and began, without meaning to, to hear the prayers.

That was new. Since the Godstone road, since Fenmarrow's fading voice had started threading through their dreams, Casper had been able to feel them—a pressure behind the ears, like weather coming. And here, in this square, the pressure was a flood.

*Alder, god of thresholds, keep my son's feet on the road.*

*Maren of the harvest, we saved nothing, we saved nothing, we saved nothing—*

*Anyone. Anyone who's listening. I'll give anything.*

And underneath, fainter, sadder than all the rest: the prayers nobody said aloud anymore because they'd stopped believing anyone answered. Prayers said the way you talk to a grave.

"They can't hear it," Casper whispered. They'd stopped at a well where a woman knelt with her hands pressed flat to the stones, lips moving. Maybe forty. Maybe older; grief ages people unevenly. "The gods can't hear any of it. The covenant's broken. All these people are praying into a locked room."

"Yes," Wren said.

"And Aldric wants to weld the lock shut."

"Yes."

Casper crouched beside the woman at the well without quite deciding to. She flinched, then saw only a tired traveler in a travel-worn coat, and some of the fear went out of her face.

"You should get off the square," Casper said gently. "When the soldiers come for the granary census, they take names. Names go on lists."

"My husband's on a list already," the woman said. Her voice was terribly reasonable. "He stayed to argue with them about our tithe. He argued." A small, broken laugh. "He was always arguing. I told him, Thane, I said, you can't argue with fire."

Casper reached out—old instinct, the charm-seller's instinct—and touched the back of her hand. Just briefly. Just enough.

There was nothing to steal. That wasn't why they'd done it. But what came instead, unbidden, up through the broken threads of the covenant, was a single thread of *answer*: warmth, faint as a candle seen through fog, and the woman gasped, and for one heartbeat her face changed—hope, raw and terrible, hope like a wound reopening—

Then it faded, because Casper couldn't hold it, didn't know how, was only a leaky vessel for something that used to flow through them like breath.

"What—" The woman stared at her own hand. "What was that? Who *are* you?"

"Nobody," Casper said, and stood, and walked away fast, because if they stayed one more second they were going to promise her things they couldn't deliver, and there was already enough of that in the world.

Wren fell into step beside them. "You felt it."

"A thread. One thread. Like trying to carry a river in my cupped hands." Casper laughed, and it came out wrong. "Twelve years ago I brokered the covenant between mortal and divine realms. Do you understand that? I was the hinge. And I paid myself for the privilege and forgot, and now I stand in a square full of people praying into a locked room, and I'm the *key*, and I've been hiding under a hood selling trinkets."

"You didn't know."

"I knew *something*. I knew I was empty. I just thought emptiness was a personality." They scrubbed a hand over their face. Ahead, near the granary, witch-finders in grey were nailing up the edict, and the crowd around it was very quiet. "No more hiding, Wren. Whatever the price. I'm done paying in other people."

Wren was silent a moment. Then, softly, with the ghost of her rare crooked smile: "For the record, I liked your personality even when it was mostly emptiness. You have excellent posture for a hollow man."

"That's the nicest thing anyone's said to me since I lost my entire self."

"I contain multitudes of nice things. Most of them are insults."

* * *

### Wren

Wren smelled the trap before she saw it, which was fortunate, because Casper walked straight toward it like a moth with a death wish.

The edict board by the granary was standard: Aldric's seal, the forfeiture order, the usual threats rendered in beautiful calligraphy, because tyrants loved beautiful calligraphy the way carrion birds loved shiny things. But the witch-finders posted around it were wrong. Too relaxed. Too positioned—one on the roofline, two at the alley mouths—in the shape of a net with one obvious gap facing the square.

An invitation. Someone had described them. The woman at the well, maybe, or someone else Casper had brushed against with that leaking warmth of theirs. *God-touched. Traveling with a red-haired woman. Moves like a thief.*

"Casper," Wren murmured. "Left. Now. Don't look."

"Don't look at what?" Casper said, at full conversational volume, looking directly at it.

Wren sighed the sigh of the long-suffering and took Casper's arm, steering them left with the firmness of a shepherd and the expression of someone about to be extremely rude to several armed men. The roofline watcher shifted. The alley watchers stepped out.

Well. She'd been hoping to save her strength.

"Fenmarrow," she breathed, and let the wolf rise.

It came up through her like dawn—her god's last gift, the borrowed shape he'd pressed into her bones when exile emptied him. Fur and fang and the old green dark of the northern forest, all folded into a woman-shaped vessel running out of refills. She felt him flicker, thin as the last of a candle, and heard his voice like wind through a keyhole:

*Spend me well, little huntress. There is little left to spend.*

Three witch-finders. Iron chains, null-charms, the grey lacquered masks that hid their faces and, Wren suspected, their capacity for shame. The one in front raised a hand and spoke the word that unraveled small magics.

Wren did not use small magic. She used teeth.

It was brief and inelegant and over in the time it takes to tell it: a blur of grey fur, a scream, a chain snapping taut and then simply snapping, a null-charm shattering like sugar glass against hide that was only partly of this world. Two witch-finders fled. One did not flee anywhere ever again, which Wren regretted in the abstract and not at all in the particular.

She turned back to find Casper standing frozen in the middle of the square—surrounded, she realized with a lurch, by a ring of refugees who had all seen everything, and who were not running. They were staring at Casper with that same terrible hope the well-woman had worn.

"It answered," someone whispered. "The prayer—it *answered*—"

"I didn't—" Casper's voice cracked. "I can't fix this. I'm not—I'm only—"

Wren shifted back, human again, shaking, Fenmarrow's ember guttering low in her chest. She took Casper's hand in front of all of them, deliberately, publicly.

"They're right about one thing," she told the square. "This one is the key. And we are going to open the door."

Casper looked at her—wide-eyed, smoke-streaked, more frightened than they'd been in front of Serah Vole's knives—and Wren watched the guilt in their face finish hardening into something else. Something with edges.

"The Godstone," Casper said. Not a question. A decision, arriving whole.

"The Godstone," Wren agreed. Then, because the moment demanded it and because humor was the only weapon she owned that never dulled: "Try not to trip over any more destiny on the way out. You nearly knocked over a bread cart."

"There was a bread cart?"

"There is always a bread cart. It's a gift."

They ran for the north gate together, past the burning towns, past the praying crowds, while behind them the bells rang their broken three-and-three—and far off, at the edge of hearing, Wren felt something answer. Not Fenmarrow. Something older, and waking, and turning its attention toward the two small figures fleeing north with a covenant in their keeping.

It was watching them.

And it was not alone.
