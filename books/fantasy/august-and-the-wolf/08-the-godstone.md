## 8. The Godstone

The mountain had been a god once.

Wren understood that the way she understood old scars—by the shape of what was missing. The Godstone had not shattered so much as it had been *broken*, deliberately, the way a man breaks a horse or a spirit: the great black spire that had once risen from the summit like a held breath now lay in pieces across a mile of scree, each fragment taller than a house, their surfaces still faintly warm to the touch even after three hundred years. Lichen grew on them in silver whorls. Snowmelt ran through the cracks and came out singing.

Casper stood at the edge of the broken field and said nothing for a long time.

"You're doing the face," Wren said.

"What face?"

"The one where you're deciding whether to be flippant about something enormous."

"I don't have a face like that."

"You absolutely do. You did it at the drowned chapel when Fenmarrow spoke through the tide-pools. You did it when I told you my name. You're doing it right now."

Casper's mouth twitched—the ghost of the laugh she remembered better than they did—and then settled into something graver. "It's just stone," they said quietly. "I thought it would feel like more."

"It feels like less because you're standing in its shadow," Wren said. "Wait until it notices you."

* * *

They made camp that night in the hollow of a fallen fragment, a curved slab of god-stuff that sheltered them from the wind off the peaks. Wren built a small fire out of habit, though the stone around them gave back heat like a sleeping animal. Casper sat close—not touching, but near enough that Wren could feel the warmth of them along her whole left side, which was, she reflected, either a comfort or a torment depending on how honest she was willing to be with herself. She had been avoiding honesty for days.

Fenmarrow had been quiet since the chapel. Too quiet. She had felt him the way you feel an ember under ash—a dimness where there should have been hunger, a patience where there should have been the long howl of him. She had told herself it was rest. She had told herself a great many things.

"Casper," she said. "If he shows you something tomorrow—if he spends what's left of himself to show you—I need you to remember that seeing isn't the same as agreeing."

"That's very wise."

"I'm a huntress. Wisdom is mostly knowing when not to chase."

"And if what he shows me is true?"

"Then it's true," Wren said, "and you get to decide what to do about it. That's the whole point of being mortal. Even gods have to ask."

Casper looked at her across the firelight, and for a moment their eyes were the strange deep grey she'd seen only twice before—the color of the covenant's thread tugging at them, the color of someone remembering. "You keep talking as if I'll have a choice."

"Everyone has a choice," Wren said. "That's the first thing Fenmarrow ever taught me. The second thing is that most people spend their lives pretending otherwise, because choosing is terrifying." She poked the fire. "I intend to keep reminding you. Consider it a service."

"A service I pay for how?"

"With your charming company. Which, I'll note, you've been withholding. You haven't been flippant once today. I'm starting to worry."

That earned her the real laugh, brief and startled, and Wren held onto it the way a drowning woman holds driftwood, though she would never have admitted it.

* * *

Fenmarrow came at dawn.

Not as a voice this time, and not through water. He came as the light itself—the sunrise bending wrong, gathering, pooling above the largest shard of the Godstone until the air there shivered like heat over a forge. Wren felt him pass through her on his way, the way weather passes through an open window, and she gasped, because there was so little of him left that what touched her was almost tender. Almost apologetic.

*Little wolf,* he said, in the voice that lived behind her ribs. *Stand aside. This will want all of me.*

"Fenmarrow—" she began, but he was already gone, pouring himself into the space between Casper and the stone.

What happened next, Wren could only watch.

The light took Casper's hand and set it against the shard, and the shard woke. Silver ran through the black stone like veins filling with blood, and the veins spread—outward, outward, leaping from fragment to fragment across the whole broken field until every piece of the Godstone glowed and hummed and the snowmelt sang in harmony, and above it all hung images in the air like frost-flowers forming:

A younger Casper, kneeling where Wren now stood, barefoot in summer grass that no longer grew here. Younger by twelve years and lighter by everything since—no scar on the brow, no wariness in the shoulders. Around them, figures robed in storm-grey and dawn-gold: the gods themselves, assembled, waiting.

And Casper speaking the oath. Wren heard it in her bones rather than her ears, the old words, the first words, the words that had bound mortal prayer to divine answer since before Vell had walls:

*I will hold the door. What passes between heaven and earth shall pass through me, and I will carry the cost of the carrying.*

The vision turned. It showed Casper over years—decades? longer; the oath-keeper aged slowly, the covenant feeding them life as they fed it passage—standing at shrines, walking between worlds, brokering the small daily traffic of grace: rain here, mercy there, a child pulled from a river by a hand no one saw. And then it showed the severing. Aldric's predecessors, kings grown hungry, chipping at the covenant's edges. And Casper, cornered at last, making the terrible arithmetic: the covenant failing everywhere at once unless it was paid for, and only one coin large enough.

Themselves. Not their life—their *memory*. Every year of it. The price of reforging what kings had cracked: one soul's entire recollection of itself, poured into the stone, so that the door could close gently instead of tearing off its hinges and taking the world's prayers with it.

The vision showed Casper choosing it. Showed them smiling, actually—smiling as they knelt a second time, as the stone drank them down, as they forgot their own name mid-oath and kept swearing anyway because the body remembers duty longer than the mind does.

Showed them waking in a gutter in Vell with nothing, twelve years ago, and beginning again. Selling charms. Stealing memories. Surviving.

The light guttered.

Fenmarrow's voice, when it came, was thin as winter grass. *Now you know. The stone can be reforged—but only by the first oath-keeper, freely offering what was taken before. Your twelve years, Casper Blackwood. All of them. Everything you have clawed back. Everything you might yet make.* A pause, and in it, something like grief. *I am sorry. I was too weak to tell you sooner, and too proud to let it end unwitnessed.*

The light went out. Somewhere up-slope, a fragment of the Godstone cracked, softly, like ice on a pond in spring.

* * *

Casper didn't move for a long time.

Wren watched them stand in the dead grass with the sun coming up behind the shards, and she watched their hands—the hands that wove charms, that stole hours from strangers, that had touched her face in the drowned chapel with such careful wonder—and she saw them trembling, and then she saw them stop trembling, and somehow that was worse.

"Don't," she said.

"They didn't take it from me," Casper said. Their voice was very steady. "Did you see? I *gave* it. Both times. Nobody made me kneel."

"I don't care. I'll make you a third option. I'm resourceful. We'll go north like I said, past the salt marshes, there are islands—"

"Wren."

"—islands where witch-finders don't come, and Aldric can be half-divine all he likes on the mainland, and you can learn to fish, you'd be terrible at it, it would be very funny—"

"*Wren.*"

She stopped. Her throat hurt. She realized distantly that she had crossed the distance between them without deciding to, that she was gripping Casper's shoulders hard enough to leave marks, that she was shaking and they were not.

"I just got you back," she said, and hated how it sounded, and said it anyway. "Do you understand? I spent twelve years looking for a person who didn't know they were lost. I found you selling charms in a night market like any cutpurse, and you laughed at my jokes, and you didn't know my name, and I stayed anyway, because some part of me knew that if I could just get you to the stone—" She stopped. Breathed. "And now the stone wants the last twelve years of you, and those twelve years are *mine*, Casper. They're ours. The chapel. The salt road. All of it. If you pour them out, I'll be the only one holding them, and I don't—" Her voice failed and she despised it. "I don't want to be the only one."

Casper reached up and covered her hands with theirs.

"Wren," they said gently. "I remember."

She blinked. "What?"

"All of it. Fenmarrow didn't just show me the past—he gave me back the thread. It's all there. My mother's kitchen. The first oath. The kings. The kneeling. And—" their thumb moved over her knuckles, slow, deliberate, memorizing— "the night market. The drunk soldier whose memory I stole and immediately regretted. The candle relighting itself. You, stepping out of an alley with a bow and the most insulting opening line I've ever heard."

"'You smell like stolen goods,'" Wren quoted automatically.

"'You smell like stolen goods,'" Casper agreed. "I have all of it. I am, for the first time since I knelt in that grass, entirely myself. Whole." They smiled, and it broke her heart, because it was the smile of someone who has already decided. "Which means I finally have something worth giving."

"No."

"The covenant is dying. You felt Fenmarrow spend himself just to show me one morning. The refugees' prayers going unanswered while towns burn. The gods going out one by one like candles in a draft." Their voice stayed gentle, maddeningly gentle. "There's exactly one person alive who can fix it, and she's currently holding onto my shoulders in a field of broken god."

"*No.*"

"Yes," Casper said. "Anyway."

And Wren, huntress of Fenmarrow, who had tracked the anchor of the covenant across half a world, who had faced witch-finders and burned towns and the slow death of her own god without weeping—Wren put her forehead against Casper's chest and wept.

Casper held her. Above them the shards of the Godstone hummed, patient, waiting, and far to the south, on the wind, came the first faint smell of smoke.

"They're coming," Casper said quietly, into her hair. "Aldric. I can feel the mark Vole left on me pulling, like a hook. He means to take godhood at the moment of reforging—which means he needs me alive, and means to be standing where I stand." They lifted her chin, and their grey eyes were calm and clear and terribly kind. "So we have until he arrives. Help me up the stone, Wren. And stay angry at me the whole way. It helps."

"It doesn't help."

"It helps *me*," Casper said, and smiled again, and began to walk toward the light.
