## 9. Aldric Ascendant

The Godstone did not burn so much as *remember* fire.

Casper stood at its foot and watched the old light crawl up through the cracks—veins of gold in grey flesh, pulsing slow as a sleeping heart—and thought, absurdly, of the night market in Vell. Of the little brass scales they'd used to weigh charms. Of Mrs. Halloway's face when she'd held her husband's laughter in her palm for the first time in six years.

*You gave people back what you lost,* Wren had said once, on the Salt Road, her shoulder warm against theirs. *Maybe that wasn't theft at all. Maybe it was practice.*

"Practice," Casper murmured now, to no one. Their hands were shaking. "Twelve years of practice for one very bad afternoon."

Fenmarrow lay along the broken altar like a mountain that had decided to lie down. The wolf-god was thinner than starlight now, more suggestion than substance, his ribs showing through fur gone grey as ash. When he breathed, the tide-pools scattered across the shattered plateau filled and emptied in time.

"The oath-keeper approaches the stone," he rumbled, and his voice came up through Casper's boots as much as their ears. "It knows you. It has been waiting twelve years to be annoyed with you."

"That makes two of us," said a voice from the ridge.

Wren came down the scree with a bow slung across her back and an expression Casper had learned to read as *I am fine* and which meant, reliably, *I am not fine but will fight you about it.* She had braided her hair back for battle, which she claimed was practical and which Casper suspected was ceremonial—the huntress's braid, worn by Fenmarrow's pack since before Vell had walls.

"You're supposed to be resting," she said.

"I'm supposed to be sacrificing myself. Resting seemed off-brand."

"Casper."

"Wren." They turned to face her fully, because if this was the last hour of their remembered life, they were not going to spend it looking at their shoes. "Before anything else happens—I want you to know I've been keeping count. Of everything. The chapel. The salt on your lips. The way you say my name like you're checking I'm still there. If it all goes into the stone tonight, I want you to know I noticed. All of it. I was paying attention the whole time."

Wren's jaw worked. For a moment the huntress who had faced witch-finders and worse looked like a girl standing in an August field, which was, Casper would later learn, exactly where they had first met her, though they could not yet remember it.

"You're going to make me do all the remembering," she said finally. "That's a terrible division of labor."

"I've always been lazy."

"You have not. You once brokered a covenant between heaven and earth at nineteen years old—"

And then the sky went wrong.

* * *

It began as a silence. Not the absence of sound but the absence of *answer*—the plateau's wind died mid-gust, the tide-pools stilled, and even Fenmarrow's breathing seemed to hold itself out of politeness. Then the western ridge bloomed with torchlight, rank upon rank of it, and the drums started: the flat, patient rhythm of Aldric's witch-finders, who did not hurry because they had never once needed to.

"They followed the mark," Casper said. Serah Vole's brand still sat cold beneath their collarbone, a small silver ache. "I led them here. I'm sorry. I thought—"

"You thought correctly," Wren said grimly, nocking an arrow. "He couldn't find the covenant's heart. So he let you carry him to it. That's not your failure, Casper. That's his whole strategy. He's been doing it to kingdoms for twenty years."

Down from the ridge came the vanguard—not soldiers first, but the *hollow ones*, men and women whose eyes had been emptied and refilled with the king's purpose. Behind them, on a pale horse that cast no shadow, rode King Aldric himself.

Casper had expected a monster. What they got was a tidy man in his fifties with a scholar's stoop and a general's patience, the kind of person who apologized to furniture when he moved it. He reined in fifty yards from the Godstone and regarded it the way a man regards a house he intends to buy.

"So," Aldric called, and his voice carried without effort, the way lies do. "The oath-keeper. I wondered what became of you. You cost me a great deal, twelve years ago. A whole covenant's worth of leverage, poured down the drain to keep the gods *poor*."

"They weren't poor," Casper called back. "They were answerable. There's a difference. You'd know that if you'd ever answered to anyone."

A ripple went through the hollow ranks—something almost like discomfort, the ghost of consciences not quite fully removed. Aldric smiled as though at a fond memory.

"I offered them everything," he said. "Every god on this stone refused me. Do you know why? Because divinity, properly kept, is a *burden*. A leash. Well." He dismounted, unhurried, drawing a blade of black glass that drank the torchlight. "I have come to take the burden off their hands. And you—" the black blade tipped toward Casper, courteous as a dinner invitation— "are going to open the door. The stone answers to its oath-keeper. Refuse, and my people will spend the night teaching your huntress what her god's last breath sounds like. Accept, and everyone walks away. Even her."

"He's lying," Wren said.

"Obviously," said Casper. "But thank you for the dramatic tension."

* * *

The pack came out of the dark like the dark deciding to move.

There were eleven of them left—Fenmarrow's last, gaunt wolves with ember-light under their fur, and they hit the hollow ranks from the flank with a sound like a forest falling over. Wren loosed three arrows before Casper finished blinking; each one found a throat or an eye, unhurried, precise, a woman doing arithmetic. Then she dropped the bow, drew her knives, and waded in after her packmates, and Casper lost sight of her in the crush of bodies and torchlight and the terrible quiet efficiency of people who had stopped expecting to survive the season.

"CASPER!" Fenmarrow's voice cracked across the plateau like a glacier calving. "THE STONE! NOW! WHILE HE'S BUSY BEING FLATTERED BY HIS OWN ARMY!"

Casper ran.

The Godstone's surface was warm under their palms—warm as skin, warm as the chapel floor where Wren had kissed them for four seconds before the hunters came, warm as every good thing they had ever stolen back for someone else. The gold veins pulsed faster as they climbed, and the stone *sang*, low and wordless, a note Casper realized they knew. They had knelt on this stone at nineteen. Their body remembered the posture before their mind did: knees finding the grooves worn by a hundred generations of oath-keepers, spine straightening, hands opening.

Below, the battle turned. Casper saw it in flashes between the stone's shoulders: Wren taking a sword-cut across the thigh and not going down; a hollow one dropping with one of her knives in his chest and something like *relief* crossing his emptied face; Aldric striding through his own melee untouched, black blade raised, eyes fixed upward on the oath-keeper kneeling above him like a man watching his inheritance ripen.

"CASPER BLACKWOOD!" Aldric roared, and now there was nothing tidy in his voice at all. "OPEN IT!"

"You know," Casper said conversationally, to the stone, to the gods, to the shaking gold light rising around them, "everyone keeps shouting my name tonight. It's very flattering. It's also the first time in twelve years anyone's used it like it means something."

They pressed both palms flat against the burning stone and spoke the old oath.

They didn't learn it. It rose through them the way water rises through a root—words older than Vell, older than Aldric, older than the erasure:

*"What I was, I give. What I loved, I lend. Let the doors stand open between the high and the humble, and let no king bar them, and let no fear close them, and let this be paid gladly, gladly, gladly—"*

The stone took them at their word.

* * *

It did not hurt. That was the worst part. It felt like the market at closing time, like setting down a heavy tray at last, like exhaling after holding your breath through a whole winter.

Vell went first. The night market unspooled from them lantern by lantern—Mrs. Halloway's charm, the drunk soldier's first snowfall, the smell of fried dough and wet wool, the brass scales tipping in their hands. Casper felt each memory lift free and pour away into the gold, and felt the covenant *drink*, and felt—far off, everywhere—the old gods straighten in their long sleep like travelers hearing their names called at last.

Then the harder years. The fence's back room. The first theft, trembling, apologetic. Twelve years of learning to be small and quick and nobody. Each one went easier than the last, and Casper understood with distant clarity that the stone was being gentle with them, that somewhere Fenmarrow was spending his final strength making the giving kind.

Then Wren.

The chapel. The kiss. The salt road and the drowned bells. Her laugh, which was half bark and entirely unguarded. Her saying *I know your face, your name, your laugh,* on the first night, and Casper thinking *what a strange thing to claim.*

"No," Casper heard themselves whisper, even as the memory lifted. "No, wait, keep that one, keep—"

But they had said *gladly*. And the stone, honoring the letter of the oath, took it gladly.

The last thing Casper knew was the battle below going suddenly, utterly silent—and Wren's voice, raw and furious and breaking, screaming their name up through the gold light, and their own mouth shaping, without any memory left to tell it why, the word *"August."*

Then the covenant closed like a hand made of morning, and Casper Blackwood, empty as a clean page, fell forward into the light.

* * *

Far below, King Aldric reached the summit of the Godstone too late, black blade raised, and found the door already shut and locked and *answered*—found the gods returned and awake and looking at him with the mild, terrible attention of creditors. He opened his mouth to claim what he had come for.

The gold light took him instead.

And in the sudden hush, Wren—bleeding, kneeling, alive—caught the falling weight of the person she loved, who looked up at her with polite, blank blue eyes, and asked:

"I'm sorry—do I know you?"
