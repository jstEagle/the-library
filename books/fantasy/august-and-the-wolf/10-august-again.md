## 10. August, Again

The silence after the sealing was not an absence. It was a presence—vast and warm and breathing—and it rolled across the shattered Godstone like a tide coming home after twelve years of drought.

Wren felt it in her teeth first. Then in the old scar along her ribs where Fenmarrow's claw had once marked her as his own: a heat, a pulse, a *recognition*. The wolf-god's voice, which had been fading for years to a whisper in tide-pools and dying embers, suddenly roared back through her like spring through frozen ground.

*HUNTRESS.*

She was on her knees in the ash. She hadn't remembered falling.

*I am here,* she said—or thought, or prayed; with Fenmarrow there had never been much difference. *I'm here, I'm here—*

*SO IS EVERYTHING ELSE.* And she could have sworn, in that vast rolling voice, that the old god was laughing. *GO AND SEE.*

Around her, the Godstone was no longer shattered. The great black monolith that had stood broken on its hill since before Wren was born—the wound at the center of the world—was whole again, seams of gold light knitting through the cracks like dawn through shutters. And from those seams, from every seam, the gods were pouring back into the world.

Wren saw them because she had always been able to see them, even when the rest of Vell pretended otherwise. She saw the river-god rise out of the estuary in a column of silver spray, shaking herself like a dog, delighted. She saw the hearth-gods come home to every cold chimney in the city below, one by one by one, until the whole gray sprawl of Vell began, faintly, to glow. She saw small gods she had no names for—gods of thresholds and bees and lost buttons—scattering into the air like startled birds.

And she saw Aldric.

The king stood at the base of the stone where he had fought his way, bleeding and blazing, to seize godhood at the moment of its reforging. He had been magnificent, for about four seconds. Wren would give him that. He had torn off his crown and thrown it into the fire and opened himself up to receive divinity the way a man opens his arms to catch rain.

But divinity, it turned out, was not rain. It was a covenant. It was a *relationship*, old as the world, and it did not fall on the uninvited. It fell on the faithful, the kept, the ones whose names were written in the stone—and Casper Blackwood had written theirs there long ago, kneeling on this very hill, and had paid for the writing with everything they were.

Aldric stood with his arms open and nothing came. Nothing came, and nothing came, and then—everything left. Whatever borrowed light he had hoarded over the years of his usurpation drained out of him all at once, drawn home to its proper owners, and King Aldric of Vell crumpled like a coat dropped off a hook.

He didn't die. Wren checked, later, when the witch-finders had thrown down their iron and their witch-finder captain Serah Vole had walked away without looking back, which was its own kind of confession. He simply sat in the ash of his own ambition, an ordinary man in an extraordinary amount of trouble, muttering *I was owed, I was owed* to nobody at all.

The gods, Wren noticed, did not bother answering him. They had twelve years of prayers backed up like a flooded millrace, and they were busy.

* * *

She found Casper in a field below the Godstone hill.

It was, absurdly, a field of August grass—dry and gold and full of crickets, sloping down toward the estuary where the river-god was still showing off. A battlefield had been fought across it not two hours past. There were scorch marks. There was a dead witch-finder's helmet half-buried in the seed heads. None of it mattered to the grass, which went on being August anyway, indifferent and golden, and Casper sat down in the middle of it as though they had grown there.

They looked—Wren stopped at the edge of the field and made herself look properly, because they deserved that much—they looked like a house after everyone has moved out. Clean. Swept. Waiting. Their eyes were open and calm and utterly unoccupied. Twelve years of memory, poured into the covenant: the night market, the charms, the widow's husband's happiest hour, the salt road, the drowned chapel, the kiss—

The kiss. That had been hers too, in a sense. Given away with all the rest, spent like a coin in the great transaction, and Wren felt the loss of it as a specific ache, a tooth gone from the jaw.

But Casper was alive. Fenmarrow had promised nothing—he was a god, promises were above his pay—but the covenant had taken what was offered and left the offerer standing, empty-handed and breathing, and Wren decided on the spot that this was the single greatest miracle of the day, gods returning notwithstanding.

She crossed the grass and sat down beside them.

Casper turned their head slowly, the way you turn toward a sound in an unfamiliar room. They studied her face with polite, careful blankness. Not fear. Just nothing to hold on to.

"Hello," Casper said. Their voice was the same voice. That undid her a little.

"Hello," Wren said.

"Am I—" Casper frowned, feeling along the edges of themselves the way you pat your pockets for keys. "I don't know what I've lost. I don't know what I'm missing. I can feel the shape of it. Like a word on the tip of the tongue."

"That's the most honest thing anyone's said on this hill all day."

"Who are you?"

Wren took a breath. Somewhere behind them, the Godstone hummed like a struck bell that intended to go on ringing for a very long time. A cricket sawed away in the grass, entirely unimpressed by history.

"My name is Wren," she said. "I'm a huntress. I served the wolf-god Fenmarrow, who is currently so pleased with himself that he's howling at the moon and it isn't even dark yet. And I—" Here was the hard part. Here was the part she'd rehearsed on the salt road and gotten wrong in every rehearsal. "I know you, Casper Blackwood. I knew you before today. You're going to have to take my word for it, because I'm apparently the only one who does."

Casper blinked. "We were lovers?"

"No." Wren smiled despite herself. "You kissed me in a drowned chapel and then we got attacked, which I maintain was not my fault, and then we were both too busy almost dying to discuss it further. So. Unresolved."

"Oh," said Casper. And then, with the ghost of something—not a memory, but the groove where a memory used to sit—"That sounds like me."

"It is exactly like you." Wren pulled her knees up, settled in. The sun was low and honey-colored and the whole field smelled of warm dust. "Here's what happens now. The healers will want you. The priests will want you. The historians will want you very badly, and we are going to disappoint all of them, because you and I are going to sit in this field until sundown, and I am going to tell you everything."

"Everything?"

"Everything. From the beginning. Which, for our purposes, is a night market in Vell, twelve years ago, where a charming young thief sold a widow a charm woven from her husband's laugh, and cheated her, because the laugh wasn't her husband's—it was yours. You gave her your own happiness because you couldn't bear to sell her anything false." Wren watched that land. Watched Casper's hand drift unconsciously to their own chest. "You've always been like that. Terrible businessman. Magnificent person."

"I don't remember that."

"No. But your hands might." She nodded at them. Casper looked down at their fingers, long and quick and ink-stained, resting in the dry grass, and something in the set of them—some old habit of grace—seemed briefly to agree.

"And after tonight?" Casper asked quietly. "When you've told me everything? What then? Do I just... take your word for my whole life? Forever?"

"For now," Wren said. "For as long as it takes. And here is my vow, and I want you to understand that I have made exactly three vows in my life—one to Fenmarrow, one to my mother's grave, and this one—so weigh it accordingly." She turned to face them fully. "I will be your memory, Casper Blackwood. Every day, if you need it. I'll tell you who you were until you believe it. I'll tell you the things you loved, the people you saved, the truly appalling jokes you told. And when remembering gets too heavy, I'll carry it a while longer. For both of us. That's the vow."

Casper was silent a long moment. The crickets sang. Down in Vell, the first candles of evening were lighting themselves in windows, and this time no one was afraid of it.

"Why?" Casper asked at last. Not suspiciously. Genuinely, openly, the question of someone with no defenses left to hide behind. "Why would you do that for a stranger?"

And Wren laughed—a real laugh, helpless, half grief and half joy—and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand.

"There it is," she said. "There's the laugh. Fenmarrow told me once that a soul leaves grooves, like water wearing stone. The memories get poured out, but the grooves stay. You laughed like that the first night I met you, and you'll laugh like that when you're ninety, and you won't ever know why it sounds familiar." She bumped her shoulder gently against theirs. "As for why. Because I remember enough for both of us right now, Casper. And what I remember is that I was starting to love you somewhere around the drowned chapel, and I never got to finish. So I intend to stay and finish it. Consider yourself warned."

Casper looked at her for a long time. The empty eyes weren't empty anymore, quite—there was something moving behind them, tentative, like the first green shoot through burned ground. Not a memory. The beginning of one.

"Tell me about the chapel," Casper said.

So Wren told them. About the tide-pools and the fading god, about the cold and the candlelight and the kiss interrupted by hunters, and she was halfway through—Casper had laughed twice, and the second time had been *almost* right—when a shadow fell across the grass.

They both looked up.

Fenmarrow stood at the edge of the field in the shape of an enormous gray wolf, which was unusual, because he had been spending most of his strength staying a whisper. He was solid now. Real as weather. His eyes were amber and ancient and fixed on Casper with an expression Wren had never seen on a god's face before.

It looked, unmistakably, like gratitude.

"You're welcome," Wren said to him.

The wolf ignored her, as was traditional. He lowered his great head until his nose nearly touched Casper's knee, and breathed in, slow and deliberate, the way wolves greet what belongs to the pack. Then he spoke, and his voice was the roll of far thunder, and every cricket in the field went respectfully silent.

*LITTLE OATH-KEEPER. THE COVENANT HAS A DEBT TO PAY YOU, AND GODS PAY THEIR DEBTS IN FULL. THERE IS A THING LOST THAT MAY YET BE FOUND—NOT ALL OF WHAT YOU POURED AWAY WENT INTO THE STONE. SOME OF IT, THE STONE KEPT. AS A SEED KEEPS SPRING.*

Casper's breath caught. "What does that mean?"

Fenmarrow grinned—all teeth, all delight—and turned, and loped away up the hill toward the humming stone, pausing once at the crest to look back at them both.

*COME AND SEE WHAT AUGUST KEPT FOR YOU.*

Wren stood, and held out her hand.

"Well," she said. "Shall we?"
