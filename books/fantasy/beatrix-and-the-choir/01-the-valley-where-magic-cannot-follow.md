## 1. The Valley Where Magic Cannot Follow

The bees never asked Mara Whitlock what she had done.

That was, in her opinion, their finest quality. The bees of Vesper Hollow cared about two things: whether the clover was flowering and whether her hands smelled of smoke. They did not care that she had once been able to pull lightning out of a clear sky, or that she had been cast out of the Stormspire Order in disgrace, or that the last time she had felt her own magic move through her, it had felt like a door slamming shut behind her forever.

"Good morning, your majesties," she murmured, lifting the lid off the third hive. The colony hummed up at her, warm and golden and utterly indifferent. "Yes, yes. I know. I'm late."

She was not, in fact, late. But apologizing to bees was one of the small rituals she had built her life around, and after ten years in the valley, she had learned that a life was mostly rituals. Smoke the hive. Check the brood comb. Walk the long way home so as not to pass the eastern ridge, where the ward-stones stood in their crumbling ring and the air, even now, even after a decade, made her teeth ache like a struck tuning fork.

Because that was the joke of Vesper Hollow, the joke nobody outside the valley ever believed: the place was warded so thoroughly that magic simply could not follow you in. Cross the boundary and whatever you were—a storm-caller, a hedge-witch, an archmage with a legion at your back—became, instantly and permanently, nobody. Just a person. Just hands and breath and a heartbeat.

The Stormspire had thought exile here was a punishment.

They had been half right. It was a punishment the way a locked room is a punishment—until you realize the lock works from the inside too, and that nobody, in ten years, had come to open it.

Mara set the hive lid down gently and flexed her fingers. They ached. They always ached, on mornings like this one, when the wind came over the ridgeline from the east carrying the smell of a storm that was still two hundred miles away and would never, ever reach her. Her body remembered what it was made for. Her body had opinions about the weather the way a caged bird has opinions about the sky, and it expressed them in the only language it had left: a dull, grinding ache in the bones of her hands, a pressure behind her eyes, a restlessness that no amount of work could wear out.

"Doesn't matter," she told the bees, because telling the bees was safer than telling herself. "Doesn't matter at all."

The bees, being honest creatures, did not pretend to believe her.

* * *

She dressed for the date the way she imagined a soldier dressed for a battle she intended to lose beautifully.

This was not false modesty. Mara had a documented history with hope, and the record was not good. Hope, in her experience, was the thing that got you to say too much at a tribunal. Hope was the thing that made you believe the order would listen to reason if you just explained about the village and the flood and the children on the rooftops. Hope was the thing that had a name now—Beatrix Ives—and a laugh that carried across the market square like something worth following, and a habit of looking at Mara as though she were a puzzle that might be pleasant to solve.

Mara stood before her narrow mirror in the cottage she rented from the miller and held up one shirt, then another, then the first one again, and hated herself a little for each of them.

"You're a grown woman," she informed her reflection. "You have commanded storms. You have stood on the highest terrace of the Stormspire while the sky itself bent to listen to you. You will not be defeated by a linen shirt."

The reflection, which had her mother's dark eyes and her father's stubborn jaw, looked skeptical on both counts.

The problem—the actual, humiliating, bees-would-never-let-me-hear-the-end-of-it problem—was that Mara could handle thunder. Thunder was simple. Thunder wanted things, and you gave them, or you didn't, and either way the sky respected you afterward. What Mara could not handle was being *known*. And Beatrix Ives, with her ink-stained fingers and her terrible, wonderful habit of asking follow-up questions, wanted to know things. Where Mara had learned to read weather so well. Why she'd come to the valley. Why she never talked about before.

And Mara had spent ten years building an answer to all of those questions out of silence, and silence was not a thing you could wear on a first date.

In the end she chose the blue shirt, because Beatrix had once said—offhand, months ago, in the way people say things they don't know are being carved into stone—that blue suited her. Then she spent four minutes deciding whether remembering that made her pathetic or attentive, concluded that it was both, and went to meet her doom.

* * *

They were meeting at the Hollow's archive, which had been Beatrix's idea, and which Mara had agreed to with the particular enthusiasm of a woman agreeing to her own ambush.

"It's the most romantic building in the valley," Beatrix had said, "and I will hear no arguments."

"It's a room full of tax records."

"It's a room full of tax records *with a dome*. Mara. The dome. The light comes through the oculus in the afternoon and it looks like the whole room is full of honey."

Mara had not had a counterargument for that. Mara, it was becoming clear, did not have counterarguments for Beatrix Ives in general. This was a tactical problem she had not yet solved.

She arrived early, because of course she did, and stood across the lane from the archive pretending to examine a notice board while her heart performed maneuvers it had no business performing. The valley went about its evening around her: the baker's boy hauling the last trays in, two farmers arguing cheerfully about drainage, the lamplighter making his rounds with his long pole and his air of profound boredom. Ordinary life. The thing she had been exiled into and had, against every expectation, learned to love.

And then Beatrix came around the corner, and ordinary life briefly lost its meaning.

She was wearing her good coat—the dark green one—and she had clearly also had a crisis about shirts, because she was wearing what was unmistakably a second-best shirt under the good coat, and she was carrying a paper bag that smelled, gloriously, of the baker's honey buns.

"You brought bribes," Mara said, because her mouth had decided to act before her fear could stop it.

"I brought *provisions*," Beatrix said. "There's a difference. Bribes imply I want something. Provisions imply I'm prepared." She held out the bag. "Also I want something. Take a bun."

Mara took a bun. It was, annoyingly, perfect.

"So," Beatrix said, and there it was—that look, the puzzle-look, warm and sharp at once. "The archive. The dome. Try not to swoon."

"I make no promises."

They went in. The archivist, a ancient man named Pell who had the temperament of a well-loved boot, waved them toward the reading room with the air of a man granting a great favor and went back to his ledger. And the dome was exactly as Beatrix had promised—the late light came down through the oculus in a wide golden column, and the dust motes turned in it like slow, patient bees, and Mara felt something in her chest loosen by a single dangerous degree.

They sat at a reading table with the buns between them, and Beatrix talked, and Mara listened, and the evening did the thing that evenings with Beatrix did: it made the ache in her hands go quiet.

Beatrix talked about the choir—her choir, the one she'd inherited from the old teacher, Mrs. Aldous, and had rebuilt from nine indifferent farmers' children into something that could actually hold a tune. She talked about the boy, Tam, who sang like an angel and fidgeted like a kettle. She talked about a hymn she'd found in the archive's depths that nobody remembered teaching, with harmonies that made the hair on her arms stand up.

"And you like this?" Mara asked. "The hair-standing-up part?"

"I *love* the hair-standing-up part." Beatrix tore her second bun in half and offered Mara a piece without looking, which was somehow the most intimate thing that had happened to Mara in a decade. "That's the whole point of music, isn't it? The part where you stop being exactly one person in exactly one place. For a second you're—" She gestured with the bun, searching. "You're the chord. All of it at once."

Mara looked at her, at the light coming down through the dome and catching the ink on her fingers and the earnest, unguarded certainty of her face, and thought: *I could tell her.* The thought arrived whole and warm and catastrophic. *I could tell her what I was. What I am. She would want to know. She would ask the follow-up questions and she would not flinch.*

And then the older thought arrived, the one with ten years of practice behind it: *And then you would be the exiled storm-caller. The thing that broke its law. The reason the order still watches this valley's border. You would be known, and known things can be taken away.*

"Careful," Mara said, and heard her own voice go light and deflecting, the voice she'd worn so long it fit like her own skin. "Talk like that and I'll start thinking this valley has magic in it after all."

Beatrix laughed—she laughed easily, that was the trouble with her—and said, "Would that be so terrible?"

And Mara, who had once held lightning in her cupped hands like a bird, who had been feared in three provinces and loved in none of them, who had crossed the ward-stones with nothing but a name she was trying to outrun, looked at this woman in the honey-colored light and could not make herself say *yes*.

"It might be," she said instead, softly. "I wouldn't know. I've never tested it."

"That," said Beatrix, "sounds like a story."

"It sounds like one," Mara agreed. "It isn't one I tell on first dates."

"Then I'll have to earn a second."

The words hung there between them, half a joke, half a door left open, and Mara found she could not look away from it. Beatrix went on talking—something about the hymn, about wanting to show Mara a book she'd found, something in the archive's locked cases that the archivist never let anyone touch—and Mara nodded along and let herself, just for the length of an evening, in the honeyed light, want things.

Her hands ached. Far to the east, past the ridgeline and the ward-stones and two hundred miles of distance, thunder was rolling over country she would never touch again. The valley held its breath around her, as it always did, holding her safe and holding her still.

She should have known, she would think later, that an evening this quiet was the sky's way of gathering itself.

"Come on," Beatrix said, standing and brushing crumbs from her coat, her eyes bright with the particular mischief of a woman about to do something she's been planning all evening. "Before Pell locks up. There's a case in the back—the iron one, the one he pretends doesn't exist. I want to show you what's inside it."
