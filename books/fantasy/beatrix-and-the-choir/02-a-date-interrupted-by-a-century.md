## 2. A Date, Interrupted by a Century

Mara Whitlock had survived lightning strikes, a tribunal of seven archmages, and one truly catastrophic attempt at making cheese. She could survive a first date.

Probably.

She stood outside the Hollow's archive at dusk, holding a jar of honey she had absolutely not overthought, and rehearsed her opening line for the fourth time. *I brought you honey.* No. Too much like a bribe. *The bees wanted you to have this.* Worse — that made the bees sound like matchmakers, and honestly, they'd earned no such credit after the incident with the bonnet.

"You're talking to the jar," said Beatrix Ives.

Mara looked up. Beatrix was coming down the archive steps with her coat half-buttoned and her hair escaping its braid in the way it always did by evening, as though the day itself kept tugging at her. She was smiling. That was either very good or very bad.

"I was negotiating," Mara said. "Honey is a serious commodity."

"For what?"

"Your continued company."

Beatrix laughed — a bright, unguarded sound that made two pigeons on the eaves relocate in disgust — and took the jar. "You know most people bring flowers."

"Flowers die. Honey keeps. I'm planning ahead."

"Ahead to what?"

And there it was: the question Mara had been circling for ten years without ever landing on an answer. Ahead to what? To a second date? To a winter? To being known — actually known, storm and scar and all — by someone who might still choose to stay?

"To dessert," Mara said, which was cowardly, but the kind of cowardice that came with plum cake, so she felt mostly fine about it.

* * *

Inside, the archive smelled of dust and lamp oil and old paper — a smell Mara privately considered more honest than any perfume. The Vesper Hollow archive was less a grand library than a long room of shelves and cases, presided over by a stove that heated exactly one corner of the room and no others, out of what seemed like pure spite.

"It's not exactly dancing," Beatrix said, lighting a candle from the stove and setting it between them on the reading table. "I know. But I thought—" She hesitated, and Mara watched her decide to be honest, watched it happen the way one watches a bird decide to sing. "I thought you might like somewhere quiet. You always look like you're listening for something."

Mara's chest did something complicated. "I'm always listening for thunder," she said lightly. "Occupational habit."

"Occupational?"

"Beekeeping. Storms are bad for hives." True, technically. Also a lie so large it needed its own barn.

"Well." Beatrix pulled a chair out for her, formal as a dance instructor. "Then let's hope the valley behaves tonight."

They read. That was the date, and it was better than any Mara had imagined during the ten years she hadn't allowed herself to imagine dates. Beatrix found a folio of old valley songs and hummed fragments under her breath, absently, the way other people tapped their fingers — and every time she did, the candle flame leaned toward her like a listener. Mara noticed. Mara decided not to notice. There was a great deal of that going around.

"Tell me something true," Beatrix said suddenly, looking up from the folio. "As a date activity. One true thing each. Fair trade."

Mara turned her cup of tea slowly on its saucer. "That's a dangerous game in this valley."

"Why?"

Because the ward strips magic but never secrets, Mara thought. Because I've been carrying mine for ten years like a stone sewn into my coat. Because if I say one true thing aloud I don't know how many will follow it out.

"Your turn first," she said.

Beatrix considered. "All right. True thing: I moved here because my grandmother used to sing me a song about this valley, and when I finally saw it, it matched. Every hill, every bend of the river. Like walking into a memory I'd borrowed." She shrugged, embarrassed. "Silly."

"Not silly," Mara said quietly. It was, in fact, deeply strange, and she filed the strangeness away next to all the other things about Beatrix Ives that didn't quite add up — the way animals trusted her instantly, the way her choir sounded like weather. "My turn. True thing: I haven't been on a date in ten years."

"That's not surprising, you live in—"

"No," Mara said. "Ten years exactly. To the week."

Beatrix set down her cup. The candle flame stood perfectly straight between them, unnaturally still, though there was a draft coming under the door that had been worrying the flame all evening. "Mara—"

And then the candle guttered.

Not the way candles usually gutter — not a flutter, not a lean. It dropped flat, as if pressed down by a thumb, and the whole room went dark except for one thin ribbon of smoke rising off the wick. The stove in its spiteful corner went cold. Mara felt the change before she understood it: a pressure in her ears, like standing on a mountaintop, like the held breath before a storm breaks.

"Beatrix," she said. "Don't move."

"Why? What is—" Beatrix's voice caught. "Mara, the case."

Across the room, the iron-bound display case against the far wall — locked, Mara knew, because Beatrix had tried it earlier and joked about the lock being older than the town — had fallen open. Not broken. Opened. The glass lid lay back gently, almost courteously, and inside, something was glowing faintly, the way foxfire glows, the way certain pages glow when they've been waiting a very long time to be read.

Neither of them remembered crossing the room. Later, Mara would swear the floor simply arranged itself so that they arrived.

It was a book. Small, leather-bound, cracked along the spine, resting on a bed of cloth gone gray with age. A brass plate on the case read, in letters worn nearly smooth: *ACCESSIONED 1899. DO NOT OPEN.*

"Well," said Beatrix. "Someone should have listened."

"Beatrix."

"It opened itself, Mara. I feel like the warning has been overtaken by events."

This was, infuriatingly, reasonable. Mara reached in anyway — some instinct older than caution — and lifted the book out. It was lighter than it should have been, the way a bird is light, the way a held breath is light. On the flyleaf, in faded brown ink:

*A record of things to come, set down by the hand of Edith Ives, Warden of Vesper Hollow, in the year 1899, who saw it in the water and the wax and wished she had not.*

Mara's hands went still.

"Ives," said Beatrix, behind her. Very quietly. "That's my family name."

* * *

Beatrix had never put much stock in her family name. It was just a name — the kind that got misspelled on school rosters, the kind her grandmother said with a particular weight sometimes, late at night, humming that valley song. She had assumed the weight was grief. Grandmothers were allowed grief.

Now she stood in a dark archive watching Mara hold a book that her own great-great-something had written, and the weight rearranged itself into something else entirely.

"Careful," she whispered, absurdly, as if the book were a sleeping animal. Which, she was beginning to suspect, it was.

Mara turned to the last page. And stopped breathing.

Beatrix saw it happen — saw the color leave Mara's face in the candleless dark, saw her grip the table's edge with her free hand as though the floor had tilted. "What?" Beatrix asked. "What does it say?"

Mara didn't answer. So Beatrix did the thing she perhaps should not have done, the thing she would think about later with the specific regret of a person who has touched a door marked *do not touch*: she leaned in and read it aloud.

"*On the night the ward grows thin,*" she read, and her own voice sounded strange to her, resonant, too large for the room, "*two shall come to the house of records by candlelight. One shall carry honey and hide a storm behind her teeth. The other shall wear a blue coat with a loose thread at the cuff, and her braid shall be undone by evening.*"

Beatrix's hand rose, without her permission, to her cuff. There was a loose thread there. She had been meaning to fix it for a week.

"*Their names are written here as they are written in the ward itself:*" — and here the page showed two names in ink that somehow, impossibly, still gleamed wet — "*Mara Whitlock. Beatrix Ives.*"

The silence afterward was total. Even the river outside, even the wind in the pines — everything held its breath. Somewhere very far away, beyond the rim of the valley, beyond the ridge where the ward-stones stood their ancient watch, thunder rolled. Once. Low. Patient. Like something clearing its throat.

Mara closed the book with a snap that echoed.

"Okay," Beatrix said, because someone had to say something, and her voice came out approximately one octave too high. "Okay. That's — coincidences exist. Names repeat. Blue coats are common. Loose threads are practically universal—"

"Beatrix."

"—and honey, plenty of people bring honey, honey is a normal gift, statistically—"

"*Beatrix.*"

"What?"

Mara held up the book. In the dark, faintly, the last page was glowing through the cover — a soft, patient gold, like a window lit in a distant house. Like something waiting to be let in.

"The candle went out," Mara said, "the moment we stopped pretending this was a normal evening. The case opened for us. The book is glowing. And there is thunder on the ridgeline, which — trust me — should not be possible tonight. The sky over this valley hasn't cleared enough for storms in ten years." She exhaled slowly. "I need to tell you something true. The game we were playing. I need to tell you several true things, and I need you to not run."

Beatrix looked at her — at this woman she'd been falling for since spring, this quiet beekeeper with storm-grey eyes and calloused hands and a laugh she rationed like wartime sugar — and discovered that fear and tenderness could occupy the same heartbeat without crowding each other.

"I brought plum cake," Beatrix said. "Whatever you're about to tell me, I'm going to want to sit down for it. Possibly forever."

Mara made a sound that was half laugh, half something rawer. "There's a bench by the stove."

"There's always a bench by the stove. It's the only warm spot in this building. Whoever designed this place hated scholars."

"Mm." Mara tucked the book under her arm, and it pulsed once against her ribs, warm through her shirt, like a second heartbeat that had opinions about the seating arrangements. "Beatrix — the woman who wrote this. Edith Ives. Your family name isn't just a name in this valley. Do you understand? Wardens built the ward. Wardens *were* the ward. If your blood wrote this prophecy—"

"Then maybe," Beatrix said slowly, following the thought down into its cold, vertiginous depths, "my grandmother wasn't singing me a lullaby at all."

Outside, the thunder rolled again — closer now, or louder, hard to tell — and the birds of Vesper Hollow, every robin and wren and finch in the valley, went silent all at once, the way a choir goes silent when the conductor lifts a hand.

In the dark archive, the book glowed on, patient as a lit window.

And Beatrix Ives, ordinary choir teacher, owner of one blue coat with a loose thread, sat down on the bench by the stove and said, "Start from the beginning. Whatever your beginning is. We've got cake, and apparently, we've got until the end of the world."

Mara sat beside her. Close enough that their shoulders touched.

"My beginning," she said, "starts with why I really came to this valley. And you're not going to believe a word of it until you see the ward-stones yourself."

"Then show me," said Beatrix, and reached for the candle to relight it — and found, when she touched the wick, that it flared alight beneath her fingers without any flame at all.
