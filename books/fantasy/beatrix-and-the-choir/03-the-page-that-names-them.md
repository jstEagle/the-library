## 3. The Page That Names Them

The candle had gone strange.

Beatrix noticed it the way she noticed wrong notes—first as a small itch at the edge of attention, then all at once. The flame wasn't flickering anymore. It stood perfectly still, a little golden teardrop suspended above the wick, as though someone had painted it there.

"Mara," she said. "Is your candle doing that too?"

Mara looked up from the second shelf of the archive's eastern wall, where she'd been pretending to alphabetize ledgers she clearly couldn't read and didn't care about. Her eyes went to the flame. Something in her face tightened—not fear exactly, but the look of a person who has heard a floorboard creak in a house they thought was empty.

"Beatrix." Her voice was very calm, which from Mara, Beatrix was learning, meant the opposite of calm. "Put it down."

"Put down what? The candle?"

"The candle. Slowly."

Beatrix set it on the reading table with exaggerated care, the way one sets down a sleeping cat that has recently expressed opinions about being moved. The flame did not so much as lean. Around them, the archive of Vesper Hollow sat in its usual amber hush—dust motes hanging motionless in the light, shelves of valley records going back two centuries, the smell of old paper and beeswax and, faintly, of rain that hadn't fallen yet.

It had been, until four minutes ago, an excellent date.

* * *

In fairness, Beatrix reflected, most of her dates did not end with locked cases opening themselves. But then, most of her dates were not with Mara Whitlock, who had moved to the Hollow eight years ago with no history, no family name anyone recognized, and a way of looking at thunderstorms that Beatrix could only describe as *homesick*. Most of her dates were not conducted entirely inside a valley that, according to three separate farmers and one extremely confident goat-herder, magic simply could not enter.

"You said this place was boring," Beatrix had teased when they'd arrived, and Mara had said, "Boring is my favorite thing," with such naked sincerity that Beatrix had nearly kissed her right there among the tax rolls of 1841.

She was still thinking about kissing her when the case fell open.

It was a glass-fronted display case bolted to the wall—the kind used for things too fragile or too precious to shelve. It held, as far as Beatrix knew, nothing but a cracked communion pitcher and a stuffed owl with an attitude problem. There was no lock on it that Beatrix had ever seen; the curator, old Mr. Fenwick, liked to say the case was sealed by "a mechanism beyond my pay grade," which everyone understood to mean he'd lost the key in 1987.

At 7:42 in the evening, with their candles burning and the last daylight dying blue outside the windows, the case had made a sound like a held breath being released—and swung open.

Inside, where the owl should have been, lay a book.

Not a grand book. That was the first thing Beatrix noticed, before the dread arrived. It was small, leather-bound, its corners worn round with handling, the kind of book a person might carry in a coat pocket. Its cover bore no title, only a stamped design: a circle of interlocking lines, like ripples in a pond seen from directly above, and at the center, a single note—a musical notation, a whole note, drawn with the careful precision of someone who loved it.

A date was pressed into the leather beneath it. **1899.**

"Mr. Fenwick will want to know about this," Beatrix had said, reaching for it.

And Mara—who had been standing three feet away, who had crossed the room faster than Beatrix would have believed possible—had caught her wrist and said, "Don't," in a voice Beatrix had never heard from her. Not sharp. Frightened.

They had stood there a long moment, hand on wrist, breathing.

"It's just a book," Beatrix said gently. "It's over a hundred years old. Whatever it is, it already happened."

"That is," Mara said, "either the most comforting or least comforting thing you could possibly say, and I genuinely don't know which."

But she had let go. And Beatrix, because she was who she was—a woman who had spent eleven years teaching children to sing and had never once left a question unanswered if she could help it—had opened the book.

* * *

Now the candle burned without moving, and the book lay open on the table between them, and Beatrix was reading.

Most of it was ordinary prophecy-book stuff, or what she imagined ordinary prophecy-book stuff to be: dense, self-serious verse about *the wheel* and *the turning* and *the shadow between the hills*. The handwriting shifted every few pages—different hands, different decades, as though the book had been added to by a succession of people who each believed, sincerely and incorrectly, that they were writing about their own time.

"See?" Beatrix said, tracing a line with her finger. "*When the seventh bell tolls upon the mountain, the shepherd shall divide his flock.* This is from 1911. There's no bell on the mountain. There's never been a bell on the mountain. Whoever wrote this was guessing, Mara. Educated guessing, but guessing."

"Mm," said Mara, who had not relaxed since the case opened. She stood with her arms folded, positioned—Beatrix realized—between Beatrix and the door. Not blocking it. Guarding it. "Keep going."

"Why? You clearly think something's wrong."

"I think," Mara said carefully, "that I'd like to know what we're dealing with before it deals with us. Humor me. Read the last page."

"The last page is blank. Look—" Beatrix flipped to the end, and stopped.

It had been blank when she'd opened the book. She was almost certain it had been blank. She remembered the specific disappointment of it, the little deflation—*all that atmosphere for nothing*. She had even said so, out loud, and Mara had made a sound low in her throat that might have been relief.

The page was not blank now.

The ink was still settling into the paper as Beatrix watched, letters surfacing like fish rising in a pond, one after another after another, until a full passage stood gleaming wetly in the candlelight. The handwriting was not any of the ones that came before. It was new. It was, Beatrix realized with a lurch, *neat*. Round, careful, teacherly handwriting. The kind of handwriting that wrote choir rehearsal schedules and comments on music exams.

It was, in point of fact, Beatrix's own handwriting.

"Don't read it out loud," Mara said.

Beatrix hadn't told her what the handwriting looked like. She was sure she hadn't. Her mouth had gone dry and her mind had gone white and she had said nothing at all—but Mara was watching her face the way you watch weather, and whatever she saw there made her step forward and put her hand flat on the page, as if to hold the words down.

"Beatrix. Listen to me. Some things are doors. Reading aloud is how you walk through them. We close the book, we go find Mr. Fenwick, we let this be his problem—"

"Mara." Beatrix's voice came out smaller than she intended. "It's dated tonight."

Mara went still.

"*The ninth of October,*" Beatrix read silently, her eyes moving over the fresh ink while her lips refused to cooperate. "*In the hundred-and-twenty-fifth year of the ward.* Tonight. Today was the ninth of October. Mr. Fenwick had mentioned it, complaining that the archive's heating allowance wouldn't arrive until November, and Beatrix had made some joke about the ninth of October being the most forgettable date of the year.*

*The ninth of October. In the hundred-and-twenty-fifth year of the ward.*

Vesper Hollow's founding festival was celebrated every spring, but the ward itself—the invisible boundary the valley was named for, the thing that kept out everything the valley wanted kept out—had a birthday too, and Beatrix only knew it because her grandmother had been pedantic about local history. The ward had been raised in the autumn of 1900. Which made this year its hundred and twenty-fifth.

Below the date, the page named the hour. *When the candle stands still.*

Beatrix looked at the candle. The candle stood still.

Below that, the page named the place. *In the house of records.*

They were in the archive. The archive was, technically and officially, the House of Records; the brass plaque outside said so, and children on field trips snickered at it every year.

And below that—below all of it, in the last lines, the ones the ink was finishing even now, the final strokes curling into place like a signature—

"*Two women,*" Beatrix whispered. She couldn't help it. It was barely reading aloud. It was hardly anything. "*One who carries the storm and one who carries the song. The storm-caller who renounced her sky. The singer who does not know her blood. When the ninth hour finds them together among the records, the ward shall know its heirs, and the sky shall know its own, and the last quiet year of Vesper Hollow shall end.*"

The silence afterward was total.

Then Mara said, very quietly, "I asked you not to do that."

"I know." Beatrix's hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against the table. "I know. I'm sorry. I—it was in my handwriting, Mara. My handwriting. How could it be in my handwriting? I've never seen this book before in my life."

"I don't know."

"'The storm-caller who renounced her sky.'" Beatrix looked up. Mara's face had gone the color of paper. "That's a figure of speech, isn't it? That's poetry. People say things like that. 'Storm-caller' is just—"

"Beatrix."

"—it's just a dramatic way of saying someone with a temper, probably, someone whose grandmother was weather-sensitive—"

"*Beatrix.*"

"What?" Beatrix demanded, and her voice cracked in the middle of the word, and that was when she realized she was crying, which was absurd, because nothing had happened, nothing had actually happened, it was only words on a page—

Except the words weren't on the page anymore.

* * *

Beatrix saw it happen and would spend years afterward failing to describe it properly. The ink lifted. That was the closest she could ever come: the ink lifted, the way steam lifts off morning fields, the letters peeling up off the paper in fine dark-gold threads, gathering, swirling, the whole passage rising into the air between them like a constellation assembling itself. For one impossible moment Beatrix could read it floating there, glowing softly, *her own handwriting written in light*—

—and then it moved.

It moved the way water moves when it finds a crack. It poured toward them. Toward her. She felt it touch her skin and it did not burn, it *sank*, a warmth sliding under her skin along her forearms, up past her elbows, spreading through her chest like swallowing something hot and sweet and enormous, and she heard Mara make a sound—her name, maybe, or a word in a language Beatrix didn't know—and then the light found Mara too, and Mara did not run, though every line of her body wanted to. She stood in it with her eyes shut and her jaw set, the way a person stands in cold surf, letting it take her.

Then it was done. The light was gone. The page was blank again, blanker than blank, the paper smooth and pale as an eggshell, as though it had never held anything at all.

The candle flames leaned—finally, belatedly, as if catching up—and steadied.

Outside the tall archive windows, Vesper Hollow lay in its evening blue: lamplit windows, chimney smoke, the far green shoulder of the ridge. Everything exactly as it had been.

Except the birds.

Beatrix noticed it because the birds were always there. Every evening of her life in the Hollow, the rooks had come down off the churchyard elms at dusk in a great ragged argument, hundreds of them, wheeling and complaining, the soundtrack of every autumn she could remember. Her choir kids imitated them when they got silly. She had once written an arrangement with a rook-call in the alto part as a joke.

The sky over the valley was empty. No rooks. No sparrows in the eaves. No late thrush. Nothing. The silence was so complete she could hear her own pulse in her ears, and under it—faint, rhythmic, patient—the sound of the candle flames burning.

"Mara," she said. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "Where are the birds?"

"Gone to ground." Mara's eyes were open now. They were fixed on the window, on the ridge beyond it, and her expression was one Beatrix had never seen on her: an old grief wearing a new alarm. "Birds know. Before anything else happens, birds know."

"Before what happens?"

Mara didn't answer. Because that was the moment the thunder came.

It rolled down from beyond the ridge—from *outside*, from the world past the valley's rim, from the direction of the high passes where no storm cloud hung tonight, where the sky through the window was clear and star-pricked and innocent. The thunder was distant and enormous, the kind of sound that makes your ribs hum, and it did not fade the way thunder fades. It rolled once, paused—*listening*, Beatrix thought wildly, it sounds like it's listening—and rolled again, closer, though nothing moved in the sky.

The ward, said a voice in her head that was not hers and somehow was, *the ward hears it too.*

"Get away from the windows," Mara said. She had moved without seeming to cross the space, and her hand closed around Beatrix's arm—gentle, but with a grip like iron underneath, the grip of someone who had done this before, whoever she really was, whatever she really was. "We're leaving. Now. You're coming to my house, and you're going to tell me everything your grandmother ever said to you, every story, every half-remembered piece of valley nonsense, and you are not going to leave my sight until I understand what—"

"*What I am?*" Beatrix laughed, and it came out broken. "Mara, I'm a choir teacher. I have thirty-one students and a kettle with a chip in it and a mortgage. I sang a duet at the harvest festival in a cardigan. I am nobody's heir. I'm not *anything's* heir. That page is wrong. Books get things wrong. Prophecies especially—they're vague on purpose, that's the whole con—"

"The page described your clothes."

Beatrix stopped.

Because it had. In the lines she'd read aloud, in the ones she'd read silently, in the ones she'd only glimpsed as the ink finished itself—there had been details. *The singer in the grey wool, with chalk dust on her sleeve.* Beatrix looked down at her sleeve. Grey wool. Chalk dust, from holding the board eraser during rehearsal that afternoon, because Tam Voss had knocked it off the ledge again.

Her knees decided, independently, that sitting down was happening. Mara steered her into the curator's chair with surprising tenderness and crouched in front of her, both hands now wrapped around Beatrix's cold ones.

"Okay," Mara said. Her voice had changed—lower, steadier, stripped of its usual dry deflection. This was a voice that had talked people through something terrible before. Beatrix found herself wondering, distantly, *when?* "Look at me. Breathe. In for four, hold for four—that's a musician trick, right? You'd know it better than I do."

"That's box breathing," Beatrix managed. "My students use it before recitals."

"There. See? You're already teaching me. You're fine." Mara's thumbs moved over her knuckles, slow and deliberate. "Listen to me. Whatever that page was, whatever it thinks we are—you were a person five minutes ago and you're a person now. Nothing that matters has changed."

"Everything's changed." Beatrix's laugh wobbled. "The birds are gone, Mara. The birds are never gone. And there's thunder coming from a clear sky, and a hundred-year-old book wrote my *handwriting*, and it called you a storm-caller, and you didn't say 'what a strange thing to write,' you said '*don't read it out loud*' like you *knew*—like you've known rules about this sort of thing your whole life—"

Mara flinched. It was small, but Beatrix had spent eleven years watching children try to hide things, and she knew a flinch when she saw one.

"You do know," Beatrix said quietly. "You know exactly what that book was talking about. Don't you."

The thunder rolled again, nearer now, and beneath the archive's floorboards—Beatrix felt it more than heard it, a deep hum in the soles of her feet, in her teeth, in the hollow of her throat where every note she'd ever sung seemed to live—something vast and old turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to wake.

Mara closed her eyes. When she opened them, she looked less like a woman on a first date and more like a soldier reporting for a war she'd hoped was over.

"Yes," she said. "I know. And I'll tell you everything—I swear I will, tonight, over the strongest tea I own. But first I need you to stand up, because I don't think we should be sitting still right now, and Beatrix—" her grip tightened, "—whatever happens next, whatever you see me do: remember that ten minutes ago you were thinking about kissing me, and I was thinking about letting you."

Despite everything—the dead sky, the waking ground, the blank page that had eaten its own words—Beatrix huffed out something that was almost a laugh.

"That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me," she said, "and I hate it."

"Welcome to knowing me," said Mara, and pulled her to her feet as the thunder reached the ridgeline, and the first stone of the old ward-stone circle, three miles east, began—though neither of them would learn it until morning—to glow.
