## 4. What the Ward Remembers

Mara had three rules for living in Vesper Hollow, and she'd kept all of them for ten years.

One: never touch the ward-stones at the valley's edge. Two: never speak of storms in the present tense. Three: never let anyone close enough to ask why her hands trembled when thunder rolled over the ridgeline.

She was about to break two of them before breakfast, and possibly a third by sundown, because Beatrix Ives was standing in her kitchen holding two cups of chicory coffee and looking at her like the answer to every question she hadn't yet asked was written somewhere on Mara's face.

"You didn't sleep," Beatrix said.

"I slept."

"You have the look of a woman who slept the way a stone sleeps in a river. Constantly moved, never resting."

Mara took the cup because it gave her hands something honest to do. "The bees were restless."

"The bees," Beatrix repeated, with the gentle skepticism of a choir teacher who could hear a false note from across a sanctuary. "At midnight."

"They're sensitive to barometric pressure."

"Mara." Beatrix set her own cup down on the counter with a small, deliberate click. "I read a book last night that knew my name. It knew what I was wearing. It described the candle going out *before* the candle went out. I have been awake since then, and I have made a list of everything I know for certain, and it is a very short list. It contains: I am a choir teacher. I like you. And something in this valley has been waiting for me."

The morning light came through the kitchen window the way it always did in early autumn—thin and gold, catching the dust above the sink, making even the ordinary look briefly consecrated. Mara had spent ten years learning to love that light precisely because it demanded nothing of her. No power. No price. Just morning, arriving like a neighbor.

"I can't tell you everything," Mara said. "Not because I don't want to. Because some of it will sound like madness until you've seen it."

"Then show me the part that won't sound like madness."

"It's all the same part."

Beatrix smiled—the particular smile that had undone Mara at the harvest dance, patient and warm and utterly without guile. "Good. I've always wanted to see madness. I hear it has excellent harmonies."

* * *

They walked out past the orchard while the mist still clung to the low fields, Beatrix carrying a basket she'd packed without being asked—bread, hard cheese, a jar of Mara's own honey, which struck Mara as either very sweet or faintly presumptuous. The path climbed through birch and bracken toward the valley's western rim, where the land rose in folds of grey granite, and where Mara had not gone in ten years except once a year, at midwinter, when she walked close enough to confirm the stones still stood and then walked away again before they could notice her.

Noticing was the thing. Everything here came down to noticing.

"You're afraid," Beatrix observed, half a switchback up.

"I'm cautious. There's a difference. Fear is loud. Caution is just good manners toward danger."

"And what are we being polite to?"

Mara stopped where the trees thinned. Below them the whole valley lay out in its morning colors—smoke from the bakery chimney, the schoolhouse roof, the millpond holding a perfect copy of the sky. Above them, half-hidden in gorse and lichen, stood the first of the ward-stones.

It was not impressive, if you didn't know what you were looking at. A standing granite pillar, taller than a man, leaning slightly east like an old woman listening for a knock. Its surface was carved with spirals worn nearly smooth, and it was furred with moss, and birds had been using it as a perch for longer than anyone could remember.

But Mara knew what she was looking at. She had grown up among mages who spoke of Vesper Hollow's ward the way sailors speak of the sea—something vast, ancient, and not to be trusted. The Hollow was one of the last warded places in the world, a valley sealed inside a lattice of sung stone that unraveled magic the way water unravels sugar. Nothing cast within its borders held. Nothing cast against them from outside could enter. For a hundred years it had simply *worked*, silent and self-renewing, and the people inside had forgotten it was there at all, the way you forget your own heartbeat.

"That's it?" Beatrix said. "That's the famous ward?"

"One of thirty-one anchor-stones around the rim. That's the nearest one. There's a heart somewhere under the valley, but nobody alive knows where."

"What does it do?"

"It keeps magic out." Mara hesitated. "And in."

"In?"

"People like me." She said it lightly, testing the weight of it. "Come here. Stand where I'm standing."

Beatrix stepped beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, and Mara watched her face as the ward noticed her.

It happened the way it always happened to ordinary folk—a faint pressure behind the eyes, a hum felt in the teeth rather than heard, the sense of walking through a curtain of cool water. Beatrix blinked. "Oh. That's strange. Like the moment before a sneeze, but it never comes."

Then the stone flared.

Light ran up the worn spirals like fire up a fuse—not the sickly green of old ward-magic, which Mara remembered well from her training, but a deep gold, warm as lamplight, brightening until the moss itself seemed lit from beneath. The hum deepened into something almost like a chord. Almost like—

Almost like a voice, clearing its throat after a hundred years of silence.

Beatrix stumbled back a step. Mara caught her arm. The light pulsed twice more, slow and deliberate, like a great heart remembering how to beat, and then sank back into the granite, leaving only a shimmer along the carvings that faded by degrees, reluctant, like someone letting go of your hand.

Neither of them spoke for a long moment. Somewhere downslope, a jay screamed once and went quiet.

"That," Beatrix said finally, in the level voice of a woman determined not to shriek, "was not a sneeze."

"No."

"It recognized me."

"Yes."

"How? I've lived here six years. I shop at the same market as everyone else. I teach children to sing 'The Miller's Daughter' badly. I am the least magical person in this entire valley, Mara. I once tried to make tea with a spell-book as a joke and nothing happened, not even steam."

Mara turned to face her. This was the moment. She had rehearsed versions of this confession for ten years—in the bath, in the bee-yard, in the dark—and every version had ended with the listener backing away. But Beatrix was still holding her hand, and her grip hadn't changed, and Mara found that all her rehearsals evaporated anyway, the way fog burns off when the sun commits.

"My name isn't just Mara Whitlock of nowhere," she said. "I was born in the Stormspire, in the order of storm-callers. We raise weather. We bind lightning. We're the reason the eastern kingdoms pay tribute and plant their crops by our calendars. I was trained from the age of seven. I was *good*, Beatrix. I could pull a thunderhead apart with my hands."

Beatrix said nothing, but her thumb moved once across Mara's knuckles, and Mara kept going because stopping would have been worse than any ending.

"When I was twenty-two, there was a village called Lowmarsh, outside our territory. Drought year. Their wells were dust, their crops were ash, and the order wouldn't help because the local lord hadn't paid his weather-tithe. So I went anyway. Alone. I called rain for them—three days of it, enough to save the harvest and fill every cistern they had." She laughed, short and unamused. "Do you know what the law says? Weather belongs to those who pay for it. Rain given freely is theft. I stole a sky, and I gave it away, and for that they stripped my rank, burned my name from the rolls, and exiled me. They meant to send me somewhere worse. But the tribunal had a sense of irony. They sent me here. To the valley where magic cannot follow. To live out my life as an ordinary woman, powerless, among people who'd never know what I was."

"And the bees?"

"The bees are real. The bees are the one thing I chose." Her voice cracked on it, and she pressed on. "Ten years, Beatrix. Ten years of being no one, and I was almost happy. I had rules. Don't go near the stones. Don't talk about storms. Don't let anyone—" She stopped.

"Don't let anyone close enough to be hurt when they find out," Beatrix finished quietly.

"Something like that."

Beatrix looked at the ward-stone, its spirals still holding a ghost of gold, and then back at Mara, and whatever Mara had braced herself for, it wasn't what came: Beatrix reached up and tucked a wind-blown strand of hair behind Mara's ear, unhurried, as though they had all the time in the world and the world agreed.

"You gave a village rain," she said, "and they punished you for it. And you've spent ten years thinking that makes you dangerous."

"It makes me *exiled*. Those aren't the same thing, but they weigh the same."

"Mara Whitlock." Beatrix's voice was soft and absolutely certain, the voice she used when a frightened first-year singer forgot the words in front of everyone. "You showed me a stone that lit up like a festival lantern when I touched its attention, and a history that explains why you flinch at thunder, and the thing I feel most strongly right now is that I would like to sit down and eat the bread I brought, because confessions are hungry work. Is that all right?"

Mara blinked. "That's—yes. That's all right."

"Good." Beatrix settled onto the flat rock beside the path and opened the basket with the composure of a picnic general. "Because I have questions, and I intend to ask all of them, and you're going to answer every one, and then—" she paused, knife poised over the cheese, suddenly less certain than she'd been all morning, "—then I think you should tell me why the stone glowed for *me*. Because I've been turning it over, and there's a word sitting at the bottom of it that I don't want to say out loud yet."

Mara sat down beside her. The valley spread below, ordinary and golden and utterly unaware that anything had changed. But the stone at their backs still hummed, very faintly, like a struck bell that hadn't finished ringing, and when Beatrix handed her the bread their fingers brushed and the hum deepened—one clear note, unmistakable now, rising from the granite like recognition.

Like a name, remembered.

"Say it anyway," Mara said.

Beatrix looked at the stone, then at her own open palm, and said, in the small voice of someone reading a diagnosis:

"Warden. I think I'm a warden's daughter."

And far below them, at the valley's edge where the mist still pooled, the second ward-stone flared gold in answer—then the third, and the fourth, a line of quiet fire kindling around the rim of Vesper Hollow, one after another, like lamps being lit down a very long road.
