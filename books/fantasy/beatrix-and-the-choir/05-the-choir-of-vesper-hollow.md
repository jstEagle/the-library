## 5. The Choir of Vesper Hollow

Beatrix Ives had conducted the Vesper Hollow choir for six years, and in that time she had learned three immutable truths: old Mr. Fenwick would always come in a half-beat early on the word "grace," the Prewitt twins could not be seated next to each other without an incident involving at least one hymnal, and no rehearsal had ever ended on time.

She was counting on none of these things to save her. But standing before her thirty-odd singers on Thursday evening, watching them open their books to the same worn hymnal they'd used since before she was born, she found herself hoping anyway.

"From the top," she said. "And please—*please*—someone remind Mr. Fenwick that 'grace' arrives when I say it arrives."

A ripple of laughter. Fenwick raised his chin with tremendous dignity. "I have been singing 'grace' early since 1961, Miss Ives. I don't intend to reform now."

"Then we'll simply build the cathedral around you," Beatrix said, and lifted her hands.

The hymn was "The Valley's Keeping," number 112 in the old green book, and it was the strangest piece of music Beatrix had ever taught—which is to say, she hadn't taught it at all.

That was the thing she couldn't stop turning over. She'd discovered it two nights ago, after everything at the archive, after the light had sunk into her skin and Mara's hand had closed around hers like a lifeline thrown into dark water. She hadn't slept. So she'd done what she always did when she couldn't sleep: she'd sat down at the piano in the empty chapel and played through the hymnal, page by page, looking for something steady, something ordinary, something that belonged to the world as it had been before a book dated 1899 decided to describe her clothes.

She'd reached page 112 and stopped cold.

She knew the tune. Of course she knew it—the whole valley knew it; they sang it every harvest festival and every funeral, and Beatrix had been humming it since childhood. But the words under her fingers were not the words she knew. The familiar verses about grain and hearthlight were still there, yes—but threaded between them, in ink that looked older than the rest of the page, ran a verse she had never seen:

*When the ward grows thin as morning mist,*
*and riders gather on the rim,*
*the storm shall kneel, the song shall rise,*
*and hollow voices answer him.*

Her name wasn't in it. Neither was Mara's. But *storm* and *song* sat in those lines like two chairs set out for guests, and Beatrix had read them four times with her heart hammering against her ribs before she noticed the truly impossible part: the melody beneath the new verse fit perfectly. Not approximately. Perfectly—as though the verse had always lived inside the hymn, folded up small, waiting for someone to unfold it.

She had checked three other hymnals. The chapel's copy, the school's copy, the one in the archive that smelled of dust and candle smoke. All identical. All with the verse there, patient as a spider.

No one remembered teaching it. No one remembered writing it. When she'd asked old Mrs. Alder at the bakery, the woman had squinted and said, "Why, that verse has always been in 'The Valley's Keeping,' dear. My mother sang it." And when Beatrix asked what her mother thought it meant, Mrs. Alder had shrugged and said, "Storms and singing? Nothing, I expect. Hymns are full of weather."

Hymns were indeed full of weather. But hymns did not usually rearrange themselves overnight.

So here she stood, two days later, with her choir assembled and the verse copied onto fresh sheets because she could not bring herself to explain why she needed them to sing it—and because some part of her, some part she didn't entirely trust, wanted to hear it in thirty voices at once.

"We'll skip to the third verse," she said lightly. "It's less familiar, so eyes on your music."

Thirty heads bent. Thirty pairs of eyes found the page. Beatrix breathed in, breathed out, and gave the downbeat.

The first phrase went exactly as rehearsals always went: wobbly, earnest, Fenwick surging ahead like a man determined to arrive somewhere before anyone else. Beatrix was already shaping her correction—"watch the interval there, altos, it climbs higher than you think"—when the second phrase began, and the room changed.

It was subtle at first. The sopranos found a harmony Beatrix had never marked, a suspended fourth that shouldn't have worked and instead made the air feel thicker, sweeter, like the moment before rain. Then the basses answered a half-step below, low and round, and the sound stopped being thirty people in a drafty chapel and became something else—a single voice with many throats, wide as the valley itself.

Beatrix's hands kept moving. Her mind had gone very quiet and very far away.

On the piano lid, someone had left three candles burning against the evening chill. As the choir swelled toward the verse's final line—*and hollow voices answer him*—the flames did something Beatrix had only ever read about in her grandmother's letters, which she had not yet finished reading, which she was trying very hard not to think about.

They stood still.

Not guttering. Not wavering. Standing upright and motionless as three little golden soldiers, utterly indifferent to the drafts that came under the chapel door every autumn evening of Beatrix's life. The whole room seemed to lean toward them. Even Fenwick, mid-"grace," faltered and fell silent, staring.

The last note faded. The candles flickered back to life like animals waking.

Nobody spoke for a long moment. Then Delia Prewitt, age eleven, said in a hushed voice, "Miss Ives, did we do that?"

"I don't know," Beatrix said honestly. "Did anyone feel anything?"

"It felt like—" Tam Voss, the youngest, screwed up his face in concentration. He was nine, gap-toothed, and sang with his whole body the way only children and fools do. "Like the floor was humming. Like the floor knew the song too."

"The floor," repeated his brother, with the weary tone of a boy who had heard many claims about floors.

"No, really." Tam pressed both palms flat against the pew. "It's still doing it. A little."

Beatrix knelt and put her own hand on the wood. She felt nothing—or told herself she felt nothing—but the chapel was warm in a way the chapel never was, and outside the windows the evening birds had gone strangely quiet, all at once, like an orchestra waiting for a cue.

"All right," she said briskly, standing, because if she did not stand and be brisk she was going to sit down on the floor and possibly cry. "That was lovely. Unusual, but lovely. Let's take it again from the second verse, and this time, altos—"

"Miss Ives?" Delia's hand was up. "Is it true there's a lady staying with you who can make thunder?"

The question landed in the middle of the room like a dropped hymnal.

"Where did you hear that?" Beatrix asked, aiming for mild and missing by a mile.

"My mum says the Whitlock woman at the bee farm called lightning down on a tax collector once. Before she came here." Delia delivered this with relish. "He burst into flames."

"He did *not*," said Tam's brother.

"My mum says he did."

"Your mum also says the miller's cat is a demon."

"The miller's cat *is* a demon, Ollie, everyone knows that."

"Children," Beatrix said, "we are not discussing my—our—Ms. Whitlock's alleged criminal history. We are discussing a hymn."

"But is it true?" This from Tam, wide-eyed, hopeful, clearly wanting very much for it to be true.

Beatrix thought of Mara's face in the archive candlelight, lit gold and terrified, the prophecy's light sinking into her skin. She thought of the way Mara had held her hand walking home—not romantically, or not only romantically, but the way you hold someone when the ground feels untrustworthy. She thought of the ward-stone they'd visited yesterday, flaring blue-white at Beatrix's touch while Mara watched with an expression Beatrix still couldn't name.

"She keeps bees," Beatrix said. "Very well-behaved bees. Now—second verse, everyone, and this time let's try not to make the furniture hum."

* * *

They took it again. And again. By the fourth pass, Beatrix had stopped pretending to conduct corrections and started simply listening, because the choir was doing something she could not take credit for and could not explain.

It wasn't just the harmonies, though the harmonies were impossible—voice parts finding intervals no one had written, resolving tensions Beatrix hadn't known were there. It was the way the singers leaned together without meaning to, shoulders drifting toward shoulders, the circle of them tightening like a family around a fire. It was the candles again, standing at attention through entire verses now. It was Tam, whose small clear voice kept slipping underneath the others like a root under a wall, holding everything up.

And it was the feeling. Beatrix had sung in choirs since she was four years old. She knew the ordinary warmth of it, the good tired ache of it. This was different. This was belonging with teeth in it—the sense that the song was not merely being sung by them but *through* them, that they had been chosen as instruments by something older and more patient than any composer.

She should have stopped. Some clear-eyed part of her, the part that graded papers and budgeted sheet-music orders and kissed Mara Whitlock goodnight on a porch with bees drowsing in the dark—that part whispered *this is how traps work, Beatrix. They don't look like traps. They look like home.*

But the other part of her, the tired part, the part that had spent ten years in this valley being cheerful and competent and quietly, privately alone—that part drank the sound in like water and did not want to stop.

"One more," she heard herself say. "One more, and then we'll break for cider."

They sang it one more. The windows fogged with warmth. Somewhere far off—so far it might have been imagination, might have been a wagon on the ridge road—thunder rolled, once, soft as a clearing throat.

Beatrix's hands trembled on the downbeat. Only Fenwick noticed, and he said nothing, only watched her with old, kind, unsettlingly perceptive eyes.

* * *

Afterward, with the choir dispersed into the blue evening and the candles pinched out, Beatrix stayed behind to stack chairs and think.

Mara arrived at half past eight, as arranged, carrying a jar of honey "for morale" and wearing the guarded expression she wore everywhere except, lately, in the last five minutes before sleep. She paused in the chapel doorway, nostrils flaring slightly, like a deer testing wind.

"You sang it," she said.

"You can tell?"

"The whole walk over, the air tasted like—" Mara set the honey down on the piano and flexed her fingers, frowning at them as if they'd reported something strange. "Static. Good static. Like the minute before a summer storm, but *kind*. Storms aren't usually kind." She looked at Beatrix. "What did you do?"

"I taught them a hymn." Beatrix pulled the copied sheets from her folder and laid them out. "Or—it taught itself, maybe. Mara, this verse wasn't in the hymnal last week. I've played from this book for six years. And now it's in every copy in the valley, and Mrs. Alder swears it's always been there, and tonight my choir sang it and the candles—" She laughed, a little wildly. "The candles stood up straight. Like soldiers. And Tam says the floor is humming, and honestly, Mara, I put my hand on it and I think he's right."

Mara had gone very still. She picked up the sheet and read the verse, lips moving silently, and Beatrix watched the color leave her face line by line.

"*The storm shall kneel,*" Mara read aloud, barely above a whisper. "*The song shall rise.*"

"That's us. Isn't it. That's the book at the archive, that's—" Beatrix stopped. "Mara. Say something."

"I'm trying to decide whether to tell you to stop teaching it." Mara set the paper down carefully, the way you set down something that might be sleeping. "Because everything in me says this is dangerous. Prophecies don't weave themselves into children's hymns out of friendliness. Something wants this song sung. Something is *helping* it along—rewriting books, teaching harmonies, making candles behave. And I have never once in my life met a power that helped for free."

"And the other option?"

Mara was quiet a long moment. Outside, the first star came out over the ridge, and the valley lay dark and golden-lit and utterly ordinary, the way it had been for a hundred years, the way it was not going to be much longer.

"The other option," she said finally, "is that whatever's helping is the valley itself. The ward. Your grandmothers' work, waking up because their heir finally touched a stone. And if that's true, then the song isn't a trap, Beatrix. It's a hand reaching out." Her mouth twisted. "I'd like to believe that. I'm not good at believing things. Believing things is how I got exiled."

"You got exiled saving a village."

"I got exiled *believing* I could save a village." Mara's voice was gentle and tired. "There's a difference, and it cost me ten years."

Beatrix looked at the sheet music, at the verse sitting there in its patient old ink. She thought of Tam's palm flat on the humming floor, of Delia's rapturous face, of thirty neighbors leaning shoulder to shoulder in the candlelight, belonging so hard the room itself had joined in.

"They're going to keep singing it whether I teach it or not," she said. "You heard them. They love it. Whatever wrote that verse knew exactly what it was doing—it made something beautiful. If it's a trap, it's the cruelest kind." She straightened. "So here's what I think. I think we find out what the song is *for* before the new moon. You know storms. I know songs. And I know my grandmother's trunk is sitting in my attic full of things I haven't opened yet, and I think it's time I did."

Mara studied her for a long moment, and then something in her face shifted—the guardedness easing, just slightly, the way ice eases at the edge of a thaw.

"There she is," Mara said softly.

"There who is?"

"The warden's heir." Mara picked up the honey jar and held it out like a peace offering, like a promise. "All right, choir teacher. Trunk first. But Beatrix—if we're doing this, we do it together. No more reading things aloud without me. Deal?"

"Deal," Beatrix said.

And beneath their feet, faint and steady as a heartbeat, the chapel floor went on humming.
