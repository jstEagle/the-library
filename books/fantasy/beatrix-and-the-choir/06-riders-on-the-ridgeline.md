## 6. Riders on the Ridgeline

The bees knew before I did.

I had learned, over ten years in Vesper Hollow, to read them the way other people read weather. When they came back to the hive heavy with clover, the valley was at peace. When they clustered at the hive mouth and hummed in that low, urgent register—half complaint, half alarm—something was wrong somewhere, even if the somewhere was only a fox in the henhouse or a storm rolling in from a range the mountains would swallow before it reached us.

On the morning after the prophecy book, they would not settle at all.

I stood among the white boxes with my veil pushed up and my hands bare—beekeeping was the one work I did without gloves, a small stubborn penance, a way of saying *I trust something*—and watched them pour out and pour back and pour out again, a river with nowhere to go.

"Alright," I told them. "Alright. I hear you."

The truth was I had heard it all night. I had lain in my narrow bed above the honey-house listening to the valley's silence, which was not the ordinary silence of a place at rest but the strained silence of a held breath. No owls. No foxes. And beneath everything, so faint I could have pretended it away, a sound like a finger run around the rim of a wet glass—a thin, endless, singing tension, coming from everywhere and nowhere.

From the ward.

I had spent a decade not listening to that sound. It was a skill I had practiced the way other people practiced scales, and I had gotten very good at it. But last night a book had fallen open in a locked case and named me by name, and skills like mine, it turned out, were only ever borrowed against a day like this one.

The ward-stones stood at the valley's rim, a ring of them, gray and lichen-eaten, looking for all the world like the sort of standing stones farmers lean fences against and children dare each other to touch. There were eleven of them that I knew of. There had been twelve, once. The twelfth had fallen sometime before my exile and no one had ever moved it, because in Vesper Hollow the general attitude toward ancient things was *leave it be, it's not hurting anyone*, which was, I had to admit, the same attitude that had taken me in.

I walked the rim that morning with the bees' alarm still in my ears and my storm-sense—the thing I had spent ten years pretending I did not have—stretched thin and aching toward the ridgeline.

The aching was the worst of it. People imagine that losing magic is like losing a limb: a clean absence, a phantom itch. It isn't. Mine was still there, all of it, coiled in my chest like a sleeping animal, except that it never slept and it could never wake, because the ward drank everything I raised before it could become anything at all. Every day for ten years I had reached for the sky out of habit and felt the reach stop at my own skin, the way a voice stops at a shut window.

Except that lately—this week, this month, since the first cold snap—the window had begun to open a crack.

I told myself it was the ward settling. Old stone settles. Old wards, I supposed, did too.

I told myself a great many things that morning.

* * *

I found the twelfth stone by accident, which is to say I found the place where it had fallen, and then I found that it was no longer lying where it had lain for a hundred years.

The clearing at the valley's northwest shoulder was one of those places Vesper Hollow pretended not to have: bramble-choked, fern-deep, the kind of ground you only crossed if you were a poacher or a girl with something to hide, and I had been both in my time. The fallen stone had been here since before me. I knew because in my first year, raw and furious and looking for the edges of my cage, I had walked the whole rim and counted, and twelve had been eleven standing and one sleeping, and I had put my hand on the sleeping one and felt nothing, which had been the whole of my life then, and I had hated it with a purity I could not afford anymore.

Now there was a hollow in the ferns where the stone should have been, and drag-marks through the bracken, and at the end of the drag-marks—

The stone stood upright.

Someone had raised it. Someone had hauled a four-hundred-weight of ward-granite out of the ferns and stood it on its foot, and the lichen on its face was unbroken, unscarred, which meant it had not been done recently, which meant—

Which meant someone had done it quietly, and kept it quiet, and I stood in the bracken with my heart going like a hive in a thunderstorm and understood that I was not the only person in Vesper Hollow who had spent years watching the rim.

The stone's face, where the lichen thinned, was carved. Not the plain standing stones' blankness—there were letters, old ones, worn to whispers, and beneath them a sign I knew from nowhere and had always known: a circle with a line struck through it, like a coin on edge, like a door seen from the side.

I had seen that sign two nights ago, inked in the margin of a book dated 1899, next to a page that named Beatrix Ives.

I put my hand on the stone. For the first time in ten years, it was warm.

* * *

Beatrix came up the rim path an hour past noon, carrying a basket she had clearly packed as an excuse and defended as a necessity.

"You said you were checking the hives on the north slope," she said, slightly out of breath, slightly accusatory, entirely lovely, and I felt the whole tangled morning rearrange itself around her the way a room rearranges around an open window. "The north slope hives are *south* of the honey-house, Mara. I did the geography. I'm a choir teacher, geography is not my discipline, and I still did the geography."

"I got turned around."

"You got turned around for four hours."

"There's a lot of bracken."

She set the basket down on a flat rock with the deliberate care of a woman who wanted to slam it, and looked at me, and whatever she saw in my face made the indignation go out of her like a tide.

"You found something," she said.

I should have lied. I want to be honest with you, whoever you are, whoever I'm telling this to—I should have lied, because lying was the craft I had mastered in the order and the one art the valley had never managed to teach me out of, and the thing I had found was going to cost us both. But she was standing in the fern-light with flour still on her wrist from the morning's baking and her hair coming loose from its braid, and she had done the geography, and I was so tired of being a locked case.

"Show you," I said.

I showed her the raised stone. I watched her face do what faces do when a story they've agreed to treat as strange-but-manageable grows another head. She touched the carved sign with one fingertip, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal, and the stone warmed under her hand the way it had warmed under mine, and she snatched her fingers back and then, being Beatrix, put them back again.

"It knows me," she said. Not a question. Her voice had that steadiness it got in rehearsal when the sopranos were falling apart and someone had to be the floor they stood on. "Mara. The stone knows me, the book knew me, the—there is a word for what I am and you know it, and you've known it since the archive, and you've been walking around up here for four hours deciding whether to tell me."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because once I tell you, you can't unknow it." The words came out of me rough, scraped. "Believe me. There are things I would give a great deal to unknow, and I have never once managed it."

She was quiet for a moment. Below us the valley lay in its afternoon light—smoke from the bakery, the mill's slow wheel, someone's laundry, someone's dog, the small ordinary machinery of a place that believed itself safe—and I watched her look at it the way you look at a house you've just learned is built over a cave.

"Tell me anyway," she said.

So I told her what I knew, which was almost nothing: that the ward was a ring, that a ring with a fallen stone was a broken circle, that someone had mended it in secret, that the sign on the stone matched the sign in the book, that the book had named tonight and named her, and that the thin singing under the valley's silence was the sound of a hundred-year promise coming due.

"And the other thing?" she said. "The thing you're still not telling me."

I looked at the ridgeline. The ridgeline looked back, in the way of high places.

"The other thing," I said, "is that a broken circle doesn't mend itself. And a ward that's mending is a ward that's *needed* again. And there is exactly one reason a ward that has slept for a century would need to be a ward again."

"Someone's coming."

"Someone's coming," I said. "And I know who."

* * *

We saw them at dusk, and I will tell it plainly because I have earned the right to at least one plain sentence: I felt them before I saw them, and what I felt was home, and I have never in my life been so sickened by a feeling.

Storm-magic has a taste. Ours did, anyway—the order's did. It tastes like the air before lightning, like metal and rain and the green smell of a struck tree, and for ten years the ward had stood between me and that taste, and I had called that mercy. Now the taste came over the ridgeline thin as smoke through a keyhole, and my whole body turned toward it like a sunflower, traitorous and starving, and I had to put my hand against a birch trunk and breathe.

"Mara?" Beatrix's hand on my back. Warm. Ordinary. The most precious thing on the mountain.

"They're on the ridge," I said. "Don't look with your eyes yet. Look with—"

I stopped. Ten years of not teaching anyone anything.

"Look how?" she asked, and there was no fear in it, only the question, only her, and I thought: *this is what it would have been like, if I'd stayed. If I'd been allowed to be someone's teacher instead of someone's criminal.*

"Softly," I said. "Don't reach for it. Just—notice that the evening tastes like a struck match. Notice where the taste is thickest."

She was quiet a moment. Then, in a small voice with a wobble of wonder in it that I will remember until I die: "It's coming *down* the mountain. Mara, it's coming down the mountain like water."

"Scouts," I said. "Three of them, maybe four. They can't cross—the ward will still stop their feet. But the ward's gone thin as morning ice, and thin ice lets you *see* the fish."

We saw them from the shelf of rock above the old shepherd's stair, and I made Beatrix lie flat, and I lay flat beside her with my chin in the bracken like a girl playing at soldiers, except that I had actually been one.

Four riders on the ridgeline, dark against the last light. They did not try to descend. They sat their horses along the rim the way you'd walk a fence line, and below them, where the ward's edge ran, the air itself had begun to shimmer—faintly, fitfully, like heat-haze in October, like the valley's skin goosefleshing. And through that shimmer, when the wind moved it right, the scouts could see in. I watched one of them raise a hand, a small precise gesture, and the shimmer brightened, and I knew the gesture, knew the school of it, knew the teacher who had drilled it into a hundred students on a parade ground I had once stood on myself: *part the veil, gently, gently, we are only looking.*

Only looking. That was how it always started. Looking, then measuring, then mapping, then coming.

"They can see the village," Beatrix whispered. "They can see the lights."

"Yes."

"What do we do?"

"Nothing," I said, and hated it. "Tonight, nothing. They're measuring. If we spook them, they report a defended valley instead of a sleeping one, and a defended valley gets a vanguard instead of a survey."

"You sound like someone who's done this before."

"I *was* this before," I said. "That's the whole problem, Beatrix. I was this, exactly this, on exactly that ridge, and the woman leading them—"

I stopped, because the wind had turned, and the wind had brought me a voice.

Storm-mages speak to their horses the way everyone does, low and half-tender, and the wind off the ridge carried the words in shreds. But I didn't need the words. I had spent four years of my life listening to that voice across a mess hall, across a training field, across a pillow, once, in a season I do not talk about. A low voice, a dry voice, a voice that put the sardonic on top of the grief like icing on a brick.

"—not tonight, you great gray menace, we go down when I say—"

Wren Calloway.

I lay in the bracken with my heart going wrong and looked at the small dark figure on the ridgeline, and ten years folded up like a map.

She had been my closest friend. That's the sentence, and I've had a decade to soften it and I won't: she was my closest friend, and when the order put me on trial for the crime of saving a village, Wren Calloway stood up in a hall full of gray cloaks and testified, in a clear and reasonable voice, that Mara Whitlock had broken the Concord of Stillness, had called a storm over settled land, had shown the temperament that made her a danger to the order and to herself. Every word true. That was the devilry of it—every word true, and arranged like a knife, and I had sat in the dock and watched her do it and thought: *she's crying. Why is she crying, if it's true?*

They took my storms. They put me out at the ward's edge with a week's bread and a warning never to return. Wren did not come to the gate. I had ten years to decide what that meant, and I had decided, in the end, on the kindest reading, because the kindest reading was the only one I could live with: that she hadn't come because coming would have made it real for her too.

And now here she was, real, on the ridgeline, with a commander's knot on her cloak and four horses and the whole weight of the order behind her thin dry voice, looking down into the valley where I kept bees and was falling in love.

"She's crying," Beatrix said.

I turned. Beatrix was watching the ridge with her sight half-open—my student for one evening, a natural, which figured—and her face was puzzled and tender and appalled all at once.

"The one in front. I can't see properly, it's like looking through water, but—Mara, she's *crying*, and she's making the others look away while she does it."

I put my face down into the bracken. The fern smelled of rain that hadn't fallen yet.

"I know," I said. "I know she is. She cried at my trial, too."

"Mara—"

"It didn't stop her." I said it to the ground. "That's the thing you have to understand about Wren Calloway. It never once stopped her. She'll weep over every farm she burns and she'll burn it anyway, because she believes—she *believes*, all the way down, that order is mercy and the order's mercy is worth any price, and she testified against me with tears running down her face because she thought she was saving me from something worse." I lifted my head. Below us the shimmer along the ward's edge flickered, steadied, held. "And now she's found the something worse. She's found me, and I'm not the girl she testified against anymore, and I have—" I glanced at Beatrix, and the words rearranged themselves in my mouth, and what came out was the truest thing I'd said all day: "I have a valley full of people who took me in when my own order threw me away. And she's going to ask me to choose, and Beatrix, the worst part, the part I need you to hear—"

"Say it."

"The worst part is that I understand her. Every step of the way down that ridge, I'm going to understand her."

Beatrix was quiet. Then she reached over the bracken and took my hand, flour and all, and held it the way she held a note she meant to keep.

"Then we'd better make sure," she said, "that she understands us."

On the ridgeline, the scouts turned their horses. The shimmer along the valley's edge guttered and went out, and the night came down whole again, and the last thing the wind brought me was Wren's voice, pitched not to her riders but to the valley itself, low and clear and carrying the way a voice carries when it's been trained to carry across a parade ground and a decade of regret:

"Three days, Whitlock. Then I come down."

The bees, miles away in their white boxes, went silent as the grave.

And in my chest, behind the window the ward had kept shut for ten years, something that was not asleep anymore rolled over, and opened one eye, and waited.
