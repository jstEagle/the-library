## 7. The Warden's Inheritance

The trunk had been sitting at the foot of Beatrix's bed for eleven years, and in all that time she had used it for exactly three purposes: storing winter blankets, storing summer blankets, and, during one regrettable autumn, storing a choirboy's confiscated harmonica collection.

She had never once opened the small iron-banded box that lived inside it.

Her grandmother had left instructions, of a sort. *When you're ready,* she'd said, her hands folded on the quilt like two patient birds, *you'll know.* And then she'd died the way she'd done everything else—quietly, on her own schedule, between one breath and the next, leaving Beatrix a house full of sheet music, a garden gone gloriously to seed, and a locked box that had spent a decade radiating the particular patience of things that intend to be opened eventually.

Beatrix had assumed "when you're ready" meant something ordinary. When you're ready to grieve properly. When you're ready to sort the linens. She had not, until this week, considered that it might mean *when a century-old book writes your name in ink that hasn't dried.*

She sat on the floor of her bedroom now, Sunday afternoon light slanting through the window, the trunk open before her, the little box in her lap. It was smaller than she remembered—smaller than it had any right to be, given how much it seemed to weigh. Not physically. Physically it was light as bread. But there was a gravity to it, the way there is a gravity to an unopened letter from someone who loved you.

Mara had offered to come over. Beatrix had said no, and then immediately regretted it, and then immediately been relieved by the regret, which was becoming a familiar emotional roundabout where Mara Whitlock was concerned.

*This part's mine,* she'd said. And Mara—who understood better than anyone alive about things that were yours whether you wanted them or not—had nodded and said, *I'll be at the bees. Shout if anything glows.*

Which was, Beatrix reflected, possibly the strangest sentence anyone had ever said to her before noon on a Sunday.

The lock was not locked. That was the first surprise. She'd expected rust, resistance, some final test of worthiness. Instead the hasp lifted at her touch like a held breath released, and the lid came up on a smell of cedar and old paper and something underneath both—ozone, almost, faint as memory.

Inside: a ring. A bundle of letters tied with ribbon gone soft as moth-wing. And a folded square of cloth, dark blue, embroidered along one edge with a pattern Beatrix knew intimately, because she had spent four days staring at it in the archive—the same interlocking circles that ran down the spine of the prophecy book, the same design carved into the ward-stone Mara had shown her, flaring gold at Beatrix's touch like a dog recognizing its family.

She picked up the ring first, because rings are honest objects. It was heavy silver, unadorned except for the signet face: those interlocking circles again, cut deep enough to hold wax. It fit her middle finger perfectly, which should have felt like coincidence and instead felt like measurement. Like something made for her hand by someone who had never met her but had expected her anyway.

"Oh," Beatrix said, to the empty room, in the voice of a woman whose floor has just tilted a few degrees. "Oh, no."

Because here was the thing she had been carefully not thinking since the ward-stone lit up under her palm: she had grown up in Vesper Hollow. Her whole life, twenty-nine years of it, and she had never once questioned why her grandmother took her, every spring equinox, to walk the valley's rim and touch each of the seven stones and say nothing at all. She'd thought it was a quirk. Grandmothers were allowed quirks. Nana Ives had also believed you could cure hiccups by reciting the alphabet backwards into a well, and Beatrix had loved her for it.

But quirks didn't leave signet rings that fit.

She untied the ribbon with fingers that weren't quite steady and unfolded the topmost letter. The handwriting was her grandmother's—younger, sharper, the loops less forgiving—but the paper was older than her grandmother could have written on. Much older. The date at the top read 1897.

*To the warden-heir who comes after,* it began. *If you are reading this, then either the ward has failed—and God forgive us—or it has held, and held, and held, long past any hope we had of seeing it hold. Either way, you are the last of us, or near enough, and you deserve the truth your mothers and fathers paid for in full.*

Beatrix read it twice. Then a third time, slower, the way she'd learned to sight-read difficult music—one line at a time, trusting the next would arrive.

Her family had built the ward.

Not guarded it. Not tended it. *Built* it—her great-great-great-grandmother Edith Ives and six others, names Beatrix half-recognized from the cemetery on the hill, names she'd walked past her entire life without knowing they were load-bearing. They had sung the valley's borders into being with a canticle so old its composer's name was already lost, binding storm and silence together into a wall that magic could not cross. They had built it as a refuge. A place where the hunted could set down their power like a heavy pack and finally rest.

And then, in 1901, the storm-mages had come for the exiles their order had cast out, and when the wardens refused to hand them over—

Beatrix set the letter down on the quilt, very carefully, as though it might bruise.

The second letter confirmed what the first had only implied. There had been nineteen Iveses in the valley that winter. After the massacre, there were three. Her grandmother's grandmother, hidden in a root cellar beneath a neighbor's bakery, had been one of them. The wardens' houses burned. Their songbooks burned. The order declared the warden line extinguished, the valley's protection lapsed, and went home satisfied.

Except it hadn't been extinguished. One girl in a root cellar had grown up, married a baker, kept her mouth shut, and taught her daughter the walking-of-the-stones every spring equinox without ever saying why. Seven generations of saying why not. Seven generations of an inheritance disguised as a quirk.

Beatrix laughed. It came out wrong—half sob, half something wilder—and she pressed the back of her hand to her mouth.

All those years she'd thought herself ordinary. Deliberately, fondly ordinary: the choir teacher with the sensible cardigans and the apartment above the music shop, the woman whose greatest ambition was getting the alto section to stop rushing the descant. She had chosen smallness the way other people chose comfort, and worn it like a coat that fit.

It hadn't been a coat. It had been camouflage. And it had been handed to her folded, with love, by women who died so that she could have the luxury of forgetting.

* * *

She took the letters out to the garden, because the walls of her bedroom had started to feel like they were listening, and because Nana Ives had always said hard thoughts went down easier with dirt under your fingernails. The bee-farm's fence line ran along the back of her property, and she could see Mara out among the hives, moving slow and smoke-soft, doing whatever it was storm-callers did instead of calling storms. Watching bees, apparently. Being gentle with small industrious things. Beatrix found it unfairly endearing and firmly put the feeling in a drawer for later.

She sat on the back step with the letters spread around her like fallen leaves and read them all, oldest to newest, a hundred years of a conversation conducted entirely with her.

Some were practical—ward-stone maintenance schedules, warnings about which verses of the canticle strained the singer (*"the third verse takes; sing it seated"*), a recipe for salve that Beatrix suspected was just goose fat and stubbornness. Some were tender, notes passed between warden mothers about children and harvests and a choirmaster named Pell who "sings the binding verse flat and refuses correction, may he be haunted." Reading them felt like eavesdropping on a family dinner across a century-long table.

And some were the kind of thing you wrote only because no one living would ever read them.

*"They ask me if I regret it,"* wrote a woman signing herself only as E., in 1911, in handwriting that shook. *"Handing them over, I mean. The exiles. If we had given them up, my sisters would live. I tell them no. I tell everyone no. But some nights I take out their names—all nine, I keep the list—and I say them aloud so someone remembers they were people and not a principle. Tonight it was Aldous. He whistled while he worked. I cannot make peace with a world where the price of mercy is itemized."*

Beatrix sat with that one for a long time, the garden buzzing gently around her, until her eyes stopped stinging.

Then she thought: *nine exiles. Hidden in Vesper Hollow, in 1901.* And her mind, traitorous and quick, went straight to the archive, to the prophecy book, to Mara Whitlock standing in candlelight with dread written all over her beautiful guarded face.

Ten years ago, a storm-caller had broken her order's law to save a village. Ten years ago, she'd walked into a valley where magic couldn't follow, and set her power down like a heavy pack, and rested.

The valley her family built for exactly that purpose.

"It was never a coincidence," Beatrix said aloud. A bee droned past her ear, unimpressed. "None of it was ever a coincidence."

Mara hadn't stumbled into Vesper Hollow. Exiles came *here*. That was the whole point. Somewhere in the order's records there was probably still a map with this valley marked in old ink: *safe, if the ward holds.* Mara had come seeking sanctuary without knowing the sanctuary had a landlord, and the landlord's heir had asked her on a date, and somewhere a book written in 1899 had looked at the two of them and started taking notes.

Beatrix gathered the letters, squared their edges, retied the ribbon. Her hands were steadier now. Grief, she was discovering, came in two kinds: the kind that hollows you, and the kind that furnishes you. This was the second kind. Every name in these letters was a room she hadn't known she owned. Nineteen Iveses. Three survivors. Seven generations of silence bought at massacre-price, all of it spent to purchase exactly one thing:

That a girl in Vesper Hollow could grow up ordinary. Could teach children to sing. Could fall for a woman with storm-scars and a guarded heart, and never know the ground under her feet was a promise her ancestors had died keeping.

Her ordinary life had been the inheritance. All of it. The cardigans, the choir, the quiet Sundays—they weren't the absence of a legacy. They were the legacy, delivered intact, wrapped in a century of careful, stubborn love.

Nana, touching the ward-stones every spring, saying nothing: *still ours. Still holding. Sleep well.*

Beatrix stood, brushed off her skirt, and slid the signet ring onto her finger where it fit like an answer.

* * *

She found Mara at the fence line, ostensibly checking a hive but actually, judging by her posture, watching Beatrix's kitchen window with the expression of someone trying very hard not to worry and failing in a way she'd deny later.

"You're wearing a ring," Mara said, by way of hello.

"I'm wearing a *signet*." Beatrix held out her hand. "Feel free to react appropriately."

Mara took her hand—she did that now, casually, like it was nothing, like it wasn't the single best part of Beatrix's week—and turned the ring toward the light. Her thumb traced the interlocking circles and stopped. Beatrix watched the recognition land, watched it travel through Mara's whole body like a struck bell.

"That's a warden's mark."

"My great-great-however-many-times-grandmother Edith's, specifically. Would you like the abridged version or the version with crying?"

"Beatrix."

"There was a massacre, Mara. In 1901. My family hid exiles—people like you, literally people like you, nine of them by one count—and the storm-mages burned them out of their homes for it. Three survived. One of them was my ancestor, hiding in a root cellar under a bakery." She heard her own voice go thin and made herself push through. "My grandmother knew. They all knew, for a hundred years, and they never told us, they just—walked the stones every spring and kept the secret and let us be *normal*, because normal was the whole point—"

"Hey." Mara's hand came up to her jaw, warm and certain. "Breathe. You're rushing the descant."

Despite everything—despite the grief sitting in her chest like a stone with a name—Beatrix laughed, wet and startled. "Did you just use choir direction as a comfort technique?"

"Is it working?"

"...Appallingly, yes."

They stood there a moment, foreheads nearly touching, the hive humming behind Mara like a second heartbeat. Then Mara said, quietly: "I need to tell you something, and I need you to hear it before you decide what that ring makes you."

"Mara—"

"When I came to the Hollow, ten years ago, I didn't choose it at random. I was half-dead and running, and an old woman in the pass gave me bread and pointed down and said, *the valley below will take what you can't carry anymore.* I thought she meant it kindly. I thought it was luck." Her eyes were fierce and frightened. "But Beatrix—if your family built this place as a harbor for exiles, then I didn't find sanctuary. I was *invited*. By your people. Before either of us was born."

The afternoon light caught the signet and threw a small crooked circle of gold onto Mara's cheekbone, and Beatrix thought about a woman named E. saying nine names aloud into the dark so no one would be forgotten, and about her grandmother's hands folded on the quilt, and about the word *ready*.

"Nana said I'd know when it was time to open the box," Beatrix said. "I thought she meant after the grieving. I think—" She looked down at the ring, at the mark her family had died rather than surrender. "I think she meant today. I think she meant *him*."

"Him who?"

"The archmage. Whoever sent Wren Calloway to the ridgeline. Whoever's been circling this valley waiting for the warden line to forget itself." Beatrix straightened, and felt something settle in her spine that she recognized, distantly, as posture inherited from seven generations of women who stood in doorways. "They didn't extinguish us, Mara. They just made us quiet. And I am *done* being quiet."

Somewhere beyond the eastern ridge, faint but unmistakable, thunder rolled across a cloudless sky.

Mara went very still. "That's not weather," she said. "That's a summons. Someone's testing the ward." She turned toward the sound, and Beatrix saw ten years of exile rearrange themselves behind her eyes into something older and sharper. "New moon's in five days."

"Then we've got five days," Beatrix said, and touched the signet, and did not let go of Mara's hand. "Tell me everything my family built. Starting with the singing."

Mara blinked. "The—what?"

"These letters mention a canticle. A binding verse. My family sang the ward into existence, and I have a choir of forty children who owe me a term of discipline anyway." She smiled, and it felt, for the first time in a week, like a weapon being politely unsheathed. "I think it's time somebody taught them the family repertoire."

Above the hives, the bees rose in a golden spiral, humming on a note that Beatrix—with a chill and a thrill she'd examine later—could have sworn was a perfect fifth below the thunder.
