## 8. Terms of Exile

The bees knew first.

I was at the hives behind Tam's family's mill—no, not Tam's family's mill, the Aldous mill, I had to stop thinking of the whole valley as a cast of characters from Beatrix's choir—when every bee in every box went still at once. Not calm. Still. The difference matters, if you've kept bees as long as I have. Calm bees hum. Frightened bees go quiet the way a house goes quiet when someone you don't want to see knocks on the door.

I straightened up, smoker in hand, and looked east toward the ridgeline where the ward-stone crouched in its ring of birches like an old dog pretending to sleep.

Thunder. Far off, and wrong. Thunder that came from beneath the clouds instead of within them.

"Right," I told the bees. "Don't wait up."

* * *

By the time I reached the ward-stone, half the valley had reached it before me, which is what happens in a town of four hundred people where the news travels faster than the horses. Mrs. Aldous had brought bread. Mrs. Aldous always brought bread, on the theory that any crisis could be improved by bread, and honestly, ten years in Vesper Hollow had not given me evidence to the contrary.

"Is it the mages?" someone asked. "Is it war?"

"It's one rider," I said, "under a white flag."

The rider was already crossing the ward.

I want to be precise about that, because it mattered more than anything else that day. The ward had been weakening for weeks—I'd felt it in my teeth, in the ache behind my sternum where my storms used to live, in the way the stone had started to hum at night like a kettle deciding whether to boil. But watching Wren Calloway ride through it was different. Watching the ward part around her horse like a curtain around a guest was the difference between hearing a floorboard creak and watching the door open.

She looked exactly as I remembered and nothing like I remembered. The same copper-brown skin, the same crooked nose she'd broken falling out of an observation tower at sixteen because she'd wanted to see the storm from *inside*, the same way of sitting a horse like she was apologizing to it. But the storm-grey cloak of the Order sat on her shoulders now with weight, and her eyes—her eyes had gone from quick to careful, and careful is what quick becomes when it's been broken on the wheel of institutional politics for a decade.

She stopped the horse ten paces from the stone and looked at the crowd of us, and her gaze found me the way a compass finds north, and she said, "Hello, Mara."

"Hello, Wren," I said. "You're standing on Josiah Pell's turnip field. He'll want compensation."

A few nervous laughs in the crowd. Wren didn't smile. She dismounted, planted the parley flag in the turnips, and said, "I'm asking for parley. Warden law, if it still stands here."

And that was the moment the crowd turned to look at Beatrix, who had arrived breathless at the back of it with her choir folder still under her arm, because apparently she'd been on her way to rehearsal and the end of the world had seemed like a thing she could do after.

"Why are they looking at me?" Beatrix whispered.

"Because you're the warden," I whispered back.

"I'm a *choir teacher*."

"You're both. It's a valley of many talents."

Beatrix looked at the flag, and the rider, and the crowd, and I watched her do the thing she did—straighten her spine, lift her chin, become the woman who could silence forty singing children with one raised finger. "Parley is granted," she said, and her voice carried the way it carried in the church hall, warm and absolutely non-negotiable. "One hour. Under truce. Mrs. Aldous, would you—"

"Tea," said Mrs. Aldous, who had been waiting her whole life for this sentence. "And bread."

* * *

We held the parley in the meeting hall, because Warden Beatrix Ives was not about to conduct diplomacy standing in a turnip field, and because, as she muttered to me on the walk over, "if I'm going to be a warden I'm going to be a warden with a table."

So there we sat: Beatrix at the head of the meeting-hall table with the warden's signet—her grandmother's signet, the one she'd found in the trunk, the one she still wore like it might bite her—on a ribbon around her neck. Wren across from her with her hands flat on the wood. Me beside Beatrix, because Wren had said, "I'll speak with the exile," and Beatrix had said, "You'll speak with both of us or neither," and Wren had looked at her for a long moment and then at me, and something in her face had said *ah*.

"Ten years," Wren said to me. "You look well."

"You look tired."

"I *am* tired." She leaned back. "Mara. I'm going to say things in the next few minutes that you're going to hate, and I need you to understand before I start that I am not your enemy. I have never been your enemy. I need you to hold onto that."

"You testified against me."

"Yes."

"You stood in front of the conclave and said I'd broken the First Law, that I'd called a storm in anger, that I was a danger to every soul in the Order's keeping."

"Yes," Wren said, and her voice didn't waver, and I hated her a little for that, and loved her a little for it too, because I remembered the girl who'd broken her nose to see a storm from the inside and I knew how much that steadiness was costing her. "And every word of it was true. You did call a storm in anger. You did break the First Law. You saved that village, and the storm would have killed half the Order's escort if you hadn't, and none of that changes what you did or what I said. I've had ten years to make peace with the fact that both things are true at once. The storm and the saving. I'd hoped you had too."

The tea came. Mrs. Aldous had also brought bread, and jam, and a plate of honey cakes that I recognized as coming from my own hives, which felt like the valley itself offering a small vote of confidence.

Wren wrapped her hands around her cup and didn't drink. "Here's the offer. The conclave will reinstate you. Full rank, restored standing, your name struck from the exile rolls. You come back to the Spire, you resume your training, and in time—given your bloodline—given your *mother's* bloodline—you might sit on the conclave itself."

The room went very quiet. Beatrix's hand found mine under the table.

"And in exchange," Wren said, and now her eyes came up and met mine and they were careful again, so careful, "you surrender the Ives woman to the Order's custody, and you surrender the prophecy book."

"No," I said.

"You haven't heard the terms—"

"No," I said again, and it came out steadier than I expected, which surprised me, because inside I was a kettle deciding whether to boil. "Wren. You're asking me to hand over the woman I—" I stopped. Beatrix's hand tightened. "You're asking me to hand over an innocent woman to an order that has spent a century pretending her family never existed. You're asking me to hand over a book that hasn't harmed anyone. And in exchange I get a chair in a tower full of the people who exiled me. That's not an offer. That's an insult with a ribbon on it."

"It's the only offer there is," Wren said, and for the first time her carefulness cracked, just at the edge, just enough to show the girl underneath. "Mara, listen to me. I crossed that ward under a flag because I argued for a flag. There are people behind me on that ridgeline who argued for fire. The archmage has the vanguard. He has siege-lanterns. He has *patience*, which is worse. The conclave's offer was the soft version, and I bullied three seats into backing it because I thought—I *hoped*—you'd take it. Because if you don't, the next person who comes down that hill won't be me."

"And if I do take it?" I said. "If I hand her over. What happens to her?"

Wren was quiet.

"That's what I thought," I said.

"She'd be questioned," Wren said finally. "Humanely. The Order doesn't—"

"The Order questioned my mother," I said. "Humanely. Ask the ward how that went."

And there it was—the thing I'd found in the heart-chamber, the thing I hadn't told Beatrix yet, the thing that sat in my chest like a stone I'd swallowed: the carved names in the binding circle, and among them, in the old hand, *Elara Whitlock*. My mother. A founding warden's line. The Order hadn't exiled me because I broke the law. The law was the excuse. They'd exiled me because a Whitlock heir alive outside their walls was a threat to the archmage's claim on the ward, and I had only figured that out a week ago and had not yet found a way to say it out loud to the woman whose testimony had shipped me here.

I looked at Wren, and I thought: *she doesn't know*. I looked at her and thought: *or she knows and she's here to find out how much I know*. And I thought: *ten years ago I would have known which. Ten years ago I could read her like weather.*

"Reinstatement," I said instead. "That's the whole of it. My storms back. My name back. My mother's name back."

"Your mother's name was struck from the rolls thirty years ago," Wren said, puzzled. "Before you were born. Treason, the records say."

"The records say," I repeated, and let it sit there between us like a stone on the table.

Wren looked at me for a long moment. I watched the quickness come back into her eyes, just a flicker, the old Wren smelling a storm on the wind. "What do you know, Mara?"

"What I know," I said, "is that the answer is no. To all of it. The woman stays. The book stays. And I stay, because this valley took me in when your order threw me away, and I have spent ten years learning that belonging isn't something you're granted by a conclave. It's something you *keep*. By staying. Especially when it's hard."

Beatrix made a small sound beside me. I didn't look at her. If I looked at her I'd lose the thread.

Wren closed her eyes. Opened them. "Then I'm sorry," she said, "because I have to give you the rest of it. The archmage's terms, since the conclave's terms are refused. You have until the new moon. Nine days. At the new moon, the Order comes over that ridgeline in force, and the ward will not stop us—we've already tested its edges, Mara, it's threadbare, it's a net with holes, and every day it gets thinner. Surrender the book and the heir before then, or the valley burns. Those are the terms. I was ordered to say them exactly that way." She stood. "And I was not ordered to say the next part, so listen. Whatever you're planning—and you're planning something, you always were, you used to plan escape routes out of the nursery—plan *fast*. And Mara?" She paused at the door. "The girl who broke her nose to see a storm from the inside is still in here somewhere. When you find her, tell her I'm sorry about the nose."

She left. The flag stayed in the turnips, white and ridiculous, like a surrender nobody had made.

* * *

Afterward, Beatrix and I walked the long way back along the millstream, because neither of us wanted to go indoors and both of us wanted to be somewhere the walls couldn't hear.

"Nine days," she said.

"Nine days."

"She offered you everything you lost."

"She offered me a cage with my old name on it."

"You could have taken it." Beatrix's voice was very even, the way it got when she was working hard at even. "You could have taken it and been *safe*, Mara. Real safe, not valley-safe. And instead you stood in front of the scariest woman I've ever seen and chose—" she gestured at the stream, the mill, the hills, the whole small damp ordinary valley, "—*this*. Us. Me. A choir teacher with a neck ribbon."

"You're the last heir of the wardens who built the ward that's the only reason this valley exists."

"I'm also a choir teacher. Both things are true at once." She stopped walking. "Wren said that. About you. Both things true at once."

I looked at the water. "She did."

"Mara. What aren't you telling me?"

The stone in my chest turned over. *Elara Whitlock*, carved in the dark a hundred years before my mother was born. *Treason, the records say.* Nine days. I thought about telling her everything, right there on the stream bank, and I opened my mouth to do it, and what came out was: "Soon. There's something in the heart-chamber I need to show you, and I need to be sure first. I need to be sure it means what I think it means. Because if I'm wrong, I'll have taken the last thing you had that was ordinary."

Beatrix studied me the way she studied a choir that was singing slightly flat—with love, and with the absolute certainty that she would fix it.

"Nine days," she said. "Don't spend more than four of them being sure."

And then, upstream, the mill bell began to ring—not the alarm, the *evening* bell, the one that meant bread and supper and the small stubborn machinery of a town refusing to be frightened—and Beatrix sighed and said, "Rehearsal. I have forty children and a canticle and apparently the fate of the valley, and I'm going to be late for all three."

I watched her hurry off along the stream, folder under her arm, signet bouncing on its ribbon, and I turned east toward the ridgeline where the siege-camp fires would be lighting one by one in the dark like a constellation with bad intentions.

Nine days. And somewhere out there, an archmage who thought the Whitlock line was dead.

He was about to have a very educational new moon.
