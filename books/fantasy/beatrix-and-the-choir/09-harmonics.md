## 9. Harmonics

Beatrix Ives had conducted the Vesper Hollow choir for six years, and in that time she had learned three immutable truths: Mr. Aldous Petch would always arrive late and blame the goat; the Fenwick sisters could not be seated next to each other without a theological dispute breaking out by the second hymn; and no rehearsal had ever ended on time.

She was counting on none of these things to save a valley. And yet here she was, standing before forty-two singers on a Thursday evening, holding a scrap of parchment covered in her grandmother's handwriting, trying not to look like a woman whose entire understanding of music—and magic—had been upended in four days.

"From the top of the old harvest hymn," she said. "And please, *please*, watch me on the third bar. Tam, that means you."

Tam Osgood, twelve years old and all elbows, grinned at her from the front row. His voice had just begun its treacherous migration, cracking between notes like ice on a spring pond, but he sang with his whole chest, which was more than she could say for half the altos.

The choir began. *Green grow the valleys, green grow the grain—*

It was an ordinary hymn. It had been an ordinary hymn for as long as anyone in Vesper Hollow could remember, which, Beatrix now understood, was precisely the problem. Nobody remembered teaching it. Nobody remembered learning it. It simply existed, the way the ward-stones existed, the way the valley's strange windless calm existed. The people of the Hollow sang it every autumn because they had always sung it every autumn, and if you asked them why, they'd shrug and say something about tradition, or their mothers, or the fact that it went well with pie.

Four days ago, standing in the archive with Mara, Beatrix had read a prophecy book dated 1899. Four days ago, words had lifted off a page as light and sunk into her skin like rain into thirsty ground. And two days ago, in her grandmother's trunk, she had found letters that turned her tidy little life inside out like a pocket searched for coins.

Her family hadn't just lived in Vesper Hollow. Her family had *built* it—the ward, the valley's protection, the invisible wall that kept storm-mages out and exiles safe. Built it, the letters said, with stone and blood and *song*.

Song. That word had snagged in her mind like a splinter and refused to come out.

*Green grow the valleys* — the choir reached the third bar. Tam cracked spectacularly on "grain," producing a sound like a startled goose, and Mrs. Fenwick (the elder) shot him a look that could curdle cream.

"Hold," Beatrix said, raising her hand. "Again from the third bar."

They repeated it. And there — there it was again, so faint she might have imagined it, except she had heard this exact thing happen twice before and once in the archive with candle flames standing perfectly, impossibly still.

When the choir hit the harmony on the fourth bar — sopranos up, altos holding the fifth, basses rumbling underneath like contented cattle — the air in the meeting hall *changed*. Not the temperature. Not the light. Something subtler. A pressure, a fullness, the feeling of a room just before a summer storm, except gentle. The candle on the piano straightened its flame until it burned like a little white spear.

Mrs. Fenwick the younger stopped singing and stared at it. "Beatrix," she said. "Is your candle meant to do that?"

"No," said Beatrix honestly.

"It's done it three rehearsals running," said Aldous Petch, arriving late as prophesied, shaking rain off exactly nothing, since it hadn't rained. "I thought perhaps you'd bought better candles."

"I haven't changed the candles, Aldous."

"Well." He settled onto the back bench with the dignity of a man who has never once been early for anything. "It's very nice, whatever it is."

Beatrix looked at her choir — her ordinary, pie-loving, squabbling, beloved choir — and felt the splinter in her mind slide into place with an almost audible click.

They weren't doing anything wrong. They were doing something *old*. Every Thursday night for years, they had gathered in this hall and sung harmonies that made the air hum, and the ward-stones at the valley's edge — stones nobody tended, nobody visited, nobody even thought about — had drunk those harmonies like rain.

And now the ward was failing. And now the harmonies were the only thing slowing the collapse. She didn't know how she knew this. She knew it the way she knew where middle C was: somewhere below thought, in the marrow.

"Everyone," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt, "I'd like to try something new tonight."

* * *

The problem, she discovered over the next hour, was that the warden's canticle did not want to be taught the way ordinary music is taught.

She had found it in her grandmother's trunk, folded inside the last letter — a single page of notation in a hand that slanted hard to the right, with lyrics beneath in Old Warden script that Beatrix could only half-translate. The melody was strange. It climbed where melodies usually fell. It asked the singer to hold intervals that sat a quarter-tone apart from anything in the modern scale, close enough to sound like error and far enough to make the teeth ache.

"This is unsingable," announced Prudence Fenwick the elder, after one attempt, with the satisfaction of a woman confirming the world's order.

"It's not unsingable. It's unfamiliar."

"It's *wrong*, is what it is. There's no note between B and C, Beatrix. God drew the lines where He drew them."

"And yet my grandmother wrote it down, so someone sang it."

"Your grandmother also kept bees and claimed they told her the weather."

"They probably did," said Beatrix, who had spent enough evenings with Mara Whitlock to know that the people everyone called eccentric were often just correct. "From the top. Sopranos only first."

The sopranos tried. The melody bucked them off like an unbroken pony. Tam, forbidden from attempting it because he was twelve and she was responsible, watched from the front row with the naked hunger of a boy who wanted desperately to be forbidden from something.

By the third attempt, something interesting happened. The choir couldn't find the canticle's intervals — but their voices kept *reaching* for them, sliding toward the strange quarter-tones like plants toward sun, and every time they did, the candle flames leaned. All of them. In unison. Toward the singers.

"Stop," Beatrix whispered. "Look at the candles."

Forty-two people looked at the candles. The flames stood upright, quivering faintly, like soldiers awaiting orders.

Silence. Then Aldous Petch, from the back, in the tone of a man discussing crop rotation: "Well, that's new."

"What *is* it?" Prudence demanded. "What are we doing?"

Beatrix weighed lying. She was, by nature and profession, a terrible liar — she once failed to convince her own students that the recital had been cancelled when it hadn't — and besides, the valley deserved better than lies. The valley had taken her in too, hadn't it? Hadn't it taken in Mara?

"You remember the trouble at the border," she said. "You've seen the ward-stone flicker. You know the storm-mages are coming."

Nobody argued. The whole valley knew; the whole valley pretended not to, in the way of small places confronting large things.

"The ward that protects us wasn't built the way we were taught. It isn't just stone. It was built with song. My family built it — the Ives family, going back — and they bound it with music, and the music has been fading for a hundred years because nobody remembered it. Because—" her throat tightened unexpectedly, "—because the people who remembered it were killed for it. My family included."

The silence in the hall was different now. Softer. Attentive.

"So this canticle," she went on, holding up the parchment, "is theirs. Ours. It's the oldest thing any of us owns, and I need you to help me learn it, because I think — I *believe* — that every harmony we sing slows the damage at the border. I think our Thursday nights have been holding up a wall for a hundred years without any of us knowing it."

Prudence Fenwick the elder stared at her for a long moment. Then she adjusted her shawl, opened her hymnal to a blank page, licked the tip of her pencil, and said:

"Well. We'd best get it right, then. From the top. And someone wake Aldous."

"I'm awake," said Aldous, who was not.

* * *

They practiced until nine, then past nine, which had never once happened in the history of the Vesper Hollow choir. Progress was slow and strange. The canticle resisted being learned in parts; it seemed to want to be learned *whole*, the way you don't memorize a river section by section. When the sopranos finally held the impossible interval between B and C — that ghost-note, that door-crack between keys — the windows rattled gently in their frames, and outside, Beatrix would swear later, the crickets paused to listen.

Tam sang anyway. Of course he sang. She caught him mouthing the melody under his breath during the alto break, and when she glared at him he gave her a look of such pure, unrepentant joy that she lost the thread of her own scolding entirely.

"It wants to be sung," he said, when she came to stand beside him. Not defiantly. Wonderingly. "Don't you feel it? It's like — it's like it already knows us."

Beatrix did feel it. That was the unsettling part. The canticle fit in her mouth like a key in a lock she'd never noticed, and every time she sang it, she felt her grandmother's hands on the page, her great-great-grandmother's breath in the phrases, a long line of Ives women leaning over her shoulder saying *yes, like that, you're nearly there.*

Purpose, she thought, was supposed to announce itself with trumpets. Instead it had arrived through a church-hall window on a Thursday evening, wearing cardigans and complaining about the seating chart.

At half past nine she dismissed them. They filed out slowly, reluctantly, humming fragments into the dark — she could hear the ghost-interval trailing down the lane like a lantern being carried home. Prudence the elder paused at the door.

"My mother used to hum something like that," she said abruptly. "When I was small. Said her mother taught her. Said it was for keeping things *in*." She frowned. "Or out. I forget which."

"Both," said Beatrix. "Probably both."

"Hm." Prudence considered this, found it acceptable, and departed.

Beatrix stayed behind to snuff the candles. The last flame — the one on the piano, the stubborn spear — wouldn't gutter when she blew at it. It stood straight and bright, leaning faintly toward her, toward the parchment still open on the stand.

"All right," she told it. "I hear you."

She gathered her things and stepped out into the night. The valley lay quiet under a sky scrubbed clean of clouds, stars sharp as tacks. To the east, past the orchards, she could just see the dark line of the ridge — and along it, faint as breath on glass, a shimmer that hadn't been there a month ago. The ward, flickering. Holding. Barely.

Mara was waiting by the gate, as she had waited every evening this week, arms crossed against the chill, pretending she hadn't been waiting. Beatrix had come to love that pretense.

"Well?" Mara asked. "How did it go? Did Prudence declare war on the quarter-tone?"

"She's mobilized. We're all in." Beatrix hesitated, then took Mara's hand — the left one, the one Mara kept gloved lately, though she hadn't asked why. Not yet. "Mara, when we sang it tonight, the whole hall *hummed*. The candles, the windows, everything. I think the canticle is reaching the ward. I think it's working."

Something moved across Mara's face — hope and fear tangled together, inseparable, like the interval between B and C.

"That's good," Mara said. "That's — Beatrix, that's good."

"Yes," Beatrix agreed. "Except."

"Except?"

"Tam sang the whole thing tonight. Every verse, including the ones I told him not to touch." She looked east, at the shimmering ridge, and felt the splinter shift again — not pain, exactly. Premonition. "And afterward he told me the melody sounded different to him than it does to the rest of us. Like it was missing something. Like it was waiting for a verse nobody's written down."

The wind moved through the orchard, carrying the smell of late apples and, far away and thin, the memory of thunder.

"He says," Beatrix finished quietly, "that he can hear how it ends."
