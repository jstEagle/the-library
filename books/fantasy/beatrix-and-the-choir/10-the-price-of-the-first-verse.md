## 10. The Price of the First Verse

The rehearsal hall smelled of beeswax and old wood and, faintly, of the honey cakes Beatrix had promised her singers if they got through the canticle without giggling. Mara had come to stand at the back, as she always did now, arms folded against the doorframe like a woman guarding an exit she had no intention of using.

She told herself it was strategy. The ward was failing; the choir was the only thing slowing its collapse; therefore someone ought to listen to the choir with a storm-caller's ear. That was the story she gave Wren's scouts in her head, the story she'd give the valley council if anyone asked why the exiled witch spent three evenings a week in a room full of children singing at candle flames.

The truth was simpler and more embarrassing: she liked it here.

She liked the way Beatrix moved when she conducted — not grandly, not like the storm-mage cantors who had once made thunder into theater, but with small precise hands, as though she were kneading something delicate out of the air. She liked that Tam Voss, nine years old and missing two front teeth, sang louder than anyone because he hadn't yet learned to be embarrassed by his own voice. She liked that nobody in Vesper Hollow looked at her sideways anymore for standing in corners. Ten years of practice had made her very good at corners. It was strange, and slightly alarming, to discover she might be getting worse at them.

"Again from the third verse," Beatrix said. "And remember — soft on *holdfast*, strong on *dawn*. The song is supposed to sound like the valley, not like a parade."

"It could sound like a parade," offered Prue Halloway, fourteen and perpetually negotiating. "Parades are nice."

"The valley," Beatrix repeated, with the gentle iron that made her singers adore her, "is not a parade."

Mara smiled at the floorboards. Then the candles did the thing they had started doing, and she stopped smiling.

It was subtle, still. When the choir found the harmonies right — and lately they found them right more often than wrong, which was its own quiet miracle — the flames would straighten. Not flicker, not lean toward the sound the way fire does when a door opens somewhere in a house. They would simply *stand*, each one a perfect golden column, attentive as soldiers. Old hymnals said warden-song could do that. Mara had assumed the hymnals were flattering themselves.

Tonight the flames stood taller than she'd ever seen them.

"Third verse," Beatrix said again, and raised her hands.

They began. And Mara, listening with the ear she couldn't help having, heard what she had been half-hearing for a week and refusing to name: the canticle wasn't only being sung. It was being *drawn*. There was a current under the melody, a slow pull like water finding a drain, and every voice in the room was leaning into it — Tam most of all, because Tam leaned into everything, because Tam sang the way other children ran downhill.

*Holdfast,* they sang, soft as falling snow. *Holdfast, stone and root and river.*

The nearest candle flame stretched upward, thin and impossibly steady.

*And when the dark comes down the hill—*

Tam hit his note. He hit it perfectly, wide open, whole-hearted, the way he hit everything —

—and the flame bent toward him like a compass needle finding north.

Mara saw it before anyone else saw anything. She was moving before she understood why, crossing the hall in four strides, and later she would remember that she shouted something — *stop, stop singing* — but the choir didn't hear her, or the song didn't let them, and Tam's note went up and up and up, brighter than a boy's voice had any business being, and the light came off him like heat off summer stone—

He stopped mid-word.

Not faded. Stopped, the way a clock stops, all at once, and folded sideways off his stool.

* * *

Afterward, Mara would count the seconds. Four, maybe five, between Tam's collapse and Beatrix reaching him — Beatrix moving faster than Mara had ever seen her move, knocking a music stand flat, catching the boy before his head found the floorboards. But in the moment there were no seconds. There was only the silence where the song had been, and Tam lying boneless in Beatrix's arms, gray as dishwater, and twenty-two children staring, and the candles guttering back to ordinary fire as though nothing had happened at all.

"Tam." Beatrix's voice was steady. Mara knew that steadiness; she knew what it cost. "Tam, sweetheart, can you hear me?"

His eyes opened halfway. He smiled the gap-toothed smile. "Did I do it right?" he whispered.

"You did—" Beatrix's voice cracked clean through the middle. She pressed her lips together, tried again. "You did beautifully. You're going to lie still now."

"I'm tired," Tam said, with enormous dignity, and closed his eyes.

"Fetch his mother," Beatrix said over her shoulder, calm as glass over deep water. "Prue — run, love, don't walk. Mrs. Voss, at the tannery. Tell her Tam fainted, tell her he's awake, tell her—" A breath. "Tell her I'm carrying him home myself."

Mara knelt on Tam's other side and put two fingers to his throat. His pulse was there, slow and even, a sleeper's pulse. That should have been a relief. It was not. Because she had felt pulses like this before, once, long ago, in a training yard after a demonstration gone wrong — a first-year who had reached too greedily for the sky and been emptied by it. Storm-mages called it *hollowing*. They had a word for it because it happened. Ordinary choirs were not supposed to have a word for it.

"He's breathing," she said quietly. "Strong and slow. Like sleep."

"Like sleep." Beatrix gathered the boy up; he weighed almost nothing in her arms, which was somehow worse than if he'd been heavy. At the door she paused, and looked back at her choir — pale, silent, Prue already gone at a dead run — and Mara watched her make the choice, the same choice she made every time, to be the thing they needed instead of the thing she was.

"Everyone goes home," Beatrix said. "Everyone eats something warm. Nobody sings tonight — not humming, not whistling, nothing. We'll meet Thursday, and we'll talk about it, and until then you are brave and kind to each other. Go on."

They went. Small feet on the stairs, whispers dying away, and then just the two of them and the boy between them, and the candles burning like ordinary candles, guilty of nothing.

* * *

Mrs. Voss met them at her own door before they'd knocked, drawn by Prue's pounding arrival, and took her son with the practiced competence of a woman who had raised four children through falls and fevers and one memorable incident involving a hay wagon. She listened to Beatrix's account — *he fainted, ma'am, mid-note, he woke and spoke* — with narrowed eyes, thanked them, and shut the door with the particular firmness of a mother reserving judgment.

Then Beatrix stood on the Vosses' step in the blue dusk and shook.

Mara had seen people shake before. Soldiers after their first battle. Herself, ten years ago, walking out of the order's judgment hall with her name struck from its rolls. It was never about the danger. It was about the moment afterward, when the body finally got permission to say what it really thought.

She put her hand on Beatrix's shoulder. Beatrix turned into her — not quite an embrace, more a controlled collision — and pressed her forehead against Mara's collarbone and stayed there while the evening cooled around them.

"He asked if he did it right," Beatrix said, muffled.

"I know."

"He's nine, Mara. He stood up on that stool and gave that verse everything he had, because I asked him to. Because I've been teaching them that song." She pulled back, and her eyes were wet and furious in equal measure. "I taught a child a song that drank him."

"We don't know that's what happened."

"Don't we?" Beatrix laughed, and it was not a kind laugh. It was aimed at herself. "You were there. You saw the candle. You moved before he fell — you knew. You've known for days, haven't you? I've seen you watching the flames."

Mara considered lying. She was, historically, excellent at lying; ten years of exile had kept the skill sharp. But Beatrix was looking at her with the whole ruined evening in her face, and lying to her suddenly seemed like one more way of spending something precious.

"I suspected," Mara said. "I hoped I was wrong. Hoping is easier than saying."

"What did you suspect? Say it plainly. I want it plain."

Mara took a breath. The street was empty; a lamp flickered on in a window across the lane; somewhere a dog complained about the cold coming down off the ridge, where the siege-lanterns burned beyond sight of anyone sensible.

"When the wardens built this valley's protection," she said, "they didn't build it out of stone. Stone is just where they parked it. They built it out of resonance — sung resonance, sustained harmony, the kind of working that has to be fed or it dies. The old orders fed their wards with trained voices. Chanters, in shifts, day and night. It's in the histories nobody reads anymore." She watched Beatrix's face go carefully blank, the way faces do when they're bracing. "The canticle your grandmother's people wrote isn't a song *about* the ward, Beatrix. It's a song that *is* the ward. And a working like that doesn't run on good intentions. It takes power from wherever power is standing closest."

"Power," Beatrix repeated. "From my choir. From children."

"From whoever sings it truest." Mara's jaw tightened. "That's the ugly arithmetic of it. The song doesn't care about age. It cares about openness. Tam sings with no walls at all — you've seen it, everyone sees it. So when the ward pulled, it pulled hardest on him."

Beatrix was quiet for a long moment. Down the street, the Vosses' window glowed warm, and behind it Mara could see shapes moving — Mrs. Voss with broth, probably, and Tam propped on pillows already complaining that he was fine, honestly, could he please have the big piece of cake.

"He'll sleep twelve hours," Mara said, "and wake up hungry enough to eat a boot, and by Sunday he'll be climbing the mill fence again. Hollowing from a single verse, in a healthy child, passes. It's the sustained kind that kills." She said it gently, and watched it land anyway, and hated that gentleness couldn't soften arithmetic.

"But it will happen again." Beatrix's voice had gone very level. Level the way ice is level. "Every rehearsal. Every time they find the harmonies — and they're finding them better every week, because I keep teaching them, because the ward keeps needing them. I'm feeding my singers to a wall, one verse at a time, and calling it music lessons."

"No." Mara caught her hands. Beatrix's fingers were cold. "Listen to me. There is another way. There has to be. The original chanters were adults, trained, rotating — they shared the cost so no one carried it alone. Your choir stumbled into this by accident; they shouldn't be carrying any of it. What we need is to find how the first wardens did it properly — the rotations, the safeguards, the—"

"The what?" Beatrix said softly. "The records, Mara? The instructions?" She squeezed Mara's hands back, hard. "Where would those be? In the archive? The archive that opened itself and handed us a book with our names in it? Or maybe in the heart-chamber under the ward-stone, the one neither of us knows how to reach?"

Mara had no answer. The wind came down the street, smelling of woodsmoke and, underneath it, faintly, of ozone — the smell of the ridge, of the storm-mages' lanterns, of everything waiting.

"Thursday," Beatrix said at last. She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, the way she probably did in front of second-graders. "I told them Thursday. So we have until Thursday to figure out how to teach a song that doesn't eat children. And Mara—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "When we figure it out. Whatever it costs. It comes to me first. I brought them into this. My blood built the thing that's hungry. Do you understand me?"

"I understand you," Mara said. "I don't agree with you. But I understand you."

"That's a start." Beatrix managed something that was almost a smile. "Go home. Eat something. You look like you've been standing in doorways all evening."

"I have been standing in doorways all evening."

"Well. You're very good at it." And she kissed Mara's cheek, quick and fierce, and walked up the Vosses' path to check on her singer one more time, because of course she did.

* * *

Mara did not go home.

She told herself she was going home. She got as far as the lane behind the tannery, where the valley's roofs gave way to pasture and the pasture to the dark shoulder of the ridge, and then her feet made their own argument and carried her east, along the sheep track, to the place where the old ward-stone stood in its ring of trampled grass.

It was worse than last week. She felt it before she saw it — a wrongness in the air, the sensation of a held breath going ragged. The stone itself looked the same, squat and lichen-crusted, but the field around it had changed. The grass inside the ring had gone silver-gray, drained of color, and when Mara stepped to the edge of it she felt the pull — faint, patient, immense — like standing near the lip of a well.

The ward was drinking. All of it, everywhere, all the time now. The choir's harmonies bought it hours; the stone spent them by morning. And somewhere past the ridgeline, forty storm-mages with siege-lanterns and a parley deadline were counting down to the new moon, and they could feel it too. A failing ward was an invitation wearing a thin disguise.

Mara crouched at the ring's edge and laid her palm flat on the ground, the way she used to read weather as a girl, before reading weather meant law-breaking. The land hummed under her hand, thin and strained. Familiar. Terribly familiar.

Because she knew this feeling from the inside. She had spent ten years feeling exactly this: the ache of a vessel emptied, the hunger of a thing built to hold power and holding none. She had thought it was exile's mark on her alone. It had never occurred to her — and she was angry at herself, genuinely angry, that it had never occurred to her — that the valley might feel it too. That the ward Beatrix's ancestors died to build had spent a century running on dregs, and that the reason it recognized Beatrix's bloodline, flared for her, *pulled* for her, was that it had been waiting all that time for its makers to come home.

And now its makers' heir was feeding it children.

"No," Mara said aloud, to the stone, to the dark, to the arithmetic. Her breath fogged. Somewhere far above, beyond the veil of the ward, thunder grumbled — natural thunder, probably, or maybe not, maybe the ridge answering her voice the way it always used to, the way it still dreamed of doing. "You don't get the boy. You don't get any of them."

The stone said nothing. Stones are polite that way.

But as she stood to go, the pull at the ring's edge shifted — just slightly, just for a heartbeat — and Mara felt it turn, unmistakably, toward *her*. Toward the hollow place in her chest where her storms used to live. As if the ward had finally noticed what she was. As if it had been waiting, patient as lichen, for the one person in the valley who had power to spare and nowhere left to spend it.

Mara walked home fast, hands in her pockets, and did not let herself think about how much of her answer had already been yes.
