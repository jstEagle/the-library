## 11. What Mara Renounced

The trouble with secrets, Mara had learned over ten years of keeping them, was that they were like bees: they needed somewhere to live, and if you didn't give them a proper hive, they'd build one in your walls.

She had been carrying the secret of the heart-chamber for three days now, ever since the ward-stone at the eastern boundary had flared gold under Beatrix's palm and hummed a name into the air between them—a name that was not Ives, and not Whitlock either, but something older, something carved in a script Mara had only seen once before, scratched into the margin of a stolen book.

*Vaelora.*

Beatrix's grandmother's name. The warden's signet. And beneath all of it, humming like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing, the knowledge Mara had been sitting on since she was nineteen years old and too proud to ask questions:

There was a chamber under Vesper Hollow. She had found it her first winter in exile, half-frozen and furious, following a thread of warmth through the snow like a fool following a will-o'-the-wisp. She had stood at its threshold for exactly as long as it took to feel what was down there—the vast, patient weight of the ward's sleeping heart—and then she had climbed back out and told no one, because telling someone would have meant admitting she'd gone looking, and going looking would have meant admitting she still wanted magic, and wanting magic was the one thing she'd sworn off along with everything else.

Ten years. Ten years of walking past that hillside with her eyes carefully elsewhere, the way you avoid the house where you used to be happy.

And now the ward was failing, a choirboy lay pale as milk in his bed from singing one verse of a song he shouldn't have known, storm-mages were camped on the ridgeline counting days until the new moon, and Beatrix Ives had looked at her across a kitchen table scattered with century-old letters and said, "You know something about what's under us. You've known this whole time. Haven't you?"

Mara had said yes. It had felt, strangely, like setting down a heavy basket.

Which was how she came to be standing on the hillside above the old mill race at dawn, with Beatrix beside her in a coat two sizes too big and a lantern she'd insisted on bringing despite the fact that Mara could find the entrance blindfolded, in fog, upside down.

"It's behind the waterfall," Beatrix said, squinting at the thin ribbon of water sliding down the rock face. "Of course it is. Everything important in this valley is behind a waterfall or inside a beehive."

"The hive thing was one time."

"One time I got stung eleven times, Mara."

"You were wearing my hat. That's practically an invitation." But Mara's heart wasn't in the teasing, and Beatrix knew it, and after a moment Beatrix took her hand—calloused palm against calloused palm, beekeeper and piano teacher, both of them holding on—and squeezed once, and let go, and that was better than any speech.

"Ready?" Beatrix asked.

"No," Mara said honestly. "But I've been ready for ten years, which I suspect is worse."

She pressed her shoulder to the wet stone where the waterfall thinned, found the seam by touch—the same seam, exactly where her hands remembered, though her hands had been twenty-four then and hadn't yet learned what frostbite did to knuckles—and pushed. The stone swung inward with a groan of old hinges that should have rusted solid decades ago and hadn't, because nothing in this valley decayed the way things were supposed to. The ward kept even its doors.

Cold air breathed out, smelling of deep earth and lightning before a storm, though there had been no storms here in ten years except the ones Mara dreamed.

"Ladies first," Mara said, and stepped back so Beatrix could go ahead of her into the dark, because she was a coward, and because she wanted to watch Beatrix's face when the lantern light found the stairs.

It was worth it. Beatrix went very still, lantern raised, and the light ran down a spiral stair cut from living rock, each step worn smooth in the center by feet long dead, and the walls were covered—floor to unseen ceiling—in carved lines that caught the flame and seemed to hold it, running in patterns that weren't decoration, couldn't be, because they repeated with the discipline of sheet music.

"They're verses," Beatrix whispered. "Mara. They're *staves*. This whole wall is written music."

"I know," Mara said. "I never learned to read it. I told myself I didn't want to." She started down. "I lied to myself a lot, back then. It was practically a hobby."

* * *

The stair went down farther than the hill was tall, which was the sort of thing Mara had decided ten years ago not to think about too hard, and ended in a chamber that made her breath catch even the second time.

The lantern light simply gave up partway across the room. Not dimmed—gave up, the way sound gives up underwater—as though the air itself past a certain line belonged to something older than fire. Beyond that line, the chamber glowed faintly from within: a floor of pale stone inlaid with a circle thirty feet across, rings upon rings of carved staves converging on a center where a single stone rose waist-high, smooth as a river cobble, humming.

That was the sound Mara had heard in her dreams for a decade. Not thunder. The moment *after* thunder, stretched out forever—the held breath of the sky.

"The binding circle," she said. Her voice came out flat and strange. "The original. Everything else—the ward-stones, the borders, the whole invisible roof over this valley—it's just... echoes of this. Fingers pointing at the hand."

Beatrix had drifted forward, lantern held high, and stopped at the edge of the light like a swimmer at cold water. "How do you know all this? You said you never came down here."

"I came down here once. My first winter. I stood right about where you're standing, and I listened to that—" she nodded at the humming stone, "—and I turned around and left, and I spent ten years pretending the hillside was boring." She laughed, and it came out cracked. "I'm very good at pretending. Ask anyone. Ask me. I'll pretend I'm good at it."

"Mara." Beatrix turned. In the strange half-light her face was all shadow and shine. "Why are we really here? Not for the ward. Not for Tam. Why did *you* want to come?"

Because, Mara thought, because the ward-stone sang your grandmother's name and mine has been silent for ten years and I need to know if it was always going to end like this, if I was always walking toward this room, if exile was a door or just a hallway—

"Because of my mother," she said aloud, and was surprised to hear it, and then wasn't, because Beatrix had that effect; she was the human equivalent of a well-tuned string, and things near her tended to ring true whether they meant to or not.

"She was a storm-caller," Mara went on, words coming faster now, the bees swarming out of the walls at last. "A great one. Third voice of the order, they called her. When I broke the law—when I called the storm over Marrow Fen to drown the fever before it drowned the village—I wasn't inventing anything. I did exactly what she taught me. Find the water in the air, ask it politely, mean it. She died when I was sixteen. Lightning strike, which everyone said was fitting and I thought was obscene. And I have spent ten years being angry at her for teaching me the thing that got me exiled, and angrier at myself for missing her anyway, and I never—" She stopped. Made herself stop. "I never asked why a woman of the order would teach her daughter the forbidden forms. The ones that break the law. Nobody teaches what they don't believe in, Beatrix. So either my mother was a hypocrite, or..."

"Or she believed the law was wrong," Beatrix said quietly.

"Or she knew something." Mara lifted the lantern and walked to the edge of the glowing circle, close enough that the light touched her boots, and looked down at the innermost ring of carved names.

She had seen them once before, that first winter, from the doorway, too far away and too proud to read them. Now the lantern brought them close: rows of names in the old script, cut deep and true, the founding wardens of Vesper Hollow. Names of people who had built a cage of quiet around a valley and poured their song into it, and whose descendants had been hunted for it, if Beatrix's letters told the truth, hunted and scattered and burned out of history until only one girl with a piano and a choir remained.

She read the names the way you read a list of the dead, with respect and distance. Halvard Stone. Maren of the Low Fields. Oskar Brightwater. On and on, around the ring.

And then, at the circle's southern point, where the carver had set the name apart with a border of small thunder-signs, a name she knew.

Not the surname. Surnames could be changed, dropped, buried. But the given name, cut in that confident deep hand:

*Saoirse.*

Mara's knees decided independently of her that the floor was a good place to be. She sat down hard on cold stone, lantern tilting, and stared.

"That's her," she heard herself say. "That's—that's my mother's name. Saoirse Whitlock. Born Saoirse Vale, before she married my father, before—" Her voice did something humiliating and she let it. "She's carved in the founding circle. She's not a descendant, Beatrix, look at the cutting, look at the depth, she's *original*. She was here. A hundred years ago. She helped *build* this."

Beatrix crouched beside her, and didn't touch her yet, and Mara loved her a little for knowing that.

"But the founding wardens were massacred," Beatrix said slowly. "My letters—my grandmother wrote that the order hunted them down, generation after generation. If your mother was one of them..."

"Then she didn't die of a lightning strike." The thought arrived whole and terrible, like weather. "Or she did, and it wasn't weather. Oh—oh, *fool*, Mara, you absolute—" She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "They sent me to spy on her. Don't you see? I was nineteen, I was talented, I was loyal, and the archmage assigned me to the third voice's daughter, and I thought it was an honor. Everyone thought it was an honor. And then I broke the law spectacularly enough to be exiled instead of executed, and I spent ten years thinking I'd failed everyone, disgraced her memory, wasted the gift she gave me—"

"You didn't fail," Beatrix said. "Mara. Look at the circle. Look at where her name is."

Mara looked. Southern point. The point of *binding*, if she remembered the old geometry her mother had drummed into her over kitchen tables and storm-fronts—the anchor-point, the place where the circle held fastest, where the whole structure leaned its weight.

Her mother hadn't been hiding in the order. She had been *infiltrating* it. And when her daughter—the daughter she'd raised on forbidden forms, raised deliberately, raised like a seed banked against winter—was threatened with execution, Saoirse Whitlock had done the only thing that would save her and serve the work at once.

She had gotten her exiled. To the one valley in the world the order could not follow. To the place her own name was carved in the floor.

"She didn't fail to protect me," Mara whispered. "Exile *was* the protection. This whole miserable, beautiful decade—she chose it for me. She put me here like—" a wet, disbelieving laugh, "—like planting a bee swarm in a new hive. And I spent it sulking. I spent ten years treating the greatest gift anyone ever gave me like a prison sentence."

"You didn't know."

"I didn't *ask*. There's a difference, and it's mine to carry." Mara wiped her face with the back of her wrist, beekeeper-fashion, efficient and graceless, and Beatrix finally did touch her then, arm around her shoulders, chin resting on her hair, and they sat together on the edge of a hundred-year-old spell while it hummed its held breath.

After a while, Beatrix said, "The stone. The one in the middle. It's been humming since we came in, but listen—it's changed."

Mara listened. She was right. The note had risen, subtly, the way a choir shifts when the right voice joins it. And it was leaning—not sound, but attention—toward Beatrix.

"It knows you," Mara said. "Your blood built it. Your grandmother's name is probably down there too, somewhere in the outer rings." She paused. "Beatrix. When Wren comes back—when the new moon comes—we can't fight the order with weather. I've tried; the ward eats my storms before they clear my wrists. But if the ward runs on song, and the last warden-heir is standing in its heart..."

"Then maybe the valley doesn't need a soldier," Beatrix said slowly. "Maybe it needs a conductor." She was quiet a moment, and Mara watched the idea take root in her face, terror and wonder fighting for the same ground. "Tam's verse took something out of him. Whatever this is, it takes what it needs. If I sing here—if I bind myself into this circle—"

"Then we'll find out together," Mara said, "and not one step sooner, and not alone. That's a rule now. New rule. No more planting people places without telling them."

Above them, muffled by a hundred feet of rock, a sound reached the chamber that did not belong underground: a bell. The Hollow's warning bell, rung hard, rung fast, the rhythm every child in the valley knew and dreaded.

Three-and-one. Three-and-one. *Riders on the rim.*

Mara closed her eyes and felt, for the first time in ten years, the faint far-off pressure of a storm front gathering beyond the ward—massing, patient, and aimed straight down the mountain at the home her mother had chosen for her.

"The new moon's four days away," Beatrix breathed.

"Then somebody's impatient," Mara said, and got to her feet, and offered Beatrix her hand.
