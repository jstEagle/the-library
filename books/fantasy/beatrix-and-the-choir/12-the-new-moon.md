## 12. The New Moon

The night the new moon came, Beatrix Ives was grading sight-reading exercises at her kitchen table when the sky over the valley's rim turned the color of a bruise.

She knew it wasn't weather. She had spent three weeks learning the difference—learning it in her bones, in the way the ward hummed through the floorboards of the schoolhouse, in the way candle flames leaned toward the north as if listening. Real storms rolled in from the west and smelled like rain and cut grass. This was coming from the north, where no storm should come from, and it smelled like ozone and old iron and something else beneath it, something like fear.

The knock on her door came before she could even stand.

"Mara." Beatrix opened it to find Mara Whitlock on her step, still in her beekeeping jacket, veil pushed up, hair wild with smoke-smell. Behind her, half the town was already in the lane with lanterns, faces tipped toward that wrong-colored sky.

"They're here," Mara said. No preamble. They'd passed the point of preambles somewhere around the third night of teaching the canticle to a room full of farmers' children. "Wren's back. And she didn't come alone this time."

"How many?"

"Enough." Mara's jaw did the thing it did when she was swallowing something sharp. "Vanguard. Two hundred riders, maybe more behind them. Siege-lanterns on the ridgeline."

"Siege-lanterns," Beatrix repeated. "I'm sorry, siege *lanterns*? What do lanterns have to do with a siege?"

"You'll see." Mara held out her hand. It was shaking, just slightly, and Beatrix took it without comment, because some things you acknowledged and some things you simply held. "Come on, choir teacher. Warden law is waiting."

---

They rode to the valley's edge in Tam's father's cart, wedged between crates of late apples, because apparently when your world ends you take whatever transportation is available. The whole way down the valley road, Beatrix watched the northern rim and tried to understand what she was seeing.

It looked, at first, like someone had strung festival lights along the ridge. Dozens of them. Hundreds. But they burned the wrong color—not gold, not warm, but a cold white-blue that hurt to look at directly, and they didn't flicker. They *pulsed*, all together, in a slow rhythm like breathing. Like a heartbeat.

And then she understood what they were for, because each pulse sent a thin thread of lightning crawling down the mountainside, licking at the air where the ward stood, tasting it. The ward flared in answer—a shimmer only visible now, in the dark, like heat-haze made of moonlight—and where the lightning touched it, the shimmer thinned.

"They're not attacking," Beatrix said slowly. "They're *measuring*."

"Now you're thinking like a warden." Mara's voice was hollow. "That's what siege-lanterns are for. You don't break a ward by hitting it. You find the note it can't hold, and you sing it until it shatters." She glanced sideways. "Sorry. Bad word choice."

Beatrix almost laughed. Almost. "No, it's—it's actually useful. If they're singing at it, then we know what we're doing matters. We've been doing the same thing from inside."

"We have. And we're losing." Mara pointed. "Look at the mill road. See how the haze stops? There's a gap there now. Three days ago that was solid."

The cart jolted to a stop at the gathering ground below the ward-stone—the great leaning monolith that had flared for Beatrix's blood three weeks ago and hadn't stopped humming since. Half the valley was there: farmers with pitchforks they clearly hoped never to use, Old Marta from the bakery with flour still on her apron, the Hollis brothers who ran the ferry, and behind them, arranged in nervous rows on the schoolhouse steps, her choir.

Her choir. Twenty-two children and eleven adults who had learned a dead warden's canticle in three weeks because their teacher had asked, and who were now standing in the cold at nine o'clock at night holding candles, because nobody had told them to go home and everybody had understood anyway.

Tam found her first. He was still pale from the surge—he'd been drained so badly he'd slept two days—but his eyes were bright and his scarf was wrapped up to his nose. "Miss Ives. Is it starting?"

"It might be, love. I need you to stay with the others."

"I heard something. In my fever. About the last verse—"

"*Stay with the others,*" Beatrix said, gentler than the words deserved, and Tam went, but not before she saw the knowledge settle deeper behind his eyes. That was a conversation for a day when the sky wasn't bleeding. She added it to the growing list of conversations she was postponing, right under *tell Mara about the letters* and *figure out what a warden-heir is actually supposed to do.*

Then the parley horn sounded from the north, and every postponed thing in the world went quiet.

---

Wren Calloway rode down through the gap in the ward alone, under a white flag, and Beatrix watched Mara watch her.

They'd told her about Wren, of course. The friend. The one who'd stood in the judgment hall ten years ago and said the words that put exile in Mara's mouth instead of mercy. Beatrix had imagined her a hundred ways—a monster, a zealot, a woman carved out of ice. What dismounted at the base of the ward-stone was none of those things. She was maybe thirty-five, broad-shouldered, gray-eyed, with a sword she left sheathed and a face that looked like it had been doing arithmetic about regret for a decade and never once gotten a number it liked.

"Mara." Wren's voice carried across the gathering ground. "You look well."

"Don't," Mara said.

"All right." Wren stopped an honest twenty paces away and spread her empty hands. "Then let's do this properly. I'm here under parley law, old law, the kind your order pretends to respect. And I'm not speaking to you tonight, Mara. Not first." Her gray eyes moved past Mara and settled on Beatrix, and something in them shifted—recognition, or its cousin. "I'm speaking to the heir."

The whole gathering ground seemed to lean in. Beatrix felt the ward-stone hum against her spine like a struck bell that wouldn't finish ringing.

"I'm not an heir," Beatrix said. "I teach children to sing rounds. I keep bees' worth of paperwork. Until a month ago my most significant inheritance was a soup tureen."

"And until a month ago I believed the Whitlock line ended in a judgment hall." Wren's voice didn't waver, but her hands, Beatrix noticed, were very carefully still. "The archmage knows differently now. That's why the vanguard is on your ridge instead of a diplomatic envoy at your door. He doesn't want the prophecy book, whatever he told us. He wants the ward's claimant either bound to him or buried, and he wants it done before the new moon passes and warden law lets you call a formal convocation."

"Which is why," Beatrix said, and heard her own voice steady itself the way it steadied when a rehearsal fell apart and she had to rebuild it mid-phrase, "I am invoking it. Right now. In front of everyone."

"You don't know how—"

"I read the letters, Wren Calloway. My grandmother kept everything." Beatrix stepped forward, past Mara, past the ward-stone, until she stood in the open space between the valley and the rider, and she made herself say the words she'd memorized by candlelight, words written in a hand that had been massacred for writing them:

*"By warden law, older than the order, older than the archmage's seat: any claimant of the ward may call a truce of three days, that the valley may gather its voices. During the truce, no hand raised in malice shall cross the ward, nor lightning fall within it, nor siege be laid. So it was sworn at the building, and so it holds while one heir remains to speak it."*

Silence. The kind of silence Beatrix knew intimately—the silence after a soloist's first note, when the whole room decides whether to believe.

Wren stared at her. Then, slowly, she laughed—one short, disbelieving breath that fogged in the cold. "You actually said it. Word-perfect."

"There was a lot of time to practice. Someone keeps burning the valley's edge."

"The truce binds me as much as you," Wren said. "Understand that. When the three days end, I ride at the front, and I will not stop again, because if I do, the man behind me will send someone who enjoys stopping." She swung back into her saddle, then paused, looking down at both of them—at Mara's scarred hands hidden in her pockets, at Beatrix's chin lifted like a student who refuses to sing flat. "Mara. For what it's worth. I testified because he ordered it, and I've hated myself in four languages since. That's not forgiveness. It's just true."

"Wren." Mara's voice cracked down the middle. "Whose order was it? Whose *order*, Wren?"

But Wren was already turning her horse, and the gap in the ward swallowed her, and the siege-lanterns along the ridge pulsed once—all together, like a held breath—and dimmed to embers.

---

Afterward, the valley did not go home.

That was the part Beatrix would remember longest, later. Not the lanterns, not the lightning, not even the words of the law in her own mouth. It was Old Marta marching straight from the gathering ground to her ovens, because "scared people need bread, and tomorrow they'll need it more." It was the Hollis brothers dragging the ferry up onto the bank and nailing it shut. It was Tam's mother organizing bedrolls in the schoolhouse without anyone asking her to, and the choir, her ridiculous brave choir, refusing to blow out their candles until she promised—promised—that rehearsal would happen in the morning, truce or no truce.

"They think the song will save them," Mara said quietly. They were standing at the edge of the lamplight, watching the ridge, where the ember-lanterns breathed their slow cold rhythm. "Three days of rehearsal and a hymn. Against two hundred storm-mages."

"People thought a soup tureen was my most significant inheritance," Beatrix said. "People are often wrong about what matters."

Mara huffed something that was almost a laugh. Then it died, and in the dark Beatrix saw her look down at her own hands—flexing them, curling them, the way she'd been doing more and more lately, like they ached, like they were hiding something.

"Mara. Your hands."

"They're fine."

"They're not fine. You flinched when you took the bread at dinner. You've been wearing your gloves indoors. And three nights ago I saw the bandages under your sleeve when you reached for the honey jar, and I said nothing, because I was tired, and because I trusted you to tell me." Beatrix kept her voice low and level, the voice she used for the boy who insisted he wasn't crying. "We are standing at the edge of a war, and I have exactly one person I cannot lose, and you are lying to me about your *hands.*"

For a long moment Mara said nothing. The lantern-light on the ridge pulsed. Somewhere behind them, the choir's candles guttered and held.

"When I tried to rekindle my magic," Mara finally said, "to defend the valley—the ward ate it. All of it. And it took payment." She pulled off her glove. Even in the dark, Beatrix could see the scars: silver-white lines branching across her knuckles and up her wrists like frost on a window, like lightning frozen mid-strike. "Storm-scar. It's what happens when power goes through a body that isn't grounded. I've been hiding it because—" Her breath caught. "Because you already carry enough. Because you're spending yourself teaching a dead canticle to children, and I thought if you saw what it costs, you'd stop. And I was afraid of what happens to this valley if you stop."

Beatrix looked at the scars for a long time. Then she took Mara's bare hand in both of hers, careful as handling a fledgling, and pressed her thumb gently along one silver branch.

"This is what it costs," she said. "And you don't get to decide alone what I can carry. That's the whole lesson, Mara. That's the entire canticle. Nobody sings the verse alone."

Mara made a sound that was half laugh, half something wetter, and leaned her forehead against Beatrix's shoulder, and for one full minute, while the enemy breathed light on the ridge above them, they simply stood there, two ordinary impossible women, holding on.

Then Beatrix lifted her head.

"Three days," she said. "The truce gives us three days, and Wren gave us one more thing whether she meant to or not. She said the archmage wants me bound or buried *before* the new moon passes and warden law lets me call a convocation." She turned toward the schoolhouse, where twenty-three candles still burned in young hands. "Which means there's something warden law lets me do that frightens him more than any army. And I intend to find out what it is before those lanterns come back to life."

Above the valley, the new moon rose—thin, dark, patient—and the ward-stone at Beatrix's back began, very softly, to sing.
