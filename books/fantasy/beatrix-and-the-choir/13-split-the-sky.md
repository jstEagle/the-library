## 13. Split the Sky

The night before the new moon, Mara Whitlock climbed the hill behind the mill with a jar of honey, a coil of copper wire, and every intention of doing something profoundly stupid.

She had told Beatrix she was going to check on the hives. This was even true, in the way that a door is true when you close it behind you. The hives did need checking—late autumn was when the bees clustered tight and hummed their low winter note, and Mara liked to press her palm flat against the wood and feel that small, stubborn warmth and pretend it was enough to live on. But the wire coiled over her shoulder had nothing to do with bees, and neither, if she was honest, did the storm gathering itself beyond the ridgeline like a dog that had caught a scent.

Three days. That was what Beatrix had wrung out of Wren Calloway at the valley's edge, standing in her plain coat with her choir-teacher's voice and refusing to move while lightning leaned on the horizon like bored soldiers. Three days of warden law, and two of them were already gone. Tomorrow night the moon would be a hole in the sky, and Wren would come back through it with everything the order had.

And Mara would be standing there with her hands empty, because that was what she had chosen ten years ago, and choices, she had learned, were the one magic that never faded.

She sat down on the cold grass beside the tallest hive—Old Bertha, she called her, though the beekeepers' almanac insisted queens should be named something dignified—and set the jar down between her knees. The honey inside caught the last gray light. She had taken it from the pantry shelf that morning without asking, which in Vesper Hollow was practically a crime wave.

"All right," she said to the hive, because talking to bees was normal and talking to yourself was not, and a woman needed to keep her categories straight. "I'm going to tell you a secret, and then I'm going to do something foolish, and you're not going to judge me, because you spend your whole lives flying into things that sting you."

The hive hummed. It always hummed. Ten years in this valley and it was still the sound she loved best—the sound of a thing alive and unbothered by prophecy.

Here was the secret: her magic wasn't gone.

Not entirely. Not the way she'd told Beatrix, not the way she'd told herself on the bad nights, lying awake in the cottage listening for thunder that never came. The ward ate magic; that was its whole purpose, its great patient hunger, the reason exiles like her had crawled into this valley for a century and found it kind. But *eaten* was not the same as *erased*. A storm-caller's power didn't sit quietly in the blood like other magics did. It moved. It wanted. And on certain nights—when the pressure dropped and the air went metallic and the hair on her arms lifted—Mara could feel it down in her bones like water behind a dam, thin as thread, but moving.

She had spent ten years pretending not to notice. That had been the vow, after all. She had broken her order's law to save a village, and the price they'd named was exile, and exile into Vesper Hollow meant surrender—not just leaving the storms behind but letting the valley take them, letting the ward drink her dry year by year until she was only a woman who kept bees and flinched at weather.

She had thought she'd made peace with it. Then the prophecy book had fallen open in the archive and put her name on a page beside Beatrix's, and the ward had begun to fail, and Wren Calloway had come to the ridgeline with a parley flag and an offer shaped like a knife, and Mara had discovered that peace was a thing you could lose track of, like a key, like a season.

Tomorrow the storm-mages came. And the valley—this soft, ridiculous, honey-scented valley full of people who left bread on her porch and asked her opinion on jam—would have nothing between it and them but a failing ward, a choir teacher, and forty children who knew three verses of a canticle that took what it needed from whoever sang it.

Unless the valley also had a storm-caller.

"Thread," she said aloud, testing the word. Her grandmother had used it once, long ago, before everything: *the power runs thin in you, girl, but thin thread still sews.* Mara had thought she understood. She hadn't. Understanding was going to require the copper wire, the highest point in the valley, and a willingness to be struck by lightning on purpose, which was, she admitted, the most storm-caller plan she had ever conceived, and proof that some parts of her had never been exiled at all.

* * *

The hill behind the mill was called Widow's Rest, which Mara had always felt was unnecessarily ominous, and it rose above the eastern fields where the ward-stone stood half-buried in bracken—the one that had flared for Beatrix, that had recognized her bloodline and burned blue for the first time in a hundred years. From up here Mara could see the whole shape of the valley: the crooked lanes, the mill with its wheel turning slow, the archive roof, the schoolhouse where light still burned in a window because Beatrix was inside marking scales or writing letters or simply existing in a way that pulled Mara's attention across half a mile of dark like a fish on a line.

She unwound the copper wire and began to drive its end into the earth with a rock, working by feel more than sight. Lightning-lore was mostly plumbing, her mother had said once, and Mara had laughed then, and did not laugh now, because thinking of her mother still felt like pressing on a bruise she couldn't locate.

The theory was simple. The practice was going to hurt.

Storm-magic answered callings—a raised hand, a spoken word, a need shouted into the sky. But the ward lay over this valley like a second skin, and anything Mara called would have to pass through it, and the ward was hungry, and the ward was failing, and a starving thing does not sip. If she called lightning down onto Widow's Rest, the ward would take it. All of it. The strike, the charge, the answering surge in her own blood—everything, drunk down through the ground before it could do more than flicker.

But.

But the ward was *failing*. And a failing thing has cracks. And if she stood at the center of the calling, holding the thread of it in her body instead of letting it fly free—if she made herself the rod, the vessel, the cup—then maybe, maybe, she could catch a little of it back before the valley swallowed the rest. Enough to feel it again. Enough to remember the shape of it. Enough that tomorrow, when Wren came over the ridge, Mara might have one storm in her hands instead of none.

One storm against an order. It was not a good trade. It was, however, the only one on the table.

"You could stop," she told the hive-less dark, the wind, the distant window-light. "You could walk down the hill and knock on the schoolhouse door and say, Beatrix, I'm frightened, and she would—" She stopped. Beatrix would do any number of things, all of them warm, all of them involving her hands and her voice and that look she got when Mara pretended to be fine, the look that saw straight through to the wiring underneath. Beatrix would hold her hands and say something about how fear was information, not instruction, because she was the sort of person who had sayings like that and meant them.

Which was exactly why Mara was not going to knock on that door.

She planted the last length of wire, stripped the insulation from the top with her teeth, and stood back. Above the ridge, the clouds had organized themselves into something with intent. Far off, too far to be natural, thunder rolled in a rhythm almost like drums. The storm-mages were already singing their weather into shape. She could hear the order's harmonies under the thunder if she listened—the same training she'd had, the same scales, weaponized. Wren had probably written half those progressions. They'd been girls together, before. Before the trial. Before the testimony. Before the word *exile* got carved into Mara's life like a name on a stone.

"Don't think about her," Mara advised herself. "Think about the lightning."

She raised her hands.

For a moment, nothing. Ten years of nothing, the familiar silence, the ache like a phantom limb. Then the air changed. Pressure dropped so fast her ears popped, and the hair on her arms rose, and somewhere below her sternum something *turned over*—thin as thread, yes, but moving, answering, remembering. Joy hit her first, sharp and shameful, joy before fear, joy like a struck match, and she let the calling build, let it climb her spine, let the sky lean down—

The world went white.

There was no separation between the bolt and her body. There was no time in which to regret. There was only the strike, and then the ward, and the ward was not a crack, the ward was a *mouth*, and she had fed herself into it. She felt it drink—the lightning, the charge, the surge, all of it pouring out of her through her palms and into the hungry ground—and she clung to the thread the way her grandmother had taught her, *hold the middle, girl, the middle is yours*, and for one impossible heartbeat she held it, she held a piece, she felt the storm in her blood again, vast and blue and hers—

Then the ward noticed what she was doing, and took that too.

The backlash came up through her arms like a river reversing. She was on her knees before she knew she'd fallen. The copper wire glowed dull orange and sagged. Every nerve from shoulder to fingertip screamed a single syllable, and it took her a long, ringing moment to understand that the syllable was *no*, that the ward had spoken, that the valley itself had put its mouth against her ear and said: *not this. not you. not again.*

She knelt in the wet grass and breathed.

The pain settled slowly, the way floodwater settles, finding every low place. When she finally turned her hands over, the lightning had left its signature. Lichtenberg figures, her mother would have called them—ferns of pale silver branching up from both palms, across the wrists, fading toward the elbow like frost on a window. They didn't bleed. They didn't even hurt anymore, now that the first agony had passed. They just *were*, delicate and strange, the mark of a thing that had tried to pass through her and been refused.

Mara looked at them for a long time.

Then she started to laugh, quietly, helplessly, there on her knees in the dark, because of course. Of course. Ten years of surrender, honored perfectly, and the one night she reached for it back, the valley said no—and worse, funnier, crueler: the attempt had cost her anyway. She could feel it, the thinness in her blood grown thinner, the thread worn nearly through. She had paid a tithe to a god she'd renounced, and gotten nothing back but scars shaped like ferns.

"Well," she said to the hive-less dark. "That went great."

* * *

Hiding the hands turned out to be easier than expected, which should have been her first warning.

She washed in the rain barrel behind the cottage, hissing through her teeth, and wrapped her palms in the linen strips she kept for bee stings, and told herself it was temporary, it was practical, stung hands were common enough in autumn. By morning the silver ferns had faded to faint white lines, visible only if you knew to look, and Mara had gotten very good, over ten years, at arranging her life so that no one looked.

Beatrix looked. That was the problem. That had always been the problem, since the archive, since the candle guttered and the case fell open and a century-old book said *tonight* in ink that flew into their skin. Beatrix looked at Mara the way she read music—completely, patiently, hearing the parts underneath the melody—and Mara had spent two days being grateful for it and terrified of it in equal measure.

So when Beatrix appeared at her gate that morning with a basket of pears and a face full of determined cheer, Mara performed. She performed the way she'd performed at the trial, actually, which was a thought she stepped around carefully, the way you step around ice.

"Pears," Beatrix announced. "From the Hendersons' tree. They said to tell you thank you for the smoke, whatever that means."

"I smoked their hive for them last week. Free labor. It's how I launder my reputation." Mara kept her hands in her pockets. It was cool enough for pockets. It was, she noted, the first genuinely cool morning of the season, which felt less like luck than like the valley conspiring with her, and she chose not to examine that.

Beatrix set the basket on the gatepost and studied her with the particular stillness she got before difficult passages. "You weren't at the rehearsal yesterday."

"I had bees."

"You always have bees."

"They're very demanding."

"Mara." Just the name. Beatrix said it the way she corrected a singer who was reaching for a note they were afraid of—not with blame, but with certainty that the note was in there somewhere. "Wren comes back tomorrow. Tam's still gray around the eyes from the canticle. Half the town is packing carts and the other half is sharpening things, and you—" she gestured at all of Mara, the composed surface of her, "—you are making jokes about laundry."

"Laundering reputations is serious work."

"Mara."

Something in Mara's chest pulled tight, the thread of her, fraying. She wanted—gods, how she wanted—to put her ruined hands in Beatrix's careful ones and say *look what I tried, look what it cost, I only wanted to protect you.* The words climbed her throat like the calling had climbed her spine. She swallowed them. Because here was the truth she'd found on Widow's Rest, kneeling in the wet grass: if she told Beatrix, Beatrix would stop her. Beatrix would find a way to make the plan safer, slower, shared—and there was no safe version, no slow version, no version where anyone else carried it. What Mara intended to do tomorrow night, when the storm-mages crossed the line, required exactly one person willing to stand in the open and be the rod. Everyone else would just be in the way of the lightning.

Some sacrifices you made alone or not at all. That was the pride talking. She knew it was the pride talking. She let it talk anyway, because pride, unlike magic, had never once been taken from her.

"I'm fine," she said, and smiled, and the smile held, because she was excellent at this, because she'd had ten years of practice at being fine. "Truly. Tired. Everyone's tired. Ask the bees."

Beatrix held her gaze a moment longer. Then she picked up the basket, took out a pear, and pressed it into Mara's hidden hands through the pocket fabric—briefly, warmly, a benediction disguised as fruit distribution.

"Eat something," she said. "And Mara? Whatever you're carrying—it weighs less split in two. That's not poetry. That's physics. I teach children to carry buckets; I know about load-bearing."

She walked off down the lane toward the schoolhouse, coat swinging, humming something under her breath—three notes of the warden's canticle, the ones that made candle flames stand still—and Mara stood at her gate with a pear in her scarred hand and watched her go, and felt the lie settle into place between them like a stone laid carefully in a wall.

It fit well. That was the awful part. It fit so well that from the outside you'd never know it was there.

Above the ridgeline, the clouds had finished organizing. The air tasted of copper and coming snow. One day left—one day of rehearsals and pear baskets and the ordinary miracle of Beatrix's voice drifting from the schoolhouse windows—and then the new moon, and the riders, and whatever Mara was going to become when the sky finally broke.

She ate the pear. It was very sweet.

She did not tell anyone about her hands.
