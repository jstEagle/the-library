## 14. The Secret Between Verses

Beatrix found the ledger by accident, which was how all the important things in her life seemed to arrive—candle-guttered prophecy books, warden's signets, and now a slim volume of accounts tucked behind a jar of pickled fiddleheads in Mara's pantry.

She hadn't meant to snoop. She'd come to drop off a loaf of honey bread and a pot of the chamomile salve Old Fenwick swore by for burns, because Mara's hands had been bandaged since the night she'd tried to call lightning out of a sky that no longer answered her, and Beatrix had decided that worrying silently was a form of cowardice dressed up as politeness.

Mara wasn't home. The note on the door said *Gone to check the north stones. Back by dusk. Help yourself to anything but the bees' opinion of you.*

So Beatrix let herself in, set the bread on the counter, and knocked over the jar of fiddleheads reaching for it.

The ledger fell out from behind it, spine cracked, pages soft as cloth from handling. She should have put it back. She told herself she would put it back. But it had fallen open, and the handwriting was Mara's, and the first line she saw was dated four years ago.

*Withdrawal: 3 measures. Ward-stone at the mill ford holding, barely. Hands numb for two days after. Worth it.*

Beatrix sat down on the kitchen stool without meaning to.

She turned pages. The entries went back further than four years. They went back ten years, to the first autumn after Mara had come to Vesper Hollow with nothing but a satchel and a name she didn't use anymore. And every few pages, in a hand that grew steadier over the years and then, in the last two years, began to tremble slightly at the ends of letters, the same kind of entry appeared.

*Withdrawal: 5 measures. East stone singing flat again.*

*Withdrawal: 7 measures. Winter is hard on the ward. So is winter on me. Do not tell anyone. Especially not her.*

*Especially not her* was underlined twice, and beneath it, in smaller writing, almost apologetic: *B. would try to stop me. B. would be right. Doing it anyway.*

The bread went cold on the counter. The salve sat unopened. Beatrix read the whole ledger from beginning to end, twice, while the light moved across the kitchen floor, and by the time she heard footsteps on the path outside, she had stopped being able to feel her own hands around the book's edges.

* * *

Mara came in smelling of pine resin and cold air, stamping her boots, mid-sentence about a cracked capstone, and stopped dead when she saw Beatrix sitting in her kitchen holding her most closely guarded secret like a hymnal.

"Oh," Mara said.

"Three measures," Beatrix said. Her voice came out very level, which surprised her, because inside she felt like a struck bell. "Four years ago. What's a measure, Mara? In plain words. No mage arithmetic."

Mara set down her satchel very carefully, the way people do when they're buying time. "It's—it's not a unit anyone uses anymore. It's roughly what a candle spends in an hour. Of a person. Of their—" She gestured vaguely at her own chest. "Vitality. Life. Whatever word doesn't make you put the book down and walk out."

"And seven measures in winter."

"The ward works hardest in winter. Cold makes the lattice brittle. It's physics, sort of. If physics were also a song and also a debt."

"Mara." Beatrix stood. She hadn't decided to stand; her body had simply concluded that this conversation required height. "Ten years. You've been doing this for ten years. Since before I knew you. Before *anyone* knew you. You've been pouring yourself into these stones a spoonful at a time and letting this entire valley believe your magic just—withered in exile. That it was taken from you."

"It *was* taken from me," Mara said, and there was the old bitterness, reflexive, automatic—and then it faltered, because they both heard it now for what it was. A story. A coat she'd worn so long she'd forgotten it was a coat.

"That's not true," Beatrix said quietly. "Is it. The order stripped your power when they exiled you—that's what you told me, that first night at the archive, before the book. That's what you've told everyone. But the ledger says otherwise. The ledger says you gave it up yourself, measure by measure, year by year, to keep a ward standing that nobody else even knew was failing." She turned a page with a finger that shook only a little. "*Do not tell anyone. Especially not her.* Who did you think you were fooling, writing 'her' like I wouldn't find out? You could have written my name. You wrote it everywhere else in here. Page forty-one. *B. laughed today and the east stone hummed along.*"

"That's—" Mara's ears went pink. "That's an observation of acoustic phenomena."

"You're impossible."

"I'm *careful*," Mara said, and then, because she was honest underneath everything, always, eventually: "I'm a coward. There. Is that what you want? I was afraid if I told you, you'd look at me the way everyone else does—the poor storm-caller, broken by her own arrogance—and I couldn't stand it coming from you. So I let you believe the tidy version. The exile version. Where the tragedy happened *to* me instead of me choosing it, one spoonful at a time, for a decade."

Beatrix looked at her—at the bandaged hands, the gray threading into her dark hair though she was barely thirty-five, the fine lines around her eyes that Beatrix had privately attributed to squinting at bees—and felt something crack open in her chest that was equal parts fury and grief and a tenderness so large it frightened her.

"Your hands," she said. "The backlash scars. From trying to rekindle your magic against the ward. Except it wasn't backlash, was it? The ward wasn't fighting you. It was *drinking* from you. Because you've been its cup for ten years and it knows where the water is."

Mara sat down slowly on the opposite stool, like a puppet with half its strings cut. "Yes," she said. "That's exactly it. I thought—I thought if I could get my storms back, I could defend the valley properly, fight the mages off, stop needing to bleed into the stones one candle-hour at a time. Instead the ward took what I offered and asked for more, and I got these." She held up her bandaged hands. "Poetic justice. My mother would have laughed."

"Don't joke."

"I'm not. She genuinely would have. She had a terrible sense of humor about self-destruction. It ran in the family." Mara looked up, and her eyes were wet and defiant at once, which was the most Mara expression in existence. "Say what you're going to say, Beatrix. I can see you loading it."

What Beatrix was going to say was a great many things, and they were all tangled together like the choir's harmonies on a bad rehearsal day. She said: "You lied to me."

"Yes."

"For months. While we—you and I—we've been *courting*, Mara. Badly, awkwardly, with bees interrupting, but courting. And the whole time you were spending your life a candle-hour at a time and letting me plan dates."

"Yes," Mara said again, softer. "And I'd do it again. That's the part you're allowed to hate me for."

"I don't hate you." Beatrix pressed the heels of her palms against her eyes. "That's the problem. I keep waiting for the anger to be the biggest thing in the room and it isn't. The biggest thing in the room is that some lonely woman moved into a valley that strips magic, ten years ago, and discovered the ward was dying, and nobody else could hear it—not the wardens' descendants, not the town council, nobody—and so she just... started paying its bills. Alone. In secret. For a decade. And the reason she wouldn't tell the one person who loves her is that she knew that person would try to make her stop."

"See?" Mara said, wretchedly. "Right. Exactly that."

"*Of course* I would try to make you stop! Mara, Tam collapsed from *one verse*. One! And you've been singing the canticle alone for ten years without any choir to share the weight, without anyone to catch you when it pulled too hard—how many times did you nearly end up like him? How many mornings did you wake up on the ground by some stone and walk home and bake bread and pretend?"

Mara was quiet for a long moment. Outside, a bee bumped lazily against the window glass, insistent and stupid and alive.

"Twice," she said finally. "Nearly, twice. The winter of the bad ice, and once last spring." She flexed her bandaged fingers. "I got better at rationing. I learned the ward's rhythms the way you learn a piece of music—where it swells, where it rests. I stopped thinking of it as losing magic and started thinking of it as... tithing. Ten percent of a life, spread thin, in exchange for six hundred people getting to keep theirs. It seemed like good arithmetic."

"It's terrible arithmetic. It's the arithmetic of someone who thinks she deserves less than other people."

"Well," Mara said, with a ghost of her dry smile. "I broke the law of my order to save a village. I testified against myself rather than lie. I have a well-documented history of believing rules apply to me more than to others. Why should self-worth be exempt?"

And Beatrix, furious and undone and helplessly fond, crossed the small kitchen, took Mara's bandaged hands in hers, and said, "You absolute idiot. You brave, stubborn, lying idiot. You don't get to decide alone anymore. Do you understand me? Not the ward, not the ledger, not the rationing. If the valley needs feeding, we feed it together. If it costs, we count the cost out loud, where I can hear it. You spent ten years being the only warden this valley had and never once used the word. Well, I'm using it now. You're a warden, Mara Whitlock. And wardens—" her voice caught, because she was suddenly thinking of her grandmother's trunk, of the letters, of the massacre, of bloodlines and inheritance— "wardens don't work alone. That's the entire point of them. That's why they had *choirs*."

Mara stared at her. Something moved behind her eyes—relief, terror, and the particular dazed look of a person who has been carrying a boulder for so long she'd forgotten it wasn't part of her spine.

"You're not leaving," she said. It wasn't a question, but it wasn't quite a statement either. It was a man testing ice.

"No," Beatrix said. "I'm furious, and I'm scared, and I'm going to be both of those things for a long time, probably, and I reserve the right to bring this up at every argument for the rest of our lives. But I'm not leaving. Loving someone who spends themselves to protect you turns out to be—" she searched for the word— "it turns out to be the whole job. Nobody warned me. I thought love was mostly picnics."

"Picnics are involved," Mara offered weakly.

"Occasionally. Interrupted by prophecies."

"The best ones are."

Beatrix laughed despite everything—a wet, cracked laugh—and Mara's face did something complicated and grateful, and for a moment they just stood in the little kitchen holding hands over a ledger full of quiet heroism, while the bees muttered at the window.

Then Beatrix's eye fell back on the open page, and the laugh died.

Because she was a choir teacher, and a teacher learns to read ahead. And there, in the last entry—one she'd skimmed past in her first reading, dated only three days ago, in that trembling hand—was a line she'd missed.

*Final withdrawal made. Everything left goes to the border stones tonight. After that, whatever happens at the new moon, the valley holds. Tell her after. Tell her gently. She'll grieve better if the valley is already safe.*

"Mara," Beatrix said, very calmly, in the voice she used on singers who were about to do something foolish during a solo. "What did you give them three days ago? What's *everything left*?"

Mara went still in a way that had nothing to do with calm.

"It's not what it sounds like."

"It sounds like you planned to spend your entire remaining life-force on the ward before the new moon and leave me a *note*."

"A considerate note!"

"Mara Whitlock." Beatrix gripped her hands tighter, feeling the bones beneath the bandages, feeling the pulse there—stubborn, ordinary, mortal, and for the moment, thank every listening stone, still beating. "Look at me. Whatever you gave three days ago, whatever you were planning to give tonight—you're going to teach me the withdrawal rite instead. Both of us. Together. Because I have a choir of thirty-two voices learning a canticle that runs on given strength, and a warden's signet in my grandmother's trunk, and I am *not* going to let you martyr yourself out of a valley I intend to grow old in. We are going to find another way to feed this thing. Even if I have to write the harmony myself."

For a long moment Mara didn't answer. Then, slowly, she reached up with her bandaged hand and touched Beatrix's cheek, as if checking whether she were real, or perhaps whether she herself were.

"There might be another way," she said at last, hoarse. "Something my mother wrote about. In the heart-chamber. I never understood it until—" She stopped. Swallowed. "The new moon is in five days. Wren comes back in five days. If there's another way, we have to find it before then."

"Then we'd better start tonight," Beatrix said. "Get your coat. And Mara—bring the ledger. Every secret you've kept gets read aloud from now on. Starting with whatever your mother wrote."

Outside, far beyond the valley's rim, thunder rolled—distant, patient, counting down.

But inside the kitchen, for one more moment, there was only the smell of honey bread and the sound of two people deciding, deliberately, to carry the same weight.
