## 15. Tam's Choice

Tam Pike had decided that being struck down by a song was, on balance, the most interesting thing that had ever happened to him.

It was certainly better than being struck by anything else. Old Man Ferrow's cart had nearly gotten him twice. A wasp had once gone down his collar during a sermon and caused what his mother still called The Incident. But no one in Vesper Hollow — not even the twins at the bakery, who claimed to have been present at every disaster since the flood — could say they'd been drained pale as milk by a hymn and lived to tell it with dramatic pauses.

The dramatic pauses were important. Tam had been practicing them for three days now from his bed in the choir room's back alcove, where Mistress Ives had insisted he stay until he stopped looking like "a candle guttering in a draft," which was exactly the sort of thing a choir teacher would say instead of *you scared me half to death*.

"You're supposed to be resting," said his mother, for the hundredth time, setting down a bowl of barley soup on the stool beside him.

"I *am* resting," Tam said. "Resting is mostly lying here thinking. Nobody can stop me from thinking."

"Your father managed it for thirty years."

"That's because Papa thinks about fences. There's nothing to think about in fences."

His mother smoothed his hair with the particular roughness she used when she was worried and pretending not to be. She'd been doing it a lot lately. Everyone had. Since the night the ward broke and the sky over the valley filled with siege-lanterns like fallen stars arranged by someone very angry, the whole of Vesper Hollow moved through its days quietly, the way you walk through a house where someone is sleeping upstairs.

Three days of truce, Mistress Warden Ives had won them. Three days. Tam had heard the grown-ups counting them like coins they were afraid to spend.

He thought about the dream instead.

He'd been thinking about the dream since the fever broke, turning it over the way you turn a river stone in your pocket, feeling for the shape of it. It didn't feel like a normal dream. Normal dreams came apart when you tried to hold them. This one got *heavier*.

In it, he was standing in a place made of singing stone — he knew it was stone, though there was nothing to see, only the way sound behaves differently against rock than against air — and the canticle was all around him, the one Mistress Ives had taught them, the warden's lost song. Except in the dream it kept going past where they always stopped.

They always stopped after the third verse. Mistress Ives had never written anything beyond it, and Tam knew why now: there wasn't anything beyond it on her page. The page ended. The song did not.

In the dream, the fourth verse rose out of the floor of the world like water rising in a cellar, slow and certain, and it was the most beautiful thing Tam had ever heard, and it was also — this was the part he hadn't told anyone, the part that sat in his chest like a swallowed hook — it was asking him something.

Songs didn't ask things. Songs were just songs. You sang them at weddings and funerals and when the bread came out right. But this one asked, in a voice that was somehow both his own voice and much older, whether he would give.

Not *give up*. Not *give away*. Just — give. Freely. The way rain gives itself to the ground. And Tam, who was ten years old and had never in his life been asked to decide anything larger than which jam to have, had understood in the dream exactly what it meant, the way you understand things in dreams without any of the reasons, whole and terrible, like knowing how to swim before you've ever touched water.

A life freely given. His life. The final verse needed a voice willing to end so the ward could begin again.

And in the dream, he had opened his mouth to say yes.

That was where it always ended. He woke with the word unspoken behind his teeth, tasting like lightning, and lay there listening to his own heart go until it seemed real again.

He hadn't told anyone. He'd told himself it was because they'd worry. But really — and Tam was honest with himself in the way of children who have been caught lying enough times to know the flavor of it — really it was because the yes was still in there somewhere, waiting, and he was afraid that if he said the verse aloud to anyone, the yes might come out with it.

* * *

On the second morning of the truce, Mara Whitlock brought him honeycomb.

This was an event worth recording. Tam had known Mara for years as the bee woman who wore storm-gray like armor and answered questions with either one word or a story, depending on some internal weather of her own. Since the night everything changed, she'd become something else — the exiled storm-caller, the traitor, the hero, depending on who in the Hollow was talking — but to Tam she was still mostly the person who let him hold the smoker that one time and then said, when he coughed for five minutes straight, "Now you know what bees think of us."

She sat on the edge of his cot, careful of his feet, and held out the comb wrapped in cloth like an offering.

"Healer says you're mending."

"I'm mending," Tam agreed. "I'm mending *slowly*, though, because if I mend too fast they'll make me go home, and home doesn't have a window facing the square."

"That's tactical thinking. Your mother will be proud." She watched him take a piece of comb, and her eyes did the thing they did sometimes, going distant, like she was reading weather far off. "You gave more than you should have, that day. The song took from you."

"I know."

"Do you?" Her voice was gentle, but underneath the gentleness was something sharp, attentive, the way she probably listened to storms. "Children are bad at knowing what's been taken from them. They're used to having less than everyone else. They think it's normal."

Tam considered this while the honey dissolved on his tongue. Then he said, "Do you ever hear it? In your sleep?"

Mara went very still.

"The song," Tam said. "Past where we stop."

The silence stretched long enough that Tam heard the choir rehearsing faintly in the main hall — Mistress Ives running them through the third verse again and again, stopping each time at the bar line where the page went blank, like a cart reaching the end of a bridge and backing up.

"Yes," Mara said at last. "I hear it."

"What does it ask *you*?"

"It doesn't ask me anything." Her mouth did something complicated. "I think it already knows my answer."

Tam looked at her hands. He'd noticed them before — the silvery scarring across her knuckles and palms, like frost burned into skin. He'd assumed it was from the bees.

"Mistress Whitlock," he said carefully, "in my dream—"

"Tam." She cut across him softly, and there was such weight in it that his mouth closed on its own. "Whatever you heard, whatever you saw — you keep it. You don't sing it. You don't hum it. You don't whistle it while you're feeding the chickens, which is your habit, I've seen you do it. Some knowledge is a door, and doors don't care how old you are when you open them."

"But what if the door is supposed to open?"

"Then someone older and stupider will open it first." She stood, ruffled his hair exactly the way his mother did, and paused at the alcove curtain. "You were very brave. Braver than most. That's why I'm telling you: brave people have to be *more* careful, not less. Everyone else runs away. We run toward."

She left. Tam ate the rest of the honeycomb and thought about doors.

* * *

It was Beatrix Ives herself he finally told, two days later, and it happened almost by accident, which is how true things usually get said.

She came at evening with the choir's practice sheets to organize, and stayed to sit with him while the light went gold and then gray through the alcove window. She'd started bringing her work to his bedside rather than leaving him alone, and Tam suspected it was less for his sake than hers — she had the look of a person who couldn't bear empty rooms anymore. The whole town had that look. The mill was still broken. People still flinched at thunder, and the thunder these days had an enemy's timing.

"How's the arm?" she asked.

"Stronger. Watch." He lifted the water pitcher above his head and held it there, wobbling slightly, until she gently pushed it back down.

"We'll call that a triumph and never speak of it again."

"Mistress Ives," Tam said, "can I ask you a question about the canticle?"

Her face did the thing it always did when the canticle came up — a tightening around the eyes, like a hand closing around something precious and fragile at the same time. "Ask."

"Why does it stop?"

She looked at the practice sheets in her lap. "Because that's all that was written. The warden's book ends after the third verse. Whoever wrote it left the ending blank."

"Or tore it out."

Beatrix's head came up sharply. "Why would you say that?"

Because in the dream the missing verse had edges, Tam wanted to say. Torn edges, ragged where it had been ripped free, and the rip was old, older than paper should be. Because the song didn't feel unfinished; it felt *amputated*. But what came out instead, because he was ten and tired and the fever had loosened the lid on everything:

"In my dream, I heard the rest."

The room went quiet in a way rooms rarely manage — not silent, but listening. Even the fire in the little stove seemed to lean in.

Beatrix set the papers down very slowly. "Tam."

"It comes every time I sleep. The same. The fourth verse. It's beautiful, Mistress Ives, it's the most beautiful—" His voice cracked and he hated it and pushed on. "It asks. It asks if you'll give. A life, freely given. That's the words. *A life freely given, a lamp unlit willingly.* It wants someone to say yes, and the ward wakes up, and everyone lives, and the someone—" He stopped, because the end of the sentence was the worst part and saying it would make it true-shaped in the air between them.

Beatrix had gone white. Whiter, Tam thought, than he'd been.

"And in the dream," she said, very quietly, "what do you answer?"

Tam looked at his hands. They were a child's hands, still, despite everything — knuckles scabbed from climbing, a healing blister from bowing his fiddle badly.

"I wake up before I say it," he whispered. "But I want to say it. That's the bad part. I *want* to. Isn't that strange? It's like when you stand on the bridge rail and part of you looks at the water. I'm not sad, I promise, I love everything, I love jam and my fiddle and the chickens. It's just that the song is so *sure*, and it makes me sure too, for a minute, and then morning comes and I'm glad to be a coward about it."

Beatrix crossed the space between them and pulled him into her arms, hard, and Tam felt her shaking, which confused him, because he was the injured one, technically.

"Listen to me." She held him at arm's length, and her eyes were wet and fierce, and she was suddenly not his choir teacher at all but something older — a warden, whatever that meant, wearing an ordinary woman's cardigan. "You will never sing that verse. Not in the dream, not awake, not humming, not whistling at the chickens. Not if the ward falls. Not if the sky itself comes down. Do you hear me? If the last note of the world depends on it, it can wait for someone else, because I am *forbidding* you, and I am the warden, and forbidding is apparently one of the few things I'm allowed to be good at."

"But what if nobody else—"

"*Nobody*," Beatrix said, "is going to be a child. Whatever the song wants, it will have to ask adults, who at least have the decency to make their own terrible decisions. You're a singer, Tam Pike, and singers are how the valley survives — but you're a child first, and any future that costs a ten-year-old everything isn't a future, it's just a slower kind of defeat. Promise me."

Tam promised. He felt the promise settle over him like a blanket, and under it, annoyingly, the yes shifted but didn't leave.

Beatrix stayed until he fell asleep. He surfaced once, near midnight, and saw her still sitting there in the dark with the torn-edge feeling all around her, staring at nothing, mouthing words to herself — counting something, or arguing with something, or maybe just learning a verse she hoped she'd never have to teach.

* * *

But dreams don't honor promises. That was the trouble with them. You could forbid a boy all you liked in the daylight, and the night would simply wait, patient as water, and put the question to him again.

That night Tam dreamed of the singing stone, and the fourth verse rose like a tide, and the voice that was his own and older asked, gentle as rain: *will you give?*

And this time — because he was Tam Pike, who ran toward things, who had been told twice in one week by two different women to be careful, which in his experience meant he was standing exactly where something important was happening — this time, before waking, he did not answer yes.

He answered, "Ask my mistress. She sings better than me anyway."

The song went silent. The whole dream went silent, stone and tide and all, as though it had never occurred to the ancient thing beneath the valley that it could be refused, redirected, *negotiated with.*

Then, far off, in the dark below the dark, something began — very slowly, very attentively — to listen for Beatrix Ives.

Tam woke at dawn with the taste of lightning in his mouth and the absolute certainty that he had just done something enormous, and no idea whether it was the best or the worst thing he would ever do.
