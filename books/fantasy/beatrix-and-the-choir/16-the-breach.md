## 16. The Breach

The new moon came three days early.

That was the first thing Mara understood, standing in Beatrix's kitchen with a cup of tea going cold in her scarred hands: the storm-mages had never intended to honor the deadline at all. Wren's parley had been a courtesy extended to a dead woman, and courtesies, in Mara's experience, were the things people gave you right before they took everything else.

She knew it the way she'd once known weather—through her skin, through the ache in her bones where her magic used to live. The ward had been humming for two days, a low note beneath the valley floor that only she seemed to feel, like a violin string wound too tight. And tonight the note had changed pitch.

"Beatrix," she said quietly.

Beatrix was at the stove, reheating the tea she'd already reheat­ed once, humming the canticle under her breath because she couldn't seem to stop lately—it leaked out of her the way tunes leak out of anyone who teaches music for a living. She turned. "You've got your look."

"What look?"

"The one where you're about to ruin dinner."

"I'm about to ruin more than dinner." Mara set down the cup. "They're coming tonight. The ward's note just went sharp. It does that when something pushes against it."

Beatrix looked at her for a long moment, and Mara watched her do the thing she did—the ordinary miracle of it—where she took terrible news and folded it into something she could carry. She untied her apron. She hung it on its hook. She said, "Then we'd better wake the choir."

* * *

By midnight the valley knew. Word traveled the way word always traveled in Vesper Hollow: Mrs. Aldous told the baker, the baker told everyone who bought bread, and by the time the moonless dark settled over the ridgeline, every soul in the valley had heard *storm-mages at the border* whispered in tones usually reserved for deaths and weddings.

Mara stood at the eastern edge of town, near the old mill, and watched the sky above the ward-line do something sky should not do.

It bulged.

There was no better word for it. The stars beyond the ridge seemed to press inward, as though the night itself were a membrane and someone on the far side was pushing a fist against it. Lightning moved inside that pressure—not striking, not yet, but crawling along the underside of the clouds like veins of fire behind skin. The storm-mages had brought their whole arsenal to bear. She could feel them even without her magic: dozens of wills, braided together, hammering on the valley's door.

"They can't get through," said Tam's father, Josiah, beside her. He had a woodcutter's axe in his hands, which would have been touching if it weren't so useless. "Right? The ward held a hundred years."

"The ward is tired," Mara said. "And they are not."

Below them, along the millrace, lanterns bobbed—the evacuation line, townsfolk moving toward the heart of the valley where the stone walls were thickest. Beatrix was somewhere back there with the choir, getting the children underground into the cellar of the meetinghouse, singing to them softly to keep them calm. Mara had kissed her an hour ago, hard and brief, and neither of them had said anything foolish or brave. They'd agreed on that. No declarations at doorways. Save it for after.

The ward groaned.

Not a sound, exactly—a feeling, transmitted up through boot-soles and teeth. Mara had spent ten years pretending she couldn't feel the ward, and now, with her magic spent feeding it, she felt it more intimately than ever. She felt it like an old dog trying to stand.

"Get back from the line," she told Josiah. "Everyone back. If it breaks, it'll break outward too—the backlash—"

The sky split.

There was a sound like the world's largest bell being struck with the world's largest hammer, and then a sound like nothing at all, which was worse, because it was the sound of a hundred years of protection simply ceasing. The pressure above the ridge popped like a soap bubble. Starlight poured through the gap, cold and indifferent, and through the starlight came the lightning.

Mara was running before her mind caught up with her body.

* * *

The mill was the nearest building to the breach, because of course it was. The universe had a sense of humor like a bruise—always finding the same spot.

The first bolt hit the millwheel and turned it briefly into a wheel of light, spokes of white fire spinning, and then the roof caught, and then the eastern wall came down in a single exhalation of dust and timber, and Mara ran into all of it with nothing but her hands and ten years of practice at being useless in exactly the ways that mattered least.

"THE MILL!" she screamed over her shoulder. "ANYONE WHO CAN CARRY A BUCKET!"

The miller's family lived above the grinding floor. She knew this. She bought their flour every week; she'd complimented the miller's wife's roses last spring; she knew there were four of them and that the youngest, a girl named Pip who was maybe nine, liked to sit on the sill and dangle her feet over the race.

The stairs were gone. The stairs were a smear of charcoal and splinters against the far wall.

Mara stood in the burning doorway of the grinding floor and did the arithmetic she'd been doing her whole life: what she had, versus what was needed. What she had was no magic, no strength beyond a beekeeper's shoulders, and a body that had been quietly hemorrhaging itself into a failing ward for a decade. What was needed was a miracle.

"Hello?" Her voice cracked against the smoke. "Can anyone hear me? Make noise! Knock! Sing, I don't care—"

Something knocked. Twice. Above her and to the left, where the loft room still held, its floor sagging but intact, wedged between surviving beams like a bird's nest in a broken tree.

"I'm coming up!" she called, and then stood there for one full second being honest with herself: there was no way up. The staircase was ash. The wall was vertical. She was forty-one years old and her most impressive physical feat of the past decade had been relocating an uncooperative hive.

She found the water trough. She found the old loading ladder, half-burnt, leaning against the outer wall. She climbed it with her palms screaming—her scarred palms, the ones Beatrix didn't know about, the ones that had been quietly scalding themselves against ward-stone for years—and hauled herself onto the loading platform as the second story exhaled another gout of flame.

The window of the loft room glowed orange. Through it, small and smudged, a face.

"Pip!" Mara shouted. "Pip, listen to me! Can you open the window?"

"It's stuck!" Small voice, terrified, furious—the fury was good, Mara thought wildly, fury keeps children alive—"The frame's warped, I can't—"

"Step back from the glass."

Mara took hold of the nearest thing—part of the burnt loading crane, an iron arm as long as her leg—and swung it at the window like a woman who had spent ten years lifting beehives and had opinions about it. The glass burst inward. Smoke rolled out around the girl's face.

"Now listen carefully. There's a beam below your window. Not the burning one—the thick one, to the left. I need you to climb out onto it and I need you to not look down."

"I'm already looking down!"

"Then look down *confidently.*"

God help her, the girl laughed. Somewhere in the smoke and the roar of the collapsing mill, a nine-year-old laughed, and Mara thought: *this. This is the whole job. This is what I broke my oath for, this exact stupid beautiful laugh.*

Pip climbed out. Her nightgown was singed at the hem. She got one bare foot on the beam, wobbled, and Mara reached across the gap and caught her wrist, and for one suspended moment they hung there together—Mara braced against the crane-mount, the girl dangling over thirty feet of burning dark, both of them held up by nothing but grip and stubbornness.

Then the beam cracked.

Mara didn't think. Later, reviewing it, she would find no thought anywhere in the memory—only motion. She swung Pip inward, wrapped both arms around her, and jumped for the loading platform as the loft floor let go behind them. They landed hard. Something in Mara's shoulder made a sound she chose not to hear. Splinters took her forearm. The platform shuddered, held.

They lay there gasping while the mill burned above them like a struck match.

"You're the bee lady," Pip said finally, into Mara's collarbone.

"I'm the bee lady."

"My mum says you talk to your bees."

"I do. They don't listen either."

Another laugh, wetter this time. Mara got them both upright and down the ladder, and handed the girl off to Josiah, whose axe had fortunately found a better use as a tool for prying open the water cistern. Then Mara turned around and went back toward the smoke, because the knocking hadn't stopped.

* * *

She pulled the miller out through the race-gate at the bottom of the wheel-pit, coughing and half-drowned, because the man had tried to go up after his daughter himself and gotten cut off by fire. She dragged the miller's wife from under a fallen joist with Josiah and two others, four hands apiece, counting—one, and two, and lift. By the time the roof fell in entirely, sending up a column of sparks visible from every farm in the valley, everyone who had been in the mill was out of it, lined up along the race in blankets, coughing, alive.

Mara stood among them, soaked to the ribs, bleeding from a dozen shallow cuts, her left arm hanging at an angle she was ignoring, and felt absolutely none of the triumph she'd imagined such a moment might carry.

This was the thing no ballad ever mentioned, she thought. Courage without power wasn't glorious. It was wet and smoky and it hurt, and afterward you didn't feel like a hero—you felt like a bucket that had been used to bail a sinking ship, grateful and exhausted and aware that the ocean was still there.

Because the ocean was still there. Beyond the burning mill, beyond the town, the breach in the ward glowed like a wound in the night, and through it, figures were coming.

* * *

They came down off the ridge in wedge formation, robed in storm-grey, and the lightning walked ahead of them like a leashed animal. But here was the thing—the thing Mara clung to as she watched them descend, her heart doing something complicated in her chest:

They came slowly.

They came slowly because the ward was still fighting. Broken, breached, wounded nearly to death—but the valley's foundations were sung into being, and Beatrix's choir had been teaching the canticle for weeks now, and even scattered, even underground, even frightened, the singers of Vesper Hollow were holding the note. Mara could feel it rising faintly from the meetinghouse cellars, a thread of harmony threading through the chaos, and wherever it touched, the storm-mages' lightning guttered and bent aside.

They were winning ground. Inch by inch, at cost, but winning it. The vanguard reached the orchard road and stalled there, hammering against a resistance they could see but not understand—an invisible wall that flared whenever a hundred voices found the third interval.

"We hold the line at the square," Josiah said beside her, and around him other men and women said versions of the same thing, farmers with billhooks and bakers with nothing but their bodies and their refusal, and Mara looked at these people she had lived among for ten years, these people who had taken in a disgraced storm-caller and asked no questions except whether she liked honey cake, and she felt something crack open in her chest that had nothing to do with her ribs.

They weren't fighting because they were wardens. Most of them weren't anything but what they'd always been. They were fighting because it was home, and home was worth staying for.

"Mara!" A boy sprinting up the lane, one of the archive apprentices, breathless. "They're asking for you—at the square! Beatrix says—" he gulped air— "she says Wren Calloway's been taken. Caught in the fighting at the orchard road when the line stalled. The militia's got her trussed in the meetinghouse, and half the town wants to—well." He swallowed. "Half the town has opinions."

Mara closed her eyes. Wren. Her friend, her betrayer, the woman whose testimony had ended her life and whose conscience had, apparently, been eating her alive ever since. Trussed in the meetinghouse while the war she'd marched with burned the valley's edge.

"And Beatrix?"

"She says come quick. Says—" the boy hesitated, embarrassed— "says you'll want to hear what Wren's saying. Says it's about why you were really exiled."

The fires roared behind her. The choir sang beneath her feet. Somewhere in the smoke-wrecked middle of it all, an answer ten years overdue sat tied to a chair in the meetinghouse, waiting.

Mara Whitlock picked up her ruined, courageous, powerless body and ran.
