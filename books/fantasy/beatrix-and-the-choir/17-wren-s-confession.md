## 17. Wren's Confession

The cellar of the Hollow's meetinghouse had been, until three days ago, a place where the town stored jam. Now it was where they kept Wren Calloway, and the jam, and she found the juxtaposition humiliating in a way that defeat itself had not quite managed.

She sat on an upturned barrel because they had taken her chair. Her wrists were bound with—she had checked, twice, out of professional respect—ordinary hemp rope. No warding knots, no suppression sigils. The people of Vesper Hollow had simply tied her up like a runaway goat and gone back to their lives. Somewhere above her, she could hear the meetinghouse clock ticking, and beneath that, faintly, a choir rehearsing. The melody was one she didn't know, and it made her teeth ache, and she suspected it was supposed to.

The door at the top of the stairs opened. Light spilled down, along with the smell of bread, and then Mara Whitlock came down the steps with a lantern and a plate of said bread, and Wren's chest did the thing it always did, the old familiar lurch, ten years out of practice and still perfectly on pitch.

"You're supposed to be interrogating me," Wren said. "Not feeding me."

"I'm doing both." Mara set the plate on a second barrel, out of Wren's reach, and sat on a third. "The bread is leverage."

"It's very good leverage."

"I know. Beatrix baked it." Mara said the name the way other people said *home*. "She insisted. She said prisoners interrogated on an empty stomach lie twice as much."

"She sounds sensible."

"She's the best person I've ever met, and if you hurt her, Wren, I will find a way to do it without magic, and it will be slow."

"Understood." Wren meant it. She'd seen Mara's hands—had seen the scarring when they'd dragged her past the mill wreckage, the burn-patterns of backlash, the fingers that no longer closed all the way. Mara Whitlock without magic was still the most frightening person Wren had ever known, because Mara had never needed the lightning to be dangerous. She'd only ever needed a reason.

The clock ticked. The choir above sang its aching, tooth-hurting melody. Wren looked at her old friend—her *former* friend, her *accused-and-abandoned* friend—and found, to her complete lack of surprise, that she was tired of lying.

"You testified against me," Mara said. It wasn't a question. It had never been a question. It was the wall between them, ten years high, and Mara had walked up to it and put her hand on it the way she did everything: directly, without flinching.

"I did."

"Say the rest of it."

"You first," Wren said. "Tell me what you think happened. I want to know how close you are."

Mara's jaw tightened. "I think you were ambitious. I think the archmage offered you my seat on the Circle, or my standing, or whatever it is ambitious people want, and you took it, and you told the tribunal I broke the law at Aldenmere because it was true and convenient. I think you slept fine afterward."

Wren nodded slowly. "That's a good story. It's even mostly true. I was ambitious. I did want your standing. I did sleep fine, for about a year, and then I stopped sleeping at all." She leaned forward as far as the rope allowed. "But you're missing the part that matters, Mara. The part where none of it was my idea."

* * *

She told it in order, because Wren had always been a creature of order, and because if she told it out of order she would lose her nerve somewhere in the middle.

"You remember the tribunal," she said. "You remember who called for it. Not the village elders. Not the families whose granaries you saved. The archmage. Corvess. He filed the charges himself, which everyone thought was *diligence*. The great man, personally attending to a junior storm-caller who flooded a levy to save a village." She watched Mara's face. "You never wondered why the most powerful mage in the order cared about one small act of mercy?"

"I assumed it was politics."

"It was politics. Just not yours." Wren took a breath. "Mara. What's your mother's name?"

The silence that followed was long enough for the choir above to finish a verse.

"Sereth," Mara said finally, and her voice had gone careful, the way it did when she was holding something heavy. "Sereth Whitlock. She died when I was nine. Why?"

"What did she tell you she did? Before she had you?"

"She was a wanderer. A mapmaker. She—" Mara stopped. Wren watched her stop, watched the sentence die in her mouth as the shape of it changed. "She never talked about the order. I thought it was because she'd left. I thought—" Mara's hands, scarred and half-closed, turned over in her lap. "Wren. What did you find?"

"I found the archive," Wren said. "After your exile, Corvess assigned me to catalog the sealed records. A punishment posting dressed up as an honor, or the other way around, I never decided which. And in the sealed records there is a founding charter for the Vesper Hollow ward. Signed by nine names. The nine wardens who built it, a hundred and twenty years ago." She held Mara's eyes. "One of the names is Whitlock."

The lantern flame didn't flicker. The choir above held a note that seemed to hold itself.

"The wardens were hunted," Wren went on, quietly now. "You know that much, everyone knows that much—the order's official history says the wardens were *corrupted*, that the ward was a *prison built by traitors*, that the purge was *necessary*. That's what they teach the novices. I taught it. I believed it." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "And then I read the actual correspondence, Mara. The wardens didn't build the ward to imprison anyone. They built it as a *refuge*. A valley where magic could not follow, for people the order had broken—exiles, heretics, anyone who ran. And the order's leadership of that era couldn't allow it, because a valley that mages can't enter is a valley where the order's enemies can't be reached. So they massacred them. All nine bloodlines. And they rewrote the history, and they kept the ward, because a ward that strong is useful even if you can't walk through it. A wall you can't open is still a wall."

"Sereth," Mara said. Just the name.

"Sereth Whitlock was the last of her line. The wardens' blood didn't die in the purge—some of it scattered, hid, forgot. Corvess found the charter and understood what the order's founders had understood: that a living warden heir could *claim* the ward. Open it. Unmake it. Or—" Wren's mouth was dry. "Or bind it. The ward answers its makers, Mara. It always has. And Corvess has spent thirty years positioning himself to be the one who controls it when it wakes. He needs the valley. He needs the ward's heart. And there was exactly one person alive whose blood could stand in his way."

"No," Mara said. It wasn't denial, not exactly. It was the sound of a person watching a wall of years fall down all at once and finding the landscape behind it unrecognizable. "She was a mapmaker. She was *nobody*. She ran from everything—she taught me to run from everything. *Never be found*, she said. *Never be remarkable.*" Mara's voice cracked on the last word. "She wasn't hiding from the order. She was hiding from *him*. And I— I spent ten years thinking I was exiled for saving a village, and the truth is I was exiled for *existing*—"

"You were exiled for existing," Wren said. "Yes. The charge was a pretext. The tribunal was theater. Corvess chose me to testify because we were close, because my word against yours would hurt the most, and because—" she made herself say it, made herself look at Mara while she said it, "—because I wanted what he offered, and he knew it, and he was right. Reinstatement. Standing. A seat on the Circle within five years. All of it contingent on one testimony. I told them you'd broken the law at Aldenmere. I told them you were dangerous. I told them you'd do it again." Her voice finally failed her, and she let it. "Every word was technically true and every word was a knife, and I handed them over with both hands, and I have not slept properly in ten years, and I would like to tell you that I've regretted it every single day but the truth is worse—I *didn't*, not at first, not for a long time, and by the time I did it was too late to matter."

* * *

Mara didn't speak for a while. She got up, eventually, and moved the plate of bread within Wren's reach, which was either forgiveness or an insult, and Wren genuinely could not tell which, and suspected Mara couldn't either.

"You could have told me," Mara said at last. "Any time in ten years. A letter. A rumor. A *whisper*."

"To what end? You were safe. That was the one good thing I'd done—the exile worked, Mara. Corvess couldn't reach you inside the ward. As long as you stayed here, you were the one thing in the world he couldn't touch, because he couldn't come in after you. Telling you the truth would only have given you a reason to leave." Wren looked down at the bread and didn't eat it. "And then the prophecy book surfaced. And the ward started failing. And Corvess stopped pretending."

"The scouts on the ridgeline. The siege. The breach." Mara counted them off. "He's not here to punish me. He's here to claim the ward before anyone else can wake it."

"He's here to claim it *through* you, if he can. You're more useful to him alive and bound than dead. A Whitlock heir under his thumb is a key. A Whitlock heir dead is just a closed door." Wren finally looked up. "But Beatrix—Ives, the choir teacher—she's not a key, Mara, she's a *threat*. Warden blood, warden training she doesn't even know she has, and a voice that the ward is apparently answering like a long-lost daughter. Corvess doesn't leave threats standing. Whatever he offered at the parley, whatever terms are on the table, understand that the terms for her were never terms. They were a delay."

Mara was quiet. Above them the choir had stopped, and in the new silence Wren could hear the wind moving along the valley's rim, and under the wind, faint and getting fainter, the hum of the ward itself—the sound she'd been hearing in her sleep for weeks now, like a struck bell that wouldn't finish dying.

"Why are you telling me this?" Mara asked. "You're still his commander. This is treason."

"I was captured in a battle he started to cover his own ambition, in a valley he lied about for a century, defending a purge he ordered against people who only ever wanted to be left alone." Wren smiled, and it was a tired, crooked thing. "Mara, I have been treasonous for years. I've only just now picked a side."

"And which side is that?"

"Yours. Obviously. It was always going to be yours. I just took the scenic route." She flexed her bound hands. "Now untie me, or feed me more of that bread, or ideally both, because I have about a hundred more things to tell you and most of them are worse."

Mara didn't move. "One more question first."

"Ask."

"Sereth. My mother. Is there anything in the records about what happened to her? After she ran?"

Wren had known the question was coming. She'd had three days in a jam cellar to rehearse her answer, and she still wasn't ready, and she said it anyway, because Mara deserved the whole weight of it.

"There's a correspondence file," she said. "Corvess to his predecessors, going back thirty years. He never stopped looking for her. And the last letter in the file is dated the year you were born. It says the trail had gone cold—*the Whitlock woman has vanished beyond recovery; the bloodline is presumed ended*." She paused. "Mara, he didn't know about you. For thirty years, he didn't know you existed. You weren't exiled for being a Whitlock. You were exiled for being a storm-caller who broke the law—and it was only *afterward*, when he pulled your family records to build the case, that he found out what he'd actually caught."

Mara sat down slowly on the barrel.

"He stumbled into it," she said. "He exiled me by accident."

"He exiled you by accident," Wren agreed. "And then he spent ten years thanking his luck for it, because a Whitlock heir locked outside the ward, alive, controllable—that's better than any plan he could have made. You were never his enemy, Mara. You were his *inventory*." She held out her bound wrists. "And now he's coming to collect. So. Untie me, and let's talk about the heart-chamber, because I've read his campaign orders, and there's something under the valley he wants more than the ward itself—and I don't think you know what it is."

Mara stood. For a long moment she just looked at Wren—ten years of wall and rope and bread-leverage between them—and then she drew a small knife from her belt and cut the hemp in one stroke.

"Talk fast," she said. "The choir's about to learn the second verse, and I have a feeling we're running out of quiet."
