## 18. The Last Verse

The archive smelled of candle smoke and burnt courage.

Beatrix stood in the wreckage of the reading room—shelves toppled, the great window cracked into a spiderweb that let in the gray light of a sky still bruised from battle—and tried to think like a librarian instead of a woman whose heart had been cracked open two nights ago and had not yet learned how to close again.

*It never demanded a sacrifice.*

She had said it aloud to the empty room three times now, as if repetition could make it true. The fourth time, she said it to Tam, who sat on an overturned crate with his arm in a sling and a fever-bright look in his eyes that she did not entirely trust.

"You're sure," he said. Not a question. A boy who had carried a terrible verse in his dreams for a week had earned the right to state things rather than ask them.

"I'm sure I *hope* it," Beatrix said. "Hope and certainty are cousins, but they don't share a bed."

Tam considered this with the gravity of a twelve-year-old who had recently been drained nearly lifeless by a song. "Grandda says something like that about weather."

"Your grandda is a wise man." She handed him the last intact candle stub. "Hold this where I can see."

The prophecy book lay open on the trestle table before her—or what remained of it. When the words had lifted off the final page and sunk into their skin, the book had not simply gone blank. It had gone *quiet*, the way a room goes quiet when someone important has just left it. But quiet was not empty. Beatrix had spent ten years teaching children that a rest in music was not silence; it was sound held in suspension, waiting its turn.

She had spent last night proving the same was true of paper.

Holding each page near a flame—not touching, never touching—she had coaxed the old ink back like breath returning to glass. Faint brown ghosts of letters, legible only at certain angles, in certain light. She had copied every recoverable word onto butcher's paper, spread the sheets across the floor of the archive, and knelt among them like a woman kneeling in a garden she'd only seen in winter.

Now, by Tam's candlelight, she read the whole of it for the first time as one thing.

*When the ward grows thin as morning mist,* the opening verse ran, *a daughter of the builders shall stand where her grandmothers fell, and a storm that walks as a woman shall kneel beside her.*

"Kneel," Beatrix murmured. "Not burn. Not bleed. Kneel."

*And the valley shall ask a price,* said the third verse—the one Wren's archmage had surely read, the one that had sent armies marching. *A life freely given, poured into stone, until nothing remains but the giving.*

That was the verse everyone had heard. That was the verse that had made Mara go white as chalk when Beatrix had first read it aloud, made her say *we have to leave the valley, we have to leave tonight*, made her spend years—God, *years*—feeding herself quietly into the failing ward like a woman bailing out a boat with her own hands.

But there were other verses. Verses no one had bothered to read closely, because fear is a loud reader and skips ahead.

Beatrix found the seventh verse near the bottom of the fifth sheet, half-eaten by water damage, and her hands began to shake so badly that Tam had to hold the candle steady for her.

*Yet mark the old covenant, older than the ward:* it read. *No single voice may bind a storm. No single storm may hold a song. What was joined must be joined again—two streams into one river, given together, kept together, or the giving is only dying and the ward will know the difference.*

"It's a duet," Beatrix whispered.

"A what?"

"A duet, Tam. It was always a duet." She laughed—a strange, cracked sound, half sob, half the laugh she used when a nervous soprano finally hit the high note. "One person pouring themselves into the ward alone just *dies*. That's all sacrifice ever was. Dying with good posture. But two people—" She pressed her hand flat against the page. "Two people bound together, sharing the cost, holding each other up inside the giving. The ward doesn't want a corpse. It wants a *harmony*."

Tam looked at her for a long moment. Then he said, with the devastating practicality of children, "You have to tell her."

"I know."

"She thinks you hate her."

"I don't hate her."

"She doesn't know that either."

Beatrix closed her eyes. Two nights ago she had stood in the rain outside the mill ruins and shouted at Mara Whitlock—shouted words she could not take back, *you let me believe you were ordinary, you let me fall in love with someone who didn't exist*—and Mara had taken every word like weather takes stones, standing in it, letting it break over her, saying only, *I'm sorry. You're right. I'm sorry.* And then she had walked away into the dark to pull survivors out of the wreckage, because that was what Mara did: she absorbed the storm and called it penance.

"No," Beatrix said, opening her eyes. "She knows I love her. That's why it hurt. You can't betray someone you don't love, Tam. Betrayal is just love with nowhere to go."

He wrinkled his nose. "That's very romantic and also gross."

"Both things are often true at once. Hold the candle higher."

* * *

She found Mara at the eastern pasture, where the breached border still smoldered along the ridgeline like a seam of fire in a gray coat. The storm-mages' siege-lanterns burned out there beyond the broken ward, a constellation of hostile stars, and between the lanterns and the town the valley's people had built barricades out of hay wagons, mill timbers, and one remarkably stubborn stone wall that had belonged to the Hendersons since before anyone could remember.

Mara stood at the edge of the pasture with her scarred hands wrapped around a fence post, watching the enemy lights, and she did not turn around when Beatrix's boots crunched the frost behind her. But her shoulders changed. Beatrix knew her well enough now to read her like weather: the small settling, the way a barometer falls before rain.

"The choir sang at dawn," Mara said quietly. "I could hear it from here. Even past the breach."

"We sang the second verse of the canticle. The strengthening one." Beatrix came to stand beside her, close enough that their sleeves touched. "Mrs. Aldous's candles stood perfectly straight the whole time. She's telling everyone it's a sign of divine favor. I haven't the heart to tell her it's resonance."

"Don't tell her. She'll be insufferable either way, but this way she's happy."

Despite everything—despite the shouting in the rain, despite the scars on Mara's hands that Beatrix had only just been allowed to see, despite the years of secret spending laid bare between them like an unpaid debt—despite all of it, Beatrix felt her mouth twitch. This was the trouble with loving someone. Even mid-ruin, they could make you laugh, and then you had to live with the laughing.

They watched the lanterns together. Somewhere out there, the archmage was waiting for the new moon's darkness to finish falling.

"Mara." Her own name in her own mouth felt too small. She started again. "I found the rest of the prophecy."

Mara went very still. "The pages were blanked."

"Ink isn't gone. It's sleeping. Fire wakes it." Beatrix pulled the folded butcher's sheets from her coat. "I've spent two days copying everything the flame would give back. And Mara—the part everyone reads, the price, the life freely given—it's real. But it's not the whole covenant. There's a seventh verse. An older one. Listen."

She read it aloud. She read it twice, because the first time her voice shook on *two streams into one river*, and Mara Whitlock deserved to hear it steady.

When she finished, the silence between them was long enough to grow things in.

"That's why my mother's name is carved in the heart-chamber," Mara said at last, slowly, the way a person feels their way along a wall in the dark. "Whitlocks were wardens. Wardens bound the ward in pairs. Storm and song. My mother would have known—I think maybe she *did* know, and that's why the archmage wanted our line outside the order, scattered, powerless—" Her breath caught. "And I spent ten years feeding the ward alone. Alone. Like trying to sing a duet with only one throat."

"And it took and took and gave nothing back, because it couldn't accept a single voice. It wasn't refusing you, Mara. It was *incomplete* without you. Both of you." Beatrix turned to face her fully. "The prophecy never asked one of us to die so the other could live. It asked both of us to give *together*. Which means—" Her throat tightened. "Which means every year you spent alone in that heart-chamber, every drop, it was never going to be enough, and it was never supposed to be yours to carry."

Mara's jaw worked. Frost crept unconsciously from her fingers across the fence post, little ferns of white. "I thought I was protecting you."

"You were. Badly. Beautifully. Badly." Beatrix reached out and took one scarred hand—the left one, where the lightning-backlash had written its silver script across the palm. Mara flinched, then let her. "I'm still angry. I want to be honest about that. I'm angry about the years, and the lying, and the way you decided for both of us what I could bear. But I read a book last night that says the ward itself will not accept a gift given alone, and I thought—well. I thought the universe has finally caught up to something I figured out on our first date."

"What's that?"

"That I don't want a hero. I want a partner. Someone who tells me when the boat is leaking so we can bail *together*." She laced her fingers through Mara's scarred ones. "So. Here is my proposal, storm-caller. We go down into your mother's binding circle, and we finish this the way it was always meant to be finished. Together. And if the price is everything, then it's everything *split two ways*, which any child in my choir can tell you is a much smaller number."

Mara looked at their joined hands. Then up at Beatrix. Her eyes were wet and furious about being wet.

"You understand what you're offering," she said hoarsely. "If the seventh verse is wrong—if it's wishful thinking, if the ward really does take everything—"

"Then we lose everything together, which is still better than you losing everything alone." Beatrix lifted Mara's hand and pressed her lips to the scarred palm. "Besides. I have evidence the duet works."

"Evidence?"

"Ten years of a failing ward, fed by one exhausted woman, and yet Vesper Hollow still stands. Still shelters. Still strips magic from everyone who enters—including, I might add, a certain exiled storm-caller who should by rights have been defenseless against every danger in that valley, and instead survived a decade of beekeeping." Beatrix smiled. "The ward has been holding you gently this whole time, Mara. Even half-starved, even incomplete, it recognized what you were trying to do and met you halfway. Imagine what it does when we actually finish the sentence."

For the first time in days—maybe for the first time since the night the prophecy named them—Mara laughed. It was a small laugh, cracked and disbelieving, but it was real, and it thawed something in the air between them that no fire could have reached.

"The bees," Mara said. "You're citing the bees as theological evidence."

"I cite my sources."

"You're ridiculous."

"I'm a choir teacher. Ridiculous is a professional requirement." Beatrix sobered, folding the prophecy sheets and pressing them into Mara's free hand. "There's more. Getting to the heart-chamber means crossing the breach. The border is shattered on the eastern ridge—that's the fastest way down to the old warden road, and the warden road runs straight to the chamber entrance. But the breach is also exactly where the archmage wants us. He'll expect us to run for it."

"So we don't run."

"So we don't run." Beatrix nodded toward the town behind them, where the bells of the meetinghouse had begun to ring—not alarm, now, but assembly. "Wren gave us until the new moon. The new moon is tomorrow night. Which gives us one day to do the hardest thing I've ever asked a choir to do."

"Which is?"

"Sing the whole canticle. All seven verses. In harmony. While marching." Beatrix watched Mara's eyebrows climb. "The ward was built on sung resonance. Every step the choir takes singing the canticle re-knits the border behind them—we won't sneak through the breach, we'll *heal* through it, a moving corridor of warded ground, straight down the warden road. And above us—" she squeezed Mara's hand, "—the storm you're going to call. Because you *will* be able to call it, Mara. Not alone. With me. With all of us. The prophecy says the storm walks as a woman, but it never says she walks by herself."

Mara stared at her. Beyond the ridge, the siege-lanterns flickered. Somewhere in the town, Mrs. Aldous was almost certainly already arguing with someone about alto parts.

"One day," Mara said. "To teach a village choir a seven-verse warden's canticle, march them through a war zone, and duel an archmage with harmony."

"When you say it like that it sounds impossible."

"How else would I say it?"

Beatrix grinned, fierce and terrified and more alive than she had ever been in her quiet, careful, pre-prophecy life. "Exactly like that. Now come on. We've got a rehearsal to run, and if we're late, Mrs. Aldous will assume command of the entire resistance, and nobody wants that."

Mara laughed again—louder this time, startled out of her—and let herself be pulled away from the fence, away from the enemy lights, toward the bells and the town and the impossible day ahead.

Behind them, on the far side of the breach, the first thunder of the gathering storm rolled down off the mountains like an answer.

Or perhaps, Beatrix thought, tightening her grip on Mara's hand, like an accompaniment.
