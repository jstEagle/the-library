## 19. March on the Hollow

The sky over Vesper Hollow had been wrong for three days.

Mara felt it in her teeth before she saw it—a pressure like a held breath, like the whole atmosphere leaning its weight against the valley's rim and waiting for permission to fall through. She'd spent ten years learning to read weather as a farmer reads soil, and this was not weather. This was intention, given a horizon.

She stood on the mill's broken foundation—still half-ruined from the breach, still smelling of scorched grain and old timber—and watched the ridgeline where the storm-mages' camp had grown from a scatter of siege-lanterns into a city of them. And above it all, arriving that morning like a bruise spreading across the dawn, hung a cloud formation she knew better than her own heartbeat: the Archmage's Crown, a ring of cirrus so high and cold it caught the sun and turned it to a halo of knives.

"He's here," she said, though no one was beside her yet. She said it anyway, because some things needed saying aloud to become real, and because if she didn't say them aloud they tended to coil up inside her chest and turn into lightning, which was a habit she was trying very hard to break.

By midmorning the whole valley knew. Word traveled the way it always did in Vesper Hollow—not by messenger but by a kind of osmosis of worry, passing between doorways and market stalls and the long queue outside the bakery until every soul in the town had absorbed the same fact and was pretending, loudly, not to have.

Mara found Beatrix at the schoolhouse, standing at the window with her arms crossed, watching the ridgeline the way one watches a pot that is definitely about to boil over.

"You've heard," Mara said.

"I've heard." Beatrix didn't turn. "Mrs. Fenwick told me. Then Mr. Aldous told me. Then Mrs. Fenwick told me again with more detail, most of which contradicted her first version. According to one of them, the archmage is eight feet tall and eats children."

"He's not eight feet tall."

"That's what I said."

"He's worse than that," Mara said. "He's patient."

Beatrix finally turned, and Mara saw that under the dry humor her face was pale and set, the face of someone who had spent three days negotiating with an army and had run out of clever clauses in warden law. Three days. That was what Beatrix had bought them at the border, standing alone in a doorway of lantern-light with a signet ring on her finger and her chin up, invoking laws older than the order that wanted to ignore them. Three days, and two of them were gone.

"The choir's downstairs," Beatrix said. "I couldn't make myself send them home. I know I should have. Every sensible instinct says scatter them to their families and bar the doors."

"But?"

"But they wouldn't go." A ghost of a smile. "Tam organized them. He's got them running scales. The boy collapsed channeling a surge for this ward four days ago and he's down there right now arguing with Petra about whether the third verse goes up or down."

"Who's winning?"

"Petra. Tam is never going to win an argument about pitch and we both know it, but I admire his commitment."

Mara laughed despite everything—the wrongness in the sky, the ache in her scarred hands, the countdown ticking down beneath all of it. That was Beatrix's particular magic, the one no prophecy had needed to name: she could make the end of the world feel survivable for exactly as long as you were standing next to her.

Downstairs, faintly, the choir began to sing. Not the canticle—Beatrix hadn't dared teach them more than fragments—but something older and simpler, a harvest hymn, and through the floorboards Mara felt the ward answer it the way a sleeping animal answers a familiar voice. A low hum in the stones. A steadiness.

"They can feel it," Beatrix said quietly. "That the singing matters. I never taught them that. They just... know."

* * *

The archmage did not come at once. That was the worst part.

He let them wait. He let the Crown hang on the ridgeline all day while his vanguard tightened its lines below it, and he let the valley watch him watch them, because Aldric Vane had built his reputation on the principle that fear did half the work of any army and cost nothing in soldiers.

Mara spent the afternoon doing something she'd sworn off years ago: she went among the townsfolk and told them the truth.

Not all of it. Not the parts about her mother carved into the heart-chamber stone, not yet—that revelation belonged to Beatrix and her, and there would be time, if there was any time left at all. But the shape of it. That the storm-mages hadn't come for the prophecy book or even for her. That they'd come because a Whitlock heir lived and breathed in Vesper Hollow, and a living warden-heir outside the order's control was a threat to everything Aldric Vane had spent forty years building toward.

"And that's us," she finished, standing in the square outside the tavern where perhaps two hundred people had gathered—farmers with dirt still on their hands, the baker with flour to his elbows, old Mrs. Fenwick front and center because Mrs. Fenwick had never in living memory missed an event, however grim. "You should know that when he comes, he comes for Beatrix and me. Anyone who wants to leave the valley tonight, the eastern pass is still open. No one will think less of you. I'll think less of no one. I mean that."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight.

Then Mr. Aldous, the beekeeper whose hives Mara had tended alongside her own for a decade, cleared his throat and said, "Is this the part where we're supposed to panic?"

"It's the part where you're allowed to."

"Hm." He considered. "I'll pass. My wife's buried in the churchyard here, my bees are on the north slope, and I don't care for the eastern pass—it's gravelly." He looked around at his neighbors. "Anyone else leaving? Speak up, don't be shy."

No one spoke up. A few people shifted their feet. Someone near the back—the farrier's apprentice—said, "My mum says if we're evacuating she wants to bring the good chairs," which got a ragged laugh, and then Mrs. Fenwick rose from her front-row seat with the ponderous dignity of a ship coming about.

"I fled a war when I was nine years old," she announced, in the voice she used for correcting choir members and misbehaving weather alike. "I spent my whole childhood being carried from place to place by people who meant well. Do you know what I remember of those years? Nothing. Nothing but doors closing behind us. When I came to this valley I swore I would never again stand in a doorway watching my own life get packed onto a cart." She fixed Mara with a look that could have curdled milk at forty paces. "So you may tell your archmage, dear, that Vesper Hollow does not close its doors. We open them. There's a difference, and he's about to learn it."

And that was how it went, all through the town, all through the evening. Nobody fled. The baker banked his ovens early so they'd be hot at dawn—"armies march badly on empty stomachs, even evil ones, probably especially evil ones"—and the farrier checked every hinge and latch in the village as though a well-oiled door were a fortification, and maybe it was. The farmers brought their wagons in from the fields and parked them in a ring around the square, which was not a strategy anyone had agreed upon; it simply happened, the way a body happens around a wound.

Belonging, Mara thought, watching lamplight spread window to window across the Hollow like a constellation deciding to hold its ground. It wasn't blood. It wasn't law or inheritance or a name carved in stone. It was this: two hundred people with every reason to run, choosing instead to oil their hinges and heat their ovens and argue about whether the third verse went up or down.

She had spent ten years believing she could never belong anywhere, because belonging required a self you could offer, and hers had been confiscated at the border along with her storms. Standing in the square, she understood—for the first time, fully—that she'd had it backwards. Belonging wasn't something you offered. It was something other people did, stubbornly, on your behalf, whether you'd consented or not.

* * *

He came at moonrise.

There was no new moon for another night—Beatrix's three days ran out tomorrow at dusk—but Aldric Vane clearly felt the deadline was a formality, and Mara had to admit the theater was effective. He descended the ridge road at the head of his vanguard with the Crown of cloud wheeling above him, and where he walked, the siege-lanterns dimmed, as though light itself were making way.

Mara met him at the breach, because it was hers to meet. Beatrix stood at her shoulder—she had refused, in terms that brooked no argument, to be anywhere else—and behind them, filling the road back into town, stood the valley.

Vane was not eight feet tall. He was ordinary-sized, silver-haired, with the mild face of a beloved schoolmaster, and his eyes were the color of the sky just before hail. He stopped ten paces off, beyond the ragged edge where the ward's remnants still flickered—thinner now, gauze-thin, a candle guttering—and regarded them with something almost like affection.

"Mara Whitlock." His voice was warm. That was the horror of it; it was genuinely warm. "Ten years. You look well. The valley agrees with you."

"Aldric." She kept her voice flat. "You're trespassing on warden ground."

"I'm reclaiming order property." He glanced past her at the town, at the lit windows, at the ring of farm wagons, and his smile deepened by a degree. "And what is this? A militia of bakers?"

"It's a valley," Beatrix said.

Vane's gaze moved to her, and Mara watched him take her in—the plain dress, the ink-stained fingers, the signet ring—and watched something shift behind the mild eyes. Recognition, and beneath the recognition, hunger, carefully plated as courtesy.

"The warden-heir herself. Beatrix Ives." He inclined his head. "Your grandmother and I corresponded, once. Before your family's unfortunate accident. She was a remarkable woman. Stubborn." The word *stubborn* came out with an edge fine enough to draw blood. "Stubbornness runs in families, I find. As does foolishness. Give me the book, surrender the exile, and open the ward's heart to me, and I will leave your town untouched. You have my word."

"Your word," Mara said, "is worth exactly what Wren Calloway's testimony was worth."

Something flickered across his face then—the mask slipping, just a hair, showing the machinery underneath. "Ah. So the traitor talked."

"She confessed. Under guard, wounded, with nothing to gain." Mara stepped forward, and felt Beatrix's hand brush hers—steadying, anchoring. "You engineered my exile because my mother's name is carved in the heart of this ward, and a living Whitlock outside your order threatened everything you've stolen. You didn't come for a book, Aldric. You came because the truth lives here, and truths are the one thing you can't abide."

For a long moment the only sound was wind moving through the breach, carrying the smell of the storm-mages' camp—ozone and cold iron.

Then Vane smiled again, and this time there was nothing warm in it at all.

"Tomorrow at dusk, then. Your little deadline." He turned, and his vanguard turned with him like a single organism. "I do hope the choir sings something nice. I've always found dying towns so much more affecting with music."

He walked back up the ridge road, and the Crown wheeled overhead, and the siege-lanterns flared back to life one by one, a second constellation arrayed against theirs.

Behind Mara, in the dark, someone began to hum.

It was Tam—of course it was Tam—standing in the front rank of the town with his arm still bandaged, humming the opening phrase of the warden's canticle, the one that took what it needed, the one Beatrix had forbidden him to sing entire. One by one the choir picked it up, then the baker, then the farrier's apprentice, then Mrs. Fenwick in a key of her own devising, until the whole valley was humming a fragment of a song a hundred years dead, and the ruined ward-stones along the border flickered—flickered—like a pulse finding its rhythm again.

Mara looked at Beatrix. Beatrix looked at the ridge, at the lights, at the impossible stubborn constellation of her town, and Mara saw the exact moment the last hesitation left her face.

"The heart-chamber," Beatrix said quietly. "Tomorrow. Before dusk. We end this where it started."

Above the ridgeline, the Archmage's Crown began, very slowly, to spin.
