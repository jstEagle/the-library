## 20. Storm and Song

**Mara**

The heart-chamber smelled of dust and old lightning.

Mara had expected stone. She had expected the cold, patient dark of a place built to be forgotten. What she found instead was a room that felt *expectant*—a circular chamber deep beneath the valley's roots, its walls carved with the same spiraling script she'd seen on the ward-stones above, except here the carvings were worn smooth in places, as if generations of hands had touched them the way you'd touch a doorframe on your way out of home.

The binding circle sat at the center: seven grooves cut into the floor, radiating outward like the spokes of a wheel, meeting at a hollow basin no wider than a washbucket. When Mara knelt beside it and pressed her scarred palms to the rim, something far below her stirred—a slow, tidal attention, like a whale turning over in deep water.

"Hello," she whispered, feeling absurd. "I'm the one who's been feeding you scraps for ten years."

The circle pulsed once beneath her hands. Warm. Almost fond.

Behind her, Beatrix's boots scraped on the stair. "Tam says the choir's in position. Wren's holding the eastern ridge with what's left of her scouts—she says—" Beatrix stopped, breathless from the descent, and Mara turned to look at her and forgot, briefly, every tactical detail she'd spent three sleepless nights arranging.

Beatrix was wearing her grandmother's signet on a cord at her throat, and her hair was coming loose from its braid, and there was soot on one cheekbone from the mill fires, and she was looking at Mara with an expression that made the word *goodbye* feel like a foreign language.

"Wren says what?" Mara managed.

"She says the archmage's column will reach the breach by moonrise." Beatrix crossed the chamber and knelt across the circle from her, close enough that their knees nearly touched. "She also said—and I quote—'Tell Whitlock if she dies down there I'll never forgive her, which will be inconvenient, since I already don't.'"

"That sounds like Wren."

"Mara." Beatrix's voice dropped into the register she used when a rehearsal had gone sideways and she needed everyone to stop talking. "Before we do this. Before the singing starts and everything gets loud. I need to say some things."

Mara's chest tightened. "Beatrix—"

"No. Let me." Beatrix took a breath. "Ten years you spent pouring yourself into this valley a little at a time, and you let me believe your magic just... faded. You lied to protect me, and I'm still angry about it, and I'm going to stay angry about it for a while, because that's how love works apparently—it doesn't file anything away neatly, it just keeps *everything*." Her voice wobbled. She steadied it. "But I need you to know that I'm not doing this because the prophecy says to. I'm not doing it for the wardens or the valley or even the choir, though God help me, those children up there are going to sing like the sky is falling, because for them it might be."

"Then why?"

"Because you asked me once, on our first date, what I wanted my life to sound like." Beatrix's hand found hers across the circle's rim. "And I said I didn't know yet, and you said that was the most honest answer anyone had ever given you. Well. This is mine. I want it to sound like *this*. Like us. Even if it only sounds like us once."

Above them—far above, through forty feet of earth and root—the first faint thread of the canticle began.

* * *

**Beatrix**

Singing had never felt like this before.

Beatrix stood at the head of her choir on the archive steps, where their whole strange story had started with a candle and a locked case, and the valley spread out below them in the last bruised light before moonrise. To the east, siege-lanterns burned along the ridgeline in a jagged constellation—hundreds of them, more than Wren had promised, more than anyone had counted. The archmage had brought his entire order.

And her choir—her ragged, wonderful, terrified choir—stood in two rows behind her, farmers and bakers and the blacksmith's apprentice and old Mrs. Fenwick, who had sworn for thirty years that she couldn't carry a tune in a bucket and was now holding the drone line like a woman anchoring a ship.

Tam stood at her right hand. He was still thin from the draining, still pale, but his eyes were clear, and when he'd taken his place he had looked up at her and said, very seriously, "I won't sing the last verse, Miss Ives. But I'll sing everything else as loud as I can."

"You do that," she'd told him, and had to pretend to adjust her sheet music for a moment.

Now she raised her hands, and sixty voices drew breath together, and Beatrix felt the ward answer before the first note even landed—a shiver through the ground, a leaning-in, the way a room full of students goes quiet when they sense something important is about to happen.

They sang the opening verse, the one Tam had bled for, and the candle-flames in the archive windows behind them stood perfectly straight.

They sang the second verse, the one woven through the old hymn, and the ward-stones along the valley's rim began to glow—not the sickly guttering flicker of the past weeks but a steady, gathering light, like coals remembering they were fire.

And then Beatrix lifted her voice alone over the top of the harmony, carrying the melody down through the earth, down through stone and root and darkness, to the woman waiting in the binding circle, and she sang not words exactly but a *name*, the way you sing a name when you're calling someone home.

Below her feet, she felt the storm wake up.

* * *

**Mara**

It came back all at once, and it came back *hungry*.

Ten years of dormancy broke like ice on a spring river. Every storm she had ever called—every gale she'd bent around a burning village, every thunderhead she'd argued with, every bolt she'd caught bare-handed to save a child whose name she could no longer remember—came flooding back into her at once, and Mara Whitlock, kneeling in the dark with Beatrix's song pouring down around her like rain, threw back her head and *laughed*.

Because it hurt. Because it was glorious. Because for the first time since her trial, the power in her blood wasn't a wound—it was a gift being handed back, and the hand offering it was Beatrix's voice, and the price was written plainly in the circle's design: *everything you have, given freely, or nothing.*

The archmage's magic hit the valley's edge at that exact moment.

Mara felt it as pressure—a fist of ordered, imperial lightning slamming against the newborn ward, testing it, finding it soft, hammering again. Above her, dust sifted from the ceiling. Somewhere in the choir's harmony a voice cracked and another voice caught it, folded it in, carried it.

"Not yet," Mara whispered to the storms roiling inside her. "Not yet. Wait for her."

She placed both palms flat in the basin at the circle's heart. The carved grooves lit beneath her hands, and her mother's name—*Elowen Whitlock, who gave freely*—blazed gold among the founders' names, and Mara finally understood what the prophecy had been asking for all along.

Not a sacrifice. A *duet*.

"Now," she breathed, and let go of everything.

* * *

**Beatrix**

Later, no one in Vesper Hollow would ever quite agree on what the light looked like.

Mrs. Fenwick swore it was silver. The blacksmith's apprentice insisted it was green, like the aurora his grandmother remembered from the northern coast. Tam, who saw more clearly than any of them, said it was both colors braided together, turning around each other like two dancers who had finally learned the same steps.

What Beatrix knew, from inside it, was this: she sang, and the storm answered, and neither one led.

Mara's power rose through the binding circle and met Beatrix's voice halfway—in the stone itself, in the space between verses, in the impossible seam where thunder became music without either of them being able to say where the change happened. Beatrix felt Mara's whole life pour past her: the village fire, the trial, the testimony, the long gray exile years of bees and honey and loneliness, the date at the archive, the candle guttering, the page that named them. All of it went into the circle. All of it went into the ward.

And the ward *drank*.

Light climbed the walls of the buried chamber and raced outward along the grooves, up through the roots of the valley, bursting from every ward-stone along the rim in pillars of white-gold fire. The archmage's assault—which had been peeling the border open like fruit—struck the reborn ward and simply *stopped*, the way a wave stops against a cliff.

Then the ward pushed back.

Beatrix felt it happen through her own throat, a resonance so vast it barely registered as sound: the valley exhaling. The archmage's stolen lightning, all that imperial, hoarded, centuries-deep power, met the one force in the world it had no defense against—magic given away freely instead of seized—and rebounded. The ridgeline lit up brighter than noon. Siege-lanterns went out by the hundred. And a scream rolled down off the mountains, distant and furious and abruptly, terribly *small*.

In the sudden hush, the choir held their final chord. Beatrix held it with them, eyes closed, tears cutting clean lines through the soot on her face.

Underground, in the dark, the storm went quiet—but it did not go out. She could feel it now, everywhere, woven into the valley's bones the way a song is woven into a singer: permanent, patient, *home*.

* * *

**Mara**

Silence, afterward, has a texture. This silence felt like linen dried in sunlight.

Mara knelt in the basin's afterglow, empty in a way that should have been terrifying and somehow wasn't. Her hands lay open in her lap, unscarred for the first time in weeks—the backlash marks faded to pale threads, as if the ward had taken even those into itself, filed them away with everything else.

She was trying to decide whether she could stand when footsteps came down the stair, fast and uneven, and then Beatrix was there, dropping to her knees in the circle's glow, grabbing Mara's face in both hands, checking her eyes, her pulse, her mouth, as if cataloguing her for damage.

"I'm fine," Mara rasped. "I'm—well. I'm entirely ordinary now. But fine."

"You're *alive*."

"I'm alive."

Beatrix pressed her forehead to Mara's and stayed there, breathing, while the light in the grooves slowly softened to embers. "The archmage?"

"Unmade, if Wren's scouts can be believed. His power scattered back into the sky where it came from. The rest of the order is—" Mara laughed, a little unsteadily. "Beatrix, half of them are kneeling on the ridgeline. Warden law says a defeated order sues for peace under the reigning warden's authority. They're waiting for *you*."

"They're waiting for—" Beatrix pulled back and stared at her. "No. No, absolutely not, I teach *recorder* to nine-year-olds—"

"And you just out-sang an archmage," Mara said. "You negotiated a parley with a sword drawn at your doorway. You read a century-old prophecy aloud knowing it would change you forever, because someone had to." She reached up and tucked a loose strand of hair behind Beatrix's ear, gently, the way you handle something you've decided to keep. "Vesper Hollow is going to kneel to you tomorrow, whether you've prepared a speech or not. So I have a proposal."

"A proposal," Beatrix repeated faintly.

"The peace terms. When they come to you. There's a clause in warden law—I found it in the trunk, in your grandmother's letters—that lets the reigning warden appoint a *storm-warden*. An advisor. Someone who knows the other side of the sky." Mara's heart was pounding, absurdly, after everything. "Someone who spent ten years learning this valley from the ground up. Someone who owes it everything, and would like to spend the rest of her life repaying it. Beside you. If—if that's—"

Beatrix kissed her before she could finish the sentence, which Mara would reflect, later, was the correct parliamentary procedure.

When they finally climbed the stairs together, hand in hand, dawn was breaking over Vesper Hollow—and along the valley's rim, the new ward-stones were singing.
