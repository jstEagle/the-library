## 22. Beatrix and the Choir

The bees came back in the spring.

Mara had not expected them to. She had spent a decade learning the valley's small cruelties—late frosts, wasp raids, the way a hive could fail for no reason you could name—and she had braced herself for an empty spring, a silent apiary, one more thing the war had taken. Instead, on the first warm morning after thaw, she had opened the hives to find them humming, fat with survivors, as if they had simply been waiting out the winter of it all.

She had stood there in the smoke-smell and the gold light and cried into her veil like a fool, and then gone inside and made tea, because that was what people did in Vesper Hollow when the world surprised them kindly. You wept, you poured tea, you got back to work.

A season later, the work had a shape.

She climbed the hill behind the schoolhouse now with her satchel over one shoulder and a jar of honey under her arm, past the mill—which was a mill again, its wheel turning, though the new timbers were pale as bone against the old dark ones—and up to the little stone circle that the valley had, without anyone quite deciding it, started calling the Warden's Garden. The ward-stone at its center still bore the scar of the breach, a seam of glassy black running through it like a healed wound, but around it the ground had greened early and stayed green, and wildflowers grew there that Mara could not find in any of her books.

Her students were already waiting. There were six of them this season—the first warden apprentices Vesper Hollow had trained in a hundred years, chosen by the stone itself, which had flared warm under each of their palms when Beatrix's choir sang the canticle over them at midwinter. Three farm kids, a baker's daughter, the miller's boy, and Tam.

Tam sat cross-legged at the edge of the circle, hands folded, watching the stone with the particular seriousness he brought to everything since the fever. He had recovered fully—Beatrix had checked him a hundred times, and so had the healer, and so had Tam himself, reporting daily on his own pulse like a tiny physician—but he had come back from the fever dreams quieter, older in one narrow band of himself, the way a tree comes back from lightning with a stripe of scar that never quite matches the bark.

"You're late," said Odette, the baker's daughter, who was eleven and had appointed herself keeper of standards.

"I'm exactly on time," Mara said. "You're all early."

"We're *keen*," said the miller's boy, Joss, who was nine and used words he'd heard adults use, hoping they'd stick.

"Keen is good. Keen is how you don't die." Mara set down her satchel and looked up at the sky, reading it the way she'd read it every morning for ten years, except that now she taught what she saw instead of just surviving it. "Now. What do we see?"

"High cloud, thin, moving east," said Odette.

"Wind from the south, but it keeps trying to turn west," said Joss.

"Good. And what does thin high cloud moving east with a shifty wind mean by evening?"

"Rain," said Tam.

"*Why*, Tam."

"Because the wind wants to go west and the cloud is going east, so something above them both is pulling against something below, and when they stop arguing—" he spread his hands, "—it falls."

Mara smiled. Ten years ago, that answer would have earned him a place in the storm-caller order's lowest tier, and a decade of discipline and fear. Here it earned him a nod and the honey jar to pass around, bread from Odette's pockets, and the whole circle of them sitting in the flowers eating breakfast while the sky did its slow arithmetic overhead.

"The rain will come after dusk," she told them. "It won't be much. But I want you to watch it arrive. Not the falling—the arriving. The way the light goes strange first. The way the birds change their minds. Weather doesn't sneak up on you if you learn its manners." She tapped the ward-stone gently with two fingers. "Neither does anything else. That's the whole lesson. Everything announces itself, if you've learned to listen. The rest of it—" she shrugged, "—is just remembering to listen when it matters."

"When it matters," Tam repeated, solemnly, writing nothing down because none of them wrote anything down; the apprenticeship was all watching and saying-back, the oldest kind of school there was.

They finished the bread. The wind turned west, right on schedule, and Mara felt a small private satisfaction that never got old—that the sky still kept its promises to her even now, even empty-handed, even human. She had lost the storms. What remained was the knowing, and it turned out the knowing was enough to fill a life, if you let it.

Above them, faint and clear across the valley, the dawn bell rang.

And then the choir began.

* * *

Every morning since midwinter, at the bell, Beatrix Ives had walked the length of the Hollow's main street with forty singers strung out behind her like a comet's tail, and they had sung the reborn ward awake.

It wasn't required. Nothing about it was required. That was the thing Mara had needed a full season to understand: the valley did it because it wanted to. The canticle had passed from the choir into the town the way a song does—unplanned, unstoppable, hummed by bakers while they kneaded, whistled off-key by farmers who couldn't carry a tune in a bucket but knew every word. At dawn the whole valley sang, badly and beautifully, and the ward-stones along the rim lit soft blue one after another, west to east, like lamps being carried down a road, and the light ran the full circle of Vesper Hollow and settled into a low steady glow that lasted until dusk.

Wren Calloway, who had stayed through the winter under warden law's terms—parley extended into penance, penance slowly becoming something else—had watched it happen once from the ridge with an expression Mara had never seen on her face before, and had said only: "We built engines. They built a *chorus*. No wonder we lost."

Mara stood in the garden with her six apprentices and listened to the dawn song roll over them. The children went quiet, the way everyone went quiet for it, even now, even every day. High above, thin as thread, Beatrix's voice rose over the others—not louder than the choir, never louder, just *clearer*, the way a keel is clearer than a boat—and the ward-stone beneath their feet warmed like a sleeping animal waking.

Tam closed his eyes. His lips moved with the words. Mara watched him, and did not worry, because Beatrix had rebuilt the canticle verse by verse with the whole choir carrying every line together, so that no single voice ever bore more than its share. The last verse—the true one, the terrible one—was sung by no one. It was written in the book, and the book stayed locked in the archive case, and once a season Beatrix unlocked the case and read it silently to herself and locked it again, and that was all. A life freely given, demanded by no one, offered by no one. It could wait a hundred years. It could wait forever, if everyone was careful, and everyone was being very careful, and also very alive, which was the point.

The song ended. The bell finished ringing. Somewhere down in the village, Odette's father swore cheerfully at his ovens, having burned the morning loaves again because he always sang along and always lost count.

"Class dismissed," Mara said. "Watch the light go strange tonight. Report tomorrow."

They scattered down the hill, arguing happily about whether wind could be *rude*. Mara gathered her satchel, touched the ward-stone once—it hummed against her palm, familiar now, almost affectionate—and started down toward the archive.

She had a date to get ready for.

Well. She had a date to *ask* for. Whether she got it remained, as it had ten months ago on these same steps, entirely up to Beatrix Ives.

* * *

The archive smelled the same as it had that first night: dust and candlewax and old paper, the particular sweetness of a building that kept its memories well. The broken case had been repaired—new hinges, new lock, the prophecy book resting inside on a bed of cloth, tended now like the relic it was. Beatrix had the key on a cord around her neck. She wore it everywhere. Mara had stopped pretending not to notice.

Mara arrived early, because she was nervous, and dealt with the nervousness the way she dealt with all weather: by doing useful things near it. She swept the steps. She straightened the notice board. She moved a bench three inches and moved it back. The townsfolk passing by nodded to her—*Morning, Mara, lovely light going strange tonight*—and she nodded back, and it still caught her sometimes, sideways, how easily they said her name. Ten years of being the quiet beekeeper at the valley's edge, and one war had made her *Mara Whitlock, weather-teacher, the one who pulled folks out of the mill*, and apparently that was enough. Belonging, it turned out, was not a door you were given a key to. It was a door you stood outside of long enough, usefully, until someone forgot it had ever been locked.

At noon exactly, Beatrix came up the street with her choir trailing her like ducklings, fresh from the dawn walk's second rehearsal—she drilled them mercilessly, and they adored her for it—and stopped at the bottom of the steps when she saw Mara standing there.

"You swept my steps," Beatrix said.

"The valley's steps."

"My steps. You're stalling." Her eyes went to the satchel, to the jar of honey Mara was holding for no defensible reason, to Mara's face, which she had learned to read better than any sky. "Oh," she said softly. "Today's the day, isn't it."

"I don't know what you mean," said Mara, who had rehearsed this moment forty times and was now going to improvise badly, as planned, because the plan had always been that there was no plan. "I was in the neighborhood. Sweeping. Carrying honey."

"Mm."

"There's a bench."

"There is."

"It has historical significance. We sat on it once. Something happened. I forget what."

"A century-old book fell open and ruined our first date," Beatrix said, climbing the steps, "and I read it aloud against your explicit warning, and light crawled into our skin, and the birds went silent, and thunder answered from beyond the ward, and it was the worst date anyone has ever been on."

"And yet," Mara said.

"And yet." Beatrix reached her, took the honey jar out of her hands, and set it on the bench with great ceremony. "You were saying."

Mara looked at her. The sun was high and ordinary and kind. Down the street, the choir had pretended to disperse and was in fact clustered behind the well, watching with the total lack of subtlety unique to singers between the ages of eight and sixty. Tam gave Mara an enormous encouraging thumbs-up, which nearly ended everything right there.

"Beatrix Ives," Mara said. "Ten months ago I asked you to dinner and a look through the archive, and it turned into the end of the world. I've thought about it a lot, and I've identified where I went wrong."

"Go on."

"I didn't specify that the world should stay ended-off. That was an oversight. So." She took a breath, and found her hands weren't shaking, which was new, which was the season's doing, which was hers. "Second date. Tonight. Dinner—I'm cooking, and I have gotten *better*, ask literally anyone except the people I've cooked for. Then a walk to the western stones to watch the light go strange before the rain. And then—" she faltered, just slightly, and Beatrix waited, patient as a held note, "—and then whatever we want. Nothing prophesied. Nothing named. No page anywhere with our clothes described. Just an evening, in a valley that sings itself awake, with you."

Beatrix was quiet for a moment. Behind the well, forty people held their breath in unison, which should not have been audible and absolutely was.

"You know," Beatrix said, "I spent my whole life thinking I was an ordinary woman in an ordinary valley. Choir teacher. Grandmother's trunk. Quiet life." She picked up the honey jar and pressed it back into Mara's hands, closing Mara's fingers around it, keeping her own hand there, warm over Mara's knuckles. "Then I found out my family built the walls of the world and died for strangers, and that my voice could hold back a siege, and that a book written before my grandmother was born had been waiting on a shelf for me to walk in and ruin everything." Her thumb moved once across Mara's hand. "And do you know what I concluded? After all of it? The war, the ward, the archmage, the whole terrible year?"

"That you were never ordinary?"

"That I was *exactly* as ordinary as I chose to be." She smiled, and it was the smile from the first night, the one that had made Mara forget every careful rule she'd built around her heart. "Belonging isn't blood, Mara. You taught me that. Or the valley did. Same thing. I belong here because I stay here. I stay here because—" she squeezed her hand, "—well. Because of the honey, obviously. And the sweeping. And the person doing them."

"Is that a yes?"

"That's a yes to dinner, a yes to the walk, and a *strong* maybe to whatever-we-want, depending on the quality of the cooking."

Behind the well, the choir erupted. Forty voices cheered, immediately dissolved into harmony out of pure habit, and began—there was no stopping them—to sing. Not the canticle. Something newer, sillier, four-part, unmistakably about honey and sweeping and two women on some steps, lyrics that Odette had clearly been composing in secret for weeks. Beatrix closed her eyes in mortified delight. Mara laughed until she had to sit down on the historically significant bench.

"They've been working on that since midwinter," Beatrix admitted. "I was supposed to act surprised."

"I *am* surprised. I'm astonished. I've never been serenaded by a baked goods conspiracy."

"Get used to it. This valley sings everything now. Weddings, funerals, market days, arguments. Wren got a ballad about the parley flag. She pretends to hate it. She knows all the words."

Mara looked out over the Hollow—the pale new timbers of the mill, the washlines, the gardens going in, the far rim where the ward-stones glowed their quiet blue, the sky above it all doing its slow honest arithmetic toward evening rain. Ten years ago she had come here to hide, and hidden so well she'd nearly disappeared. Now her name was on the schoolhouse roster, her honey on every table, her storms woven into the ground under everyone's feet, singing back to the choir every dawn in a language only she and Beatrix fully understood. She had surrendered everything she was to save this place, and the place had handed her back a life larger than the one she'd renounced. That was the trade no prophecy had written. That was the one they'd made themselves.

"So," Beatrix said, sitting beside her, shoulder warm against hers, the choir's ridiculous song swelling around them like weather. "Second date. Where were we, before the century interrupted?"

Mara laced their fingers together and watched the light begin, very faintly, to go strange.

"Nowhere yet," she said. "That's rather the point. Take me somewhere."

Beatrix smiled, and stood, and pulled her up, and the whole valley sang them down the steps and out into the ordinary, unprophesied, entirely theirs afternoon.
