## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The ewe's name was Sorrel, and she was going to die, and nobody in the Valley of Stillwater would have thought that mattered enough to lie about.

Odile Brightwater lay in the straw beside her anyway, one hand flat on the animal's warm flank, and told her the truth the way she told it to no one else.

"It's not your fault," she murmured. "Bones don't ask permission to go wrong. You stepped in a hole, that's all. The world made a hole and you were walking."

Sorrel sighed—a long, wet, put-upon sound—and rested her blunt grey head against Odile's knee. Above them the barn loft creaked under the weight of hay and the weight of the morning, and through the gap in the boards Odile could see a stripe of sunlight crawling across the floor, slow as honey off a spoon. Somewhere outside her father was calling the dog, and the dog was pretending not to hear, and the ordinary noise of it settled over the barn like a blanket.

This was the part no one could ever know. Not the lying in the straw—she could explain that, a girl with a soft heart for a lame ewe, that was almost respectable. The other part. The listening part.

Because Sorrel answered her.

Not in words. Words were the wrong shape for what passed between them. It was more like warmth moving through a wall—fear first, when Odile had first come near, the sharp vinegar-smell of it in the back of her mind. Pain, dull and red and heavy as a wet coat. And underneath, when Odile scratched the spot behind her ear that she couldn't reach herself, something that could only be called gratitude, simple and bottomless as a well.

*Stay,* Sorrel said, in the way she said things. *Stay, and scratch, and stay.*

"I can't stay," Odile whispered. "You know I can't. Da will be looking for me."

The ewe's answer was a huff of breath that fogged in the cold barn air, and Odile laughed despite herself—a small, guilty laugh, swallowed fast, the way she swallowed everything. That was the rule of the valley, older than the valley's fences, older than the Brightwater name. *Keep your head down. Keep your voice low. Keep whatever is strange about you folded up small and hidden, the way you'd hide a knife.*

Her grandmother had taught her the rule before she could read. *The valley forgets, Odile. That's its mercy and its law. It forgets magic the way a river forgets a stone—everything that isn't smooth gets worn away. So be smooth, child. Be water. Be nothing the valley needs to wear away.*

Odile had asked, once, aged seven, what happened to things the valley wore away. Her grandmother had gone very still, the way she did, and said, *They get forgotten,* in a voice that made Odile never ask again.

So Odile was smooth. Odile was water. Odile Brightwater, sixteen years old, good at cheese and better at silence, the girl who could walk through the market and leave no more impression than a draft through a door. She had gotten so good at it that sometimes she scared herself—sometimes she'd catch her own reflection in the wash-water and feel a flicker of surprise, as if the valley had worn even her away from herself, and what was left was just a shape where a girl should be.

But not in the barn. In the barn, with her hand on Sorrel's side, she was the only magic in Stillwater, and she let herself be it for as long as the light stripe took to cross the floor.

*Hurts,* Sorrel told her.

"I know."

*Hurts, and the other sheep don't wait for me, and the grass runs away.*

"Grass doesn't run."

*It does. When you're slow, everything runs.*

Odile's throat tightened. She scratched the ear. "Then I'll be slow with you," she said. "For a while. For as long as we've got."

And because she was sixteen and alone and the ewe's breath was warm and her own heart was full past the brim, she did the thing she had never done, not once in all her secret years of beast-speech. She prayed.

Not to anything. There was nothing to pray to—the old gods were gone from the valley, worn smooth as everything else, and her grandmother had said the new god, the quiet one the elders mumbled to on rest-days, was a god of silence, a god who kept things as they were, and you didn't ask a god like that for change. But the words came up out of her anyway, the way water comes up through chalk, and she whispered them into Sorrel's wool:

*Something old and something kind, if anything old is left behind—*

She didn't even know where the words came from. That was the frightening part, later, when she had time to be frightened. They felt borrowed. They felt like a key turning in a lock she'd never noticed in her own chest.

*—look on this small hurt of mine, and mend what mending finds.*

The barn went quiet.

Not barn-quiet. Not the soft, busy quiet of animals and dust and sunlight. This was the silence that lived at the bottom of wells. The silence under the silence.

Sorrel's head came up off her knee. Her ears went forward, then back, then flat. Every hair along Odile's arms stood up beneath her sleeves, and her hand—she could not have said why—pressed flatter against the ewe's flank, as if the animal were a door and Odile could hold it shut.

The light stripe on the floor stopped moving.

That was the thing she would remember, all her life, in the years when she would lie awake and take this moment out and look at it: the light stopped. Honey-slow, patient, ordinary light—and it stopped, the way a walker stops when they hear their name called from a house they thought was empty.

And then something answered.

It did not come from outside the barn. That was the worst of it, or the best of it; she could never decide. It came from underneath. From below the flagstones, below the clay, below the roots of the valley itself—something vast turning over in its sleep, the way a mountain might turn, the way the world might shift its weight from one elbow to the other. And a voice, if it could be called a voice, said—not in words, not in beast-warmth, but in a pressure, a meaning pressed flat against the inside of her skull:

***WHO PRAYS IN STILLWATER?***

The ground trembled.

Just once. One long, low shudder, like a cart passing on a far road, except there were no roads under the barn, there was nothing under the barn but the bones of the valley, and the bones of the valley had just moved. Dust sifted from the rafters in a fine gold veil. Sorrel screamed—a thin, terrible, human-sounding scream—and scrambled, and her bad leg folded, and Odile threw her arms around the ewe's neck and held on, held on, her heart hammering like a fist on a door.

*I'm sorry,* she thought, wildly, to whatever she had woken. *I'm sorry, I didn't mean— I didn't know anyone was—*

***NO ONE HAS SPOKEN TO US IN THE VALLEY FOR A HUNDRED YEARS.***

The voice was not angry. That somehow made it worse. It was the voice of something that had been waiting so long that anger had worn away, the way everything wore away in Stillwater, and what was left underneath was just hunger, and just hope, and both of them were enormous.

***WHO PRAYS?***

"Nobody," Odile whispered. Her voice cracked. "Nobody. I'm nobody. I'm water. I'm smooth."

***YOU ARE BRIGHTWATER.***

The name went through her like a hook through a fish. The pressure of the attention of it—she felt *measured*, felt the way Sorrel felt when the butcher's boy came into the yard: small, and seen, and weighed.

And then, beneath the vast attention, something else. A second voice. Smaller. Older. Worn almost to nothing, a voice like a leaf's ghost, like the last ring of a bell. It came up through the same deep place, faint as a memory of a memory, and it said only:

*...Beatrix...?*

Odile stopped breathing.

Her grandmother's name. Her grandmother's name, spoken from underneath the world, in a voice so tired it was barely a voice at all—and the great pressure of the ancient thing's attention shifted, just slightly, off Odile and down, down into the dark, like an eye turning to look at something else in the room.

The light stripe began to move again.

Dust settled. Sorrel's heart slowed under Odile's hand, from a hammering to a gallop to something like a trot. Outside, her father called the dog again, and the dog barked, and the morning went on being an ordinary morning, as if the floor of the world had not just spoken.

But Odile stayed in the straw a long time, her arms around a lame ewe, listening to the silence settle back into place like snow.

*Beatrix.*

Her grandmother had been dead for three years. Her grandmother, who had taught her to be smooth, to be water, to be nothing the valley could wear away. Her grandmother, who had died on a cold morning with her hand closed around something small that the family had never found, and who had left behind a house of locked drawers and a ritual every year on her death-day that she had never once explained.

*Beatrix.*

The valley forgets, her grandmother had said. That's its mercy and its law.

But something under the valley had not forgotten. Something under the valley had been holding a name in the dark for a hundred years, waiting for a voice to say a prayer into the silence—and Odile had prayed, and the silence had broken, and now the name was out, and the ground had moved, and there was no prayer in the world smooth enough to put it all back.

Sorrel leaned her weight against her, warm and heavy and alive, and said, in the way she said things: *You're shaking.*

"I know," Odile whispered.

*What was that?*

Odile looked at the light on the straw, moving again, ordinary, innocent. She thought about her grandmother's locked drawers. She thought about the death-day, which was coming, as it came every year, in nine days' time.

"I don't know," she said. And then, because the ewe deserved the truth even if no one else did: "But I think I'm going to find out."
