## 2. Thirteen Moons on the Doorpost

The morning of Grandmother's death-day, Odile swept the doorstep thirteen times.

It was the ritual's first law: one sweep for each moon on the doorpost, and never a fourteenth stroke, because the thirteenth moon was the grandmother's own and you did not sweep her away. Odile had asked why when she was six. Grandmother had said, *Because some doors only open once a year, child, and we must not leave them ajar.* She had said it lightly, the way she said most true things, so that you could pretend afterward she'd been joking.

Odile was seventeen now, and she no longer pretended.

She set the bowl of barley water on the left side of the step, the dish of honey on the right, and the sprig of rowan in the center, its berries gone soft and dark as old blood. Then she knelt, pressed her palm flat to the cold stone, and spoke the words she'd been taught before she could read them.

"Beatrix Brightwater, who walked out of the dark and into this house, be welcome. Beatrix Brightwater, who kept us fed and unremarked, be thanked. Beatrix Brightwater, who is owed what she is owed—"

Her voice caught there, as it always did. The last line of the ritual had always been spoken by Grandmother herself, low and quick, like something spat out rather than recited:

"—be paid."

Odile had never been allowed to say those two words alone. *You'll say them when I'm gone,* Grandmother had told her, the winter before she died, her hands around Odile's like a trap closing gently. *And not before. And never anywhere but this doorstep.*

So Odile knelt on the doorstep with her palm on the stone and did not say them, because Grandmother wasn't quite gone yet—not in the way the valley counted such things. The valley counted a person gone when the stones forgot them. And the stones of Stillwater forgot everything eventually; that was their whole purpose, if stones could be said to have one.

She rose, brushed off her knees, and went inside to wake Tam.

* * *

Tam slept like a felled tree, one arm hanging off the bed, his hair a black tangle against the pillow. He was nineteen and broad through the shoulders and he snored like a landslide, and looking at him Odile felt the familiar ache of loving someone so much it was inconvenient.

"Tam." She shook his shoulder. "Death-day. Get up."

He surfaced slowly, blinking. "Is it barley cakes?"

"It's barley water and honey. The cakes are for her."

"The dead get cakes and the living get water." He swung his legs out of bed. "That's backwards."

"That's tradition." She threw his shirt at his head. "Wash your face. You have flour in your hair from yesterday."

"I don't—" He ran a hand through his hair and came away dusted white. "Huh."

They ate standing up, as they always did on death-day, because sitting down at table on the day of the dead invited the dead to sit with you, and Grandmother had been firm about wanting her own chair back. Their mother had laid the fire before dawn and then gone out to the fields without speaking, which was how Mama observed all holy days: by working harder and saying less. Grief, in the Brightwater house, was a chore like any other.

The day passed in the ordinary work of keeping invisible. Odile fed the hens and did not sing to them, though they clustered at her feet begging for it, silly feathered things that had learned her voice the way children learn a lullaby. She mended the fence where the ewes had leaned. She carried water from the well and did not let herself listen to the well-water's slow underground murmur, which sometimes sounded almost like words, almost like *stay, stay, stay*.

In the Valley of Stillwater, a gift for hearing beasts and brooks and birdsong-with-meaning-in-it was not a gift. It was a sickness. It was the thing that got families burned or banished or—worse, in Stillwater—*noticed*. Magic did not grow here. It smoldered under a jar. The valley's founders had made sure of it, whatever that meant; nobody would say. The elders said only that Stillwater was safe, and safe meant silent, and silent meant you buried every strange thing about yourself so deep even you couldn't find it anymore.

Odile had gotten very good at burying things.

Except, of course, for the ewe.

* * *

The ewe was lame in her near hind leg, had been since the ice went out of the ground, and Papa wanted her culled. Odile had begged a week's grace, and now she spent that grace in the far paddock with her hands on the animal's warm woolly skull, whispering.

"Hold still. I know. I know it hurts."

*Hurts,* agreed the ewe, in the way sheep agree—with a heaviness behind the eyes, a slump of the whole self. Sheep didn't use words the way people imagined animals might. They used weight. *Hurts, hurts, cold-in-the-bone, hurts.*

"I'm going to try something," Odile whispered. "I've read about it. Well—I've read about the shape of it. Grandmother's books don't exactly come with instructions."

She closed her eyes. She thought of warmth the way she thought of it when she comforted the hens after a fox-raid night, but deeper, older, pushed down through her palms instead of just her voice. She thought of spring ground. Of thaw.

And because she was seventeen and tired of hiding and it was death-day and grief makes people reckless, she heard herself add, barely aloud, half a prayer:

"*Please.* Old ones, whoever listens—if anything listens—let it mend. Let her walk. Please."

The valley held its breath.

Odile felt it hold its breath—the whole valley, the stones and the soil and the air itself, everything going taut as a wire. For three heartbeats there was nothing but the ewe's wool under her hands and her own pulse in her ears.

Then the ground trembled.

Not much. A shiver, like a horse shaking off a fly. The water trough rang with a thin bright note. Somewhere beneath the paddock, beneath the topsoil and the clay and the bedrock, something vast turned over in its sleep—and *listened*.

*Who prays?*

The words were not words. They came up through her knees, through her bones, through the soles of her boots, in a voice like a mountain deciding to speak. Odile snatched her hands off the ewe and sat down hard in the grass.

The trembling stopped. The trough went quiet. The valley exhaled and became again what it had always been: dull, safe, silent, magic-dead Stillwater, where nothing answered prayers because nothing was awake to hear them.

Nothing was supposed to be awake to hear them.

The ewe stood up. All four legs, evenly, the lame hind planted square and strong. She took a step. Another. She looked back at Odile with mild golden eyes, and there was nothing wrong with her at all.

Odile sat in the grass and shook, and did not know whether she was terrified or triumphant, and suspected, with a sick lurch, that the two feelings were going to be twins for the rest of her life.

* * *

She told no one. Not Tam, not Mama, not Papa. Especially not the elders, whose idea of mercy was a quiet cart ride to the valley's edge and a warning never to come back.

But she could not stop thinking about it through the evening meal, through the washing up, through the long grey dusk while Mama spun and Papa whittled and Tam argued cheerfully with both of them about whether the south field should go to turnips or beans. Something had listened. In Stillwater, where nothing woke, something had woken. And it had asked *who prays*, in a voice that sounded less like a god than like a creditor.

When the dishes were done, Odile climbed the ladder to the loft where Grandmother's things were stored in a cedar chest, because death-day was also the one night a year the chest could be opened. That rule had been drilled into her too. *Once a year, on my death-day, open the chest and take out what you need. Nothing more. Nothing sooner.* Grandmother had never explained that either. Grandmother had explained almost nothing, which Odile was beginning to understand was its own kind of teaching.

The chest smelled of cedar and dried lavender and, underneath, faintly, of smoke. Inside lay Grandmother's good cloak, folded. Her bundle of letters, tied with string. A bone whistle, pale as a finger, which Odile lifted and set aside without knowing why. A stack of herbals and almanacs. And at the very bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, a book she had never seen before.

It was a ledger. Real ledger-leather, real brass corners, the kind merchants kept. Odile frowned. The Brightwaters were farmers three generations deep; they had never had accounts worth keeping.

She unwrapped it and opened it by candlelight, and the first page stopped her breath.

Thirteen columns ran across the page, ruled in faded ink, headed by thirteen small drawn moons—one for each moon of the doorpost, one for each year of the ritual. But it was the writing beneath that made her hands go cold. It was Grandmother's hand, unmistakably, the sharp slanted script Odile knew from the margins of every herbal in the house. Except the dates were impossible. The earliest entry predated Grandmother's birth by sixty years.

*The debt passes down,* the page read. *One soul per generation of keepers, held in reserve, called due when the Thirteenth Moon completes its turn. Paid once, in my great-grandmother's time, in coin of blood. Deferred twice, in my mother's time and mine, by payment of memory. The court beneath the mountain does not forgive. The court beneath the mountain waits.*

Odile turned pages with fingers that did not feel like hers. Entries in different hands—her great-grandmother's cramped and angry, her grandmother's careful, her own grandmother's—the same account kept alive across a century like a coal passed hand to hand. Payments listed. Deferrals listed. Interest, if souls could bear interest, accruing in a column marked only with a symbol Odile didn't recognize: a circle with a door in it.

And on the last written page, dated the winter before Grandmother died, a single entry in her strongest, steadiest script:

*The turn completes soon. I bought the valley forty years with silence and forgetting, and the bill has found me anyway. If I cannot pay, it falls to the next Brightwater. God forgive me. God forgive me. I chose the valley over my own blood and I would choose it again, and that is the worst thing about me.*

Below it, smaller, as if added later in a weaker hand:

*Do not let it be Tam.*

Odile sat in the loft with the ledger open on her knees and the candle guttering, and she understood, in one long falling moment, why the ritual ended with *be paid*. Why Grandmother had gripped her hands that last winter. Why the valley was silent, why magic smoldered under a jar, why the elders repeated *safe* like a spell. The silence wasn't the valley's nature. It was a purchase. Her family had bought Stillwater's safety, generations ago, from something beneath the mountain—and the price had never finished being paid.

The thirteenth moon completed its turn at midwinter. That was five weeks away.

*Tam,* she thought. *They're going to take Tam.*

* * *

She did not sleep. She lay in her bed listening to the house settle, to Tam's landslide snore through the wall, to the wind combing the treeline at the valley's edge, and somewhere past midnight she finally drifted into a shallow, splintery doze.

She woke to the sound of the front door opening.

For a moment she lay still, telling herself it was the wind, the latch, the house dreaming. Then she heard footsteps—slow, even, bare feet on floorboards—and she was up and at the loft rail before her mind caught up with her body.

Tam was walking down the stairs.

His eyes were open. That was the detail that would live in her nightmares afterward: he was not stumbling, not blind, not flailing like a sleepwalker in a story. He walked the way a servant walks who knows the route by heart. His arms hung loose. His face was smooth and empty as an unbaked loaf.

And his eyes were silver. Not catching the light—*giving* it, a pale moonlit gleam with no lamp in the room to explain it.

"Tam?" she whispered.

He did not answer. He opened the front door—he had unlatched it without a sound—and stepped out into the frozen yard, and Odile came down the ladder fast enough to skin her shin and did not care, and followed him.

Frost silvered the grass. The thirteen moons on the doorpost caught the starlight. Ahead of her brother walked, unhurried, straight-backed, toward the treeline at the valley's edge—a quarter mile of open field between the house and the dark trees, and he covered it steadily, and she ran and caught his arm and he did not stop, did not turn, simply kept walking and dragged her along like a boat dragging its anchor.

"TAM." She dug her heels in. "Tam, wake up, wake up, it's me, it's Odile—"

His head turned. Slowly. The silver eyes fixed on her, and his mouth moved, and the voice that came out was his voice and not his voice, flattened and distant, like speech heard through a wall of ice.

*"The account stands,"* he said. *"The turn comes. One soul is owed."*

Then he blinked, and shuddered, and his knees buckled, and Odile caught him as he collapsed, all his weight suddenly real and human and heavy, his skin freezing cold, his breath coming in ragged gasps.

"Odie?" His voice was his own again, small and confused. "What—why are we outside? Why am I—" He looked at the treeline, close enough now to see the individual black trunks, and something in his face went white and young. "Why was I walking there?"

"You were asleep," Odile said. Her teeth were chattering and not entirely from the cold. "You were sleepwalking."

"I don't sleepwalk."

"I know," she said.

She helped him stand. Together they turned back toward the house, toward the lamplight in the window where Mama had risen and was calling their names, frightened. Behind them the treeline stood silent and dense and patient, and Odile felt it watching the way the thing beneath the paddock had listened—something old, something waiting, something that had already begun to count down.

Five weeks until midwinter. Five weeks until the thirteenth moon completed its turn.

She had a ledger hidden under her mattress, a bone whistle in her pocket that she could not explain, and a brother who was already being collected.

Whatever came next, it would not wait for her to be ready.
