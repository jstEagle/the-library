## 4. Leaving Stillwater

The house kept its secrets the way the valley kept its silence: carefully, and for so long that no one remembered there had ever been another way to live.

Odile learned where they were hidden before she was old enough to understand why they needed hiding. Grandmother Iva had shown her once, on a night of rain, tapping the third floorboard from the hearth with her cane and saying only, *When it's time, you'll know.* Then she had looked at Odile a moment too long, the way she sometimes did, as if reading something written just under the skin.

It was time now. Tam had been gone three nights, and the house had stopped pretending he was merely sleeping late.

She waited until their mother's breathing went slow and even behind the thin wall, until the lamp in the front room burned down to its last finger of oil. Then she knelt at the hearth, worked her fingernails under the third board, and lifted.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, lay three things.

The ledger first — a book bound in grey leather gone soft as skin, its pages crowded with their grandmother's small, slanted hand. Odile had read it twice since the death-day ritual, by candlelight, with the door barred. Most of it was accounts: grain sold, wool weighed, candles rendered. Ordinary life, recorded with a merchant's precision. But threaded through the ordinary ran a second ledger, written in the margins, in ink that shifted color when she wasn't looking directly at it. *One soul owed to the court beneath the mountain,* it said, over and over, in variations. *Due when the thirteenth moon completes its turn.* And beneath the last entry, fresher than the rest, as if the ink were still deciding whether to dry: *Tam Brightwater. Claimed.*

She wrapped it again and set it inside her coat, against her ribs, where she could feel it like a second heartbeat.

Beside where the ledger had lain was the whistle. Bone, pale as milk, no longer than her thumb, carved with spirals so fine she could feel them but never quite see them whole. There was no note explaining it. There never was, with Grandmother Iva. Only the way she had held it, once, when Odile was small — not offering it, exactly. Showing her that it existed. *For calling things that have ears in strange places,* she'd said, and put it away again, and never mentioned it in daylight for the rest of her life.

Odile took it.

Last, folded flat and smelling faintly of cedar and thirty years of the chest it lived in: Tam's baby blanket. Their mother had kept it because mothers keep such things; Odile took it because of what the wolf had said, standing at the treeline with his scarred face half-turned toward her, his voice like gravel rolled smooth by centuries of water.

*Souls leave threads,* Ashen had told her. *The court cuts them clean. But a thread holds better to cloth that knew him before he knew himself.*

She didn't fully understand it. She understood enough.

She packed in silence: bread wrapped in cloth, a knife, flint, the good wool socks their mother knitted every autumn without ever quite admitting whom they were for. She wrote no note. A note would be a confession, and a confession would bring their mother running into the dark after her, and one soul owed to the mountain was already one too many. If Odile vanished quietly, the valley would do what the valley always did. It would forget. It was very good at forgetting. It had been built to forget.

That was the part that made her hands shake as she laced her boots. Not the leaving. The knowing that the leaving would work.

* * *

She went to the sheep pen first.

The ewe — the lame one, the one whose name she'd never spoken aloud because names given freely could be heard by things that traded in them — stood at the rail as if she'd been waiting. Her bad leg was better. Three days of whispered comfort and the ewe's own stubborn will had done what the valley's silence should have prevented, and Odile tried not to think about what that meant: that even here, smothered under the jar-lid of Stillwater, her gift leaked through like light around a shutter's edge.

"I'm going," she murmured into the coarse wool at the ewe's neck. "I don't know when I'm coming back."

The ewe turned her amber eye and regarded her with the flat, ancient patience of sheep, which is either nothing at all or everything, depending on the day.

"They say the wild country eats girls who wander." Odile managed something that was almost a laugh. "They say it about boys too. They say it about anyone who looks at the boundary stones too long." She scratched behind the ewe's ear, where the skin was thin and warm. "They're right, probably. Probably it does eat girls like me. But it has my brother, and I find I mind that more than I mind being eaten."

The ewe leaned into her hand. That was all. It was enough.

She left the pen gate latched. Whatever else happened, the flock would need tending, and their mother would need the small mercy of chores that continued as though the world hadn't tilted.

* * *

The boundary stones stood at the valley's eastern mouth, where the river narrowed between two shoulders of granite and the road became, officially, no road at all. Seven stones, each taller than a man, each worn featureless by weather that somehow never seemed to touch anything else in Stillwater quite so thoroughly. The elders called them the Watchers. Children were taught to spit — discreetly, politely — when passing them, an old gesture nobody could explain anymore.

Odile had passed them a hundred times. To market in Harrow's Bend, to the high pasture, once, memorably, to retrieve a runaway ram. Every time, she had felt the same thing: a softening at the edges of her senses, like going briefly deaf in one ear. The world beyond the stones was muffled, gray, uninteresting. Everyone felt it. That was the point. That was the bargain, though none of them knew they'd inherited it — the valley's silence, bought and paid for, generations ago, with a debt that had come due in the shape of her brother walking silver-eyed into the trees.

Now she stood before the stones with a ledger against her ribs and a bone whistle in her pocket and dawn still an hour off, and she let herself feel, for one long breath, how much she did not want to do this.

Because here was the truth she'd never said aloud, not even to herself, not even in the dark of the barn with the ewe's warm flank against her cheek: the silence had been kind to her. Terrible and suffocating, yes — a jar over a candle — but kind. In Stillwater, her gift was a stunted thing, a whisper where it should have been a voice, and that whisper had been easy to hide. Easy to deny. She had spent twenty years telling herself she was almost normal, nearly ordinary, a girl with an odd fondness for animals and nothing more, and the valley had agreed with her, had wrapped the lie around her like the blanket now folded in her pack. Safe. Invisible. Small.

Beyond those stones, she would be none of those things. Beyond those stones, every beast for fifty miles might hear her coming like a bell through fog. And worse — the things that weren't beasts. The tithe-collectors. The gods. The court beneath the mountain, which had already taken one Brightwater and would surely be interested in the other.

"You could turn back," she said to herself, and her breath hung white in the cold. "You could go home and bar the door and grow old teaching yourself not to listen."

But Tam was walking somewhere hollow-eyed, pouring wine for hungry gods, wearing his body like a coat someone else had buttoned for him. And she had seen his face in the ledger's shifting ink, or dreamed she had, and either way it was the same face he'd made at six years old when their father died — the face of someone waiting for the world to make sense again and slowly understanding it wouldn't.

No one was coming to make sense of it for him. So it would be her.

"Right," she said, and stepped forward.

* * *

The stones did not stop her. She had half-expected them to — some wall of force, some elder's voice crying out across the fields. But they were only stones, wet with pre-dawn frost, and she walked between the second and third as she had a hundred times before, and then she was past them, and then—

The world opened.

There was no gentler word for it. No gradual waking. One step she was in the valley's hush, and the next, magic came through her like a river through a broken dam, and Odile dropped to her knees on the frozen mud because her legs simply declined to hold her up.

It was everywhere. It was *in everything*. The grass under her hands sang — actually sang, a thin green note of growing, of reaching, of the blind dumb joy of roots in darkness. The river roared not with sound but with *presence*, a vast cold attention that noticed her crossing and found her interesting. The wind carried voices the way water carries silt: fragments of argument from the crows in the pines, a complaint from the fox denned under the far bank, something older and stranger moving in the deep water that she chose, firmly, not to listen to yet. Above it all, the sky itself — the sky *hummed*, low and enormous, a chord sustained since before the valley was founded, and Odile realized with a lurch that this was what prayer sounded like from the outside. Centuries of it. The whole wild country was soaked in it, marinated, alive.

And underneath the hum, fainter, from the direction of the mountain: hunger. A long, patient, hollow ache, like a stomach that had forgotten food but not the wanting.

She vomited, neatly, into the grass, and lay back gasping among the singing blades, and started, helplessly, to laugh.

Because it was glorious. That was the unbearable thing. All those years in the jar, and she had told herself the dark was comfortable, and it had been, the way a numb limb is comfortable — and now the blood was rushing back in and it *hurt*, it hurt like fire, and it was the most magnificent pain of her life. Every sense she'd owned was a keyhole, and someone had just taken the entire door off its hinges. She could hear the worms turning the soil. She could feel the frost deciding, molecule by molecule, whether to become dew. Somewhere east, a hawk woke and thought, in its fierce simple grammar, *morning, hunting, mine*, and the thought landed in her mind as clean as a struck bell.

This was her inheritance. Not the silence. This.

"Oh," she said aloud, to no one, to everything. "Oh, you *lied* to us. All of you. You lied about what the world was."

The grass rustled agreement, or sympathy, or possibly just wind. Hard to say. Everything out here had opinions.

She got up slowly, wiping her mouth, shaking, grinning like a madwoman, tears freezing on her cheeks — exhilaration and grief arriving together, inseparable, twins. Because the same flood that filled her also carried the knowledge of what it had cost. Her grandmother had known this. Had stood on this side of these stones with this roar in her ears, and had chosen — or been forced — to go back into the quiet and raise children there and bury a husband there and die there, keeping the secret, paying the interest on a debt Odile was only beginning to understand. What must it have cost her, to know the world sang and to spend her life in a room with the door shut?

*I'm sorry,* Odile told her silently, one hand flat on the ledger through her coat. *And I'm angry at you. Both. I expect I'll be both for a long time.*

Then she squared the pack on her shoulders and turned east, toward the Graywater, toward the burnt country beyond it, toward the mountain sitting on the horizon like a closed fist.

* * *

She made four miles before the sun cleared the ridgeline, and it was the strangest four miles of her life, because she was learning, minute by minute, to walk inside the noise. The trick, she discovered, was not to shut it out — she couldn't, any more than a fish could shut out water — but to stop gripping it. When she relaxed, the voices settled into a kind of weather around her instead of a storm through her. Crows arguing over a dead hare. A squirrel's monologue of grievances. The river's vast indifferent attention, already losing interest in her. She catalogued them the way she'd once catalogued the valley's few permitted sounds, and found, to her surprise, that she was good at it. Twenty years of listening through a keyhole had trained her ear.

The wolf found her at the ford.

She didn't hear him approach — which frightened her more than his appearance, because by now she should have heard *everything* — and then he was simply there at the water's edge, grey and scarred, watching her with eyes the color of old iron.

"You're loud," Ashen said.

"I'm—" She gestured helplessly at the world. "Everything is loud. How do you stand it?"

"Centuries." He waded into the shallows without waiting for her, and the current broke around his legs with unusual deference, she thought, or maybe she was imagining things. "You'll learn to fold it small. Your grandmother could walk through a forest of talking things and pass for deaf. It's a discipline. We'll begin today."

"We?" She crossed the ford beside him, gasping at the cold. "You're coming, then. You didn't say you were coming."

He gave her a look that contained, compressed into a single canine expression, several decades of exasperation. "I've waited forty years for a Brightwater to ask for my help. Do you imagine I'd miss the asking?"

"No," she admitted. "But I'm glad you said it anyway."

Something moved behind his eyes — grief, maybe, or its fossil record — and then he turned downstream and trotted along the bank, and she followed, because following a wolf who had loved her grandmother felt, absurdly, like the safest thing left in the world.

They had gone perhaps another mile when Ashen stopped so suddenly she nearly trod on his tail.

His hackles rose along his spine like a row of drawn knives. His nose swung north, toward the high ground above the river, and his lips peeled back from his teeth in a snarl so old and practiced it seemed to belong to the landscape itself.

"What?" Odile whispered. She reached for the bone whistle in her pocket and didn't take it out. "What is it?"

"Smoke," Ashen said softly. "On the wind, from the ridge. Woodsmoke, and under it—" His nostrils flared. "Under it, nothing. A smell like a room where a fire has gone out. Like a person-shaped absence." He looked back at her, and for the first time since the treeline, the wolf looked afraid. "Tithe-collector, girl. It's crossed your trail already. It knows a Brightwater is out of the valley."

Far off, on the ridge, a thread of smoke rose straight into the morning air.

Ashen turned east. "Run."
