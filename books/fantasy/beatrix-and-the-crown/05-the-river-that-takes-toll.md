## 5. The River That Takes Toll

The Graywater announced itself before we saw it.

For two days the land had been tilting downhill, the burnt road giving way to meadow gone wild and waist-high, and then the sound arrived—a low, ceaseless hush, like a crowd whispering behind a wall. Ashen stopped at the crest of a hill and looked back at me with that flat yellow patience of his.

"The crossing," he said. "We reach it by midday. There are things you should know before we do."

I had learned in two days of walking that Ashen did not waste words. He spoke the way a knife cuts—only when there was something to divide.

"Tell me," I said.

"Rivers are older than gods." He turned and started down the slope, and I had to hurry to keep up. "Older than the court beneath the mountain, older than the names your grandmother kept in her ledger. A river does not care what you are or why you've come. It only cares what you carry."

"I don't have anything to pay a toll with," I said. "I took bread from the last farmstead. Half of it's left."

"Bread is for beggars and kings," Ashen said. "The Graywater takes memory. True memory. Something you lived, not something you were told. And it will know if you try to cheat it."

My stomach went cold, colder than the morning fog still clinging to the meadow grass. "You mean it eats remembering?"

"I mean it drinks it," Ashen said. "There's a difference. What you give it isn't destroyed. It's *kept*. Somewhere in the water, in the silt, in the long green dark under the current. You'll lose it the way you lose a tooth—you'll feel the gap every time your tongue goes looking."

I walked in silence for a while, turning this over. The meadow sloped away below us, silvered with dew, and somewhere down there the whispering grew louder.

"What did you pay?" I asked.

Ashen's ears flicked backward toward me, the only sign I'd surprised him. "A river crossing is for the living," he said finally. "I go where you go. But I crossed the Graywater once, long ago, carrying a message for a woman who would not be refused." He said nothing more about it, and I had learned not to press him. His silences had weight, like stones at the bottom of a well; you could drop questions into them all day and never hear them land.

* * *

We came upon the river through a stand of alders, and my first thought was that it was too small for its voice.

The Graywater ran perhaps thirty feet bank to bank, shallow-looking over pale gravel, moving fast but not violently. Yet the sound of it filled the whole valley of the air—the whispering I'd heard from the hilltop resolved now into ten thousand small voices braided together, none of them quite words, all of them almost words. Like standing inside a seashell the size of the sky.

And the color. I understood the name then. The water wasn't gray the way ash is gray. It was gray the way a mirror is gray when no one stands before it—every color held in suspension, waiting, reflecting nothing because it hadn't yet decided what it wanted to show you.

"There's no bridge," I said.

"There's a ferryman," Ashen said. "Of a kind. She'll come when she smells a traveler. Listen to me now, Odile Brightwater, because after this moment I can't help you." He sat on his haunches at the treeline, and his scarred face was very grave. "She will ask for a toll. Give her something true, but do not give her everything. Do not give her the first thing she asks for, and do not let her choose for you. Choose yourself, quickly, before she chooses. Do you understand?"

"Why does it matter which memory? They're just—they're just memories."

Ashen looked at me for a long moment. In the shade of the alders his eyes caught the light strangely, gold going green going gold.

"When you're finished paying," he said, "you will not remember what you paid. But everyone else who remembers it will remember that *you* don't. Your mother will speak of things you cannot find. Gaps have shapes, Odile. Choose a small shape."

Before I could answer, the river changed.

It happened between one blink and the next: the surface stilled, the whispering hushed, and out of the middle of the current rose a figure, unfolding upward like smoke deciding to be a woman. She was tall and thin as a heron, dressed in layers of wet silk the color of the water, and her hair moved around her shoulders as though she stood at the bottom of a waterfall instead of atop a calm one. Her face was beautiful the way weather is beautiful—impressive, indifferent, liable to kill you without malice.

"Well," she said, and her voice was the whispering, all ten thousand voices speaking in unison. "A Brightwater. At *my* crossing."

"You know my name?" My hand found the bone whistle in my pocket without my meaning it to.

"I know the taste of your family on the water." She drifted closer—not walking, the current simply delivering her, the way a leaf is delivered. "Your grandmother crossed here four times. Paid each time. Good coin, too." Her eyes were the gray of the river, and they had no bottoms. "What do you carry, little one? Let me see."

"No," I said, and my voice cracked on it, so I said it again, steadier. "No. I'll pay a toll. One toll. But I choose what."

Something moved across the spirit's face that might have been a smile on a face that had never needed one. "Ohh. Grandmother taught you that much, did she? Very well. Show me what you have."

I closed my eyes and opened my memory like a peddler opening a pack, and immediately understood Ashen's warning, because the river-spirit was *in* there, looking, the way you look through a window into a lit house from the dark outside. I felt her attention slide past my father's hands mending a net, past Tam teaching me to whistle through a blade of grass, past the ewe-lamb born in the storm—

*That one,* the whispering voices said, sweet as honey poured into an ear. *The brother. The blanket. The night he fell asleep against your shoulder and you counted his breaths until yours matched. Give us that.*

My whole body flinched. My hands closed into fists. Because that memory was warm, was *alive*, was the thing I'd been feeding on since the day Tam's eyes went silver and he stopped being my brother—and the spirit knew it, knew exactly what it was offering to take, the richest coin in my purse.

*I said I choose,* I thought, hard, and slammed the pack shut on that memory like a lid.

Then, before she could push, I grabbed the nearest thing—recklessly, desperately, the way you grab whatever's at hand when the wolf is at the door.

The taste of my mother's bread.

Not the bread itself, not the kitchen, not even my mother's face—but the taste. That specific taste: rye and caraway, the crust that cracked like ice on a puddle, the faint sweetness of the starter she'd kept alive in a crock by the hearth for as long as I'd been alive, longer. The taste of the heel of the loaf torn off while it was still too hot, butter sinking into the steam. I had eaten that bread ten thousand mornings. I gave her all ten thousand at once.

"It's done," I said aloud, and my voice shook. "Take it."

The river exhaled.

I felt the memory leave me the way Ashen had described—not torn, but *drunk*, drawn up smoothly like water through a root. And then it was simply gone. I reached for the taste of my mother's bread and found a smooth blank place where it had been, a gap with a shape, exactly as promised. I knew I had loved it. I knew I would spend the rest of my life knowing I had loved something I could no longer taste. Somewhere in Stillwater my mother was baking loaves right now, and I would never again know what they were like.

Grief hit me sideways, sudden and stupid and enormous, over a *taste*, of all things, and I had to stand there with my eyes shut and breathe through it while the river-spirit watched with her bottomless gray eyes.

"Mmm," she said at last, and the whispering voices curled around the sound like cats. "Warm bread. There hasn't been warm bread on this water in forty years." She drifted a half-circle around me, considering. "You paid clean, Brightwater. No cheating, no clutching. Most humans fight me and lose something worse for it." The current set her drifting nearer, near enough that I could smell the river on her—stone and weed and deep cold places. "So. A gift for good coin, as is proper. Ask."

"Ashen said—" I began.

"Ashen," the spirit repeated, and all ten thousand voices went quiet for a heartbeat, and the river itself seemed to lean in. Then the whispering resumed, softer. "Yes. He would say that. Ask, child. The Graywater keeps everything ever given to it, and some of what it keeps is knowledge."

I glanced at Ashen. He sat motionless at the treeline, watching, and gave me the smallest dip of his scarred head.

"The court beneath the mountain," I said. "Tam's soul is owed there. Tell me how to get it back."

The spirit laughed, and the laugh was terrible—it was every voice that had ever whispered along this water, laughing together, and the alders shivered though there was no wind.

"Oh, little one. Oh, you sweet, doomed thing." She bent close, and her breath smelled like snowmelt. "Listen, then, since you paid clean. The court beneath the mountain trades in souls the way men trade in salt—because souls keep, and souls are wanted, and there is always someone desperate enough to sell. But salt is only salt. A soul is a *name*. When they take your brother's soul, they take the name he answers to, the name his own heart calls him by. He will walk and eat and sleep, hollowed, until someone gives him a new name—or until his old one is returned."

"How do I return it?" My voice came out small.

"That," said the river-spirit, "is not mine to tell. But I will tell you this, freely, because you amused me." Her gray eyes fixed on mine, and for one instant I saw something in their depths that was not indifference—something old and tired and almost pitying. "The court is starving. The old prayers have stopped coming, and gods cannot live on what mortals have forgotten to give. A starving god will bargain harder than any merchant—and break bargains easier, too. Whatever price you offer beneath that mountain, child, understand that hunger makes poor accountants." She straightened, already thinning, already becoming smoke again. "Now go. Cross while the water remembers your payment kindly."

"One more thing," I said quickly. "My grandmother. Beatrix. Did she—"

But the river was already moving again, the figure dissolving into the current, and the last of the ten thousand voices scattered across the water like a handful of thrown coins:

"*Ask the wolf.*"

* * *

The crossing itself was almost gentle. The water parted around our knees with a deference I didn't trust, and the far bank received us onto soft mud printed everywhere with tracks—heron, deer, fox, and one set of prints like a woman's bare feet, trailing out of the shallows and vanishing mid-stride.

I didn't speak for a long time. I couldn't. Every few minutes my mind reached automatically for the taste of my mother's bread and slid off the blank place where it had been, and each time it was like pressing a bruise to check if it still hurt. It always still hurt.

It was late afternoon when Ashen finally broke the silence.

"You chose well," he said.

"I chose *fast*," I said. "There's a difference."

"Fast is most of choosing well, at a river." He trotted ahead a few paces, then paused, waiting for me to draw level. "But you made a mistake."

The words landed like a slap. "Excuse me?"

"You showed her everything before you shut the lid. She saw the blanket. She saw Tam." His yellow eyes held mine, unblinking. "The Graywater keeps what it's paid, but spirits talk, Odile. River to rain to root to beast. By tomorrow, everything between here and the mountain will know what you carry and what you'd pay to protect it. You are a purse walking through a world of thieves, and today you showed them where the heavy coins sit."

I stopped walking. The meadow stretched empty and golden around us, harmless-seeming, and suddenly it felt like a room full of hidden listeners.

"Then teach me," I said. "You said you'd teach me to shield it. On the road you said—" I fumbled for it. "You said my grandmother made you promise. Well, I'm asking. Teach me now."

Ashen sat. For a long moment he only looked at me, and I had the strange sense of a door being considered from both sides.

"Close your eyes," he said at last. "Feel your gift. Where does it live in you?"

I closed my eyes. This part was easy; I'd been doing it secretly my whole life. The gift lived behind my breastbone, or felt like it did—a warm place, a listening place, the spot that woke when the ewe limped to me, the spot that had heard the ground answer my prayer. I found it and held it gently in my attention.

"That warmth," Ashen said. "That is a lit candle in a dark field. Every hungry thing for fifty miles can see it. Now—do not put the candle out. That's the mistake your valley made, smothering magic until it died. Instead, build a lantern around it. Walls with a door you control. Can you feel the edges of the warmth? Where it stops?"

"Yes."

"Pull those edges inward. Make the warmth smaller. Not gone—*smaller*. A candle seen through a keyhole looks like nothing at all."

I pulled. It was harder than it sounded, like trying to cup water with bare hands—the warmth kept spilling out past my grip, leaking, glowing. Sweat started at my hairline. Twice the warmth flared instead of shrinking, and I heard Ashen make a low warning sound in his throat.

"Steady. Smaller. Like hiding a coal under ash—not dead, just dim."

Under ash. That image helped. I thought of the banked hearth at home, the coals at dawn, gray on the outside and alive underneath, ready to breathe into flame with one puff of bellows. I drew my gift down the way my mother banked her fire every night, and slowly, slowly, the warmth contracted to a pinpoint, a secret, a coal under ash.

The relief was immediate and disorienting—like taking off a pack I hadn't known I was wearing. All my life I had been *visible*, I realized. Even in the valley, even hidden, I had glowed. And out here, in a country full of tithe-collectors and debt-gods and rivers that drank memories—

"Good," Ashen said quietly. "Hold it. Hold it while you walk. Hold it while you sleep. It will slip—that's expected. When it slips, you'll feel cold, like a draft. Bank it again." He rose and shook himself, scars rippling along his flank. "Do it ten thousand times and it becomes breathing."

I opened my eyes. The golden meadow looked the same, but I felt different inside—smaller and safer and stranger, a lantern instead of a bonfire.

"Ashen," I said. "The spirit said to ask you about my grandmother. About Beatrix."

The wolf went very still. A pheasant called somewhere in the alders behind us; otherwise the world held its breath.

"She crossed this river four times," I pressed. "She paid clean, the spirit said. And the spirit knew your name, Ashen. Knew it the way you know a stone you've tripped on more than once." I took a step toward him. "You said you'd tell me when I needed to know. I'm telling you: I need to know. My brother's soul is sitting under a mountain wearing a hole where his name used to be, and everyone I meet seems to already know the story except me."

Ashen looked east, toward the mountains I could barely see, blue smudges on the horizon. When he spoke, his voice was low and rough, like something dragged a long distance.

"Not here," he said. "Not in open country, with the light failing. But soon, Odile Brightwater, I will tell you what I promised her I'd tell—and you may wish I'd stayed silent another forty years." He turned and started toward the treeline, then paused. "Because the story doesn't begin with a bargain your grandmother made."

He looked back at me, and his yellow eyes were old, old, old.

"It begins with the soul she stole."
