## 6. Beasts of the Burnt Road

The road out of the Graywater country was not so much a road as a scar that had forgotten how to heal.

Odile had seen burnt ground before—kitchen fires, lightning-struck oaks—but nothing like this. For two days the land on either side of the way had been black and glassy, as if something enormous had knelt here and breathed. Trees stood in rows like the charred ribs of some beast that had died standing. The soil was grey ash packed hard by rain, and where the wheel ruts cut deep, she could see the old road beneath: pale stone, older than the valley, older than anything her grandmother's ledger described.

Nobody had come to rebuild. That was the thing that frightened her most, more than the burn itself. In Stillwater, when the mill bridge flooded, half the village turned out with timber before the water even dropped. Here, whole villages had burned and no one had so much as stacked the stones.

"They don't come back," Ashen said, reading her face. He walked ahead of her, grey against grey, and only his eyes moved. "Burnt Road runs from the mountain's foot to the sea. Armies used it. Gods used it. Now it belongs to whatever is strong enough to hold a piece of it."

"And what holds this piece?"

"You'll see."

She saw at dusk.

They made camp in the shell of a waystation, its roof open to the sky, its hearth still holding a bed of coals that no one had tended. Odile stared at those coals for a long time before she understood what she was looking at. They weren't dying. They were waiting. And around them, settling onto the broken rafters one by one, came the crows.

There were dozens of them. Then scores. They arrived without a single caw, folding down through the smoke-colored air like scraps of night being sewn back together, and they arranged themselves along the rafters in a silence that felt deliberate, courteous, and utterly wrong.

"Don't run," Ashen said quietly. "And don't offer them anything you'd miss."

One crow dropped from the rafter and landed in the coals. It did not flinch. It turned its head—that quick, hinged, mechanical tilt—and looked at Odile with an eye like wet obsidian, and inside that eye she saw herself reflected very small, and beside her reflection she saw something else: a shape of silver light, curled up small, like a coin of moonlight wrapped in wings.

*Her gift.* The crow could see her gift.

"Hello," Odile said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "You can see it."

*We see everything on the Burnt Road,* said the crow, and its voice was not sound so much as pressure behind her eyes, a voice made of remembered arguments and the smell of cold iron. *We are the road's memory. Everything that happens here, we keep.*

"I'm not from here."

*No,* the crow agreed, with something that might have been amusement. *You are from the jar. The quiet place. The place where prayers go to die against the stones.* It hopped closer, claws clicking on hearthstone. *But your prayer didn't die, little keeper-of-beasts. We heard it. We heard it echo.*

Odile's mouth went dry. "That was weeks ago. I only said—"

*A prayer is a prayer,* the crow said. *It doesn't matter how small the voice is if the listening things have grown hungry enough.* It cocked its head the other way. *You want news. Everyone who comes up the Burnt Road wants news. We trade.*

"With what?"

*Shin-bones.* The crow's eye glittered. *Left or right, we're not particular. Fresh ones preferred, but we are not proud. Grief has made this whole road flexible.*

"That's—" She stopped. Looked at the rafters full of patient black shapes. Looked at Ashen, who gave her a look back that said, plainly: *I told you not to offer anything you'd miss.*

"What do I get," Odile asked slowly, "for one shin-bone?"

*News of the waking,* said the crow, and every bird on every rafter leaned forward at once, a hundred heads tilting in unison, and the temperature of the room seemed to drop. *News of why the old gods are opening their eyes after three hundred years of sleep. News of the court beneath the mountain, which has begun to send its collectors out again. News of the thirteenth moon, which is coming faster than any almanac says it should.*

Odile thought of Tam, walking into the treeline with silver eyes. Thought of the ledger's neat, patient arithmetic. One soul, owed.

"All right," she said. "One bone."

She limped to the corner of the waystation where she'd left her pack, and dug out the bundle of dried meat, the flint, the coil of rope—and beneath all of it, wrapped in linen, the bone whistle from her grandmother's box. Beside it, she found what she was looking for: the shank-bone of a mutton joint, saved from her last meal in the last inn, because her grandmother had taught her never to travel without a bone in her pocket. *Bones remember the animal they belonged to,* she used to say. *And animals remember everything else.*

It wasn't a shin-bone. It was close enough.

The crow took it from her hand with terrible gentleness, beak barely grazing her skin, and carried it to the hearth, and set it down among the coals. The other crows watched. The bone began, very softly, to sing—a high, thin note like wind over a bottle mouth—and Odile realized with a lurch that she could feel it singing, feel the small ghost of the sheep it had belonged to, feel the dumb contentment of a creature that had never once wondered about anything beyond the next tuft of grass.

*Accepted,* said the first crow. *Now listen, because we say this once.*

* *

The news came not as speech but as images pressed directly into her mind, each one arriving with the weight and texture of memory, though none of them were hers:

A mountainside splitting open like a loaf, and light pouring out—not firelight, not sunlight, but a slow silver radiance, and shapes moving within it, tall and wrong, stretching limbs that had been folded for centuries.

A village kneeling in a wheat field, hundreds of them, faces lifted, mouths moving—and their prayers rising off them like heat-shimmer, visible now, tangible, streaming upward in ribbons of pale gold. And above them, something vast turning over in its sleep, beginning to notice.

A woman alone in a cellar, praying with such desperation that her words tore holes in the dark, and through the holes, things looked in.

*Three hundred years,* said the crows, all of them together now, the rafters thrumming with their joined voice. *For three hundred years the gods slept because no one prayed to them truly. Oh, people went through the motions—harvest rites, naming days, the little polite ceremonies. But polite prayer is thin broth. It does not reach the deep places. The gods starved and slept, and the world forgot them, and that was fine. That was safe.*

*But desperate prayer is different. Desperate prayer has teeth. War came to the Burnt Road, and plague, and famine, and people began to pray the old way again—the way you pray when there is nothing else left. Kneeling in burning fields. Screaming into cellars. And every desperate word fell like meat into dark water, and the old things began to wake, and stretch, and remember that they used to be fed.*

*Your valley kept its silence, little Brightwater. Your ancestors bought it. But silence cannot hold forever against a world that has started screaming. The prayers pass over your valley like weather. And the thirteenth moon is rising, and the court beneath the mountain has woken hungry, and it has sent out its collectors to gather what it is owed.*

*You are being hunted, Odile Brightwater. You have been hunted since the moment your prayer echoed.*

The images stopped. The crows settled back onto their rafters, folding their wings, becoming merely birds again—which was somehow worse.

"The collectors," Odile said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "What do they look like?"

*Like grief,* said the first crow. *Like someone you almost recognize. They walk upright and they smile correctly and their eyes are windows with nothing behind them. If you see one, run. Do not speak to it. Do not let it speak to you. Their voices open doors, and behind the doors is the mountain.*

Ashen rose to his feet, hackles lifting along his spine, ears swiveled toward the broken doorway of the waystation.

"It's too late for running advice," he said.

* *

Odile heard it before she understood it: footsteps on ash, unhurried, evenly spaced, coming up the road from the south. Not the gait of any traveler she'd ever known. Travelers tired. Travelers shifted their weight, favored a leg, varied their pace. These footsteps were identical, one to the next, like coins dropping.

Through the empty doorway, silhouetted against the last violet light, came a figure in a long coat the color of wet slate. It was human-shaped. It was even, at a distance, almost handsome, in the way a well-made chair is handsome. It walked to the threshold of the waystation and stopped, and smiled—a correct smile, calibrated, showing exactly the right number of teeth—and Odile looked into its face and felt her stomach drop through the floor of the world.

Because the face was almost familiar. It was assembled from familiar things: the set of her father's jaw, the gray of her mother's eyes, the particular way Tam's hair fell across his brow. All the beloved pieces, fitted together by something that had never loved anything, the way a magpie builds a nest out of stolen silver.

"Well," it said, and its voice was warm and reasonable and completely empty, like a hearth in a house where no one has lived for years. "Here you are. We've been looking everywhere, Odile Brightwater. The court does so dislike loose ends."

Every instinct screamed at her to answer—to apologize, to explain, to smooth things over the way she'd smoothed things over her whole life, the good girl, the invisible girl, the girl who knew how to make herself small and pleasant and safe.

Instead, she did the thing her whole life had trained her never to do.

She opened her gift.

She had spent seventeen years keeping it leashed, muffling it, tucking it away under her tongue like a coin she was ashamed to spend. Now she let it out all at once, wide and raw and blazing, and she threw it at the creatures of the Burnt Road like a net cast over a flock.

*Crows!* she called, in the speech-under-speech that needed no words. *The road's memory! You took my bone—you owe me passage! Every bird that flies, every wing that beats—I call it in!*

The rafters exploded.

A hundred crows came off the beams at once, a black detonation, and Odile flung herself sideways behind the fallen door of the waystation as the collector raised one slate-gray arm. Something passed through the space where she'd been standing—a pressure, a cold, a sense of a door swinging open onto a darkness with appetite in it—and struck the hearth instead. The coals went out. Not dimmed. *Out*, all of them, snuffed to black in a heartbeat, and frost bloomed across the hearthstones in feathered patterns.

The crows hit the collector like a storm of knives.

It staggered—it actually staggered, that perfect balanced gait breaking for the first time—as a hundred beaks and a hundred pairs of talons went for its eyes, its throat, the seams of its borrowed face. It made a sound then, and the sound was the worst thing yet: not pain but *inconvenience*, a thin note of bureaucratic annoyance, as though a clerk had found a smudge on a ledger page.

"Unnecessary," it said, batting birds out of the air, and each one it touched fell frozen, dropping from the sky like stones. "This is theft from the court, little beasts. This will be entered in the—"

Ashen hit it from the side.

She had never seen the wolf move that fast. He came out of the shadow of the doorway low and flat, all the centuries in him gathered into one line of muscle, and he took the collector's legs out from under it and they went down together into the ash, and Odile was already running, already scrambling through the broken back wall of the waystation, because the crows' voice was screaming in her skull—

*GO, girl, GO, north along the rut-line, don't stop, don't look back, we'll scatter and regather, you've paid, you've PAID—*

She ran.

She ran through the charred forest with thorns raking her arms and ash rising in clouds under her boots, and behind her she heard the collector's voice rise to something that was no longer trying to sound human—a flat, ringing tone like a bell struck in an empty church—and she heard Ashen snarl, heard the crackle of frost, heard wings, hundreds of wings, a whirlwind of black feathers and shrieking—

Then a hand closed on her collar and hauled her sideways off her feet, and she was tumbling down into a hollow beneath the roots of a dead oak, and Ashen was on top of her, panting, a rime of frost melting on his fur, one ear torn and bleeding.

"Breathe," he said. "Quietly."

Above them, muffled by earth and root, the bell-tone rang twice more. Footsteps passed—identical, patient, spaced like coins. Once. Twice. A third time, circling. Then, gradually, they faded northward, unhurried, unbothered, the way a creditor leaves a debtor's door knowing the debt isn't going anywhere.

Odile lay in the dirt with her heart slamming and became aware, distantly, that she was laughing. Small, shaky, disbelieving laughter, pressed between her teeth so it wouldn't carry.

"It knew my name," she whispered. "It had my family's face."

"It will always know your name now." Ashen lowered his head, and his remaining good ear flicked toward the road. "And it will tell the others. You've been entered in the ledger, Odile. There's no hiding after this—not for you, not anywhere."

She should have felt terror. She found, searching herself, that what she felt instead was something hotter and stranger: the memory of the moment she'd opened her gift and thrown it outward, the feeling of a hundred minds answering her call, the net of wings that had bought her life.

All her life she had treated the gift like a wound to be concealed. Tonight it had been a blade.

"They froze your crows," she said. "Some of them. The ones it touched."

"Some," Ashen agreed. "Crows keep count of debts better than gods do. The road's memory won't forget what that thing cost them." He looked at her sidelong, and there was something new in his ancient eyes—something that might, in a less guarded creature, have been approval. "You spent your gift like a weapon tonight. First time?"

"Yes."

"It showed." He rose, shook the ash from his coat, and nodded north, toward a horizon where the first stars were pricking through the smoke-haze. "Rest while you can. Tomorrow we cross the open country, and there's a market town two days on where the roads braid together. Towns mean thresholds. Thresholds mean—"

He stopped.

His head came up. Every muscle in his body went rigid, and Odile followed his gaze to the ridgeline above the hollow, where the last light was failing.

Standing against the sky, motionless, was a second figure in a slate-gray coat.

It was too far away to see its face. But as the darkness closed over the Burnt Road, Odile saw its eyes catch the starlight and throw it back—two points of silver, exactly the color of Tam's, the night he walked into the trees.

And it raised one hand, and beckoned.
