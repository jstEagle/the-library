## 7. The God of Small Debts

The town was called Brackenhollow, though the sign at its edge had been gnawed by weather until it read only *BRACKEN*, and Odile thought that was probably more honest. A town that had lost its hollow. A town that had lost whatever used to live inside it.

She had never seen so many people in her life.

Stillwater held perhaps two hundred souls, and she could have named every one of them, along with their dogs, their grudges, and which of their children had been born in which season. Brackenhollow spilled down a hillside like a dropped basket—hundreds of roofs, hundreds of chimneys bleeding smoke into the grey morning, hundreds of voices braiding together into a sound that wasn't words at all but simply *noise*, warm and overwhelming as a wool blanket thrown over her head.

She stood at the town's edge for a long moment, one hand buried in Ashen's ruff.

"They'll see you," she said.

"The market sees stranger things than a wolf." His voice was a low rumble she felt more than heard. "Meat-sellers keep half-tame wolves to impress buyers. Walk beside me like it's ordinary. Ordinary is the best disguise there is."

"Is that what we are? Disguised?"

"We're a girl buying provisions and her dog." His amber eye slid toward her. "Say nothing with your gift inside these walls unless I tell you otherwise. Towns have ears of their own, and some of them belong to things that wear the town like a coat."

She wanted to ask what that meant. She was learning that Ashen answered questions on his own schedule, and pushing him only made him go quiet as a stone. So she squared her shoulders, arranged her face into the blank politeness she'd worn her whole life in Stillwater—the face of a girl who noticed nothing—and walked into the noise.

* * *

The market square smelled of wet wool, frying onions, tallow, and under all of it something sweeter and stranger that Odile couldn't name until she realized: it was magic, thin and diluted, the way a river smells different after passing through a city. Magic that had been used and used again until it was tired.

She kept her eyes down and bought what they needed. Dried apples. Hard cheese. A twist of salt. She counted out coins her grandmother had sewn into a hem years before she died, coins that still smelled faintly of lavender and secrets, and the cheese-woman didn't look at her twice, and Odile began to think that maybe ordinary really was a coat that fit.

Then she saw the stall.

It sat at the mouth of an alley where no other seller would set up—a narrow table draped in green cloth gone grey at the folds, and on it, arranged with terrible neatness: keys. Dozens of them. Iron, brass, bone, one of glass. Keys to doors that had burned down centuries ago. Keys to doors that had never existed. And hanging from a nail above the table, a small wooden sign, hand-lettered:

*DEBTS RECORDED. THRESHOLDS MEASURED. FAIR RATES.*

There was no one behind the table. There was a stool, and on the stool, folded neatly like a coat someone had stepped out of, there was a shape of shadow shaped exactly like a small man.

Odile should have walked past. Every instinct Stillwater had ever taught her said *walk past, be invisible, be nothing.* But the glass key caught the light, and in its facets she saw—not reflections, but rooms. A nursery. A kitchen with bread rising under a cloth. Her own kitchen, in her own house, in a valley she'd left three days ago.

Her feet stopped. That was the mistake. In this country, she was learning, stopping was always the mistake.

"You're looking for a door," said a voice like a drawer sliding open.

The shadow on the stool unfolded upward and became a man—or the memory of one. He was small and neat, with a green coat buttoned to the throat and a face like a coin rubbed smooth: features present but worn away, as if he'd been handled too long by too many hands. Only his eyes were sharp, and they were the color of ledger ink.

"I'm not—" Odile began.

"You are." He smiled without any warmth reaching it. "Everyone who comes through Brackenhollow going north is looking for a door. The mountain has one, you know. Under the roots. Very hard to find without help." He laced his fingers together on the green cloth. "I sell help."

"I don't have money for help."

"Oh, I don't take money." The worn face creased with something almost tender. "Money is for people who have nothing better. I take *names*. Or memories, if the name doesn't suit. Or favors, if the memory is poor stock." He tilted his head. "You have a brother, I think. Lost recently. Soul-weight, not body-weight—he'll be walking around up there looking fine, eating supper, laughing at jokes, and none of it will be *him*. Am I close?"

The world went very still and very loud at once. Beside her thigh, Ashen's muscles bunched like coiled rope.

"How do you know that?" she whispered.

"It's my trade." He spread his hands, and she saw now that his palms were covered in fine lines of script, tiny letters running across the skin like watermarks. "Debts and thresholds, thresholds and debts. Every door in this country opens onto my books somewhere. And your family's account—" his ink-dark eyes glittered— "is one of my favorites. Generations deep. Interest compounding like frost."

"My grandmother's bargain," Odile said. "You know about it."

"I know about everything that crosses a threshold, girl. That's the whole of my divinity. Small, yes. Humble. But thorough." He leaned forward, and his voice dropped to the register of a secret shared between friends. "So here is my offer, freely made, fairly priced. I will tell you where your brother's soul sits tonight—which hall, which bench, whose hand holds its leash. Exact directions. Worth more than any map, because maps go stale and souls stay put when they're collateral. And in exchange you give me your name."

"My name?"

"Not the one your mother called you across the yard. Your *true* name. The one the valley smothered to keep you safe. The one that hums under your skin when you speak with beasts." He smiled again, and this time there was hunger in it, old and patient. "Names are nourishment, little sister. I haven't eaten well in decades."

The square went on around them—bargaining, laughter, a cart wheel squealing—but Odile felt as though she stood in a pocket of silence cut out of the world. She thought of Tam asleep in his bed with silver washing over his eyes. She thought of her mother setting a place for him at table out of habit, then covering it with her apron and pretending she hadn't.

"What happens," Odile asked slowly, "if I give it to you?"

"Then you won't have it anymore. You'd still answer to 'girl,' to 'Odile' if anyone shouts it, but the true name—the deep one—that comes home with me, and I keep it in my books, and I am *very* careful with my books." He tapped the table. "You'd feel lighter. Most do. Names are heavy things. That's why people give them away so cheaply."

"And what would you do with it?"

"Wear it, sometimes. On cold nights. A true name is warm." The god's worn face did something complicated—wistful, briefly, before smoothing back into commerce. "But mostly I'd hold it. Collateral appreciates. And you'd want it back someday, wouldn't you? Which means you'd owe me a favor to redeem it. And that favor would open another account, and that account another interest, and so on, world without end." He sighed happily. "It's a beautiful system. I invented it."

"Ashen." Odile didn't look away from the god. "What do I do?"

For a moment the wolf said nothing, and she felt his stillness change quality—from watchful to *weighing*. Then he stepped forward, past her hip, and set one scarred forepaw on the edge of the green cloth.

"Harrowgate," he said.

The god went absolutely still. Even the market noise seemed to bend around that word.

"That's not my name anymore," the god said quietly.

"It's the one I knew." Ashen's voice was flat as slate. "Three hundred years ago, maybe more. When you were a door-god with a proper door, and a proper town, and people who left offerings on the sill instead of haggling with you in markets."

The worn face flickered—grief, rage, shame, all crossing it like weather. "And where is that town now, wolf? Where is that door? Burned or forgotten, both. Gods don't die of forgetting, we just get *smaller*. I keep accounts now. It's honest work."

"It's scavenging."

"It's *surviving*." The ink-eyes blazed. Then, slowly, they narrowed. "Why do you speak to me as an equal, wolf? You know me. Few living things do." A pause, and Odile watched the god arrive at the answer the way one watches frost creep across a window. "You were hers. The Brightwater woman's. The witness." His gaze slid to Odile, and something in it recalculated entirely, the way a merchant reprices goods upon discovering they're rarer than believed. "Oh. Oh, this is *delicious*. The oath-bound beast, walking with the debt-holder's granddaughter. Tell me, does he know what he's carrying? Does he know what clause sleeps in his hide?"

"He knows," Ashen said, "what he owes *you*. Which is why we're leaving, Harrowgate, and why you're going to let us."

"Am I?" The god rose from the stool, and though he remained small, the alley behind him seemed to lean in, the shadows gathering like an audience. "Let's review the ledger, shall we? Three hundred years ago, a certain wolf came to a certain door-god on a winter night, carrying something wrapped in his jaws, and asked sanctuary for it. And I gave it. Freely. And such gifts create debts, wolf. Debts are *my* element. You've been carrying mine for three centuries, drawing interest the whole while, and I have never once called it due." He smiled, wide and worn. "Until now."

"What did he carry?" Odile asked.

"None of your—" the god began.

"A soul-token," Ashen said. His voice didn't change at all, but Odile heard something underneath it, something cracked. "A stolen soul-token. Hers. I carried it to him for hiding, because the court beneath the mountain was hunting it, and he hid it well enough that they never found it. That's the favor. That's the debt."

"And now," said the god of small debts, rubbing his scripted hands together, "now I call it. Here is my price, wolf, and hear me fairly: not the girl's name. I release her from that offer—freely, witnessed, binding. Instead, you will owe me one favor. One. To be named by me, at a time of my choosing, within the next year. Refuse, and the old debt stands called due in full, and I promise you, wolf, I have grown *creative* about collection since we last spoke."

Ashen was silent a long time. The market roared on, oblivious. Somewhere a child laughed, bright and mortal, and Odile thought absurdly of Tam laughing at her when they were small, when he'd put a frog down her back and run.

"One favor," Ashen said at last. "Named by you. Within the year. Witnessed by the girl."

"Witnessed by the girl," the god agreed, delighted. "Excellent. She has good ears—I can tell. Now." He turned to Odile, and his voice shifted into something brisker, professional, almost kind. "As a gesture of good faith between new partners: your brother's soul is held in the Weighing Hall, third gallery, in the keeping of a tithe-collector with a burnt face. It will remain there until the thirteenth moon completes its turn, at which point it gets weighed against the original debt and consumed, most likely, because the court is starving and soul-fat renders down nicely." He shrugged apologetically. "That part's free. Consider it advertising."

"Why free?"

"Because, little sister, I want you to succeed." The worn face bent close, and for one unguarded instant Odile saw how ancient and exhausted it truly was, a face like a coin rubbed nearly blank by centuries of desperate hands. "A starving court is bad for everyone's business. And if you reach the mountain—if you somehow make them an offer they can't refuse—remember who helped you remember where the door was." He straightened, buttoned his green coat, and settled back onto the stool, folding himself down into shadow. "Favors compound. Start yours early."

They walked away. Odile's legs felt strange, too light, as if she'd narrowly avoided selling something heavier than a name and knew it.

At the mouth of the square she finally spoke. "What favor will he ask of you?"

"I don't know."

"You *owe* him and you don't know what for?"

"No one ever knows. That's how it works." Ashen's shoulder brushed her leg as they passed under the town gate, out toward the northern road where the land went wild and brown. "He could ask me to carry a message. He could ask me to stand aside at a crucial moment. He could ask—" the wolf's jaw tightened— "anything. That's the price of old kindnesses. They don't expire. They wait."

Odile looked back once. The stall was already indistinct in the market crowd, just a table, just a sign, just a shadow where a man should be. But she could have sworn the glass key caught the light one final time, flashing her own kitchen back at her—bread under a cloth, a chair pushed in, a door standing open.

Every helper keeps a ledger, she thought. Every kindness is a loan. Her grandmother had known it; the whole wild country ran on it, a web of owed and owing stretching back centuries, and she had just watched Ashen tie himself deeper into it to keep her whole.

"Thank you," she said quietly. "For what it cost."

"Don't thank me yet." The wolf's gait lengthened toward the north, toward the dark line of hills where the mountain waited behind its roots. "Save your gratitude. If Harrowgate calls his favor at the wrong moment—at the *door itself*—" He didn't finish.

Ahead, the road climbed into country Odile had never seen, and far off, half-hidden in cloud, the mountain stood with its crown of stone, patient as a creditor.

Somewhere beneath it, in a gallery lit by borrowed moonlight, Tam's soul sat waiting to be weighed.

And behind them, in a market town that had lost its hollow, a small god opened his books and wrote two names down—one in red, one in black—and began, softly, to count the days.
