## 8. Ashen's Long Memory

A wolf does not forget. That is the first thing you should understand about me, and the last thing I wish were true.

Men forget. They misplace their years like coins through a torn pocket, and by the time they notice the loss they have already invented new ones to spend in place of the old. But a wolf carries every winter he has ever slept through, every kill, every kindness, every debt. We are ledgers with legs. And I have been carrying one particular ledger for sixty-one years, and its weight has bent my spine and silvered my muzzle and taught me that an oath kept too long is not a virtue. It is a wound that refuses to close.

Her name was Beatrix Brightwater, and I loved her the way wolves love: once, entirely, and without any hope of being understood for it.

* * *

The valley was not always silent.

I want you to know that. When Odile crosses those boundary stones and gasps at the flood of the world rushing back into her senses — and she will, she is her grandmother's granddaughter down to the marrow — she will believe she is feeling magic for the first time. She will be wrong. The Valley of Stillwater was once so loud with it that the stones themselves hummed lullabies at dusk, and the river spoke in full sentences, and the hares argued theology with the owls and lost, always, because owls cheat.

I was young then. Two winters old, which is to say a fool with good teeth. I had wandered out of the high country following a smell of thunder, and what I found was a girl of nineteen standing barefoot in a frozen creek, arguing with the water.

"You will not drown my sister's goat," she was saying, "because the goat has done nothing to you, and if you take her I will come down here every day for the rest of my life and insult your singing."

The creek went quiet. Even then, Beatrix Brightwater could silence a river with a threat.

She saw me at the treeline and did not run, which should have told me everything about her that mattered. She looked at me the way no one had ever looked at me — not as a beast, not as a danger, but as a stranger whose opinion she had not yet decided on.

"Well?" she said. "Are you going to eat someone, or are you going to help me fish a goat out of the ice?"

I helped her fish the goat out of the ice. It bit me. She laughed until she cried, and bound my ear with a strip of her own skirt, and that was how it began. Sixty-one years ago. The scar is still there, under the newer ones. I keep them all. A wolf keeps his scars the way a miser keeps coins.

* * *

You must understand what she was running from, though she never told me all of it, and I have hated myself for sixty-one years for never asking hard enough.

She came from under the mountain. From the court. She was born there, in that cathedral of bone and moonlight, daughter of a soul-servant, and she was marked from birth as property — a tithe-child, raised like a lamb in a pen, waiting for the night the gods grew hungry enough to eat her. Except Beatrix was not a lamb. Beatrix stole something. A token — a small thing, a sliver of moonlight pressed into bone, a soul-token, the kind of object the court issues when it holds a soul in escrow and means to collect. Whose soul it held, she never said. Only that it was hers now, or someone's, and that she would rather die in the snow than give it back.

So she ran. North, into the wild country, and then further, into the green throat of a valley where the old gods' voices echoed strangely off the stones, and there she found people who had forgotten how to pray and were happier for it, and she thought — I am certain she thought this, because she told me once, drunk on honey-wine, weeping — *here, at last, nowhere can find me.*

But debts do not get lost. Debts only wait.

* * *

The winter the bargain was struck, I was old enough to know better and loyal enough to do it anyway.

The court had found her. Of course it had. A collector came over the mountains in the dead season, hollow-eyed, wrapped in a cloak that moved wrong, and stood at the edge of the valley while the snow refused to land on him, and he said the word *arrears* the way other men say *fire.*

Beatrix met him at the boundary stones with her chin up and her hands shaking where I alone could see them. I stood beside her with my hackles up and my teeth bared, and the collector looked at me the way you look at furniture.

"The valley," the collector said, "is unprotected. The old gods wake hungry, and hungry gods do not distinguish between the guilty and the innocent. Give us a claim on this place — a lien, a hold, a promise payable at need — and the court will pass over it. Your silence for our patience. No prayer will echo here. Nothing will hear you. Nothing will hunt you. You may live out your small life unremarked, and die old, and be forgotten gently."

"And the price?" Beatrix said. She knew. You could hear in her voice that she already knew.

"A soul," the collector said. "One soul, owed to the court beneath the mountain, due when the thirteenth moon completes its turn. Not yours. Not now. Descending through your blood until it comes due." He smiled, if the movement of a mouth that had forgotten smiling could be called a smile. "The court is patient. The court has always been patient."

And here is the thing I cannot forgive, the thing I have chewed on for six decades like a bone with no marrow left in it: she hesitated. My Beatrix, who stared down rivers and collectors and gods, hesitated — and then she looked back at the valley, at the smoke rising from the chimneys of people who had taken her in and asked no questions, at children sledding on the frozen mill-pond, and she said:

"Done."

Not for herself. Never for herself. She bought their safety with a soul not yet born, because the alternative was watching the hungry gods come down into the valley that winter, and she was not capable of standing aside from that. She was the best person I have ever known, and she did the worst thing I have ever witnessed, and both of those sentences are true, and I have carried them both ever since.

Because I was there. Because I was *bound* to be there.

* * *

You should know about the binding, since it is the hinge of everything and the reason I am telling you this instead of howling it at the moon until the mountain cracks.

When the bargain was struck, the collector required a witness. That is the law of the court: no debt exists without a witness, for a debt unwitnessed is merely a wish. And the collector looked around the frozen clearing, past the trembling trees, past the silent stones, and fixed his hollow eyes on me.

"The wolf," he said. "It has lived long among you. It will do."

"I don't consent—" Beatrix began.

"It is not yours to consent for," said the collector, and he spoke a word in the old tongue, the tongue from before names, and it went through me like a hook through the jaw.

This is what he made me: a witness. Bound to remember the bargain in every particular — the words, the seal, the sum owed — and bound to carry that memory until the debt was paid or discharged. And bound, above all, to one clause that the collector added with a dry little pleasure he did not bother to hide:

"*The witness shall not intervene. Not to warn, not to aid, not to hinder. The witness shall act only when a Brightwater freely asks.*"

Freely asked. Do you see the cruelty of it? He built the lock and gave the key to a door that might never open. If no Brightwater ever learned of the debt and chose to ask me — freely, knowingly, of their own will — then I would stand at the edge of their lives forever, watching, remembering, forbidden to say one word. He knew what I was to her. I think he enjoyed it. Gods and their servants feed on more than souls; they feed on suffering arranged just so.

I tried to break it. In the early years I threw myself against the binding like a wolf against a fence, night after night, trying to force the words out, to warn her, to tell her what she had signed away. The binding stopped my throat. I would open my jaws and the oath would pour in like cold water and fill them, and nothing would come out but a howl, and she would hear me howling at the valley's edge and think I was mourning the moon.

In a way, I was.

* * *

She married. Of course she did. A good man, a quiet man, a farmer named Iversen who deserved better than a wife who woke screaming some nights with a word on her lips — *arrears* — and would not say why. She bore children. She buried two of them, which nearly broke her, and I sat outside the window of the house those nights because the binding kept me from the graveside itself, and I howled, and inside the house she heard a wolf mourning and pressed her hand flat against the glass and whispered, "I know, old friend. I know."

She knew I was there. All those years, she knew. She would leave bones at the treeline — not scraps, but good bones, knuckle-bones and marrow-bones, laid out neat as an apology. Once a year, on the anniversary of the frozen creek, she walked to the boundary stones alone and stood there a long time, and I stood on my side of the invisible line the binding drew around my tongue, and neither of us said anything, because I could not and she did not know what to ask.

Once — once — she came very close. Her last winter, when the sickness was in her and we both knew the shape of it. She walked out to the stones leaning on a stick, gray as ash herself by then, and she looked straight at me across the snow, and she said:

"There's something I never told you. Something I signed away, a long time ago, before you—"

And the binding seized me. Not my throat this time — my *feet*. I stood rooted in the snow while she talked, and every word she said brushed against the edge of the question, the free asking, the key turning in the lock — and the binding poured frost into my legs and silence into my chest, because the collector had been thorough, and a near-asking is not a free asking, and he wanted no loopholes.

She faltered. She was dying and tired and she looked at me, at this scarred old wolf who had shadowed her family for fifty years, and whatever courage it takes to confess a sin like hers to the one creature who loved you without condition — she ran out of it. She turned back toward the house.

At the door she paused. She did not look at me. She said, so quietly the snow barely carried it:

"If anyone ever comes asking, Ashen — tell them everything. Tell them all of it. Promise me."

And the binding, which is a precise instrument, weighed her words and found them wanting. *If anyone comes asking* is not a free asking. It is a wish, a hope, a mother's dying instruction — but it was not *her* asking, and it was not free, because she did not yet know what she was asking for.

I howled until dawn. She died three days later, and I was not permitted to attend the burial, and I have never decided whether that was the binding's mercy or its final cruelty.

* * *

After her, I watched the children. Then the grandchildren.

I watched Odile Brightwater grow up in the silence her grandmother bought, a silence that lay over the valley like a jar over a candle. I watched her kneel in the dirt of the sheep-pasture when she thought no one could see, and put her small hand on the brow of a lame ewe, and *listen* — really listen, the way her grandmother used to listen, the way no one in that valley had listened in two generations. I watched her flinch from her own gift as if it were a crime. I watched her hide it, fold it small, bury it alive.

And I could not tell her. Do you understand? I stood at the treeline with sixty years of truth behind my teeth — the bargain, the ledger, the thirteenth moon, the soul already promised and descending through her blood toward her brother — and the binding sealed my jaws, and I *waited*, because the oath said wait, because the collector had written *freely asked* into the marrow of the world, and no child had asked me anything except once, years ago, when Odile was seven and had found me at the stones and said, "Do you know my grandma?"

I could answer that. Oh, I answered that. I said her name — the first time it had crossed my tongue aloud in decades — and the child's eyes went wide, and then her mother called her home, and the moment closed like water over a stone.

For twenty years after that, nothing. I grew old in the shadows of her growing up. I told myself the binding would end one way or another — that the debt would come due, that the thirteenth moon would rise, that the collector would come, and that on that day, at least, I would finally be permitted to *act*. I rehearsed it. Every night, lying in the snow beyond the treeline, I rehearsed what I would say to her. *Your grandmother traded a soul for your safety. The bill has come due. Your brother is the payment. Come with me, and I will show you the road, and I will stand between you and everything on it, because I failed her and I will not fail you.*

Sixty-one years of rehearsal. And then, three nights ago, the ground trembled under a lame ewe, and a muttered prayer echoed off the stones of the Valley of Stillwater for the first time in a generation, and something ancient turned over in its sleep beneath the mountain and *listened* — and I felt the binding shift, subtle as a lock sensing its key turning somewhere far away. Not open. Not yet. But listening for the asking.

And last night, Tam Brightwater rose from his bed with silver in his eyes and walked toward the treeline like a puppet testing its strings, and I broke the pattern of twenty years and stepped out of the dark onto the valley road, scarred and gray and shaking, and spoke to his sister by name.

She asked me who I was. She asked me what I knew.

She has not yet asked me the question that unlocks me. But she is her grandmother's granddaughter, and she is angry, and she is frightened, and she is standing at the edge of the only world she has ever known with a brother going silver-eyed in the night — and I watched her face while I told her about the weighing of souls, and I saw the exact moment she decided not to believe the elders, not to bow to the silence, not to be a candle under a jar anymore.

She will ask. Freely. I would stake my centuries on it.

And when she does — when the lock finally opens and sixty-one years of hoarded truth comes pouring out of me — I do not know whether she will thank me for keeping the oath or curse me for taking so long. I only know that the tithe-collectors have caught the scent of that answered prayer too, and they move faster than grief, and the mark they carry finds what it seeks.

The mountain has begun to reach for her. I can smell it on the wind from the south, thin and wrong, like iron under snow.

We are out of time, and I am done waiting.
