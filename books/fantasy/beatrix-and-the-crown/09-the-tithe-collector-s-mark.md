## 9. The Tithe-Collector's Mark

They found the burnt road's end by nightfall, where the crows had said the forest thickened and the collector's reach grew thin. Odile had begun to believe it. That was her mistake.

She was kneeling at a stream, filling the waterskin, when Ashen went rigid beside her. Every hair along his spine lifted like a field of dark wheat bending before a storm.

"He's here," the wolf said. "Move."

She didn't ask how he knew. She had stopped asking, mostly. But her hands were slow with cold and weariness, and the waterskin slipped, and in that half-second of fumbling she heard it: a sound like wet cloth dragged over stone, unhurried, patient.

The tithe-collector came out of the trees the way fog comes off a marsh—not walking so much as arriving, piece by piece, until all of him was there. He looked worse than before. The hollows of his eyes had deepened into true darkness now, as if something behind his face were eating its way out. His mouth smiled without any part of his face agreeing to it.

"Little speaker," he rasped. "You run *beautifully*. But the mountain has grown tired of watching you run."

Odile did the only thing she could think of. She threw the waterskin at him.

It was not a heroic gesture. It bought her perhaps two heartbeats, which she used to bolt upstream along the bank, Ashen a gray blur at her side. Behind her the collector made no sound of pursuit, and that was worse than footsteps. She could feel him the way you feel a hand hovering over the back of your neck.

"Left," Ashen snapped. "Into the alders."

She crashed through them, branches whipping her cheeks, and burst into a clearing—and stopped, because there was nowhere left to go. The alders ringed the space like a fence. The ground was soft moss, beautiful, open, a trap dressed as a meadow.

"No," she breathed. "No, no—"

The collector stepped from the treeline. Unhurried. Smiling.

"You've cost me a great deal of trouble," he said. His voice was dry leaves and cellar air. "The court does not forgive trouble. So I am instructed to make certain you are never lost again."

He raised one hand. The fingers were too long, jointed like a heron's leg, and at the tip of the index finger something glowed—a bead of light the color of moonlight on snow, cold and absolute.

Odile backed away until the alders caught her shoulders. "Stay back."

"Or what?" He tilted his head. "Will you speak to the trees? They are asleep, little speaker. Will you call the beasts? There are none here. I chose this place."

Ashen hit him from the side.

The wolf moved faster than she had ever seen him move—a streak of scarred muscle and fury—and took the collector at the knees. But the collector did not fall the way men fall. He folded, like paper, like smoke given temporary shape, and reformed standing, looking down at Ashen with something almost like pity.

"Wolf," he said. "Old wolf. You should have stayed dead with your mistress."

Ashen's snarl was terrible. "Touch her and I will remember you for a thousand years, and every year of it will be pain."

"Sentiment." The collector sighed. "The court has no use for it. Hold still, child. This is mercy, really. The marked are never hunted twice."

The light on his finger stretched—became a needle of white fire—

And then the forest screamed.

Not the trees. Not birds. Something vast and wounded bellowing from the ridge above, and the ground shuddered, and the collector's needle-hand faltered as he turned toward the sound. In that instant of distraction, Odile felt Ashen's teeth close gently on her sleeve, hauling her sideways, down, through a gap in the alders she would have sworn hadn't existed a moment ago.

They ran. Down a slope, through bracken, across a stream that soaked her to the knee, up again, until her lungs burned and her legs shook and Ashen finally pulled her into the hollow beneath the roots of a fallen oak, a cave of earth and shadow.

For a long time neither of them spoke. Odile pressed her back against the root-wall and tried to breathe quietly and failed.

"He let us go," she whispered finally. "Didn't he? Partly. He wanted to mark me more than he wanted to catch me."

"Yes." Ashen's voice was grim in the dark. "A marked quarry is worth ten unmarked ones. You cannot hide from the mountain now, Odile. Whatever happens next, it sees you."

* * *

She didn't know she'd been branded until morning.

That was the cruelest part. In the chaos of the escape—the screaming thing on the ridge, the flight through the dark—she had never felt it land. But when dawnlight filtered down through the root-hollow and she pushed back her sleeve, there it was on the back of her right hand: a mark like a crescent moon crossed by a line, fine as an etching, silver-white against her skin.

It did not hurt. That frightened her most of all. It simply *was*, cool and precise, as if someone had written her name on her in a language she couldn't read.

"It's beautiful," she said, and hated how her voice shook. "That's horrible, isn't it? That it's beautiful."

"It's meant to be." Ashen lay with his chin on his paws, watching the entrance of their hollow. "The court believes itself elegant. Everything it touches is meant to look like a gift."

"What does it do?"

"Everything you do, the mountain knows. Where you walk. What you say. Perhaps what you dream—I don't know; no one who's been marked has ever come back to describe it." He glanced at her, gold eyes steady. "I'm sorry. I should have been faster."

"You saved my life."

"I was too slow by half a second." He said it flatly, without drama, the way he said everything, but she heard the self-judgment underneath, old and worn smooth like a stone carried in a pocket for decades. "Half a second is how Brightwaters die."

"Well." She flexed her fingers, watching the mark catch the light. "Then I'll have to stop being a person things happen to in half-seconds."

She expected him to argue. Instead, after a long moment, he huffed something that might have been a laugh. "Your grandmother used to say nearly the same thing. Word for word, almost."

"I'm starting to think I never knew her at all."

"None of us knew her," Ashen said. "That was rather the point of her."

* * *

They traveled that day, because staying still felt like dying, though Odile suspected the mark made stillness and motion equally visible. The forest around them changed character as they went—older trees, deeper shadow, and always, always the sense of being watched. Once, crossing a high meadow, she saw far below them a figure moving along a road: robed, slow, wrong. Another collector, or the same one circling. She couldn't tell. The mark on her hand prickled when she looked at it, faint as a spider's step.

By dusk she was exhausted in a way that sleep wouldn't fix, and the mark had begun to ache—a deep, cold ache, like a splinter of winter lodged under the skin. And that was when the stag found them.

She heard him before she saw him: a heavy, labored breathing in the brush, and then he stepped into the small clearing where they'd stopped—an enormous red stag, twelve points on his crown, his coat gone gray at the muzzle. An arrow stood in his shoulder, broken off at the shaft. The wound beneath it was black.

"Oh," Odile said softly. "Oh, you poor thing."

The stag looked at her. And then, in the way of beasts when she truly listened, meaning arrived—not words exactly, but shapes of thought, clear as bells:

***Small-speaker. You smell of the mountain.***

"I'm sorry," she said. "It was put on me. Against my will."

***Nothing is against our will,*** the stag said, ***that we do not fight hard enough against.*** He swayed. His legs trembled. ***But you are not the mountain. Your heart is open. I am done running, and I would not die alone in the dark. May I rest near you?***

Her throat tightened. "Yes. Of course. Come here."

He lowered himself with terrible care, folding his long legs beneath him, and Odile knelt beside him and put her hands on his neck. The heat of fever came off him in waves. Beneath her palms, his heartbeat was huge and slowing, a drum at the end of its song.

"Ashen," she said. "Can anything be done?"

The wolf circled the stag once, sniffing. "The arrowhead is deep. It's festering. No." He said it gently, for a wolf. "Let him be comfortable."

So she sat with the stag while the light died, and Ashen kept watch, and the stag told her things in the slow, drifting way of the dying—which is to say, he told her true things, the kind the living are too busy to say.

***I was king of this wood once,*** he said. ***Before the burning road came through. Now the young stags fight over scraps of forest, and the hunters grow bolder every year, and the gods who used to keep the balance sleep or starve or sell themselves.*** A long breath, rattling slightly. ***You are going to the mountain, little speaker.***

"How did you know?"

***The mark points at you like a compass needle. And you have the look.*** His great dark eye reflected the first stars. ***My grandmother met your grandmother. Did you know that? Beatrix Brightwater passed through this wood fleeing the court, and my grandmother sheltered her three nights in a hollow like this one.***

Odile's breath caught. "She never told us. She never told anyone anything."

***That was her nature, I think.*** The stag's breath steamed in the cold. ***Listen, then, and carry this forward, since I can carry nothing else. The thirteenth moon—the door-moon—it is not what the court believes it is. They think it opens one way. A door for taking. For collecting. For drawing souls down into the dark.***

"And it doesn't?"

***A door,*** the stag said, ***works both ways.***

The words settled over her like snowfall.

***What goes down may come up. What is taken may be returned. The court has forgotten this, because no one has ever had the courage—or the foolishness—to push the door from the other side.*** His breathing was slower now, longer between. ***You could be the first.***

"The door opens both ways," Odile repeated slowly. "Tam could walk out."

***Or be walked out.*** The stag's eye closed, then opened again with effort. ***But hear the warning in it, child. If the door works both ways, then while it stands open, things can come *up*. Things the court keeps below. Things even the gods fear. When you open it—and you will, I see it in you, stubborn as your grandmother—you must be ready for what follows you out.***

The mark on her hand pulsed, cold and bright, as if it had heard.

"What things?" Odile asked. "What's kept below?"

But the stag's breathing had changed. It came slower, and slower, and each breath a little shallower, and his great head sank lower until his muzzle rested on the moss. She kept her hands on his neck and felt the drum of his heart stretch its rhythm further and further apart, like a runner lengthening stride at the end of a race.

***Thank you,*** he said, so faintly she almost missed it. ***For not letting me die listening to my own fear. Your grandmother's blood runs true in you. Go to the mountain. Open the door. And when the hungry things stir behind you—run, little speaker. Run beautifully.***

The last breath went out of him long and slow, and the drum fell silent, and the clearing was very quiet.

Odile stayed kneeling a long time. Then she did the only thing she could think to do: she gathered fallen branches and built a cairn of stones over his body, working until her arms ached, whispering to the stones as she placed them, telling them what he'd been, asking them to hold him well. Whether the stones listened she couldn't say. But it felt less like hiding and more like honoring, and that was new, and she held onto it.

When she finished, Ashen was standing at the edge of the clearing, ears pricked, staring north.

"What is it?"

"The moon," he said.

She followed his gaze. Above the black teeth of the northern peaks hung a thin sliver of moon—but not alone. Beside it, faint as breath on glass, hung a second sliver, and beyond that a ghost of a third, thirteen moons in a slow procession climbing the sky like beads on a string, each fainter than the last, the thirteenth barely visible at all, a smudge of silver at the edge of seeing.

"That's not possible," Odile whispered.

"It's the count beginning," Ashen said. "Thirteen nights. On the thirteenth night, the moons complete their turn, and the door stands open." He looked at her, and in the starlight his scars stood out white. "The mountain knows it too. That's why the collector came tonight. They want you marked and tracked before the door opens—because they know, little speaker, that you're the one who can push it from either side."

Odile looked down at her hand. The crescent-and-line mark gleamed in the moonlight, cold and silver, and for the first time she didn't flinch from it.

Let them watch, she thought. Let the whole mountain look. They thought the mark was a leash. They hadn't considered it might also be a claim—hers, on them.

"Thirteen nights," she said aloud, and her voice didn't shake. "Then we'd better stop running north like prey." She turned to Ashen, and the mark burned cold on her hand, and she smiled with her grandmother's stubbornness rising in her like a tide. "We're done being hunted, Ashen. From tonight, we hunt."

Far to the north, beneath the sleeping peaks, something ancient felt her say it—and stirred.
