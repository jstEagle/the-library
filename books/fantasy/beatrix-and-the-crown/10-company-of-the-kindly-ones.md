## 10. Company of the Kindly Ones

The stag's warning had come with a price of its own: three days of running, and no direction but *away*.

Odile followed it anyway, because away was the only word her body understood now. The mark on her palm had settled into a dull heat, like a stone held too long near a fire, and every few hours it flared without warning—a pulse of pain that made her gasp and clutch her hand against her chest as though she could smother it there. Each flare meant the same thing. *He sees you. He is telling them where you are.*

Ashen kept pace beside her, gray as road dust, his scarred muzzle always angled toward the wind.

"You're limping," he said on the second evening.

"I'm walking."

"You're limping *and* walking. There's a difference, and the difference matters when something is hunting you."

She didn't answer. Her boots had been good valley leather once, made to last a lifetime of soft ground and short errands. Three days of burnt country had opened their soles like mouths. She could feel every stone through the wrapping she'd torn from her hem.

Ahead, the land changed. The burnt road—crows and cinders and the tithe-collector's shadow—gave way at last to hills that still remembered green. Not the tame, obedient green of Stillwater's fields, but a wilder thing: hawthorn gone riotous over old walls, birch crowding in where orchards had been, bramble swallowing a lane whole. And threaded through all of it, faint as a smell of rain before it comes, Odile felt something she had never felt outside her own skin.

Magic. Loose in the world. Unhidden.

She stopped so suddenly Ashen bumped her hip.

"What is it?"

"It's—" She pressed her hand flat against the air, absurdly, as if she could touch what she sensed. "It's everywhere here. Like standing in a river after a lifetime of drinking from a cup."

"Then you understand why we don't stay in the open." Ashen's ears swiveled forward. "And why you need to stop broadcasting."

"Broadcasting?"

"You've been leaking since the stag died. Grief does that. Fear does that. Your gift is shouting into the dark, girl, and everything with ears is listening." He turned his head, and for a moment his yellow eyes held hers. "There are those who would help you. And there are those who would sell you by weight. We want the first kind, and the only way to find them is to be quiet enough that they find *us* instead."

"How do I stop? I've spent my whole life hiding this. Hiding it is the one thing I know how to do."

"Hiding isn't quiet," Ashen said. "Hiding is loud—it's all clenched teeth and held breath. You've been clenching since you were small enough to fit in your grandmother's apron pocket. I need you to do the opposite. Open your hand. Let the gift sit in it like water. Don't drink, don't pour. Just hold."

It sounded like nonsense. It felt like nonsense, right up until she tried it.

She uncurled her fingers—the marked one—and breathed out, and instead of shoving the strange bright sense of the world away, she let it rest. The magic of the hills settled over her like a blanket rather than a flood. And underneath it, so faint she might have imagined it, she caught a thread of something else. A rhythm. A pull. Somewhere north and east, something was calling—not to her specifically, but to anything wounded and gifted and looking for a door.

"There," she whispered. "Do you feel that?"

Ashen's tail moved once, slowly. "I hoped I would. Follow it. And walk like a woman going somewhere ordinary."

* * *

The door, when they found it, was a ruin.

An old mill, or what remained of one—the wheel long rotted, the race choked with reeds, the roof half-collapsed into a skeleton of black beams. Ivy owned the walls. To any passing eye it was exactly what it appeared: dead, damp, abandoned for a generation.

But the thread pulled straight toward it, and when Odile stood before the broken doorway and let her gift rest open in her hand, she saw the truth laid over the lie like a second skin. The ruin was a mask. Beneath the rot-smell and the tumbleweed, the stones hummed. Wards, woven so finely they felt less like spells than like stitching—invisible thread holding an illusion together the way a seam holds cloth.

"Someone very skilled lives here," she murmured.

"Someone very frightened," Ashen corrected. "Skill and fear make the same stitches. Knock."

"With what? My knuckles?"

"With the truth. It's the only coin they take."

So Odile stood in the doorway of a dead mill in a country that wanted her dead, and she did the hardest thing she had ever done, harder than crossing the boundary stones, harder than paying the river its toll.

She spoke aloud, to strangers, the sentence she had never said to anyone.

"My name is Odile Brightwater, and I can speak with beasts, and I'm being hunted, and I need help."

For a long moment, nothing. Wind in the reeds. Ashen's breath beside her knee.

Then the ivy shifted. Not stirred—*shifted*, parting along a seam she couldn't see, and behind it was not a wall but a corridor, lamplit, dry, and lined with doors. A woman stood at the far end. She was old, broad-shouldered, dressed in a dyer's stained apron, and her hands were blue to the wrist from years of woad. She looked at Odile, then at Ashen, and her face did something complicated.

"A wolf that talks," she said. "Well. It's been a slow season."

"You're not afraid?" Odile asked.

"Child, I turn wool blue with a thought and pretend it's the vat. Fear stopped being useful to me around the time my hair went gray." She stepped aside. "I'm Maren. Come in before you let the cold in, and mind the third threshold—it bites."

* * *

The mill was bigger inside than out, which Odile was beginning to understand was simply how magic worked when it wasn't being strangled. Corridors opened into rooms that shouldn't have fit: a kitchen with a hearth big enough to roast an ox, a dormitory of neat beds, a workshop where the smell of dye and tallow and cut wood mingled into something almost like home. And people—two dozen of them, maybe more, moving through the lamplight with the unhurried air of folk long used to one another.

They were all working. That was the first thing she noticed, and the second, and the third, because it was so obviously deliberate. A young man carved spoons, and the spoons came off his knife already smooth as riverstones, though she never saw him sand them. A girl no older than Odile kneaded bread, and the dough rose between one fold and the next, quick as a heartbeat. An ancient man mended nets, and the knots tied themselves under his fingers while his hands stayed loose and idle, resting on the rope.

Mundane craft, every bit of it. Except none of it was mundane.

"You hide it inside the work," Odile said slowly, watching the net-mender. "The magic looks like skill."

"Looks like skill, smells like skill, taxes like skill." Maren steered her toward the kitchen and pressed a bowl of stew into her hands. "A charm is a confession. Craft is just competence. The collectors can hang a witch, but who do they hang for good bread?" She snorted. "We call ourselves the Kindly Ones. It's a joke. The old stories use it for the fair folk, because you don't say their true names and you don't say *your* true name either, not where the wrong ears hang. Here, everyone works under a trade-name. I'm the Dyer. That's the Baker. The Spoon-Carver. The Net-Mender. You'll want one."

Odile looked down at her stew, then at Ashen, who had folded himself beneath the table with the patience of a creature who had waited decades and could wait through dinner.

"The Speaker," she said at last.

Maren's eyebrows rose. "Bold."

"I'm done being careful with the name of it."

Something flickered across the older woman's face—approval, maybe, or grief for the boldness, since boldness got people burned. "Eat," she said. "Then you'll want Sable. She's our weaver. If you're carrying paper with the mountain's stink on it, she's the one who reads it."

* * *

Sable's room was at the heart of the mill, and it was dark, and it was full of looms.

Not two or three—dozens, great upright frames and small hand-held ones, some strung with wool, some with linen, some with threads Odile couldn't name, colors that seemed to move when she wasn't looking directly at them. And in the middle of it all sat a small woman on a low stool, silver-haired, her eyes closed and sunken, her hands never still. Even blind, even sitting, she was weaving—fingers moving over a scrap of frame no bigger than a book, tying off threads with the speed of long habit.

"Maren says you carry a ledger," Sable said, before Odile had crossed the room. Her voice was dry and light, like paper turning. "And that it smells of the court beneath the mountain. Set it in my hands."

Odile hesitated. The ledger had been her grandmother's. It had ridden against her ribs for a hundred miles, warm from her own body, and handing it to a stranger felt like opening a vein.

Ashen's nose touched her ankle. Once. *Trust.*

She set the book in Sable's palms.

The blind weaver went utterly still. Her fingers moved over the cover, the spine, the edges of the pages—but not reading the way Odile read. Feeling. And as her hands traveled, the threads on her little loom began to move on their own, shifting, tightening, a pattern assembling itself in silence.

"Oh," Sable said softly. "Oh, you poor child."

"What? What is it?"

"This isn't a ledger. Well—it is. But it's more." Her fingers found the first page and rested there. "It's woven. Your grandmother wrote her accounts in ink for anyone who might steal the book, but the true record is stitched into the binding itself, thread by thread, in knot-script. Old craft. Older than me." She drew a breath. "Anyone can read ink. Only a weaver reads the binding. And the binding says—"

She stopped.

"*Says what?*" Odile's voice cracked on it.

Sable turned her ruined eyes toward the sound, and though she could not see, Odile felt seen down to the marrow.

"Says that the Valley of Stillwater was not always silent," Sable said. "Says that once upon a time it sang—small gods in the streams, blessings on the seed-corn, charms on the doorposts, the whole old country alive the way this hill is alive. And says that a woman went down to the court beneath the mountain and bought that singing to a stop." Her fingers tightened on the binding. "Bought its silence. Bought a veil over the whole valley, thick enough that the gods couldn't hear prayers from inside and the collectors couldn't see magic either. A hiding place. Safety for everyone in it, for generations."

"That's—" Odile's mind was racing ahead, grasping. "That's good. That's what she did for us. That's why we hid, why the gift had to stay buried, why—"

"Why the debt exists," Sable finished quietly. "Nothing is bought for free, child. Especially not from the hungry. Turn to the last pages. Feel how the thread changes."

Odile took the book back. Under Sable's direction her own clumsy fingers traced the binding, and yes—she could feel it now that she knew to look. The early knots were firm and even. But near the end, the thread grew finer. Desperate. Crowded.

"The silence was bought," Sable said, "with a soul-token. One soul, owed to the court, payable when the thirteenth moon completed its turn. Your grandmother bargained for a valley's safety and put up a life as surety—and not her own. She was clever that way. She put up a life that did not yet exist." The loom-threads trembled. "A descendant's life. A child of her children's children. The debt fell down the family line like rain down a roof, waiting for the eave."

The kitchen warmth drained out of Odile all at once. She heard her own voice from very far away.

"Tam. Tam wasn't even born. He was a *maybe*. She traded a maybe."

"She traded the only coin the court accepts from the living," Sable said gently. "A future. It's why the bargain held so long. Futures are cheap to promise and dear to pay."

"But he's not a maybe anymore." Fury rose in Odile like floodwater, sudden and total, filling every space inside her. "He's *Tam*. He's twenty years old and he laughs at his own jokes before the punchline and he can't sleep unless the window's open and he taught me to whistle with a blade of grass when I was six. He's not a coin. He's not a *line item*—"

She was shouting. She didn't care. The spoon-carver's head turned in the corridor; the baker's hands went still in her dough. Odile stood in the lamplight with her marked hand burning and her voice shaking and the fury pouring out of her like heat off a forge.

"She bought *safety*," Odile said, lower now, ragged. "Our safety. Hers. Mine. Every lamb I ever healed in secret, every prayer I swallowed down my whole life—we were all hiding inside a cage she paid for with my brother. And she *knew*. She must have known, she wrote the ledger, she watched the moons turn, and she never told us. She let us love him. She let *him* be born."

The mark on her palm flared white-hot. She cried out and gripped her wrist, and Ashen was up and pressed against her side in an instant, a solid gray weight, growling low at nothing, at everything.

"Breathe," Sable said. "You're shouting your location to the mountaintop. Breathe, Speaker."

Odile breathed. It took a long time. The others drifted back to their work, tactful as cats, and the mill's small noises closed over the wound of her anger like water over a thrown stone.

When she could speak again, her voice came out flat and cold, and she barely recognized it as her own.

"She's dead. I can't ask her. I can't hate her properly, because she's *dead*, and she's also the reason I'm alive, and the reason Tam is *not*—" Her fist closed over the burning mark. "So I'll hate the ones I can reach. The court. The collector. Whatever god sat there and took a maybe for payment and called it justice."

"Careful," Sable said. "That's the fire that burns witches. Aim it well."

"Oh, I'm aiming it." Odile looked down at the ledger in her hands—at her grandmother's careful ink, her hidden knots, her generations of silence. "Tell me the rest. All of it. What the binding says about the thirteenth moon, about the door, about how debts get paid. Because I walked into that court's shadow once already and walked out again, and I am not leaving without my brother."

Sable was quiet a moment. Her hands resumed their weaving, small and ceaseless.

"There's one more thing in the binding," she said at last. "One knot I've never been able to read. It's not in your grandmother's hand." Her sightless eyes fixed on Odile with terrible gentleness. "It's older. And it's signed with a wolf's paw."

Every hair on Odile's arms lifted. Slowly, she looked down at Ashen.

The old wolf did not look away. But he did not speak, either—and in all the miles they had traveled, it was the first time he had ever chosen silence.
