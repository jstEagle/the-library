## 11. What the Ledger Did Not Say

I have been blind for forty years, and in that time I have learned that the world is more honest without eyes. Faces lie. Voices lie. But thread does not. Thread tells you everything—the tremor in a hand, the grief knotted into a hem, the lie a person has worn so long it has gone smooth as river glass.

The girl who came to us out of the rain carried all three.

I heard her before I saw her, which is to say I heard the wolf first. Ashen's tread has a particular weight, old and deliberate, like a man walking through a house he built himself. And beside him, lighter, half-skipping to keep pace: Odile Brightwater. I knew the name before Maren spoke it aloud. Some names you keep woven into your memory the way I keep knots in my fingers—tied off years ago, waiting for the day they'd be needed.

"Her grandmother," Maren said to me quietly, when the girl had been fed and dried and given the corner bed by the loom. "The one from the ledger."

"I know whose granddaughter she is," I said. "She walks like Beatrix."

"You can't see how she walks."

"No," I agreed. "But I remember."

* * *

Here is what the ledger did not say, because ledgers are written by debtors and debtors edit themselves:

Beatrix Brightwater was not born in the Valley of Stillwater. She was born beneath the mountain, in the court of bone and moonlight, daughter of a soul-servant, and she was fifteen years old when she ran.

I know this because I was there. Not beneath the mountain—I have never had the courage for that place—but at its mouth. I was a young woman then, with eyes still good enough to see by candlelight, and I lived in a market town on the burnt road where the tithe-collectors came twice a year to count what the valley owed. My mother wove. Her mother wove. We were not gifted, not exactly—we were *useful*, which in those days was safer. The court left useful people alone.

It was a night in autumn, the thirteenth moon fat and low over the ridgeline, when a girl came stumbling down the under-road's entrance with blood on her feet and something clutched against her chest like a stolen loaf of bread.

Which is nearly what it was. A stolen soul.

I felt it before she reached me. Everyone on the street felt it—a wrongness in the air, a hush like the moment before lightning. She was carrying a soul-token, one of the small bone coins the court mints from what it collects, and it was burning cold against her skin, and she was running from the only home she had ever known.

She collapsed at our door. My mother took her in. That was the kind of woman my mother was, though it cost us—though it cost *me*, though I will come to that.

The girl would not say her name at first. Names, she whispered, could be traced. So we called her nothing, and fed her, and watched the token she carried. It was small, white as a fingernail, and it hummed. If you held it too long your teeth ached. Inside it was a soul—not hers, she swore it was not hers—and she had taken it because the court was starving even then, even sixty years ago, and starving courts begin eating their own servants' children.

"They were going to feed me to the Debt-God," she told us on the third night, when she finally slept enough to dream and woke screaming. "Not eat me. Worse. They were going to make me *forget* first. They take the memories out and serve them separately, like wine and meat. The soul goes in one bowl and the love goes in another, and the gods drink the love and let the rest sit." Her hands shook around a cup of broth. "I couldn't be both bowls. Do you understand? I'd rather run than be divided."

I understood. I was sixteen, and I already knew what it was to be divided—to be the useful daughter, the quiet daughter, the one who saw things and said nothing.

We should have sent her back. I have had sixty years to consider this, and I know now that we should have sent her back, or at least returned the token, or at least asked the court's permission, which she would never have gotten but which might have softened what came after. Instead my mother taught her the roads south, and gave her boots, and told her about a valley where magic went to die—a place so smothered, so silent, that no god's eye could find you there. A place to hide a stolen soul.

And Beatrix—who chose her name from a flower growing in our dooryard, because a girl needs some name and she refused to carry her true one into hiding—went south with the token pressed to her heart, and I never saw her again until the ledger came north, decades later, asking questions.

* * *

"What did she steal?" Odile asks me now, sitting across from my loom with her hands wrapped around a mug she hasn't drunk from. She has been here two days. She has not slept well; I hear it in the way her chair creaks, the way she keeps touching the mark on her hand like a bruise. "Everyone keeps saying *the bargain*. Nobody says what was traded."

"The ledger says protection," I tell her. "Your valley's silence. Its blindness to the old world. In exchange, one soul, due when the thirteenth moon completed its turn—which is a court way of saying *when we feel like collecting*, dressed up in astronomy."

"But what did she *steal*?"

I reach for the shuttle at my side—an old habit, thinking better with thread in hand—and wind a strand of undyed wool around my finger. "A soul-token," I say. "One coin from the court's treasury. She took it the night she fled, and she carried it into Stillwater, and she hid it somewhere in that valley so thoroughly that even the Debt-God's collectors never found it. And then she made her bargain."

Odile is quiet. I hear Ashen shift behind her, the soft click of his jaws settling. He knows this story too—or part of it. He was bound to witness the original bargain, Maren told me, and I confess I did not ask what that cost him. Wolves who wait sixty years outside a valley's edge are paying for something.

"So the debt isn't just a debt," Odile says slowly. "It's a *collection*. They're not taking Tam because of a promise. They're taking him because—" 

"Because your grandmother robbed them," I finish. "Yes. The bargain was her payment plan. Protection now, a soul later. And the soul they claimed was never specified, because the court doesn't specify. It lets the debtor choose, and then it takes whatever the debtor loves most when the bill comes due." I let the wool drop. "That is the cruelty of it, child. Your family wasn't marked for punishment. You were marked for *repayment*. There is a difference, and the difference is that punishment ends."

I hear her breath catch. Good. I am not gentle. Gentleness is a luxury of people who can afford to soften truth, and I weave for a living—I know what happens to cloth when you leave out the hard threads. It falls apart in the wash.

"She was a child," Odile says at last, and her voice is fierce, and I like her for it. "Fifteen. Running from being eaten. What was she supposed to do?"

"Nothing different," I say. "That's the tragedy. She did the only thing a frightened child could do. And it was still a theft, and thefts have interest, and the interest was your brother." I lean forward. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? Your courage is inherited, girl. So is your sin. Both of them live in you, and neither one cancels the other out. You don't get to be only the innocent party in this story. None of us do."

* * *

Maren thinks I tell her too much. Maren thinks I should have kept the map back, tested the girl longer, made her prove herself the way we prove everyone who finds this place. But I have listened to Odile Brightwater for two days now, and here is what thread has told me that eyes never could:

When she speaks of her brother, her voice drops into a lower register, steadier, the way voices do when they are carrying weight they've decided not to put down. When she speaks of her gift, her voice does the opposite—it lifts, quickens, and then catches itself, ashamed, the way Beatrix's voice used to catch whenever anyone mentioned the mountain. And when she sat with our youngest last night, the boy who was born hollow-marked and screaming at shadows, she put her hand on his chest and murmured something too quiet for me to hear, and the boy slept, truly slept, for the first time in a month.

She is her grandmother's granddaughter. God help her. God help all of us, if there are any gods left worth the word.

So on the third morning I call her to the loom, and I give her what I have been keeping.

The map is not paper. Paper burns, and paper can be read by the wrong eyes, and the under-roads rearrange themselves anyway—any map of them is a lie within a day. What I have instead is cloth. Sixty years of cloth. I began weaving it the week after Beatrix left, though I didn't know why then; I thought I was making a blanket. But my hands knew. My hands remembered the routes the tithe-collectors muttered among themselves, the passages the servants used when they slipped away to trade with market towns, the ways down and the ways that only go down. And every year since, on the thirteenth moon, I have added to it. Knots for junctions. Color-shifts for the passages that move. A border pattern that looks decorative and is actually a warning system—if the threads along the edge ever darken on their own, the road has changed, and the map must be read again from the beginning.

I fold it into her hands and watch her fingers learn it, tracing the raised work the way I taught my own children to read.

"It won't hold forever," I warn her. "The under-roads are alive, or close enough. They shift when unobserved, which means they shift constantly, because nobody sane observes them. Trust the structure, not the details. And trust the beasts." I tap the section where I've worked in a pattern of moles and bats, copied from stories the collectors told. "The court's servants fear the deep creatures. The deep creatures owe the court nothing. Your gift may open doors down there that no map can."

"Thank you." Her voice is thick. She means it. I wave it off, because gratitude makes my skin itch.

"Don't thank me yet. Because now comes the part you won't want to hear, and I'd rather you hear it from me than from the mountain."

She waits. Even Ashen goes still.

"The court may simply refuse to trade," I say. "You've been traveling with a wolf who remembers bargains, and you've met a god of small debts who talks like a merchant, and somewhere along the road you've started believing that everything down there is negotiable. It isn't. The parliament of gods is not a market. It is a *hunger* wearing the shape of a market. When a hunger negotiates, it is only deciding how much of you to take. The law of renegotiation exists—I won't lie to you, the witnesses-clause is real, and it may save your brother's life. But understand what you are walking into. You are not entering a courtroom. You are entering a stomach that has learned to speak."

"And if they refuse?"

"Then you run, or you die, or you become one of the silver-eyed ones pouring wine, and your brother serves beside you until the moon turns again." I say it flatly, because flatness is mercy for some truths. "Sixty years ago a fifteen-year-old girl ran rather than be divided. You may have to do the same. And if you do, I want you to know—before you go, while you can still hate me for saying it—that running would not make you a coward. It would make you a survivor. And survivors are the only reason any of us gifted are still alive."

For a long moment she doesn't answer. Then I hear her stand, and cross the floor, and her arms come around me—thin, strong, smelling of woodsmoke and rain—and she holds on tight enough that my ribs ache.

"My grandmother," she says into my shoulder, "did she ever come back? After? Did she ever try to return the token, or fix it?"

I stroke her hair, the way I haven't stroked anyone's hair since my daughter died. "No," I say. "She hid it, and she buried the knowledge, and she raised her children to be invisible, and she died in silence. That was her penance. Whether it was enough—" I shrug, and feel the motion travel through her. "Debts between people can be forgiven. Debts to gods only get paid."

She pulls back. I hear her tuck the map into her coat, next to the ledger and the whistle and whatever else she carries. I hear Ashen rise, joints cracking like green firewood.

"One more thing," I say, before they reach the door. "The token she stole. It's still in the valley, isn't it? Hidden. Unfound."

A pause. "I don't know what you mean," Odile says.

I smile. Lying runs in the family too, apparently, and badly.

"When you get beneath the mountain," I tell her, "and you stand in front of their parliament, and they ask what right you have to speak—remember that you are not just a debtor's descendant begging leniency. You are the heir to something they *want back*. Sixty years they have starved and searched and sent collectors crawling over the world, and the thing they lost is sitting under a jar in Stillwater gathering dust. Find it before you go. Whatever it costs you. Because a thief with leverage gets a hearing, child." I turn back to my loom, fingers finding the warp. "And a beggar only gets eaten."

* * *

They leave at dusk, the girl and the wolf, out the eastern gate where the hill drops toward the burnt road. I stand in the doorway and listen until I can't anymore—the footfalls fading, the wind rising, Maren's soft voice behind me asking if I did the right thing.

"I gave her the truth and a map," I say. "What she does with either is hers."

"And if the court refuses to trade? If she dies down there with my map folded in her pocket?"

Then I will add another knot to the border, I think. Another dark thread. Another year of waiting for a Brightwater to come asking questions, the way they always do, eventually, because courage and sin both run in families, and neither one ever quite dies out.

"Then the map comes back to me," I say aloud, "and I start weaving a new one. For whoever comes next."

Behind me, the loom creaks in the evening draft, and far to the north—so far I cannot possibly hear it, and hear it anyway—the mountain begins, very softly, to hum.
