## 12. Under the Mountain's Root

The under-roads began where the mountain forgot to be a mountain.

That was how the weaver had described it, her blind hands moving over Odile's as she traced the entrance on the woven map. *You won't see a door. You'll see the place where the rock stops pretending. Rock is honest, girl. It only lies when something makes it.*

Odile found it at dusk, three days past the Kindly Ones' camp, in a fold of the foothills where the stone curved wrong—where shadow pooled against the slope of the light instead of with it. Ashen stood beside her, hackles lifted, a low sound in his chest that wasn't quite a growl.

"You feel it too," she said.

"I feel old," he said. "There's a difference, but not a large one."

The entrance was a crack barely wide enough for her shoulders. She went in sideways, the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her back, the bone whistle cold against her sternum, and the mountain closed behind her like a mouth.

For the first hundred paces there was nothing but dark and the scrape of her boots and Ashen's breathing somewhere behind her, a sound she'd learned to steer by. Then the dark changed quality. It wasn't absence anymore. It was texture. And the mark on her hand woke up.

It had been a dull ember since the collector branded her—warm when she thought about the court, hot when the court thought about her. Now it flared like a coal breathed on, and by its light she saw the road.

Not a tunnel. A road. Paved with slabs of pale stone, wide enough for six abreast, running straight down into the dark with a confidence that made her skin crawl. Nothing natural ran straight. Nothing natural ran *down* with purpose.

"It knows we're here," Ashen said.

"The mark?"

"The mark. The road. Both." He came up beside her, and in the ember-light his scars stood out like a map of old wars. "From here on, we don't stop moving. And we don't stop watching. The under-roads rearrange when no one's looking. That's not a rumor. That's their nature. The court built them to be loyal to the mountain, not to travelers."

"Rearrange how?"

"You'll see. Try not to be behind me when you do."

* * *

She saw it in the third hour.

They had stopped to drink from a seep of water that wept from the wall—Ashen tasted it first and pronounced it clean, or at least clean the way under-mountain water was clean, which was a different thing. Odile had looked away for the length of a swallowed mouthful, no more. When she looked back, the road behind them was a wall.

Not rubble. Not collapse. A wall, fitted and mortared, ancient and seamless, as though the road had never existed there at all. And ahead, where the passage had bent gently left, it now bent gently right.

Her heart went off like a struck flint. "Ashen."

"I know." His voice was flat, controlled. "How long did you look away?"

"Two seconds. Maybe three."

"Then we have a rule now. Someone's eyes are on the road at all times. If we sleep, we sleep in shifts. If we eat, we eat walking." He turned in a slow circle, nose working. "It's testing us. Or it's herding us. With the roads, those are often the same thing."

"Herding us where?"

"Toward the court, if we're lucky. Toward something that eats travelers, if we're not." He looked at her, and the ember-light caught the gray of his muzzle. "Which is why we're not going to trust the road to choose for us. We're going to choose for ourselves. You said the weaver's map shows the junctions."

"It shows something." Odile unwrapped the oilcloth and held the woven map up to her hand's glow. The weaver's threads had been dyed in colors Odile couldn't name, and they shifted when she wasn't looking too—but slower than the road did, like the map remembered where things had been. "There should be a junction ahead where three roads meet. A place called—" she worked her mouth around the weaver's word— "*Vellum's Rest.*"

"Named for a traveler who rested there," Ashen said dryly, "and was never seen again, if the old stories have it right."

"Wonderful. Let's hurry."

* * *

The junction, when they reached it, was occupied.

They heard it before they saw it: a scrabbling, a grinding, the sound of many small bodies moving stone. Odile raised her hand and the mark's light spilled forward, and the floor of the junction moved.

Moles. But not the soft brown diggers of the valley fields. These were the size of her two fists together, gray as the rock they ate, eyeless, their forepaws broad as spades. There were dozens of them, and they were *repairing* the junction—filling in a passage mouth with rubble, methodically, the way a housekeeper fills a mouse-hole.

"Stone-moles," Ashen breathed. "I thought they'd gone. They were old when the mountain was young."

One of them turned its eyeless face toward her light and went utterly still. Then, in a voice like gravel poured slowly, it spoke—not to her, but to the others, a ripple of clicks and scrapes. Odile didn't need the words. She'd grown up on the speech of things without eyes. Fear sounded the same in every tongue.

She crouched, slow, and set her palm flat against the cold floor, and let her gift open the way she'd once opened it for a lame ewe in a dark barn—gently, without demand, the way you'd open a hand to show it held nothing.

*I'm not here to hurt you,* she said in the deep grammar of burrow and stone. *I'm only passing through.*

The junction went silent. Then the largest mole—the one who'd turned first—came forward, whiskers working, and pressed its blunt snout to her fingertips.

*You speak,* it said, and its voice in her mind was the sound of a tunnel being dug, patient and dark. *The two-legs who come down here carry fire and iron and take without asking. You ask. That is old. That is very old.*

"I need to reach the court beneath," she said aloud, because speaking aloud helped her think, and because Ashen needed to hear. "The under-roads keep changing. I need a path that stays put."

The mole made a sound that might have been laughter, if gravel could laugh. *The roads change because the mountain dreams. You cannot ask a dream to hold still. But we diggers know the bones under the dream. The old ways. The ways that were dug before the court built its pretty roads, back when the mountain was only rock and rock does not lie.* Its snout tapped the floor. *We will dig you a road that stays. But digging is work, and work is owed.*

"What do you want?" Odile asked. She was learning. Everything down here kept a ledger. At least the moles were honest about it.

*Warmth,* said the mole simply. *The mountain's heart has gone cold since the gods stopped being fed. Our young chill in the deep nests. Bring us warmth—any warmth, fire or flesh-heat or the heat of a promise—and we will dig you through to the court's back door before the thirteenth moon rises.*

Odile looked at her hand, at the branded mark burning there. She thought of the valley, of the hearth-fires she'd crept past all her life, of her mother's banked stove.

"I can't carry fire deep enough," she said slowly. "But—" and here the plan arrived whole, the way her best ideas always did, sideways and slightly reckless— "the court's mark on me is a *claim*. Claims burn. What if I gave you the heat of it—not the mark itself, I need that to get in—but the heat it makes? I could hold my hand against the stone of your deep nest-road, and the mark would warm it, and the warmth would stay. Stone holds heat longer than flesh. You told me so yourselves, in the way you dig toward the deep warm veins."

The moles went still again. Then a ripple ran through them, whisker to tail, and the largest one made its gravel-laugh once more.

*The two-legs offers to bank her own brand in our walls. The old ones would have liked you.* It turned and clicked an order, and the junction came alive, moles pouring into the rubble-choked passages and *unfilling* them, stone flying in disciplined streams. *Dig with us, walker. And keep your eyes on the road. The mountain is dreaming harder tonight. It knows something is coming that it does not want to dream.*

* * *

They traveled with the moles for what felt like a day and might have been three. Down here, Odile learned, time was another thing the mountain rearranged. She slept in shifts with Ashen, her hand on his back to anchor him, his eyes open over her while she slept. She held her branded palm to the stone at every rest, and the moles channeled the heat down their diggings into the dark, and somewhere far below, she liked to imagine, cold nests were warming.

In exchange, the moles taught her the mountain's bones.

*The dream-roads shift,* the eldest told her as they traveled, its voice rumbling up through the floor itself now, through her boot-soles. *But the bones do not. Here—* and it had her press her ear to the stone, and taught her to listen past the groan of shifting passages to the deep, slow sounds underneath: the tick of old faults settling, the hum of water in channels older than the court. *The dream-roads make noise like wind. The bones make noise like sleep. Follow the sleep.*

She got good at it. That was the thing that surprised her most, walking mile after mile of dark with her ear against the stone and her gift spread wide—not the bargaining, which she'd always known how to do, but the *fluency*. In the valley her gift had been a whisper she smothered under a jar. Down here it was a language, and she was discovering she was not a tourist in it. The bats came next, and the bats proved it.

* * *

The bats found them at a place where the bone-road crossed a vertical shaft that breathed cold air upward, slow and rhythmic, like the mountain exhaling in its sleep.

They poured out of the shaft in a river of leather wings, hundreds of them, and Odile's first instinct was the valley instinct—shrink, hide, be small. Ashen's growl rolled out before she could stop him.

*No,* she said, and put a hand on his shoulder. *Wait.*

She reached up into the dark with her gift, the way she'd once reached into a hayloft full of panicked swallows, and said, in the high thin grammar of echo and wing: *We're travelers. We're going down. We won't touch your young.*

The river of bats slowed. Swirled. Converged. And then a dozen of them hung in the air before her face, upside down, their blind faces inches from hers, and one of them—old, its wings tattered as the weaver's map—said:

*The speaker. The mark-bearer. The mountain's dreams are full of you.*

"Then the mountain dreams true," Odile said. "I need to know what's ahead. The moles gave me the bones of the road. But bones don't tell me what's *waiting* on the road. You fly the shafts. You see the roads from above, where the rearranging can't reach. What's between here and the court's back door?"

The old bat clicked its teeth, a dry sound like a lock turning. *Between here and the door is the Long Gallery, and the Long Gallery is watched.*

"Watched by what?"

*By the mountain's ear.* The bats stirred, a shiver of wings. *The court lost its tithe-collectors' road when the old prayers stopped—the collectors used to come and go by the surface, but the surface has grown strange to them, so now they listen down here instead. There is a chamber where the mountain's dreaming pools, and in that chamber the court has set a listening thing. It hears heartbeats. It hears the mark. It heard you the moment you entered, mark-bearer. It has been listening to you come for three days.*

Odile's stomach dropped. Ashen's growl came back, low and helpless.

"Then we're already caught," she said.

*No,* said the old bat, and there was something in its blind face that might have been respect. *Caught is for things that are heard and understood. You are heard, but you are strange. The listening thing hears your heartbeat and your mark, yes. But it also hears the moles, and the water, and the stone settling, and a thousand small honest noises of the deep. You are a strange noise among honest noises, and the listening thing is old and stupid with hunger—it was made to hear gods, and gods have stopped coming. It has forgotten what anything else sounds like.*

*But,* the bat went on, and Odile felt the word land like a stone, *it will remember when you get close. So you must pass it as the deep passes. Quiet heartbeat. Quiet mark. The moles can dig you a way that goes around the chamber—but around is long, and the moon is close, and the long way may be too long.*

"And through?"

*Through is short. Through is fast. Through is a heartbeat away from being heard.* The old bat folded its wings. *But we owe the deep a debt older than your grandmother's bargain, speaker, and the deep says you pay what you promise. So we will tell you the listening thing's one blindness. It hears hearts, but it does not hear stone that has been spoken to. Stone that has been told what to be, holds its sound. Your gift, speaker. The deep has heard you giving the rock its manners since you entered. Do it to the chamber walls. Tell the stone to hold its silence around you, and your heart's noise will not carry. But you must speak to the stone the whole way through, without stopping, without sleeping, and the chamber is a night's walk wide.*

"A night's walk of talking to rock," Ashen said flatly. "Without stopping."

*Without stopping,* the bat agreed. *And mark-bearer—when the mark burns, and it will burn, because the listening thing will pull at it like a hook pulls at a fish—the stone will want to answer your pain instead of your command. You must not let it. The stone obeys the voice, not the pain. If you cry out, the walls will cry with you.*

* * *

The chamber, when they came to it, was a cathedral of listening.

It was round and vast, its walls ribbed like the inside of a shell, and every rib was carved—or grown, she couldn't tell—into the shape of an ear. Hundreds of them. Thousands. All of them turned inward, all of them listening to the dark, and in the center of the floor sat the listening thing itself: a pool of absolute stillness, black as the space between stars, that did not reflect her hand's ember-light at all.

The mark on her hand screamed.

Not burned—*screamed*, a white heat that shot up her arm and into her teeth, and Odile understood suddenly and completely what the bat had meant. The pool had felt her. The pool was pulling at the brand the way a fisherman tests a line, and every instinct in her body was to cry out, to run, to make it stop.

She didn't. She put her palm flat against the wall—against the nearest stone ear, which was a grotesque intimacy she tried not to think about—and she spoke.

*Hold,* she said to the stone, in the deep grammar, in the voice the moles had taught her, the voice that was sleep and patience and the long memory of rock. *Hold your sound. Hold your silence. Be the deep, be the dark, be the sleep beneath the sleep. Nothing passes here but the deep itself. Nothing passes here but the deep.*

And the stone—blessed, honest stone—obeyed.

The screaming in her arm didn't stop, but it stopped *carrying*. She felt the difference the way you feel a shout swallowed by snow. Her heartbeat, her ragged breath, the crackle of her mark, all of it fell into the walls and stayed there, held, muffled, told what to be.

She walked. She talked. Ashen pressed against her left leg the whole way, and she drew strength from his warmth the way the moles drew heat from her brand, and she did not stop speaking, not for breath more than a word's length, not for the cramp in her jaw, not for the hour when the pool's pulling grew so hard she felt it in her back teeth like a toothache made of attention.

*Hold. Hold. Be the deep. Be the dark. Nothing passes here but the deep itself.*

Halfway across, the pool did something new. It *spoke*—not in words, but in a pressure, a question made of silence: *what are you, what are you, what are you.*

And Odile, walking, talking, holding the walls around her like a mother holds a child in thunder, answered it in the only way she could.

She made the stone lie for her. *Nothing,* she told the walls, and the walls told the pool, and the pool heard a chamber full of stone saying *nothing, nothing, nothing* in her voice, in the deep's voice, in a hundred honest stone voices. *Nothing passes here but the deep.*

The pulling eased. Grudgingly. The way a hand eases on a rope it suspects but cannot prove.

She talked the chamber dead. She talked it silent. And when the far wall finally rose out of the dark—real stone, mole-dug stone, warm with the heat she'd banked in it days ago—Odile's voice gave out on the last word, and she stumbled through into the passage beyond and folded up against the warm rock, and Ashen's body was there before she finished falling.

For a long time neither of them spoke. Her hand throbbed in time with her heart. Her throat was ruined silk.

"You talked a god's ear off," Ashen said at last. "There's a joke in there somewhere. I'm too tired to find it."

"Find it later." She pressed her forehead to the warm stone. "Ashen. The pool knew something was there. Even through the stone, it knew. It's going to tell the court."

"Maybe. Or maybe all it can say is *nothing, nothing, nothing*, in your voice, forever." He laid his chin on her knee, and his eyes were tired and proud and old. "You did down here what you couldn't do in the valley. Do you see it yet?"

"See what?"

"Your gift was never the thing you hid. It was the thing you hid *behind*." He stood, shook the dark off his coat, and looked down the passage—down toward the back door of the court beneath the mountain, which the moles' warm road had delivered them to at last, a seam of paler stone in the black, breathing cold air that smelled of moonlight and old bone.

Beyond it, faint as a memory, she could hear singing. A hundred voices, hollow and silver, chanting the hours of a court that was about to be fed.

And under the singing, closer, from the seam itself: the sound of footsteps. Unhurried. Coming toward the door from the other side.

Someone knew they were there.
