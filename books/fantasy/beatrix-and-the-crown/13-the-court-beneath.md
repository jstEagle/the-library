## 13. The Court Beneath

The under-road ended the way a held breath ends.

One moment Odile was crawling through a passage that smelled of cold iron and old stone, Ashen's breath warm against her ankle, the mark on her hand burning like a coal pressed into her palm—and the next, the ceiling simply opened, and she was standing in a cathedral built of bone.

Not bone-shaped. Bone. Ribs arched overhead in vaults wide enough to swallow the whole valley, each one pale as milk and veined with faint silver light. Pillars of knuckle and vertebra rose into darkness so far above that the light gave out before it found a roof. The floor beneath her feet was smooth as river glass, and when she looked down she saw stars in it—stars moving, drifting slow as fish under ice.

Moonlight came from everywhere and nowhere. It pooled on the floor in great silver sheets, and where it pooled, Odile could see that the cathedral was not empty.

They sat in tiers along the walls, rising like a parliament or a jury or a congregation that had been sitting there since before the world learned to count: the old gods. She had imagined them from the ledger's margins, from the weaver's careful warnings, from Ashen's low growls at night—but imagining had not prepared her for *this*.

Some were vast and half-formed, shapes of storm-water and root and smoke that only occasionally bothered to have faces. Some were small as children and sharp as knives. One wore the pelt of a bear with nothing inside it but starlight; another was a woman to the waist and a river below it, and its hair moved against a current that wasn't there. A god made entirely of threshing-floor dust turned its head when she stepped onto the glass, and Odile's heart slammed once, hard, before it looked away again.

"They don't see us," Ashen breathed beside her ear. His scarred flank pressed against her leg, solid and warm, the one real thing in all this pale enormity. "Not yet. Not unless they're looking. Keep to the shadow of the pillars. And Odile—" his voice dropped until it was more growl than word, "—whatever you see, whatever you feel, do not speak first. In this place, speech is an offering. Speak only when you choose to give."

She nodded, because her throat had closed.

*You are a soul-servant now,* she reminded herself. That had been the plan, hatched in the dark of the under-roads while bats whispered directions in exchange for the promise of a fat summer moth: the mark on her hand would read as service, not trespass, if she walked like she belonged here. Shoulders down. Eyes lowered. Move like someone who has been hollowed and filled with errands.

She had practiced the walk for two days. It felt obscene. It felt necessary.

* * *

They moved along the wall of ribs, pillar to pillar, and Odile made herself look. Made herself learn.

The gods were quarreling.

It took her a moment to understand that the sound filling the cathedral—the low, ceaseless wash of it, like surf heard through a mountain—was argument. Voices layered over voices, some beautiful, some grinding, some speaking languages that hurt her teeth. A god of hearth-smoke accused a god of winter of hoarding warmth-prayers. Something enormous and blind demanded to know who had eaten the last of the harvest songs. Near the front, two gods who shared a single body between them argued with themselves, each mouth saying opposite things, and both mouths were right, and both mouths were furious.

And underneath the quarrel, thin as thread, ran the hunger.

She felt it before she understood it—a pulling sensation behind her sternum, gentle as a hook through cloth. Every god in that cathedral was starving. She could see it now that she knew to look: the way the great shapes flickered at their edges, the way the small ones had gone translucent as communion wafers, the way every head turned, again and again, toward the doors at the far end of the hall.

Toward the souls coming in.

They came in a line. Odile watched them cross the moonlit floor—figures walking slowly, barefoot, unhurried, their eyes silver as coins at the bottom of a well. Some were old. Some were young. One was a child carrying a wooden horse she no longer seemed to remember how to hold. They did not weep. They did not stumble. They walked to the base of the tallest pillar, where a basin of moonlight stood like a font, and they stepped into it one by one, and the light drank them, and the gods leaned forward in their tiers like beggars at a bread window, and somewhere high above something sighed with something that was almost satisfaction and almost grief.

Odile's legs went cold. Ashen's teeth closed gently—not painfully, just firmly—on the hem of her coat, holding her still.

"Not here," he murmured. "Not yet. Breathe."

She breathed. She kept her eyes down. She walked.

* * *

It was the wine that broke her.

There was a procession moving through the central aisle—servants, dozens of them, all with that same silver-eyed calm, carrying ewers and cups up the tiers to the quarreling parliament. The wine itself caught the moonlight strangely; it poured thick and slow, and where a drop fell to the glass floor it did not splash but sank, and Odile realized with a lurch that it wasn't wine at all. It was memory. She could taste it in the air, faint as a neighbor's cooking: someone's wedding day, someone's mother's voice, someone's first snow. The gods drank remembrance because prayers had grown too thin to feed them.

And then she saw him.

He was third in the line of servants, pouring for a god of gates and hinges—a lean god whose body was a doorframe standing upright, whose face was a keyhole. Tam held the ewer in both hands, steady, graceful, his arm extended in an arc of perfect service. He wore gray. His hair had grown long and lank. He moved without any of the loose, laughing clumsiness that Odile had spent seventeen years cataloguing—the way he'd tripped over the gate latch every spring, the way he whistled through his teeth when he thought no one listened, the way he'd once carried her a mile home on his back when she sprained her ankle, narrating an epic about it the whole way.

None of that was in him now. What was in him was a shape wearing her brother's body, and it smiled at the doorframe-god with lips that curved correctly, and it said, "Will there be anything else?" in a voice like a room after everyone has left it.

The ewer tilted. Silver liquid ribboned into the cup. Tam's eyes never blinked.

Odile stopped walking.

The mark on her hand flared white-hot, and she understood dimly, distantly, that it was warning her—that her heart had become a shout in a quiet room, that grief this loud could be *heard*. Somewhere in the tiers, a head turned. She didn't see which one. She fixed her gaze on the floor, on the drifting stars beneath the glass, and she put one foot in front of the other, and she did what she had done her whole life in the Valley of Stillwater: she made herself small, she made herself quiet, she made herself nothing.

But inside, where no god could reach, something screamed her brother's name until it was ragged.

*Tam. Tam. I'm here. Hold on. I'm here.*

His head did not turn. But as she passed, three pillars away, close enough to count the stitches in his gray sleeve, the ewer trembled—one fine, fine shiver across the surface of the poured memory, like a reflection disturbed by wind.

Then it stilled, and he moved on, and Odile bit the inside of her cheek until she tasted blood and kept walking, because that was the whole of the law in this place: keep walking, keep silent, keep your soul behind your teeth.

* * *

They found a hollow between two rib-pillars where the moonlight thinned, and there Odile crouched with her back against bone older than nations and shook until her teeth chattered.

"I saw him." Her whisper came out in pieces. "Ashen, I saw him, he's—he pours their wine, he asked if there would be *anything else*, he didn't—" Her hands wouldn't stay still. She pressed them flat against her knees. "He didn't know me. He was three steps away and he didn't know me."

"He knows nothing," Ashen said quietly. "That is what they take first. Not the body. The knowing." His good eye reflected the distant basin of moonlight where the souls went in. "The body keeps. The body serves. I have seen souls serve in this hall for centuries, Odile. The court is patient. It does not spend what it can keep."

"Then how—"

"How will you get him back? I don't know yet." Honesty, at least; he had never lied to her, not even kindly. "But I know you cannot do it tonight, weeping behind a pillar. You need to understand this place. Its law is older than its hunger, and its hunger obeys the law. Learn the law, and you'll find the crack in it. There is always a crack. Your grandmother taught me that, if she taught me nothing else."

Odile wiped her face with the heel of her hand. Under her sleeve, the mark pulsed, slower now, sated by her fear perhaps, or merely patient. She thought of the stag's dying words in the burnt country: *the door works both ways.* She thought of the weaver's harder truth: *the court may simply refuse to trade.* She thought of Tam's trembling ewer, that one shiver of the brother still buried somewhere inside the servant.

He was in there. Whatever they had taken, they had not taken everything. Souls were being drunk from a basin upstairs, memories poured like wine, and still something in Tam had shivered at the nearness of her. Love left residue. Even here.

"All right," she said. "Teach me the law."

"There isn't time to teach. So we listen." Ashen nodded toward the tiers, where the quarrel had risen in volume. "Gods gossip worse than crows, and crows at least have the decency to charge for it. Sit. Watch. Count who owes whom."

So she sat, and she watched, and she counted.

* * *

The hours—if hours meant anything under a moon that never set—taught her more than she wanted to know.

She learned that the parliament had seats, actual seats, carved into the ribs of the cathedral, and that rank was measured in *fullness*: the gods who glowed steadiest sat highest, and the ones flickering at the edges of existence sat lowest, closest to the floor, closest to the doors where the souls came in. She learned that the doorframe-god collected tolls and hated the Debt-God, whose name the other gods would not say aloud, and that hatred, among the hungry, was a kind of currency too.

She learned that the silver-eyed servants slept standing in alcoves between sessions, folded up like tools, and that no one guarded them, because no one had ever come for one. Why would anyone? Who crosses into the country of the hungry gods to steal back a debt?

Someone had, once. A girl with a stolen soul-token, generations ago. The weaver's story sat in Odile's chest like a swallowed coal. Her grandmother had run from this place. Odile had walked into it.

And she learned—crouched in the bone-shadow, Ashen a warm weight against her side—that the court was afraid.

It showed only in glimpses, in the pauses between quarrels, but once she saw it she couldn't unsee it. The lowest gods glanced constantly at the doors. The great ones spoke of *the last prayer* the way starving men speak of the last loaf. One ancient shape, mostly smoke, said into a lull in the argument, in a voice like ash settling: "When the thirteenth moon completes its turn, the accounts come due. All of them." And a dozen heads turned toward it, and the silence that followed had teeth.

The thirteenth moon. The ticking clock her grandmother's ledger had started. Due date of a bargain struck before Odile was born.

Something was going to happen when that moon rose. Something the gods feared, or wanted, or both.

"Ashen," she breathed. "The thirteenth moon. What happens—"

A bell rang.

It had no rope and no tower; it simply rang, a note that came up through the glass floor and down through the vaulted ribs at once, and every god in the cathedral went still. Even the smoke stopped smoking. The servants in their alcoves unfolded in perfect unison, a hundred gray figures straightening like wheat before wind, and Odile's stomach dropped through the glass and the stars beneath it, because she knew that movement. She had seen it an hour ago, in miniature, in the tilt of a wine ewer.

The parliament was convening. Fully. Formally.

"The moon turns tomorrow night," Ashen murmured, and his fur stood along his spine. "They're calling the full session early. That hasn't happened in—" He stopped. His scarred eye narrowed at the highest tier, where the fullest gods were taking their seats, and his voice went very quiet. "Odile. Look at the doors."

At the far end of the cathedral, where the souls came in, the twin doors of moonlight were opening wider than she had yet seen them—wide enough to drive a cart through, wide enough for something large to enter.

And entering it, unhurried, wrapped in a cloak of ordinary darkness that somehow made every god in the hall lean toward it, came a figure Odile knew.

Hollow-eyed. Patient. Carrying a ledger of its own.

The tithe-collector had found the court—and it was walking straight up the aisle, past pillar after pillar, toward the tiers where the gods assembled, and Odile would have sworn on her grandmother's grave that as it passed her hiding place, its blank face turned, just slightly, toward the shadow between the ribs.

Toward her.

The mark on her hand began, very softly, to sing.
