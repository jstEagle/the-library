## 14. A Seat at the Parliament of Gods

The wine was warm.

That was the first thing Odile understood about the court beneath the mountain: everything here was almost right, and the almost was worse than wrong. The wine in her tray should have been cold—it was a dark red vintage, the kind her mother had once described from a wedding she'd attended before Odile was born—but it sat blood-warm in its cups, and the cups themselves were bone, thin as eggshell, carved with names she couldn't read.

She carried the tray because carrying it kept her alive.

Three days—she thought it had been three days; time moved strangely under the root of the world—since she had walked through the last gate of the under-roads with Ashen's parting words in her ears. *They don't look at servants. Servants are furniture.* He'd pressed his scarred muzzle to her marked hand one final time, the brand searing where his breath touched it, and then he had turned back into the dark to find another way in, because a wolf, even a wolf who remembered centuries, could not pass for furniture.

So she walked alone through the cathedral of bone and moonlight, and she did not look up, and she did not look at Tam.

She looked anyway. She couldn't help it. Every circuit of the long hall brought her past the dais where the gods sat in their quarreling parliament, and every circuit brought her past the figure who moved among them with a silver ewer, pouring wine into bone cups with hands that were still her brother's hands, longer now than she remembered, paler, the knuckles sharp as cutlery.

Tam's eyes were silver. Not glinting silver, not the flash of light on water—silver all the way through, iris and white alike, like coins sunk in milk. When he poured, he did not spill. When the gods snapped their fingers, he was there before the snap finished. And when he passed within arm's reach of Odile, carrying his own tray on the opposite circuit, he did not see her at all.

That was the mercy and the horror of it. She had spent three days rehearsing what she would say if he saw her—*Tam, it's me, it's Odile, I came for you*—and three days discovering that the words didn't matter, because there was nothing behind his eyes left to hear them. The court had drunk whatever it wanted from him and left the shape.

*Don't reveal yourself before you understand the rules.* That was what the stag had said, dying in the burnt country with the collector's mark still smoking on her hand. *A door that works both ways, girl. Learn which way is which.*

So she learned.

She learned by listening, because that was the one gift no god had thought to forbid. The parliament never stopped talking. That surprised her at first—she had expected silence, dread, the hush of a temple—but the old gods bickered like farmers at a market over a bad harvest, and in a way, that was exactly what they were.

"—the third tithe this season and *thin*, thin as ditchwater," a goddess was complaining as Odile refilled her cup. She was vast and gray, draped in robes that might have been fog or might have been hair, and her voice came out of her like wind off a lake. "You call this worship? A child's mumbled grace before supper? I have forgotten more prayers than this whole valley remembers."

"The valley remembers nothing," said another god, lean and brown and jointed like a locust, "that's rather the point of it. Your Brightwater bargain bought your silence, sister, and now you starve politely in the dark and blame the menu."

"I blame the *cook*."

Odile's hands did not shake. She had learned that too—her hands belonged to the tray, and the tray belonged to the hall, and if she let anything tremble, the whole chain would rattle. She moved down the row of seats, pouring, and the gray goddess took her cup without looking, and Odile caught the smell of her: rain on stone, deep and old, the smell of the thing that had answered her prayer over a lame ewe half a lifetime ago.

*Something ancient,* she had thought then. *Something that heard me through the valley's smothering silence.*

Now she knew what had heard her. Now she knew why the ground had trembled. A starving god, feeling the first true prayer to reach it in a generation, had leaned against the walls of the world hard enough to shake them—and then, being a god of debts, had done what debt-gods do.

It had waited to be repaid.

* * *

The law revealed itself on the fourth day, in the middle of an argument so vicious that two lesser gods actually rose from their thrones.

It began, as most things in the court began, with a soul.

The soul in question stood in the center of the hall, on a disc of moonlight that fell from somewhere above—a hole in the vaulted bone ceiling through which Odile could just see a sliver of sky, and in that sliver, impossibly, the moon. It was not the moon she knew. It was swollen, cracked along one edge like old porcelain, and it hung lower than any moon had a right to hang, close enough that she could see the shadow of mountains on its face.

The thirteenth moon. She felt it in the brand on her hand before she understood it in her head: a slow, patient heat, like a hand warming itself at a fire that hadn't been lit yet.

The soul was a farmer. She could tell by the hands, by the sunburn even death hadn't fully faded, by the way he stood the way men stand when they're waiting for a verdict they already know. Two hollow-eyed collectors flanked him—the same kind of creature that had branded her on the burnt road, though these wore livery, bone-white tabards stitched with silver thread.

"Claimed against the harvest-bargain of the Corrow lands," one collector intoned. "Third generation. Debt due and collected."

"He died owing," said the other. "One soul, standard weight."

And then an old woman pushed forward from the crowd of petitioners and servants and lesser spirits that lined the hall's edges, and Odile's heart went into her throat, because the woman was *angry*, furious, unafraid, shaking a knotted stick at the parliament itself.

"Standard weight!" the woman cried. "My husband plowed the Corrow fields forty years! The bargain took his father and his grandfather, and now it takes him, and for what? For *rain*? For rain we dug our own wells for, in the end?" Her voice cracked across the hall like a whip. "I renegotiate. You hear me? I invoke the law. Witnesses—" she jabbed the stick at the assembled gods, at the servants, at Odile, at everyone and no one— "*witnesses* are present. I offer a greater price. Take my years instead. Take fifty of my years and give me back my husband's soul!"

The hall went quiet in a way that made the earlier quiet seem loud.

And then the gray goddess laughed—not cruelly, which somehow made it worse, but with the tired amusement of someone who has seen this play performed ten thousand times. "Oh, the *law*. Yes. Let us say it plainly, since the widow has been bold enough to say it first." She rose, and her fog-hair robes unfurled until they brushed the floor. "Any debt may be renegotiated before witnesses, if a greater price is offered. That is the oldest law of this court, older than the mountain, older than some of my siblings." She smiled, and her smile was full of patience. "But hear the whole of it, widow. The court must weigh the offer. And if the court finds the offerer *more valuable* than the price—" her smile widened— "the court may take the offerer instead."

The widow's stick lowered slowly.

"That is the law," the goddess finished gently. "We did not write it to be cruel. We wrote it because everything offered to a hungry thing is food, and we are very, very hungry."

For a moment the widow stood perfectly still. Then she nodded once, sharply, like a woman signing a contract she'd read to the last line, and said, "Then weigh us," and the hall filled with a sound like a great bell struck underwater, and Odile turned back to her circuit before she could watch what happened next, because watching was how servants got noticed, and noticing was how servants became souls.

But she had heard it. All of it.

*Any debt may be renegotiated before witnesses, if a greater price is offered.*

*The court may take the offerer instead.*

Her mind, which had been a small trapped animal for three days, went suddenly, terribly still. Because Tam's soul was a debt. Grandmother's bargain, generations old, due when the thirteenth moon completed its turn—and the thirteenth moon hung overhead now, cracked and low and nearly full. Which meant the debt was not yet *closed*. It was open, on the table, in front of witnesses, in a hall where the law of renegotiation had just been spoken aloud for anyone to hear.

Anyone. Even furniture.

* * *

She found the flaw in her plan the same night, and the flaw had a name.

She was stacking cups in the scullery—a cavern of dripping bone arches where soul-servants worked in silence—when the temperature of the room changed. Not colder. *Attentive.* The way a room changes when something in it starts paying attention.

"You count well," said a voice behind her. "Most of them can't count. They pour and pour and never notice when a cup comes back short."

She turned. The god standing between the arches was smaller than the others, worn, wrapped in a coat patched with a hundred different cloths, and his eyes were the color of ledgers closed for the night. She knew him instantly. Rain-on-stone. The smell of the thing that had answered a frightened girl praying over a lame ewe in a valley that forgot.

The Debt-God.

He tilted his head at her, and his gaze dropped, deliberately, to her left hand—to the mark beneath the servant's wrappings, the brand that burned whenever the thirteenth moon shifted in its cradle of sky.

"Your cups," he said mildly, "are going to come back short one of these nights, little servant. And when they do, this court will ask where you learned to count."

Odile's mouth was dry. Every instinct screamed at her to bow, to mumble, to be furniture. But the stag's voice was in her ear—*learn which way is which*—and the widow's voice, too—*then weigh us*—and underneath both, steady as a heartbeat, the memory of Tam at nine years old teaching her to whistle through a blade of grass, laughing so hard he fell in the creek.

She bowed. Exactly as deep as a servant should. No deeper.

"The cups are counted properly," she said quietly. "I promise you that."

Something flickered across the god's worn face. Recognition, maybe—or the pleasure of a creditor who has just watched a debtor show her teeth. He studied her a long moment, and the brand on her hand burned hotter with each second, singing the moon's slow approach.

"No," he agreed softly. "I don't suppose they'll come back short at all." He stepped closer, and his voice dropped to almost nothing, a secret priced and sold in the same breath. "The parliament convenes at moonrise tomorrow, little servant. Full session. Both offers will be weighed—the widow's, and one other that has been whispered about since before you were born." His ledger-colored eyes held hers. "Debts of blood come due before witnesses. And this court is always, *always* hungry."

Then he straightened, adjusted his patchwork coat, and walked away into the dripping dark, leaving Odile alone among the bone arches with her heart hammering and her mind racing faster than fear could follow.

Because he knew. He had known since the valley—known her, known her gift, known whose daughter she was—and he had said *nothing*. Had let her walk these halls unseen, unchallenged, carrying her tray past the brother she'd come to save.

Which meant he wanted her here. Which meant he wanted something.

And tomorrow, at moonrise, the parliament would convene, and the thirteenth moon would hang at its fullest, and the debt of the Brightwater family would be weighed before witnesses—

—and Odile Brightwater, soul-servant, cup-counter, liar in a borrowed uniform, intended to stand up in that hall and offer the gods a price greater than any soul they had ever tasted.

She only had to survive long enough to speak.
