## 15. The Debt-God's Bargain

The Debt-God came for her in the hour when the court's moonlight went thin, when the bone cathedral's pillars drank their glow and the quarreling parliament dissolved into alcoves and shadow. Odile had learned the rhythm of it by then—two days as a soul-servant, carrying ewers of wine that tasted like other people's childhoods, keeping her branded hand hidden inside her sleeve, keeping her eyes down the way the hollow ones did.

She was scrubbing a basin near the parliament's eastern colonnade when the air beside her changed temperature. Not colder. Older.

"You scrub like your grandmother," said a voice like coins settling at the bottom of a well. "She never could leave a thing dirty. Even when dirt was the safest place to hide."

Odile's hands went still in the water. She did not look up. In two days she had learned that much: you did not look up at gods unless invited.

"Little Brightwater," the Debt-God said. "Or should I say little *Beatrix*? The name runs in families like water runs downhill."

Her heart slammed against her ribs. "I don't know what you mean."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You don't. That's rather the point of you." A long finger, gray as candle-wax, hooked under her chin and lifted her face to his. Up close, the god who collected debts in memory had eyes like ledger pages—ruled lines and faint columns of numbers drifting behind the irises, entries being made even now, even while he looked at her. His face was kind the way a contract is kind: precise, binding, offering exactly what it says and nothing more.

"Walk with me," he said. It was not a request.

* * *

He led her through corridors Odile hadn't known existed, past doors of whale-bone and doors of birch, until they came to a small chamber where a single shaft of true moonlight fell through a crack in the mountain's root and struck a table of black stone. On the table lay an object that made Odile's breath catch: a prayer-bowl, clay-fired, unglazed, chipped at the rim.

She knew that chip. She had traced it with her thumb a hundred times, sitting on her grandmother's knee, asking why the bowl sat on the high shelf where no one used it.

"That's mine," she said before she could stop herself. "That's—that was my grandmother's."

"It is," the Debt-God agreed. "And before that, it was the vessel of the last prayer spoken openly in the Valley of Stillwater. Do you know what a prayer sounds like, from our side of the world?"

He didn't wait for her answer. He turned the bowl over in his gray fingers, and sound poured out of it—not words, but the shape of words, a woman's voice cracked with exhaustion and love and something close to desperation:

*Keep them safe. Keep my children safe, and their children, and theirs. I will give whatever the giving costs. Only keep the hungry things away from Stillwater.*

Odile knew that voice. She had heard it every day of her childhood, singing over bread dough, muttering over lame ewes, telling stories by the fire. Her knees nearly buckled.

"She prayed," Odile whispered. "Grandmother prayed. But the valley—the valley has no magic. Nothing answers there. The stones eat it. That's what everyone says."

"Everyone says," the Debt-God murmured, "because everyone was told to say it. Your valley is not dead, child. Your valley is *fed*. Fed on one soul per generation, paid into our court beneath the mountain, and in exchange—" he gestured with the bowl, and the moonlight shaft flickered, and for one instant Odile saw through the walls, saw the Valley of Stillwater laid out like a map seen from very high above, its stones glowing faintly with a warding light, a shell of quiet wrapped around it like cupped hands—"in exchange, silence. Safety. Smothering, if you like. A candle under a jar burns longer than a candle in the wind. We made your family a jar."

"The bargain," Odile said. Her mouth had gone dry. "The ledger. One soul owed. Due when the thirteenth moon completes its turn."

"One soul owed," he confirmed. "Your grandmother signed it with her own blood and her own true name, which I will not speak here because names are currency and I am not wasteful. She bought your valley's safety with a promise: that when the debt came due, a Brightwater would walk here freely and pay it. Not dragged. Not stolen. *Freely.* That is why we waited. That is why the mark found your hand instead of your throat." He tilted his head. "You came. Remarkable. Most debtors run until the running kills them."

"I came for my brother."

"Yes. Tam." The god's eyes drifted through their ledger-columns, reading. "A good soul. Sweet-tempered. The hollow work suits him poorly—he weeps in his sleep, did you know? Souls do that, the fresh ones. The old ones forget how." Something moved across his wax-gray face that might have been distaste. "Which brings us to the difficulty."

"Difficulty."

"The court is starving, Odile Brightwater." For the first time his pleasantness dropped away, and what lay underneath was vast and tired and terribly old. "You have seen us. A parliament of quarrelsome shadows, drinking wine pressed from memories because nothing else is offered. Once, prayers rose from every hearth in the wild country—small prayers, grateful prayers, *thank-you-for-the-rain* prayers, *keep-the-wolf-from-the-door* prayers. Worship is food. Gratitude is food. And the world stopped setting the table." He set the prayer-bowl down with a click that echoed. "We are gods of small debts, and no one owes us anything anymore, and a god who starves becomes something else. Something with teeth. You have met the tithe-collector. Imagine a hundred of him. Imagine what the court becomes in another century of hunger."

"So take the debt," Odile said. "Take Tam's soul. That's what you're owed. That's the whole point of me walking here."

"And then?" The god spread his hands. "Then Tam serves wine for a thousand years and forgets his own name, and your valley's ward renews, and in another generation another Brightwater walks this road, and another, forever. That is the bargain as written. Unless." He paused, and the ledger-eyes fixed on her with terrible attention. "Unless a greater price is offered. Court law. Any debt may be renegotiated before witnesses, if the new price exceeds the old. You learned that already, I think, from watching. From listening. You have clever ears, little Brightwater. Your grandmother's ears."

Odile's skin prickled. "What price?"

"Memories." He said it softly, almost tenderly. "Not Tam's. Yours. Every memory you carry of him—his first steps, his laughter, the way he looked at you when your mother died, every shared meal, every quarrel, every secret. All of it. The love braided through it. Love is the richest food left in the world, Odile Brightwater, and yours for your brother is the finest vintage I have tasted in three hundred years." He leaned closer, and the moonlight caught the ruled lines of his eyes. "Give us that, freely, and Tam walks free. Whole. His soul returned, his name restored, the debt discharged entire. He goes home and lives his life and never knows what it cost you."

The chamber was very quiet. Somewhere far above, water dripped through the mountain's root, counting seconds.

"He wouldn't remember me either," Odile said slowly. "If I lose all my memories of him. He'd come home and I'd be—I'd be a stranger in his house."

"A stranger who saved him," the god said. "Is that so terrible a trade? Think of it fairly. You lose the past. He gains the future. Grief fades, they say. Memory fades anyway—ask any mortal. You would only be doing early what time does to everyone." His voice gentled into something almost seductive. "And you would feel nothing of the loss, child. That is the mercy in the design. Memories given cannot mourn themselves. You would wake tomorrow knowing only that you once had a brother, and that he is home, and that some price was paid you cannot recall. An ache without a shape. People live whole lifetimes inside such aches."

It sounded fair. That was the horror of it—it sounded *fair*, the way a moneylender's terms sound fair, the way a jar sounds like protection. Odile thought of Tam's laugh, the gap in his teeth, the way he'd held her hand at their mother's grave. She thought of losing it and felt her mind flinch away from the flinching, unable even to imagine the absence properly.

And that inability was the trap's teeth closing.

"You've been starving," she said quietly. "All of you. For how long?"

"Centuries."

"And my grandmother's prayer—her fear, her love, her desperation—that fed you too, didn't it? Desperate prayers reach deepest. That's what the crows told me. That's why the old gods woke. Not because the valley broke its silence. Because the prayers got desperate enough to be heard again." She looked at him. "My family's fear has been your harvest for four generations."

The Debt-God did not deny it. He picked up the prayer-bowl and turned it slowly, the chipped rim catching light.

"Your grandmother bargained well," he said. "For her time. Silence in exchange for safety, in an age when the hungry things walked openly and ate children in their beds. She was fifteen years old, Odile. Fleeing this court with more courage than sense, and she traded you all a jar to live in, and the jar held. Judge her kindly if you can. She paid for it her whole life—we watched her pay, year after year, in the currency of never quite sleeping soundly." His ledger-eyes softened by some fraction. "I liked her. I collect debts; I know what they weigh. She carried hers without complaint for sixty years. When she died, I marked the account closed and the new account open, and I confess I wondered which of her grandchildren would come. I hoped it would be you."

"Why?"

"Because you pray," he said simply. "Over ewes. Over lame sheep, in a valley where prayer is forbidden, you kneel in the dirt and whisper thanks and petitions to powers you've been told don't exist. Do you know what that is, from where I stand? That is a hearth-fire lit in a house the world condemned. The first true worship offered in this country in a generation—and it came from *you*, untaught, unwarned, at fourteen years old, to a ewe." He smiled, and for one moment the smile was not contractual at all. "The ground trembled. You remember. That was me, listening. I have been listening to you for six years, Odile Brightwater, waiting for the debt to come due, wondering if you would ever lift your eyes from the dirt and ask for something larger."

The chamber swayed around her. Six years. The trembling ground. The crack of wonder she had spent her whole life learning to smother, answered all along by a god crouched at the valley's edge with his ear to the stones.

"What happens," she asked, "if I refuse?"

"Then the debt stands as written. Tam stays. The valley keeps its silence. And in a century or two, when the last of our strength fails, the wards fail with it, and everything your grandmother bought comes due at once—with interest." He shrugged, gray shoulders rising and falling. "I do not threaten. I merely read the ledger aloud. Threats are for gods with armies. I have only arithmetic."

"And if I accept?"

"Then we go before witnesses, tonight or tomorrow night—the thirteenth moon rises soon, and doors open best under it—and the renegotiation is sealed. Tam walks free under the full thirteenth moon. You walk free also. Everyone gets what they wanted." He extended his hand, palm up, wax-gray and patient. "Well, almost everyone. The memories stay with us. As worship. As food. As—" the ledger-eyes flickered, and Odile saw, just for a heartbeat, genuine hunger there, ancient and bottomless, "—as the finest meal I will have eaten in three hundred years."

Odile looked at the offered hand. She thought of Ashen, somewhere in the dark of the under-roads, waiting, his scarred face turned toward the mountain. She thought of the stag's warning: *the door works both ways.* She thought of her grandmother's voice in the bowl—*I will give whatever the giving costs*—and understood, finally, that she was standing in the exact shape of her grandmother's life, being handed the exact same pen.

But Tam. Tam weeping in his sleep, hollow-eyed, pouring wine for shadows. Tam coming home.

"How much time do I have?" she asked.

The Debt-God withdrew his hand, unhurried, and consulted the drifting columns of his eyes.

"The thirteenth moon rises tomorrow night," he said. "At moonrise, the door opens, and the court convenes to hear all offers before witnesses. Come with your answer. Come with your price. Or come with nothing, and watch your brother pour wine forever." He turned toward the dark of the corridor, then paused. "One kindness, freely given, since you gave me six years of free listening: whatever you decide, decide it *whole*. Half-offered prices bounce. The court does not forgive bounced payments." A pause. "And child—if you bring the wolf, tell him the witness-clause still stands. He will know what I mean."

He was gone between one drip of water and the next, leaving only the prayer-bowl on the black table and the thin blade of moonlight, and Odile alone with the arithmetic of her own heart.

She touched the bowl's chipped rim, once, the way she had as a child.

Then she went to find Ashen, because she had an answer forming—half-formed, dangerous, not yet whole—and one night left to make it true.
