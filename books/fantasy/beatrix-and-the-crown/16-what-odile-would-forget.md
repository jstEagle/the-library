## 16. What Odile Would Forget

The Debt-God's chamber smelled of cold candlewax and old paper. He had left her alone with the offer, which was its own kind of cruelty—gods, Odile was learning, rarely needed to be cruel on purpose. They simply had to wait.

She sat on a bench of black stone in a room that was not quite a room, more like the inside of a held breath, and she did what she had been doing since he left: she counted.

Not coins. Not hours. Memories.

*One.* Tam at four years old, standing ankle-deep in the millrace, holding up a frog with both hands as if it were a crown he'd found in the mud. *Look, Dilly. Look what I caught for you.* The frog's heartbeat against his palms. She had felt it through her gift even then, before she knew she had a gift—the small wet drum of another creature's life, offered to her like a secret.

*Two.* Tam teaching himself to whistle through a blade of grass behind the barn, failing forty times, succeeding once, and then refusing to demonstrate again because "it doesn't count if you're watching."

*Three.* The winter their mother died, when Tam was nine and Odile eleven, and he had crawled into her bed without asking and said nothing all night, just pressed his cold feet against her shins, and she had understood that this was how he said *I am frightened* and also *I know you are too.*

She had made a list in her head, over and over, since the Debt-God laid out the terms: *your memories of him, freely given, every one.* A list of everything she would lose. And beneath that list, a second list she hadn't meant to make—a list of what she could bear to give up.

That second list was short. That was the problem.

The taste of stolen apples split between them under the plum tree. Gone. Fine. She could live without stolen apples.

The sound of him laughing so hard at the midsummer fair that he snorted, and their grandmother laughed harder, and a passing stranger smiled without knowing why. Gone. Harder. But bearable.

The way he'd stood between her and their father's anger the first time she was caught talking to the goats, fourteen years old and trembling, saying *she wasn't doing anything wrong,* which was the bravest lie anyone had ever told for her sake.

No.

Her hands closed into fists on her knees.

*No,* she thought. *Not that one.*

And there it was—the trap inside the bargain, the thing the Debt-God had not bothered to hide because he didn't need to. She could not choose which memories to give. He had been clear about that. *All of them,* he'd said, with the gentle terrible patience of a creditor reading terms aloud. *Memory is not a purse from which you may draw one coin. It is a cloth. Pull one thread and the whole weave comes away.*

So it was not a question of which memories she could spare. It was a question of whether she could survive becoming a person who had never had a brother.

She tried to imagine it. She sat very still and tried.

A girl named Odile Brightwater, alone in the valley, no one to press cold feet against her shins, no one who had ever defended her, no one whose laugh she knew better than her own heartbeat. A girl with a hole in her shaped exactly like Tam, and worse—not knowing it was a hole. Just walking around with the absence and calling it her whole self.

She couldn't do it. Her mind slid off the imagining like rain off slate.

And yet—

And yet Ashen's voice came back to her, low, from the night before, when they'd sheltered in the Debt-God's antechamber among shelves of unlabeled jars: *There are other ledgers, Odile.*

* * *

She found him at the edge of the court's outer gallery, where the bone columns gave way to rough-hewn stone and the moonlight that fed this place ran thin as watered milk. He lay with his scarred flank against the floor, chin on his paws, watching the distant glow of the parliament hall where the gods murmured like a hive in winter.

"You've been rehearsing," he said. Not asking. Wolves' noses, he'd told her once, could smell grief the way hers could hear a sparrow's fear.

"I've been counting," she said. She sat down beside him, close enough that her shoulder nearly touched his fur. "Do you know what I figured out?"

"Tell me."

"It's not the memories they want." She turned her hand over, looked at the mark the tithe-collector had burned into her palm—the court's sigil, still faintly warm, still faintly watching. "I've been going through them one by one, trying to weigh them, and I kept thinking: why would gods want these? A frog. A grass whistle. Cold feet in the night. What use is any of that to a parliament?" She shook her head. "But when I hold each one, it isn't the memory itself. It's—" She struggled. Words had always come easily to her with beasts; with people, with truths this size, they jammed in her throat. "It's the love in them. That's the part they eat. The love is the meal. The memory is just the bowl it's served in."

Ashen was quiet a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had the careful flatness he used when something mattered most. "Yes. That's what starvation does to gods. Prayer stopped reaching them generations ago—your valley saw to that, and the world beyond it forgot the old names one funeral at a time. Souls keep a body alive. But love—" his ear flicked, "love is the only thing that ever made them feel like gods instead of parasites. They've been eating dregs for so long that your brother's childhood is a feast to them. Your whole heart, poured out. They'll drink it down and call it worship."

"Then it's a fair trade," Odile said, and hated how steady her voice sounded. "Tam lives. I forget. Everyone gets fed. My grandmother would have called that arithmetic."

"Your grandmother," Ashen said, "would have called it theft."

He rose, stiff in the foreleg where an old wound had never healed right, and paced two steps away and back. In the thin light his scars looked like rivers on a map of some country Odile had never seen.

"There is another account," he said. "I told you there were other ledgers. Hear me out before you refuse—I know you're already refusing. I can smell it."

"Ashen—"

"Four hundred years." He said it simply, the way a man might state the depth of a well. "Four hundred years I have carried my memories of Beatrix Brightwater. Every one. From the day I found her, seventeen years old and bleeding, running from this mountain with a stolen soul-token in her fist, to the last morning, when she was ninety-one and asked me to help her walk to the valley stones so she could look at the sky one more time. Four hundred years, Odile. Do you understand what that means, for a wolf? We do not hoard gold. We hoard time. And I have spent mine entirely on her."

Odile's throat had gone tight. "You can't."

"I can. The witness-clause cuts both ways. I witnessed the original bargain; I may substitute payment for any party bound by it. The Debt-God confirmed it himself, the night we arrived—he owes me an old favor besides, though I'd rather not spend that if I can help it." His eyes, pale as frost, fixed on hers. "My centuries of devotion. My remembering of her. Give them to the parliament instead of your memories of Tam. Let them feed on four hundred years of love instead of twenty of yours. It's a richer meal by far, and they know it. The Debt-God's eyes went bright as coins when I mentioned it."

"No." It came out of her before she could dress it up in reason. "Absolutely not. You waited decades for me. You crossed the burnt country. You took the collector's brand across your shoulder in the under-roads—you think I didn't see it? You don't get to empty yourself out for my family twice."

"Once," Ashen corrected quietly. "For your family, once. This would be for you."

"That's worse!"

"It is not worse. It is the only arithmetic I have left." He sat back down, heavily, and for a moment he sounded very old, older than four hundred years, older than the stones. "Odile. Listen to me, because I will only say this once. I have watched humans my whole long life, and I have learned one thing about them that wolves have never understood: you measure love by what it costs to keep it. You build little scales in your hearts and you weigh everything—every kindness against every debt, every gift against every guilt. Your grandmother did it. She carried that soul-token for seventy years like a stone in her chest, and she loved you all fiercely *because* of the weight, not despite it. You're doing it now. I can hear the scales creaking."

"And you don't?"

"Wolves don't keep accounts." Something moved through him that might have been, in a creature with a face less scarred by dignity, a smile. "We simply stay. I stayed with her for four hundred years because staying was what I wanted, the way running is what a river wants. There was no cost. There was only her, and me, and the road between us, walked together. If I give those centuries to the starving gods—if I let them devour every day I spent at her side—what remains of me will not grieve. Grief requires the memory. What remains will simply be a wolf who has forgotten why he stayed, standing next to a girl he has just met." He looked at her, and his voice dropped to almost nothing. "It is not a sacrifice, Odile. It is a gift. Those are different things. Your people have confused them for so long that you've forgotten the difference."

Odile stared at him.

She thought about the ledger hidden behind her grandmother's doorpost, thirteen moons carved into its cover, one soul owed. She thought about the blind weaver's words: *your family's safety was purchased with a child not yet born.* She thought about her own list, the one of memories she could bear to lose, and how short it was, and how the making of it had felt like peeling off her own skin a strip at a time to pay a toll.

Everyone in her life had measured love in losses. Her grandmother, who bought silence with a soul. Her father, who hid his grief under work until it calcified. Even Odile herself, sitting in the dark counting memories like coins, deciding which ones she could live without—as if love were a purse and not a wolf who stays.

"You're wrong about one thing," she said finally.

"Am I."

"It's not a gift either. If you do it, it's a theft—you'd be stealing my chance to pay my own debts. That's what my grandmother did, isn't it? She decided for everyone downstream of her. She weighed her children's lives against her own guilt and chose, and chose, and never asked." Odile stood. Her legs were unsteady but her voice, thank all the small gods, held. "I won't let you become her."

Ashen's ears flattened. For a moment he looked, absurdly, like a scolded hound—and then the look passed, and underneath it was something rawer. "Then what?" he asked. "The moon rises tomorrow night. The thirteenth moon completes its turn, the door opens, and the parliament convenes to judge the offers on the table. Yours or mine, Odile. There is no third thing. There is no clever way out. I have watched this court for four hundred years and I am telling you: they take what is offered, and if nothing is offered, they take what stands nearest. Tomorrow night, that is you."

"Then I'll find a third thing."

"There is no third thing."

"There's always a third thing," Odile said. "That's what beasts know and gods have forgotten. When two predators fight over a kill, the cleverest creature at the carcass is usually the crow." She flexed her marked hand, feeling the sigil pulse faintly, hot as a held coal. "They're starving, Ashen. Did you hear what the Debt-God said? Not hungry—*starving*. Starving creatures accept strange food. They have to. And I have spent my whole life learning to speak to things everyone else thought were voiceless."

Ashen regarded her for a long moment. The murmur of the parliament drifted down the gallery like wind through wheat.

"You're going to try to feed gods something that isn't a memory," he said slowly, "and isn't a soul, and isn't four hundred years of a wolf's devotion."

"I'm going to try."

"And if it fails?"

"Then tomorrow night," Odile said, "one of us pays. And I promise you, wolf—it will be me."

Somewhere above them, deep in the cathedral of bone, a bell that had not rung in three hundred years began, softly, to toll. The thirteenth moon was climbing toward its fullness, and the door was beginning to open.
