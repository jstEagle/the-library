## 17. Before the Thirteenth Moon

I had kept one secret for eighty years, and I meant to spend it before the moon rose.

The court beneath the mountain did not sleep, but it dimmed. Between deliberations the great cathedral of bone went quiet in the way a hearth goes quiet—not dark, only banked, coals of moonlight breathing under ash. The gods withdrew to their alcoves to feed on whatever thin prayers still drifted down through stone. Servants moved along the walls with their silver eyes and empty hands. And in a gallery of rib-arches above the parliament floor, a wolf sat very still and rehearsed what he would say.

Below me, Odile slept.

She didn't know she was doing it, exactly. She had curled into the hollow between two of the court's vast vertebrae—pillars, they would have called them, if the court had ever been honest about being built from something that once lived—and pulled Tam's baby blanket around her shoulders like a cloak. Her hand, branded with the collector's mark, lay open on her knee even in sleep. She had stopped hiding it. That was the thing about Odile Brightwater that her grandmother had never managed: she could not stop reaching toward the truth, even unconscious, even here, even with every instinct of survival screaming at her to curl small and invisible.

I had watched Beatrix learn to make herself small. I had watched her do it so well that she taught it to her daughter, who taught it to her children. A whole lineage folding itself flat like paper, generation after generation, because of a bargain struck in fear on a night I was forbidden to speak of.

Forbidden no longer. That was the clause. *A witness bound may not intervene until a Brightwater freely asks.* She had asked—hadn't she? On the burnt road, when the tithe-collector's shadow fell across us, she had turned to me with blood on her lip and said, *Ashen, help me.* Freely asked. Freely given help. The oath had cracked open like river ice in spring, and everything I had hoarded behind it for eighty years came flooding toward the light.

I looked at my paws. Old scars crossed them like fence lines. Under the fur, under the skin, under the bone, I carried something the court wanted more than it wanted anything else left in the world: centuries of remembered devotion. Not worship—the gods had never gotten worship from me, and that was precisely the point. What I carried was older than their hunger and stranger to them: the memory of loving someone without being asked to, without payment, without prayer. Eighty years of watching Beatrix Brightwater plant gardens and bury friends and lie awake worrying about children not yet born. Every season of it stored whole in a wolf's long memory, because a wolf's memory is not a human memory. Humans forget on purpose. We keep everything, and we keep it *warm*.

To the starving gods, that warmth was a feast beyond anything in their larders.

And Odile was about to offer them the same thing from inside herself. Her memories of Tam. Not just the memories—I had heard the Debt-God explain it to her in his dry, ledger-keeping voice—but the love folded inside them, the only nourishment left in a world that had stopped praying. She would walk out of this mountain with her brother alive and her heart gutted, carrying a hole shaped like her childhood, and she would smile at him and not know why her eyes stung.

She thought I didn't know. She had rehearsed her memories by firelight for three nights, whispering them like a woman counting coins, deciding which ones she could bear to lose, and she had done it in the smallest voice she owned because she thought I slept. Wolves do not sleep the way you think we sleep. I heard every word. *The time he fell in the creek and laughed instead of crying. The way he hummed when he mended fences. His hand, smaller than mine, holding the whistle I carved him.* She was pricing out her own heart like a merchant at market, and every price she named broke me a little more.

So. The clause. The witness-clause. I had read it over Beatrix's shoulder the night the bargain was made, and I had carried its exact wording for eighty years the way other creatures carry fleas: always with me, always itching.

*Should the debtor fail, the debt passes to the blood. Should any party offer substitution before witnesses, the parliament must weigh all offers equally, and none may be refused for want of novelty.*

None may be refused for want of novelty. Old law, written when the gods were younger and hungrier and slightly more honest. It meant that when Odile stood before the parliament and offered her memories of Tam, anyone else standing beside her could offer a substitute—and the parliament would have to weigh mine too.

It meant I could take her place.

I rose, stiff in the joints the way old things are stiff, though my stiffness was a kind of costume; I had been old for a hundred years and would have been old for a thousand if I'd let myself. I picked my way down the rib-arches, silent on the bone floor. A servant passed me with a ewer of wine that was not wine, and its silver eyes slid over me without seeing. The mark on Odile's hand made *her* visible to the court. Nothing marked me. That had always been my gift and my curse—to witness everything and be witnessed by nothing.

Except by her grandmother. Beatrix had seen me the first day, a young wolf with a fresh scar across my eye, sitting at the edge of the valley stones where nothing wild ever came. She hadn't screamed. She had said, "You're a long way from anywhere," and offered me half her bread, and that was the beginning of the longest love of my life—not the kind poets mean, or maybe exactly the kind poets mean, the kind that is mostly patience.

I found the Debt-God where I knew he would be, in the alcove he favored, counting.

He counted everything. Prayers, debts, seconds, sins. He sat behind a desk that had grown out of the cathedral floor like a mushroom out of a corpse, and his fingers moved over an abacus whose beads were teeth, and when I padded into his alcove he did not look up.

"The wolf," he said. "Come to watch your girl sell her childhood?"

"Come to stop her."

That got his attention. He raised his head, and his face did the thing faces did in this place—shifted, briefly, into something older and less human, then settled back into the shape of a tired clerk. "Stop her how? You can't unmake her offer. It's been entered."

"I can supersede it."

His fingers went still on the beads. "Ah," he said softly. "The witness-clause." He smiled, and it was almost fond. "I wondered when you'd remember you were a party to that contract. You've been so good at playing the dog."

"I've been good at keeping an oath."

"You've been good at *suffering*," the Debt-God said. "There's a difference, and it's the difference I deal in." He set down the abacus. "Let me tell you what will happen, wolf, since you clearly haven't run the numbers. The parliament convenes at moonrise. Two offers stand: the girl's memories of her brother, and—" he gestured vaguely upward, toward the vault of bone where the thirteenth moon was already beginning to gather itself behind the stone, "—whatever the moon decides to do about the original debt. Your offer isn't entered yet. You'd enter it tonight, before witnesses, forcing a weighing. Do you know what happens in a weighing?"

"I've read the law."

"You've read the *words*. I mean the thing itself." He leaned forward. "When two offerings of love are weighed against each other, the parliament doesn't compare sizes. It compares *flavor*. And let me tell you what they'll taste in yours. Eighty years of devotion to a dead woman. Devotion that was never once asked for, never once requited in the way you wanted, never once spoken aloud. Do you know what unspoken love tastes like to a starving god?"

"No."

"Bitter," he said. "Bitter as unsalted bread. They'll take it, because they're starving, but they'll take it the way beggars eat from bins. Whereas the girl's memories—" he tapped the teeth-beads, one, two, three, "—fresh grief, fresh love, a brother's laugh still warm in the remembering. They'll fight over hers. They'll *sing* over hers. Yours is a noble offering, wolf, and nobility is the least nourishing substance in creation."

"Then they'll refuse it."

"They can't refuse it. None may be refused for want of novelty—that clause cuts both ways, which is why we wrote it before we learned caution." He sighed, and for a moment the clerk-shape slipped entirely, and underneath was something vast and tired and terribly hungry, a thing that had eaten prayers for ten thousand years and was still starving. "They'll accept both offers if they must. That's the danger you haven't counted. Substitution means *replacement*, wolf, but a weighing means *choice*, and parliaments are cowardly arithmetic. If your offer weighs heavier by even a tooth, they take yours AND hers, because the debt needs paying and the moon is rising and no one in this room has ever left a meal on the table out of politeness."

The cold went through me then, the real cold, the one that lives under the ribs. "Then I'll make sure mine weighs heavier."

"How? By what arithmetic? Love isn't weighed in years."

"It's weighed in what it cost," I said. "You told her that yourself. The love in the memory is the nourishment. Mine cost eighty years of silence. Hers costs one night of courage. I know which one I'd call the greater price."

The Debt-God studied me for a long moment. In his alcove, the teeth-beads ticked softly, though neither of us touched them, as if the abacus were counting on its own.

"You love her," he said at last. "The granddaughter. Not as a proxy. As herself."

"She asks for help the way her grandmother never learned to. Yes. I love her."

"And you'd go out of the world hollow rather than let her carry a hole where her brother used to be."

"I'd go out of the world having finally paid for my bread," I said. "Beatrix fed me half a loaf the day we met. I've owed it since."

Something moved behind the god's face—pity, maybe, or its close cousin, envy. "The parliament convenes at moonrise," he said. "I am obliged to inform you that I will argue against your offer."

"I know. You want the fresher meal."

"I want the *surviving* meal," he snapped, and the alcove shuddered. "Listen to me, wolf, because I am about to do a thing gods almost never do, which is tell the truth for free. If the parliament takes your centuries, you will not die. Death would be a mercy and mercy is not in our charter. You will become what the servants are. Hollow, silver-eyed, pouring wine for things that used to be prayers. And the girl will walk out of here whole, and she will grieve you for a season, and then she will do what humans do, which is heal, which is *forget*, which is the one power you beasts never had. Is that the trade you want? To be forgotten by the second person who ever loved you?"

I thought about it honestly, the way you must when the question is large enough.

"Yes," I said. "Because she won't forget. She's a Brightwater. Forgetting is the family trade, and she's the first one in four generations who's any good at the opposite."

* * *

The parliament convened as the moon began to rise, and I will say this for the old gods: they knew theater.

The vault of the cathedral opened like an eye. Stone ground back from stone, and through the gap came the light of the thirteenth moon—not white, not silver, but the color of a page before writing, the color of a door before opening. It fell down the length of the hall and lit the parliament seats, each one carved from a different bone, each one holding a god who wore a body the way a liar wears a borrowed coat.

Odile stood on the parliament floor in her patched coat with her brother's blanket at her throat, and her chin was up, and her branded hand was open at her side, and she was not small. Whatever else happened tonight, they had already failed at the thing the valley was built to do to her. No jar in the world would fit over this candle now.

"The court recognizes the debtor's blood," intoned the Speaker—a god with a stag's skull and a lawyer's voice. "The offer stands: the memories of Tam Brightwater, with the love therein, tendered against the debt of one soul, due this moon."

"It stands," Odile said. Her voice didn't shake. I was proud of her and it hurt to be proud of her, knowing what I was about to do.

"Does any party offer substitution?" The Speaker's skull turned, slow, scanning the benches. "Before witnesses. Speak now, or the weighing proceeds with a single plate."

Now. Before she could look at me, before her eyes could find mine in the dark and read the betrayal there—because she would call it betrayal, I knew her, she would rather have paid herself and hated me forever than be saved by stealth—

"A witness speaks."

My voice went out across the bone cathedral, and every god turned, and every servant turned, and Odile turned last of all, and the look on her face was exactly the look I had feared and exactly the one I deserved.

"I am Ashen," I said, walking onto the parliament floor with my scarred head high, "bound witness to the original bargain, sworn to Beatrix Brightwater, called Beatrix in another life and under another sky. By the witness-clause, I offer substitution." I stopped beside Odile, close enough that my shoulder brushed her knee. "Eighty years of remembered devotion. Kept warm. Never spent. Take mine, and let the girl keep her brother and her memories both."

The silence afterward had weight, like snow.

Then the parliament erupted—not in voices but in hungers, a rustling through the benches like wind through winter wheat, every god leaning toward me, nostrils flaring at the smell of a meal eight decades in the curing. And above us all, through the opened vault, the thirteenth moon cleared the last lip of stone and began, visibly, to rise.

Odile grabbed my scruff with both hands. "No," she whispered, fierce as a badger. "*No.* Ashen, take it back—"

"I'm sorry, little one," I said. "Some oaths you keep by breaking others."

On the far bench, the Debt-God rose to his feet, spread his clerk's hands, and said, in a voice pitched to carry to every hungry ear in the hall:

"The parliament will hear arguments. And I will begin by telling you why the wolf's offer should be refused—for it is bitter bread, my colleagues, bitter as famine—and why the girl's should be taken entire."

The moon climbed. The door began to open.
