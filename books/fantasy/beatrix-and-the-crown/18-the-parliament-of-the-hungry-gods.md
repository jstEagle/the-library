## 18. The Parliament of the Hungry Gods

The court convened the way a storm convenes: no announcement, no bell, only a pressure change in the air and every light in the cathedral of bone leaning toward the dais at once.

I had been standing among the soul-servants for three days by then, wearing a dead girl's gray shift, my branded hand wrapped in linen, learning to pour wine without looking up. Three days of watching gods eat. That was the thing no one had told me—not Ashen, not the weaver, not even the Debt-God with his soft merchant's voice—that hunger was not what the court did. It was what the court *was*. The old gods had been prayers once, I think, or something like prayers, and now they were only appetite wearing crowns of antler and riverstone and moth-wing.

When the conch horn sounded—though there was no horn, only the memory of one, blown through the bones of the mountain—the servants knelt. I knelt too, my heart slamming against my ribs like something trying to get out.

Ashen was brought in on a chain of woven shadow.

They had not hurt him. They didn't need to. But they had taken his scars' story away while he waited—I could see it, the way he kept glancing at his own flank as though checking for something missing—and that was cruelty enough. He walked to the center of the bone floor with his head high anyway, an old wolf in a room full of hungry eternity, and when his eyes found me among the kneeling servants, he did not let them linger. He looked at me the way you look at a door you've decided not to run for.

*Don't,* I thought at him. *Whatever you're planning, don't.*

He had already planned it. He'd told me nothing, which was how I knew.

"The parliament sits," said the Debt-God from his low seat near the steps. He alone did not rise. "Two offerings stand before this court. Let each be weighed."

* * *

They weighed Ashen first.

A god of moths—small, tattered, trembling at the edges like a candle flame—took the wolf's centuries in her hands the way you'd take a skein of wool, and unspooled them into the light. And oh, I saw them. Everyone saw them. Decades and decades of gray mornings beside a girl named Beatrix Brightwater, before she was anyone's grandmother, when she was only a runaway with a stolen soul-token clenched in her teeth and a wolf pacing the treeline because the court's law forbade him to come closer. I saw winters slept through side by side. I saw him carry her children on his back when the fever came through the valley. I saw him sit at her deathbed for nine nights without eating, keeping a vigil no one asked for, because his oath said *witness*, and he had chosen to make witness mean *love*.

The parliament went quiet in a way that was worse than noise.

"That is devotion," said the moth-god, and her voice shook. "That is *worship*. Freely given, never once demanded. Do you know how long it has been since any of us tasted worship that wasn't scraped out of a hollowed soul?"

"Long enough," said a god of riverstones, flat and heavy.

"The offering is sufficient," said another. "The debt is paid. Release the boy's soul to the offering's source, take the centuries, close the ledger."

"No." My voice came out before I decided anything. It cracked across the cathedral like a stone through ice, and three hundred silver eyes turned toward the kneeling servant girl who had spoken.

The Debt-God closed his eyes briefly, the way a man closes his eyes before a wave breaks over him.

"Stand," said the queen of the court—a tall figure whose crown was made entirely of held breath, or so it seemed; the air around her head bent and shivered. "Remove your hood, servant. You have a face I have seen somewhere before."

I stood. I pulled back the hood. I unwound the linen from my hand and held up the mark, burning silver-black against my palm, and I let every god in that cathedral see me plainly.

"I am Odile Brightwater," I said. "Granddaughter of Beatrix. Sister of Tam, whose soul you hold. And I am not letting him pay my debt with his life's memory while I stand here wearing a dead girl's dress."

"You would refuse the wolf's gift?" the queen said.

"I would refuse to be saved *by* him," I said. "There's a difference, and I think most of you know it, because you've spent a thousand years being paid for things you never chose."

Murmurs ran through the parliament like wind through wheat. Somewhere above us, faint and far, I felt the thirteenth moon beginning its climb—not risen yet, but gathering, the way a held breath gathers.

"The wolf's offer stands regardless," said the riverstone god. "You cannot spend another's coin, child, however loudly you object."

"Then hear mine," I said. "Because it isn't a coin at all."

* * *

Here is what I knew, standing on that bone floor with my heart going like a war drum: the court was starving. The Debt-God had told me so himself, two nights past, in the pantry dark where the wine casks breathed. *The old prayers stopped coming. Not fewer—stopped. Your valley smothered its magic under silence, and the roads filled with people too frightened to name us, and we have been eating stored souls ever since, and stored souls are ash. They keep us alive the way a wick keeps a flame alive. Technically.*

And here was what else I knew, from the crows, from the stag, from my own blood: the old gods were waking everywhere, all at once, because desperation was rising off the world like steam off a wet field. Desperate prayers reached farther than comfortable ones. But desperate prayer is thin food. A prayer screamed into a storm feeds for a night. A soul devoured feeds for a season and leaves nothing behind but a hole shaped like a person.

I had watched my brother serve wine for three days with silver eyes and no Tam behind them. I knew exactly what kind of meal a hollowed soul was.

So I did the thing my grandmother's ledger had taught me without meaning to: I read the terms of the room.

"You want memories," I said. "Mine. His. The love inside them, because love is the only thing left that tastes like anything. And you'll take Ashen's centuries, and you'll be fed for a while—a year, ten years, whatever a wolf's devotion weighs—and then you'll be starving again. Because everything on your table is *finite*. Every soul, every memory, every hoarded century. You are gods who eat down your own stores, and the stores end."

"Careful," said the riverstone god.

"No," I said. "Listen. For once in your long, long lives—listen. I'm not offering you a meal. I'm offering you a *kitchen*."

Silence. Even the moth-god stopped trembling.

"The valley of Stillwater has been silent for sixty years because my grandmother bought its silence with a bargain she was too young and too frightened to understand. But silence ends tonight if you release my brother, because I'm walking home through that door when the thirteenth moon opens it, and I am going to do the thing our family has been afraid to do for three generations." I raised my marked hand, and I swear the brand flared like a struck match. "I'm going to teach them to pray again. Out loud. By name. The small gods first—the god of thresholds, the god of the ewe-lamb, the lady of the still water herself—and then the greater ones, until the valley rings with it. Living worship. Not devoured. Not stored. Renewed every morning like bread, like rain. You will never eat another soul again, and you will never be hungry again either. That's the trade."

"And why," said the queen, and her held-breath crown tightened, "would starving gods believe a mortal promise?"

"Because you don't have to believe me," I said. "You have to *bet* on me. And here's why the bet is good." I turned, slowly, so the whole parliament could see me turn. "Your own law says debts may be renegotiated before witnesses if a greater price is offered. Is a lifetime of living worship greater than a wolf's dead centuries? Ask the moth-god. She tasted his devotion just now—ask her whether she'd rather have that once, finished, or have a valley full of it, every dawn, forever."

The moth-god's wings shivered. When she spoke, her voice was very small. "It is not a comparison," she said. "It is a famine against a harvest."

"But the risk," pressed the riverstone god. "Mortals forget. Mortals die. Mortals make peace with their fears and go quiet. Sixty years ago a Brightwater stood where this one stands and promised—"

"My grandmother promised nothing," I said. "She bargained. There's a difference, and the difference is that a bargain can be kept in secret and a promise has to be kept in public, in front of everyone, forever, or it dies of shame. I know which kind I'm making." I looked at the queen. "Also, you should know: I already answered one prayer in that valley. Before I knew what I was doing, before I knew anyone heard. Something ancient answered me through the stones. So the door is already open. All I'm asking is that what comes through it comes as blessing instead of debt collection."

The Debt-God opened his eyes.

He had been watching me the whole time with the expression of a merchant watching two customers argue themselves into his shop, and yet there was something underneath it, something almost like hope, worn thin as old cloth. He rose from his low seat—the only god in the room who sat low, the only one who dealt rather than took—and he said:

"The court will weigh it."

* * *

They deliberated the way glaciers move. Time went strange in the cathedral; the bone walls drank the light and gave back older light, and I lost track of whether minutes or hours passed while the gods argued in voices that sometimes were speech and sometimes were weather. Ashen stood chained at the center of it all, patient as stone, and twice he caught my eye, and both times I tried to tell him with my whole face: *you don't get to erase yourself for me. Loving someone doesn't mean vanishing for them. Grandmother knew that. She wrote it in a ledger nobody was supposed to read, between the lines.*

At last the queen lifted her hand, and the held breath around her crown released, and every candle in the cathedral guttered at once.

"The parliament finds thus," she said. "The wolf's offering is accepted in principle. The girl's promise is—" a pause, and I felt the whole room lean in, "—accepted in principle. Both may stand. Both cannot pay, for the debt is one. Therefore: let the thirteenth moon decide. It rises within the hour. When its door stands open, the debt must be settled before it closes, or the original terms resume and the boy's soul is forfeit entire."

"And how do we settle it?" I asked. "Which offer—"

"That," said the queen, and something that was almost amusement moved through her crown of bent air, "is the negotiation, isn't it? One hour, Brightwater. Negotiate well."

The chains fell off Ashen. He crossed the bone floor to me at a walk that wanted desperately to be a run, and when he reached me he did not touch me—he only stood at my side, shoulder to shoulder, the way he must have stood at my grandmother's side sixty years ago, and said quietly, for my ears alone:

"You magnificent, impossible girl. You just offered the gods a subscription."

"Is that madness?" I asked. "Tell me honestly. Because it felt like either madness or the only true thing anyone's said in this room in a thousand years."

"It's both," he said. "Those are usually the same thing." Then his ears went back, and I followed his gaze to the far wall, where the Debt-God was waiting beside a doorway of black stone I had never noticed before, and beyond the doorway, climbing up the inside of the mountain like a slow white tide—

The thirteenth moon was rising. And the Debt-God beckoned.

"One matter remains, Odile Brightwater," he called softly, so the parliament could pretend not to hear. "Every promise needs surety. Come and learn what I will require—and pray, child, that you find the price kinder than the ones this court is used to charging."

Above us, through a thousand feet of mountain, I felt the moonrise begin, and somewhere on the other side of it, I felt Tam—faint as a pulse under snow—begin to wake.
