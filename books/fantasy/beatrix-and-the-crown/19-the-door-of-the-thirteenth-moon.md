## 19. The Door of the Thirteenth Moon

The silence after Odile's offer lasted longer than any silence she had ever stood inside.

It was not the dead quiet of the valley, which smothered things before they could speak. This was the silence of a hundred hungry gods considering whether a promise could fill a belly. Their parliament had gone still — the bone cathedral holding its breath, moonlight pooling in the hollows of the thrones like milk in the dents of old spoons.

Tam stood at the edge of the dais with a wine jug in his hands, silver-eyed and swaying slightly, as though he were a coat hung on a hook and someone else was wearing him. He did not look at her. That was the worst of it. She had rehearsed this moment on the whole long road — his face when he saw her, the crack in the hollow mask — and instead he poured wine for gods who never drank it, and she might have been anyone.

*Say yes,* she thought at them. *Say yes before I lose my nerve.*

The Debt-God rose from its seat.

She had learned, in their private conversation, that the Debt-God wore whatever shape suited the debt. Tonight it was an old woman in a gray shawl, the kind who sold eggs at market, except that where her eyes should have been there were two small moons, waxing even now. It had been the Debt-God's voice that answered her prayer over the lame ewe, back when the valley still felt safe. It had heard her first. Perhaps that was why it kept looking at her with those patient, terrible eyes — like a merchant who has finally found the customer worth waiting a lifetime for.

"The court accepts," the Debt-God said, and the word went through the cathedral like a bell through water. "A living worshipper, freely given, who will carry our names into the world and teach the forgetters to pray again. Souls are meat. Worship is bread. The court has starved long enough on meat."

Somewhere among the thrones, something that looked like a stag made of river-reeds hissed its displeasure. Another god — all teeth under a crown of crows — laughed low in its throat. But no one moved to overturn it. Hunger makes parliaments agreeable.

"However," the Debt-God said, and Odile's stomach dropped, because there was always a however; there had been a however on every bargain since the first one her grandmother struck, "a promise is wind until it is kept. The court requires surety."

"Name it," Ashen said.

He stood beside her, close enough that his fur brushed her hand. He had argued with her half the night — quietly, in the way of wolves, which is to say with his whole body pressed against hers so she could feel every word — and he had lost, and he had stayed anyway. That was Ashen. Every kindness arrived already paid for.

The Debt-God turned its moon-eyes on him.

"The wolf," it said. "His centuries. Held in trust. Until the first new prayer is spoken in the Valley of Stillwater — spoken openly, by a Brightwater, and answered."

"No." The word came out of Odile flat and hard, a stone thrown. "You can't—"

"I can," the Debt-God said, not unkindly, which was somehow worse. "That is what surety means, child. It means I hold something you love until you do what you swore. Your grandmother understood this. You understand this. The wolf understands it better than anyone here."

Ashen's tail moved once, slowly. When he spoke, his voice was the one he used for telling old stories by the fire — level, unhurried, as if they had all the time in the world, which for him had nearly been true.

"It's fair," he said.

"It is not fair—"

"Odile." He looked up at her, and his scarred face was calm in a way that frightened her more than any of the gods. "I have carried those centuries for two hundred years. They are heavy. Do you know what it is to remember everything? Every winter, every grave, every name?" His muzzle touched her wrist, brief as a heartbeat. "Set them down for a while. If you keep your word, I take them up again. And if you don't—" the scarred corner of his mouth lifted, "—then perhaps forgetting is its own mercy."

"You'll be empty," she whispered. "You won't know me. You won't know *her.*"

"I will know enough," he said. "I will know you smell like pine smoke and stubbornness. That has always been most of it."

The Debt-God extended a hand — gray-shawled, grandmotherly, with fingernails like crescent moons — and waited.

Ashen stepped forward.

"Ashen, don't—" Odile grabbed for his scruff and got only air, because he was already moving, and there was a dignity in the way he walked toward the god that made her stop reaching. He had done this before, she realized. Given things up. Waited decades without intervening, bound by oath, watching her grandmother grow old. He knew exactly how to pay.

At the Debt-God's feet, he sat. The old woman's hands came down on either side of his head, gentle as a mother's, and light began to pour out of him.

Not firelight. Not moonlight. Something older and warmer — the color of hearths, of banked coals, of all the winters he had ever witnessed. Odile watched two centuries rise out of a wolf like steam off a spring morning: she saw, or almost saw, a girl with her own dark hair running through a young forest; a ledger being written by candlelight; a burial in the rain; years and years and years of faithful, patient, grieving watching. All of it drawn upward into the Debt-God's cupped palms, where it condensed into a small stone the color of ember-light.

The god closed its fist around it.

Ashen swayed. When he looked up at her again, his eyes were different — not emptied, exactly, but *new*, the eyes of a wolf seeing the world for the first time and finding it interesting rather than familiar. He blinked at her. Tilted his head.

"You smell like pine smoke," he said slowly, tasting the words. "And stubbornness."

"That's me," Odile managed. Her voice cracked in the middle. "Mostly."

"Good." He settled against her leg, heavy and warm and strange. "I think I liked you anyway."

The Debt-God tucked the ember-stone into its shawl, directly over its heart.

"Surety held," it said. "Now go. The moon is rising, and doors do not wait on sentiment."

* * *

Odile crossed the bone floor to the dais, and the gods watched her come, and none of them stopped her, because she was theirs now — a bargain walking, an investment with legs.

Up close, Tam was worse than from a distance. The silver in his eyes wasn't light; it was absence, a frost over glass. His hands were elegant and empty around the wine jug. There was a fine dusting of pale powder on his sleeves, and she understood with a lurch that it was ground bone, that he had been serving the gods out of himself, that the court consumed its servants slowly the way a candle consumes itself.

"Tam," she said.

Nothing.

"Tam. It's Odile. It's your sister. You used to steal my boots and blame the goats. You cried at Grandmother's funeral and pretended you had ash in your eye. You—"

She ran out of history. Two days' walk away, in a valley that forgot, she would have had a lifetime of it. Here she had only what she'd carried, and she'd offered the rest away, and the gods had taken the centuries of a wolf instead, and now she stood in front of her brother with nothing but her voice.

So she used the voice the way she'd used it on the ewe, on the crows, on the stone-moles in the dark — not commanding, not begging. *Speaking.* The gift that had been a death sentence in Stillwater and salvation everywhere else, the thing that was simply true about her: she talked to what other people couldn't hear.

"Tam," she said softly. "Come home."

And beneath the frost, something flickered.

It was small — a flutter behind the silver, like a fish under ice — but she saw it, and she lunged on it the way you lunge on a rope thrown to someone drowning. She took his free hand. His skin was cold as the mountain's root, and for one horrible moment it was like gripping a statue.

Then his fingers curled. Weakly. Wonderingly. Like a man remembering he had hands.

"...cold," Tam said. His voice was rusted. "Why is it so cold in here?"

"Because we're leaving," Odile said, and hauled.

Above them, the ceiling of the cathedral — ribs of bone arching into darkness — began to change. A seam of light opened along its spine, thin as a knife cut, and widened. Through it poured the light of the thirteenth moon, which was not like other moons: it was the color of the moment just before waking, and it fell across the parliament of hungry gods and made every one of them look, for one naked instant, exhausted.

The door was open. Both ways. The dying stag had warned her, and the weaver had warned her, and she gripped Tam's hand tighter and put herself between him and the light, because doors that work both ways let things *out* too.

"Go," the Debt-God said. It had not risen from its throne. Its moon-eyes were fixed on the opening seam with something that was almost longing. "Quickly, child. And remember — the first prayer. Spoken aloud. Answered. I am not a patient creditor, but I am a *fair* one, and I would very much like to be believed in again."

"What happens if I'm slow?" Odile asked, because she had learned on this road to always ask.

The Debt-God smiled with the old woman's cracked lips.

"Then I keep what I hold," it said, "and your wolf stays a stranger, and the court waits for the next Brightwater. We are very good at waiting. It is practically our religion."

Behind her, Ashen pressed against the backs of her knees — new-eyed, loyal anyway, loyal *first*, before memory, which was either the saddest thing she had ever known or the truest.

"Then I won't be slow," she said.

* * *

The climb through the door took a year and no time at all.

The passage beyond the seam was moonlight given walls — soft, resisting, like wading upstream through warm water. Tam stumbled and she caught him and they went together, his weight against her shoulder, his breath ragged at her ear. Behind them the cathedral's glow dimmed, throne by throne, as though the court were closing its eyes to sleep. Once, far below, she heard the crow-crowned god call out something in a language made entirely of debts, and the reed-stag answer, and then both voices faded into the particular silence of a parliament adjourning.

Halfway up, Tam spoke.

"I served them wine," he said. His voice was coming back in pieces, like a house being rebuilt room by room. "For — how long?"

"Nine days," Odile said. "Here. Longer where they are. Time runs differently under the mountain."

"There was a night," he said, "when I almost remembered my own name. One of the little gods — a small one, a moth-winged thing that hid from the others — it whispered it to me. *Tam. Your name is Tam.*" His grip tightened on her hand. "Did you come because of the ledger? Because of Grandmother's bargain?"

"I came because you're my brother," Odile said. "The ledger just told me where they'd taken you."

They broke the surface into night air, and Odile gasped as if she'd been underwater the whole way.

They stood on the mountainside above the under-road's mouth, and the sky was enormous, and the thirteenth moon hung over the peak behind them — full, white-gold, already beginning its slow slide away from the alignment that had opened the court. Below, the burnt country spread out black and silver to the horizon, and beyond it, invisible at this distance but vivid in her mind, lay the valley: the stones, the treeline, the ewe she'd healed, the family that didn't know yet.

Tam sank to his knees on the cold grass and breathed like a man surfacing. Ashen pressed his new-self against Tam's side, sniffing him thoroughly, cataloguing him, deciding — visibly — that he approved.

"She sent a wolf," Tam said wonderingly. "Grandmother sent a wolf."

"She waited a long time to send anything," Odile said. "We all did. That's over now."

She looked back once, at the top of the world. The seam in the air above the peak was closing, drawing shut like a wound healing, and through its last thin line of light she saw the bone cathedral going dim, throne after throne, a hundred hungry gods banking themselves down to embers to wait. Waiting on her. On a promise. On a prayer.

The Debt-God's parting words followed her down the mountain like a hand between her shoulder blades:

*A promise is wind until it is kept.*

"We should rest," Tam said. "Before we walk."

"In a while." Odile turned her back on the mountain. Her marked hand ached where the collector's brand sat under the skin — the court could still see her, would see her until the debt was paid — but for the first time the mark felt less like a leash and more like a compass needle. It pointed home. "We walk now. While the moon's still up and I still remember the way."

"And when we get there?" Tam asked. "What then?"

Odile looked at her brother — hollow-cheeked, silver fading from his eyes, alive — and at the wolf who no longer remembered why he loved her but loved her anyway, and at the long dark road winding down toward a valley that had spent a generation smothering its own magic under a jar.

"Then," she said, "I teach them to pray."

She took Tam's hand, and the three of them started down.
