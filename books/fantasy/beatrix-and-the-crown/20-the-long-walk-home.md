## 20. The Long Walk Home

The first thing I remembered was bread.

Not the eating of it—something older than that. The smell of it rising through the floorboards of our house, my mother's flour-dusted hands, the way she hummed when she thought no one was listening. It came back to me on the third day of walking, in the middle of a field of blackened wheat, so sudden and so whole that I stopped dead in the road and wept like a child.

Odile didn't laugh at me. She just stood there with her marked hand curled against her chest and waited until I could breathe again.

"Bread," I said. "That's all. Just bread."

"That's not all," she said quietly. "That's everything."

I didn't understand her then. I understood almost nothing then. But I was learning that understanding would come back to me the way water comes back to a dry well—not all at once, but seep by seep, if I was patient, if I kept walking, if I let my sister's voice keep calling me home.

* * *

I remember the mountain in pieces, and the pieces don't fit together right.

I remember cold stone under my knees and wine in cups I never saw being filled. I remember hands—so many hands, none of them warm—passing me from one shadow to another. I remember thinking it was an honor. That was the worst part, the part that still makes my stomach turn: somewhere beneath all the hollow, some part of me believed I was chosen. Serving gods. Pouring for kings of bone and moonlight. I remember feeling proud of my emptiness, the way a cup might be proud of being clean.

I remember silver light that never came from any sun. I remember names spoken over me like weights set on a scale. I remember a door of moonlight opening once, twice, a hundred times, and every time hoping someone would walk through it, and every time no one did.

And then, at the very end—I remember a voice.

It wasn't loud. That's what I want people to know, someday, if anyone ever asks. It wasn't thunder or trumpets or any of the things you'd expect. It was Odile's voice saying my name the way she used to say it when we were small and I'd climbed too high into the rowan tree and frozen there, too scared to come down. *Tam. Tam, look at me. I've got you.*

She said she had me. And God help me, I believed her before I even knew who she was.

* * *

We walked through the burnt country because there was no other road.

The war that had scarred this land was old—older than me, older than Odile, maybe older than the valley itself—but nothing had grown back right. Trees stood like burnt matches. Rivers ran gray as dishwater. Once we passed a village where every door had been painted with a white circle, and Odile went stiff all over and pulled me off the road until we were well past it.

"What is it?" I asked.

"Tithe-marks," she said. "Places that paid. Or places that tried." She didn't explain further, and I didn't push. There was a new hardness in her that I didn't recognize, and I was afraid of it, and I was ashamed of being afraid of it, and mostly I was grateful for it, because that hardness was the thing keeping us alive out here.

Her hand was the strangest thing about her now. The court's mark sat on her palm like a brand of dark silver, and it moved. I swear it moved—crawled a little when danger was near, cooled when we were safe, burned hot enough to make her hiss the night something followed us along a ridgeline for half a mile before giving up. She couldn't hide anymore. In the valley, hiding had been her whole life; I'd known that without knowing it, the way you know your sister flinches from loud noises without ever asking why. Out here, the mark made her a lantern in a dark country. Every hollow-eyed thing, every hungry god, every desperate prayer could see her coming.

And still she walked toward them. For me. She'd walked *into* the mountain for me.

I tried to say thank you once, around a campfire on the fourth night, and she cut me off before the words were out.

"Don't," she said. "Don't thank me yet. We're not home."

"We're going home, though." I looked at her across the flames. "Aren't we?"

Something crossed her face—grief, maybe, or fear, or both wearing each other's clothes. "Yes," she said finally. "We're going home." And then, softer, almost to herself: "Whatever home turns out to be."

* * *

The wolf came back to us on the fifth day.

I'd seen him only in fragments—a gray shape at the edge of the moonlit hall, a growl like grinding stone—but Odile spoke his name like he was family. Ashen. He was enormous, scarred from ear to haunch, and his eyes were old in a way that made my skin prickle. When he looked at me, I felt weighed. Measured. Found wanting, maybe, though he never said so.

"You're the brother," he said. His voice was gravel and patience.

"I'm the brother," I agreed.

"Hm." He circled me once, slow. "You smell like the court. Wine and cold stone. It'll fade."

"Will it?" I asked, and hated how young my voice sounded.

Ashen stopped and regarded me for a long moment. "Mostly," he said. "Some things stay. A wolf who's been in a trap remembers the trap. That's not damage, boy. That's wisdom. You'll learn which memories to trust and which to let lie." He lay down by the fire then, done with me, and I sat there turning his words over like stones in my hand.

Later, when I thought he slept, I heard him and Odile talking in low voices. I caught only pieces. Something about centuries. Something about collateral. Something about *until the first new prayer is spoken*, and Odile saying, fierce and cracked, "You should have told me," and Ashen answering, "Would it have changed what you offered?"

Silence. Then Odile, very quietly: "No. But I'd have carried it with you instead of behind you."

I didn't understand any of it. But I understood that my sister had made a bargain I didn't know the shape of, and that the wolf had paid something enormous for a family that wasn't his, and that I owed debts I couldn't count to people who wouldn't let me count them.

I put more wood on the fire and pretended I hadn't listened. It seemed the least I could do.

* * *

The memories came back unevenly, like rain on dry ground—some spots soaked, others still dust.

One morning I woke up knowing how to mend a fishing net and having no idea what season it was. Another morning I remembered my mother's face perfectly and my father's not at all, and spent the whole day quietly terrified that his face was gone forever, until that evening it returned all at once, mid-laugh, and I had to sit down on a fallen log until it passed.

Odile learned the rhythm of it. She learned to read my silences, to know when a memory was surfacing and when one had drowned. She started carrying things for me without being asked—the waterskin when my hands shook, the blanket when I shivered even in warm air. She never made it a kindness. She just did it, the way you breathe.

Once, in the ruins of an old waystation, I found myself standing in a corner facing the wall, and I didn't know why, and I couldn't stop. My body remembered something my mind hadn't found yet. Odile came and stood beside me, not touching, and after a while she said, "Was it the corner? Did they make you stand in corners?"

"I don't—" My voice broke. "I don't know. Maybe. Maybe worse."

"Then we'll find out together," she said. "Or we won't. Either way, you're not facing the wall alone."

She was sixteen years old, my little sister, and she said it like a general. I faced the wall and cried, and she stood beside me the whole time, and when it passed we walked on, and neither of us ever mentioned it again. Some mercies are silence.

* * *

On the ninth day, the land began to change.

It was gradual—first a green shoot in the ash, stubborn as a fist, then a stand of birches with their bark unblackened, then actual birdsong, actual birds, three of them arguing in a hawthorn. Odile stopped when she heard them and pressed her marked hand flat against her sternum like something inside her had knocked loose.

"We're close," she said. "The valley's edge. The stones."

I felt it too, though I couldn't have named how. A pressure lifting. A taste in the air like the moment before a storm breaks—or after one. The birdsong got louder, and underneath it, faint as a heartbeat, I heard something else. A low hum in the ground. In the stones themselves.

"The valley used to be silent," Odile said. She was looking at the horizon, at the line of hills that hid our home. "Magic-dead. Smothered. That was the bargain—that was part of it, anyway. Silence in exchange for safety." Her jaw tightened. "It won't be silent anymore. Not after what I promised them. Not after the first new prayer."

"What does that mean?" I asked. "For us? For everyone?"

"I don't know," she said. And then, with a ghost of a smile that was more frightened than glad: "Isn't that something? I bargained with gods and I still don't know."

* * *

We camped that last night within sight of the valley stones, close enough to see them pale against the darkening hills, far enough that we couldn't hear whatever they were humming.

I couldn't sleep. I sat up watching the thirteenth moon—it was still up there, I realized, still hanging fat and strange above the ridge, though the door it opened had closed behind us—and Odile sat up beside me without a word, the way she always had, the way she used to when we were kids and the storms rolled down the valley and I pretended I wasn't scared.

"I remember the rowan tree," I said.

"Yeah?"

"You climbed it first. I only went up because you dared me."

"I didn't dare you," she said. "You dared yourself. I just watched."

"You watched," I agreed. "You always watched. Even when I didn't know you were there." I turned to look at her. The moonlight turned her mark to hammered silver. "How long? How long did you carry it alone?"

She was quiet long enough that I thought she wouldn't answer. "Since I was small," she said finally. "Talking to beasts. Hearing things. Knowing things. In Stillwater, that's not a gift, Tam. It's a death sentence with extra steps. So I hid it. We hid everything. That was the family trade—invisibility." She laughed, short and bitter. "I'm done with it. Whatever comes home with us, whatever I am now—I'm done hiding."

"Good," I said.

She blinked at me. "Good?"

"Good," I said again. "Because I spent months pouring wine for things made of bone, and do you know what I learned? The worst thing isn't being seen. The worst thing is being *used* and never seen at all." I shrugged, embarrassed, but I made myself finish. "You were never invisible to me, Odile. Even when I didn't know why you flinched. Even when I didn't know what you were carrying. I saw you. I just didn't know I was seeing."

For a moment she didn't move. Then she leaned her shoulder against mine, the way she had when we were small enough that the world was just the two of us and the space between, and we sat like that while the thirteenth moon slid slowly down behind the hills.

"Tomorrow," she said.

"Tomorrow," I agreed.

Neither of us slept much.

* * *

At dawn we crossed the stones.

There was no fanfare. No trembling ground, no voice out of the sky. We just walked between them, Ashen padding silent at our heels, and the hum in the earth rose up through my boots and into my teeth, and the air changed—thicker, sweeter, alive in a way I hadn't known it had never been before.

And then I heard it.

Singing. Faint, from the direction of home. A woman's voice, wavering, uncertain, singing a tune I knew down in my bones—my mother's tune, the one she hummed over bread—and the words were wrong, were new, were words I'd never heard anyone in the valley dare to say aloud:

*Old ones, small ones, hear us now—*

Odile went rigid beside me. Her marked hand flew open, and the brand on her palm blazed like a coal.

"That's—" she whispered. "That's not possible. Nobody knows the names yet. I haven't taught anyone. I haven't—"

The singing stopped. And in the silence after it, from the fields below, a single voice called out—thin, cracked, unmistakable, a voice I'd know in any world and every afterlife:

"*Tam?*"

Our mother stood at the edge of the barley field, flour on her hands, staring at us across forty paces of morning light.

Beside me, Odile's breath caught. Beside her, Ashen's ears went flat. And under my feet, the valley stones began—for the first time in living memory—to sing back.
