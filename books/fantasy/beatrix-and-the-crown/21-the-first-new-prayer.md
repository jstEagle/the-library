## 21. The First New Prayer

The valley stones had not moved in a hundred years.

That was what everyone said, anyway—that the ring of gray monoliths at Stillwater's heart was older than the valley itself, older than the farms and the fences and the careful, quiet lives built between them. Grandmother used to say it differently. She'd said: *They're waiting, Odile. Everything in this world that holds still is waiting.*

I stood among them at dawn with Tam's hand in mine and Ashen's shoulder pressed against my leg, and I understood, finally, what she'd meant.

Tam was thin. He was going to be thin for a long time—the healers said his body had forgotten how to be hungry for anything but moonlight—but he was here, breathing, blinking against the sunrise like a boy waking from a fever instead of a soul returned from under a mountain. His fingers were cold. I held them anyway.

"You don't have to do this," he said. His voice still came out silvered sometimes, like it had been polished by someone else's hands. "Whatever this is."

"I know." I looked up at the tallest stone, the one shaped like a door if you squinted, like a tooth if you didn't. "That's what makes it mine."

Ashen made a low sound—not quite a word. He hadn't spoken since we crossed back over the boundary stones three days ago. At first I thought it was exhaustion; the walk home had hollowed him, each mile taking something out of him that didn't come back. Then I understood, on the second night, watching him stare into the fire with eyes that were just eyes now, amber and animal and *small*—and I understood what the Debt-God's surety had cost him while it sat as collateral in the court beneath the mountain.

His centuries were gone. Not spent. Not eaten. Just—held elsewhere, like coins locked in another man's strongbox, and until my prayer was spoken they would stay there.

He remembered me. He remembered Tam, the road, the mountain, the court. But when I asked him about Grandmother—about Beatrix, her true name, the girl who fled the dark with a stolen soul-token clenched in her fist—he looked at me with the polite blankness of any wolf looking at any human, and my heart broke a little more each time.

He had given up the only thing that made him more than a wolf. And he'd done it without asking me, without telling me, the way he did everything: quietly, completely, and then pretending it was nothing.

"Say your words," he said now, and his voice was rougher than I remembered, plainer. A wolf's voice. "The sun's up. Gods hate waiting."

"Gods love waiting," I said. "It's all they have."

But I turned to face the stones.

* * *

The whole valley had come.

I hadn't asked them to. I'd only told the truth, which after everything felt like the bravest and most reckless thing I'd ever done—I stood in my mother's kitchen with the court's mark still branded into my hand and said, *My grandmother bargained away a soul to keep this valley silent. My brother paid it. And there is magic here, and always has been, and I'm done hiding it.* Mother had cried. Father had gone very still, the way he went still before storms. And then old Marta from the mill had said, in a voice like a door opening, "I heard the ewes singing last spring. I thought I was mad."

She wasn't mad. None of them were. That was the thing about the Valley of Stillwater—it hadn't killed the magic. It had only taught everyone to pretend they couldn't hear it.

So they came. Farmers and millers and children too young to know they should be afraid, gathered in the frost-bright morning around a ring of stones that hummed, faintly, the way a struck bell hums after the sound seems finished. I could feel them listening through my gift—a field of small warm attentions, sheep and sparrows and one nervous milk-cow, all of them wondering what the two-legs were doing.

And underneath, deeper, older: the gods. Faint as embers under ash. Hungry. Waiting.

I had promised them living worship instead of devoured souls. Standing there, mouth dry, I wondered if I'd promised something no god could stomach. Worship was easy when you were starving in the dark; worship from comfortable people, in daylight, with bread in their bellies—that was a different meal entirely.

"Ashen," I said quietly. "If this doesn't work—"

"It will work." He didn't look at me. He was looking at the tallest stone with an expression I couldn't read anymore, because reading him had required knowing him across four hundred years, and I knew him across one winter. "Your grandmother stood where you're standing once. She almost said them. She chose silence instead, because she thought silence would keep you safe." A pause. "She was wrong. But she was brave first. Remember that part."

"I can't remember it," I said. "You were supposed to remember it for both of us."

His ears flattened. For a moment he was the wolf from the burnt road again, vast and scarred and carrying centuries behind his eyes—and then it passed, and he was only a wolf, and the loss of him went through me like cold water.

"Say your words, Odile Brightwater," he said. "Before I remember why I'm angry at you."

I laughed. It came out wet. I turned to the stones.

* * *

I had rehearsed this. Of course I had. I was my grandmother's granddaughter; I understood ledgers, debts, the exact weight of a promise. I had written the prayer out in my head a dozen times, formal and careful, every clause balanced.

What came out of my mouth was none of that.

"Hello," I said, to the stones, to the embers, to the hungry parliament far beneath the mountain whose light I had walked out of with my brother's hand in mine. "It's me. The girl who wouldn't shut up."

Someone in the crowd made a startled sound. Tam squeezed my fingers.

"You answered a prayer I whispered over a lame ewe, back when I thought no one was listening. You took my brother because of a bargain made before he was born. You nearly took me instead, and you would have been right to—you kept your books clean, I'll give you that. Every debt honored. Every price paid." My voice shook. I let it. "But I watched you starve. All of you. I saw what a thousand years of being fed on fear does, and I'm offering you something else, and it's going to taste strange at first, so bear with me."

I knelt. The frost soaked through my skirt. Behind me, the whole valley held its breath.

"My name is Odile Brightwater. My grandmother's name was Beatrix, and she carried this burden so I wouldn't have to, and she was wrong, but she was *brave*, and I'm choosing truth over the invisibility that kept us safe. I'm going to teach these people your names—the small ones, the threshold-gods and the river-tollers and the god of small debts who collects in memory and gives terrible advice. They're going to leave you offerings at their doors. Bread and salt and the first pour of cider. They're going to say thank-you out loud, in daylight, where the sky can hear it. It won't be souls. It won't be fear. It'll be better than both, because it will keep coming."

I pressed my marked palm flat against the coldest stone.

"This is the first new prayer. Hear it. And give back what you're holding."

For a moment—nothing. The valley held its silence the way it always had, smothered, jarred, a candle under glass.

Then the stone warmed under my hand.

Not gradually. All at once, like a heart remembering its beat. Warmth ran through the monolith and down into the earth and out along roots I couldn't see, and the humming in the stones rose into something almost like voices, almost like a chord, and above us—

Above us, the thirteenth moon, pale as a fingernail in the morning sky, flickered.

And closed.

* * *

It happened everywhere at once, and it happened small, and that was how I knew it was real.

The milk-cow lowed, and her voice carried a note I'd never heard a cow make—something that shimmered. Old Marta gasped and grabbed her neighbor's arm, pointing at the well, where the water had begun to glow faintly, softly, like it was lit from underneath by a kind sun. Frost lifted off the grass in spirals that were not wind. A child shrieked with delight as dandelions opened out of season all around her feet, dozens of them, bowing.

And Ashen—

Ashen sat down.

He sat down hard, the way old dogs do, and his eyes went wide and then wider, and I saw it happen: the centuries pouring back into him. Four hundred years of mornings. Four hundred years of a girl named Beatrix running through alder woods with a stolen token in her fist and a scarred wolf at her heels. Every conversation, every winter, every night he had kept watch over a bloodline he loved and could not speak to. It came back into him like a river into a dry bed, and I watched his face try to hold all of it, and I watched it fail, and I watched him weep—which wolves cannot do, except that apparently they can, when they are not ordinary wolves after all.

"Ashen?" I knelt beside him. "Ashen, it's me. It's Odile. Do you—"

"I know." His voice cracked open on the second word. "Odile. *Odile.*" He pressed his great scarred head against my chest, hard enough to knock me sitting, and breathed me in like scent was scripture. "You smell exactly like her. You have no idea. You have *no idea*—"

"Tell me," I said, holding onto his fur with both hands. "Tell me everything. Tell me all of it."

"I will." He pulled back, and his eyes were ancient again and bright with four hundred years, and he was laughing, or whatever wolves do instead. "Years of it. Decades. Starting with the time your grandmother stole a pie from a shrine and blamed a fox, and the fox has *never forgiven*—"

"*Ahem,*" said a voice like a ledger closing.

We both turned. Standing at the edge of the stone circle, where no one had been standing a moment before, was the Debt-God.

He looked worse than he had under the mountain. Thinner, somehow, though gods don't have flesh to lose—or maybe they do, maybe flesh is exactly what they lose, worn away by hunger. His coat was patched. His smile was the same: patient, bookkeeperly, faintly amused, the smile of someone who has seen every possible ending and is grading yours.

"You spoke," he said. "Loudly. In daylight. With witnesses." He glanced around at the frozen crowd, the glowing well, the out-of-season dandelions. "Sloppy work. No clauses. No sureties. No collateral whatsoever." He sighed the sigh of a professional surrounded by amateurs. "It was magnificent."

"Thank you," I said carefully. "You're... releasing the surety, then? Ashen's centuries are returned?"

"They were returned the moment the prayer landed. I keep clean books, Brightwater. You know this about me." He crouched, joints cracking, and studied Ashen with something that might have been fondness. "Hello, witness. Four hundred years. You were the longest entry in my ledger. It feels strange, seeing the account closed."

"What happens to you now?" I asked. "All of you. Under the mountain. If the prayers come—if it works—"

"Then we eat." He said it simply, without shame. "Not souls. Not memories. The other thing. The thing you gave us a taste of when you stood in our parliament and offered a promise instead of a payment." His hollow eyes fixed on me. "Do you know what worship tastes like when it's freely given? Neither do we. Not in a thousand years. We are very much looking forward to finding out." He straightened. "Some of us may not survive the transition. Hunger is a habit, and habits die hard. But that is our debt to manage, not yours."

"And yours?" I asked. "With me. Are we settled?"

The Debt-God smiled his bookkeeper's smile and reached into his patched coat and produced—of all things—a single page, torn from a ledger, and held it out to me. On it, in ink that shifted like water, was written:

*BRIGHTWATER — PAID IN FULL.*

"One copy for you," he said. "Keep it somewhere safe. Show it to any god who comes collecting. There are others, you know. Worse than me. Hungrier." His gaze drifted toward the treeline, and for just a moment his amusement thinned into something older and colder. "The thirteenth moon opens a door that works both ways, Brightwater. You closed it from this side. But doors remember being open."

Then he tipped an invisible hat to Ashen—"Witness"—to Tam—"Wine-boy, no longer"—and to me, last and longest:

"Prayer-teacher."

And the space where he'd stood was empty, and the frost spirals kept turning, and somewhere out in the fields a ewe began to sing.

* * *

We stayed at the stones until noon.

The valley didn't know what to do with itself, and did all of it at once. People touched the monoliths and snatched their hands back and touched them again. Children chased lights that were probably nothing and probably weren't. My mother stood at the edge of the circle with tears running openly down her face, and when I came to her she didn't scold me, didn't warn me, didn't say *careful, Odile, quiet, Odile*. She just held my face in her flour-dusted hands and said, "Your grandmother would have—" and stopped, and started again. "She would have been so afraid for you. And so proud. Both at once. That's what it's like, loving someone brave."

Tam found me at midday, sitting against the tallest stone with Ashen's head heavy in my lap, both of us listening to the wolf talk. He talked and talked and talked—Beatrix at seventeen, Beatrix bargaining with a river-spirit, Beatrix standing in this exact circle with the words on her tongue and swallowing them. Tam listened with his silvered eyes going slowly brown again, and when Ashen described the night the bargain was sealed, Tam said quietly:

"She traded a soul for our safety. And we almost lost two anyway." He looked at his own hands, hands that had poured wine for gods. "I don't remember most of it. Under the mountain. Little pieces. Your voice, mostly. You calling me back." He swallowed. "Did you mean it? What you said to them? About teaching the names?"

"Every word."

"Then teach me first." He sat down across from me, cross-legged, the way we used to sit as children trading secrets. "I served them, Odile. I carried their cups. If anyone in this valley should know their names, it should be me."

I looked at my brother—hollowed, mending, asking to carry the same burden I carried—and grief and grace braided together in my chest so tightly I couldn't tell one thread from the other.

"All right," I said. "First lesson. The smallest god I know. God of thresholds and small debts. He collects in memory, he gives terrible advice, and he kept his books clean even when it would've been easier not to."

Tam smiled—a real smile, crooked, his own. "What's his name?"

And I opened my mouth to answer—

—and every bird in the valley went silent at once.

Ashen's head came up off my lap. His ears swiveled toward the treeline, toward the north, toward the mountains, and the fur along his spine rose the way it used to rise on the burnt road, before the tithe-collector, before the mark, before everything.

"Odile," he said, in his old voice, his four-hundred-year voice, and there was something in it I had never heard before.

Fear.

"The door works both ways," he said. "And something just knocked."
