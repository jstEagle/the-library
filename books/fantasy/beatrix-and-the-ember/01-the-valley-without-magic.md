## 1. The Valley Without Magic

The kettle in the warden's cottage had a whistle like a dying goose, and Edmund Yarrow had long ago decided this was his favorite sound in the world.

It was not a high bar. The valley offered perhaps six sounds worth counting: the goose-kettle, the wind through the pass, the creak of the gate's iron hinges when he oiled them on the first of every month, the stream behind the cottage, his own boots on the gravel path, and — on rare and glorious occasions — the voice of another human being. The last of these he had heard forty-one days ago, when old Marta Tull had come up from Lowbrook to trade him a wheel of cheese for three jars of honey and had stayed to complain about her knees for the better part of an hour.

Edmund thought about that hour often. He rationed it out in his mind like sugar.

He poured the tea — blackleaf, bitter, grown in a strip of garden that stubbornly refused to die no matter how little attention he paid it — and carried it out onto the porch where he could watch the morning do its slow work on the valley. Mist sat in the low fields like spilled milk. The terraces below were green with barley just past its first knee. Somewhere down there, a dog barked twice and gave up, because even dogs in the valley understood the economy of effort.

And at the end of the path, half-swallowed by ivy and older than any living memory, stood the gate.

It was not much to look at. Two pillars of grey stone, a lintel cracked clean through but never falling, and between them a set of doors banded in iron gone black with age. No lock. No keyhole. The doors simply did not open, not for pushing, not for prying, not for the battering ram one over-ambitious bandit lord had dragged up the pass ninety years before Edmund's time and abandoned at the treeline, where it still lay rotting into the hillside like the skeleton of some enormous patient animal.

The valley folk called it the Quiet Gate, and they called Edmund the Quiet Warden, and both names were accurate in the way that most true things are accurate: completely, and a little insultingly.

Edmund drank his tea and looked at the gate the way a man looks at an old dog sleeping by the fire — with affection, familiarity, and the mild ongoing suspicion that it was dreaming about something he would never understand.

"Morning," he said to it.

The gate, as always, said nothing back.

* * *

His father had taught him the rounds, and his grandfather had taught his father, and somewhere back beyond that the lessons blurred into legend, which was how the Yarrow family preferred their history — unexamined. The rounds went like this:

At dawn, walk the wardline. The wards were invisible, which made walking the line a largely ceremonial act, but ceremony mattered. You walked from the eastern marker stone to the western one, you laid your palm flat on each stone, and if your hand came away warm, all was well. If your hand came away cold — well. Nobody knew what happened then, because in four hundred years of Yarrows, no hand had ever come away cold. Edmund's grandfather had written in the ledger, in handwriting like a spider drowning in ink: *Cold stone means trouble. Pray you never learn what kind.*

At midmorning, check the gate itself. This meant pressing an ear to the iron and listening. For what? For *nothing*, specifically. The gate should be silent. If the gate ever made a sound — a hum, a whisper, a knock — the warden was instructed to write it down immediately and then, per the ledger's own words, *sit down and have a strong drink.* Edmund had listened to the gate every morning of his life and heard only the sea-sound of his own blood in his ear, and he had come to think of that as the gate's heartbeat, though he knew better than to write such a thing in the ledger. His grandfather had underlined *immediately* three times.

At noon, tend the bees. The bees were not part of the duty. The bees were Edmund's own idea, and the best one he'd ever had, because bees filled the silence with purposeful noise. A hive humming is a sound like contentment given a body. He talked to them while he worked the frames. They were better conversationalists than the gate.

In the evening, write the ledger. One line, every day, without fail. *Wards warm. Gate silent. Bees cross. Weather fair.* Four hundred years of nearly identical lines, bound now into thirty-one volumes that lived in a cedar chest under the window. Sometimes Edmund read back through them on long winter nights, not for information but for company — the accumulated handwriting of dead men who had also stood on this porch, also drunk bitter tea, also talked to bees. It was the closest thing he had to a conversation across time, and he treasured it more than he would have admitted aloud, if there had been anyone to admit things aloud to.

That was the whole of it. That was the life. Dawn, midmorning, noon, evening. The valley kept its peace; Edmund kept the valley's peace; and neither of them asked anything more of the other.

What the valley did not know — what nobody knew, because Edmund had buried the knowledge so deep even he sometimes lost track of where he'd put it — was that the peace was a kind of wound, and Edmund was its dressing.

Magic did not work here. That was the whole point of the valley, the reason the gate existed, the reason the Yarrows had been bred — though the ledger never used that word — generation after generation into a family that magic simply *slid off*, like rain off glass. Hedge-witches found their charms going dull at the pass. Cursed objects arrived as ordinary junk. Once, decades back, a wounded sorcerer had crawled halfway down the mountainside trailing hunters, and the moment he crossed the wardline his pursuers' spells had guttered out like candles in a draft, and the man had wept into the dirt with gratitude while Edmund's father awkwardly patted his shoulder and offered him soup.

Inside the line, everyone was equally powerless. That was the gift. That was also, Edmund suspected on certain nights when the tea wasn't strong enough, the cage — though a cage built for whom, exactly, the ledger declined to say.

* * *

The trouble with a life defined by what it excludes, Edmund had learned, is that exclusion is very quiet work. There were no battles. There were no triumphs. There was only the daily confirmation that nothing had happened, again, and the slow accumulation of days into years into a man of thirty-four years who owned two shirts, one good coat, and a vocabulary of small talk he deployed almost exclusively on bees.

Marta Tull, on her last visit, had squinted at him over the cheese and said, "You've got your grandfather's look about you, Edmund. Like a fence post that's decided to be philosophical."

"I'll take that as a kindness."

"It weren't one." She'd laughed until her knees complained, and then she'd said, quieter, "You ought to come down to Lowbrook more. There's a harvest dance coming. Young Widow Ansel will be there."

"Widow Ansel is sixty."

"She's fifty-two and she makes a plum wine that would make a bishop lie." Marta had patted his arm with a hand like a dried apple. "Loneliness is a habit, boy. Same as any habit. Break it early or it'll break you late."

He had not gone to the harvest dance. He had told himself it was because the rounds required tending, which was true in the way that most excuses are true: technically, and uselessly. The rounds required nothing that couldn't survive one evening. What the rounds actually required, Edmund feared, was that he stay near the gate, because some part of him — the part his grandfather's blood had built into him before he had any say in the matter — did not trust the world to hold together unless he was watching it.

So he stayed. And the valley stayed. And the days stacked themselves like the ledger volumes, identical and comforting and faintly suffocating, and Edmund told himself this was enough, and mostly believed it, and on the nights he didn't believe it he whistled to the bees until the feeling passed.

This was the shape of his life, right up until the night the sky broke open and something bled through his wards.

* * *

The storm came down off the pass an hour after sundown, and it was wrong from the start.

Edmund knew storms the way farmers know weather and sailors know waves — intimately, superstitiously, by the feel of them in his teeth. Valley storms came up the river valley from the south, grumbling and theatrical, dumping their rain on the terraces and moving on. This one came *down* from the pass, from the north, from the direction of the gate, and it moved against the wind.

He stood on the porch with his tea forgotten in his hand and watched lightning walk down the mountainside in long vertical strokes, each one white as bone, each one landing closer to the wardline than the last. Thunder followed not as an echo but as a pressure, a fist against the chest. The bees, sensibly, had sealed their hive and gone silent hours ago. Even the stream sounded afraid.

"That's not weather," Edmund said to the empty porch. "Weather doesn't *march*."

The first hail hit like flung gravel. Then the rain came sideways, and Edmund retreated inside, lit the lamp, and did what four hundred years of Yarrow training demanded in a crisis: he got out the ledger, uncapped the pen, and wrote, *Storm from the north. Against the wind. Unnatural.*

Then he sat down and had a strong drink, because the ledger had been very clear about the other half of the instruction, and Edmund was, above all things, a man who followed instructions.

He was on his second finger of plum brandy — Marta's Christmas gift, hoarded for emergencies, and he supposed a storm that marched downhill qualified — when the light changed.

Not the lamplight. The *wardlight*. Every Yarrow learned to see it eventually, or believed they did: a faint shimmer along the wardline on the darkest nights, like heat-haze standing upright, the boundary of the valley drawn in breath on glass. Edmund had seen it perhaps a dozen times in his life, always steady, always gentle, always the same.

Tonight it flickered. Tonight it flared — once, twice, a stuttering pulse like a lantern in a gale — and through the window Edmund saw the northern marker stone lit up pale blue, and he saw a figure stagger out of the storm and fall against it.

The figure was small. Dark-cloaked. And it left a smear on the shimmer as it slid down it, the way a hand leaves a smear on a frosted pane.

Edmund was out the door before he had decided to be, brandy bottle still in his fist, boots skidding in the mud. The rain hit him like a thrown bucket. Lightning turned the world white, and in the white he saw her clearly: a woman, young-ish or made young-looking by thinness, dark hair plastered to her face, one hand pressed flat against the marker stone as if holding herself to the world by it. Her cloak was soaked through and darker in places that had nothing to do with rain.

She looked up at him. Her eyes were fever-bright and absolutely ferocious.

"You're the warden," she said. Not a question. Her voice was rough as a file. "Yarrow?"

"How—" He had to shout over the storm. "Who's asking?"

"A friend of a friend of a very old debt." She tried to push off the stone, made it two steps, and folded. He caught her around the shoulders, and she was lighter than she should have been, and warmer — far too warm, fever-hot even through soaked wool. "Gate," she gasped against his collar. "Is the gate—"

"It's fine. It's always fine. Who hurt you?"

"The interesting question," she managed, and laughed, and the laugh turned into wet coughing, and the coughing brought up something that gleamed red-black in the lightning. "Get me inside, warden. Please. I didn't crawl down a mountain to die on your lawn. Bad manners."

There was blood on his hands now, slick and hot, and the storm was closing in like a fist, and forty-one days of quiet loneliness cracked open all at once like ice on a spring river.

Edmund Yarrow, who had spent his whole life keeping the world out, hauled the world's broken messenger into his arms and carried her inside.

* * *

He got her out of the wet cloak and onto his bed, which was the only bed, which meant he was suddenly a guest in his own cottage, kneeling on the floor beside a stranger while the storm hammered the shutters like a creditor. She was hurt worse than he'd thought — a long tear along her ribs, crudely stitched with black thread that seemed to writhe when he looked away from it and lie still when he looked directly at it, which he decided firmly not to think about. Burns, too, on her palms and forearms, the kind that came from gripping something hot for too long rather than touching it briefly. Her fingers were curled as if they still held whatever it had been.

"There's a healer in Lowbrook," he said. "Marta can ride for her—"

"No healers." She caught his wrist with shocking strength. Her eyes had cleared a little, or fixed themselves, the way a drowning woman fixes on a rope. "No riders. No messages. Nothing leaves this valley tonight. Swear it."

"You're bleeding into my mattress—"

"*Swear it.*" And then, softer, with something underneath the fierceness that sounded horribly like fear: "They can't follow me here. That's the only reason I came. But if word goes out — if a rider leaves, if a spell goes up — they'll know where I am, and they'll come, and they won't care about your wards, they'll burn through them, they'll burn through *you*. Please. One night. One night of silence."

Edmund looked at her — at the writhing stitches, at the burned hands, at the ferocity running on fumes — and did the arithmetic of his life. Forty-one days since a human voice. Four hundred years of silence kept like a treasure. And here was a woman asking him to keep it one night longer, at the price of her life.

"All right," he said. "One night. But you drink my terrible tea and tell me who 'they' are."

Her mouth twitched. "Terrible tea. Gods. Dying in a house with terrible tea." She let her head fall back against the pillow. "Beatrix," she said. "Beatrix Ash. Remember the name, warden. Someone has to."

Outside, the storm screamed against the wardline and, for the first time in four hundred years, the wards screamed back — a low, groaning note from the marker stones, like a great door being tested on its hinges.

Beatrix Ash heard it too. Her eyes went wide and fixed on the ceiling, and she whispered, with the last of her strength and absolute certainty:

"They found me anyway."
