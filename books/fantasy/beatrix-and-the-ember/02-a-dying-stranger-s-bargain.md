## 2. A Dying Stranger's Bargain

Edmund Yarrow had a system for emergencies, and the system was: make tea.

It was not a sophisticated system. It had never been tested against anything more dramatic than a snapped gate-hinge or a wasp in the kitchen, but it had served him faithfully for thirty-one years, and so at dawn on the morning after the storm — with a bleeding stranger unconscious on his floor and frost creeping up the inside of his windows where no frost had any business being — he put the kettle on.

The kettle was copper. It had been his mother's. It whistled in a slightly mournful key, like something that had seen better decades, and Edmund found its small stubborn noise steadying while he tried to think.

The stranger lay where he'd dragged her, on the rug before the hearth, because the bed upstairs required stairs and she had stopped breathing properly somewhere around the second step. She was maybe thirty, sharp-boned, sun-browned, with the kind of lean hardness that came from living outdoors and being in a hurry about it. Her leathers were scorched in three places. Her left boot was missing entirely, which struck Edmund as somehow the most alarming detail of all. People did not lose boots to weather. Boots were lost to running.

He had cleaned her wounds as best he could — boiled water, linen strips, the good honey from the jar he saved for sore throats. There was a long gash along her ribs that should have needed stitching by someone who knew how, and Edmund had done his best with needle and thread meant for sailcloth, apologizing aloud the entire time, which helped him more than it helped her.

Now he stood at the counter with two cups and watched her chest rise and fall in shallow, uneven intervals, and he thought: *I don't even know your name.*

The kettle screamed. He poured.

* * *

She woke at midmorning, all at once, the way soldiers and animals wake — no drifting, no blinking, just eyes open and hand already moving toward a knife that wasn't there anymore, because Edmund had taken it off her belt and set it on the high shelf, out of what he hoped was obvious politeness rather than suspicion.

"Easy," he said. "You're through the wards. Nothing can follow you here."

Her eyes found his and held them, gray and fever-bright and absolutely furious with consciousness. "Where—" Her voice cracked like dry kindling. She swallowed, tried again. "The valley."

"You're in it," Edmund said. He crouched down to her level, keeping his hands visible, an instinct he couldn't explain. "Yarrow's Hollow. I'm the keeper. Edmund." He hesitated, then offered the cup. "Willow bark and honey. For the fever. It's not magic, it's just tea, but around here that's nearly the same thing."

Something flickered across her face — pain, and under the pain, a crooked almost-smile, like humor was a muscle she hadn't used in weeks and it hurt to stretch. "A man who apologizes to his own needlework," she rasped. "You stitched me up?"

"I did. Badly. You're welcome to lodge a complaint when you're stronger."

"With whom? The valley's complaint department?" But she drank, wincing, and some of the terrible brightness went out of her eyes, replaced by something merely human. She looked around the room slowly — the low beams, the herb bundles drying by the window, the gate-key hanging on its iron hook by the door, polished from generations of handling. "You live alone here."

"It's a posting, not a punishment," Edmund said, and then heard himself, and winced. "That's a lie. It's a bit of both."

"How many keepers?"

"One. Me. My father before me, and his mother before him, back and back until the records get vague and boastful." He refilled her cup. "Why do you ask?"

"Because you're the first person in four hundred miles who didn't look at me like I was either a meal or a miracle." She said it flatly, factually, the way other people reported the weather. Then her gaze sharpened. "Keeper. Your family keeps the gate. The sealed one."

"The sealed one," Edmund confirmed. "Nothing goes through. That's rather the point of it."

"Good," she said, and then ruined the word entirely by trying to sit up.

The room tilted for her — he saw it happen, saw the blood leave her face in a single tide — and Edmund caught her shoulder and eased her back down with the gentle firmness of a man who had spent his life moving heavy things carefully. "Whatever you're about to say, say it lying down. I've found most arguments go better that way anyway."

She laughed — a short, cracked sound that turned into coughing, and the coughing brought red to her lips, and Edmund's stomach dropped through the floorboards into the cold cellar below.

"That's not from the gash," he said quietly. "Is it."

"No," she said. She wiped her mouth with the back of her hand and looked at the red there with the weary disinterest of long acquaintance. "No, it isn't."

* * *

She told him her name was Beatrix Ash, and she told him it the way people tell you things they expect to be the last thing remembered about them — clearly, completely, looking him in the eye so he'd get it right.

"And before you ask," she added, "yes, Ash is my real name, and yes, it's grim, and yes, my mother had a sense of humor about it, and no, I won't tell you the story, because the story takes an hour and I don't have an hour."

Edmund sat on the floor beside her, his back against the settle, his untouched tea going cold in his hands. Outside, the valley went about its business — a cart rattling toward the mill, children shouting by the stream, ordinary sounds from a world that did not know yet that something had bled its way past the wards in the night.

"What happened to you?" he asked.

"Hunters. Veyra's hunters." She said the name the way you'd spit out a bad tooth. "Six days ago I had eleven friends and a plan. Now I have a map and a floor to die on, and honestly, Keeper, your floor is very clean, so points for that."

"Don't joke."

"Somebody has to. You're clearly not going to." But there was no bite in it. She closed her eyes, breathed through a wave of pain that Edmund could see traveling through her like weather, and when it passed she opened her eyes again and they were steady. Steadier than his felt.

"I need you to listen now," Beatrix Ash said. "Not comfort me. Not argue. Listen. Can you do that?"

"I can listen," Edmund said. "Arguing is separate. We'll see how I do."

"Fair enough." She worked her arm beneath herself, slowly, and pushed up until she was half-sitting against the settle, propped among the cushions he'd arranged. From somewhere against her skin — wrapped in oilcloth, bound to her ribs beneath the ruined leathers — she drew out a scroll case, black iron, unremarkable except that the air around it shimmered faintly, the way air does over a summer road.

Edmund felt it before she even opened the case. A warmth. A pressure. Like standing too near a hearth with the flue blocked.

"This is a map," Beatrix said. "Not a map of anywhere you've been. It shows the way to the Ember."

"I don't know what that is."

"Nobody does, really. Not even the people who think they do." Her breath caught; she rode it out. "It's a fire. An old one. Older than the kingdom, older than the crown, and right now it's sitting in a vault under the capital doing the worst job any fire has ever done: it's burning King Aldric's will away, piece by piece, and feeding what's left to the woman wearing his crown. Veyra doesn't rule the kingdom, Keeper. She rules the king. Everything else follows from that."

Edmund stared at her. "That's — you're telling me the king is—"

"A prisoner in his own body. Yes. And every order signed in his name, every levy, every burning, every free city put to the torch — his hand wrote them. His will didn't choose them." She watched him absorb it, and there was something almost gentle in how she watched, as if she'd delivered this news before and knew exactly how it landed. "There are people fighting this. There have been for years. Most of them are dead now. I'm the last courier carrying the only map to the thing that can end it, and I am dying on a stranger's floor in a valley with no magic in it, which — I'll be honest — was not the plan."

"Then go back out," Edmund said, and hated how quickly it came, how much it sounded like fear. "Rest, take food, take my horse if — I don't have a horse, I have a mule, but take the mule—"

"The map can't leave the valley," she said simply. "I tried. The wards push it back. Whatever your ancestors built here, they built it to hold things like this *in* as much as out." A ghost of a smile. "Congratulations. Your prison has caught a prisoner."

* * *

She made him swear nothing. That was the strange part, later — the part he turned over at night like a stone with something underneath it.

He had expected begging. He had expected the dying-woman's grip on his wrist, the trembling voice, the weight of a stranger's whole life pressed down onto his shoulders like a yoke. Stories prepared you for that. Every ballad the peddlers ever sang had a scene like that in it, and Edmund had always assumed that when his turn came, he would know what to do, because the songs told you: you swore, and you went, and you became someone larger than yourself.

Instead, Beatrix Ash unwrapped the oilcloth with fingers that shook, and showed him the map, and asked him nothing at all.

It was not drawn on vellum or paper. It was drawn on something like leather, something like bark, something alive, and it was marked in lines of heat — glowing ember-orange traceries that shifted subtly when he wasn't looking directly at them, roads rearranging themselves like ants finding a new path around rain. At its center burned a small fierce knot of light, and Edmund understood without being told that the light *was* the destination, that the map was less a picture than a compass, that it wanted to be followed the way rivers want the sea.

"It's beautiful," he said, before he could stop himself.

"It's a burden," Beatrix said. "Beautiful ones weigh the most." She held it out to him. "Take it."

And Edmund — who had spent his whole life being careful, being steady, being the man who checked the latch twice — reached out and took it.

The heat hit his palms and he flinched — but it didn't burn. It was hot the way a loaf is hot coming from the oven, hot the way a fevered brow is hot, a warmth that sank through skin and sat in the bones of his hands like a bird settling on a branch. The ember-lines brightened at his touch, rippling outward from his fingers, and for one vertiginous moment Edmund had the unmistakable sensation that the map had *noticed* him. That it had been waiting.

Beatrix exhaled, long and slow, and slumped back against the cushions as though she'd been holding a rope for six days and had finally let go.

"So it's true," she murmured. "The wardens' line. Null-blooded. The map likes you, Keeper. It hasn't liked anyone since I took it from the last person who carried it, and she's ash on the Salt Road now." Her voice frayed on the word *ash*, and Edmund realized with a lurch that the woman's name might have been Ash too, or might have become it, and that he would never know which.

"I don't want it to like me," Edmund said. His hands were still holding the map. He found he could not make them put it down. "I want it to go away. I want yesterday back."

"Yesterday, the Ember was caged and the king was a puppet and nobody knew but dead people." She smiled at him, tired and terrible. "Yesterday's gone, Edmund Yarrow. It went the moment I crawled through your wards."

"Then give it to someone else. Ride to Correnvale, to the free cities, to — there must be others, there must be a whole—"

"There were." Flat. Final. "There aren't."

* * *

They argued for most of the afternoon. Or rather, Edmund argued, and Beatrix answered him with the patience of a woman who had already fought this battle in her head and lost, and was now simply walking him through the wreckage so he could see it for himself.

He refused the oath. He said it plainly, kneeling on his own clean floor with the map's warmth soaking into his knees: "I won't swear it. I'm sorry. I'm sorry you came all this way, I'm sorry about your friends, I'm sorry about — all of it. But I keep a gate. That's the whole of me. If I walk out that door, the valley has no keeper, and the gate has no hand on it, and I don't know what happens then, but my family has spent three hundred years making sure nobody finds out."

"I know," Beatrix said.

"You *know*?"

"I've read the histories, Keeper. The wardens of Yarrow's Hollow. Sworn to the seal, bound to the soil, generation after generation of people who gave up the whole wide world so the rest of the world could keep pretending everything was fine." She tilted her head. "Did you ever wonder why the valley needs guarding at all? What's behind the gate?"

"Nothing," Edmund said automatically. "That's what the records say. The gate seals nothing. It seals the *absence*. Magic can't cross it, can't pool here, can't root here. The valley is a place where the world holds its breath."

"Mm." Something moved behind her eyes — pity, or its close cousin. "And who benefits from a whole valley of people believing their wall guards nothing?"

He had no answer for that. He noticed that he had no answer, and filed the noticing away in the part of himself where he kept all the questions he wasn't ready to open, a room that was getting crowded lately.

"Swear or don't swear," Beatrix said. "I'm not going to haunt you either way. I haven't got the strength, and frankly I'd rather spend my last hours drinking decent tea and insulting your stitching." She looked down at her bandaged ribs. "Which are atrocious, by the way. Truly. Sailcloth thread. You absolute farmer."

"I apologized at the time."

"You did. It was very polite. It was also happening while you sewed me shut like a torn sail." But she was smiling, really smiling, and for a moment the fever-light in her face softened into something younger, something that might once have laughed easily in taverns and stolen apples off carts. "Here's what I'll tell you instead of an oath, since oaths scare you. Facts. Facts are free.

"Fact one: the map burns anyone who touches it who isn't null-blooded. Sera tried. Odo tried. Odo cried, though he'll deny it. Fact two: it can't leave the valley except in null hands, so unless you plan to post it to someone, it stays on your floor. Fact three—" and here her voice dropped, and the smile went out of it like a candle pinched between wet fingers, "—fact three is that Veyra's hunters are crossing the mountains behind me. They can't feel the map in here, the wards smother it, but they can count. One courier went in. Sooner or later, one courier comes out, or they come in after her. Your valley's peace has an expiry date now, Edmund. I'm sorry. I truly am. I didn't choose your doorstep. The road did."

The fire crackled. Somewhere outside, the mule complained about something, in the tone of a creature whose grievances were ancient and constitutional.

"And if I carry it out," Edmund said slowly, "and deliver it — where? To whom? You said everyone's dead."

"Not everyone." She fumbled at her collar and drew out a cord, and on the cord hung a ring — plain iron, worn smooth, with a tiny notch cut into the band like a bird's footprint. "The Company of the Broken Road. Outlaws, exiles, and worse. They'll be looking for me. Find them first, and they'll find the Ember with you." Her breath hitched. "Sera will know what to do. Sera always knows what to do. Tell her—" She stopped. Started again. "Tell her I'm sorry about the ford. She'll understand. Nobody else will, but she will."

"Beatrix—"

"And tell Pip it wasn't his fault. He'll argue. Don't let him. Tell Marlow he owes me a song and now he never gets to pay it, and that's the cruelest thing anyone's ever done to me." Her eyes were shining, and she blinked hard, furious at the shining. "Tell Odo he was right. He'll know about what. He's been right about it for ten years and I never once told him so."

"I can't remember all that," Edmund said helplessly. "I'm a gatekeeper, not a — a messenger of last words, I'll mix them up, I'll tell Sera about the song and Marlow about the ford—"

"Then stay alive long enough to write them down," Beatrix said, "like a sensible man," and laughed, and the laugh broke apart into wet coughing, and this time there was so much red.

* * *

She died an hour after sunset, with the fire burned low and the frost ferning up the windows, and she died badly and bravely and much too fast, and Edmund held her hand through all of it because there was nothing else to hold.

At the end she gripped his fingers with surprising strength and pulled him close, and her whisper was mostly breath, and he had to bend until his ear nearly touched her lips.

"Carry it," she said. "Don't wield it. There's a difference. The difference is everything."

Then her grip loosened, and the frost crept a little further up the glass, and the valley outside was silent as a held note.

Edmund sat for a long time. The map lay on the floor beside him where he'd set it, glowing its quiet ember-orange, patient as a banked coal. When he finally reached for it — to wrap it, to hide it, to do *something* with his hands, because his hands needed a task or they would start shaking — the heat rose to meet him, warm and familiar now, almost glad.

And when, sometime deep in that black night, grief-muddled and half-dreaming, he picked the map up to move it to the table, the ember-lines flared — and shifted — and redrawn themselves into a shape he recognized with a cold jolt of recognition:

The road out of the valley. Through the gate.

Pointing at him.

Outside, far up the mountain pass, something howled — not wolf, not wind — and every ward-stone on the boundary flickered once, like a lamp guttering before the oil runs dry.
