## 3. What the Map Wants

Edmund Yarrow had always believed that a man's life could be measured in small, sensible rituals, and he clung to that belief the way a drowning man clings to a fence post — not because the fence post is going anywhere, but because letting go seems worse.

So on the morning after Beatrix Ash died on his floor, he did exactly what he did every morning. He rose before the sun. He banked the fire. He swept the floor — carefully, respectfully, around the place where her body had been, though the body itself was gone, because the valley had a way of taking back what it was given, quietly, overnight, the way the valley took everything. He put the kettle on. He made porridge. He ate the porridge standing at the window, looking at the gate.

The map lay on the table where she had pressed it into his hands, and it was still warm.

That was the thing Edmund kept circling back to, the way his tongue returned to a chipped tooth. Paper should not be warm. Paper, left overnight on a table in a cold house in a valley where the wind came down off the mountains with the specific intention of making everyone miserable, should be cold. It should be the coldest thing in the room. Instead, when he touched it — and he told himself it was only to move it, only to tidy it away into a drawer where dead women's errands belonged — it warmed against his palm like a loaf just out of the oven, and it did something worse than warmth.

It *leaned*.

There was no better word for it. The map leaned toward the gate the way a plant leans toward light, the way a dog leans toward the door when it hears its person's boots on the path. It was a flat, gentle, insistent pressure against his hand, as though the paper had grown a small and very patient muscle.

"No," Edmund said to it.

He was aware that this was not a promising opening to a negotiation. He was aware that he was standing alone in his kitchen, in his nightshirt, arguing with a piece of parchment. But the valley had been short on conversation for thirty-one years, and a man takes his debates where he finds them.

The map leaned harder.

* * *

The trouble — and Edmund had spent the whole of the previous sleepless night identifying troubles, the way other men counted sheep — was that Beatrix had not actually asked him to carry the map anywhere. She had asked, and then she had died before he could say no, which Edmund considered a low trick even for a dying woman, and he had told her so, to her face, while her face was still capable of hearing it.

"I'm not a courier," he had said. "I'm a warden. I keep a gate. I've never been through it. I've never been past the first ridge in either direction. I don't know what's out there, I don't know who you people are, and I am *certainly* not getting mixed up in whatever's got kings and usurpers and — " he had gestured at the blood on his floor — "all this."

She had smiled at him. It had been a terrible smile, the smile of someone doing arithmetic with the last of their strength, and it had said, as clearly as words: *you will.*

And then the map had burned Odo's — no. Edmund stopped. He was doing it again. The big man with the burned hand, the one who had come through the storm after her, calling her name — that had been a dream. It had to have been a dream, because when Edmund had opened his door at dawn there had been no one on his step but the snow, already filling in the tracks of a single set of boots that led *away*, downhill, toward the gate, and stopped at the wardline as though the walker had simply been erased mid-stride.

He poured his second cup of tea and refused to think about the boots.

* * *

By midmorning the map had escalated.

It began, as these things do, politely. When Edmund carried his breakfast things to the wash basin, the map tugged at his sleeve — he had, without quite deciding to, picked it up, and it now rode in his shirt against his chest like a hot stone, and he could not for the life of him remember putting it there. When he went out to check the ward-stones along the eastern fence, the tug became a pull. When he turned west, toward the garden, the pull became a *drag*, a gentle but unmistakable lean in his own body, as though someone had hooked a finger in his sternum and was reeling him in, an inch at a time, with the unhurried confidence of a fisherman who has done this ten thousand times.

Edmund planted his feet in the frozen mud of his garden and glared at the gate, half a mile off across the white valley floor.

"I live *here*," he informed it. "Do you understand that? Everything I have is here. My house is here. My — " he gestured at the garden, at the frost-killed bean rows, at the woodpile he had spent three weeks of autumn stacking into a shape that could only generously be called a wall — "my *wall* is here. I have responsibilities."

The map pulsed against his chest, warm and indifferent, and pulled.

And that — that was the word he kept avoiding, wasn't it. *Responsibilities.* Because Beatrix had put her hand over his, both their hands around the burning map, and she had said, in a voice already more ash than woman: *It has to be you. It burns everyone else. I've tried. God help me, I've tried.* And the big man in the dream had reached for it and snatched his hand back as if from a stove, and Edmund had watched the skin blister and heal in the space of a breath, which was not a thing skin did, not in this valley, not anywhere that Edmund was prepared to believe in.

He had refused her. He remembered refusing her. He had said the words *no* and *I won't* and *find someone else*, and she had died anyway, on his floor, with her hand still curled around his as though she were passing him something too heavy to name.

And now the map wanted to go home, and it had apparently decided that Edmund was its legs.

* * *

The first sign that refusal had a price came at noon, and it came in the beans.

Edmund noticed it because he noticed everything in his valley — thirty-one years of watching a single enclosed world will do that to a man; he could tell you the day the swallows left, the day the first frost would bite, which ward-stone hummed flat in wet weather. So when he walked the eastern line and found the bean rows withered — not frost-bitten, not drought-curled, but *withered*, gray and collapsing, in a perfect line that began at his fence and ran, straight as a plumb line, toward the gate — he did not need anyone to explain it to him.

He knelt in the dirt. The soil was cold, but it was cold the way ash is cold, the cold of something that has been *emptied*. He pushed his fingers into it and felt nothing. No damp. No worm. No slow blind life turning over in the dark. Just a fine gray dust that came away on his skin like the residue of a burned thing.

The withering ran in a line. The line pointed at the gate. The gate was where the map wanted to go.

"Oh," Edmund said quietly. "Oh, that's how it's going to be."

He stood up too fast and his knee crackled — thirty-one years of kneeling in garden mud will do that to a man — and he looked out across his valley, really looked, for the first time in years, the way you look at a face in a coffin.

The valley was small. He had always known that, in the way you know your own hands, but knowing and *seeing* were different things, and he saw it now: a bowl of green and gold cradled between mountains, two miles end to end, one house, one gate, one man. Everything in it lived because the wards held. That was the whole of the arrangement, older than his family, older than any record his family had kept: the valley kept its magic out, and in exchange the valley kept its people safe, and the Yarrows kept the gate, and nobody asked what would happen if the arrangement stopped, because it had never stopped, not once, not in living memory or dead memory either.

The wards were flickering.

He could see it from the bean rows — a stutter in the light along the western ridge, where the ward-stones stood in their ancient ring like gray teeth. They were supposed to glow at dusk, a faint blue breath, steady as a heartbeat. Instead they pulsed. Bright. Dark. Bright. Dark. Like a lamp running out of oil. Like something being *drunk*.

The map pulsed against his chest in the same rhythm.

Edmund stood in his dying garden with his hand pressed flat over the warm paper over his heart, and he understood, with the slow cold certainty of a man watching water rise around his ankles, that this was not a request. It had never been a request. Beatrix had not asked him to deliver a map. The map had asked *him*, and the valley — his valley, his whole small world, every bean and bee and ward-stone and breath in it — was the collateral.

"Refusal will kill everything I protect," he said aloud, to the mountains, to the map, to whatever was listening. "Is that it? That's the bargain? That's what she couldn't tell me because I'd have thrown her out in the snow?"

The map pulsed. Warm. Patient. *Yes.*

* * *

He spent the afternoon doing what Edmund Yarrow did best, which was tending things, because a man facing the end of his world should at least face it with a full woodpile.

He harvested what could still be harvested — half the beans, the last of the onions, a sad handful of carrots that came out of the ground already gray at the tips. He topped up the feed for the hens, who had gone strangely quiet, all six of them sitting in a row on their perch with their feathers puffed and their eyes half-shut, like old men at a funeral. He checked the root cellar. He mended the hinge on the gate of the goat pen, which had needed mending for two years and which he had been meaning to get to, and he found that his hands were shaking, and he made himself finish the hinge anyway, because that was the whole of the Yarrow creed: *tend what is yours, and do it properly.*

The withering spread as he worked. He could track it by walking the fence line — a gray tide creeping outward from the map's chosen path, a finger's width an hour, then a hand's width, then faster. The grass went first, then the clover, then the low hedges. The old pear tree by the well — his grandfather's pear tree, the one he had climbed as a boy, the one that had fed every Yarrow for four generations — dropped its leaves in a single afternoon, all of them at once, a brown snowfall with no wind to carry it.

By dusk the hens were dead. All six. They simply did not wake from their roost, and when Edmund gathered them down, one by one, and lined them up on the bench by the door because he could not think what else to do with them, they weighed nothing at all, as though whatever a hen is made of besides feathers had already gone somewhere else.

He sat on his step in the dark and looked at his six empty-feeling hens and his gray garden and the flickering ward-light on the western ridge, and he laughed. It was not a nice laugh. It was the laugh of a man who has just realized that the fence post he has been clinging to is attached to nothing.

"Thirty-one years," he told the hens. "Thirty-one years I kept this valley, and it turns out I was keeping a candle in a windstorm and calling it a hearth. One piece of paper walks in the door and the whole arrangement comes apart like wet bread."

The hens did not answer. The map pulsed. Somewhere on the ridge, a ward-stone went dark and did not come back.

* * *

He did not sleep. He packed instead, because packing is what hands do when a mind will not stop, and Edmund's hands had always been wiser than his head.

He packed the way he did everything — sensibly, thoroughly, with the gentle humor of a man who has caught himself doing something absurd and decided to do it *well*. He packed rope, though he could not have said what for. He packed the good knife and the mending kit and the flint and a fortnight of dried food from a cellar that might, if the withering kept its pace, be the last food in the world that still had any taste of life in it. He packed his grandfather's coat, which was too big for him and always had been, and his mother's wool scarf, and — after a long moment, standing in the doorway of the little room he had always told himself was for guests, though no guest had ever slept in it — he packed nothing from that room at all, because there was nothing in it to pack, and that, he supposed, was its own kind of answer.

At the bottom of his pack, wrapped in oilcloth, he put the small tin box of Yarrow records. Not because he was sentimental. Because a warden keeps records, and he was not yet ready to stop being a warden, even if he no longer knew what the word meant.

The map had gone quiet while he packed. It lay warm against his chest, no longer pulling, no longer leaning, and Edmund understood that too. It had won. It had never needed to drag him. It had only needed to show him the price, and let him — this was the part that sat in his stomach like a swallowed stone — let him *choose*. Beatrix had died rather than force him. The map simply waited. Everything about this business, Edmund was learning, was patient in a way that made him feel very small and very young and very much like a man standing in a doorway he had spent his whole life not walking through.

He banked the fire. He swept the floor. He left the door unlatched, because that was the Yarrow way — *a keeper leaves his door open, in case someone comes in out of the storm* — and because some fool, some bleeding stranger, might still come up the path needing what he would no longer be here to give.

He stood in the yard in the last of the dark, in his grandfather's too-big coat, with his breath smoking and the stars wheeling overhead in their ancient, indifferent courses. The ward-light on the ridge was nearly out. In the gray garden, the pear tree stood bare as a bone. The valley that had held him his whole life was letting go of itself, one withered field at a time, and it would not stop until the map went where it was going, and the only place the map could go was through the gate, and the only legs it had were his.

"I want it noted," Edmund said to the mountains, "that I said no. I want it on the record that Edmund Yarrow, warden of this valley, looked at this whole arrangement and said, plainly, *no*." He hitched the pack higher on his shoulders. "And then I want it noted that I'm going anyway, and that if anyone asks, I'm doing it for the hens."

The map pulsed once against his heart — warm, almost fond, he would have sworn it — and the first ward-stone on the eastern ridge flickered back to life, one small blue breath, like a candle relit.

Edmund Yarrow took the path down toward the gate, walking for the first time in his life *toward* it instead of around it, and the frost crunched under his boots, and behind him the valley began, field by field, stone by stone, very slowly, to breathe.
