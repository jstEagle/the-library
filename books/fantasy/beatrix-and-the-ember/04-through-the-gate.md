## 4. Through the Gate

The gate had two sides, and Edmund had only ever seen one of them.

He'd thought about this, over the years, the way a man thinks about the far shore of a lake he's never swum. The gate stood at the head of the valley like a door in the middle of a wall, and behind it — so far as anyone knew, so far as *he* knew — was everything else. The world. Magic and kings and wars and whatever else happened out there when nobody was keeping a valley safe from it.

His father had gone through once. His grandfather too. That was the arrangement: one warden per generation earned the right to walk through, see what needed seeing, and come back to report that the world was still there, still dangerous, still best kept on its own side of the stone.

Edmund's father had never come back.

That was also part of the arrangement, though nobody said it aloud. The valley remembered him the way valleys remember things — in the way his mother set two cups out for years before she stopped, in the way old Tammer would sometimes pause mid-sentence and look toward the gate as if expecting a man to walk out of it with news.

Edmund had inherited the post at nineteen, along with the cottage, the key-ring, and the particular silence of being the last person between the world and everyone he loved.

Now Beatrix Ash lay dead under the rowan tree behind his cottage, wrapped in his good blanket because she deserved better than sacking, and her map was burning a hole through his coat pocket like a small angry sun.

It didn't burn him. It never burned him. That was the whole trouble.

* * *

He spent three days not leaving.

This was, he felt, a reasonable amount of time to spend not doing something that would kill everything you loved. He used it well. He buried Beatrix properly, or as properly as one buries a stranger who dies in your kitchen — deep, under the rowan, with a flat stone and no name, because he didn't know any prayers and she hadn't left him any. He'd said something anyway, standing there in the drizzle with his cap in his hands.

"You should have knocked louder," he told the grave. "I'd have let you in sooner."

The crops were worse on the second day. He walked the barley rows and found them gray at the tips, as though the color had been drunk out of them overnight. The wards along the valley rim — stones his family had tended for nine generations, each one humming faintly against his palm like a cat — had gone quiet. Not broken. *Tired.* Like lamps running low on oil.

On the third day, the well water tasted of ash.

So it wasn't really a decision. Decisions are things you make when more than one option is survivable. He packed that evening: bread, cheese, the good knife, his father's oilskin, a coil of rope he was fairly sure he wouldn't know how to use. He fed the hens extra. He wrote a note for Tammer and weighted it under a stone on the table, where it said only:

*Gone through the gate. Feed the hens. Back when I can be.*

Then, because he was who he was, he went back inside and added: *Sorry about the barley.*

* * *

Morning came up gray and clean, the first morning in four days that hadn't tasted wrong, and Edmund understood with a lurch that the valley already knew he was going. The wards had eased. Whatever hunger the land had been suffering, it had smelled the answer coming.

The gate itself was older than the word for it. Nine feet of black stone, seamless, without hinge or handle, set into the cliff wall at the valley's head. His people called it simply the Door, and the Door opened for wardens and closed for everything else, and in nine generations it had never once been forced.

Edmund stood before it with his pack straps creaking and his breath fogging, and he did the thing his father must have done, and his grandfather, and every Yarrow back to the first one whose name the records had lost: he put his hand flat against the stone.

Warm. The stone was warm, which it had never been, and beneath his palm he felt something turn over — slow, vast, patient — like a great animal waking to a familiar smell.

"Right," Edmund said, to the Door, to the valley, to himself. "I'm going, then. Nobody needs to make a fuss."

The Door opened inward, silently, onto darkness that wasn't dark — onto a light that came from everywhere and nowhere, the light of a world that ran on different rules.

He stepped through.

* * *

Later — much later, when he had time to think about it — Edmund would try to describe what the other side felt like, and he would fail, and he would keep trying anyway, because some things deserve the attempt.

The closest he ever got was rain on glass.

The world beyond the gate was *full*. That was the first truth of it, and it hit him physically, like walking into weather. The air itself was thick with something moving — he could see it, or almost see it, threads and currents and drifting motes of pale gold, like dust in a sunbeam except the sunbeam was everywhere and the dust was alive. It pooled in hollows. It streamed off the ridge tops like smoke. A hawk turned overhead trailing it from its wings like ribbon.

Magic. Real magic, ambient and enormous, saturating everything the way water saturates a sponge.

And none of it touched him.

It came at him and slid away. It broke around him the way a stream breaks around a stone, closing again behind, unbothered. He stood in the middle of a living river of it and stayed dry — drier than dry, untouched, a hole shaped like a man in a world with no holes in it.

He laughed. He couldn't help it. It came out of him startled and rusty, a sound he barely recognized as his own.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, that's — all right. That's fine."

A rabbit watched him from a bracken bank with an expression of frank assessment, and even the rabbit's attention felt like something, some small warm pressure of wildness, and even that slid off him.

He walked. There didn't seem to be anything else to do; the map in his pocket tugged gently, patiently, like a dog on a lead, and the world unrolled ahead of him in colors too loud. Grass that was aggressively green. Flowers that turned to follow him — not the sun, *him*, straining hopefully against their stems until they realized he was nothing to them and drooped, disappointed. A stream crossed his path, and where the water ran over a fallen branch, tiny lights gathered and scattered like startled fish.

Everything wanted to notice him. Nothing could.

By midday he'd stopped flinching at it. By afternoon he'd started talking to it, because the silence of thirty years alone doesn't leave a man quickly.

"No," he told a hedge that rustled invitingly at him. "Whatever you're selling, I haven't got it."

The hedge subsided, sulking.

He was, he would admit later, distracted. He was a man who had spent his life watching one horizon, suddenly dropped into a world with horizons on every side, all of them showing off. So perhaps it was forgivable that he didn't hear the horses until they were nearly on him.

Perhaps it wasn't. Either way, the lesson cost him.

* * *

They came down the slope in a wedge, five riders in gray-and-crimson, and the first thing Edmund noticed — the thing he'd remember in the cold hours afterward — was how the magic *bent around them*. Streamed into them. They wore it like armor, like a second skin, sigils stitched into their cloaks that drank the golden light and held it coiled and ready.

Hunters. He didn't need anyone to tell him. Everything about them said it: the economy of their movement, the way they spread without a signal, the way the lead rider looked at Edmund not with curiosity but with *assessment*, the way a butcher looks at a carcass.

"Stop where you are," the lead rider called. A woman, hard-faced, with a voice like a door closing. "In the Queen's name."

Edmund stopped. It seemed wise.

"I don't know any queen," he said honestly. "I'm from the valley. Over the mountains." He gestured vaguely behind him, then wished he hadn't, because four of the five riders looked, and the fifth — a thin man with ink-dark eyes — looked at Edmund instead, and frowned.

"From where?"

"Yarrow Valley. I'm the keeper."

The woman rode closer. Up close her cloak-sigils hummed, and Edmund could feel the power in them pressing outward, testing, reaching for him the way the flowers had — and sliding off, finding nothing, finding *less than nothing*.

Her frown deepened. She raised one gloved hand and spoke a single syllable that made the air shiver.

Nothing happened to him. The spell broke around him like water on oiled cloth, and he saw her feel it — saw the exact moment her certainty curdled.

"What are you?" she said quietly.

"Edmund Yarrow," he said. "Keeper of the gate." And then, because honesty had served him poorly lately but he couldn't seem to shake the habit: "There's nothing to find in me. Things just — don't stick."

The thin man swung down from his horse. "Captain," he said, low, urgent. "The report from Correnvale. The courier. Ash, the one who went to ground in the magicless zone — they say she passed *something* to someone before she died. A map."

The captain's eyes never left Edmund's.

"Search him," she said.

The thin man approached warily, the way a man approaches a dog of unknown temper, and reached for Edmund's coat.

The map burned him.

Edmund heard the sizzle before he understood it — a hiss like fat in a fire — and the thin man snatched his hand back with a shriek, cradling it, the smell of scorched leather rising between them. Through the torn glove, the skin was already blistering in the shape of fingers.

"It burns," the thin man gasped. "Captain, it *burns*—"

"Of course it does," the captain said softly. She dismounted now, walking a slow circle around Edmund, studying him the way scholars study a text written in a language they hate. "Warded against everyone but the carrier. And the carrier is immune to the Art itself." Something moved across her face — not fear exactly, but its careful cousin. "Do you know what you're carrying, gatekeeper?"

"A map," Edmund said.

"Do you know where it goes?"

"No."

"Would you tell me if you did?"

"Probably," he admitted. "I'm not built for lying. But I don't."

She smiled, and it was the worst smile he'd ever seen, because there was nothing cruel in it at all — just patience, and arithmetic. "Here is what happens now," she said. "You will come with us to the garrison at Thornmere. You will be questioned by people considerably less gentle than me. And the map will be taken from your body afterward, since apparently we'll have to take the body apart to get it." She paused. "Or — you can save everyone a great deal of unpleasantness."

Edmund looked at her. He looked at the four riders, at the horses stamping, at the wide loud impossible world spread out around him that he had known for less than a day.

And he thought about Beatrix Ash, dying on his floor, pressing heat and paper into his hands. *Carry it,* she'd said. *Don't let them take it.*

She hadn't said anything about not getting captured. In hindsight, that seemed like an oversight.

"I'd rather not," he said.

The captain sighed, genuinely regretful, and lifted her hand, and the air filled with light—

—and broke around him like surf on a cliff.

The binding-spell shattered into drifting sparks. One of the riders' horses screamed and bolted. The captain staggered back a step, staring at the space where her magic had died, and for the first time Edmund saw something honest in her face.

"Take him," she snapped. "Ropes. *Ropes*, not spells — he's null, you idiots, nothing else will hold—"

They were dismounting, fumbling for coils, and Edmund did the only thing his thirty years had trained him for.

He ran for difficult ground.

* * *

He'd grown up on scree slopes and river fords and the kind of country that punishes horsemen. The hunters had grown up, presumably, wherever people grow up who ride in wedges and wear enchanted cloaks. It showed.

He took them upslope, into the rocks, where the horses blundered and cursed and the golden light of the world streamed uselessly past him while behind him shouts cracked the air — *"Don't cast near him, you'll waste it!"* — and he learned, gasping, ducking crossbow bolts, that being a hole in the world had tactical advantages he'd never imagined.

A bolt meant for his back struck a boulder instead and the stone *shrieked*, splitting where the enchanted missile bit. He put another ridge between them and another, lungs burning, legs remembering thirty years of solitary patrols, until the shouts fell behind and the forest swallowed him and he stood bent double in the green dark, listening to his own heart.

They didn't give up. He could hear them casting about below, methodical, patient. They had dogs, or something like dogs. They would find him by morning.

He pressed a hand to the map through his coat. It pulsed against his palm, warm, insistent, pointing — always pointing — south and east, toward whatever waited at the end of it.

"Well," he panted to it. "You got me into this. Any suggestions?"

The map grew warmer. South-east. *Away.*

"That's not a suggestion," he muttered. "That's just more walking."

But it was dark, and the hunters were below, and walking was the one thing he was genuinely excellent at. He tightened his pack straps, put the slope at his back, and went.

Behind him, faint on the night wind, he heard the captain's voice carry up through the trees — calm, unhurried, certain, addressed to someone far away through whatever means captains like her used to talk to queens:

*"He's heading for the Ember. Send word ahead. And send Rell."*

Edmund didn't know who Rell was.

He was going to find out.
