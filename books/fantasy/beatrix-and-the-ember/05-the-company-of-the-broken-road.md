## 5. The Company of the Broken Road

*Three weeks before the storm.*

Beatrix Ash had a rule about campfires: never build one where you couldn't see the road. Roads meant people, and people meant either trouble or news, and she'd learned to tell the difference by the way they walked toward you.

The four of them were strung along the ridge above the old salt road like laundry on a line — Sera picking her teeth with a straw, Odo standing the way he always stood, as though at attention before a king who'd stopped deserving it, and the twins sprawled in the grass arguing about whether a horse could be taught to lie.

"It could," Marlow insisted. "Horses are honest-looking. That's exactly what you'd want in a liar."

"That's not how lying works," said Pip.

"You'd know."

"I would, actually, because I'm the one who—"

"Boys." Beatrix didn't raise her voice. She never had to. "If either of you can teach a horse to lie, I'll eat my boots."

"Both boots?" Pip asked, with the gravity of a man negotiating a treaty.

"Both boots. Buttered."

Sera snorted. "I'll bring the butter."

It was a good evening. That was what Beatrix remembered afterward, in the way you remember the last warm day before winter — not because anything happened, but because nothing did. The fire was small and well-fed. Odo had actually laughed once, a rusty sound like a gate opening after years shut. And somewhere down in the valley, the world went on being broken, and they went on planning to fix it, or at least to make the breaking more expensive for the people who profited from it.

They were looking for the Ember. Had been for two years. And they were looking for something else too, though none of them said it aloud: a way to do it that didn't end with all five of them dead in a ditch with their names struck from every record Veyra's clerks kept.

"We should go through the valley," Sera said, around the straw.

Odo's head came up. "No."

"I'm just saying—"

"Don't." His voice had gone flat, the way it did when he was holding something down. "That valley is a dead place. Nothing comes out of it. Nothing goes in. Whatever's in there, it isn't ours."

"The map fragments say otherwise."

"The map fragments say a lot of things. Half of them are bait."

"They're *her* map fragments," Sera said quietly, and the fire popped, and everyone looked at Beatrix, because it was her map, her fragments, her obsession, gathered scrap by scrap over years from smugglers' caches and burned libraries and one very memorable night in a tax collector's cellar that Pip still told wrong at parties.

Beatrix turned the largest fragment over in her fingers. It wasn't paper, quite. It was skin-thin vellum that stayed faintly warm no matter the weather, and inked on it in lines that shifted when you weren't looking was the shape of a kingdom — and near its western edge, a blank space. A hole in the map. A valley drawn as absence.

"There's a gate there," she said. "There has to be. Everything else fits around the hole. You don't leave a hole that size unless you're hiding the most important thing in it."

"Or unless the hole is hiding *you*," said Odo.

She smiled at him. He hated when she smiled at him mid-argument; he said it was cheating. "Then I suppose we'll find out which."

She never got the chance to show them. That was the joke of it, the cruel little joke she suspected she'd have appreciated if she'd lived long enough to appreciate jokes again: she spent two years assembling the way in, and then walked into the valley alone, at night, in a storm, because the fragments had finally agreed on a date and the date was *now*, and waiting felt like the one thing she couldn't afford.

She didn't tell them. If she had, they'd have come. All four of them, into a dead valley, against wards designed to keep out everything with a drop of magic in its blood — which was all four of them, every one, even Odo, who claimed his magic had been burned out of him at his disgrace trial but whose blood still sang when Sera cast near him.

Only someone empty could walk through that gate. Only someone hollow.

So she went alone, and she left them a letter that said *gone ahead, don't follow, I mean it, I will know*, and she signed it with the little ash-leaf mark she used for jokes, so they'd know it was half a joke, so they wouldn't worry.

She hoped they hadn't worried.

She hoped, in the last grey minutes on a stranger's floor, with her blood going cold and her hands already cooling around a stranger's wrists, that they were somewhere warm, arguing about horses.

---

*Now.*

Pip found the cottage by falling into it.

Technically, he found the chimney by falling into it, having misjudged a rooftop crossing in pursuit of a pigeon that had looked, from below, like it might be carrying a message tube. The pigeon had been an ordinary pigeon. The roof had been an ordinary roof. The chimney, however, had been attached to an ordinary cottage with an ordinary door, out of which burst an ordinary-sized man holding a poker, wearing the expression of someone whose week had already gone badly and was interested in making it worse.

"Sorry!" said Pip, from inside the fireplace, covered in soot. "Sorry. Wrong house. Terribly sorry. Do you have a back door? Asking for myself."

The man did not have a back door, or if he did, he wasn't inclined to share it. What he had was a poker, raised, trembling slightly — and pinned under his boot, Pip noticed, a piece of vellum that glowed.

Not reflected light. Not firelight. It glowed the way the map fragments glowed, warm and alive, and Pip forgot about the poker entirely.

"That's hers," he said.

"Get out of my house."

"That's *hers*. That's—" He scrambled up, soot cascading, and the man swung the poker and Pip caught it on his forearm and yelped and danced backward and knocked over a chair and stepped in the soup. There was soup. It had been cooling on the table. It was now cooling on Pip.

"OUT."

"Marlow!" Pip bellowed, at a volume calculated to carry three fields and possibly a province. "*MARLOW!*"

What happened next took perhaps ninety seconds and involved, in order: Marlow coming through the window instead of the door because he was the dramatic twin; Sera arriving at a dead run with her hair loose and her hands already full of green light; Odo filling the doorway like a piece of architecture that had decided to move; and the man with the poker retreating step by step until his back hit the wall, holding the glowing vellum against his chest like a shield, saying, in a voice that cracked on the second word:

"Don't touch it. It burns. It burns anyone who touches it. I've tried."

Everyone stopped.

Sera's light guttered. "Tried," she repeated.

"To give it back," the man said. His eyes were red-rimmed and steady, and up close Pip could see he wasn't young, exactly, but he wasn't old either — he was one of those faces that age had simply declined to comment on. "To bury it. To throw it in the river. It comes back. It only doesn't burn me." He swallowed. "She made me swear to deliver it. I refused. Then she died on my floor anyway, and now I can't put it down, and I don't know what it is, and there are four of you in my kitchen."

"Five," said Marlow, still half in the window. "You're forgetting me."

"I'm trying to."

Sera stepped forward slowly, the way you approach a spooked animal, or a lit fuse. "Sir. What was her name?"

The man's jaw worked. "Ash," he said. "She said — she said her name was Beatrix Ash. She said—" His voice caught, and he steadied it with visible effort, the effort itself telling Sera everything, because men who cried easily let it catch, and this man was fighting it like a flooded river fought a mill gate. "She said, *find the ones who argue about horses.*"

The silence that followed was total. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"Oh," said Sera, very softly. And then she sat down, right there on the floor, among the soot and the soup and the overturned chair, and put her face in her hands, and began, silently, to shake.

---

Later — much later, after the fire had been rebuilt and the soup situation resolved and Odo had repaired the chair with three nails and a glare — they sat around the stranger's table and he told them all of it.

His name was Edmund Yarrow. He was the keeper of a gate in a valley where magic didn't work, and he had been the keeper his whole life, and his father before him, and so on back into the murk. He spoke about it the way a man describes weather: factual, uncomplaining, faintly baffled that anyone would find it remarkable.

"She came through the wards during the storm," he said. "Bleeding. Burned, somehow — not fire-burned, more like... scraped. Like something had tried to erase her and mostly succeeded." His hands were flat on the table, careful, as though the wood might bolt. "I did what I could. It wasn't enough. She knew it wasn't going to be enough before I did, I think. She was very calm about it. It was infuriating."

"She had that effect," said Odo. His voice was rough. He was staring at the map where it lay between them, and his hand hovered near it and flinched back, and Pip saw the fresh red mark across his palm where he'd tested it earlier, alone, by the well, thinking no one watched. Everyone had watched.

"She pressed this into my hands and told me to deliver it. I said no." Edmund's jaw tightened. "I said no because I have a post. I have duties. I said no, and she died, and the map — stayed. It won't leave. I can't make it leave. So I am delivering it," he finished, with the flat dignity of a man who has lost an argument with an object, "apparently to you."

"Not to us," Sera said. Her eyes were swollen but dry now, and there was something new in her face, something that scared Pip a little because it looked like hope and hope, in their line of work, was usually a symptom. "To the Ember. We're just the road."

"The Ember," Edmund repeated. "She said that word. She said I'd understand eventually. I don't."

"No," Sera agreed. "But you will. Probably sooner than any of us would like."

Marlow leaned across the table. He'd been quiet, which was unlike him, studying Edmund with the particular intensity he usually reserved for locks and lies. "Can I ask you something strange?"

"You may ask."

"When you touched her — at the end. Did anything happen? Anything odd. Cold, or quiet, or..."

Edmund frowned. "Quiet," he admitted. "Her pain stopped. For a moment. She noticed. She smiled and said, *oh, that's nice,* and then she—" He stopped.

"Thank you," Marlow said, and sat back, and exchanged a look with Pip that contained an entire conversation, the way twins' looks did. *Null,* the look said. *A real one. A living one. The thing the fragments kept circling. The hole in the map.*

"Well," said Pip, into the silence, because someone had to, and it was tradition that it be him, and tradition was the spine of civilization. "This is the part where we decide whether to rob you and run."

"Pip," said Odo warningly.

"What! It's traditional. We're outlaws. He's sitting on the most valuable object in the kingdom and a bowl of soup he clearly can't defend. Someone has to say it out loud so we can all agree not to do it. That's how we know we're the good kind of outlaws."

"He's not wrong," Marlow said. "It's how we know."

Edmund looked around the table — at the hedge-witch with her singed sleeves, at the big man with the guard's posture and the traitor's brand hidden under his collar, at the twins who finished each other's crimes — and Pip watched him do the arithmetic, watched him arrive at the sum, watched something in his tired face almost, almost become a smile.

"You're her family," he said. It wasn't a question.

"We're her family," Sera confirmed. Her voice wobbled on the second word and firmed on the third. "And we've been looking for her for three weeks, and we found you instead, and I want you to understand that I'm not ungrateful, but I would burn down a province to trade places."

"I know," Edmund said quietly. "She told me you'd come. She said it like she knew it the way she knew rain was coming. She said, *they'll argue the whole way, but they'll come.*"

"And were we arguing when you found us?" Marlow asked Pip.

"You were arguing about whether horses can lie."

"Were we ever going to stop?"

"No."

"Then she knew us," Marlow said, satisfied, and stole the last of the soup.

---

They buried what they could bury, which was nothing, and that was the worst of it. There was no body to grieve over, no grave to dig, no weight to carry. There was a valley with a sealed gate and a law of nature standing in front of it, and four people with shovels-shaped holes in their chests and nowhere to put them.

So Sera did what hedge-witches did. She built a marker rite out of what was at hand: a candle stub, a thread pulled from her own sleeve, a cup of well water, and the smallest of the map fragments, which Beatrix had given her years ago with instructions to *keep this somewhere I can't lose it, which means somewhere I'd never look, which means your sock drawer.*

They stood in Edmund's yard at dusk, the five of them — six, counting the candle — and Sera spoke the old informal words, the ones that belonged to no temple and no crown, the ones passed hand to hand among people the records forgot.

"Beatrix Ash," she said. "Thief, liar, leader, friend. You stole a crown's worth of secrets and a beggar's worth of hearts, and you never once paid full price for either, because you were better at this than anyone." Her voice held. Pip was proud of her. He was less proud of himself; his face was doing things he'd rather it didn't. "We didn't find you. You found us first, like always. And wherever the road went after —" the candle flame bent, though there was no wind — "we're coming. You knew we would. Show-off."

The flame stood straight again.

Nobody spoke for a while. Then Odo cleared his throat, and when he spoke, his voice was the parade-ground voice, the one from before, the one he thought he'd lost. "Company of the Broken Road," he said. "Roll call. Winterbourne—" he nodded at Edmund, who started, and blinked, and after a moment, uncertainly, raised one hand, and Pip decided on the spot that this was the funniest thing he had ever seen and also that he would die before laughing at it. "Sera. Marlow. Pip." Each name like a stone set in a wall. "And Ash." A pause. "Present," Odo said, "in absentia. Dismissed."

The candle burned down. The stars came out over a valley that had never once, in Edmund Yarrow's whole guarded life, hosted a gathering of outlaws, and seemed, on the whole, willing to try it.

---

They voted at midnight, around the banked fire, in the traditional manner: everyone talked at once until Sera threatened to turn someone into a newt, and then everyone talked slightly less at once, and then Odo banged his fist on the table, which was the formal close of debate.

"He comes with us," Sera said. It wasn't a question either, but she said it like one, because votes needed asking. "He carries the map because the map insists. And because—" she hesitated, and Pip watched her choose honesty over comfort, which was the bravest thing anyone had done all day, including the chimney incident. "Because she chose him. Out of everyone alive. She walked into a dead valley knowing only someone empty could pass the gate, and she picked him. I want to know why. I think the answer is the whole quest."

"She picked me because I was there," Edmund said.

"She climbed a mountain range to get there," said Marlow. "In a storm. With wounds that should've killed her twice over. She had time to pick."

Edmund looked down at the map, warm against his palms, patient as a heartbeat. "I have a post," he said, but it came out thin, a man testing a rope he already suspected was cut.

"Had," said Odo.

"The valley—"

"Will keep," Sera said gently. "Valleys are good at it. Kings aren't. Come on, keeper. Walk us out of your valley and let's go find out what our girl died carrying."

Edmund Yarrow looked at each of them in turn — the witch, the guard, the twins — strangers, criminals, family, and Pip saw the exact moment the rope gave, saw thirty-some years of solitary duty tilt and begin, slowly, terribly, wonderfully, to fall.

"All right," he said.

And far to the east, in a tower of black glass, something ancient and attentive opened one eye, and felt, for the first time in twenty years, a silence moving in the world — a hole walking, a null born and bred, carrying a dead woman's fire toward the heart of everything.

Veyra smiled, and reached for her hunters.
