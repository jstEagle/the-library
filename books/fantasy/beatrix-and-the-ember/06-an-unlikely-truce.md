## 6. An Unlikely Truce

Odo had killed men for less than what the gatekeeper was carrying.

He sat across the fire from Edmund Yarrow and did the arithmetic he'd been doing since sundown: one man, no magic, no sword worth the name, asleep — or pretending to sleep — with a map tucked against his chest that could unmake a queen. Four of them. Sera, who could put a man to sleep with a word if she wanted to be gentle about it. The twins, who between them owned six knives and no scruples when it came to Beatrix's work. And himself, who had once been sworn to protect a king and now spent his nights doing sums like this one.

It was not even a hard sum.

"You're staring at him again," Pip said, dropping onto the log beside him. He kept his voice low, which was considerate of him, and he was grinning, which was not.

"I'm keeping watch."

"You're doing sums." Marlow settled on Odo's other side, mirroring his brother like a bad habit. "You get a look. Like a merchant counting coins that don't add up."

"They don't add up," Odo said. "One man walks out of a sealed valley carrying the thing we've bled four years looking for. The thing *she* died looking for. And we're supposed to just — walk beside him."

"Supposed to?" Sera's voice came soft from the dark beyond the firelight. She stepped into it with her arms crossed, hedge-witch's shawl pulled tight, and Odo felt the temperature of the conversation drop the way it always did when she decided to join in. "Nobody's supposed to do anything. That's rather the point of being outlaws."

"He's not an outlaw. He's a gatekeeper. He's never broken a law in his life."

"Then he'll be easy to catch," Pip said cheerfully.

Odo looked at him until the grin faded. "I'm serious."

"We know you're serious," Marlow said, quieter. "That's the problem. You're always serious, and half the time you're right, and the other half you're just tired and angry and calling it serious."

The gatekeeper shifted in his bedroll by the far side of the fire. Not asleep, then. Odo noted it without surprise. A man who'd guarded a door alone for twenty years didn't sleep easy with strangers breathing around him, however honest his face was.

Honest faces were the dangerous ones. Odo knew that better than most. He'd worn one himself for eleven years in the royal guard while the rot spread through the palace around him, smiling at ministers he should have arrested, saluting a king whose eyes had gone strange and empty, telling himself every morning that loyalty meant staying. Until the night he finally understood what staying made him, and left, and discovered too late that leaving was its own kind of betrayal — because Beatrix had stayed, Beatrix had fought, Beatrix had built something out of the wreckage while he'd been licking his wounds in a border town pretending to be a carter.

And now Beatrix was dead, and her map was sleeping against a stranger's chest, and Odo was doing sums.

"The way I see it," he said, "we have three choices. We take the map and leave him. We take the map and don't leave him. Or we follow him and hope he doesn't get us all killed."

"That's a grim little menu," Sera said.

"It's an honest one."

"And if the map burns anyone else who touches it?"

"That's a story *he* tells." Odo nodded at the sleeping man. "Convenient, isn't it? The one object in the world we need, and only he can hold it. I've served liars before. They all had reasons the rules applied to everyone but them."

Sera was quiet for a moment. Firelight moved on her face. "You want to test it," she said. Not a question. She knew him too well; four years of shared roads and shared graves will do that whether you want it or not.

"I want to *know*."

"And if it burns him?"

"It won't. It's his."

"There it is," Marlow murmured. "There's the sum."

Pip stood up, brushing off his knees. "Well. If we're going to rob a sleeping man, can we at least wake him first? It seems rude otherwise. Also he's got hands like spades and I'd rather he saw it coming."

"Pip—"

"No, actually, I mean it." For once there was nothing joking in his face. "Beatrix found people. That was her whole trick. She looked at the worst wrecks on the road and saw something worth keeping. You. Me. Sera. Marlow after the thing with the guild. And now some poor gatekeeper gets handed her dying wish in a valley with no magic in it, and our first thought is to take it off his corpse-shaped future? She'd haunt us. She'd *actually* haunt us. She told me once she'd learned to whistle through keyholes specifically for that purpose."

Despite everything, Odo felt his mouth twitch. "She did not."

"She absolutely did. Ask Marlow."

"She did," Marlow confirmed solemnly. "Keyhole whistling. Very specific about it."

But Odo was already standing, and the humor drained out of the circle as they understood what he meant to do. Sera rose too. Nobody said stop. That was the thing about their little family — nobody ever said stop, they just made sure you knew they'd seen you do it, and that they'd remember.

Odo crossed the camp. The gatekeeper's eyes opened before Odo was three steps away, steady and unsurprised, and Odo revised his estimate upward again: not a fool, then. Just a man who'd done his own sums and arrived at the same answer and decided there was nothing to be done about it.

"I'm sorry about this," Odo said, and meant it, which somehow made it worse.

He reached into the man's coat, where the map lay wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, and took hold of it.

The world went white.

Not heat exactly — or not only heat. It was heat with a will behind it, heat that *knew* him, that poured up his arm and into the old scar tissue of his knuckles and found every oath he'd ever sworn and set each one alight in turn. He heard himself shout. He heard Sera saying words in the old tongue, sharp and fast. He was on his knees, he realized distantly, with his hand cradled against his chest like a wounded bird, and the pain was already fading, which was somehow more frightening than the pain itself, because it faded like a warning. Like a voice saying: *that was the introduction.*

When he could see again, the gatekeeper was sitting up, holding the wrapped map, watching him with something awful and gentle in his face.

"I tried to tell you," Edmund Yarrow said. "I'm sorry. I did try."

"I know," Odo rasped. His hand was unmarked. That was the worst part. No blister, no scar, no evidence — just the memory of the burning written somewhere deeper than skin. "I know you did."

* * *

They didn't speak for an hour after that. Odo sat apart, flexing his unburned hand, and let the others handle the fire and the watches and the small machinery of the night. He was aware of Sera moving around him, deliberately not hovering, and of the twins taking turns walking past his shoulder with elaborate casualness, the way you check on a bear you've decided not to shoot.

It was Marlow who finally sat down across from him and said, "Your hand."

"Is fine."

"Your hand is fine. Your pride is in pieces, though, so I've brought you tea." He handed over a tin cup of something that tasted mostly of pine needles and optimism. "Pip says you're going to sulk till dawn. I said no, Odo doesn't sulk, Odo broods, it's different, it has posture."

"I'm right here," Odo said.

"You are. That's why we're talking about you. It's traditional." Marlow watched him over his own cup. "You know what I keep thinking about? Correnvale. Two years ago. When we couldn't get past the toll-wardens and Beatrix walked up to them and told them a joke so filthy that by the end they were paying *us* to leave. Do you remember what she said after?"

Odo did remember. He said nothing.

"She said, 'Everyone's a door, Odo. You just have to find out what they're keeping shut.'" Marlow shrugged. "So. What are you keeping shut?"

"You already know."

"Say it anyway. It helps. Sera says saying things is basically free medicine."

Odo turned the cup in his good hand. "I failed her," he said. "Before any of you. Back when it mattered. There was a night in the palace — I won't tell it, it's long and it's ugly — but there was a night I could have acted, and I calculated instead. I did sums. Like tonight. And my sums cost her everything, and she never once threw it in my face, which was worse than if she had." He looked up. "And now she's dead on a stranger's floor in a valley where none of us could reach her, and the last piece of her is in the hands of a man I've known for two days, and my first instinct was to take it from him. So no, Marlow. My hand is fine. Everything else is under review."

Marlow considered this with the seriousness he usually saved for lock mechanisms. Then he said, "You know she chose him."

"What?"

"Beatrix. Out of everyone she could have run to — us, the network, half the free cities — she ran to a sealed gate in a magicless valley and put it in the hands of a stranger. She wasn't delirious. Sera checked the courier's account twice. She knew what she was doing." Marlow drank his terrible tea. "Maybe your sums are wrong. Maybe hers weren't."

Across the fire, the gatekeeper was sitting up now, and Sera was beside him, and they were talking low. Odo watched them the way you watch weather.

* * *

In the grey hour before dawn, Sera woke them all, and Odo understood immediately what day it was, because she was carrying the bundle of ash-wood and red thread, and her face had the stillness it got only twice a year: on this day, and on the anniversary of the guild children, which was worse, and which nobody spoke of.

"Grave-marker rite," she said simply. "We couldn't bury her where she fell. Too close to the wards, too close to Veyra's hunters. So we mark her here, and we carry the marker, and someday — when this is over — we bring her home properly. All of you. Even him." Her eyes went to the gatekeeper. "Especially him. He's the one she trusted."

They built it together, which was the whole point of it, Sera said. A marker wasn't a monument; it was a promise with wood around it. Pip split the ash-wood because he was best with the axe and needed something to hit. Marlow bound the joints with red thread, three turns, always three. Odo carved — because his hands were the steadiest, and because Beatrix had once told him that a man who'd spent his life guarding doors should know how to make one, and this was the closest he'd come.

Into the wood he cut the sign she'd used to mark safe houses: a door, slightly open, with light coming through the gap. Underneath, because Sera told him to and because it was true:

**BEATRIX ASH — SHE CARRIED US.**

The gatekeeper stood at the edge of the work, awkward as a man at a funeral where he knows no one, which of course was exactly what he was. He'd offered to help twice and been gently refused both times — this part was theirs — and Odo had watched him accept it with a grace that annoyed him, frankly, because it would have been easier to dislike the man if the man had been dislikeable.

When the marker was finished and planted in the earth at the head of their little camp, Sera began the rite. It was old hedge-craft, older than the guilds, older maybe than the crown: each person spoke to the marker in turn, whatever they needed to say, and the wind was invited to take it wherever the dead go.

Pip told a story about a stolen pie and a magistrate's wig. It was funny and it fell apart halfway through and he finished it crying, and Marlow held his shoulder, and that was right too.

Marlow said, "You taught me that locks are just questions. I miss you every time a door opens easily."

Sera said very little, in the way of people for whom the loss is structural, load-bearing. She touched the wood and said, "Not yet. I'm not done. Wait for me," and Odo's chest hurt.

Then it was his turn, and Odo knelt at the marker with his burned-and-unburned hand flat against the ash-wood, and found that everything he'd been carrying since the valley — the sums, the suspicion, the shame of it — came up his throat at once.

"I was going to rob you," he said plainly, because Beatrix had hated pretty lies and he wasn't going to start decorating now. "Last night. Your map, off your sleeping body. Your friend here talked me out of it, and then the map itself talked me out of it harder." A rough sound went around the circle that might have been a laugh. "I've spent four years being angry that I wasn't there when you needed me. And I think — I think some of that anger was easier than the truth, which is that you found these people, and built this thing, and it works, and it worked without me for a long time. But you left a door open anyway. You always did." His voice caught. He let it. "So I'm making you a promise where you can hear it. I'll guard him the way I should have guarded you. Not because he's earned it. Because you chose him, and I'm done second-guessing your choices. They were better than mine."

He stood. His knees cracked. Somewhere above the trees the wind moved, and the red thread on the marker lifted and settled, and Sera closed her eyes briefly as if listening to something.

Then, to Odo's mild horror, the gatekeeper stepped forward.

"I don't know the rite," Edmund Yarrow said. "If it's only for family, I'll step back. But I'd like to say something, if I may."

"Say it," Sera said.

He knelt where Odo had knelt. Up close, in the early light, he looked exactly like what he was: a tired man in a warden's coat who had buried a stranger yesterday and inherited a war today.

"I only knew you for an hour," he said to the marker. "You came through my storm bleeding, and you were rude about my soup, and you made me swear an oath I refused to swear, and then you died on my floor. In twenty years at that gate, no one had ever died on my floor. No one had ever *been* on my floor." He paused. "I didn't take your oath. I want whoever travels with me to know that. I said no. And you pressed the map into my hands anyway and said, 'Then carry it, don't swear it. Refusal's just another way of holding on.'" His jaw worked. "I've been trying to understand what you saw in me. I still don't. But I've got your map, and your people keep looking at me like I'm a letter they're afraid to open, and I've decided the least I can do is deliver you safely — all of you — to whatever it is you were trying to reach. I'm not family. But I'll walk with them. If they'll have me."

Silence. The wind moved again.

Then Pip said, "Well, obviously you're having breakfast with us, don't be ridiculous," and the spell broke into the ordinary noise of a camp waking up, and Odo found Sera at his elbow.

"Well?" she murmured.

"Well what?"

"You did sums all night. Give me the total."

Odo watched the gatekeeper accept a tin cup of pine-needle tea from Marlow with the bewildered politeness of a man being adopted by wolves. He watched Pip immediately begin teaching him some card game whose rules changed whenever Pip was losing. He flexed his unburned hand, which still remembered the fire, and would for a long time, and maybe that was fitting — a reminder written somewhere deeper than skin, the map's own signature on him.

"The total," Odo said slowly, "is that I don't trust him. But I trust her. And she trusted him. And I'm too old and too tired to believe I know better than a dead woman who was smarter than me alive." He drained his tea and grimaced. "Besides, anyone who insults his soup within a minute of meeting him can't be all bad."

Sera smiled — the first real one in two days — and then it faded into something more careful, the look she got when knowledge was sitting heavy in her.

"There's something you all need to hear," she said. "Him especially. About what the map leads to. About what the Ember actually *is*, and what happens to anyone foolish enough to touch it." She glanced at the gatekeeper, laughing at something Pip had said, holding the map against his chest like a sleeping child. "And Odo — I think when you hear it, you're going to wish you'd taken the map when you had the chance. For his sake."

The fire crackled. Somewhere east, past the hills, the road bent toward a war.

"Sera," Odo said. "What does it do?"

She fed a stick to the flames and watched it catch.

"It listens," she said.
