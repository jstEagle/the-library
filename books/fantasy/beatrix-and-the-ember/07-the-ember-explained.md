## 7. The Ember Explained

Sera had learned long ago that the best way to keep frightened people from bolting was to feed them first.

So she cooked. She cooked the way her grandmother had taught her in a kitchen that smelled of rosemary and secrets — slowly, with too much butter, humming under her breath so the stew would settle kindly in the pot and not curdle out of spite. Hedge-craft was mostly that: small kindnesses done to food and fire and fevered brows, dressed up in rhyme so the clients felt they'd gotten their money's worth.

The camp sat in the lee of a fallen watchtower, half a day's walk from the gate Edmund Yarrow had walked out of with the map pressed to his chest like a man carrying a hot coal he'd been told never to put down. Which, Sera thought, watching him across the fire, was more or less exactly what he was doing.

He sat apart from the others, the way a man sits when he hasn't yet decided whether he's been kidnapped or rescued. Odo had given him a blanket. Pip had given him three separate opinions on the best way to season trail rabbit. Marlow had given him nothing but a long, measuring look, which from Marlow was practically a hug. And Beatrix's map lay against his ribs, wrapped in oilcloth, radiating a heat only Sera could feel from where she sat — a low, patient warmth, like a sleeping animal waiting for its name to be called.

She ladled stew into six bowls and handed them around, saving the one with the least gristle for Edmund, because grief and shock always sat in the stomach, and a stranger who'd watched a woman die on his floor two days ago deserved the least gristle.

"Right," she said, settling onto her pack with her own bowl. "Questions."

Pip's hand shot up immediately, which was very Pip.

"You don't have to raise your hand, love, we're not a school."

"I'm raising it anyway," Pip said. "It's polite."

"Fine. Go."

"What *is* it?" Pip said, pointing his spoon at Edmund's chest. "The map. It burned Odo. It nearly burned me, and I only touched the wrapping. But Edmund's been carrying it for two days and he doesn't even have a red mark. That's not normal. Nothing about him is normal. No offense," he added to Edmund.

"Some taken," said Edmund mildly.

"That's fair."

Marlow, sitting with his bowl balanced on his knee, spoke without looking up. "The real question is what's at the end of it." His eyes came up, dark and steady. "Because Trix didn't die walking away from something small."

The fire popped. Somewhere off in the dark, an owl asked its question of the night and got no answer.

Sera took a breath. She had rehearsed this speech in her head perhaps forty times since they'd found Edmund standing over Beatrix's grave-marker rite like a man at his own funeral, and every rehearsal had ended differently, because there was no good way to say what needed saying. There was only the true way, and the true way was heavy.

"All right," she said. "Then I'll tell you all of it, and I'll tell it once, so nobody has to ask twice. And I want you to let me get to the end before anyone decides anything. Agreed?"

Odo nodded slowly. The twins nodded. Edmund set down his bowl, which Sera noted with some approval — a man who gave a story his full attention was a man worth trusting with one.

"You all know Veyra took the capital six years ago," Sera began. "You know she calls herself Queen Regent. You know the free cities stopped answering her letters, and she stopped sending any. What you don't know — what almost nobody knows — is how she did it. A sorceress, however strong, cannot simply walk into a palace and take a kingdom. Armies resist. Guards resist. Kings most of all resist; it's practically their whole job."

"So she didn't fight Aldric," Marlow said quietly.

"No," Sera said. "She bound him."

She reached into her satchel and drew out a stub of chalk — ordinary chalk, bought for a copper in a market town whose name she'd already forgotten — and knelt by the flat stone at the edge of the firelight. She drew as she talked, quick sure lines: a circle, a flame inside it, a thread running from the flame up and out of the circle to a small figure she sketched with a crown.

"There is a thing," she said, "older than the crown, older than the kingdoms, older than most of the gods people bother praying to. The old texts call it the Ember. Not fire the way we know fire — not something that burns wood or houses. Fire the way a heart burns. Living flame. It was kept, ages back, by the first kings, sealed beneath the capital, because it does one thing and does it absolutely: it binds wills together. Whoever holds the Ember can bind another soul to their own. Permanently. Completely. The bound one keeps their mind, their memories, their voice — everything except choice. They become a hand that thinks it's still making decisions."

Nobody moved. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"Aldric wasn't taken by force," Sera said. "He was taken the way this thing takes people. Quietly. One morning he woke up king, and by evening he was Veyra's. And the worst part—" her voice caught, and she steadied it, "—the worst part is that everyone around him could see it happening and couldn't prove it. Because he still signs orders. He still speaks. He still rides through the capital on Binding Day with his crown on. He just... agrees. With whatever she wants. Forever."

Edmund spoke for the first time. His voice was low and careful, the voice of a man used to weighing words before spending them. "And the map leads to this Ember."

"It leads to the vault where it's kept," Sera said. "Under the throne room itself. Beatrix found the way in. Don't ask me how — she never told me the whole of it, and now nobody living knows except that map." She looked at him. "Except maybe you, Gatekeeper, if it ever decides to talk."

"Don't call me that yet," Edmund said. "I've only been outside a valley for two days."

"Fair enough." She turned back to the chalk drawing. "Now here's the part I need you all to hear properly, because it changes everything, and I won't have anyone saying later that they didn't know."

She tapped the little flame in the circle.

"The Ember isn't an object. You can't smash it like a lockbox. It's alive — it has been alive longer than any nation on this continent — and it is wound through King Aldric like a root through soil. Every thread of compulsion Veyra spun runs from the Ember into him. So yes, destroy the Ember, and the binding breaks." She looked up, met each of their eyes in turn. "And the king dies with it. Instantly. There's no version where you cut the root and the tree shrugs it off. The binding *is* his life support and his prison both. Burn the flame, kill the king. Free the king, and someone has to unbind the flame without breaking it. Nobody knows how. Beatrix thought she knew how. Beatrix thought a lot of things."

Silence. Pip had stopped eating, which Sera had witnessed perhaps four times in all the years she'd known him, and three of those involved actual illness.

"So it's a trap," Odo said at last. His voice was flat, guard's-flat, the voice he used when the tactical picture was bad and he wanted everyone to know he knew. "A quest with no winning move. We carry the map across a continent, past Veyra's hunters, into the most guarded vault in the world, to stand in front of a thing we cannot destroy and cannot safely touch."

"Yes," Sera said simply.

"And Trix knew that."

"Trix knew that better than any of us. She spent four years finding the way in. She used to say—" Sera smiled despite herself, and the smile hurt. "She used to say, 'There's always a door, Sera. Doors are just walls that haven't met the right person yet.'"

"She'd know," Marlow murmured. "She was the right person for most of them."

* * *

Later, when the twins had rolled into their bedrolls arguing in whispers about whether the Ember could be reasoned with ("it's a fire, Pip" / "everything can be reasoned with, Marlow, that's what talking *is*"), and Odo had taken first watch with the grim thoroughness of a man who trusted the night about as far as he could throw it, Sera stayed by the coals.

Edmund hadn't slept either. He sat with his back against the fallen tower-stone, the oilcloth bundle in his lap, turning it over in his hands like a man trying to read a letter through the envelope.

"You should rest," she said, coming to sit near him. Close enough to talk. Not so close as to crowd. She'd had a lot of practice with distances over the years; hedge-witches learned early which side of a sickbed to stand on.

"I rested for twenty years," Edmund said. "In the valley. Same gate, same path, same four seasons. I think I can manage one more night awake."

"What was it like?" she asked. "The valley. Magicless."

He considered that longer than most people would have. She liked that about him already — that he treated questions as though they deserved rent before he let them stay.

"Quiet," he said finally. "Nothing ever hummed. Nothing glowed. When I was a boy I thought the whole world was like that, and then my father told me it was just us, just the valley, and I remember feeling..." He searched. "Cheated. And then relieved. And then, after enough years, I stopped being able to tell the difference between the relief and the cheating." He glanced at her. "What's it like, the other way? Being full of it?"

"Oh, I'm not full of it," Sera laughed softly. "Hedge-craft is what magic looks like when it's poor. Grandmamma used to say the great sorcerers move mountains, and the rest of us move supper. But it's — I don't know. Like hearing music other people can't. Sometimes beautiful. Sometimes you wish it would shut up."

"And now?" He looked down at the bundle. "This thing. This Ember. Everyone who touches the map gets burned except me. Why?"

And there it was. The question she'd been circling all evening, the one she'd hoped he wouldn't ask until she'd had time to think of a kind answer, because she didn't have one — only a true one.

"I don't know for certain," she said. "But I have a guess, and you're not going to like it."

"I haven't liked much since Tuesday," Edmund said. "Go ahead."

"The map is keyed to the Ember. Made from it, maybe, or made to find it — Beatrix never said, and maps like that are old craft, older than me, older than my line. Things like that choose their bearers. They burn what they reject." She held his gaze. "It didn't reject you, Edmund. Whatever you are, whatever your family has been for however many generations behind that gate — the map looked at every hand offered to it, and it chose yours. And I'd bet my last charm-salt that's not an accident. Wardens don't get chosen for gates at random. Gates don't get built where they're not needed."

Edmund was quiet for a long moment. The coals shifted, breathing orange.

"My father said we kept the gate because our family always had," he said at last. "That was the whole answer. I asked why, once, when I was nine. He said, 'Because the valley needs keeping,' and went back to mending the hinge." A ghost of a smile crossed his face. "I thought he was avoiding the question. Now I think that was the question."

"Maybe," Sera said gently. "Or maybe he loved you too much to give you the heavy answer while you were nine. Parents do that. They carry the weight and hand you the handle."

Something in his face cracked, just slightly — grief for a father, and a life, and a certainty, all in the same small movement. Sera reached over and squeezed his wrist, once, and let go. Some comforts were better brief.

"Get some sleep, Gatekeeper," she said, rising. "Tomorrow we start walking south, and the road through Ashfall Province is long, ugly, and full of people who'd sell us to Veyra for less than the price of that stew."

"Sera." He said her name like he was testing whether it fit in his mouth yet. "If the Ember can't be destroyed, and it can't be safely freed — what exactly is the plan? Beatrix's plan. Did she have one?"

Sera stood there with the firelight on her face and told him the truth, or the shape of it.

"She had a word," she said. "'Unbind.' She wrote it on everything — margins, napkins, the back of Odo's gauntlet once, which he was furious about. She believed there was a way to loose the flame from the king without killing either. She died believing it. And tomorrow we start walking toward the only proof she ever found." She paused. "That's not a plan. I know it's not a plan. But it's a direction, and directions have saved worse people than us."

Edmund looked down at the map in his lap, and Sera saw — plain as chalk on stone — the exact moment the heat of it answered him. A faint glow through the oilcloth, warm as a banked hearth, pulsing once, twice, like something underneath it turning over in its sleep and listening.

Edmund went very still.

"Sera," he said quietly. "It's never done that before."

The glow pulsed again — and this time, faint but unmistakable, carried on a wind that touched nothing else in the camp, came a smell none of them had smelled in six years.

Woodsmoke from the capital. And beneath it, sweet and wrong, the scent of burning roses.

South, the map said, in the only language it had. *Come south.*

Sera looked at the sleeping company, at Odo rigid at his watch, at the road beyond the firelight bending toward Ashfall — and understood that whatever they'd agreed to around this fire tonight, the map had just voted too.
