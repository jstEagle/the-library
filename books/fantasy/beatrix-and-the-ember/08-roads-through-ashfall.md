## 8. Roads Through Ashfall

Pip had decided, somewhere around the third day out of the valley, that the world beyond it was a liar.

Everyone always said the world was big and bright and full of wonders. Nobody ever mentioned that it also smelled like burnt porridge. That's what Ashfall Province smelled like — burnt porridge, if the porridge had been a forest, and a village, and a granary, and a chapel, and about forty miles of everything in between. Char and wet cinder and the sweetish rot of things that had burned and then gotten rained on and then burned again for good measure.

"Cheerful place," Marlow said, walking beside him.

"You're cheerful," Pip said.

"One of us has to be."

They were the twins, Pip and Marlow, nineteen years old, and they had been walking at the back of the company for two days because the back was where you could talk without Sera turning around and giving them the Look. The Look was famous. The Look had once made a customs officer in Drellin's Ferry return a stolen barge and apologize.

Ahead of them, the road bent through a field of blackened trees. Odo walked point, hand never far from his sword, moving like a man who expected the road itself to draw on him. Sera rode the mule — the mule was called Duchess, and Duchess had opinions — because her knees were bad and she claimed walking was for people with fewer important thoughts. And Edmund walked in the middle, the way he always walked, like a man trying to take up as little of the world as possible.

Pip watched him and thought, not for the first time, that Edmund Yarrow was the strangest person he had ever met, and Pip had once shared a fence post with a talking crow for a whole summer.

The strangeness wasn't the magiclessness. Plenty of people had a little magic and most people had none at all. The strangeness was that Edmund didn't *want* anything. Pip had spent his whole life around outlaws and hedge-witches and people running from things, and every single one of them wanted something so badly it leaked out of them like heat from a forge. But Edmund just... walked. Carried the map in its oilcloth wrap against his chest. Asked polite questions about road conditions. Thanked Sera for the stew, every time, even when the stew was mostly boots.

"It's like he's a door," Marlow had said once. "You know? Like he's just standing there being a doorway."

"Marlow," Pip had said, "that's the stupidest thing you've ever said, and you once told a magistrate the moon owed you money."

But privately Pip thought Marlow was right, and privately Pip thought that was why he liked Edmund, and privately Pip did not examine why that was, because examining it would lead to the thing Pip never examined, which was that Pip was the twin without the magic.

* * *

Here was the shape of the twins, in case you'd never met them, and if you hadn't, you were missing out, according to Pip.

Marlow had the gift. Not a big gift — he wasn't going to throw fire or call storms or anything useful like that. Marlow could *find* things. Lost things, hidden things, things that didn't want to be found. He'd close his eyes and hold something that belonged to the lost thing — a button, a lock of hair, a favorite spoon — and his face would go slack and dreamy and he'd say something useless like "it's near water" and then it would be, because it was always near water, because everything was near water if you squinted.

Still. It was a gift. It was *magic*. Marlow had been claimed by the world the moment he was born, and Pip had come out four minutes later as a closed book, a blank page, a person the magic looked straight through.

Sera said it wasn't a failing. Sera said half the hedge-witches she knew would trade their gifts for a good memory and steady hands. Sera said lots of things, because Sera was kind, but kindness isn't the same as the world agreeing with you, and the world had opinions about Pip. The world had said, in a hundred small ways, *you are the spare. You are the one who watches the horses while the real twin works.*

So Pip watched the horses. And sharpened things. And learned every card trick in three provinces, none of which worked on anyone with a drop of the gift, because a gift could feel the weighted deck, and Pip had never once won a bet against his own brother.

Until now. Until this company, where the most important person was a man with less magic than a fence post, and everyone's survival hung on him like a coat on a hook.

Pip found that he liked the view from this particular world.

* * *

The rain started at midday, which was a mercy, because it put out the smell a little and started the mud instead. Ash and water make a paste that gets into everything — boot seams, hair, the spaces between your toes. By late afternoon they all looked like they'd been dug up.

They made camp in the shell of a coaching inn. The roof was half gone, but the great hearth still stood, and hearths are stubborn things; this one still had a fire in it, banked down to coals, as though the family had stepped out and meant to come back. Nobody said anything about that. Odo checked the coals for hexes, found none, and they built the fire up.

Sera produced stew. It was, as established, mostly boots.

"So," Marlow said, hunched close to the flames, "the map."

Everyone went still. The map was not a casual topic. The map was the reason they were crossing a province that Veyra had burned on her way through it, the reason they were eating boot stew in a dead inn instead of sleeping safe somewhere with a roof and a lock.

"What about it?" Edmund said carefully.

"Does it ever talk to you?" Marlow leaned in. "Sera says maps like that have wants. I could try holding it. Just to see if I can find what it's finding. Two finders are better than one."

"No," Edmund said.

It came out fast. Faster than polite. Everyone looked at him, and Edmund looked down at the fire and turned his hands over, palms up, like a man showing he held nothing.

"It doesn't like being touched," he said. "It's not — it's not that I don't trust you, Marlow. It's that Beatrix carried this through half the continent and died getting it to me, and the one thing she made me swear —" He stopped. He hadn't sworn. They all knew he hadn't sworn. He'd refused the oath and taken the map anyway because it burned everyone else. "The one thing she asked," he amended quietly, "was that I carry it. Not pass it around. Not open it up. Carry it."

"Carry it where, though," Marlow said, not unkindly. "West, the map says. But west is big, Edmund. West has a lot of *west* in it."

"I know where it wants to go," Edmund said. "It pulls. I can feel it, like — like a hand on my sternum. It's been pulling the same direction since the valley. West and a little south. Toward the capital, Sera thinks. Toward the Ember."

"Then let me help aim," Marlow said. "That's all a finder does. I don't need to touch it. I just need you to walk toward it while I hold your button."

"My button?"

"Everyone has a thing. A token. Something that's *them*. Yours is a button off your coat, I think. It's the most you thing on you. The rest of your coat is just coat."

Pip watched Edmund's face do something complicated. The man looked, for a moment, like someone standing at the edge of cold water.

"All right," Edmund said finally. "But if it hurts the map, you stop."

"If it hurts the *map*," Sera said dryly, from her seat on a fallen beam, "I'll eat Duchess."

Duchess, tethered by the door, looked up with an expression of deep equine offense.

Marlow took the button — Edmund unpicked it himself, which Pip noticed he did without hesitation, and noticed further that this was the first time anyone in the company had seen Edmund hand over any piece of the map's business without a fight — and closed his eyes. His face went slack and dreamy. His fingers turned the button over and over.

"West," he murmured. "West and south. There's — huh."

"What?" Sera said sharply.

"There's something following the same line. Behind us. Far back. Like — like a thread pulled tight, pointing the same way we're pointing." Marlow's brow furrowed. "No. Not one thread. Several. Braided together. Moving fast."

Odo was already on his feet.

* * *

They broke camp in the dark, which is a special misery, and rode — all except Edmund, who had never learned, and Duchess, who expressed her views on Edmund's riding by every means available to a mule — through the rain and the ash-mud, west and south, with Marlow's thread of pursuit pulling tight behind them.

It was near dawn when the riders came.

Pip heard them first — he was the one with the ears; Marlow had the finding and Pip had the hearing, a twin-thing, a splitting of the labor — hooves on the old paved road behind them, many, moving at a canter, no attempt at stealth. That was the frightening part. Veyra's hunters moved quiet when they meant to kill you quick. Riders who didn't care if you heard them were riders who didn't think you could do anything about it.

"Off the road," Odo barked. "Trees. Now."

They crashed into the black forest as the first riders came into view on the road behind — six of them, maybe eight, in Veyra's grey-and-scarlet, and the lead rider had his hand raised and *glowing*, and Pip's whole body went cold, because he knew that glow. Everyone from the free territories knew that glow. A seeker-light. It would find them in the trees like a lamp finds moths, because the seeker-light found *magic*, and everyone in this company had magic except —

Except Edmund.

"Edmund," Pip said. It just came out. "Edmund, they can't see you."

Edmund looked at him across the dark, rain running off his chin.

"The light finds magic," Pip said, words tumbling, his heart hammering, his mind doing the fastest arithmetic of his life. "It's a seeker-light, it finds the gift, it'll light us up like festival lamps, all of us, but not you — it'll slide right off you, it'll think you're a rock, it'll think you're a *tree* — Edmund, get behind them, get *between* them and us —"

And Edmund Yarrow — who had spent his whole life being the thing the world looked through — understood.

What happened next, Pip would remember all his life, and would tell badly at a hundred fires, and eventually tell well at one.

Edmund stepped out of the trees onto the open road, into the rain, empty hands raised, and the seeker-light swept across him and found *nothing*. It slid over him like water over glass, and the lead rider's glow stuttered, confused, and in that stutter — that one heartbeat of a spell not knowing what it was looking at — Odo came out of the trees like a landslide, and Sera's hedge-magic turned the road under the horses' hooves to grease and soup, and the twins — the twins did what twins do, which was yell and throw things, specifically Marlow throwing a finder-spark to blind them and Pip throwing an actual rock, which hit an actual rider on the actual helmet with a *bong* that Pip would treasure forever.

It was over in two minutes. Three riders down in the mud, horses fled, two escaped back east at a gallop, and one — the lead one, the one with the light — sitting in the ruins of the road with Odo's sword at his throat, saying, "What *are* you?"

Edmund looked down at him, rain streaming off his face, and said, with complete sincerity, "I'm a gatekeeper."

* * *

They didn't kill him. Odo wanted to, at first — "He's seen our faces, he knows our line of march" — but Sera said no, and Sera's no had a weight to it, and besides, the rider was barely more than a boy, and he was crying, which takes the appetite out of any argument.

They took his horse and his seeker-light — the light was a crystal on a chain, and when Edmund held it, it went dark and dumb as a river stone, which made the boy stare at him like he was watching a man drink the sea — and they sent him walking east with his boots and his dignity, such as remained.

Then they rode west, faster, because two had escaped and word would travel.

And that evening, in the lee of a fallen wall, Pip found himself sitting next to Edmund, sharing a blanket that smelled of ash, and the conversation that happened then was the conversation Pip had been walking toward his whole life without knowing it.

"You saw it," Edmund said. "Before anyone. That the light couldn't find me."

"You saw it too."

"I've known it my whole life." Edmund turned the dead seeker-crystal over in his fingers. "I just never knew it was worth anything. In the valley, being magicless was just — being empty. A room nobody visited."

Pip pulled his knees up. The rain had stopped. The stars over Ashfall were the clean, hard stars you only get when the world below has been burned clean enough to show them.

"I'm the twin without the gift," Pip said.

Edmund looked at him.

"Marlow finds things. Real finding, real magic, since we were kids. And I'm — I'm the one who watches the horses." He laughed, and it came out wrong, so he tried again. "I used to think the world made a mistake with me. Like it was writing everyone else's names in ink and mine in pencil, and then it rained."

"And now?"

"And now I watched a seeker-light look straight at the most important man in the world and see a fence post." Pip nudged Edmund's shoulder. "And I thought: the world doesn't make mistakes. It just doesn't explain itself. Maybe the pencil people are the ones it's saving for something."

Edmund was quiet a long time. Then he said, "In the valley, I used to talk to the gate. Every night. Told it what the weather was doing. Told it if I'd seen any deer." He smiled at the fire — Sera had gotten a real fire going, small and stubborn. "I thought I was talking to a door. I think maybe I was talking to the only friend I had who couldn't tell me I was useless."

"You're not useless."

"I know that now." He looked over at Pip, and there was something in his face that Pip had never seen there before, something that had been locked behind the gate his whole life and had come out with him. "That's the strange part. I spent forty years being a wall, and it took one week of being a weapon to feel like a person."

"You're not a weapon either," Pip said. "You're the company. We're — " he gestured at the others, at Odo standing watch with his sword across his knees, at Sera feeding the fire, at Marlow asleep with his mouth open, at Duchess judging them all — "we're a thing now. All of us. A weird, muddy, boot-stew thing."

Edmund looked at them for a long moment.

"A company," he said, trying the word out.

"A family," Pip said, and then went red to the ears, because he was nineteen and said things like that, and Edmund smiled — really smiled, the first real one Pip had seen on him — and said nothing, which was the right answer, because Edmund was the kind of person who knew when a thing was too true to be said out loud.

* * *

Two days later, the road climbed out of Ashfall's burn and into green hills, and Marlow, walking point with his gift open the way he'd done since the skirmish, suddenly stopped and held up a hand.

"What?" Odo said, hand on his sword.

"Correnvale's a day ahead," Marlow said slowly. "The free city. We're close. But there's something on the road between us and it." His face had gone slack and strange, the finder-face, and his voice dropped. "Something dead. A lot of somethings. And one thing that isn't dead at all."

Pip's hand found the rock in his pocket — the lucky one, the helmet-bonger — and closed around it.

"Moving which way?" Odo asked.

Marlow turned west, toward the hills, toward the white walls of the last free city, and said:

"Toward us."
