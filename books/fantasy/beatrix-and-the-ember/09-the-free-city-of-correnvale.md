## 9. The Free City of Correnvale

Marlow had been counting the days since he'd last slept under a proper roof, and the number had gotten embarrassing.

"Two hundred and eleven," he announced to no one in particular, squinting down the last stretch of road where it spilled out of the hills toward a smudge of grey and gold on the horizon. "Give or take. Pip says two-oh-nine, but Pip also once told a customs officer I was his sister, so his arithmetic is suspect."

"You *were* wearing my coat," said Pip, from somewhere near the rear of their little column. "And you cried."

"I was *acting*."

Ahead of them, Edmund Yarrow walked with the careful, deliberate stride of a man who still didn't quite believe roads could go on forever. He'd been like that for three days now — ever since the skirmish with Veyra's riders, ever since the moment Marlow had watched him stand in the middle of a spell-storm with sparks breaking against him like rain on a windowpane, utterly untouched, looking mildly apologetic about the whole thing. Marlow liked him. He liked him the way you liked a locked box that turned out to be full of something impossible.

Sera walked beside Edmund, close enough that their shoulders bumped now and then, and every time they did, Marlow noticed, she found something fascinating to study in the middle distance. Odo brought up the rear with Rell's absence hanging off him like a wet cloak — the captain had let them go two nights past and vanished into the dark, and Odo hadn't spoken more than ten words since.

But all of that, Marlow decided, could wait. Because there it was.

Correnvale.

"Behold," he said, spreading his arms as though presenting a gift. "The last free city. Jewel of the western coast. Home of the finest pastry in the known world, which I will be proving to all of you within the hour."

"It's not the finest," said Pip. "It's the second-finest. The finest is in Darrowmere, and Darrowmere burned."

"Darrowmere's pastry died a hero, then. Correnvale's carries the torch."

Edmund looked between them with the expression of a man trying to work out whether this was an argument or a ritual. "You've been here before?"

"We grew up here," Marlow said, softer than he meant to. The city was growing larger as they descended — walls of pale granite furred with moss, rooftops stacked up the hillside like crockery in a careless cupboard, and above it all the great lantern-tower, its flame burning green-white, the old free-city fire that no queen's edict had ever put out. "Or near enough. Our mother sold fish on the Tidegate steps. We were born in the room above the chandlery on Rope Walk. It's smaller than you'd think."

"And louder," said Pip.

"And louder," Marlow agreed. He felt the grin pulling at his face and didn't fight it. "Come on. There's a gate guard named Brannic who owes me money, and there is no pleasure in this world so pure as collecting a debt from a man who thought you were dead."

* * *

Brannic did owe him money. This turned out not to matter, because Brannic took one look at Marlow's face under the hood, went grey as the granite, and waved the entire company through the Tidegate without asking a single question — which, Marlow reflected, was either very loyal or very sensible, and with Brannic it had always been impossible to say which.

Inside, the city hit him like a wave.

He'd forgotten the smell of it — salt and tar and frying onions, woodsmoke and wet stone and, underneath everything, the green-white tang of the lantern-flame that permeated Correnvale the way incense permeated a temple. He'd forgotten the noise: gulls screaming over the fish-market, cart wheels grinding cobbles, six languages arguing at once in the spice rows. He'd forgotten how *full* the world could be when it wasn't trying to kill you.

"Well?" Pip elbowed him. "Say it."

"It's exactly as I remembered it," Marlow lied, because his eyes were doing something inconvenient, and if he admitted the truth Pip would never let him hear the end of it.

The others were staring too. Edmund stood in the mouth of the Tidegate street with his head tipped back, watching a lamplighter charm her ladder to climb itself while she rode it like a slow, patient horse. Sera had gone very still near a flower-seller whose blooms were opening and closing in time to her humming. Even Odo's jaw had loosened a fraction.

"This much magic," Edmund said quietly. "Just... lying around. In the open. Nobody hiding it."

"That's what free means here," Marlow said. "Veyra's writ runs everywhere else on the continent. Her mages tax every hedge-charm, license every blessing, hang anyone caught teaching unlicensed cantrips. Correnvale told her no. Twenty years ago. She's been trying to make us regret it ever since."

"Has she succeeded?"

Marlow gestured grandly at the street, the market, the lamplighter, the whole improbable, stubborn, glorious mess of it. "What do you think?"

What Marlow didn't say — what he kept folded up small behind his ribs, where he kept most things that mattered — was that the city was thinner than it used to be. That there were gaps in the rooftop lines where buildings had burned and not been rebuilt. That the market stalls numbered perhaps half of what he remembered, and that the beggars on the Tidegate steps wore the armbands of soldiers' widows. That the lantern-flame in the tower burned green-white but *low*, lower than he'd ever seen it, like a hearth banked for a long cold night.

Pip saw it too. Marlow caught his twin's eye across the crowd, and Pip's grin faded by a degree, and neither of them said anything, because saying it would make it true in front of the others.

They took rooms at the Gilded Eel, which was neither gilded nor, in any meaningful sense, eel-related, but which had clean straw, honest ale, and a landlady who owed Marlow a different kind of debt entirely — the sort you couldn't pay in coin. She took one look at the six of them, said "Back room, no questions, supper at seven," and vanished upstairs, and Marlow loved her extravagantly.

Supper was stew, and for one hour the company was almost happy. Pip taught Edmund a dice game designed to separate strangers from their money; Edmund lost four coppers with the grave dignity of a man being initiated into something sacred. Sera laughed — actually laughed, head back, hand on Edmund's arm — and Marlow filed that away with great care, the way he filed away everything.

Then he set down his spoon and said, "Right. Council chambers at dawn. Everyone get some sleep, because tomorrow we go and argue with the seven most infuriating people on this coast."

* * *

The Council of Correnvale met in the Lantern Hall, a vaulted chamber at the tower's base where the light of the free flame came down through colored glass and laid pools of green and white and gold across a long horseshoe table. Seven councilors sat in the curve of it, robed in the city colors, and Marlow knew five of them by name and three of them by reputation, and none of those reputations were flattering.

Speaker Halvard sat at the center — a broad, silver-bearded man with the patient face of someone who had spent twenty years saying *not yet* to people who couldn't afford to wait. To his left, Mistress Corvel, who ran the harbors and had the eyes of a woman calculating tonnage even while you spoke to her. To his right, young Lord Aspern, whose family had bought their seat three generations ago and who wore his silk like armor.

Marlow had rehearsed this. He'd rehearsed it on the road, in the bathhouse, in his sleep. He stepped into the pool of green light at the chamber's center, bowed precisely as deep as courtesy required and not a hair deeper, and began.

"Honored Speaker. Honored councilors. You know me — Marlow of Rope Walk, formerly of this parish, currently of nowhere in particular. My brother and I left this city six years ago, and we've come home carrying something worth more than the harbor tolls." He let the pause sit. "We've come carrying the location of the Ember."

The word landed the way it always landed — like a stone dropped into a well. He heard the breath go out of the chamber. Somewhere in the gallery above, a clerk stopped writing.

"The Ember," repeated Halvard slowly, "which every scholar in the world believes is a legend."

"The Ember," Marlow agreed, "which binds King Aldric to Veyra's will. Which is the reason the king has signed death-orders in his own hand for six years. Which is the reason your lantern burns low, honored Speaker, because the free flame draws its strength from the old royal covenant, and the covenant is being strangled in the capital even as we speak. We know where it is. We have a map. And we have—" he gestured behind him, at the strange, tall, quiet man standing among his family, "—the only person alive who can carry it."

Every eye in the chamber went to Edmund. Marlow watched them take him in — the plain clothes, the road-worn boots, the total absence of anything remarkable — and he watched the exact moment the skepticism curdled.

Lord Aspern leaned forward. "This is the famous carrier? Forgive me, Master...?"

"Yarrow," said Edmund.

"Master Yarrow. And what are you, exactly? A mage?"

"No."

"A scholar of the binding arts?"

"No."

"A soldier? A priest? A—"

"He's immune," Sera said, stepping forward, her voice clipped. "To magic. All of it. Spells slide off him. The Ember can't burn him. There isn't another soul on this continent who can touch that thing and live, and he's standing right there, and you're quibbling about his credentials."

"Credentials," said Mistress Corvel, "are how we know a thing is real rather than convenient. No offense intended to your companion. But you must understand our position." She spread her hands, and Marlow recognized the gesture — he'd seen merchants use it a hundred times, the open palms of a person about to charge you for the air. "You bring us a legend, a map none of us may see, and a man whose only qualification is a claim we cannot test. And you ask this council to do what, precisely?"

"To act," Marlow said. "Ships. Soldiers. A fleet to threaten the capital's harbor while we go under it. That's all. That's *everything*. The Ember sits in the throne vault beneath the palace. We can't crack a royal vault with six people and good intentions. But with Correnvale's navy making noise on the water, Veyra has to look seaward — and we walk in through the floor."

Halvard steepled his fingers. For a long moment the only sound was the gulls outside and the faint, sourceless hum of the lantern-flame overhead.

"Master Marlow," the Speaker said at last, gently, in the voice of a man about to disappoint a child. "Do you know how many times, in twenty years, someone has come before this council claiming knowledge of the Ember? Claiming a way to break the sorcerer-queen's power? Claiming the king lives, the king is dead, the king is freed, the king is a myth?" He counted on thick fingers. "Fourteen times. Fourteen. Two of them were spies. Three were mad. One was very nearly convincing enough that we sailed, and nine hundred of our people died on the rocks off Carrow Point for it. I signed those orders. I read the lists of names afterward. All of them."

The chamber was very quiet.

"So you will forgive us," Halvard continued, "if the price of our help is not a story, however sincerely told. Bring us proof the king lives. Proof the Ember is real, proof Aldric still draws breath beneath Veyra's crown — real proof, verifiable proof — and this council will empty its arsenals for you. Until then..." He spread those same broad hands. "We are sorry. We are truly sorry. But Correnvale survives by refusing to bet its life on hope."

"We're not asking you to bet on hope," Pip burst out from the side of the chamber. "We're telling you we *walked* here. From the ashfall provinces. Through her riders, through her hunters. Beatrix Ash died getting that map out of the valley—"

"Beatrix Ash," said Corvel softly, "is a name on a bounty list. Dead women make poor witnesses."

Marlow saw it happen — saw the fury climb up his brother's neck and into his face, saw Pip's hands curl — and he moved without thinking, catching Pip's shoulder, holding him steady the way you hold a mast in weather. "Easy," he murmured. "Easy. Not here."

But inside, Marlow discovered, he wasn't calm at all. Inside, some young part of him that had grown up on these stones, that had believed in the lantern-tower the way other children believed in gods, was screaming. *Nine hundred names.* Nine hundred names, and the council had learned nothing except caution, and caution was just another word for letting Veyra win slowly instead of quickly.

"Then we'll find your proof," he said aloud, and was surprised by how level his own voice came out. "How long do we have?"

Halvard exchanged a glance with Corvel. Whatever passed between them, it ended with the Speaker's small, tired nod.

"The autumn tides," Corvel said. "In six weeks, the fleet must sail or stay moored until spring. Bring your proof before the equinox council, and we march — sail — whatever verb you prefer. Miss it, and..." She shrugged, elegant and terrible. "Spring is a long way off. For all of us."

* * *

They came out of the Lantern Hall into grey afternoon light, and nobody spoke for the length of the square.

It was Edmund, of all people, who finally broke the silence. "Six weeks," he said. "Is that enough time?"

"For what?" asked Odo, and it was the first thing he'd said since the gate.

"For finding proof that a prisoner-king exists, in a country run by the woman keeping him prisoner." Edmund rubbed the back of his neck. "I guarded a gate for thirty years. I know what sealed doors are like. They don't open because you're in a hurry."

Marlow barked a laugh that surprised even himself. "Oh, I like him. Pip, we're keeping him."

"Already decided that," said Pip, but his heart wasn't in it. He was looking back at the Lantern Hall, at the colored glass glowing green and white above the square. "Nine hundred names," he said quietly. "That's what they remember. Not the lie Veyra told, not the war she started. Just the cost. That's what fear does — it does sums."

"Fear does sums," Marlow agreed. "And love spends recklessly. Between the two of them, we need to find a ledger the council will actually read."

He clapped his hands, briskly, because somebody had to, and because if he stopped moving he'd start thinking about the low-burning lantern and the widows' armbands and the room above the chandlery that was probably a stranger's storeroom now.

"Right. Six weeks. We have a city full of smugglers, spies, and sailors, and between the three trades, someone in Correnvale knows something about the capital that the council doesn't. Pip — the dockside taverns, you're better at getting men drunk and talkative than I am, don't argue. Sera — the hedge-witch networks, the unlicensed charmsellers; if anyone's heard whispers out of the palace, it's the people Veyra's licenses have pushed underground. Odo—" Marlow paused, and gentled his voice without meaning to. "Odo. You served the king. If anyone knows what proof looks like — what would convince even a frightened council — it's you."

Odo stared at the cobblestones for a long moment. When he raised his head, there was something new in his face — something that might, in better light, have been mistaken for purpose.

"There's a cipher," he said. "The king's private cipher. Only his household guard ever learned it. If Aldric is alive — if any part of him is still fighting — then somewhere in the machinery of Veyra's court, there will be paper. Orders. Writs. Things that pass through his hands." His jaw tightened. "And if he's signing them himself..."

"Then the handwriting is the proof," Sera finished.

"Then the handwriting is the proof," Odo agreed.

Marlow grinned, sharp and bright, and threw an arm around Edmund's shoulders — the carrier, the null, the ordinary man at the center of everything. "You hear that, Yarrow? Six weeks, one cipher, and a stolen letter. Practically a holiday."

Edmund gave him the look of a man who had guarded a lonely gate for thirty years and had thought he'd left difficult things behind him. "I miss the valley already," he said.

But he was smiling. Just slightly. Just at the corner.

And high above them, in the lantern-tower, the free flame of Correnvale guttered once in the wind — and held.

* * *

That night, Marlow couldn't sleep.

He climbed to the flat roof of the Gilded Eel with a bottle of the landlady's plum brandy and found Pip already there, knees drawn up, watching the lantern-tower burn against the stars. Marlow sat down beside him without asking, the way he'd done ten thousand times on ten thousand rooftops, and passed the bottle over.

"Low," Pip said. "The flame. Did you see?"

"I saw."

"When we were kids, it burned so bright you could read by it from the harbor. Mother said it was the city's heart." Pip drank, and handed the bottle back. "Hearts aren't supposed to dim, Marlow."

Marlow looked out at the tower — at the pale, struggling light, at the dark windows of the city around it, at the sea beyond the walls where Veyra's blockade ships sat like patient sharks, visible on clear nights as a scatter of hostile lanterns. Six weeks. One cipher. A stolen letter. And somewhere out there, Captain Rell, hunting them, and somewhere beyond that, a throne vault with a living flame and a king who might already be nothing but a hollow suit of skin.

"No," Marlow said. "But ours is still beating. That's more than most cities can say."

Below them, in the back room of the Gilded Eel, he could just make out the murmur of voices — Sera and Edmund, talking low, the sound rising through the window like smoke. Odo's deeper tones answering. A family, assembled by accident and grief, sleeping under one roof for the first time.

Marlow took a long pull of brandy and made a promise to the tower, to the city, to the room above the chandlery, to the mother who'd sold fish on the Tidegate steps and taught her sons that free things were worth bleeding for.

*Six weeks,* he promised. *We'll bring them their proof. And then we'll burn her machinery down to the waterline.*

Down in the square, a night-watchman's whistle sounded twice — the all's-well signal — and then, a heartbeat later, a third blast. Sharp. Wrong.

Marlow was on his feet before his mind caught up with his body, bottle rolling forgotten across the roof tiles. Below, at the corner of the square, a figure in a watchman's cloak was pointing up at the Gilded Eel — and beside him, moving through the shadows with the unhurried confidence of a man who owned every street he walked, came someone wearing the grey-and-silver of the Correnvale city guard.

No. Not the city guard. The colors were right, but the bearing was wrong — too straight, too silent, too *palace*-bred.

"Odo," Marlow breathed, already moving for the roof door. "Wake up. We have a problem."
