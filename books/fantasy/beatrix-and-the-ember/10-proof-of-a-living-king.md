## 10. Proof of a Living King

Odo had spent eleven years learning to read the king's hand, and three years since learning to read it in his sleep.

That was the trouble with serving in the royal guard. You didn't just protect a man; you absorbed him. You learned the way he tapped two fingers against his knee when a petition bored him, the way he said "hmm" when he meant no and "interesting" when he meant *absolutely not*, and — because someone had to carry the sealed orders from the king's private study to the chancellery, and because Odo had been that someone for most of his final year of service — you learned his cipher.

Not the official court cipher, which was a pretty thing of interlocking roses that any second-year clerk could unpick. The private one. The one Aldric had invented himself, badly, at nineteen, and never improved because he was a king and kings are never told their inventions are bad. A cipher built on the names of his father's hunting dogs. Bramble, Sorrel, Fern, Ash. Four dogs, four substitution wheels, rotated by the date and by how much wine the king had drunk the night he wrote the letter, which was information only a man who had poured that wine would have.

Odo had poured a great deal of that wine.

So when Marlow came skidding into the inn's back room in Correnvale, breathless, holding a leather dispatch tube above his head like a fisherman displaying a prize catch, Odo felt something cold settle in his stomach before the boy even spoke.

"Riders from the capital," Marlow panted. "Well — riders who *were* from the capital. Well — riders whose satchels we were briefly from the capital. Pip did the tripping. I did the lifting. Teamwork."

"You robbed a courier," Sera said flatly.

"We *intercepted* a courier," Marlow corrected. "There's a difference. The difference is mostly about who's telling the story afterward."

The tube was royal issue — grey leather, the seal unbroken, wax stamped with the crowned tower of House Aldric's chancellery. Odo turned it over in his hands and felt the weight of it, the way you feel the weight of a door you're not sure you want to open.

"It'll be written in the court cipher," Sera said. "Pip can't read it, I can't read it—"

"I can," Odo said.

The room went quiet. Edmund looked up from where he sat sharpening a knife he clearly didn't know how to sharpen, doing it the way he did everything, with a kind of stubborn patience that made Odo want to both laugh and weep. Even now, three weeks out from the valley, the man still moved like someone waiting to be told the rules.

"You can read it?" Sera asked carefully.

"The court cipher, yes. Anyone in the guard could." Odo set the tube on the table between them. "But if this is what I think it is — a field order, routed through the chancellery under Veyra's seal of countersignature — then the outer layer will be nothing. Rations, patrol rotations, something dull enough to make a thief yawn. The real order will be inside it, in the king's own hand, in the king's own cipher. Because Veyra is many things, but she is not careless. She makes Aldric write the orders that matter himself. It looks better. It *is* him signing them. And no one outside these walls—" he tapped the tube "—can read them."

"And you can," Edmund said. Not a question.

"I served him for nine years," Odo said. "I was his wine-pourer before I was his shield, and his shield before I was his disgrace. He taught me cards on the theory that a guard who plays cards with you won't sell you out, because he knows too much about how you cheat." He almost smiled. "He cheats at cards. Badly. He thinks nobody notices."

* * *

They broke the seal over Sera's objections that they should wait, and over Marlow's objections that breaking seals was half the fun and therefore should be savored, and over Pip's objection, delivered from the doorway with a strip of dried apple in his teeth, that everyone was talking too much and the wax was right there.

Inside: two documents.

The outer one was exactly as dull as promised. Grain requisitions for the Third Legion. Odo read it aloud in a monotone until Pip began quietly dying of boredom against the doorframe, then set it aside.

The inner document was smaller. Folded twice. Sealed with plain black wax, no stamp at all, which was itself a message — the king's private correspondence never bore the royal arms, because the royal arms were for the kingdom, and this channel was for the man.

Odo held it for a long moment without opening it.

"You don't have to," Sera said quietly. She'd come around the table to stand near him, the way she did, close enough that her sleeve brushed his arm. "Whatever's in there."

"Yes, I do," Odo said. "That's rather the whole point of me."

He unfolded it.

The handwriting hit him first, before the cipher, before anything. He knew that hand. He'd watched it sign death warrants and marriage contracts and, once, memorably, a pardon for a goose that had bitten a visiting ambassador. A loose, confident scrawl, letters leaning forward like a man walking briskly toward something.

Except it wasn't leaning forward anymore. The letters stood stiff and upright, evenly spaced, each one placed with a precision Aldric had never possessed in his life. It was his handwriting the way a portrait is a face — accurate and utterly lifeless.

"He didn't write this," Odo heard himself say.

"You just said—"

"His *hand* wrote it." Odo's mouth had gone dry. "Look at the spacing. Look at the — there's no rhythm. Aldric writes like he talks, all hurry and corners. This is..." He searched for the word and hated it when he found it. "This is dictation. The body writing what the will is told to write."

Sera laid her fingers lightly on the page, and her eyes went distant and grey, the way they did when she listened to things other people couldn't hear. "There's a residue," she murmured. "Compulsion-work. Old, deep-set, renewed regularly. It's in the strokes themselves. Whoever held him — held his mind while his hand moved." She looked up at Odo, and her expression was gentle and terrible at once. "It's him, Odo. It's really him. That's what makes it awful."

Odo nodded once, because if he opened his mouth he was going to say something unforgivable, and decoded instead.

Bramble for the consonant wheel, Sorrel for vowels, rotation keyed to the third of the month — the day after the new moon, when the king always drank less, because the new moon was the anniversary of his father's death and he kept vigil sober. Odo had poured watered wine on those nights and pretended not to notice the king pretending not to grieve.

The words came up through the wheels like bodies rising through dark water:

*To Captain Vess of the Third Legion. The free city of Correnvale is to be admitted to the peace of the crown by whatever means its council finds persuasive. Should the council prove obstinate, the granaries at Millbrook are forfeit. Let it be understood that mercy remains an option for those cities that open their gates, and that the example of those that do not will instruct the rest.*

*By the hand of the King.*

*Under my seal and my will,* — and here, crammed small beneath the signature, in letters so tiny they might have been an accident, a flaw in the parchment, a scratch —

*I am sorry. I am trying. Do not believe the Binding Day parade. F.A.*

Odo read it twice. Then a third time. Then he became aware that his hands were shaking, and set the paper down very carefully on the table, the way you set down something that is either a lamp or a bomb.

"Well?" Pip said, from the doorway, apple forgotten.

"It's him," Odo said. His voice came out rough. "It's Aldric. The order is his — Veyra compels his hand, but the words of command are hers wearing his authority. And underneath—" He stopped. Started again. "Underneath the signature, hidden, he wrote a message. Four words and initials. *I am sorry. I am trying.*"

Sera closed her eyes. Marlow sat down slowly, as though his legs had been unplugged.

"He's alive in there," Marlow said. "He's actually — he's *in there*, fighting."

"Fighting with a quill," Odo said bitterly. "Four smuggled words a month, if the moon phases cooperate. That's his rebellion. That's all he has left."

Edmund had picked up the outer requisition and was frowning at it. "Millbrook," he said. "Where's Millbrook?"

"Grain town, south of here. Feeds maybe ten thousand people through the winter," Sera said.

"And if Correnvale's council doesn't do what this letter says, the king's own hand sentences them to starve." Edmund set it down. He had a way of stating horrible things plainly, without decoration, that Odo was beginning to understand was not coldness but a kind of mercy — the mercy of not making people guess. "So this cuts both ways. It proves the king lives. It also proves the king is the weapon."

Silence took the room and held it.

It was Pip, of all people, who broke it. He pushed off the doorframe, crossed the room, and looked down at the letter with an expression Odo hadn't seen on him before — none of the mischief, none of the twin-showmanship. Just a young man looking at evidence.

"So we show this to the council," Pip said, "and they stop arguing about whether the king lives, and start arguing about what to do about it. Yes?"

"That's the hope," Sera said.

"And some of them will say the hidden words are forged," Odo added. "Because that's what councils do. They'll accept the order — the order serves their fear, confirms that resistance means starvation — and they'll dismiss the plea as our invention. I know how councils read. They read for what lets them keep sitting comfortably."

"Then we show them the compulsion-residue," Sera said. "I can demonstrate it. Any hedge-witch worth her salt can show them the binding in the strokes."

"And any witch in Veyra's pay can fake it," Odo said. "Which they'll also know."

Marlow groaned and put his head on the table. "So we have proof that proves everything and therefore proves nothing. Wonderful. Is this what politics is? Because I hate it. I would like to return to simple crimes."

* * *

They argued until the candles burned down to puddles.

It went the way Odo had known it would go, because he had sat through a hundred councils in another life and recognized the choreography even when the dancers were his friends. Sera argued for showing everything — the order, the plea, the residue — because truth was truth and half-truths rotted. Marlow argued for showing only the plea, because hope moved councils faster than fear. Pip argued, surprisingly, for showing only the order, because "scared councils do what you tell them, hopeful councils ask questions."

And Edmund — Edmund sat apart from it, turning the black-sealed paper over and over in his magicless hands, and finally said the thing that ended the argument, or at least wounded it mortally.

"Why does it matter what we show them," he said, "if the letter is true? Both halves. The cruelty and the plea. He really is ordering Millbrook starved and he really is sorry. We're not choosing which half to believe. We're choosing which half to *use*." He looked up, and there was no accusation in his face, which somehow made it worse. "I guarded a gate for thirty years because lying to myself about why the gate existed seemed easier than knowing. I'm not saying anyone here is lying. I'm saying I've seen what choosing your evidence does to a person."

Nobody answered that. There wasn't anything to answer it *with*.

In the end they agreed — tiredly, unhappily — to show the whole document to Councilor Vane, the one member of Correnvale's council whom Beatrix's old network had ever trusted, and let him decide how to play it. Which meant waiting for a meeting that wouldn't happen for two days, which meant two days of Marlow teaching Pip a card game involving cheating, which meant Odo standing watch on the inn's roof at hours when no watch was needed, because his hands remembered things his mind wanted to forget.

It was on the second night that Sera found him there.

She climbed the ladder with two cups of something hot and sat beside him without asking, which was one of the thousand things he loved about her and would admit to precisely no one.

"You poured his wine," she said.

"For six years." Odo wrapped his hands around the cup. Below them, Correnvale glowed with its defiant lamps, the last free light on a darkening map. "You know what the worst part is? Not the compulsion. Compulsion is horror, but it's a horror I can aim at Veyra. The worst part is that the plea changes nothing and I *know* it changes nothing, and I love him for writing it anyway. Four words. *I am trying.* Nine years I served that man, and I deserted him, and he's the one apologizing to *me*."

"You didn't desert him," Sera said. "You refused Veyra. There's a difference, and he knows it, and that's probably why he wrote it."

"He wrote it to whoever opened the letter. Could have been anyone."

"No," Sera said softly. "He wrote it in a cipher only you could read. He wrote it for you, Odo. Or for someone exactly like you. He's been leaving messages in bottles for years, hoping one of them washes up in the hands of someone who loved him enough to break the code." She leaned her head briefly against his shoulder. "You washed up."

Odo looked out at the city, at the lamps, at the granary-road running south toward Millbrook and ten thousand winter mouths, and felt the full weight of what loyalty meant when it was tested by evidence instead of swords. Evidence could be argued with. Swords, at least, only cut one way.

"If the council uses the order and ignores the plea," he said, "they'll surrender. Open the gates. Buy peace with Millbrook's grain and call it wisdom."

"And if they use the plea and ignore the order?"

"Then they'll fight, and lose, and die believing the king would thank them." He exhaled slowly. "And if they use both — if they understand that the man and the weapon are the same body and must be saved together — then they'll need proof we don't have yet. Proof beyond ink. Proof you can put in front of a frightened room."

"Like what?"

Odo was quiet for a long moment. When he answered, he heard his own voice go flat and certain, the voice he'd used in the guard barracks at night, planning things that couldn't be planned in daylight.

"Like the king himself. Alive. In front of witnesses. Before Binding Day."

Sera turned to look at him. "That's madness."

"Yes," Odo agreed. "It's also the only thing on this continent that counts as proof. Everything else is ink, and ink is cheap." He drained his cup. "Two days. Vane sees the letter tomorrow. And if he's the man Beatrix thought he was, by tomorrow night he'll be asking us the question we can't answer — *how do you know the plea isn't the forgery?* — and the only honest answer will be the mad one."

Below them, a bell began to ring in one of Correnvale's watchtowers. Once. Twice. A third time — and then a pattern, quick and urgent, that Odo didn't recognize but Sera clearly did, because she was already on her feet.

"What is it?" Odo demanded.

"Fire signal," she said. "From the south road." Her face had gone pale in the lamplight spilling up from below. "Odo — the south road runs past Millbrook."

They looked at each other, and neither of them said the obvious thing, because saying it would make it true: somewhere down that dark road, the king's compelled hand had already signed its next order, and the granaries were burning.
