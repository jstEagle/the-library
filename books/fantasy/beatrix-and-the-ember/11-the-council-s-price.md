## 11. The Council's Price

Sera had never trusted a room with good acoustics. In her experience, the places where every word carried clearly were the same places where every word was remembered, weighed, and later used against you. The council chamber of Correnvale was all pale stone and high arched ceilings, built — she suspected deliberately — so that a whisper at one end could be heard at the other. It smelled of cedar oil and old decisions.

She sat at the long table with her hands folded like a woman who had come to negotiate, which was true, and like a woman who intended to be reasonable, which was a lie of posture only.

Around her: Odo, arms crossed, wearing his old guard's bearing like a coat that no longer fit but still kept off the rain. Rell beside him, still strange and new among them, sitting very straight as though the chair might test him. Pip and Marlow flanking Edmund, the twins having silently appointed themselves his honor guard sometime around the salt flats and never rescinded the commission. And Edmund himself, at Sera's left, watching the nine councillors of the last free city with the patient, slightly baffled expression he wore whenever magic or politics happened near him — which, lately, was always.

At the head of the table, First Speaker Ysolde Vane folded her thin hands atop a ledger thick enough to stop an arrow.

"Let us review," Ysolde said, in the tone of a woman who had reviewed already and found everyone wanting. "You arrive in our city uninvited. You bring with you a dead courier's map to an artifact whose existence is officially a rumor. You claim the King of Aldermere lives, bound by sorcery, and that a man who cannot so much as light a candle with a charm—" here her gaze flicked to Edmund, not unkindly, but not warmly either, "—is the only person alive who can carry it out of the vault where it is kept."

"That's the shape of it," Odo said.

"And you want our ships. Our soldiers. Our grain, our surgeons, and, I note from your earlier requests, Master Marlow, our pastries."

"They were very good pastries," Marlow said, without a shred of shame. "The honey ones. I stand by the request."

A ripple went through the councillors — not laughter exactly, but the memory of it, quickly suppressed. Sera felt something in her chest loosen half a notch. Good. A room that remembered how to laugh was a room that could still be moved.

Ysolde did not laugh. "Correnvale has survived three years of Queen Veyra's embargo by being careful. Careful people do not hand their navy to six strangers and a map."

"Seven," Pip said. "You're forgetting Rell."

"I am attempting to forget Captain Rell," Ysolde said dryly, "on the grounds that he was, until quite recently, hunting you on Her Majesty's orders."

"Hunting them badly," Rell said. "If it helps."

"It does not help."

* * *

They had been at it for two days. Two days of committees, sub-committees, a man whose entire office seemed to concern the tariff on river eels, and a lunch on the second day during which Sera had watched four separate councillors pretend not to recognize Odo and then ask him, in strictest confidence, whether the king truly still wrote his own letters.

Now, on the third morning, Ysolde set down her quill and looked at each of them in turn, and Sera recognized the look. It was the look of a card player about to turn over the card she'd been sitting on since the deal.

"You want proof we are serious," Ysolde said. "Very well. Here is what Correnvale can offer. Twelve ships, refitted and armed. Four thousand soldiers — not our whole host, but enough to make the free cities believe, when word spreads, that resistance is possible again. Surgeons, siege engineers, grain for a winter campaign." She paused. "In exchange, you will retrieve the Ember first. Bring it to us. We will hold it as surety until the campaign is won, and then decide its fate by council vote."

Sera had expected this. She'd spent the previous night lying awake in their rented rooms above a chandler's shop, listening to the harbor bells, turning the negotiation over in her mind the way she turned herbs in a mortar, looking for the poison in the mixture. She'd found it. She'd hoped she was wrong.

"And how," Sera asked quietly, "do you propose we get into the throne vault of the most heavily warded palace on the continent? Because I've seen the wards, Speaker. They eat hedge-witches for breakfast and pick their teeth with the bones."

Ysolde's eyes came to rest on Edmund.

"We have been studying the problem," she said. "We have records, Lady Sera. Reports from Veyra's own hunters, bought at considerable cost over the years. Every account agrees on one point: wherever the null-born man goes, spells fail. Wards gutter. Tracking charms go dark. He is, functionally speaking, a hole in the world's magic." She folded her hands. "Veyra's hunters track him precisely because they cannot track anything else about your company. Which means, you see, that he is also the one thing in the world they will always follow."

The chamber went very quiet. Even the eel-tariff man stopped shuffling his papers.

"What the speaker is saying," said a councillor named Brannoc, a broad man with a merchant's rings on every finger, "is that your friend is a beacon. Light him up, point him at the capital, and every hunter Veyra has will come running — away from our ships, away from our coastal defenses, straight into ground of your choosing. He draws the sting. You take the city."

"He's not a *lamp*," Pip said sharply.

"No," Brannoc agreed. "He's better than a lamp. Lamps burn out."

Sera heard Edmund breathe once, slow and even, the way he breathed when he was holding something down. She had learned his silences across a hundred miles of road — the silence of a man thinking, the silence of a man hurt, the silence of a man deciding how much of either to show. This one had both in it.

"The plan," Ysolde continued, merciless and reasonable, "is sound. While the hunters chase your friend through the capital's lower quarters, our forces land at the harbor. The vault opens. The Ember comes out. And the man who carries it walks untouched through wards that would kill any of the rest of you, because there is nothing in him for the magic to grip." She spread her hands. "It is, frankly, the only plan with a chance of working. His nature is not a tragedy here, Lady Sera. It is the key."

"There's just the small matter," Sera said, "of asking him."

Ysolde blinked, genuinely puzzled, and Sera understood with a cold little lurch that the question had never occurred to the woman. To the council, Edmund was a resource. A fact of the war, like tide tables. You did not consult the tide.

"He is standing right there," Ysolde said. "I assumed he was listening."

"Oh, he's listening," Marlow said. His voice was pleasant. His eyes were not. "We all are. That's sort of the problem."

* * *

The council recessed — "to deliberate," though Sera suspected it was mostly to let the eel man fetch more paper — and the seven of them were shown into a side chamber with a window overlooking the harbor. Twelve ships rode at anchor out there, grey and patient. Four thousand soldiers drilled on the far quay, tiny figures moving like ants with excellent posture.

Nobody spoke for a while.

Pip broke first, because Pip always broke first. "They can't just— he's not *property*. He's not a— a diversionary goose."

"A diversionary goose," Marlow repeated thoughtfully. "There's a festival dish in that somewhere."

"This isn't funny, Marlow."

"It's a little funny. It'll be funnier later, when we're less angry. That's usually how it works."

Odo hadn't moved from the window. When he finally spoke, his voice had the flat weight he reserved for things he'd decided before saying them. "It's a good plan."

"*Odo.*"

"It's a good plan," he repeated, turning. "Twelve ships. Four thousand men. A real chance to end this before winter, before Veyra finds another way into the free cities, before she stops sending hunters and starts sending armies. I served the crown for nineteen years. I know what it costs to wait for perfect conditions. They never come. You take the ugly option that saves the most people." He looked at Edmund. "And I know what it costs to be used by institutions. Believe me. But I'd rather be used toward the right end than preserved toward nothing."

"Spoken like a man who wouldn't be the bait," Pip muttered.

"No," Odo said simply. "Spoken like a man who has been the bait. Twice. It's survivable, Pip. What isn't survivable is losing the war while we argue about dignity."

Rell shifted against the wall. "For what it's worth — and I know what it's worth, which is little — the hunters assigned to the null-signature report directly to Veyra. If they converge on him, she'll know within hours. She may come herself." He paused. "That's either the worst part of the plan or the best part, depending on how brave we're feeling."

"Depending on how lucky we're feeling," Sera corrected. "Bravery is just luck you've agreed to date."

Edmund laughed — a short, startled huff — and then pressed his lips together, because laughing in the middle of this felt wrong and he was a man who tried very hard to do right-feeling things. Sera's heart did something complicated and inconvenient, the way it had been doing for weeks now, whenever he laughed or didn't, whenever he stood too quietly at the edge of their firelight like a man still not convinced the fire was for him.

"Say what you actually think," she told him. "All of you keep talking around him like he's made of glass. He guarded a gate alone for thirty years; he can survive a conversation."

Edmund looked at her. There was something in his face she couldn't read yet, some door half-open, and it frightened her more than the council's arithmetic had.

"I think," Edmund said slowly, "that Odo's right. About the plan. I think twelve ships and four thousand soldiers is worth more than my comfort, and I'd be ashamed to trade the free cities for a quiet conscience." He paused. "I think I'd have agreed, if anyone had asked me before they started dividing me up like the last pork pie at a fair."

"Edmund—" Sera began.

"No, let me finish, because I've been practicing this in my head since the salt flats and I might lose my nerve." His voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "Back home, in the valley, nobody needed me for anything except walking the wall twice a day. Then Beatrix died on my floor and handed me a map, and suddenly I mattered, and I told myself that was fine, that mattering was just a thing that happened to other people and now it was happening to me. But there's a difference," he said, "between being chosen and being *spent*. And I've been listening to this council for three days, and to all of you just now, and every single one of you has been very careful to explain why using me is necessary, and not one of you — not one — asked whether I'd mind."

Silence. Out in the harbor, a bell rang the hour, indifferent and clear.

"We voted on the ford crossing," Pip said miserably. "We voted on everything else."

"You voted on things that concerned all of us. This concerns me like a knife concerns bread." Edmund shook his head. "I'm not saying no. I'm saying I notice. That's all. I notice, and I wanted you to know that I noticed."

He said it without heat, without accusation, and that was the worst of it — because Sera knew Edmund, knew that his anger came slow and deep as groundwater, and knew that this quiet noticing was the first crack of it. The first fracture. She had watched found families break before, back in her hedge-witch years, and it never broke along the lines of betrayal. It broke along the lines of *unspoken*, silence accreting in the joints until the whole thing came apart in someone's hands.

She would not let that happen. Not over this. Not over him.

"Then let's fix it properly," she said aloud, and stood. "Right now. Before the council comes back. We vote — all seven of us, equal voices, and Edmund's counts double, because it's his neck."

"That's not how votes work," Marlow said.

"It's how ours works. I just invented it. Hedge-witch privilege." She looked around the room. "All in favor of taking the council's bargain, with Edmund's consent given freely and his conditions heard first?"

One by one, reluctantly, hands rose — Odo first, then Rell, then Marlow, then Pip, whose hand came up like it was being pulled by fishhooks. Sera raised her own last.

Everyone looked at Edmund.

He was quiet a long moment. Then: "My conditions. One — nobody uses me as bait without telling me the shape of the trap beforehand. All of it. No convenient omissions for my protection; I've had enough protecting to last a lifetime. Two — if it goes wrong, you leave me. Swear it. I'm the one thing in the world their magic can't touch; I'm also the hardest person in the world to rescue. Don't die trying. Three—" and here his voice caught, just slightly, and Sera saw him steady it, "—three, somebody tells me the truth even when the truth is ugly. That's the price. That's all of it."

"Agreed," Odo said at once.

"Agreed," said Rell.

"Agreed, and I'm sorry," Pip said, and Marlow put a hand on his twin's shoulder and nodded his own agreement, and Sera said, "Agreed, and Edmund — I should have said it in the chamber, in front of all of them. I should have stood up and asked you first. That failure's mine, and I'll carry it."

Something in his shoulders eased, a fraction of an inch. "Then we have a bargain," he said. "Goose and all."

"Diversionary goose," Marlow confirmed solemnly. "I'm having that embroidered on a banner."

* * *

When the council reconvened, Ysolde read the terms of their agreement aloud in a voice like a notary reading a will, and the seal was pressed, and it was done. Twelve ships. Four thousand soldiers. The Ember, retrieved first, held as surety. Edmund Yarrow, beacon and key, consent given freely before witnesses.

As they filed out, Brannoc the merchant caught Sera's sleeve.

"A word, lady?" He drew her aside, lowered his voice. "The speaker believes this plan because she needs to believe it. I've traded in Veyra's ports. I know her hunters. They don't chase bait." His rings clicked softly against his belt. "They herd it. Remember that, when the time comes. Whatever corner of the capital they drive your friend toward — ask yourself who chose the corner."

He walked away before she could answer, leaving Sera standing in the pale stone corridor with the harbor bells ringing behind her and a new cold settling into her stomach, the particular cold of a potion that has changed color when it should have stayed clear.

Above the chandler's shop that night, she packed her satchel by candlelight and found herself arranging the bottles with more care than usual, as if precision could hold off what was coming. Through the thin wall she could hear the others — Marlow's laugh, Pip's low murmur, Odo's boots — and beneath it, once, Edmund's voice saying something soft that she couldn't catch.

Three days ago she'd have knocked and gone in. Tonight she sat with her hand on the door latch a long moment, and did not lift it.

Outside, far off across the water, a light moved where no ship should be — brief, green, and wrong — and was gone.
