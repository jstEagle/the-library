## 12. Hunted Across the Salt Flats

The betrayal came, as betrayals so often do, wearing a friendly face.

Her name was Dellet, a clerk of Correnvale's council with ink-stained fingers and a habit of pressing warm honey-buns into Pip's hands whenever the company passed her desk. She had drawn them maps of the salt flats herself, marked the wells in careful brown ink, and wished them fortune with such evident sincerity that Edmund had liked her on sight.

It was Sera who noticed, three days out from the city, that the well at Brackwater — the first one on Dellet's map — was not merely dry but *filled*. Stones and rubble packed down hard, as if someone had been in a hurry to make sure no one drank there.

"That's not drought," Sera said, crouching over it. "That's intent."

"Maybe it collapsed on its own," Odo offered, though his voice carried no conviction.

"Maybe." Sera looked up at Edmund, and there was something in her face he'd learned to dread — the look of someone about to say a thing that could not be unsaid. "Or maybe someone told Veyra's people exactly which road we'd take."

They stood around the ruined well in the flat grey morning light, five travelers and a dead man's map, and nobody said the obvious word: *bait*. The council had used him as bait; now someone had sold the bait along with the hook, the line, and the whole sorry fisherman. Edmund felt the old familiar shame rise in his throat — the sense of being a burden wrapped in a quest — and was surprised to find Odo's hand on his shoulder before it got there.

"Whoever sold us," Odo said quietly, "sold all of us. That's not your doing, Yarrow. That's just what councils do when they're frightened."

"Frightened people make fast friends with monsters," Marlow said. He said little these days, Marlow, but when he spoke the twins both went quiet to listen, which struck Edmund as its own kind of magic.

"Then we don't be frightened," Pip said. "We be fast instead. Can we be fast?"

"We can be fast," Sera said, and shouldered her pack.

* * *

The salt flats began an hour later, and Edmund had never seen anything like them.

The land simply gave up. No grass, no trees, no hills — just a vast white plain stretching to every horizon, cracked into hexagonal plates like the floor of some abandoned temple, glittering under a sun that seemed to have taken personal offense at their journey. The air shimmered. The silence was enormous. When Pip sneezed, the sneeze rolled away across the flats for what sounded like a full minute before it died.

"It's beautiful," Edmund said, meaning it.

"It's a killing floor," Odo said, also meaning it. "No cover. No water between here and the Cinder Wells, two days' march. If they come for us out here—"

"They'll see us from miles," Marlow finished. "And we'll see them. Fair enough, for once."

For half a day it was almost pleasant. The twins invented a walking game involving naming clouds, of which there were none, which somehow made it funnier. Sera walked beside Edmund and taught him the names of the salt-crusted stones they passed — *glaucite, whisperstone, fool's quartz* — and when he asked how she knew them she said, "A hedge-witch learns the ground under her feet or the ground eats her," and smiled at him in a way that made the flats feel considerably less hostile.

Edmund had begun, cautiously, to feel something adjacent to happiness.

Which was, of course, when the horizon grew riders.

* * *

Sera saw them first — a smudge of dark against the white, then a line, then nine shapes resolving into horses and men in the lacquered grey of Veyra's mage-hunters. Even at distance, Edmund could see the wrongness of them: the way light bent oddly around their shoulders, the faint blue crawl of ward-spells stitched into their cloaks.

"Nine," Odo said, drawing his sword out of habit and then, surveying two miles of featureless salt, putting it away again. "Against five, one of whom is twelve, one of whom is me, and none of whom can outrun horses across open ground."

"I'm thirteen," Pip said with dignity.

"Run anyway," Marlow said. "There's a ridge of broken stone off the map's edge — Dellet marked it as shelter. If she marked it truly."

"And if she marked it falsely?" Edmund asked.

"Then we die somewhere interesting," Pip said cheerfully, and ran.

They ran. The flats were treacherous going — the salt plates cracked and shifted underfoot, and twice Edmund went down hard enough to taste blood and brine. Behind them the riders came on without hurry, without shouting, with the terrible patience of people who knew the prey had nowhere to go. And as they rode, Edmund saw the lead hunter raise a hand, and saw the air ahead of the company begin to *curdle*.

"Spellwork!" Sera shouted. "He's laying a snare-net — if we run into it—"

"What does it do?" Edmund gasped.

"Stops you. All of you. Mid-stride, mid-heartbeat, until they arrive to collect you."

The curdling air thickened ahead of them, a wall of shimmering wrongness spreading from ridge to ridge, closing the gap. Edmund felt it reach toward him — and slide past, the way rain slid off glass, the way everything magical slid off him and always had. But the others—

Pip stumbled, caught in the net's outer fringe, and cried out — a small, strangled sound, like a bird in a fist. Marlow hauled him back by the collar. Sera's stride broke. Odo swore.

And Edmund did the only thing he could think of, which was the thing he had spent thirty years doing at a gate in a valley where nothing magical could follow: he planted himself in front of the wall of spellwork and *pushed*.

Nothing happened. Everything happened.

Where his hands met the shimmering net, it didn't burn or spark or fight him. It simply *stopped being*, unraveling like a knot pulled loose, thread by thread, the way frost gives up a windowpane in the morning sun. A hole opened in the snare wide enough for four people and a boy. Through it, the flats lay ordinary and white and empty.

"Through!" Edmund roared, in a voice he didn't know he owned. "GO!"

They went. He went last, backing through his own hole, and behind him heard the lead hunter's shout of fury and confusion — *"What unmade it? WHAT UNMADE IT?"* — and then they were through, and running, and laughing, actually laughing, wild and disbelieving, even as the riders wheeled to come around the flank of the dead trap.

"You *punched* a spell," Pip panted, keeping pace beside him, eyes enormous. "You punched a spell with your FACE—"

"With my hands, mostly."

"I'm going to tell everyone. I'm going to tell it WRONG, so it sounds better."

* * *

The broken ridge Dellet had marked turned out to exist after all — a spine of shattered black rock jutting up from the salt like the ribcage of something vast and long-dead. Whether the clerk had been honest about this one kindness, or whether she'd simply sold them accurately, Edmund supposed they'd never know. They wedged themselves into its hollows as the riders fanned out below.

From the rocks they watched the hunters confer. Nine figures, small with distance, gesturing. Then six dismounted and began to climb.

"Six on foot," Odo murmured. "Three holding the horses. They mean to dig us out."

"Can you fight six?"

"No. Not mages. Not in the open." Odo's jaw worked. "In the guard we had a saying: never fight a spellcaster where they've had time to prepare. This is their prepared ground."

"Then we make it unprepared," Sera said. She had gone very still, in the way she did when working something through. "Their spells work fine out here — plenty of room, plenty of sky. But they keep trying to cast *at close quarters*, and every time they get near Edmund, things go wrong for them. Did you see the net? It didn't resist him. It *forgot itself*."

"So we put Edmund next to them," Marlow said slowly.

"So we put Edmund next to them," Sera agreed. "Repeatedly. Aggressively."

Everyone looked at Edmund.

"I'd like the record to show," he said, "that I have no idea what I'm doing."

"Neither does anyone," Sera said, taking his hand. Her grip was warm and steady and made his heart conduct itself disgracefully. "That's rather the point. You're the only one here who gets to be honestly ignorant."

The plan, such as it was, took shape in whispers among the rocks: Odo and Marlow would make noise and shadow on the ridge's far side, drawing climbers toward the narrow defile where the rock pinched tight. Sera would hold the high ground with what hedge-craft she had — small things, misdirection, a wind-trick or two. And Edmund would be where the fighting wasn't, until suddenly it was, at which point he would simply *be there*, next to whatever spell was being woven, and let his presence do what thirty years of guarding a sealed gate had trained him for absolutely nothing else except: standing still in the wrong place, on purpose.

"It's a terrible plan," Odo said.

"It's the plan we have," Sera said.

"I want it noted I said so."

"Noted," said everyone, including Pip, who had produced a stub of charcoal and was apparently noting it on his arm.

* * *

It went wrong immediately, and then, somehow, right.

The defile trick worked better than it had any business working — three hunters funneled into the pinch of rock where Odo's sword and Marlow's sling made the narrow space a butcher's corridor. But the fourth and fifth were smarter. They hung back, and Edmund watched one of them plant his feet, spread his arms, and begin to *sing* — a low, grinding chant, and the whole ridge shuddered, and cracks of blue light raced up the black stone like veins of lightning frozen mid-strike.

"He's bringing the rocks down," Sera screamed. "On ALL of us—"

The chant rose. The blue veins brightened. Odo, bloodied, backed out of the defile dragging Marlow, and the hunters closed in below, and Edmund understood with perfect clarity that in about ten seconds the ridge would collapse and bury them all, and that there was exactly one person in the world who could walk into that song and make it stop.

So he walked into it.

Later, he would try to describe what it felt like, and fail. The best he ever managed was this: it was like stepping into a river and discovering the river had been waiting for him. The chant hit him and broke apart — not violently, not with fire and thunder, but gently, the way a held note dies when the singer forgets why they were singing. The blue veins in the rock dimmed, flickered, and went out. The hunter's voice cracked. He stared at Edmund with naked horror, tried again, and this time the words wouldn't even form — they slid off his tongue like water off glass, like everything slid off Edmund, always, uselessly, *wonderfully*.

The hunter drew steel instead. That was a mistake, because Odo was faster, and angrier.

When it was over — when the last of the six had fled downslope screaming for the horses, when the three riders remaining wheeled and galloped away westward rather than face whatever had unmade their brothers' craft — the company stood among the black rocks in the ringing silence, looking at Edmund as though seeing him for the first time.

Pip broke it, naturally.

"You're not useless," he announced, with the gravity of a king conferring a duchy. "You're the opposite of useless. You're *anti-useful*. You're the most useful person who isn't useful that I've ever met."

"That may be the nicest thing anyone's ever said to me."

"It's true, though," Sera said softly. She touched his cheek, where a scratch from the rocks had left a thin line of blood. "All those years at your gate, thinking you were the valley's least of its people. And the whole time—" She shook her head. "You weren't locked out of the world, Edmund. You were the world's lock."

Odo cleaned his sword in silence, then said, gruffly, "Decent work. For a gatekeeper."

From Odo, Edmund had learned, this was roughly equivalent to a sonnet.

* * *

They moved again at dusk, putting miles between themselves and the ridge while the light lasted, and camped that night in a shallow bowl of salt that glowed faintly under the moons like a bowl of crushed stars. The Cinder Wells lay a day ahead. Beyond them, the map promised, the road bent south toward the mountains, and the pass where — according to Marlow's grim reading of Dellet's other markings — worse things than riders waited.

But that was tomorrow's weight. Tonight, Pip fell asleep mid-sentence describing Edmund's future legend ("and then he punched the SECOND spell, but with his MIND"), and Marlow covered him with a blanket with the tenderness of a man handling something breakable, and Odo took first watch humming an old guard-song under his breath.

Edmund sat apart with Sera, their shoulders touching, watching the salt shine.

"You saved us today," she said. "Really saved us. How does it feel? Being strong, instead of being spared?"

He thought about it honestly, the way she always made him honest.

"It feels like the gate," he said at last. "Thirty years I stood at that door thinking I was the thing being kept safe inside. Turns out I was the hinge." He paused. "I don't entirely know what to do with that."

"You will." She leaned her head against his shoulder. "You're learning the ground under your feet, Edmund Yarrow. Same as any witch."

Far off across the white flats, something glinted — briefly, at the edge of vision, gone when looked at directly. Edmund told himself it was moonlight on salt.

But Odo, on watch, had stopped humming.
