## 13. The Witch of the Ford

The river found them on the ninth day out of Correnvale, sliding silver through a fold of green hills as if the war had never touched it. Sera smelled the water before she saw it — clean stone and cold mud and willow — and felt something in her chest unknot for the first time since the salt flats.

"A ford," Marlow said, shading his eyes. "A real one. Not a swamp pretending to be a river."

"It's a river pretending to be shallow," Pip said darkly. "I've been fooled before."

"You fell in a *creek*, Pip."

"The creek was *deceitful*."

Sera smiled and let them argue. That was the trick of traveling with twins: they generated enough noise to fill any silence, and lately there had been silences that needed filling. Edmund walked at the rear with his hood back, squinting at the water like a man who had spent his whole life behind a wall and was still learning what weather could do. Odo ranged ahead with Rell, the two of them arguing about old campaigns in the low, comfortable way of men who had once tried to kill each other and were now mostly over it.

And Sera walked in the middle, where she always did, carrying her grandmother's satchel and a secret she hadn't told anyone yet.

Not a big secret. A small one. A practical one.

She'd been dreaming of fire.

* * *

The witch lived in a house made of boat halves.

That was Sera's first thought, anyway — upturned hulls lashed together with rope and moss, chimneys made from figureheads, a porch built from what had once clearly been a very fine prow. The structure sat on stilts above the western bank of the ford, tilted like a gull riding wind, and smoke curled from three different chimneys in three different colors, which was either impressive craft or a chimney problem of heroic proportions.

"She's real," Rell said quietly. He'd gone still in the way he did when memory ambushed him. "Maren of the Ford. Beatrix used to tell stories about her. Said Maren read her fortune when she was sixteen and told her she'd die twice."

"And?" Pip asked.

"Beatrix laughed," Rell said. "She said, 'Good. I've got plans for the second one.'"

Nobody had anything to say after that, so nobody said anything, which was how this company honored the dead — a shared silence, brief and heavy, then moving forward because the dead would have wanted their map carried.

Sera went up alone.

The door opened before she knocked. The woman inside was small and brown and wrinkled as a walnut left in the sun, with eyes the pale gray of river fog and hands stained green to the elbow, as though she'd been caught mid-murder by a garden.

"Hedge-witch," the old woman said. It wasn't a question.

"Sera Thistlewick."

"I know what you are, girl, I asked what you're called." Maren stepped aside. "Come in before the midges do. Mind the third step, it bites."

The inside of the boat-house smelled of drying herbs and river water and something older underneath, something like the inside of a drum. Bundles hung from every rafter. A kettle sat on a fire that burned without wood, which made Sera's professional pride prickle — *show-off* — and a cat the size of a loaf of bread regarded her from a shelf with the flat disapproval only cats and senior librarians can manage.

"You're crossing," Maren said. "All of you. West, toward the capital." She clicked her tongue. "Foolish. Sit down. Tea?"

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet." She poured two cups from a pot that had no handle. "You want something. Hedge-witches always want something when they find me. A charm. A reading. A cure for a curse they gave themselves by being clever."

Sera wrapped her hands around the cup. It was warm in a way that had nothing to do with temperature — warm like a hand on your shoulder.

"Not a charm," she said. "Well. Maybe a charm. But first—" She hesitated. This was the part she hadn't planned to say aloud, and here it was coming out anyway, the way truth does around witches, leaking past all your careful dams. "I've been dreaming of fire. Every night for a week. A fire in a cage, and I'm standing too close, and I know — I know the way you know things in dreams — that if I reach into it, I can put it out. And I also know it will take my hands. Both dreams are true at once. I wake up with my palms burning."

Maren looked at her for a long moment. The cat jumped down, crossed the floor, and sat at Sera's feet, which Sera chose to interpret as sympathy rather than an omen.

"Show me your palm," Maren said.

Sera held it out. The old witch took her wrist with fingers surprisingly strong and turned her hand toward the window light, studying the lines the way a farrier studies a hoof.

"Hm," said Maren.

"That's not a reassuring sound."

"No line is ever reassuring, girl. That's why we call them lines and not roads." She traced one with a cracked thumbnail — a long line running from the base of Sera's thumb clear across the palm and off the edge of her hand, which Sera was fairly sure hadn't been there last winter. "When did this arrive?"

"I don't—"

"Witches don't get to not-know. When did it arrive?"

"...After the salt flats," Sera admitted. "After I pulled everyone through the null-wake. After—" She stopped.

"After you saved the magicless man," Maren said, "and liked doing it."

Heat climbed Sera's neck. "He's not — he's our ward-bearer. It's duty. We protect him because the map burns anyone else, and because he's kind, and because he carries Marlow's ashes without being asked, and because he laughs at Pip's jokes even the bad ones, especially the bad ones, and none of that is—"

"Girl." Maren patted her wrist once, briskly, the way you'd settle a spooked horse. "I've been reading palms since before your grandmother was a disappointment to *her* mother. You love him. It's written across your face in letters a blind man could sound out. The only interesting question is whether you'll admit it before or after it costs you."

Sera looked into her tea. The surface had gone still as a mirror, and in it — she could have sworn, just for a heartbeat — she saw a lantern glowing ember-orange, and someone's hand holding it, and the hand was hers, and it was fading at the edges like paper near a flame.

She blinked. Just tea. Just steam.

"What does the line mean?" she asked quietly.

Maren let go of her wrist and sat back. For a moment the old woman looked very tired, and very old, and very much like someone who had given bad news before and hated the taste of it.

"Come back at moonrise," she said. "Bring the man if you like. The cards do better with a full deck, and yours has been playing short-handed since the courier died. But if you come at moonrise, girl, understand: I don't lie, I don't soften, and I don't repeat myself. What I say, you carry. That's the price of asking."

Outside, downstream, Edmund was teaching Pip to skip stones. Pip's stones sank with the dignity of drowned kings. Edmund's skipped four times, and Pip accused him of cheating via gatekeeper magic, and Edmund laughed — really laughed, head back — and Sera watched him from the doorway of a house made of boats and felt the new line across her palm hum like a plucked string.

"After," she said, mostly to herself. "I'll admit it after."

* * *

They camped that night on the eastern bank, under willows, with the witch's boat-house leaning against the sunset across the water like a drunk old friend.

Odo cooked. Odo's cooking was a military operation — efficient, filling, aggressively seasoned — and the company ate with the focused silence of people who had learned not to waste good food complaining about it. Rell produced a bottle of plum wine from somewhere in his pack, and when Marlow demanded to know where, exactly, Rell said "confiscated," and Marlow said "from whom," and Rell said "the future," and passed it around.

Sera sat with her knees drawn up, watching the fire, and did not drink.

Edmund noticed. Of course he noticed; he noticed everything, quietly, the way a gate notices weather. He drifted over and sat beside her without asking, which was another thing about him — he never asked permission to be near her, he simply arranged himself within talking distance and waited, patient as masonry, until she was ready.

"You met the witch," he said.

"I met the witch."

"Is she going to help us cross? Rell says the deep ford floods this time of year."

"She'll help us cross." Sera turned her cup — she'd brought the tea, cold now, unable to make herself throw it away. "Edmund. Can I ask you something strange?"

"I live with twins and a disgraced knight and a defected enforcer and a hedge-witch who talks to her own satchel. My threshold for strange is on the floor. Ask."

"When you touch the map. When you hold it. What does it feel like?"

He considered that seriously, because he considered everything seriously; it was one of the hundred things she loved about him, and admitting the number had reached triple digits was a problem for a braver season.

"Warm," he said. "Like a bird that trusts you. Sometimes it pulls — toward the capital, mostly west and slightly south — but gently. Like a dog on a leash who smells dinner. It doesn't hurt me. Sera—" He glanced at her. "It doesn't hurt me, but I've seen it blister Odo through two layers of glove. Why?"

"Just cataloguing," she said lightly. "Hedge-witch habit. Everything gets written down eventually."

"Liar," he said, with such mildness that it took her a second to realize he'd said it at all.

Sera stared at him. Across the fire, Pip was trying to teach Rell a drinking song and failing, because Rell knew the song and was deliberately singing the wrong words to watch Pip suffer. Odo was laughing into his beard. Marlow's banner — the scrap of blue cloth they'd cut from his cloak, now sewn to Pip's pack — fluttered on a willow branch where Pip had hung it, absurd and holy.

"I'm a terrible liar," Sera said finally.

"You're an excellent liar," Edmund said. "That's what worries me. Terrible liars give themselves away. Excellent liars just go quiet, and you've been quiet for three days." He wrapped his hands around his knees and looked at the fire instead of her, giving her the privacy of his profile. "Whatever the witch said, or whatever you think she's going to say — you don't have to carry it alone. That's the whole point of us. That's the entire company charter, as far as I can tell. Nobody carries anything alone."

Sera's throat tightened. She looked at the tea, at the steam that wasn't there anymore, at the ghost of a lantern only she could see.

"Moonrise," she said. "She wants to read the cards at moonrise. Full deck, she said." A breath. "You could come. If you wanted."

"Do you want me to?"

Yes, said everything in her that had a voice. No, said the new line across her palm. *If he hears it, he'll try to stop it, and if he tries to stop it, he'll die, and if he dies, the map dies, and the king dies, and everything Beatrix bled for dies in a ford in the hills while the plum wine goes around.*

"I want you to sleep," Sera said, and heard her own excellent lying voice, smooth and warm and false. "Moonrise readings are witch business. Old superstition. You'd be bored."

Edmund looked at her a moment longer. Then he nodded, and rose, and said goodnight, and Sera sat by the fire feeling the exact weight of what she'd just done — not the prophecy, whatever it turned out to be, but this: the first small brick of the wall she was already building between them, laid carefully, mortared with kindness.

* * *

The moonrise reading happened in the boat-house, with the door open to the river and the three-colored chimney-smoke drifting up through the willows like the world's most ominous festival.

Maren shuffled a deck that looked older than the concept of decks. Cards of bone, some of them; cards of bark; one card that was clearly a piece of roof slate with something scratched on it. The cat sat on the table throughout, tail curled, supervising.

"Cut," Maren said.

Sera cut. The cards came out in a spread that Maren studied for a long time, longer than any reading Sera had ever done herself or received, long enough that the fire popped twice and the river changed its mind about its current.

Then the old witch began turning cards, and each one landed with a sound like a small door closing.

"The road," she said, tapping the first. "Long, and it bends toward fire. No surprise there; I could've drawn that with a stick in the mud." Second card. "The cage. The flame in the cage. It's close now — closer than your council friends told you, closer than the map admits. It knows you're coming. Things that eat wills have appetites, and yours has been starving for years."

Third card. Maren paused over it.

"The hollow man," she said softly. "The king. He's still in there. Barely. A candle-flame in a storm cellar. Whatever you do at the end, girl — whatever choice the fire offers you — remember there's a man under the crown, and he has been screaming for six years, and nobody has heard him but the one man who can't hear anything magical at all." Her fog-gray eyes flicked up. "Your gatekeeper. Isn't that something. The gods do love their jokes."

"Please," Sera said. Her mouth had gone dry. "The line. On my palm. Tell me what it means."

Maren turned the fourth card.

It showed a lantern. In the lantern, a flame. Around the flame, a shape — a woman's shape, drawn in outline only, transparent as glass, holding the light up so others could see the road. The artist had taken care with the flame. The woman was barely there at all.

Maren didn't touch the card. She looked at it the way you look at a grave.

"You asked," she said. "So hear it. There is a moment coming — not at the end of the road, but near it, near enough to smell — when the fire will need a vessel or it will take the king whole. The ritual will fail. Your cleverness will fail. Your charms will fail. Everything you've built your life on, all the little stitches and wards and tricks, will fail, because the Ember is older than stitching." She turned the card fully upright. "And in that moment, you will see exactly what to do. You will save the man you love. Everyone you love. The king, the cities, the lot of them."

Sera's heart was a fist in her chest. "And the cost."

Maren met her eyes.

"You will lose yourself," the witch said. "Not your life — worse, and better. Yourself. The flame doesn't kill what carries it. It *uses* it. It burns through a vessel slowly, the way a candle burns a wick, and what's left at the end is light and warmth and no Sera Thistlewick at all. You'll fade, girl. Gently. Kindly. Everyone around you will have months, maybe a year, of watching you become a beautiful memory of yourself." She gathered the cards in, unhurried, terrible. "That's the price of the reading. Now you carry it."

The river ran. The cat yawned. Somewhere outside, faintly, Pip was singing the wrong version of the wrong song, and Odo was laughing, and the world went on being warm and ordinary and doomed.

"Can it be changed?" Sera asked. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. Witches learn early how to speak through fear; it's half the trade.

"Fates are doors, not walls," Maren said. "There's always another door. But every other door I can see from here is worse — the king dies, or the gatekeeper dies, or the fire takes the city instead of the woman. This is the best door, girl. That's the cruelty of it. The sacrifice isn't forced on you. It's *offered*." She stood, joints cracking like green kindling, and began banking the fire. "Which means it will work. Because you'll choose it. Freely. With love. And no one will be able to blame anyone, least of all you, and that — mark me — is the hardest grief there is. The kind with no villain in it."

Sera walked back across the ford in the dark, boots in her hand, the cold water climbing her shins, and by the time she reached the far bank she had done three things.

She had cried, briefly and thoroughly, standing hip-deep in a river with the moon watching, because some things must be done somewhere private even from yourself.

She had decided. Fully, finally, the way a knot decides. If the moment came, she would reach into the fire. She would not tell Edmund. She would not tell Odo, who would forbid it; not the twins, who would try to bargain with fate, and fate ate bargainers for breakfast; not Rell, who had lost Beatrix to exactly this kind of choosing and would break. She would carry it the way Beatrix had carried the map — alone, burning, smiling.

And she had practiced the lie she would tell tomorrow, when Edmund asked what the witch said.

*Old woman read my grandmother's name in the cards. Told me to stop overworking my wards. Charged me an egg.*

She climbed the bank, wrung out her skirts, and looked up.

Edmund was awake. Of course he was awake. He sat by the low fire with his arms around his knees, and when he saw her he didn't ask questions, didn't press, just shifted over and made room in the warmth without a word, the way he did everything — patiently, solidly, like a gate that had decided, after thirty years, to open for one specific person.

"Bad news?" he asked softly.

"No," said Sera, and laid her head on his shoulder, and felt the new line across her palm pulse once against his sleeve like a second heartbeat. "Just an old woman's nonsense."

Across the dark water, in the boat-house, the witch Maren stood in her doorway watching them, and shook her head, and went inside.

And far to the west, past the hills, past the salt, in a tower above a sleeping capital, something ancient and hungry turned over in its cage — and for the first time in six years, it dreamed of a woman made of light, and woke its mistress to whisper her name.
