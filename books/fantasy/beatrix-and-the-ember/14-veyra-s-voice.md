## 14. Veyra's Voice

The Ember sang to Veyra Ashvane every morning, the way a caged bird sings to the woman who feeds it — gratefully, and always a little accusingly.

She rose before dawn, as she had for eleven years, and climbed the spiral stair from her apartments in the Spire to the Vault of Hours, where the kingdom's true crown hung suspended above a basin of black glass. Not the gold circlet Aldric wore at ceremonies. The other one. The living one.

"Good morning," she said to it.

The Ember pulsed. It always pulsed. Eleven years of mornings, and it had never once said good morning back, which Veyra considered a failure of manners on its part and not, as Sera the hedge-witch's ilk would have it, a sign that it was *suffering*. Flames did not suffer. Flames burned, or they went out, and this one had been burning since before the first Yarrow nailed the first ward-plank over the first valley gate.

She pressed her palm flat above the basin, careful not to touch — touching was for rituals, and rituals were for Tuesdays — and let the current run up her arm like cold water in reverse. Through it she felt the king, three floors down and two wings over, waking in his bed with his eyes already empty, reaching for a cup of wine he would not taste. She felt the garrisons in the east, the Binding Day preparations, the tax levies, the thousand small threads of will she had wound through a continent one patient year at a time.

And beneath all of it, faint as a hairline crack in fine porcelain, she felt the thing she had felt for six days now and told no one about.

A silence. Moving. South by southwest, toward the capital, at walking pace.

A hole in her web the exact shape of a man.

* * *

"You're frowning," said Mistress Corvel, her chamberlain, arriving with the morning ledger and the particular expression of a woman who had served three queens and intended to outlive a fourth. "You only frown when something is wrong, and when something is wrong you usually tell me within the hour so that I may begin denying knowledge of it to the appropriate officials."

"I am not frowning. I am *considering*."

"You are considering with your eyebrows down. That is frowning with ambition." Corvel set the ledger on the table. "The eastern lords want more grain. The Correnvale council has sent another letter asking, delicately, whether Your Majesty intends to burn them this season or next, so they might plan their harvests accordingly. And the Master of Coin has requested an audience regarding—"

"Deny the grain. Answer Correnvale with warmth and no promises. And the Master of Coin may wait, because I have a more interesting problem." Veyra turned to the window. Below, the capital sprawled in morning haze, obedient and gray. "Something is walking across my country that I cannot see."

Corvel did not write that down, which meant she understood its importance. "A person?"

"A person-shaped absence. Every spell I own slides past it. Every scrying thread I've spun in thirty years finds nothing there — no resistance, no shield, just *nothing*, moving south at the speed of tired feet." Veyra tapped the windowsill once. "Do you know how difficult it is to make nothing, Corvel? I have tried. Nothing resists being made. And yet here comes a quantity of it, strolling through my Ashfall province like a tourist."

"Could it be a flaw in your instruments?"

"My instruments do not have flaws. They have features I have not yet explained to me." But even as she said it, Veyra's mind was elsewhere, running backward through eleven years of ledgers and bloodlines, hunting for the word *null* in old files. Wardens. The valley. The gate her predecessor's predecessor had sealed and everyone had agreed, in that comfortable way institutions agree, never to think about again.

"The Yarrow valley," she said slowly.

"What of it? It's a hole in the map full of turnips and people who marry their cousins. We leave it alone because there's nothing there worth taxing."

"There's nothing there worth *scrying*, which is different, and I have been very careful to keep those two things confused for a long time." Veyra began to pace, hands clasped behind her back, which Corvel knew meant the thinking had gotten serious enough to require movement. "A magicless valley needs a magicless keeper. That was the entire design. Generations of them, bred and bound to the place. And now a null walks out of nowhere, heading south, and my hunters report that Beatrix Ash died at the edge of that same valley six days ago."

Corvel's pen stopped. "Ash. The thief."

"The thief who stole my Ember's first key twenty years ago and has been a thorn shaped like a person ever since. Yes. Her." Something moved through Veyra's chest at the name — not grief, she told herself firmly, because grief was for people who had lost things, and Veyra had merely misplaced Beatrix Ash, temporarily, for two decades. "She died at the warden's door. Which means whatever she was carrying, she handed off. To him."

"To a turnip farmer."

"To a *lock*, Corvel. A lock that has learned to walk." Veyra smiled, and Corvel, who had seen that smile precede two purges and a wedding, quietly moved the inkwell out of reach. "And locks, unlike keys, cannot be picked. They must be broken. Or better—" she paused at the window, watching the light come up over the free cities' distant smoke, "—they must be *used*."

* * *

The audience hall received petitioners until noon, because tyranny that skipped the boring parts stopped being tyranny and started being chaos, and Veyra had no patience for chaos. She settled a boundary dispute between two barley barons by giving the land to neither and both, in a manner that would occupy their lawyers for a decade. She confirmed a new provincial governor, watched his pupils dilate as the binding thread settled into him, and noted with mild disappointment that he wept. They usually wept. It was the will leaving that did it — the sensation, she'd been told, of setting down a weight you'd carried so long you'd forgotten it wasn't part of your body.

She remembered the sensation herself, from the other side. From the day she had knelt in this same hall, a hedge-sorcerer's daughter with ash still in her hair from the purge of her village, and old King Maren had set the binding thread on her and called it *mercy*. Spare your life, serve the crown. She had served. She had smiled. And she had spent nine years learning exactly how the thread was made, exactly what fed it, exactly where the cage's hinges were —

— and then the Ember had chosen her, of all the bound souls in the palace, because the Ember was not loyal. The Ember was hungry. It wanted a will strong enough to hold it, and hers had been tempered in a way no royal upbringing could match.

Maren had died believing himself beloved. Aldric had inherited the throne believing himself beloved. Beloved was such a useful word. It covered so much.

By the time the last petitioner bowed out, the silence to the southwest had grown louder, if a silence could be said to grow loud. Veyra stood alone in the hall's vast hush and reached for it again, carefully, the way one reaches toward a sleeping animal.

There. Past the salt flats. Past the ford. A knot of travelers, most of them faintly warm with hedge-craft — a witch, a couple of cutpurses, someone with guard-training, someone else with guard-training gone sour — and at the center of them, wrapped around the whole company like the eye of a storm, the null.

Traveling together. Protecting each other, by the look of the spacing. Carrying something.

"Well," Veyra said to the empty hall. "Hello, little rebellion."

It was almost sweet. It was, in fact, exactly the sort of thing Beatrix Ash would have assembled — a found family of strays and criminals, armed with nothing but loyalty and poor odds. And Beatrix was dead, which meant either the company didn't know yet, or they knew and were carrying her mission anyway, which was worse. Sentiment made people brave, and brave people were so much harder to predict than frightened ones.

Veyra did not hate Beatrix Ash. That was the truth she kept in the locked drawer at the bottom of herself, under the ambition and the arithmetic. Hatred required thinking about someone, and thinking about Beatrix required remembering a rooftop in Correnvale, twenty-two years ago, and a girl with singed fingers teaching her to pick a lock badly while both of them laughed at the absurdity of a sorcerer's daughter and a thief's apprentice planning to steal the same impossible thing for opposite reasons.

*Come with me,* Beatrix had said. *We'll give it back to the world.*

*I'm going to take it for myself,* Veyra had said, *and then the world won't need giving anything, because I'll be running it properly.*

They had both, in their ways, kept their word.

Veyra closed the drawer. There was work to do.

* * *

Captain Rell came when summoned, which was unremarkable — Rell always came when summoned, had come when summoned for eleven years, punctual as taxes and half as welcome. What was remarkable was that he arrived looking like a man who had slept in his uniform, and Veyra's enforcers did not sleep in their uniforms unless something had happened, and nothing had happened, because she would have known.

He knelt. He waited. His jaw was set in the particular way it set when he was disagreeing with her in complete silence, a skill he had refined to an art form.

"Captain." She let him kneel a moment longer than necessary. Power was mostly choreography. "Tell me about the western roads."

"Quiet, Majesty. Ashfall province reports bandit activity down since autumn. The salt flats are clear. Correnvale's patrols stay inside their walls." A pause, precise as a blade laid flat. "No disturbances worth Your Majesty's attention."

"No disturbances." Veyra descended the three steps of the dais, slow, her rings clicking against the rail. "And yet I have it on excellent authority — mine — that a company of six is crossing my country toward my capital, led by a dead woman's map, sheltered by a null, and my Captain of Enforcers, whose job is disturbances, has reported none of it."

Rell's head came up. For one unguarded instant there was something raw in his face — not fear. Recognition. As if he had been waiting six days for her to say it, dreading it, relieved by it, all at once.

"You knew," he said.

"I know everything. It is my single most tiresome quality." She stopped an arm's length away. "What I don't know, Captain, is why you knew too — and said nothing. Because you did know. Don't insult us both. You've been riding the western circuit twice as hard as the schedule requires. You've interrogated three smugglers about caravan movements out of the valley road. You've pulled every archive file on Beatrix Ash, and you returned them late, and you are many things, Rell, but you have never once been late."

The silence stretched. Somewhere high above, the vault's great flame hummed through the stones, and Veyra felt it listening, the way it always listened, hungry for the shape of people.

"She's dead," Rell said finally. His voice did not break. It did something worse — it went flat, the voice of a man reporting weather. "I heard it from a river-ferryman, third-hand. Died at the edge of the Yarrow valley. So I checked the roads myself, because I thought — if she's dead, then whoever's carrying her work is carrying it blind, and blind people walk into rivers and fall off cliffs, and the problem solves itself without me writing a report that says *the sorcerer-queen's old partner's lover is leading a rebellion, shall I arrest them.*"

"And does it solve itself?"

"No." He looked up at her fully now, and Veyra saw the thing she had always valued in him and always, quietly, feared: he had never learned to lie to her well, and he had never once stopped trying to protect someone from her. First the conscripts. Then the prisoners. Then, in the drawer of years she did not open, a thief on a rooftop in Correnvale, before either of them had chosen their sides — before Beatrix had chosen the world and Rell had chosen the law, and the law had turned out to be wearing Veyra's face. "They're six days out and getting smarter. Someone taught them to move. If I send ordinary hunters, ordinary hunters die, and then I have to explain to their families why."

"So you'll go yourself."

"I'll go myself."

Veyra studied him for a long moment. This was the trouble with keeping a man for eleven years: you learned where all his hinges were. She could order him. She could bind him — the thread sat coiled and ready, as it sat ready for every officer in her service, and Rell alone she had left unbound, because an enforcer needed to choose his cruelty freely or it curdled into sloppiness. That had seemed wise once. Standing here, watching him decide — visibly, hinge by hinge — how much of himself he was about to betray, it seemed less wise.

"Bring me the map," she said. "Bring me the null alive — he is worth more breathing than dead, and I will explain why when it no longer matters. Bring me the witch if convenient. The rest may die or scatter, whichever they prefer; I am generous that way." She turned back toward the dais, then paused. "And Captain. If you find Beatrix's grave along the way, you will ride past it. You will not stop. You will not kneel. Am I understood?"

Rell rose. He was a tall man, gray coming into his beard, and he stood the way walls stand.

"Understood, Majesty," he said, which was the first lie he had ever told her cleanly, and they both knew it, and neither of them said so.

* * *

That night Veyra climbed to the vault alone, as she did on nights when the silence in the southwest grew teeth, and stood before the Ember with her hands folded like a woman at prayer, which was, in fairness, exactly what she was.

"Someone is coming for you," she told it.

The flame pulsed. Hungry. Unbothered. It had been stolen, caged, bargained over, and worshipped for four hundred years, and it had outlasted every hand that held it. It did not fear a farmer and a witch and a grieving captain on the road.

"Underestimate them if you like," Veyra murmured. "I've read what happens to things that underestimate Beatrix Ash's people." She thought of the rooftop, the laughter, the lockpick snapping in the girl's fingers, and shut the drawer hard. "But I don't intend to find out."

Far to the southwest, a silence walked toward her through the dark, growing louder with every mile.

She sent her fastest rider after Rell with one additional instruction, unwritten, unbound, delivered by mouth alone:

*Watch him. And if he falters — bring me his heart's answer, not his body. I want to know which side he chose before he knows it himself.*
