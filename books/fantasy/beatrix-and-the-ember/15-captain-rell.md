## 15. Captain Rell

The rain had been falling for three days, and Captain Dain Rell had been following the null for all of them.

He knew it was a null before he ever saw him. You learned to read the absence of things the way other men read footprints. His trackers — two hedge-mages on loan from Veyra's own college, both of them nervous as cats in a thunderstorm — kept losing the trail in exactly the way you lost a trail when there was nothing at the center of it to hold onto. Magic left ripples. A witch's warding, a guard's charm, even the faint hum of an enchanted compass: all of it wrote itself on the world like ink on wet paper. The null wrote nothing. He was a hole shaped like a man, and he dragged five other holes behind him, because whatever wards they'd woven around their camp slid off him and refused to stick.

"Three days," muttered Wren, the younger of his mages. She was huddled under an oilcloth, trying to coax warmth into her scrying bowl with her bare hands. "Captain, I can't — the water keeps going flat. It's like looking for a fish in a pond that won't ripple."

"That's because your fish is a stone," Rell said.

Wren blinked at him.

"He doesn't disturb the water," Rell said. "That's the whole point of him. So stop looking for ripples. Look for what the ripples go *around*."

She stared at him a moment longer, then bent back over her bowl with something like hope. Rell turned up his collar and looked out at the grey country rolling away below the ridge — hedgerows drowned in mist, a river running fat and brown somewhere below, a road that hadn't seen a king's maintenance in two years. Somewhere down there, six people were walking toward the capital carrying the most dangerous piece of parchment in the world.

Somewhere down there was Beatrix Ash.

*Alive*, the reports said. The scouts who'd survived the salt flats swore to it — a woman with a burn-scarred jaw leading the null through the ford country, laughing at something one of the twins said. Rell had read the report four times. He had not slept since the second reading.

Twelve years ago, in the barracks at Carrowmere, a thief with quick hands and quicker eyes had stolen his dinner, his dagger, and then — over the course of one disastrous, wonderful winter — his heart. He'd caught her stealing from the garrison stores. She'd talked her way out of the cell. He'd let her. That was the beginning of everything, though neither of them had known it yet. For one year they'd met where the law ended and the marsh began, and she'd shown him the country the way it actually was — the taxed villages, the hanged poachers, the magistrates who took bribes from the same smugglers they executed — and he'd stopped sleeping well, and started asking questions, and eventually the questions had cost him a promotion and nearly cost him his commission. She'd laughed at him for it. *"You're the only honest man in a dishonest uniform, Dain. It's going to get you killed."*

Then Veyra had come to power, and the honest questions became treason, and Beatrix Ash had vanished into the outlaw country, and Dain Rell had done what soldiers do. He'd saluted. He'd marched. He'd told himself that order was better than chaos, that a strong hand was better than civil war, that the woman he loved was on the wrong side of history and he would grieve her quietly and serve the crown.

The crown being, as it turned out, a sorcerer-queen who wore the king like a glove.

Rell had never seen Aldric. None of the guard had, not really — a figure on a balcony, a silhouette at ceremonies, eyes that didn't track movement. He'd told himself the stories were exaggeration. He was very good at telling himself things. It was, he reflected now, the single greatest skill of his career, and it had taken him twelve years and a dead-eyed court to master it.

"Captain." Wren again, her voice tight. "I've got them. Or — I've got the hole. There's a gap in the world moving along the river road, and it's about to walk straight into Hallowmere."

Hallowmere. Rell knew the village. One street, a mill, a shrine to some river-god nobody remembered the name of. Good ground for an ambush, which meant bad ground for a fair fight, which meant it was exactly the kind of ground Rell had spent twenty years learning to fight on.

"How many?" he asked.

"The company? Six. Plus the null."

"And our side?"

"Whatever you bring, Captain."

Rell looked at the rain. He thought about the sealed orders in his breast pocket — *take the map, take the bearers alive if convenient, take them dead if not* — written in Veyra's own hand, in ink that shimmered faintly when you weren't looking directly at it. He thought about the twelve years. He thought about a woman laughing at him across a barracks fence, saying *you're too honest for this, Dain, it's going to break your heart.*

"It'll be enough," he said, and hated how easily the old lie came.

* * *

They came into Hallowmere at dusk, and Rell let them.

That was the thing he would turn over in his mind for years afterward — that he could have taken them on the open road, or at the ford, or anywhere in forty miles of empty country, and instead he waited until they were boxed between the mill and the shrine, until the twins were separated from the others checking the ferry ropes, until Sera the hedge-witch was knee-deep in the millrace resetting a ward-stone that wouldn't hold because the null was standing ten feet away making it pointless.

He came out of the shrine doorway with eight soldiers and both mages, and he put a crossbow bolt into the mill door an inch from Odo's ear, and he said, in the voice he'd used for twenty years, "Nobody moves. Nobody draws. First one who does, I let Wren start throwing fire, and she's been wanting to all week."

It went mostly the way ambushes go, which is to say badly and then worse. Odo moved anyway — of course he did; Rell recognized the man instantly, disgraced royal guard, still carrying himself like a palace pillar someone had leaned against a fence — and got a sword-point at his throat for it. The twins froze on the ferry dock, Pip holding Marlow's arm, both of them wearing identical expressions of furious calculation. Sera stood in the millrace with water streaming off her hands, and Rell watched her weigh a spell against eight crossbows and lose.

And Edmund Yarrow — the null, the gatekeeper, the hole in the world — stood in the middle of the street in the rain, doing absolutely nothing, and somehow that nothing was louder than everyone else's everything.

"Captain Rell," Odo said. "I know you. Carrowmere garrison. You testified at my hearing."

"I did," Rell said. "You were guilty of striking a magistrate. You were also right to. Both things were true, and the court only wanted one of them."

"Flattering. You've come a long way to die in a mill town."

"I've come a long way to collect a map." Rell raised his voice. "The rest of you can live. That's not generosity, it's arithmetic — Her Majesty wants the Ember's location, not six corpses she has to explain. Give me the map and the null, and you walk out of here."

Silence, except the rain and the millwheel turning.

Then Edmund Yarrow spoke, and his voice was exactly what Rell had expected from the reports: flat, tired, unhurried. "The map burns anyone but me. Your mages know it. Ask them."

"We know it," Wren said reluctantly. "It's warded with a death-binding. Touch it wrong and—"

"Then I'll take the man and we'll peel it off him at leisure," Rell said. "Bind him."

Two soldiers moved toward Edmund. Sera shouted something — a word of power, half-formed — and Wren snapped a counter-word, and for one ugly moment the air above the street rippled like heat haze—

—and then Edmund Yarrow walked forward, past the soldiers, past the crossbows, and stood directly in front of Rell, and every spell on the street died like a snuffed candle.

Wren made a small wounded sound. The older mage, Corvin, sat down abruptly in a puddle. The wards Rell had layered over the village that morning — detection webs, alarm-threads, the quiet humming lattice of a professional operation — all of it simply *stopped*, and in the sudden silence Rell felt naked in a way he hadn't felt since he was a recruit.

"You're standing in my silence," Edmund said. "Everything you brought to fight us just stopped working. So. We talk, or we stand here while your soldiers realize their captain brought knives to a fistfight."

Rell looked at him. Rain ran off the man's face. He had the eyes of a man who had guarded a gate alone for a very long time and gotten comfortable with silence — and underneath that, something rawer. Something that recognized Rell, or what Rell was, the way one stray dog recognizes another.

"You're not afraid," Rell said.

"I'm terrified," Edmund said. "I've just decided to be terrified later. It's a scheduling thing."

Despite everything — despite the mission, despite the sealed orders crackling faintly against his chest — something in Rell's chest cracked slightly. He knew that voice. He'd used that voice. It was the voice of people who had run out of options and decided dignity was the last one left.

"The map," Rell said quietly. "Where is Beatrix?"

Edmund's face changed. It was small, the change — a flicker, a tightening — but Rell had spent twenty years reading faces across interrogation tables, and he read this one like a confession.

"She's dead," Edmund said.

The rain kept falling. The millwheel kept turning. Somewhere behind Rell, a soldier shifted his weight, and Wren whispered something frightened, and none of it reached him. He was standing in a mill town in the rain, twelve years late, and the woman he had loved in the narrow space between duty and treason was dead, and he had spent those twelve years serving the thing that killed her.

"No," Rell said. It came out strange, almost conversational. "No, the reports said—"

"The reports were wrong," Edmund said. "She made it to my valley. She died on my floor. She gave me the map, and I've been carrying it ever since, and I will carry it until it's delivered, and you cannot have it, and you cannot have me, and I am sorry. I'm sorry she's dead. I only knew her for one night, and I'm sorry."

Something broke in Dain Rell then. Not loudly. Breaks like that never are. It was more like the sound a rope makes, deep in its length, when the load gets too heavy — no snap, just a shift, a settling, the certain knowledge that the next pull is the last one.

Twelve years. Twelve years of telling himself stories. That she'd chosen the wrong side. That order mattered. That the queen's peace was worth the queen's price. And the whole time, the truth had been walking around out there in the outlaw country with quick hands and a burn-scarred jaw, laughing at him across a distance he'd built himself, brick by obedient brick.

"Dain?" Odo said carefully. "Captain. You still with us?"

"My name is Dain," Rell said, to no one in particular. "She called me Dain. Nobody's called me Dain in twelve years."

He turned. He looked at his eight soldiers, standing in the rain with their crossbows slowly lowering because their captain hadn't given an order in too long and soldiers are creatures of momentum. He looked at Wren, whose scrying bowl had gone dark and cold. He looked at Corvin, sitting in the puddle, watching his captain with the expression of a man watching a building decide whether to fall.

"Lower your weapons," Rell said.

"Captain—" Corvin started.

"*Lower them.*"

They lowered them. Twenty years of habit is a powerful thing, even pointed the wrong way.

Rell reached into his breast pocket and drew out the sealed orders. Veyra's ink shimmered. He held them up so the company could see the seal, and then he tore them in half, and then in half again, and dropped the pieces in the mud.

"There," he said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "Now I'm a traitor too. Apparently that's the family business."

Sera had climbed out of the millrace. She stood dripping at the edge of the group, studying him with the particular look of a hedge-witch taking the measure of a man's soul, and apparently finding it damaged but serviceable. "You're going to catch hell for this," she said.

"I'm going to catch worse than hell," Rell said. "Corvin. Wren. You have a choice. Report that I went mad in the rain and fought the null barehanded, or ride back and tell the Queen's college that Captain Rell defected, and spend the rest of your careers being watched. I'd suggest the first one. It's kinder."

Corvin got up out of the puddle, brushed himself off with great dignity, and said, "With respect, Captain, I watched you tear up the Queen's own orders. I'm not lying to the college for you. But I'm not lying *about* you either. I'm going to report exactly what happened, and let them make of it what they will." He paused. "Which means they'll send someone worse than you. You should know that."

"I know," Rell said. "Thank you, Corvin. That's the first honest staff assessment I've had in years."

Corvin almost smiled. Then he mounted up, and Wren followed after one long, conflicted look at Sera — witch to witch, some silent apology passing between them — and the hoofbeats faded north into the rain.

And then it was just the seven of them in a drowned mill town, and Dain Rell standing in the middle of it with torn orders sinking into the mud, realizing with slow, cold clarity that he had nowhere in the world to go.

"Well," said Pip, from the ferry dock. "This is either the best or worst thing that's happened to us all month, and honestly it's been a competitive field."

"Pip," Marlow said.

"What! I'm just saying—"

"He's Veyra's captain," Marlow said. "He cornered us. He threatened to burn us. And now we're supposed to—what? Skip through the hills together?"

"He tore up the orders," Sera said.

"He tore up *one copy*. Men like him keep copies."

"I don't," Rell said. Everyone looked at him. "Kept copies, I mean. Of anything. That was always the problem. Beatrix used to say I was constitutionally incapable of covering my tracks." He looked down at the ruined orders dissolving in the mud, and something that was almost a laugh moved through him, brief and painful. "First honest thing I've done in twelve years, and it's the one that ends me."

Odo stepped closer. The two men regarded each other — the disgraced guard and the corrupt captain, the man who'd struck a magistrate and the man who'd testified against him, both of them standing in the rain a decade too late.

"Carrowmere," Odo said. "You voted to dismiss me."

"I voted to dismiss you *and* censure the magistrate. Split the difference. Lost the vote." Rell shrugged. "I've been losing votes my whole career. It's why I ended up chasing a map through the provinces instead of commanding a garrison."

"You could surrender to us," Odo said. "Make it easy. Bind yourself, give parole, come with us as a prisoner until we're sure."

"I could," Rell agreed. "Or you could do the arithmetic. You're six people walking into the capital of a sorcerer-queen, and I'm the only man alive who knows the palace under-vaults, the guard rotations, the Binding Day ceremony protocols, and which cellar doors the servants use when they don't want to be counted." He spread his empty hands. "Kill me if you want. But I'd be more useful as a traitor than a corpse, and frankly, so would you."

Silence again. Rain again. The millwheel, faithfully, again.

It was Edmund who broke it. He'd been quiet through all of it, standing in his own silence at the center of things, and when he spoke it was with the weary authority of a man who had carried a dead woman's map for weeks and was tired of deciding everything alone.

"We vote," Edmund said. "All of us. That's how this company works — I've been outvoted twice already and I expect to be outvoted again. But everyone gets a say, including him." He looked at Rell. "Including you. Say your piece."

Rell looked at the six of them. The null with the gatekeeper's eyes. The hedge-witch wringing out her sleeves. The disgraced guard standing like a pillar. The twins, identical and opposite, one curious and one suspicious. And underneath all of it, everywhere, the absence of Beatrix Ash — the shape of her in this company the way a hand keeps the shape of a thing it has held too long.

He thought: *she gathered these people. Outlaws, exiles, a magicless man off a mountain. She collected broken things and made them a family.* He thought: *the last thing she saw was a stranger's ceiling.* He thought: *she would laugh herself sick at the sight of me, dripping in a mill town, having finally picked a side twelve years too late.*

"My piece," Rell said, "is that I loved her. Past tense because it's easier to say that way, not because it's true. And I spent twelve years serving the woman who hunted her, because I told myself stories about order and duty, and I'm done. I'm done telling stories." He looked at each of them in turn. "I don't ask you to trust me. Trust is earned, and I haven't earned a copper of it. I ask you to use me. Point me at the palace and let me spend what's left of me buying back twelve years. If that's not enough, put a blade in me now, because I promise you — the moment Corvin reports, Veyra sends someone worse, and they won't stop at tearing up orders."

He knelt then, right there in the mud of the high street, in front of six strangers and a ghost, and Odo — who had once struck a magistrate for less — put a hand on his shoulder and lifted him up before his knees fully landed.

"Get up," Odo said gruffly. "We don't kneel. That's rather the point of us."

* * *

They voted by firelight, in the loft of the mill, while the rain finally thinned to a whisper against the roof.

Pip voted yes immediately, on the grounds that "anyone Veyra hates that much is probably our kind of terrible." Marlow voted no, then abstained, then — after a long look at his brother's face — switched to yes with the air of a man signing something regrettable. Sera voted yes without explanation, which from Sera was practically a speech. Edmund voted yes, saying only, "Beatrix trusted Odo, and Odo trusts him, and that's two more links in the chain than most of us had when we joined."

Odo voted last. He stood, and the loft went quiet, because everyone understood that Odo's vote was the hinge.

"I served the crown for fifteen years," Odo said. "And when the crown went rotten, I told myself the same stories he did — that I could serve the office without serving the rot. It took me four years to admit the office *was* the rot." He looked at Rell. "Dain Rell took four fewer years than me. That's not a reason to trust him. But it's a reason to believe he can learn. And I've watched this company take in worse." He glanced at Edmund. "Much worse. No offense."

"Some taken," Edmund said mildly.

"So," Odo said. "Aye. With conditions. He carries no map, takes no watch alone, and tells us everything he knows about the capital — starting tonight, starting with the Binding Day ceremony, because if we're going to do this, we do it before Veyra parades the king again."

"Aye," Rell said. "All of it. Though I warn you, the ceremony protocols alone will take till dawn, and I'm told I'm tedious."

"Oh good," Pip said, delighted. "He's *tedious*. Marlow, we've acquired a tedious ex-captain. This is the best day of my life."

Marlow, who had spent the entire vote looking like a man chewing a wasp, finally cracked — barely, at one corner of his mouth. "One condition of my own," he said. "You tell us about her. Beatrix. When we march. Not now. On the road, when it's long and ugly and we need reminding what we're carrying. You tell us who she was before the map."

Rell felt the crack in his chest shift again — not breaking wider, but setting, the way a bone sets, into something that would hold weight if not bear bending.

"On the road," he agreed. "Every mile of it."

Below them, the millwheel turned in the dark, patient as everything else in the world that didn't know a war was coming. Rell sat down among them — among *them*, among the outlaws and exiles and the magicless man, among the family of a dead woman who had once stolen his dinner and his dagger and his heart, in that order — and began, in a low and tedious voice, to describe the gates of the capital.

He did not see the rider who passed through Hallowmere an hour before dawn, splashing through puddles without slowing, bearing north at a gallop with a report sealed in shimmering ink.

But the report was already written. And somewhere ahead of them, in a tower above a sleeping city, a sorcerer-queen opened her eyes in the dark, and began, very quietly, to plan.
