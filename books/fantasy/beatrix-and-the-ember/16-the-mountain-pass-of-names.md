## 16. The Mountain Pass of Names

Pip had stolen his first name at seven years old, which was, he liked to think, the most honest way to acquire one.

Not stolen exactly. Borrowed. The tinker who'd passed through their village one autumn had called him "Pip" — a throwaway word, the kind of name you gave to a stray dog or a small boy underfoot — and Pip had decided on the spot that it fit better than the name his mother had shouted across the fields for years, a name attached to chores and disappointments and a father who'd left before the remembering got good. So he'd kept the tinker's word and left the rest behind, and Marlow had followed, because Marlow followed, that was the whole architecture of the universe.

Which was why, when Sera told them what the pass did, Pip laughed until his ribs hurt.

"It speaks your *name*," he said. "Out loud. To everyone."

"It speaks your true name," Sera corrected, tightening the strap on her pack. Her hair was coming loose from its braid again, silver-streaked and defiant, and she had the particular set to her jaw she got when she was about to be right at someone. "There's a difference. The mountain doesn't guess. It doesn't flatter. It says what you *are*."

"And what if what I am is a liar with excellent boots?"

"Then the mountain says that," Sera said, "and we all get to enjoy it."

Marlow snorted. "Finally. A mountain with taste."

They were camped at the foot of the pass in the grey hour before dawn, six of them and a mule named Duchess who had opinions about elevation. Behind them lay the salt flats and the memory of mage-hunters' spells sliding off Edmund like rain off glass. Ahead rose the Spine of Vael, and threading through it, the only route east that didn't end in Veyra's garrisons: a narrow stone throat the locals called the Pass of Names, though the locals, Marlow had reported darkly, also called it "the place where marriages end" and would not discuss it further.

Edmund sat apart from the fire, as he often did, turning his hands over like a man reading a letter written on his palms. He'd been quiet since the salt flats. Quieter than usual, and Edmund's usual was already the quiet of a man who'd spent thirty years talking to a gate.

Pip went and sat beside him. It was a thing he'd started doing somewhere on the road — attaching himself to Edmund like a burr — and he'd never examined why too closely, because examining things was Marlow's job. Pip's job was to be where the quiet was, and fill it.

"Nervous?" he asked.

"Should I be?"

"Well." Pip stretched his legs out and wiggled his boots, which were, he'd maintain under oath, excellent. "The mountain's going to say my true name in front of everyone, so yes. I'm planning to be nervous on your behalf as well. Consider it a service."

Edmund almost smiled. It was a small thing, the almost of it, but Pip had gotten good at counting Edmund's almosts the way other people counted coins.

"What do you think it'll say about me?" Edmund asked.

"Something boring and noble. 'Warden.' 'Dutiful.' 'Man Who Folds His Socks.'" Pip nudged him. "What do you think it'll say?"

Edmund was quiet a long moment. The fire cracked. Somewhere up the pass, wind moved through stone with a sound like a distant choir clearing its throat.

"I don't know," he said, and Pip heard the thing under it, the thing Edmund probably didn't know he was saying: *I've never been asked.*

* * *

The pass began gently, which Pip would later decide was the mountain's first cruelty. A wide gravel road wound up between grey walls of rock, easy walking, wildflowers nodding in the cracks — small white stars of them, stubborn as weeds. Duchess clip-clopped along with the smugness of a mule who believed herself on holiday. Odo led, hand never far from his sword out of habit, and Rell walked beside him in a silence that had, over the weeks, stopped being hostile and started being something almost companionable, the way two old dogs will lie in the same sun without acknowledging each other.

By midmorning the walls had narrowed and risen, and the light came down in a single pale ribbon, and that was when they heard the first voice.

It was very far away and very close at once — the way thunder is, the way a voice heard through water is. It said, in a tone of mild interest:

*"...she who counts what cannot be counted..."*

Sera stopped walking.

"That's not —" she began, and the voice came again, closer, resonant in the stone itself, and it said:

*"Sera of the Hedgerow. Sera Nine-Lives-Burned. Sera who loved before she learned to, and learned to before she dared."*

The company went very still.

"That's not my name," Sera said, to the mountain, in the voice of a woman arguing with weather. "My name is Sera. Just Sera. Sera of — of no fixed address, if we're being formal."

The mountain did not answer. The mountain, Pip thought, had the smugness of a mule and the memory of a ledger, and it moved on.

They walked. The voices came and went, drifting down the pass like birdsong, and each time they landed on one of them and peeled them open.

It said Odo was *"the shield that turned"* and Odo went grey and walked faster. It said Rell was *"the hound who bit the hand that fed him and found it had been feeding him lies"* — which was, Pip thought, the mountain showing off — and Rell said nothing at all, but his jaw worked like a man chewing glass. It called Marlow *"the twin who chose to be second, and made it a kingdom,"* and Marlow — Marlow, who never went quiet, whose mouth was a weapon he'd honed since birth — Marlow stopped dead in the trail and stared at the rock wall with his eyes shining, and Pip pretended very hard not to see, because that was the other half of his job.

Then the mountain found Pip.

He felt it coming. The air changed, the way it does before a name you've been dreading. And the voice said, warm as a hand on his head:

*"Pip. Little Seed. The one who was given nothing and decided to grow anyway. The laughter that keeps the dark honest. The youngest, who will not be the least."*

Pip's throat did something embarrassing.

"That's cheating," he managed. "I stole that name fair and square. You can't — you're supposed to say the real one, the boring one, the one with chores attached—"

*"You did steal it,"* the mountain agreed, sounding pleased. *"It was the first thing you ever chose. That is why it is true."*

Pip looked at Marlow. Marlow looked at him. Neither of them said anything, because there was nothing to say that the mountain hadn't already said better, and they walked on with their shoulders bumping, which was how they talked when words were too small.

* * *

The trouble started, as trouble reliably did, at the narrow place.

The pass pinched to a corridor scarce wide enough for Duchess, walls rising sheer on either side, the ribbon of sky a finger's width overhead. Echoes behaved strangely there. Every footfall came back doubled, tripled, as if the stone were rehearsing them.

And the mountain's voice came down into that corridor like a bell dropped into a well.

*"Edmund."*

Just that, first. Just the name, plain, and Pip felt Edmund flinch — because it *was* his name, wasn't it, the one he'd carried his whole life, the one the gate registers had written in a warden's careful hand. Edmund Yarrow. Thirty years of it.

The mountain considered him. Pip could feel it considering, the way you feel a storm deciding.

*"Edmund,"* it said again, slower, the way you'd repeat a word in a language you were learning. *"Edmund who is not—"*

The voice stopped.

The silence that followed had texture. Pip's skin prickled. The echoes in the corridor had gone wrong — not doubled but *held*, hanging in the air like breath held in a throat.

*"That's not right,"* Sera whispered.

*"Edmund who is called Edmund,"* the mountain said at last, carefully, like a child handling something fragile, *"who was given the name as one gives a coat to a man with no clothes. Under it. Beneath it. The name beneath the name—"*

The walls themselves seemed to lean in.

*"GATEKEEPER."*

The word didn't echo. It *landed*. It came off the stone with a weight that Pip felt in his teeth, in his back teeth specifically, which was a new and unpleasant experience, and Duchess — sensible Duchess — laid her ears flat and pressed herself against the wall as far from Edmund as the corridor allowed.

Edmund stood in the middle of the trail and did not move.

"Gatekeeper," Marlow repeated. "That's — all right, that's not a name, that's a job posting."

*"It is both,"* said the mountain.

"It's a *title*," Odo said, and his hand had come off his sword and was hovering, useless, the way hands do when there's nothing to fight. "Mountains don't hand out titles. Kings hand out titles. Who —"

He stopped. Pip watched him stop, watched the thought arrive and land and hurt.

"Who named him?" Odo finished quietly.

*"Not who,"* the mountain said. *"What. Not once. Many times. Generation upon generation. The name was set before he was born, as a lock is set before the door is hung. Edmund is the coat. Gatekeeper is the body wearing it."*

The wind moved through the pass. The wildflowers in the cracks nodded, small and white and indifferent.

Edmund turned around. He looked, Pip thought, like a man who had walked into his own house and found the furniture rearranged — everything familiar, nothing where he'd left it.

"That's not possible," Edmund said. His voice was level. His voice was *too* level, the way a table is too level when one leg is a folded piece of parchment. "My father was a warden. His father was a warden. We kept the gate. That's all. That's a job. You wake up, you check the wards, you sweep the threshold—"

*"You were made to keep,"* the mountain said, not unkindly, which was somehow worse. *"Made before you. Made in the blood. Ask the witch. Ask the hound. Ask the stone, which remembers what the valley was built to forget."*

And then, with the terrible good manners of a host who has said the unsayable and now offers tea, the mountain fell silent, and the echoes let go, and the pass was just a pass again, grey and cold and ordinary.

Nobody moved for a long time.

It was Pip, in the end, because it was always Pip, who crossed the corridor and stood next to Edmund and said, "Well. At least yours was free. I had to *steal* mine."

Edmund made a sound. It might have been a laugh. It was wet at the edges, whatever it was.

"I don't understand," Edmund said. "I'm *nobody*. That was the whole — that was the point of me. Magic slides off me. The Ember burns everyone but me. I'm the man the world forgot on purpose, and now you're telling me that was — that I was *designed*—"

"You're not nobody," Pip said. "You're the specific somebody the mountain just shouted about. There's a difference. Nobody doesn't get a title. I've been nobody. Nobody gets chores."

That got the laugh, the real one, small and cracked and real, and Pip counted it like a coin and put it somewhere safe.

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow where the walls opened just enough to hold a fire and a mule, and nobody talked about the name, and everybody talked about everything else, loudly, which was its own kind of talking about it.

Sera didn't talk at all. She sat with her ledger of herbs and notes open on her knees and didn't write anything, which for Sera was the equivalent of screaming. Pip watched her watch Edmund across the fire, and watched her watch the mountain above them, and thought: *she knows something.* Sera always knew something. It was her most annoying and most beloved feature.

It was near midnight when Edmund got up and walked a little way from the fire, up the trail, into the dark, and Pip — because it was always Pip — followed.

He found Edmund standing under the finger of sky, looking up at the stars, and Pip came and stood beside him and looked up too, because the stars up here were ridiculous, a spill of salt across black cloth, more of them than sky.

"Do you think it lied?" Edmund asked.

"The mountain? Mountains don't lie. Mountains don't do anything but be right at you for a million years."

"Then it's true." Edmund's breath fogged. "I'm not Edmund. I'm — a coat over something. A lock wearing a man's name."

"You're Edmund," Pip said. "I've met you. You fold your socks. You talk to Duchess like she's a colleague. You cried at Marlow's terrible poem about the salt flats and then denied it in front of witnesses." He bumped Edmund's shoulder with his own. "The mountain can shout whatever it likes. It's met you for ten minutes. I've met you for two months. I win."

Edmund was quiet. Then: "Made. It said I was *made*. Generations of it. Which means my father knew. My father's father. They all knew what I was for, and they handed me a broom and a gate and never said a word."

Pip considered this. Pip considered most things for about three seconds and then said them.

"Maybe they were scared," he said. "My mother knew what I was too. Knew I'd never fit in the village, never fit in the fields, knew I was a small loud wrong-shaped thing. And she never said a word about it either. Just handed me chores and hoped the wrong shape would wear off." He shrugged. "People don't keep secrets because they're cruel. Mostly they keep them because the truth is heavy and they don't want to watch you carry it. Doesn't make it right. Just makes it human."

"And the mountain?"

"The mountain's not human. The mountain's a gossip with geological patience." Pip grinned up at the stars. "Look — tomorrow we're down the far side, and the mountain can shout 'Gatekeeper' at the marmots all it wants. But tonight you're Edmund, and Edmund's freezing, and there's a fire down there with your name on it. Well. One of your names."

Edmund looked at him for a long moment. Something in his face was settling, the way silt settles in shaken water — not clear yet, but going to be.

"You're very good at this," Edmund said.

"At what?"

"Making heavy things carryable."

Pip felt his ears go warm and was grateful, for once, for the dark. "Somebody has to. Sera does the knowing, Odo does the worrying, Marlow does the talking, and I do the carrying." He turned back toward camp. "Come on, Gatekeeper. Your fire's going out."

Behind them, faint in the stone, so faint Pip could almost believe he'd imagined it, the mountain murmured — not to Edmund, not this time. To itself. A single word, repeated like a stone worrying a groove:

*"...keeper... keeper... keeper..."*

And down in the hollow, Sera closed her ledger without writing a single word, and looked into the fire, and did not sleep.
