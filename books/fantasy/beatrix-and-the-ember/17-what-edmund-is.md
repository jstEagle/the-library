## 17. What Edmund Is

The library at Thornhollow had been a granary once, before the war, and it still smelled faintly of barley. Sera liked that about it. Books always struck her as a kind of grain anyway — something you stored against winter, hoping it would still be good when you needed it.

She had been in the stacks since dawn, and it was well past noon when she found the first thread worth pulling.

*On the Wardenships of the Northern Valleys*, by some archivist whose name had been eaten by damp. The book was thin, bound in grey leather gone soft as cloth, shelved between a treatise on beekeeping law and a genealogy of minor houses long extinct. Nobody had touched it in decades; the spine cracked like a knuckle when Sera opened it.

She read the first page standing up. By the third page she was sitting on the floor with her skirts pooled around her and her heart going strangely quiet.

*It is a common error,* the archivist wrote, *to suppose that the valley gates were built to keep magic out.*

Sera's finger traced the line twice.

*They were built to keep a warden in — or rather, to keep everything else from reaching him. A gate is only as strong as its keeper is empty.*

"Empty," Sera said aloud, to nobody, in the way she did her best thinking. "That's a filthy word for it."

She read on.

---

She would have kept reading until dark if Pip hadn't come looking for her with two apples and an opinion.

"You've been down here all day," he said, dropping onto the floor beside her and offering an apple. His twin was off somewhere arguing with the innkeeper's wife about the price of oats, which meant Pip had been exiled for taking Marlow's side too loudly. "Odo says we leave tomorrow whether your book-reading's done or not. Also Edmund's been asking after you all afternoon, all casual-like, which means he isn't being casual at all."

"Mm." Sera took the apple without tasting it.

Pip squinted at the open book. "Is that about him?"

"It might be."

"The valley? The one he came out of?"

"Possibly."

Pip leaned closer. Sera tilted the page away from him on instinct, then caught herself and let him look. There wasn't much point in hiding it now. There hadn't been much point since the mountain pass, when the stones of that terrible place had called out their true names to each traveler who climbed them, and Edmund's name had come back wrong — not *Edmund Yarrow*, but *Gatekeeper*, in a voice like gravel rolling downhill.

Pip read for a moment. Then he sat back and said, very quietly for Pip, "Oh."

"Yes," said Sera.

"Oh," Pip said again. "So when the mountain said—"

"I don't know yet. That's why I'm reading."

"And the part where wardens are—" He stopped. Started again. "Bred?"

"That's the word they use."

Pip turned his apple over in his hands. For a boy who talked the way other people breathed, he could go silent like dropping a stone down a well. "Marlow and me, we were just born. Nobody made us anything. Our mum used to say we were accidents, but the fond kind."

"Somebody planned this," Sera said. "Generations of somebodies. Look." She turned pages toward him: ledgers, really, dressed up as scholarship. Lists of marriages arranged between warden families stretching back four hundred years. Notes on bloodlines. A cold little marginal sketch of a family tree pruned like an orchard. "Every child chosen for what they lack. Every marriage made to make sure the lack breeds true. They weren't raising people, Pip. They were raising locks."

Pip was quiet a moment longer. Then: "Does he know?"

"No."

"Are you going to tell him?"

Sera looked at the page. At the word *empty* again, underlined by some dead scholar's careful hand.

"He already knows half of it," she said. "He knows the mountain named him wrong. He knows magic slides off him. He knows the map burns everyone but him. He's been walking around the edges of this for weeks, pretending he doesn't notice the shape of the hole in the middle." She closed the book gently, the way you'd close a door on a sleeping child. "I'd rather he hear it from someone who'll be angry *with* him instead of at him."

---

She meant to tell him that evening. She got as far as finding him — down by the millpond, skipping stones with a skill that suggested a childhood with very little else to do — and then the words jammed in her throat like a badly cast charm.

Instead she sat down next to him and skipped a stone herself. Three skips. Respectable.

"Show-off," Edmund said.

"Hedge-witch. We're all frauds with good wrists." She watched the water settle. "I finished the research today."

He didn't ask what research. Of course he didn't. He'd known exactly what she was doing in that library, same as she'd known he knew, both of them circling it politely for days like dancers who'd forgotten the steps.

"And?" he said.

And there it was. She looked at him — this big, deliberate man who carried things because carrying things was what he understood, who had held a dead woman's body all night and dug her grave-marker himself with a borrowed spade — and every prepared speech she'd rehearsed fell apart.

"There's a lot of it," she said. "It deserves better than the pond version. Tomorrow. I promise. Tonight I'm going to buy everyone dinner and drink half a bottle of plum wine and be brave."

Edmund almost smiled. "Half a bottle."

"Courage is rationed."

But that night, courage failed to arrive on schedule. Dinner was loud — Odo telling some sanitized version of a palace story, Rell correcting it with grim precision, the twins reenacting a market swindle with such physical commitment that Pip knocked over a candle and Marlow laughed so hard he wheezed. Sera watched Edmund watch them. Watched him laugh at the right moments, eat steadily, help the innkeeper's girl stack plates without being asked. Watched the loneliness sit inside all that competence like a stone at the bottom of a clear stream.

He already suspected. That was the cruelty of it. You couldn't un-suspect a man, and every day she delayed was a day he spent alone with the shape of it.

So it was nearly midnight, and she was three-quarters through the plum wine, when she heard his boots on the library stairs.

---

She hadn't taken the book back to her room. That was the mistake, or the providence — she never decided which. It lay open on the table where she'd left it, lantern still burning, the family tree spread out like evidence at a trial.

Edmund stood in the doorway holding his own candle, and Sera saw his eyes find the book, find the sketch of the pruned orchard, find his own surname — *Yarrow* — written there seven times in seven generations.

"Sera." His voice was very even. Even voices, she had learned, were worse than shouting.

"I was going to tell you properly—"

"When?"

"Tomorrow."

"Tomorrow," he repeated. He came into the room slowly, the way he did everything, and set his candle down beside hers. He didn't touch the book. He looked at it the way you'd look at a snake you suspected was dead but weren't sure. "Read it to me. Please. I'd rather — I'd rather hear it in your voice than mine."

So Sera pulled out the chair across from him, and told him.

She told him about the founding of the wardenships, four centuries back, when the Ember was first bound to the crown and the realm's magicians had needed somewhere safe to put the things magic couldn't touch. Told him how they'd learned — God knew how, some early horror she chose not to read aloud — that the null gift ran in families, that it could be strengthened by breeding it pure, that a valley warded by a truly empty man became a place where no spell could cross, no scrying could see, no hunter could follow.

Told him that the wardens had believed, for a long time, that they were guarding the realm's prisoners.

Told him the archivist's correction, written in a later hand, bitter as alum: *The valley holds nothing. It never held anything. It is a lock built for a door that opens onto peace — and the wardens were never told, because men guard a prison fiercely and a paradise carelessly.*

Edmund listened with his hands flat on the table, absolutely still, the way he'd listened to Beatrix die.

"And the Ember," he said at last. "The map. Why me."

Sera turned to the passage she'd read eleven times, hating it more with each reading. "*The binding flame cannot be borne by any hand that magic has ever touched. It burns the gifted, maddens the strong, devours the ambitious. Only the utterly null may carry it — the living locks, bred for emptiness, the only hands in all the world the fire cannot grip.*"

Silence. The lantern hissed. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked — Rell, probably, who slept like a cat and patrolled like one.

"So," Edmund said. "My whole life."

"Your whole life," Sera agreed softly.

"They chose my grandmother's grandmother. And her husband. And every marriage after." He was doing arithmetic; she could see it moving behind his eyes, patient and terrible. "Nobody in my valley ever fell in love by accident, did they. It was all — husbandry."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. It's not yours." He rubbed his face with both hands. When they came down he looked older, and calmer, which frightened her more than grief would have. "You know the worst part? It explains everything. Why I never fit. Why the other children's games felt strange to me. Why my father taught me the wards like scripture and never once said *why*. We weren't a family. We were a mechanism. I was a hinge that thought it was a man."

"You're a man," Sera said, sharper than she intended. "Listen to me, Edmund Yarrow. Whatever they bred you *for*, they don't get to own what you *are*. I've known you eight weeks. In eight weeks you've carried a dead woman's errand across half a continent, saved six lives on the salt flats without lifting a finger, buried a stranger's body so a grieving boy wouldn't have to, and skipped stones better than anyone I've ever seen. That's not a hinge. Hinges don't choose."

He looked at her for a long moment. The lantern light moved between them.

"Beatrix knew," he said quietly. "Didn't she. When she pressed the map on me. She knew what I was."

"She must have. It's the only reason she ran *toward* your valley instead of anywhere else. The one place Veyra's magic couldn't follow. The one man who could hold what she carried." Sera hesitated. "She died choosing you, Edmund. Not using you. There's a difference, and she knew it."

"Did she?" His mouth twisted. "Because from where I'm sitting, everyone in this story keeps deciding what I'm for. The wardens. Beatrix. The map. Now the Ember itself, apparently, waiting for me like a keyhole waits." He stood abruptly, chair scraping. "I never asked to be the only one. I never asked to be *made* the only one. Do you understand what that does to a person? To learn your whole self was somebody's plan?"

"Yes," said Sera.

He stopped. Looked at her.

"My mother bound my magic before I could walk," she said. "Not enough to kill the gift — just enough to keep me small, safe, ignorable. Hedge-witch. Half-talented. Harmless. My whole life I wondered what I might have been if she'd let me grow straight instead of pruning me to fit her fear." She smiled, and it cost her. "So yes. I know what it is to discover you were shaped by someone else's hands. And I know the only cure I ever found."

"Which is?"

"Deciding what your hands do next." She stood too, and closed the grey book, and put her palm flat on its cover. "They bred a lock. Locks don't get to decide. But you're not in the valley anymore, Edmund. You walked out the gate. Everything from here forward is yours."

For a moment she thought he would argue. She almost hoped he would; anger was easier to carry than that stillness of his.

Instead he nodded, once, slow as a gate swinging shut — or open. "Tomorrow we ride for the capital road."

"Tomorrow we ride."

He took up his candle and went to the stairs, then paused, his back to her.

"Sera. The salt flats. When the hunters' spells broke around me — you looked at me afterward like you'd seen something."

"I did."

"What?"

She considered lying. Considered gentleness. Chose truth, because he'd earned it and because the wine had burned through her caution. "I saw what the Ember must feel like," she said. "Standing next to you. Like a storm meeting a wall of glass — everything sliding off, nothing getting through, all that fury with nowhere to go." She swallowed. "Veyra's power is going to break against you the same way. I think that's what you're *for*, in the end. Not the carrying. The standing."

Edmund was quiet on the stairs. Then, without turning: "Get some sleep, hedge-witch. Tomorrow sounds like it's going to hurt."

His boots faded up the steps. Sera sat alone with the lantern and the closed book and the plum wine, and did not sleep.

Because she hadn't told him everything. There was one more entry in the archivist's appendix, one she'd read eleven times and memorized despite herself — a fragment of older text, quoted secondhand, about what happened when the Ember finally met the hands it could not burn.

*The flame cannot grip the null,* the old words ran. *But neither can the null release it. What the living lock takes up, he holds forever — and the fire, denied all other fuel, will feed on the only thing he has.*

The archivist's gloss beneath it, in faded brown ink:

*His years.*

Sera looked at the lantern flame for a long time. Then she snuffed it, sat in the dark, and began — very quietly, so no one would hear — to plan how to stand between Edmund Yarrow and the thing he was born for.
