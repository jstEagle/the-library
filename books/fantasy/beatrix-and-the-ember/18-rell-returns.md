## 18. Rell Returns

Odo Vane had spent twenty years learning to read the language of trouble, and trouble had a grammar all its own. A snapped twig meant nothing. A bird going quiet meant something. But the sound that woke him on the third night out of the Mountain Pass of Names — the soft, wet drag of a body forcing itself through bracken — that was trouble's whole sentence, spoken plainly, with no room for misinterpretation.

He was on his feet with his sword drawn before his eyes finished opening.

The fire had burned down to coals. Sera slept sitting up, her chin tucked to her chest, one hand still curled around her spellbook like a child with a favorite toy. Pip and Marlow were a tangle of limbs and shared blankets, as they'd been since birth, presumably. Edmund lay on his back with his arms at his sides, sleeping the flat, untroubled sleep of a man who could not be hexed in his dreams. Odo envied him that. His own dreams were still full of the palace, full of doors he'd opened when he should have let them stay shut.

The dragging sound came again, from the treeline to the east.

"Company," Odo said quietly. "Up."

Sera's eyes opened instantly — she'd been a hedge-witch too long to sleep deeply outdoors — and she read his face in a heartbeat. Her fingers moved through a small gesture, and a thread of light kindled between them, ready to become whatever it needed to be.

Pip surfaced from sleep with a snort. "Is it breakfast?"

"It's trouble."

"Then I'll have both," Marlow mumbled into his pillow.

Odo stepped toward the trees, sword low, and said, in the voice he'd once used to halt deserters on the palace walls: "Come out slow, hands where I can see them."

A long pause. Then, from the dark, a voice roughened almost past recognition:

"Vane. If you're going to run me through, do it quickly. I haven't the strength to argue about it."

Odo's stomach dropped through the ground and kept falling.

Rell came out of the bracken the way a ship comes off a reef — badly, in pieces, leaving things behind. He was in his captain's blacks still, but they were torn from shoulder to hip, crusted black with old blood and new. His left arm hung wrong. His face — that handsome, closed-off face that Odo had spent years studying across guard rotations, trying to work out what Beatrix Ash had ever seen in it — was a ruin of bruise and scab, one eye swollen nearly shut.

He took two more steps and then his legs simply resigned their commission, and Odo caught him under the arms before he hit the ground.

"Heavier than you look," Odo grunted.

"You're fatter than you look," Rell rasped. "We've all changed."

* * *

They laid him by the fire, and Sera went to work on him with the brisk, unsentimental tenderness she brought to all wounds, human or otherwise. She clicked her tongue over the arm. She clucked over the gash along his ribs. When she peeled back the bandage he'd tied himself — badly, with a soldier's knots and no healer's sense — she made a noise of pure professional offense.

"This was done three days ago."

"Four," Rell said.

"And you *walked*?"

"I ran for two of them." He paused, reconsidering. "Crawled for part of one. There was a ditch."

Pip crouched nearby, watching with the frank fascination of a boy who had never seen a man of the Queen's Own look anything but invincible. "Who did this to you? Bandits?"

"The Queen's Own," Rell said. "I'm told I'm a bandit now. It's very efficient of them. They promoted me and outlawed me in the same breath."

Marlow, who had appointed himself keeper of the company's provisions and guarded them like a dragon, appeared at Odo's elbow holding the good cheese and an expression of deep suspicion. "Do we feed traitors?"

"Do we feed guests?" Sera countered, not looking up from her needlework.

"He tried to kill us."

"He *asked* for the map," Pip corrected. "Politely, even. Well — sort of politely. In a captain-ish way."

"He cornered us alone in a gorge with crossbowmen on the ridge," Marlow said.

"Yes, but he didn't *shoot*," said Pip. "There's a difference. I've been shot at enough to know the difference."

Odo let them argue. It was, he'd learned, how this family processed everything: grief, joy, terror, supper. They argued the way other households prayed. And underneath it, he watched Rell watch them — watched the man track each voice, each face, with that single working eye, cataloguing them the way he'd once catalogued threats, except now there was something else in the look. Something hungrier. Rell was watching people sit around a fire and bicker about cheese, and Odo would have bet his pension — the one he'd forfeited along with everything else — that no one had sat around any fire with Rell in a very long time.

Edmund came and sat down across from the wounded man, unhurried, and simply looked at him.

It was a thing Odo had seen him do now perhaps a dozen times, and it never lost its strangeness. Edmund Yarrow had no magic in him at all — less than none; magic slid off him like rain off glass — and yet when he fixed that plain, patient gaze on someone, mages twice his age found themselves confessing things. Perhaps it was because he expected nothing. Everyone else in the world wanted something from you. Edmund just looked, and waited, and the truth got embarrassed into coming out.

Rell lasted under that gaze about as long as anyone ever did.

"She sent me to bring back your head," he said. "Or the map. Preferably both. I told her I'd need time. I told her I'd follow you past the pass, past the flats, wherever you went." His jaw worked. "She gave me six hunters to make certain I followed through. Good ones. People I trained." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as a snapped bowstring. "They were supposed to make sure I didn't go soft. Instead they made sure I couldn't walk. I killed two of them before they brought me down. The others went back to report. By now she knows exactly where I am, which means by tomorrow she'll know exactly where *you* are, because I led them straight to your trail like the loyal hound I was."

Silence around the fire.

"So," Rell said. He pushed himself up on his good arm, wincing, until he was half-sitting, facing them all with the ruined dignity of a man presenting himself for judgment. "That's my gift to you, freely given: I am a walking beacon, and every hunter in Veyra's service will be pointed at this camp within a day. Kill me if you like. It would be tidy. I'd even understand it." His eye found Odo's. "But before you do — I know where the Ember is. Not guessed. *Known.* And I'm the only soul left alive outside the vault who does."

* * *

They withdrew to confer, which is to say they walked thirty paces away and argued in fierce whispers while Rell sat by the fire pretending, unconvincingly, not to listen.

"He's bait," Marlow said flatly. "Worse than bait. He's bait that *talks*. Veyra let him live because a wounded Rell crawling after us is worth ten trackers. We take him in, we take her hook in our own backs."

"That's paranoid," Pip said.

"We are being hunted across a continent by a sorcerer-queen. Paranoia is a *job requirement*."

"He saved us at the gorge," Pip insisted. "He told the crossbowmen to hold. He could have taken the map off Edmund's cooling corpse and been home by supper."

"He hesitated once," Marlow said. "People hesitate. Then they remember their pensions."

"He doesn't *have* a pension anymore, that's rather the point—"

"Enough." Sera's voice was quiet, which meant it was serious. She looked at Odo. "You've known him longest. You served with him. Say your piece."

And there it was, laid at his feet. Odo looked back toward the fire, at the man slumped against his saddle with his broken arm splinted in hedge-witch linen, and felt twenty years of palace corridors rearrange themselves in his chest.

"I'll tell you what I know," Odo said slowly. "Rell Dain was the finest officer I ever served beside, and I hated him for most of it. He was Aldric's shadow. Where the king walked, Rell stood three paces behind, and he stood there like a wall stands — like nothing in the world could move him. When Veyra's influence spread through the guard, officer by officer, Rell was the last to bend. The *last*, do you hear me? Men I'd have sworn oaths on folded inside a season. Rell held for two years." He paused. "I always wondered why. Now I think I know. You don't hold that long against a sorceress unless there's a reason stronger than duty. And there was only ever one reason Rell Dain had for anything."

"Beatrix," Sera said softly.

"Beatrix." Odo's throat tightened on the name, as it still did, as it probably always would. "When she vanished, something in him went with her. I saw it happen. He stopped arguing in council. Stopped eating properly. Started taking the assignments nobody else wanted — hunting smugglers, chasing rumors, anything far from the capital. He was looking for her. All those years, wearing the queen's black, he was looking for *her*." Odo shook his head. "And then he finds her trail at last, follows it to a gorge, and learns she's dead. That the map she died carrying is in the hands of a farmer and a pack of outlaws." He met their eyes, one by one. "You want to know if he can be trusted. I'll tell you what I trust: I trust that a man who loved Beatrix Ash will not hand her life's work to the woman who caged a king. Whatever else is broken in him, that part isn't. That part is load-bearing."

Marlow crossed his arms. "Load-bearing sentiment. Wonderful. Shall we build the war effort on it?"

"We built this company on less," Sera said.

They turned, as one, to Edmund — because they always did, eventually, though none of them liked to admit it. The gatekeeper had been listening with his hands folded, wearing the expression he wore when he was thinking about gates.

"In the valley," Edmund said, "we had a saying about locks. A lock isn't strong because it's complicated. It's strong because it was *made* to hold. Somebody built it for that purpose, on purpose, down to the smallest pin." He nodded toward the fire. "That man was built to hold. I saw it the moment he demanded the map instead of just taking it. A man truly gone bad doesn't ask. He takes, and apologizes to no one." A small, tired smile. "Also, he's bleeding on our only blanket, so somebody should decide soon."

* * *

Odo went back alone and crouched by the fire across from Rell. For a moment neither spoke. The coals ticked. Somewhere in the dark, an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

"When I left the palace," Odo said at last, "you signed the warrant for my arrest personally. Your own signature. I keep thinking about that."

"I remember."

"You could have refused. Let someone else sign it."

"If I'd refused, Veyra would have known I was unreliable, and she'd have put someone competent on your trail instead of me." Rell's mouth twitched, which on his ruined face was nearly a grin. "I signed it and then lost the paperwork for four months. Clerical error. Terrible shame. The archives are a shambles these days."

Odo stared at him. Then he laughed — a short, startled bark of laughter, the first in longer than he could say. "You magnificent bastard. Four months?"

"Five. I filed it under 'V' for Vane instead of 'Y' for Yarrow. No one could find it. It was tragic."

"You're telling me I owe my freedom to alphabetization."

"You owe your freedom to the fact that the palace clerks are idiots, yes. Take it up with them."

The laughter faded, and what replaced it was quieter and harder to say. Odo looked into the fire. "Beatrix used to talk about you. Did you know that? Back when it was just the five of us, before the twins, before any of it. She'd get a far look and say, 'There's a man in the palace who'd burn the whole thing down if I asked him to, and that's exactly why I never will.'" He shook his head. "I thought she was flattering herself. She wasn't, was she."

"No," Rell said. His voice had gone rough. "She wasn't."

"She'd have hated seeing you like this."

"She'd have laughed at me. Then she'd have fed me something and called me an idiot in at least two languages, and by morning I'd have believed things were fixable." He closed his eye. "Everything I did, I did half-hoping she'd see it and half-knowing she wouldn't approve of any of it. Now she's dead and I finally know which half mattered." He opened his eye and looked at Odo directly, and there was no captain in it anymore, no queen's shadow, just a man asking. "So. Do I crawl back to her killers, or do I die among her friends? Because those are the roads left, Vane, and I'm too tired to pretend there's a third."

Odo stood. He looked at the faces arrayed behind him — Sera's careful hope, the twins' matched stubbornness, Edmund's calm — and then he put his hand on Rell's good shoulder, the way you steady a man, the way you claim one.

"Third road," Odo said. "You live. You heal. You teach us everything you know about that vault, every corridor, every ward, every trick of Veyra's guard — and when we go in, you go in *with* us, not ahead of us and not behind. And when it's done, if we're still breathing, you help me rebuild whatever's worth rebuilding out of the mess we both helped make. Those are my terms."

"That's not sanctuary," Rell said hoarsely. "That's a job."

"It's a family," Odo said. "The job comes free."

Behind him, Pip raised his hand. "For the record, I voted for this hours ago."

"You voted for breakfast," Marlow said.

"I contain multitudes."

* * *

They moved camp before dawn, because Rell was right about the beacon he carried, and Sera worked a masking weave over their trail that cost her a headache and half her temper. Rell rode double with Odo, too weak to sit his own saddle, and complained about it the entire morning with such thoroughness that Odo began to suspect the complaining was how he stayed upright.

By midday, the man had already given them more than they'd won in months of guessing: the vault's location beneath the throne room, the blood-warded stair, the rotation of the Ember Guard, the fact — delivered grimly — that Veyra kept the king close, always close, paraded on feast days like a trophy that might otherwise forget its purpose.

"Aldric's alive, then," Odo said quietly. "Truly alive."

"Alive," Rell said. "I don't know what else to call it. I stood post outside his door a hundred nights. He never spoke. He never slept like a man sleeps. He just... waited, with his eyes open." His jaw tightened. "Whatever she's done to him, it's worse than death. Death gets a funeral."

They crested a ridge in the grey afternoon light, and below them the land fell away toward the distant smudge of the south — toward the capital, toward the towers Odo had once called home and now could hardly bear to imagine. Rell reined in beside him and looked at it a long moment.

"There's something else," Rell said, so quietly that only Odo heard it. "Something I haven't told the others. About the vault. About what's guarding the Ember besides guards." He turned, and his one good eye was bleak. "The final door doesn't answer to strength or to spells. It answers to royal blood. Only royal blood opens it, and Veyra sees through every drop of hers in the kingdom like windows in a lit house." He paused. "Except one. One man whose blood the door remembers, and whose mind she cannot touch — because he has no magic for her to grip. I checked the old registries before I left the palace. I checked them twice."

Odo felt the cold arrive slowly, the way winter does.

"Rell," he said carefully. "What are you telling me?"

Below them, oblivious, Edmund was helping Pip chase an escaped sock in the wind, laughing — actually laughing — in the thin grey light.

"I'm telling you," Rell said, "that the door at the bottom of that vault has been waiting for our gatekeeper his whole life. And I don't think he's going to like knowing it."
