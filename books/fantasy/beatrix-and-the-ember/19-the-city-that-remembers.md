## 19. The City That Remembers

Marlow had a rule about ruins: never sleep in one that still smelled of smoke. Smoke meant the fire was recent, and recent fires meant someone had lit them, and anyone who lit fires in a dead city was either a survivor or a scavenger, and both would cut your purse as soon as look at you.

The city ahead of them smelled of smoke that was forty years old.

It hung in the air like a memory nobody had bothered to bury — woodsmoke and burnt thatch and something sweeter underneath, something Marlow couldn't name until they passed through the broken western gate and he realized it was bread. Old bread. The ghost of a bakery that had been ash before Pip was born.

"Charming," Sera said, pulling her cloak tighter. "Smells like breakfast."

"It smells like a funeral," Odo said.

Marlow loved Odo for this — the man's gift for saying the grim thing out loud so nobody else had to carry it around unspoken. It was a service, really. Like carrying the heavy pack.

The city had been called Vellinmark once. Marlow knew it from his mother's stories: a river town, wealthy from wool and dye, famous for its bell tower that rang in seven-part harmony on festival days. His mother had danced there once, she claimed, at a wedding, back when dancing in Vellinmark was a thing a person could do without dying.

Now the bell tower stood with its top third sheared off, leaning against its own base like a drunk uncle at a family gathering. The streets were cobbled and cracked, furred with moss, and the houses gaped open to the sky where roofs had burned or rotted. Weeds grew in the doorways. A cat — an actual living cat, which felt almost obscene — watched them from a windowsill with the flat yellow stare of something that had made its peace with the world's ending.

"There," Edmund said quietly. He pointed to a long stone building near the center of town, its roof mostly intact, its windows dark. "That looks like it might have been a granary. Stone walls. One entrance."

"Always the gatekeeper," Sera said, but there was no mockery in it. There hadn't been much mockery in anything since the salt flats, since the night Edmund had stood in the middle of a mage-hunter's spellstorm and simply *refused*, and the magic had slid off him like rain off glass while the rest of them huddled behind rocks wondering if they were about to die.

They made camp in the granary as the light went amber and then grey. Odo built a small fire because small fires were safe and large fires were invitations, and Sera laid out the dried meat and the last of the Correnvale hardtack, which tasted like sweetened sawdust and had been the company's main sustenance long enough that Marlow had begun having complicated feelings about sawdust.

Pip sat close to him, shoulder to shoulder, the way they'd sat since they were children. Marlow could feel his brother's silence like a weight. Since Correnvale, since the council's price, Pip had gone quiet in a way that wasn't his usual quiet. His usual quiet was thinking-quiet, scheming-quiet, the quiet of a boy composing a prank. This was different. This was the quiet of someone doing arithmetic with their own mortality.

Marlow nudged him. "Bet you I can find a working well in this town."

"No bet," Pip said. "You'll just cheat."

"I never cheat. I *improvise*."

"You pushed me down a well once."

"That was improvisation with unforeseen consequences." Marlow grinned, and after a moment Pip grinned back, thin but real, and Marlow counted it a victory and moved on.

It was full dark when the bells began to ring.

* * *

Marlow woke to sound — seven bells, seven voices, ringing in harmony across the ruined town, and his first thought was that some other traveler had found the tower, and his second thought was that no traveler alive could ring bells in seven-part harmony, because that took seven ringers, and his third thought arrived when he looked out the granary window and saw the street below filling with people.

They were faint. That was the first thing. Faint as breath on cold glass, translucent as smoke given posture. They walked the street in festival clothes, in work clothes, in aprons and cloaks and fine dyed wool — Vellinmark's famous dyes, blues and crimsons that even now, even faded into transparency, glowed softly against the night. They carried lanterns that gave off no light. They carried loaves of bread. Children ran between the adults' legs, playing a game with a hoop, and the hoop rolled through a fallen cart and kept rolling, because to the children there was no cart.

"They can't see us," Sera whispered. She was at the window beside Marlow, her face pale in the ghost-light. "Or — they can't see *now*. They're not here. They're somewhere else."

"Where?" Pip asked.

"Before," Sera said. "This is before."

The bells finished their harmony and began again, and the street filled further, and Marlow understood with a slow, sickening drop that he was watching the last festival day of Vellinmark. The day before the end. The ghosts didn't know what was coming. They were baking bread and dyeing wool and running hoops through streets that still had carts in them, and above them all the bell tower stood whole, and the sky over the ghost-city was clear.

Then the sky changed.

It happened all at once, everywhere, the way weather does in dreams. The clear ghost-sky bruised purple, then black, and the festival crowd faltered, faces turning upward, and far away — impossibly far, at the edge of hearing — came a sound like the world's largest door being opened very slowly.

"The sacking," Odo said. He had come up beside them without a sound, the way he did everything. His voice was flat, controlled, the voice of a man holding himself together with discipline alone. "I've read accounts. Veyra's armies took Vellinmark in the second year of the war. It resisted a tax decree. She made an example of it."

"An example," Sera repeated.

"She burned it for three days."

Below, the festival was dissolving. The ghosts were running now, though they didn't seem to know why yet — the panic spreading through them like ink through water, mothers grabbing children, bakers dropping loaves. And down the great eastern road came the army.

Marlow had seen soldiers. He'd seen Veyra's riders up close, had run from them, had hidden from them. But these were ghosts of soldiers, and ghosts apparently had no need of numbers, because the column that poured down the eastern road looked endless — rank after rank after rank, in armor that smoked with a darkness that wasn't shadow, and above them floated things Marlow had no name for, shapes like torn banners that moved against the wind.

And at the front of the column, on a black horse, rode a woman in white.

Even faded, even translucent, she was the most terrible thing Marlow had ever seen. She didn't ride so much as *arrive*, again and again, at every point of the battle at once. Where she looked, buildings caught fire. Where she raised one hand, a section of the city wall simply folded inward, gently, like paper crumpled by a bored child.

"That's her," Pip breathed. "That's actually—"

"Don't watch her," Odo said sharply. "Watch the people. Watch what happens to the people. That's the part you need to carry."

Marlow wanted to ask why he needed to carry any part of this, but then he saw the baker.

The baker from the festival crowd — a broad man with flour on his apron — had run back into his shop. Through the ghost-walls, transparent as everything else, Marlow could see him hauling his family toward the cellar: a wife, two daughters, a grandmother who moved too slowly. The grandmother fell in the doorway. The baker went back for her. And the fire came down the street like a living thing, following the woman in white's pointing finger, and it reached the bakery, and the baker gathered his grandmother up and turned for the cellar—

—and the roof came down.

The ghosts didn't scream. That was the worst part. Marlow had expected screaming; screaming you could brace against. But the ghosts of Vellinmark made almost no sound at all, only the bells, which kept ringing, kept ringing, even as the city burned around them, as if the tower itself refused to stop marking time just because time had stopped meaning anything.

And then, when the flames had done their work, the scene changed.

It changed the way a page turns. One moment the burning city; the next, the aftermath — the same street, the same walls, but cold and black and silent, and the ghosts walking it again, except now they were grey, and slow, and they wandered among the ashes looking for things. Looking for each other. A woman stood in the ruin of the bakery, turning in circles, calling a name — no sound came out, but Marlow could see the shape of the name in the way her mouth moved, over and over, *calling, calling*. A child sat in the middle of the street where the hoop had rolled, waiting for a game that would never resume.

"They don't know," Sera said. Her voice had gone rough. "They're stuck. They replay it every night — the festival, the fire, and then... this. Searching. Forever."

"How long?" Edmund asked. He had been silent through all of it, standing at the window with his arms crossed tight over his chest, and when Marlow glanced at him he saw a man watching something he couldn't look away from and couldn't bear.

"Forty years," Odo said. "Every night, for forty years."

* * *

Nobody slept after that. They sat around the little fire and drank tea Sera made from pine needles, which tasted like punishment but was hot, and for a long time no one spoke.

It was Pip, finally, who said what they were all thinking. "That's what happens. If we fail. Every city. All of them. Forever."

"Not forever," Sera said. "Nothing's forever. But long enough. Long enough that it doesn't matter how you count it."

"And we knew that," Pip said. His voice had an edge Marlow rarely heard in it. "We knew that when we voted in Correnvale. We knew that when we let them use Edmund as bait. We sat in a nice warm council chamber and we *haggled*." He laughed, and it was an awful sound. "While places like this existed."

"Pip," Marlow started.

"No. I'm serious. I've been thinking about it since the salt flats. We keep acting like this is a quest. Like it's a story with a map and a prize at the end. But it's not. It's a war, and wars are just this—" he gestured at the window, at the faint grey shapes still wandering the street outside, "—repeated until somebody stops it. And we're the somebodies. Us. Five idiots and a man who can't do magic."

"Six," Edmund said.

Everyone looked at him.

"Six idiots," Edmund said. "You always forget to count yourself, Pip. You're not exempt."

There was a pause, and then Pip laughed — a real laugh this time, startled out of him, wet-eyed — and Marlow felt the terrible tension in the room loosen by one degree. Only one degree. But Marlow had spent his whole life learning exactly how much a degree was worth.

Edmund set down his tea. "I guarded a gate my whole life," he said slowly, "because I was told the world outside wasn't mine to worry about. My job was the valley. The valley's peace. Nothing else." He looked at the window, at the searching ghosts. "I think I understood duty. I don't think I ever understood stakes. Not like this." He paused. "In the valley, failure meant frost-blighted crops. Here—" He shook his head. "Here failure has a face. A lot of faces. That baker went back for his grandmother. That's the whole thing, isn't it? That's what we're actually carrying the map for. So that going back for someone is still possible."

Sera was looking at him with an expression Marlow recognized and found deeply inconvenient, because it was the same expression Marlow suspected he occasionally wore, and because the twins had a silent agreement never to acknowledge it.

"Well," Marlow said loudly, "when we win, I'm opening a bakery."

"A bakery," Pip said.

"Vellinmark was famous for its bread. Somebody should honor that. A memorial bakery. Free rolls on festival days. Seven kinds."

"Six kinds," Pip said. "One kind should be sawdust, in honor of Correnvale hardtack."

"You're a traitor to the crown of my future enterprise."

Odo cleared his throat, and the twins subsided, because Odo clearing his throat was a sound with authority behind it. "Tomorrow we cross the river gorge north of here," he said. "Two days' travel puts us at the mountain passes. After that, the capital's shadow. Rest tonight. All of you. Even if you can't sleep, rest."

But Marlow did sleep, eventually, in the way of the young and the exhausted — and dreamed of bread, warm from the oven, in a city that stood whole under a clear sky.

* * *

He woke before dawn to a sound that didn't belong: footsteps in the granary, soft and deliberate, moving toward the door.

Marlow was on his feet with a knife in his hand before his mind fully caught up — a trick their mother had drilled into them, *wake armed or don't wake* — and found Edmund standing at the doorway, looking out at the street.

"Sorry," Edmund said, without turning. "Didn't mean to wake you."

"You didn't. The floor did. It's got opinions." Marlow lowered the knife. "What are you doing?"

"Watching them."

Marlow joined him at the door. Outside, the grey ghosts were still wandering — thinner now, fewer, as dawn approached, as whatever held them here loosened its grip for another day. The woman who called the name was still there, turning in circles by the bakery ruin, her mouth shaping its silent word.

Edmund watched her the way he watched everything: steadily, completely, as if the thing deserved his whole attention.

"When Beatrix died," he said, "she told me to carry the map. Not to wield it. Just carry it. And I've been trying to understand the difference ever since." He nodded at the ghost woman. "I think I'm starting to. Carrying means remembering. Wielding means deciding it was worth it." His jaw tightened. "I don't want to decide her death was worth it. I want to make sure it wasn't wasted. Those aren't the same thing."

Marlow considered that. "My ma used to say grief and purpose are the same animal wearing different coats."

"Your mother sounds wise."

"She also ate dirt once on a bet, so, you know. Wisdom with asterisks."

Edmund smiled — small, tired, real. Then his gaze sharpened, fixed on something past the ghost woman, past the broken rooftops, out along the eastern road.

Marlow followed his eyes.

On the eastern road, where the ghost-army had marched, something else was moving. Not ghosts. Living men, in the grey-and-black livery of Veyra's regulars, maybe two dozen, moving with the unhurried confidence of soldiers sweeping a town they believed empty. And at their head rode a rider whose armor caught the pre-dawn light — and whose helm, as it turned slowly toward the granary, seemed to find them instantly, as if drawn by the heat of their dead fire.

"Edmund," Marlow said quietly. "Wake the others."

Edmund was already moving.
