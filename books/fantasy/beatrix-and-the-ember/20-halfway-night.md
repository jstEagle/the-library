## 20. Halfway Night

The valley they found had no name on any map Sera owned, which she said was the highest recommendation a place could have. It sat in the crook of two hills like water pooled in a cupped hand — a meadow of tall grass gone gold with autumn, a stream running cold and quick over pale stones, and at its center an orchard so old that no one alive remembered who had planted it. The trees still bore fruit: small, hard apples, green-gold and tart as a reprimand.

"Halfway," Odo announced, dropping his pack in the grass with the groan of a man setting down a decade. "By my reckoning we've come half the distance to the capital, and I say we stop here tonight and pretend the other half doesn't exist until morning."

"We could make another six miles before dark," Rell said.

"We could," Odo agreed, "and then tomorrow we'd be six miles further along and just as tired. Whereas if we stop now—" he spread his arms at the orchard, the stream, the long honey-colored light coming down through the leaves, "—we get *this*."

Rell looked at the meadow the way he looked at most things: as though calculating sightlines and escape routes. Then his shoulders came down half an inch, which for Rell was practically surrender. "One night."

"One night," Odo said solemnly, "of doing absolutely nothing useful."

Edmund set down his pack and found that his knees had opinions about the day's march, all of them complaints. He lowered himself into the grass beside the pack and let the ground hold him. Above him the apple leaves stirred, and light came through them in coins, and for a moment he simply breathed.

It was Pip who named it. He came wading back from the stream with his boots slung around his neck and his trousers rolled to the knee, hair dripping, grinning like the war was something happening in another country. "Halfway Night!" he declared. "That's what this is. We're halfway. Halfway nights are for being happy, Marlow used to say. You have to be happy on halfway nights or the road knows you're ungrateful and gets longer."

"Marlow said that?" Edmund asked gently.

Pip's grin flickered — grief did that now, surfacing and sinking like a fish under ice — but it held. "Marlow said everything. But this one's true." He flopped into the grass beside Edmund, wrung out his socks with great ceremony, and added, "Also there are apples. That helps."

* * *

They built a fire small enough to be safe and large enough to matter, and Sera cooked.

This was, Edmund had learned, an event. Sera approached cooking the way she approached hedge-craft: with intense concentration, muttered incantations that were mostly swear words, and results that varied between transcendent and mildly alarming. Tonight she had gathered apples, wild onions, a rabbit Odo had snared with an air of deep apology, and a paper packet of spices she'd been hoarding since Correnvale "for an occasion."

"It's not an occasion," Rell pointed out, watching her work.

"Halfway Night is an occasion," Sera said, chopping with more force than the onions deserved. "Pip says so, and Pip is the only one among us with any sense of ritual left. The rest of you are barbarians with swords."

"I don't have a sword," Edmund said.

"You're exempt. You're a civilian. You're practically a mascot."

"I was a gatekeeper for thirty years. I've been called worse than mascot."

She glanced up at him then, firelight catching the edge of her smile, and Edmund felt the look land somewhere beneath his ribs and stay there. This had been happening for weeks now — these small detonations, these moments where a glance or a laugh went through him like warmth through cold hands. He had spent his whole life alone in a valley where nothing changed except the weather, and he had thought himself immune to wanting. It turned out he had merely never been offered anything.

The stew, when it came, was transcendent. Even Rell said so, in the tone of a man admitting to a crime.

They ate until they were stupid with it, and then the evening arranged itself the way good evenings do, without anyone deciding. Odo produced a bottle from the bottom of his pack — where, Edmund wondered, had he been keeping a bottle through ambushes and river crossings? — and poured measures for everyone, even Pip, whose measure was one part wine to nine parts watered honey and who drank it with the gravity of a general.

"To Marlow," Odo said, raising his cup.

"To Marlow," they echoed, and drank, and the silence afterward was the good kind, the kind with a person in it rather than a hole.

Then Odo told stories. That was the other thing about Odo: give him wine and a fire and he became the archive of every battle, every bad decision, every absurd catastrophe of his twenty years in the royal guard. He told about the siege of Harrow Fen, where he and his fellows had held a bridge for three days against a rebel company that turned out to be forty farmers and a very determined goose. He told about the time he'd been assigned to guard a visiting dignitary who turned out to be a smuggler wearing the dignitary's clothes, and how Odo had saluted him eleven times before anyone noticed.

"And who noticed?" Pip demanded, delighted.

"Beatrix." Odo's voice went soft around the name, the way it always did, but easier now than it had been in the early weeks. "Of course Beatrix. She walked up to this man, this *dignitary*, bowed beautifully, and pickpocketed his seal ring while shaking his hand. Then she showed it to me and said, 'Your prisoner, I believe.' I should have arrested her. Instead I resigned my commission eight months later and followed her into the hills, and it remains the only sensible decision of my life."

"You were there too?" Edmund asked Rell. "At Harrow Fen?"

Rell was quiet a moment, turning his cup. "Opposite side of the bridge," he said. "I was the one who eventually worked out the rebels were farmers. Reported it up the chain. Nobody thanked me; the officers wanted their victory." He looked across the fire at Odo. "You were the one laughing at us. From the middle of the bridge. I remember thinking, *that man is going to die of joy someday.*"

"He nearly did," Sera said. "Several times."

Odo laughed, big and unguarded, and raised his cup again. "To the enemy," he said. "May they all turn out to be farmers."

Rell hesitated — Edmund watched him hesitate, watched the old iron discipline fight the pull of the fire and the wine and the years — and then Rell lifted his cup and touched it, across the flames, to Odo's. "To the bridge," he said quietly.

Something passed between them then that Edmund couldn't parse fully, some ledger of old wounds finally balanced. They had served on opposite sides of a dozen skirmishes before Rell had ever heard Beatrix's name; they had spent the first weeks of this journey circling each other like dogs unsure of the rules. But tonight, over the ghost of a goose-related military disaster, the circling stopped. Afterward they sat shoulder to shoulder arguing amiably about cavalry tactics until the fire burned low, and nobody needed to say that a reconciliation had happened. Some things are better left unannounced, like sunrises.

* * *

Pip and Marlow had a plan.

Edmund learned this sitting by the stream after supper, where Pip had gone to skip stones in the last grey light. Edmund had followed partly to keep an eye on him — the boy's silences still came sudden and deep, and everyone pretended not to count them — and partly because the stream was cool and quiet and his head was full of wine and warmth.

"When this is over," Pip said, skipping a stone — four hops, not bad — "we're opening a tavern."

"A tavern?"

"Me and Marlow. It was our plan since we were twelve. A tavern on the coast road, halfway between Correnvale and the sea, so travelers stop whether they're going or coming. Marlow was going to cook — he's better than Sera, don't tell her — and I was going to run the bar and cheat at cards, only kindly. Cheat so people win. There's an art to it." Another stone, five hops. "We had a name and everything. The Twin Kettles."

"That's a good name."

"It's a *great* name. Marlow thought of it. I wanted to call it The Drowned Rat and he said absolutely not, no one wants to drink in a drowned rat, and I said people drink in worse places, and he said that's exactly the problem." Pip ran out of stones and stood holding the last one, turning it in his fingers. His voice stayed light, which was how Edmund knew to be careful. "So I'm thinking I'll open it anyway. One kettle. And put his banner behind the bar. And when people ask why it's called the Twin Kettles when there's obviously only one twin, I'll tell them the second kettle's just out back, boiling."

Edmund looked at the boy — seventeen, wiry, grief worn like an oversized coat he refused to take off — and found his throat tight. "He'd have liked that," he managed.

"He'd have hated the name and loved the tavern." Pip threw the last stone flat and hard; it went six hops and vanished into the dark water, and Pip nodded once, satisfied, as though the stream had signed a contract. Then he bumped his shoulder against Edmund's arm, brief and fierce. "You'll come. When it's open. First customer. I'll cheat you at cards so gently you won't even notice."

"I'll notice."

"No you won't. Nobody notices. That's the art." He grinned up at Edmund, and for a moment the grin was entirely real, and Edmund thought: *this. This is what I guarded the gate for without knowing it. So that somewhere in the world, boys could grow up making plans.*

* * *

Sera found him later, or perhaps he found her; the memory would always be pleasantly unclear. The others had turned in — Odo snoring with operatic commitment, Rell sleeping the way soldiers sleep, instantly and lightly, Pip curled against Odo's broad back like a burr. The fire had settled to embers. Sera sat on the far side of it with her knees drawn up, turning her cup slowly, watching the coals breathe.

Edmund sat down beside her. Not too close. Close enough.

"The stew really was extraordinary," he said.

"It was adequate. Don't flatter the cook, she becomes insufferable." But she smiled at the coals. For a while neither spoke. The stream talked to itself in the dark. Somewhere in the orchard an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

"Sera." He said it before he could decide not to. "Can I ask you something honest?"

"Honest questions are the only kind worth answering. Ask."

"What happens to us. After. If we live."

She turned her head and looked at him, and the ember-light moved in her eyes. "Us," she repeated. Not mocking. Testing the word's weight, like a coin bitten to see if it's true.

"The company," he said, retreating slightly. "The — all of us."

"Mm." She let him off the hook with visible reluctance, which was its own kind of mercy. "Odo will drink his way through the peace celebrations and then get bored and start training village militias for free, because he can't help fixing things. Rell will follow Odo around pretending he has independent business nearby. Pip will run his tavern and become the most beloved criminal on the coast road. And I—" She paused. Turned the cup. "I don't know. I've never planned past the next job. Outlaws don't. Planning past the next job is how you get your hopes up, and hopes are heavy cargo."

"And now?"

"Now I'm carrying a null-born gatekeeper who burns maps and ruins spells by standing near them, and the road keeps getting longer, and I find I mind less than I expected." She said it lightly, but her ears had gone pink, and Edmund understood suddenly that courage looked different on different people. His had been walking through a gate. Hers was this.

"Sera," he said again, and this time his voice came out lower, rougher. "I spent thirty years in a valley where nothing happened. I knew every stone. I could tell you what the weather would do a week out. And I told myself that was peace." He looked at his hands — the hands that had carried a dying stranger, that had held a burning map without blistering, that were, right now, trembling slightly. "Then you all came through my gate, and I learned that what I'd been calling peace was just a very tidy kind of loneliness. And I'm not — I'm not good at this. I don't know the steps. But I want you to know that when I imagine after — when I let myself imagine it, which I'm trying to learn to do — you're in it. Every version. You're in all of them."

The silence stretched. The owl asked again. Sera set down her cup with great care, as though it might go off.

"Do you know," she said, and her voice wasn't quite steady, "I cast a little truth-charm on myself before I came over here. Just to make sure I didn't lose my nerve and say something clever instead of something true." She laughed, shaky. "And now I can't lie, so. Here it is. Every version too, Edmund Yarrow. Since the salt flats, when you stood in front of those hunters and made their magic die of embarrassment. I looked at you and thought, *oh no.* Oh no, not him, anyone but someone I could actually—"

He kissed her. Or she kissed him; the accounts would differ forever, and both would be wrong, because the truth was they met in the middle like travelers on a bridge. Her hand found the front of his shirt. The embers popped. Somewhere across the meadow the owl gave up entirely.

When they broke apart, foreheads touching, Sera whispered, "The charm's still active. Everything I say for the rest of the night is going to be mortifyingly sincere. Fair warning."

"Good," Edmund said. "I've had enough of clever."

* * *

Much later — the moon well down, the fire a red memory — Edmund lay awake in the grass with Sera warm against his shoulder, and let himself do the dangerous thing: he imagined after.

A tavern on a coast road. An orchard like this one, maybe, with a tree he planted himself. Pip cheating customers into winning. Odo telling the goose story to strangers for the hundredth time. Rell pretending to be passing through, staying a season. Sera, growing herbs badly and cooking magnificently, arguing with him about weather. Himself, with a door of his own that people walked through because they chose to.

It was such a clear picture. That was what woke him fully, in the end — not a sound, but the clarity itself, the way hope had gotten so sharp it felt like a blade laid flat against the skin. On the road, he had learned, warmth like this always arrived just before the plunge. Halfway nights were for being happy. But the road was listening either way.

Across the dead fire, Rell sat up abruptly in the dark, head cocked toward the southern sky, and Edmund saw the starlight catch the tension in his face.

"Rell?" Edmund kept his voice low.

For a long moment Rell didn't answer. Then he said, quietly, "The birds stopped."

Edmund listened. The stream, the wind, the settling coals — and underneath it all, a silence moving up the valley from the south, wide and total, like a held breath.

"Get Odo," Rell said.
