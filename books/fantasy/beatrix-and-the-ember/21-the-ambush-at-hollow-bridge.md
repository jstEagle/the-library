## 21. The Ambush at Hollow Bridge

Pip had decided, somewhere around the third morning of the highlands, that he was going to survive this war.

It wasn't bravado. It was arithmetic. Pip was very good at arithmetic — Marlow had taught him, back when they were small enough to share one blanket and one set of parents' worries, and the lesson had stuck: count what you have, count what you need, and if the numbers don't match, find more of what you have. What Pip had was a company of six (seven, counting Rell, which they did now, mostly), a road that bent south toward the capital like a river finding the sea, and a twin who could make him laugh even when his ribs ached from walking.

What he needed was to reach the end of that road with all of it intact.

So far the ledger balanced.

"You're doing the face," Marlow said, falling into step beside him. "The counting face."

"I don't have a counting face."

"You absolutely do. Your eyebrows go in like someone's asked them a difficult question." Marlow squinted at him. "There. That's the one."

Pip elbowed him. Marlow absorbed the elbow the way he absorbed everything — with a grin and a slight stagger that was at least half performance. The twins had grown up learning to be two halves of one joke, and even now, mud-spattered and underfed on the slopes of the Ashfall highlands, they could still split an audience. Sera claimed she'd once seen them make a customs officer laugh so hard he forgot to arrest them. Odo maintained that story was exaggerated. Odo also maintained that he didn't snore, so his authority on exaggeration was limited.

Ahead of them, Edmund walked with the map's pull tugging faintly at his stride, and Sera walked beside him close enough that their shoulders bumped on the narrow path, and neither of them moved away. Rell ranged out front the way he always did now, a lean shadow with a soldier's eyes, and Odo brought up the rear humming something off-key that might have been a marching song or might have been a lament; with Odo it was often both.

They were three days from the capital's shadow. Three days. Pip counted them like coins.

The bridge appeared around a midslope just past noon — a long, low span of grey stone crossing a gorge where the river ran loud and white below. Hollow Bridge, the locals called it, because in wind it sang through the arches, a low moaning note that piped up as they approached, thin and eerie over the water.

"Cheerful," said Marlow.

"It's just wind," said Pip.

"Everything's just wind until it isn't." But Marlow was smiling, and the song of the bridge was almost pretty, a held breath of sound, and the far side rose into green country that looked, for the first time in weeks, like somewhere people might live without fear.

Sera stopped them at the near end. She crouched, pressed her palm flat to the stone, and her brow creased.

"What is it?" Edmund asked.

"Old wards. Toll-wards, maybe, from before the war. They're..." She frowned harder. "They're quiet. Too quiet. Wards this old should hum."

"Maybe they're dead," said Rell.

"Maybe," said Sera, and stood, and looked at the bridge a moment longer than she needed to, and then shook it off and smiled at them all. "Well. Nothing for it but across. Single file, quick steps, no singing — Marlow."

"That was one time."

"One time collapsed a smuggler's tunnel."

They crossed in the order they always crossed things: Rell first, then Edmund with Sera at his shoulder, then the twins, then Odo last with his hand never far from his axe. The bridge sang beneath their boots, that low hollow note rising and falling, and halfway across Pip looked down through a gap between stones at the white water far below and felt, briefly, wonderfully, ordinary. Just a boy on a bridge. Just wind.

He was still smiling when the first arrow took Rell in the shoulder.

* * *

Later, Pip would try to remember how it started, and the memory would always come back in pieces: the arrow, Rell spinning with a grunt and going down behind the parapet; the note of the bridge changing pitch, the ward-song swelling from a moan to a shriek as someone on the far side fed power into the old toll-stones; figures rising from cover among the green rocks ahead — a dozen, fifteen, grey-cloaked, and at their center a mage in Veyra's black-and-silver whose raised hand made the air itself bend.

"Ambush!" Odo roared, which was redundant and exactly right, and then the world became noise.

Pip dropped flat. He'd learned that much in Ashfall: drop first, think second. Arrows hissed overhead and buried themselves in the parapet with sounds like knuckles rapping on a door. Beside him Marlow rolled up onto one knee, sling already whirling, and loosed a stone that caught a grey cloak in the teeth and sent the man staggering. Marlow whooped — Marlow always whooped — and Pip fumbled for his own sling and told himself, *count what you have*, and what he had was a bridge too narrow for their numbers to matter and a mage at the far end whose magic slid toward Edmund and broke apart like water on glass.

That was the thing about Edmund. Even now, even knowing what he was, watching it still made Pip's skin prickle. A lance of white fire came screaming down the bridge and Edmund — flat on his belly, terrified, ordinary — simply wasn't there for it. The fire parted around him like a stream around a stone and scorched the stones on either side, and Sera used the half-second of the mage's confusion to throw a wall of wind that bowled two archers off their feet.

"We can't hold here!" Rell shouted, clutching his shoulder, blood between his fingers. "The wards — they've turned the bridge against us! Get to the far side or get off it!"

"The far side is *them*," Odo bellowed back.

"Then off it!"

Off it meant down — down the gorge side, a scramble of scree and gorse toward the water, a climb Pip's legs protested before his brain finished considering it. But staying meant arrows, and arrows were simpler arithmetic. Pip grabbed Marlow's sleeve. "Down. Come on, down—"

And that was when the second wave came over the hills behind them.

Pip heard Odo swear with tremendous feeling. He turned, and there they were: riders, six or eight, cresting the slope they'd walked not an hour before, cutting off retreat, closing the trap. Grey cloaks, black-and-silver badges, and at their head a woman on a pale horse with a hunter's brand glowing at her throat — a mage-hunter, a real one, not a conscript. Her voice carried across the bridge-song, clear and cold:

"The null and the map. The rest of you may run."

"Generous," said Odo.

"She's lying," said Rell, gray-faced. "Veyra doesn't leave witnesses. Not anymore."

The company gathered in the bridge's middle, backs together, a knot of six against two directions of death, and Pip pressed close to Marlow and felt his brother's heart hammering fast and light against his arm, quick as a bird's, alive alive alive. The mage at the far end began another working — Pip felt it in his teeth, a pressure like a storm about to break — and Sera threw her hands up and met it, wind against fire, and the bridge screamed its hollow note louder than ever, and stones began to crack along the parapet.

"Marlow," Pip said. "Marlow, we have to jump."

"Jump?"

"The gorge. Into the water. It's deep here — look, the white water's deep—" It wasn't a plan. It was panic wearing a plan's clothes, and Marlow looked at him with that grin, that infuriating, beloved, older-by-seven-minutes grin, and said:

"Together, then. On three."

The mage's spell broke over Sera's wind and shattered the parapet beside them. Stone shrapnel spun. And Marlow — Marlow saw the crossbowman on the ridge behind, the one nobody else had seen, leveled and steady and aimed at Pip's back, because Pip had turned to face the riders and left his back to the hills —

Marlow saw it, and Marlow did what Marlow always did, which was put himself between Pip and the world.

"No!" Pip screamed it, later he would scream it again and again, into bedrolls and into rivers and into the dark behind his eyelids — *no* — but the bolt was already flying, and Marlow was already turning, already stepping into its path with his arms half-open like he meant to catch it, like catching things was what he was for.

The bolt took him under the collarbone.

The world went very quiet and very simple. Pip caught his brother as he fell. They went down together on the singing stones, and the bridge's moan filled everything, and Marlow's blood spread warm and shocking across Pip's hands.

"Hey," Marlow said. His voice was small. His grin was still trying. "Told you... everything's just wind... till it isn't."

"Stop it. Stop talking, we'll get you up, Sera! *SERA!*"

But Sera was holding the line of fire, and Odo was roaring and swinging, and Rell was dragging himself forward leaving a red stripe on the stone, and the war — the abstract, distant, grown-ups' war they'd been walking through for weeks — came down all at once to the weight of a boy in his brother's arms getting heavier.

"Pip." Marlow's hand found his collar. Held it, the way he had since they were children, steering him away from trouble, toward breakfast, toward home. "Listen. Listen to me. You have to... you're the one who counts. So count us out of this. Both of us. Promise me you'll..."

His eyes went past Pip's shoulder. Widened.

"*Down,*" he breathed, and shoved.

Pip hit the stones. Above him, Marlow twisted — using the last of his strength to turn his own body over Pip's, shielding him, always shielding him — as the mage's fire swept the bridge a second time. When the heat passed, Marlow sagged against him, and his breathing had changed, wet and wrong, and his hand slipped from Pip's collar and lay still on the stone.

"MARLOW!"

No answer. Only the bridge, singing its one long note, and the battle crashing on around them like weather, and Pip kneeling in his brother's blood with his whole world gone suddenly, terribly quiet inside the noise.

Then Odo's axe came down through the parapet. Then Rell was there, hauling, shouting "the gorge, take him down the gorge, MOVE," and Pip found his feet because his body obeyed even though nothing else in him did, and he lifted his twin — lighter than he should be, lighter than any brother had a right to be — and ran for the edge as the bridge finally, mercifully, began to come apart behind them.

* * *

They got down the gorge side in pieces. Rell went first, bad shoulder and all, and caught Marlow's weight as Pip lowered him hand over hand; Odo came last, having held the broken bridge-end against the riders single-handedly long enough for the rest to descend, and his face when he reached the bottom was a thing Pip had never seen on it before — old, and grim, and afraid.

Sera worked over Marlow on the gravel bank with shaking hands and every trick she knew. Edmund held the torch. Nobody spoke. The river ran loud and white beside them, indifferent, and above them the broken bridge still sang its hollow note to nobody.

Pip sat in the shallows and held his brother's head in his lap and counted.

Counted breaths. Counted the seconds between them, growing longer. Counted the freckles on Marlow's nose, the ones Pip had always pretended to be jealous of. Counted the words Marlow managed, fewer each time, each one spent like his last coin: *Pip. Don't. You're not alone. Never were.* Counted, in the end, down to one breath, and then to none, and kept counting anyway, because stopping would mean the sum was final, and Pip was not ready for the sum to be final.

When Sera's hands finally stilled, and she sat back on her heels with tears cutting clean lines through the ash on her face, Pip understood that the ledger had closed.

Marlow Ash — no, Marlow had taken their mother's name, Marlow Fen, twin of Pip Fen, seven minutes senior, keeper of the jokes and the map-memory and the other half of everything — was gone.

The war had stopped being a story on a road. It lay in Pip's lap, still and cooling, and it had his brother's face.

* * *

Dawn found them upstream in a fold of the hills, hidden, exhausted, unharmed except where it mattered. Rell's shoulder was bound. Sera hadn't slept. Odo stood watch with his back to them all, shoulders rigid, as if he could hold grief off by refusing to look at it. Edmund sat beside Pip and said nothing, which was the only right thing anyone had said all night.

Pip had not spoken either. Words felt like a currency he no longer possessed.

But near morning, something shifted in him — not healing, nothing so kind, but a settling, like sediment after a flood. He looked at Marlow's still face, peaceful now in a way he'd never been in life, and he thought about counting. About what he had, and what he needed.

What he had: a company. A map. A brother's last instruction, spoken in a dying grin: *count us out of this.*

What he needed: for it to mean something.

Edmund stirred beside him. "Pip," he said quietly. "We need to talk about... about what happens now. We can't carry him and outrun Veyra's hunters. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry."

Pip looked at his brother. At the company. At the road south, waiting.

"Yes we can," he said. His voice came out rusted, but it came. "I'll carry him myself."

And when Edmund reached out to help — when they all did, Sera and Odo and Rell, wordless, lifting the burden together because no one else could bear it alone — Pip understood that this, too, was arithmetic. That grief shared was a number that could be survived.

He stood, with his brother in his arms, and turned his face toward the capital.

"They wanted the map," he said. "They wanted us scattered. So we walk. All the way. And when we get there—" his voice cracked, and he let it, "—when we get there, I'm going to count every single one of them."

Nobody answered. Nobody needed to. Behind them the wind moved through the ruined arches of Hollow Bridge, and the bridge sang its one long note over the empty water — a lament now, thin and cold, for a boy who had stepped into the world's path so his brother wouldn't have to.

Pip walked south, carrying his twin, and did not look back.
