## 22. Ashes for a Twin

Pip did not cry.

He wanted to say that he did — that the tears came like a storm and broke over him and washed something clean, the way Sera said grief was supposed to work, the way the ballads all promised. But what actually happened was this: Marlow died at Hollow Bridge with his hand still closed around Pip's wrist, and Pip's whole body went quiet, quieter than it had ever been in nineteen years of being the loud twin, and it had not gotten unquiet since.

They made camp three miles past the bridge because Rell insisted they could not stay near it and Odo insisted they could not go far from it, and for once nobody argued, because arguing required voices and none of them seemed to have any left.

Pip sat against a dead tree with his brother's body wrapped in Marlow's own cloak and did not move.

Marlow looked wrong lying still. That was the thing nobody warned you about. Not the blood, not the grayness — those were bad, but survivable as images. It was the stillness. Marlow had never been still in his life. He'd fidgeted through sermons, drummed his fingers through sieges, talked through Sera's most dangerous spells just to see if her eyebrow would twitch. Stillness was the one thing that didn't fit him, like a coat cut for someone else's shoulders.

*Stop fidgeting,* Pip thought at him. *You're doing it wrong.*

The fire crackled. Somewhere an owl asked its one stupid question over and over.

"Pip." Sera crouched beside him. Her hands were still stained with the ash she'd used on the wound, the last desperate working that hadn't worked. "You should eat something."

He shook his head.

"You have to drink, at least."

Another shake.

Sera didn't push. She was good at that — knowing when a silence needed company instead of filling. She settled next to him, shoulder to shoulder, and stayed there while the owl asked and asked and got no answer worth having.

It was Odo who finally broke the quiet, and of course it was Odo who broke it badly.

"We should burn him tonight," Odo said to the group, low, practical, standing apart by the fire with his arms crossed. "Before dawn. The riders will double back, and we can't carry—"

"No."

Pip heard his own voice come out of him like something dragged up from a well. Everyone turned. He hadn't spoken since the bridge. His throat felt rusted shut around the word.

"He hates fire," Pip said. Each word cost something. "He always has. Since the barn in Aldenmoor. You *know* that, Odo. You've known him eight years."

Odo's jaw worked. "I know it. But—"

"Then don't stand there talking about burning him like he's a supply we're deciding how to pack."

The words came out sharper than Pip intended, or maybe exactly as sharp as he intended; he honestly couldn't tell anymore where his feelings ended and his cruelty began. Odo took it the way Odo took everything, like a blow he'd earned, head dipping slightly, and that made it worse somehow, made Pip want to hit something.

Rell stepped between them before it became hitting. "Nobody's burning anyone tonight," she said quietly. "We'll figure out tomorrow."

"There is no figuring out tomorrow," Odo said, and his voice cracked down the middle, and Pip realized with a distant shock that Odo was not being cold, Odo was drowning too, and grabbing for tasks the way drowning men grab for rope. "There's Veyra's army behind us and the capital ahead of us and a war that doesn't care that we're—" He stopped. Pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "We can't carry him through the mountains. I'm sorry. We can't."

Silence again. The bad kind.

Edmund spoke from the edge of the firelight, where he'd been sitting apart, as he often did, though less lately than he used to. Lately he'd been sitting closer, like a man learning the temperature of a room he'd never been allowed into.

"How far to somewhere we could bury him properly?" Edmund asked.

Everyone looked at him.

"There was a village on the map," Edmund went on, patient, in that flat valley voice of his that never hurried and never flinched. "Thornhollow, or something near it. A day east, if the pass holds. They'll have consecrated ground, or whatever passes for it out here. If we carry him."

"A day," Sera said. "Carrying him. With hunters on our trail."

"Yes," Edmund said.

"It will slow us down."

"Yes."

"It might get us killed."

Edmund considered this with the same seriousness he gave everything, which was one of the things Pip had decided, sometime in the last few weeks without noticing he was deciding it, that he loved about the man. "Probably not," Edmund said. "But possibly. Yes."

"And you're saying we do it anyway."

"I'm saying he's ours," Edmund said simply. "And we don't leave ours by the side of the road."

Pip's chest did something complicated and painful. He looked away, back at the wrapped shape of his brother, because looking at Edmund right now felt like pressing on a bruise on purpose.

Odo started to object — "Edmund, with respect, you weren't here when—" and then stopped himself, because that wasn't fair and everyone knew it. Edmund hadn't known Marlow for years. But he'd known him for weeks, and weeks with Marlow apparently counted double, the way dog years did.

"The vote, then," Rell said. "All in favor of carrying him to Thornhollow."

Four hands. Sera's, Rell's, Edmund's, and — after a long moment, raised slowly, like a man lifting something heavier than a hand — Odo's.

Pip didn't vote. Nobody asked him to. Everybody knew which way his hand would have gone up, and everybody knew he might not have been able to lift it, and the company had enough grace left, even now, to spare him that.

* * *

They walked through the night because nobody could have slept anyway, and because moving was better than sitting with the stillness. Edmund carried Marlow.

That was the part Pip kept returning to, later, turning it over like a stone in his pocket: Edmund carried him. All day. Up switchbacks and across scree fields and through a freezing stream that came up to his thighs, and he never once asked for relief, never once stumbled, never once treated it as anything other than what it was — the plain duty of a man who had spent his whole life guarding a door and understood, better than any of them, what it meant to keep faith with a post.

Pip tried twice to take a turn. Both times Edmund just shook his head.

"You're his brother," Edmund said the second time, gently. "You carry him every day already. Let me carry the heavy part."

Which was such an Edmund thing to say that Pip had to walk ten paces ahead so nobody would see his face do whatever it was doing.

The twins had always split the world between them without ever discussing it. Marlow was the planner, the talker, the one who charmed gate guards and negotiated prices and remembered which innkeeper watered the ale. Pip was the runner, the climber, the one who went first over walls and last through doors. Between them they made one complete person, and everyone had always joked about it — *half-wit and half-pint*, Marlow called them, delighted with himself — but the joke had teeth in it that Pip only felt now. Half of him was walking ahead wrapped in his own cloak, and the half that remained kept reaching for things that weren't there. Kept turning to say something and finding no one to say it to. Kept finishing sentences that would never be finished now, hanging in the air with their ends missing.

Around midday they passed a shrine at a crossroads, some local godling with offerings of bread gone green. Marlow would have stolen the freshest offering and left a copper in its place and called it commerce. Pip caught himself smiling at the thought and then caught himself smiling and hated it, hated that his face could do that while his brother rode on Edmund's back wrapped in wool, and punched the shrine post hard enough to split two knuckles.

Sera appeared at his elbow. She didn't say anything. She took his hand, inspected the knuckles, smeared them with something bitter-smelling from her pouch, and wrapped them with strips torn from her own sleeve.

"You're allowed to laugh," she said.

"I'm not."

"You are. Grief isn't a debt you pay by suffering correctly. There's no correct amount." She tied off the bandage. "Marlow would find it hilarious, you know. You punching a god's mailbox."

Something in Pip's chest lurched sideways. "Don't," he whispered. "Don't talk about him like he's — like he's past tense."

Sera was quiet a moment. "I'm sorry," she said. "Old habit. Hedge-witches get casual about death, and it's a rotten habit, and I'll do better." She squeezed his bandaged hand once, carefully, and moved up the line.

* * *

Thornhollow was barely a village anymore — a dozen roofs, a chapel with a sagging bell tower, terraced fields climbing the hillside like green stairs. The war hadn't reached it yet, but its shadow had; half the young people were gone, and the ones remaining watched the company come down the road with the wary arithmetic of people counting strangers' weapons.

An old priestess met them at the chapel gate. She looked at the wrapped burden on Edmund's back and didn't ask questions, which Pip suspected was the whole job description of priests in border villages these days.

"We can dig in the morning," she said. "Ground's soft after the rains. Or—" she glanced at the sky, at the light going amber and long, "—if you'd rather not wait, we can do it tonight by lantern. Some families prefer it. Saying goodbye while it's still today."

Tonight. While it was still today. As if Marlow were a letter that needed posting before the office closed.

Pip found he couldn't answer. He found, in fact, that he was furious at the woman for offering a choice, because choosing meant admitting that either option was real, and both options ended the same way, with dirt, with done, with a world rearranged around an absence.

It was Edmund again. Of course it was Edmund.

"Tonight," Edmund said. "While it's still today."

* * *

They dug the grave themselves. All of them. Even Rell, who claimed royal guards didn't dig, and even Odo, whose hands blistered within the first hour because apparently disgraced guards spent more time polishing armor than holding shovels. The priestess brought lanterns and left them on the chapel wall without being asked, six little flames against the coming dark.

Pip dug until his arms screamed. It was the first time since the bridge that his body had agreed with his heart about anything, and he threw himself into the work with a kind of gratitude, letting the ache drown out the other thing, the hollow thing, the Marlow-shaped thing.

When the grave was ready, they unwrapped him.

Marlow's face in the lantern light was peaceful, which was obscene, because Marlow had never been peaceful in his life except in sleep, and even then he mumbled arguments. Someone had cleaned the blood away — Sera, Pip realized, must have done it during the night march while he wasn't watching, another kindness performed invisibly — and someone had tucked a sprig of rowan into the fold of his cloak, and Pip didn't ask who, because knowing would have broken him open in front of everyone.

They said things. That was the rite Beatrix's company had always used, Odo explained hoarsely — no priest needed, no gods required, just the people who knew you saying true things over your body so the ground would know what it was getting.

Sera went first. "Marlow Ash," she said, voice steady until the third sentence and then not steady at all, "was the only thief I ever met who stole mostly for other people. He stole medicine for sick villages and bread for hungry twins and once, memorably, a hat for me, because he said my head looked cold, and it did, and I wore it for four years." A wet laugh went around the graveside. "He was infuriating and generous and brave in the specific way of people who joke during battles, which is the bravest way there is. I'm putting him in the ground angry that he's making me do this. He'd want it noted."

Rell: "I arrested him twice. He escaped twice. The second time he left a note in my cell apologizing for the inconvenience and complimenting my locks. I have thought about that note every day for six years." She paused. "Best criminal I ever chased. Worst loss I ever guarded."

Odo couldn't finish his. He got as far as "He pulled me out of a river in Correnvale and never let me forget it, charged me interest for eight years, and I paid every coin gladly, and I'd pay it again—" and then his voice went, and he stood there with his fists clenched, and Rell put her hand on his arm, and that was the whole eulogy, and it was enough.

Then it was Pip's turn, and Pip discovered he had prepared nothing, because preparing would have meant believing it.

So he just knelt at the grave's edge and put his mouth close to his brother's ear, the way he'd done a thousand times in a thousand shared beds and haylofts and jail cells, passing secrets under blankets after lights-out.

"I don't know how to be a whole person," he whispered. "You were always the half that knew what we were doing next. So I'm going to keep going, and I'm going to do it badly, and every good idea I have for the rest of my life is going to be one of yours, and I hope wherever you are, you're insufferable about it." His voice broke. He let it. "Tell Beatrix we're coming. Tell her we found the man she picked, and she picked right."

He kissed his brother's forehead, cold as river stone, and climbed out of the grave, and they filled it in together, all six of them taking turns with the shovel, and the priestess rang the old cracked bell once, a sound like a coin dropped down a well, and that was that.

Afterward, Pip stood at the grave's edge in the dark and felt the hollow thing stir and thought, with sudden terrible clarity: *I could just keep walking. East, past the village, past the mountains, anywhere. Nobody would blame me. Nobody would even be surprised.*

A hand landed on his shoulder. Edmund's. He hadn't heard the big man approach — he never did, for a man his size; the valley had taught him to walk like weather.

"Come sit by the fire," Edmund said.

"I'm fine."

"You're not. None of us are. That's rather the point of fires." Edmund didn't pull, didn't push. He just stood there, solid as his own gate, until Pip turned and walked with him.

* * *

The fire that night was bigger than usual. Sera fed it until it roared, and nobody told her to conserve, and the warmth pushed back the dark for a few yards in every direction, which was, Pip thought, the entire job of companies like theirs.

He sat between Edmund and Rell and drank the tea Sera pressed on him — bitter, restorative, probably medicinal, definitely an excuse to make him swallow something — and slowly, degree by degree, the rusted gate inside him began to creak open.

"He'd have hated that grave," Pip said at last, to the fire. "Too neat. He'd have wanted to be buried under a fake name with treasure he didn't actually have, just to confuse future archaeologists."

"Future archaeologists," Rell repeated.

"That's what he called historians. Said they were just grave-robbers with funding."

A laugh went around the fire — small, startled, guilty, real. And then, because grief is a strange country with strange customs, they started telling Marlow stories, and the stories got bigger and funnier as the night went on, and Pip laughed until his ribs hurt and cried in the middle of laughing and didn't bother hiding either one, and the company held all of it, the way a fire holds heat, without comment and without end.

It was Edmund who summed it up, near midnight, in his slow valley way, staring into the flames:

"My whole life, I thought keeping something safe meant sealing it away. Behind wards. Behind walls." He turned his cup in his hands. "But this — carrying him a full day, digging all night, telling stories that make us laugh when laughing feels illegal — this is keeping too. Maybe it's the only kind that works."

Pip looked at him across the fire — the magicless man, the living lock, the warden bred for a door — and thought that Beatrix Ash, whoever she had been, had chosen her courier with better eyes than any of them would ever fully understand.

"I'm coming with you," Pip said. "To the capital. To the Ember. All the way."

Nobody argued. Nobody voted. Sera just refilled his tea, which among their people was a binding contract.

It was nearly dawn when the rider came — a Correnvale messenger, lathered horse, official seal, face gray with more than the road. He handed the dispatch to Odo, who read it once, and then read it again, and then went very still in the way Odo went still before delivering the worst possible news.

"What?" Pip said, standing, tea forgotten, the hollow thing in his chest suddenly wide awake and braced. "What is it?"

Odo looked up at them all, at the fire, at the fresh-turned earth of Marlow's grave on the hill above.

"They sold us," he said. "Correnvale sold our route to Veyra. Every ford, every pass, every place we'd sleep." He swallowed. "And the messenger says her hunters are already moving. We're a day ahead of them. Maybe less."
