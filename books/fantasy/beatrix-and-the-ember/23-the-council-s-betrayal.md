## 23. The Council's Betrayal

Sera knew something was wrong before the messenger said a word. That was the trouble with hedge-witchery — it wasn't good for much in a world of sorcerer-queens and living flames, but it was very good at telling her when the weather of a room had turned.

The weather had turned. The inn at Millbrook sat snug under its thatched roof, fire crackling, Pip arguing with Marlow's ghost-memory about whether stew counted as breakfast — and underneath all of it, like a stone dropped into her chest, sat a cold, wet certainty: *someone has sold you.*

She'd felt it first as a prickle between her shoulder blades while she'd been grinding feverfew for Odo's knee, which complained these days like an old cart axle. Then the horses had refused to settle. Then the crows had come — six of them, sitting on the fence in a row, watching the inn with the particular patience of birds who know a funeral is scheduled.

"Those crows have been there since dawn," Edmund said quietly, appearing at her elbow with two bowls of porridge. He'd taken to doing that — appearing beside her without sound, which for a man his size should have been impossible. "Pip says they're spies."

"Pip says everything is spies."

"Pip is usually right," Edmund said, "which I find more alarming every day."

Sera took the porridge because refusing Edmund's cooking was against her personal religion. It was good porridge. He salted it exactly right, which she suspected was the warden training — a man who spent thirty years guarding a gate learned to do small things precisely, because precision was all he had.

She was three spoonfuls in when the rider came up the road at a pace that meant bad news wearing good news' coat.

* * *

The rider was a girl of perhaps sixteen, mud to the knees, leading a lathered pony. She wouldn't come inside. She stood in the yard, twisting her cap in her hands, looking at the company gathered in the doorway — Odo broad and gray-bearded, Rell lean and watchful, the twins, Edmund, herself — and Sera watched the girl's courage fail and rally twice before she spoke.

"I'm from Correnvale. My aunt works in the chancellery. She—" The girl swallowed. "She couldn't send this by normal post. She said if I ever wanted to look my own face in the mirror again, I'd ride hard and find the Ash Company."

"We're not—" Pip started.

"You're the Ash Company," the girl said flatly. "Everyone knows who you are. Beatrix Ash's people." Her voice cracked on the name, and Sera felt the old wound flex. Beatrix got everywhere, even into strangers' mouths. "There was a secret session of the council. Three nights ago. Closed doors, no minutes taken. Chancellor Vess and six others voted."

"Voted what?" Odo asked. His voice was very even, which Sera knew meant it was anything but.

The girl looked down at her cap. "The council sent word to Queen Veyra's ministry. A full accounting of your route. Where you crossed the salt flats. Where you sheltered. Where you're likely to be now." She fumbled in her coat and produced a folded paper, sealed with plain wax. "My aunt copied the dispatch before it went. She said — she said someone should know they were sold, even if nothing can be done about it."

Nobody moved. The crows shifted on the fence.

Sera took the paper because her hands were steadiest, which was a lie, but her hands were good liars. She broke the seal and read it aloud, because the company had long ago agreed that bad news belonged to everyone or it would rot one person from the inside out.

It was all there, in tidy chancellery script. Their crossing at the Salt Gate. The ford where the witch lived — the witch's name written down, which made Sera's stomach clench, because that old woman had been kind to them. The pass of names. The ruined city. An estimate of their numbers, their arms, their destination. And at the bottom, in a different hand, the reply from Veyra's ministry, copied in the same tidy script:

*In consideration of the Crown's gratitude, the free city of Correnvale shall be spared the general levy. Its walls shall stand. Its people shall be left to their commerce.*

*The Crown thanks the Council for its wisdom.*

"Their wisdom," Odo repeated. He said it the way a man might say a curse word in church — softly, so only the gods heard.

"They bought their lives with ours," Rell said. There was no surprise in his voice at all, and Sera remembered, not for the first time, that Rell had served Veyra for years and knew exactly how she paid her bills. "That's how she does it. She doesn't burn cities anymore. She makes them burn each other. Cheaper. Cleaner. And every city that buys itself teaches the next one to sell."

Marlow's ghost-memory drifted up through the floorboards — he did that sometimes, less a haunting than a habit, the way a dog follows you room to room after death. His voice came thin and amused, the way he'd always delivered the worst observations. *"I told you the council had soft hands. Soft hands write fast."*

"Hush," Sera said, though there was nobody to hush. Pip flinched anyway. Pip always flinched at his twin's voice, and then looked ashamed of flinching, and Sera's heart did the thing it had been doing for weeks now, the slow bruising squeeze.

"What do we do?" the messenger girl asked. "My aunt — will Veyra find out she—"

"No," Sera said, and put every gram of certainty she owned into it. "Your aunt is brave and careful, and careful brave people are the reason the world hasn't ended yet. You tell her the Ash Company says thank you, and that we don't forget debts." She dug in her pouch and pressed a charm into the girl's hand — a knotted thread, nothing grand, just a working to keep pony and rider unremarkable on the road. "Ride the river path. Don't take the bridge at Tarnwick."

The girl went. They watched her until the road bent, and then they stood in the yard a while longer, six people and one ghost, with the crows still watching.

"Well," said Pip finally, "at least the porridge was good."

* * *

They held council around the kitchen table, which was how they'd always done it, ever since Beatrix had declared that war rooms made men stupid and kitchens kept them honest. Sera set out the map — Beatrix's map, the true one, heat-marked and alive under Edmund's hands alone — and weighted the corners with teacups, because dignity was for people with better furniture.

"Options," Odo said, in his parade-ground voice. "One: we run. North, over the mountains, lose ourselves."

"And go where?" Rell asked. "Every road on that page is known to her now. We run, we die tired instead of quick."

"Two: we scatter."

"No," said four voices at once, and Marlow's ghost added a fifth, faintly delighted: *"Hear, hear."*

Odo raised his hands. "Three: we assume the information is already in Veyra's hands, stop pretending otherwise, and move faster than her response can organize. Speed is our only friend left."

"Speed and him," Rell said, nodding at Edmund. "Her hunters track the null. Which means they track us. But it also means when they find us, their magic finds a wall." He rubbed his hand absently — the hand the map had burned, back when he'd been their enemy. Old habits. "We've survived every ambush so far because of him."

"That's not a plan, that's a prayer with legs," Sera said. "And prayers with legs get shot."

Edmund had been quiet through all of it, which was usual; Edmund was quiet through most things, in the way of a man who'd spent decades listening to wind against a gate. But now he spoke, and his voice had that flat calm that Sera had learned to fear slightly more than shouting.

"How long," he said, "before her riders reach Millbrook?"

Odo did the arithmetic. Everyone watched him do it. "Two days. Three if the roads are muddy."

"The roads are muddy," Pip said. "I checked. Professionally."

"Then we have two days," Edmund said, "and we've spent half of one deciding whether to be sad about the council." He looked around the table, and Sera saw something in his face she hadn't seen in the valley — not the gatekeeper's patience, but something harder and newer. Grief, maybe, learning to stand up straight. "Beatrix died carrying this map to people she trusted. The council sold us. Fine. Institutions fail. That's what institutions do — I guarded one for thirty years." A ghost of a smile. "But we're not an institution. We're a company. Companies don't need permission."

Silence around the table. Then Marlow's ghost-memory, drifting warm through the rafters: *"Oh, I like him. Beatrix would have kissed him. Or fought him. With her it was usually both."*

"Motion to adopt the gatekeeper's plan," Odo said gruffly, "and never tell him he gave a speech."

"Seconded," said everyone, including, Sera was fairly sure, the crows.

* * *

They packed in the grey afternoon light, and Sera found herself doing the thing she did when her hands needed to be busy and her heart needed to be quiet: brewing. She set the kettle over the fire and laid out her tins, and one by one the company drifted toward the smell like moths, because that was the other thing kitchens were for.

Pip came first, as always. He sat on the stool nearest the fire and didn't talk, which was unlike him, and Sera poured him chamomile with honey and didn't ask, which was unlike her, and they were both grateful for the mutual mercy.

"He wrote it down," Pip said eventually, staring into his cup. "Marlow. In the ledger. Before Hollow Bridge, he wrote down our route — he always wrote everything down, he said memory was a liar with good handwriting." His jaw worked. "If Marlow hadn't written the route down, the council couldn't have sold it so neatly. They used my brother's handwriting to sell us."

"Oh, love." Sera reached over and gripped his wrist. "No. Listen to me. If Marlow hadn't written it down, you'd have lost the route yourself somewhere in the ash country, and you'd be dead in a ditch, and the Ember would still be in Veyra's fist. Your brother's handwriting isn't a betrayal. It's a map that got stolen. Those aren't the same thing, and anyone who tells you different gets a face full of nettle tea."

Pip laughed — wet, surprised, real. "You threaten everyone with tea?"

"Only people I'm fond of. It's a short list. Guard it."

Odo came next, and drank his tea standing, like a man who distrusted chairs. Rell took his without sugar and stood beside Odo, close enough that their shoulders touched, which was new, and which Sera pretended not to notice with great effort. Edmund sat across from her with his bowl of second porridge — the man treated grief like a laboring job, fuel it and keep working — and when their eyes met over the steam, something passed between them that neither of them named, because naming it would have required a kind of courage they were both saving for the capital.

"So," Sera said, to the table, to the fire, to the whole failing world. "Correnvale's gone. The witch's ford is burned by now, I'd wager. Every safe house on that list is a trap. Our allies are—" She stopped. Started again. "Our allies are gone."

"Not gone," Edmund said. "Reassigned." He nodded at the five faces around the table, and at the sixth presence in the rafters. "All of them."

And that was the moment, Sera thought later — not the battle, not the vault, none of the loud moments. This one. A kitchen, cooling tea, a dead man's laughter in the beams, and the slow dawning understanding that the world had pared them down to the only thing that had ever actually worked: each other.

* * *

They left Millbrook before dawn, in fog thick enough to chew, taking the drover's road south — the one road not on Marlow's ledger, because Marlow had never known it. Only Sera knew it, from a smuggling summer she'd never confessed to anyone except Beatrix, and Beatrix was past telling.

The fog muffled their hoofbeats and their breathing and, for the first hour, their thoughts. Then Pip, riding point, held up a fist, and the column stopped, and Sera felt the cold come back between her shoulder blades.

Ahead, where the drover's road bent toward the river, a figure stood in the fog. One figure. Unarmed. Unarmored. Wearing the travel-stained gray of a king's courier, and standing with the particular stillness of someone who has ridden through the night to arrive before bad news could.

Odo's sword cleared its scabbard with a whisper. Rell's knife was somehow already in his hand. But the figure raised both palms and called out in a cracked, exhausted voice:

"I'm looking for the Ash Company. I have proof the king lives — proof beyond cipher, beyond paper. Proof you can *see*." A pause, and then, quieter, almost pleading: "And I think Veyra's riders are behind me. Perhaps a day. Perhaps less."

Sera exchanged a glance with Edmund. On the fence rail behind them, unnoticed until now, the six crows lifted off together and flew south — fast, purposeful, like messengers.

Like reports.

"Well," Sera murmured, stepping forward with her tea-stained hands raised in what she hoped looked like peace and felt like surrender to the whole absurd day. "Let's hear it, then. But walk slowly, friend, and mind the fog. We've had a difficult week, and our hospitality died with the council."

The stranger laughed — a broken, grateful sound — and came forward into the light.

Behind them, far to the north, Sera felt it begin: a pressure on the edge of her senses, vast and patient, like a storm deciding which door to knock on. Veyra had read the ledger. Veyra was coming.

They had a day. Maybe less. And one road left that nobody had sold.
