## 24. Into the Capital's Shadow

The road to Veyra's capital was paved with pilgrims.

Edmund had never seen so many people in one place, moving in one direction. They came down the mountain passes and out of the wheat country in a slow, shuffling river of gray cloaks and candle-stubs, singing the Binding Day hymns in voices worn smooth by repetition. Old women carried icons of the crowned flame. Children chased each other between the carts. A man with a lute played the same seven notes over and over until Edmund thought he might go quietly mad.

"They're not pilgrims," Pip said, walking beside him with his hood up and his hands jammed in his pockets. "Well. Most of them are. But look —" he nodded subtly at a knot of travelers two carts ahead, "— that one's got soldier's shoulders under his cloak. And the woman with the icon case has been checking our backs for three miles."

"The whole city will be full of them," Odo said from Edmund's other side. He wore the gray of a penitent pilgrim as though he'd been born to it, which, Edmund supposed, in a way he had. "Veyra wants the streets packed. A coronation is only impressive if there are enough bodies to be impressed."

"It's not a coronation," Sera said quietly, from behind them. "It's an anniversary. Twelve years since the Binding. She likes to remind everyone."

Edmund glanced back at her. She looked tired. They all looked tired; the last weeks had carved something out of each of them. But she managed a small smile for him, and he felt it land somewhere beneath his ribs and stay there, warm, like a coal tucked into a pocket against the cold.

"You've done this before," he said. "Infiltrated a city?"

"I've snuck into a city," she said. "There's a difference. Sneaking in means nobody knows you're there. Infiltrating means you're inside and they still don't know. It requires better manners."

"And worse lies," Marlow said cheerfully. He was walking with a slight hitch in his stride that he kept trying to disguise, and failing. The wound at Hollow Bridge had closed, but the flesh around it was still angry and red, and every mile showed on him. He'd refused to stay behind at the ford with the witch's people. "I told the ferryman I was a relic-seller from the coast. He believed me right up until Pip opened his mouth."

"I opened my mouth *once*," Pip protested.

"Once was enough. Now we're relic-sellers from the coast who are also brothers, which is fine, except now we have to actually sell relics." Marlow patted the satchel at his hip, which contained, as far as Edmund knew, six river stones, a dried starfish, and a spoon of suspiciously fine silver that Rell had produced from somewhere and refused to explain.

Rell walked at the rear. He always walked at the rear now, and no one had asked him to. He'd simply taken up the position the way water takes the shape of its container, and after the first few days of pointed silence, the company had stopped noticing — or stopped minding. His armor was gone, traded in a river town for a mule and four gray cloaks. Without it he looked smaller. Older. Like a man who had been carrying something heavy for so long he'd forgotten it wasn't part of his body.

He caught Edmund looking and raised an eyebrow.

"You keep watching the road behind us," Edmund said.

"That's where trouble comes from."

"It hasn't come yet."

Rell smiled, thin and tired. "Captain Rell's trouble never announced itself either. It just arrived, very politely, and asked to see my orders." He nodded ahead. "Besides. Today the trouble's going to be in front of us. In a city of two hundred thousand people, we're going to walk straight toward the thing every hunter in the realm is looking for."

Edmund's hand went, without his quite meaning it to, to the inside of his cloak, where the map lay folded against his chest. It had been quiet for days — almost shy, since the vault. But he could feel it there, faintly warm, like a hand resting flat against his sternum. And beneath the warmth, fainter than ever but unmistakable now that he knew what to listen for: the whisper. The king's echo, curled up small in the null space Edmund carried with him everywhere, like a man sheltering from a storm under a bridge.

*Closer,* the whisper had said last night, when Edmund woke shivering at the edge of the firelight. *So much closer than you know.*

The city appeared at midday.

It rose out of the plain like a mountain that had been argued into existence — white walls first, then the towers behind them, then the great spike of the palace rising at the center like a needle through the heart of everything. Banners hung from every wall and window, deep crimson shot with gold thread, the crowned flame of Veyra's house snapping in the wind. The gates stood open. Of course they did. What use closing a gate against a flood?

The queue moved at a funeral pace. Around them, pilgrims pressed forward shoulder to shoulder, breath steaming, feet stamping, and the hymn rolled up the road in waves. Guards in crimson worked along the line, checking papers, waving most through, pulling aside the occasional unlucky soul for closer questions.

"All right," Odo murmured, without moving his lips. It was a skill Edmund had watched him practice over the years of their acquaintance and had never once managed himself. "Remember your stories. We are the Ashvale pilgrimage circle, come to honor the Binding. We are humble, we are bored, we are slightly cold. Sera, you're our guide. Twins, you're twins. Rell—" a pause, "—Rell, you're a pilgrim."

"I'm a pilgrim," Rell agreed.

"Pilgrims don't stand like that."

Rell adjusted his stance with the expression of a man being asked to insult his own mother. "Like what?"

"Like you're waiting for someone to attack. Shoulders back. Slouch. Think of it as —" Odo considered, "— think of it as being tired of God."

"I can do tired of God," Rell said, and slouched, and it was, Edmund thought, the single least convincing thing he'd ever seen a human being do with a spine.

They were twenty places from the gate when the guards changed.

Edmund felt it before he understood it — a prickle along his arms, the same wrongness he'd learned to read in the valley wards when weather came. The guards at the checkpoint had been bored men doing a boring job. The ones stepping up now moved differently. Crisper. And among them walked a figure in a gray traveling coat with a chain of office around her neck, and her eyes were closed, and she was *listening*.

"A seeker," Sera breathed. "A listening-seeker. They're sweeping the crowd for spellwork."

"Then we're fine," Marlow whispered. "None of us is carrying anything enchanted. Except —"

Everyone looked at Edmund.

"— except the map," Marlow finished, "which is sitting against the chest of the one man in the world whose entire personality repels magic."

"Is it enchanted?" the guard asked, arriving in front of them, and Edmund realized with a lurch that the question had been directed at *him*, and that he had been standing there silently while his family discussed his magical immunity directly in front of a member of the secret police.

"Relics," Edmund said. His voice came out lower than intended, and he cleared his throat and tried again. "Family relics. From Ashvale. For the ceremony."

The guard looked at him. Looked at the satchel. Looked at Sera, who smiled the smile of a woman who had guided pilgrimages for thirty years and found all of them tedious. The guard's gaze slid off them like rain off glass — Edmund felt the familiar flicker of his own nature doing its work, the small dead zone he made in the world sliding over his companions like a blanket pulled up over sleeping children.

The seeker passed within arm's reach of him. Her eyes stayed closed. Her brow furrowed — once, slightly, the way a person frowns at a sound they can't quite place — and then she moved on, and the line surged forward, and they were through the gates and inside the capital of the sorcerer-queen.

* * *

Inside, the city was drowning in gold.

Every surface that could bear decoration did. Garlands of gilded paper looped between the lampposts. The fountain in the first square ran dyed-water, shimmering faintly with some charm that made it glow like sunset all day long. Street sellers cried roasted chestnuts and commemorative ribbons and little tin crowns for children, and children by the dozen wore them, running through the crowds with wooden swords, shouting "For the Queen! For the Flame!"

"For the Flame," Pip repeated under his breath, in the flattest voice Edmund had ever heard him produce, and Marlow elbowed him hard enough to stagger.

"Smile," Marlow hissed. "We're celebrating. We have never in our lives been so celebratory."

Sera led them through the crowd with unerring instinct, away from the main processional route, into lanes where the bunting grew tattered and the dye in the fountain hadn't reached. She counted turns under her breath, checked sightlines, and finally drew them into the shadowed mouth of a cooper's yard, where barrels were stacked to the eaves and the noise of the procession fell away to a distant roar.

"We're here," she said. "Two days early. Ceremony's the morning after tomorrow."

"So we wait?" Edmund asked.

"We wait, we watch, we find the way in." Sera leaned against a barrel and rubbed her eyes. "Rell. The servant tunnels. How sure are you?"

"As sure as I was twelve years ago," Rell said. "Which is to say, mostly. The palace has had time to change. But the under-vaults don't change. Stone remembers." He glanced at Edmund, and something complicated crossed his face — guilt, maybe, or its cousin. "We'll go down the night before. While the whole city's drunk on ceremony."

"And the king?" Edmund asked. "Do we see him tomorrow? At the parade?"

Odo's jaw tightened. "You'll see him."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"It means," Odo said, "that you should decide now whether you want to. Because once you see him, you can't unsee it. I served that man for nine years. I'd rather remember him the way he was." He paused. "But you never met him. So maybe it's different for you. Maybe you need to see what we're fighting for. Or what we're fighting against." He shrugged, heavy. "Either way. Tomorrow, the whole city watches Aldric paraded through the streets. If you want to look — you'll get your chance."

* * *

They spent the night in the loft of a chandler's shop that Sera swore blind was safe, on the strength of a favor owed to her by a woman she'd known twenty years ago, and about whose current loyalties she became noticeably vague when pressed. Edmund slept badly on a bed of candle-wicks, waking every hour to check that the map was still quiet, that the whisper was still there, that the world hadn't ended while he dozed.

The parade began at dawn.

They took positions in the crowd along the Processional Way, wedged between a family of bakers and a delegation of wool merchants, all of them craning toward the marble balustrade that lined the route. The street itself had been washed and strewn with petals. Trumpets sounded from the walls. And then the bells began — every bell in the capital at once, a sound so vast it wasn't sound anymore, just pressure, just the city's whole heart beating at once.

"He's coming," the baker woman beside Edmund said, gripping her daughter's hand. "Look, love, look! The king!"

And the king came.

The procession entered the square at a slow walk, flanked by a hundred guards in ceremonial crimson, and Edmund's first thought — absurd, intrusive — was how *tall* Aldric was. Taller than Odo. Taller than anyone in the square. He rode a white horse that moved as though it were asleep, and he sat the saddle the way a coat sits on a hook, and the crowd roared around him, and he did not hear it.

His eyes were open. That was the worst part. Edmund had expected — he didn't know what. Closed eyes. A slack face. Some obvious ruin. But Aldric's face was composed, dignified, even handsome, in the way of a portrait. His eyes were open and blue and aimed at the middle distance, and there was nothing behind them. Not emptiness exactly. Emptiness would have been kinder. This was *absence* — the look of a house whose owner has gone out and left every lamp burning.

And above his brow, the crown.

Edmund had expected gold. It was gold, yes, but gold the way a coal is black — alive with it. Faint light pulsed beneath the metal's surface, slow as breathing, and as the king passed, Edmund saw the light *reach*. Thin threads of radiance crawling down from the crown like frost growing across a window, sinking into the king's temples, his throat, his hands on the reins. Feeding him. Wearing him.

The Ember's work. The Ember, caged in a vault beneath the palace, pouring the king's stolen will into this puppet-show, twelve years of it, every day, every parade, every wave to the crowds.

Beside him, Odo made a sound. Small. Edmund had never heard Odo make a sound like that — it was the sound of a man swallowing something too large, and it broke something loose in Edmund's chest to hear it.

"I trained under him," Odo said. Very quietly. Only for Edmund. "Third year of my service. He corrected my sword grip personally. Put my elbow where it belonged and said, 'There. Now you're holding the sword instead of apologizing to it.'" His voice didn't shake. It went very flat instead, which was worse. "That man used to laugh at his own jokes before he finished them. He used to sneak down to the kitchens at midnight because he couldn't sleep and he liked burnt bread. Burnt bread, Edmund. And now he —"

He stopped. The king's horse had reached the near end of the square, and Aldric turned his head — slowly, mechanically, the motion of a clock's hand rather than a man's neck — and smiled at the crowd.

It was a perfect smile. Every tooth in its proper place. It reached his eyes the way light reaches the bottom of a well.

The crowd roared back, thousands strong, and the baker woman was crying happy tears, and her daughter waved a tin crown, and somewhere in the press of bodies Pip had gone white as salt and Marlow had put a steadying hand on his brother's shoulder without either of them looking at each other.

And then it happened.

As the king passed directly opposite them — ten yards of petals and trumpets between them — Aldric's empty eyes swept across the crowd, and for half a heartbeat, they snagged.

On Edmund.

Nothing changed in the king's face. The smile held. The eyes stayed vacant. But Edmund felt it anyway, felt it the way you feel a draft from a door opening in a far room — a thread of attention reaching across the square and touching him, probing, curious, *hungry*, and beneath the crown's glow something vast and caged turned over in its sleep and looked at him through the king's borrowed eyes.

*Gatekeeper.*

Not spoken aloud. Spoken in the null-space, in the silence Edmund carried, in the voice he'd been hearing in fragments for weeks — but stronger now, so much stronger, close enough to have edges. And underneath the word, underneath the hunger and the terrible attention, something else. Something small and human and drowning.

*Help me.*

The procession moved on. The thread of attention snapped. The king's face swung forward again, the smile fixed, the bells thundering, and the crowd cheered its hollow king down the Processional Way and out through the far arch, and Edmund stood in the roaring dark of his own skull with his heart slamming against the map's warmth like a fist against a door.

"Edmund." Sera's hand on his arm. Her face pale, her eyes sharp. "What happened? You've gone gray. Did he see you?"

"I don't know," Edmund said. Which was true, and also completely false, because he knew perfectly well. The king had seen him. Or the thing wearing the king had. Or the thing trapped inside the thing wearing the king — and it knew his name, the name the mountain had spoken, the name he was only beginning to understand had never really belonged to a man named Edmund Yarrow at all.

"No," he said again, steadier. "No. But he —" He looked at Sera, at Odo, at the twins, at Rell standing grim and watchful at the edge of their huddle, and lowered his voice until it was barely breath. "He spoke to me. The echo. Through the binding. He said —"

The words wouldn't come easily. He made them come anyway.

"He asked for help."

Silence, in the middle of a hundred thousand cheering people. Somewhere nearby a band struck up a festival tune, bright and brassy and obscene.

"Well," said Marlow at last, into the noise. "Good news. We were already planning to."

But Edmund wasn't laughing. Because beneath the echo's plea, beneath the hunger and the drowning, he had felt something else — something that had slipped past him in the moment and only now surfaced, cold and deliberate, like a fin cutting the water.

The attention hadn't only come from the king.

Something on the palace side of the square had felt the echo reach for him. Something had *noticed the noticing*. And it had turned, slow and vast and intelligent, toward the spot where he stood — and Edmund, magicless, unseeable, ordinary Edmund Yarrow, had felt it find him anyway.

Somewhere behind those white walls, in the high tower where the banners flew, the sorcerer-queen had just learned that the Gatekeeper had come to her city.

And she had let him keep standing there.
