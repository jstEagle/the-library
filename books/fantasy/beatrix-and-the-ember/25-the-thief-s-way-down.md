## 25. The Thief's Way Down

Rell had stolen into the palace eleven times before he'd ever been caught, and once after. The first eleven had been for Beatrix — love letters, mostly, and one memorable evening a whole roast pheasant wrapped in his cloak while the night cook slept at her post. The twelfth had been for Veyra, hunting the woman who'd taught him the first eleven ways in.

He tried not to think about that arithmetic too hard.

Now there would be a thirteenth, and this time he was bringing company: a magicless gatekeeper with the posture of a fence post, a hedge-witch who kept patting her satchel like it might bolt, a disgraced royal guard who hadn't spoken to him above a sentence at a time since the salt flats, and a grieving twin who had insisted on coming because, as Pip put it, "someone has to carry Marlow's banner, and I'm not letting any of you fold it wrong."

The banner rode rolled up tight across Pip's back. Rell had offered twice to carry it himself. Both times Pip had looked at him with an expression that said the offer was noted, appreciated, and absolutely never going to happen. Rell respected that. Grief needed somewhere to live, and sometimes that somewhere was a folded piece of cloth you refused to hand over.

"Right," Rell said, crouched at the mouth of the storm drain where the pilgrim road bent behind the tannery row. "Rules of my house. Which is to say, rules of a hole in the ground I broke into when I was nineteen."

"We're honored," Sera said dryly.

"You should be. Rule one: nobody touches anything that glows, hums, whispers, or looks expensive. Rule two: if I say *down*, you go down before you finish wondering why. Rule three—" He hesitated. This was the part he'd rehearsed on the walk over, and it still came out rougher than he wanted. "Rule three. Everything I'm about to show you, I learned running messages for Beatrix Ash. So if anyone's tempted to ask why a captain of Veyra's own guard knows the servant tunnels of the palace better than the palace does — that's why. And I'd thank you not to make me say anything else about it."

Silence, except for water dripping somewhere in the dark ahead.

It was Odo who answered, which surprised him. "You don't have to explain yourself to us, Captain."

"I'm not a captain anymore."

"No," Odo agreed, and there was something almost gentle under the flatness of it. "Neither am I. It's a good club. Small meetings."

Edmund made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled by politeness. Even Pip's mouth twitched, just barely, which Rell counted as a victory worth carving into stone.

* * *

The tunnel swallowed them single file, shoulders brushing wet stone. Rell went first with a shuttered lantern cracked open a finger's width, counting turns by feel more than sight. Left at the third weep-stone. Right where the old masons had carved a fish that some apprentice centuries back had given a rude expression. Down the stair that smelled of pigeon and older things.

Behind him, the company moved as well as they could. Edmund was quiet as a man walking through his own thoughts. Sera muttered counter-charms under her breath at every junction, little breaths of warmth against the cold that pooled in these passages — the palace's foundations were laced with wards, and even dormant ones pressed against the skin like a stare.

"The thing people forget," Rell said softly, "is that palaces are built downward before they're built upward. Every king wants towers. But first you dig. Cellars, cisterns, vaults. And every dig needs servants — haulers, lamplighters, corpse-carriers." He glanced back. "The nobles get stairs of marble. The work gets stairs like this. And nobody guards what they're ashamed of."

"That's bleak," Pip said.

"That's architecture."

They passed the first trap without slowing, because Rell had already stopped for it: a stretch of corridor no longer than a cart, floor tiles laid in a pattern that looked casual and wasn't. Weight-triggered. In his day the palace engineers called it the *Gossip's Welcome*, because the only people who ever triggered it were eavesdroppers hurrying along walls.

"Step where I step," he said. "Middle of each tile, never the edges. And whatever happens, do not apologize to the floor. It doesn't care."

"Why would anyone apologize to a—"

Pip's boot clipped a tile edge. Somewhere above them, deep in the stone, a bell the size of a fist gave a single muted *tong* — a sound designed to be heard only by the ears of men paid to listen for it.

Everyone froze. Rell held up a hand, counted ten heartbeats, twenty. No answering movement. No light blooming in the dark ahead.

"Old mechanism," he breathed. "Half its teeth are gone. We got lucky."

"I'm sorry," Pip whispered to the floor.

Rell decided not to rule three him for it.

* * *

An hour down, the tunnels changed character. The rough servant-cuts gave way to older work — fitted stone, joints so tight a knife blade wouldn't enter, and carvings in the arches that Rell didn't look at directly. He'd learned young that some of the old royal iconography looked back.

Sera had no such discipline. She stopped under one arch, lantern raised, and went very still.

"This is bloodwork," she said.

"It's worse than bloodwork," Rell said. "It's *royal* bloodwork. Keep moving."

"No, wait." She traced the air an inch from the carving without touching it. Her fingers left faint heat-ghosts, the way her magic always did when she was reading rather than casting. "These aren't wards against intruders. They're keyed. Look — the lattice runs on lineage. It tastes whoever passes and checks the flavor against a family tree. Anyone without royal blood walks through fine. Anyone *with* it..." She looked at Rell. "What happens to someone with it?"

"Depends how much," Rell said grimly. "A cousin gets a headache and a mark on their skin for a week. Closer kin get worse. There's a story about a bastard prince who tried this way down, sixty years back. They found his boots."

"And the rest of him?"

"They didn't."

Sera absorbed that, then turned slowly to look at Odo.

Odo had stopped walking. He stood in the middle of the passage with his jaw set in the particular way he had when he was deciding whether to be angry or honest, and Rell — who had watched that jaw through two campaigns and one catastrophic court scandal — knew exactly which way it was going to break.

"You knew," Odo said. Not a question.

"I suspected," Rell said. "I've known you nine years, Odo. You read the king's private cipher like your own alphabet. You stand at attention in your sleep. And when we passed the boundary ward at the city wall, it sang. Wards don't sing for commoners. I figured either you were hiding royal blood or you'd eaten something interesting."

Odo was quiet long enough that the drip of water filled the space for him.

"My grandmother," he said finally, "was Aldric's father's sister. Nobody talks about it. She married down on purpose, out of the succession entirely, and made her children swear to bury it deeper than she had. I joined the guard because it was the closest I could legally get to the life I was actually born to." His laugh had no humor in it. "And now it turns out the one thing I've spent my whole life being ashamed of wanting is the one thing that can get us through this door."

"Or get you boots-less," Pip offered.

"Thank you, Pip."

"Anytime."

Sera touched Odo's arm. "We don't have to use it. There may be another way around."

"There isn't," Rell said. "I'm sorry. I checked this route three times in my head on the walk here. The under-vaults seal from the outside with a bloodlock — old Aldric's grandfather built it, paranoid as a cat in a room of rocking chairs. Royal blood is the key. There's no picking it, no burning it, no charming it. Believe me. Beatrix and I spent four nights trying, years ago, just to see if we could." He paused. "She thought it was funny. Locks that could only be opened by the family they were built to protect. She said it was the most honest thing the monarchy ever built."

Something moved through the group at her name — the small flinch they all still did, like a bruise remembered. Rell felt it too. He was getting used to carrying it alongside everything else.

"So we use it," Edmund said.

They all looked at him. He wasn't a dramatic man; when Edmund Yarrow spoke, it was usually because the thing needed saying and no one else had said it. He stood there in the lantern light with his plain face and his gatekeeper's stillness, and he said:

"Odo's spent his whole life paying for other people's secrets. Kings, councils, captains." A glance at Rell, not unkind. "This is the first time one of those secrets opens a door instead of closing one. If it were mine, I'd want to use it."

Odo looked at him for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the way soldiers nod.

"All right," he said. "But if I lose my boots, someone's carrying me."

* * *

The last stretch of tunnel sloped down in a long spiral, and the air changed — drier, colder, tasting of stone that had never seen weather. The traps thickened here. Rell called them out one by one as they came: the pressure plate disguised as a worn step (step *over* it, always over); the ceiling seam that would drop a portcullis if crossed too quickly (walk slow, insultingly slow); the alcove where a sentinel-charm slept, keyed to wake at the sound of more than two voices speaking above a whisper.

At the alcove, the entire company walked in silence for forty feet, which Rell privately considered the most harmonious they had ever been.

Then the tunnel ended, and there it was.

The door was smaller than Rell remembered — memory inflates everything, and he'd first seen this door at nineteen with Beatrix laughing beside him, so of course it had seemed enormous. It was perhaps eight feet of black iron set flush into living rock, and across its face ran the bloodlock: a basin-sized depression ringed by the same lineage-lattice Sera had read upstairs, but denser, hungrier, the carvings so fine they looked like frost.

Nobody spoke. The door did something doors shouldn't do — it seemed to be listening.

"Well," Odo said. He rolled up his sleeve.

"Wait." Sera stepped in close, hands hovering over the lock without touching. "Let me look at it first. If it takes more than a taste — if it *drinks* —"

"It doesn't drink," Rell said. "Beatrix tested it. Pig's blood, goat's blood, a very confused chicken. Nothing. It wants the real thing or nothing at all."

"How did she test a bloodlock without opening it?"

"She was Beatrix."

Sera accepted this the way everyone eventually accepted everything about Beatrix: with a nod and a private grief.

Odo knelt at the basin. He'd produced a knife from somewhere — of course he had, he was Odo — and for a moment he just looked at his own bare forearm, at the veins a lifetime of armor had hidden.

"My grandmother buried this," he said quietly. "Made her children bury it. Made me swear, when I was twelve, that I'd take it to my grave." He set the knife edge to his skin. "Sorry, Gran. It's for the king."

He cut, and pressed his palm into the basin.

For a heartbeat, nothing. Rell's stomach began its slow descent toward disappointment —

Then the frost-fine carvings lit.

Not gold, not the sickly green of Veyra's work, but a deep warm red, like coals seen through closed eyelids, and the light ran outward from Odo's hand along the lattice the way dawn runs along a mountain ridge. The door shuddered. Something enormous and patient disengaged itself, bolt by bolt, deep inside the rock.

And the lock — Rell would swear this until he died — the lock *sang*. One low note, almost below hearing, a note like recognition. Like a house acknowledging family come home after a long war.

Odo snatched his hand back and stared at it. The cut was already closing, the lattice having taken its toll and nothing more.

"It knows me," he said, sounding bewildered and a little betrayed. "That bloody door knows me better than I know myself."

"Welcome to your inheritance," Sera said softly.

The door swung inward on hinges that made no sound at all, revealing darkness beyond — a darkness that was not empty. Rell felt it before he saw it: a pressure, a presence, a vast slow pulse like a heart beating somewhere far beneath the earth.

Edmund went pale. Actually pale, the color draining out of his face in real time, and his hand rose halfway to his chest as though something in there had been struck.

"Edmund?" Sera caught his arm. "What is it? What do you feel?"

Edmund stared into the dark, and when he spoke his voice came out strange — hushed, and horrified, and underneath it something that sounded almost like pity.

"I can hear him," he said. "The king. He's screaming. He's been screaming for years, and none of you could hear it, and I—" He swallowed. "I think I'm the only one who can."

The darkness pulsed again, slow and vast, and far below them, in the vault where the Ember burned in its cage, something turned its attention upward — toward the door, toward the blood, toward the one man in the world that magic could not touch.

Rell reached for his sword out of pure habit, and hated how useless the gesture was.

"Down we go, then," he said. "Rule two. If I say *down* —"

But Edmund was already walking.
