## 26. The Vault of the Ember

The last door was made of a single piece of black stone, and it had no handle.

"Of course it doesn't," Marlow would have said. Pip didn't say anything at all these days unless the words were necessary, and even then he rationed them like a miser counting coins. He stood beside Edmund in the dark of the tunnel, holding a lantern that Sera had charmed to burn without heat, and he looked at the blank stone the way a man looks at a wall he has been asked to argue with.

Rell crouched before it, running his fingers along the seam where stone met floor. "There's a mechanism," he said. "Old. Older than Veyra. The vault predates her by centuries — she didn't build this cage, she just moved into the house."

"The house being the palace," Odo said.

"The house being the *kingdom*," Rell said quietly. "But yes."

Edmund felt the Ember before he saw it. That was the strange thing — everyone else had needed charms and wards and whispered prayers just to get this far, but Edmund had felt it since they'd entered the under-vaults, a pressure against his chest like a hand pressing gently, insistently, the way Beatrix's map had pressed against his ribs for a thousand miles. Magic slid off him like rain off glass. But this — this wasn't sliding. This was knocking.

*Come here,* something below them said, without words. *You're the one I've been calling.*

He hadn't told the others. He wasn't sure how. *The flame in the cage has been whispering my name through solid rock* sounded like the sort of thing a man says right before his companions decide to leave him behind for his own good.

"It wants blood," Rell was saying. "Royal blood. The traps we passed were keyed the same way — remember the corridor of needles? Odo bled on the lock and it opened like a flower." He glanced up at Odo, and there was something complicated in his face. They had not yet spoken plainly about what it meant that Rell knew so much about the defenses of a palace he'd served faithfully until three weeks ago. "This one will want more than a drop."

"How much more?" Sera asked. She stood slightly behind Edmund, close enough that he could feel the warmth of her shoulder near his. Since Halfway Night they had developed a habit of standing near each other without discussing it, an arrangement neither named and both protected fiercely from comment. Pip had tried once, early on, and had received a look from Sera that had reportedly singed his eyebrows.

"Enough to matter," Rell said. "A cupful, maybe. Maybe more."

Odo rolled up his sleeve without ceremony. He was a big man gone soft around the middle in the way of soldiers who'd left the army and found bread again, but his forearms were still corded, and the old scar from Hollow Bridge ran down one of them like a river on a map. "I've given blood for worse causes," he said. "I once gave a full pint to win a bet with a man who turned out to be cheating anyway."

"That's not the inspiring story you think it is," Sera said.

"It's the most honest one I have."

Rell drew a knife across Odo's forearm — carefully, cleanly, the way you cut when you've done it before — and let the blood run into a shallow channel carved into the floor, a groove so fine Edmund hadn't noticed it until now, winding away from the door like the root of some enormous tree. The blood traveled. It actually *traveled*, crawling along the groove against every law of sense, disappearing into the stone.

For a long moment, nothing.

Then the door sighed.

There was no better word for it. The black stone exhaled, a sound like a held breath finally released after years, and split down the middle into two halves that swung inward with the slow dignity of something that had been waiting a very long time and intended to make an entrance of its own.

Warm light spilled out. Not firelight — firelight flickers. This light simply *was*, steady as noon.

"Oh," Sera breathed.

They went in together, because they had agreed on the stairs that whatever waited below, no one would face it alone. Even Pip, who had barely spoken since Ashes for a Twin, kept pace at Edmund's elbow, and Edmund understood without asking: Pip could not bear another person walking ahead of him into the dark and not coming back.

* * *

The vault was round, and vast, and older than anything Edmund had ever seen. The walls were not built stone but living rock, carved with spirals that might have been decoration or might have been writing in a language the world had forgotten on purpose. And in the center, suspended in a cage of white iron —

The Ember.

It was small. That was the first shock. Edmund had imagined, without ever admitting it, something enormous — a bonfire, a sun, a catastrophe. Instead it was a flame the size of a heart, floating at the center of the iron cage, burning without fuel, casting its steady light across the whole chamber. It pulsed. Slowly. Rhythmically.

Like breathing.

Like a heartbeat.

"It's alive," Sera whispered. She had her hands pressed flat over her own sternum, as though holding herself together. "Sera above, it's *alive*. The texts called it a living flame but I thought — I thought that was poetry."

"The texts are usually understating things," Rell said grimly. "That's how they get you."

Edmund stepped forward.

He didn't decide to. His feet simply carried him, the way water finds its level, and none of the others stopped him because stopping him had never once worked and they all knew it. The cage was perhaps ten paces across, white iron forged into bars that spiraled upward and met at a point above the flame, and as Edmund approached, the pulsing quickened.

*Yes,* the Ember said, in that wordless way. *Yes, you.*

Up close, he could see that the flame was not one color. It burned gold at its heart, but around the edges ran threads of other colors — blue, green, a deep bruised violet — moving through it like veins. Like something seen through skin.

And threaded through all of it, binding it, wrapping it the way ivy wraps a tree until you can't tell which is killing which, was a second fire. Darker. Cooler. A lattice of silver-white flame laid over the gold like a net over a bird.

"There," Sera said softly, coming up beside him. Her voice had gone careful, the voice she used for hard truths. "Do you see it? The binding. Veyra wove her will into it — that's the pale fire. The gold underneath is Aldric. Or what's left of him."

Edmund reached out.

"Wait—" Odo started.

His fingers touched the iron bar, and the world screamed.

* * *

It was not sound. Sound would have been a mercy. It came through his null silence — the same silence that swallowed every spell that touched him, the emptiness he had carried his whole life like a burden and never once thought of as a *door* — and what poured through it was a mind.

A king's mind.

*Aldric.* The name arrived whole, with everything attached to it: a boy on a horse in summer, a coronation with rain on the banners, a woman laughing in a garden whose name Edmund somehow knew was Elenne and whose face he somehow knew he must never try to picture because the grief attached to it would drown him. Twenty years of memory compressed into a heartbeat, and beneath all of it, wrapped around all of it, the screaming.

Not rage. Edmund had expected rage. What he heard instead was worse.

*Help me. Help me. Help me. I am here. I am still here. I have been here the whole time. I watched my hands sign the orders. I watched my mouth give the commands. I am in the front row of my own crimes and I cannot look away—*

Edmund staggered back from the cage and the connection snapped, and he found himself on his knees on cold stone with Sera's arms around him and four voices saying his name.

"He's alive," Edmund gasped. "He's *alive*, he's in there, he's been awake the whole time —"

"We knew that," Rell said, but gently. "We hoped. We—"

"No. You hoped he was alive the way you hope a prisoner is alive. He is *aware*. Every order Veyra gave in his name, he felt himself give it. Every soldier who died carrying those orders —" Edmund's stomach heaved and he pressed his fist to his mouth. "He counted them. He counts them still. He knows the number. He knows it to the man."

Silence fell on the vault, the terrible kind, the kind that has weight.

Pip sat down slowly on the floor, cross-legged, the way he used to sit as a boy — Edmund knew this without knowing how he knew it, the knowledge arriving sideways through the grief on Pip's face. "Marlow always said," Pip said, and it was the longest sentence he'd offered in weeks, "that the worst thing Veyra did wasn't the wars. It was making everyone complicit. Making the whole kingdom her hands so no one could be clean." He looked up at the cage. "She did it to the king first."

"She did it to all of us first," Odo said. "Some of us just took longer to notice."

Edmund got his feet under him. His hands were shaking. He looked at the Ember — at the gold flame beating inside its cage of iron and stolen will — and felt something settle in him, hard and clear as frost.

"We can do this," he said. "Whatever the ritual needs. Whatever it costs. He's been in there twenty years, listening to himself destroy his own kingdom. We can do this."

"You touched the cage for half a heartbeat," Sera said quietly. "The unbinding will take hours. Edmund — I don't know if anyone can survive hearing that for hours."

"I'm the only one who can hear it at all," Edmund said. "So it'll have to be me."

He meant it as courage. It came out as a sentence, and he heard that too, and hated it a little.

* * *

They made their preparations the way travelers make camp: with routine, with small jokes, with the practiced choreography of people who had been doing hard things side by side for long enough that the hard things had worn grooves. Sera chalked the ritual circle while explaining each mark aloud, partly for the record and partly because talking steadied her. Odo and Rell went back up the stair to watch the tunnels, and Edmund caught the tail end of their conversation — something about a rotation, something about who slept first — conducted in the clipped shorthand of two men who had fought on opposite sides of a war and then a bridge and then a hundred miles of road, and had finally, somewhere along the way, stopped keeping score.

Pip stayed. Pip sat against the far wall with his lantern and his silence and watched the Ember pulse, and Edmund understood that Pip was keeping vigil — for Marlow, maybe, or for the king, or for the version of the world where brothers grew old and argued about nothing. Edmund didn't disturb him. Some vigils you keep alone even in company.

"Tell me what you need," Edmund said to Sera.

"Time," she said. "Mostly time. The circle will hold the binding still while I unpick it — think of it as pinning a sleeve so you can mend the seam. But the Ember will fight. It's not malicious, it's just — it doesn't want to come apart. Nothing living does." She looked up at him, and in the Ember's steady light her face was very young and very tired and very brave. "When it fights, it won't show you monsters. It'll show you what you want. That's how it defends itself. Everyone who ever tried to steal it was undone by their own wanting."

"And me?"

"You," Sera said, and smiled — a real smile, crooked and warm, the one he'd crossed half a continent to see. "You're the only person I've ever met who doesn't want anything magic can give. You want your gate mended and your friends safe and someone to share breakfast with. The Ember has nothing to offer you. That's why it called you. That's why it's been calling you since the valley." She touched his cheek, briefly, as if sealing a letter. "You're not the sword, Edmund Yarrow. You're the hand that can hold it."

He wanted to say something worthy of that. What came out was: "Breakfast sounds very good, actually."

"It does," she agreed solemnly. "Eggs. Real ones. After this, I'm going to eat an entire farm."

* * *

The ritual began at what Sera judged to be midnight, though underground, midnight was a matter of faith.

She stood at the circle's edge and began to speak — not the dramatic incantations of ballads but something low and patient, a working rhythm, a weaver's song. The chalked lines woke with a faint blue glow. The Ember's pulsing stuttered.

And Edmund, standing at the cage with his bare hand resting on the white iron, opened the door in his silence and let the king in.

The screaming hit him first, and then, beneath it, everything else.

*Who are you?* The voice was a man's voice, worn thin, frayed at the edges like a banner left out through too many winters. *You have no fire in you. How do you stand in my storm with no fire?*

"My name is Edmund Yarrow," Edmund said aloud, though his lips barely moved; the conversation happened in the space between heartbeats. "I kept a gate in a valley without magic. I'm here to take the binding off you."

*The gate.* Something moved through the trapped mind — wonder, or its ghost. *The wardens. I forgot the wardens. My grandfather built the valley, did you know? A place where the fire couldn't follow. He said every kingdom needs one room where the shouting stops.* A pause, and then, terribly: *I would give anything for a room where the shouting stops.*

"Hold on," Edmund said. "Hold on. It's coming off. There's a witch up here unpicking it thread by thread, and she's very good, and she's very stubborn, and she has friends who don't quit. Hold on."

*Twenty years,* Aldric said. *Do you know what she made me sign? Do you know about Correnvale's grain tariffs? About the garrison at—*

"I know about the garrison at Hollow Bridge," Edmund said, and thought of Marlow, and let the king feel it, because some things needed sharing to stay bearable. "I know what your signature cost. So does a boy sitting against that wall. So we're going to fix it. Not forgive it — fix it. Those are different jobs and we're only qualified for one."

The mind in the flame went quiet. Then, faintly, disbelievingly, almost human:

*You scold very well for a gatekeeper.*

"I've had practice. I guarded a door for thirty years. Doors require firm boundaries."

Something that might, in a healthier mind, have been laughter stirred in the gold. And then Sera's song changed key, and the binding flinched, and the Ember defended itself.

* * *

It showed Edmund his mother's kitchen.

That was the weapon it chose. Not power, not glory — the kitchen in the warden's house, bread on the table, the smell of yeast and woodsmoke, his mother humming, the specific quality of late-afternoon light through a valley window where no spell had ever fallen and no war had ever come. *Stay,* the vision murmured, wrapping around him like a blanket. *Go home. Open the gate, walk through, close it behind you. The valley is safe. The valley is quiet. No one dies there. No one you love can die there.*

And Edmund stood in the false kitchen and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, exactly why the Ember had chosen this.

Because it was true. All of it. He *wanted* it. He wanted it so badly his chest ached. He could go back. Veyra had offered him the valley itself; the Ember offered him something smaller and crueler — the ordinary life, the unmourned life, the life where Sera was a stranger he'd never failed and Marlow was alive in a world where Edmund had never met him.

"Thank you," Edmund said to the empty kitchen. "But I've seen the menu, and I'm ordering something else."

The kitchen dissolved like breath off glass.

What replaced it was the vault, and Sera's voice cracking on a high note of the working, and the binding — the pale lattice — beginning, at long last, to fray. Threads of silver-white fire were lifting off the gold like smoke rising off a doused candle, and each thread that lifted made the scream in Edmund's skull change pitch: less *help me*, more *I can breathe*.

"It's working!" Sera called, sweat shining on her face. "Edmund, keep the contact, keep talking to him — the binding holds through despair, I need him hoping—"

"He's hoping," Edmund said. "Aren't you?"

*I am,* the king said, and the gold flame surged, brightening, and for one impossible moment the vault was full of summer light and Edmund thought: *we've done it, we've actually—*

From far above them — up through the servant tunnels, up through the thief's ways, up through six floors of sleeping palace — came a sound.

A bell. One clean silver note, then another, then a third, ringing fast and getting faster.

Rell appeared at the head of the stair, and Edmund had served with enough soldiers' faces by now to read his instantly.

"The alarm," Rell said. "Veyra knows."
