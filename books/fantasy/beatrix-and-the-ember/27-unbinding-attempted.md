## 27. Unbinding, Attempted

The vault had a smell Sera would remember for the rest of her life, however long or short that turned out to be. Old stone and old gold and, underneath it, something like a hearth left burning in an empty house — warmth with no one home.

The Ember hung in the center of the vault, caged in a lattice of white iron that might have been beautiful once, before it was made into a prison. The flame inside it was small. That was the horror of it, Sera thought. All that power — a king's will, a kingdom's fate, a sorceress's leash — and it was the size of a candle flame. It pulsed slowly, like something breathing in its sleep. Or dreaming.

"Tell me again what I'm supposed to do," Edmund said. He stood at the edge of the ritual circle Sera had chalked into the ancient floor, arms folded, looking like a man asked to hold a snake.

"You're not supposed to do anything," Sera said. "That's the point of you. Stand there. Want nothing. Be a door that opens onto nothing."

"That's the second time someone's called me a door this week. I'm starting to take it personally."

"Good. Take it personally. Anger's easy to hold onto." She knelt and pressed her palm flat against the chalked circle, feeling for the old channels in the stone. They were there — grooves worn by hands a thousand years dead, made for exactly this work. Whoever built this vault had built it knowing it would need to be undone. That was either comforting or damning, and Sera hadn't decided which.

Around the vault, the others were doing what she'd asked of them, which was mostly staying out of the way and listening for trouble. Odo stood at the stairhead with his sword drawn and his back to the ritual, which Sera knew cost him — a guard who couldn't see the thing he was guarding against. Rell was at the opposite wall, armored again, though the armor sat strangely on him now, like a coat borrowed from a man he no longer was. Pip had insisted on staying, red-eyed and stubborn, holding Marlow's banner folded under one arm like a shield.

"Everyone remembers the rules?" Sera said, not looking up from the circle.

"Don't touch the flame," Pip said.

"Don't listen to the flame," Odo said.

"And if the flame shows you something lovely," Rell added quietly, "it's lying."

"It's not lying, exactly." Sera sat back on her heels. "That's what makes it dangerous. It'll show you the truest thing you've ever seen. It'll just leave out the price."

* * *

She began with the old words, the ones from the fragment Odo had recovered in the under-city, the ones that had taken three nights and two arguments to translate. The language was dead — had been dead since before the Binding — and it tasted like river water and iron on her tongue.

The circle lit.

Not brightly. A low gold shimmer, like sun through honey, running the grooves in the floor until the whole vault was webbed with light. Above her, the Ember pulsed faster.

*No,* said something that was not a sound. *No, no, no.*

"There it is," Sera murmured. "Hello to you too."

The flame flared. The iron lattice sang — a high, thin note that Sera felt in her back teeth. And then the vault changed.

It was subtle at first. The shadows deepened. The gold light of the circle warmed. And Sera felt it reach for her — not her body, not her magic, but the soft place underneath, the place where a hedge-witch keeps the things she's too busy to grieve.

It showed her a cottage.

Not any cottage. *The* cottage — the one she'd drawn in the margins of her spellbooks since she was twelve years old. Thatched roof, slightly crooked. A garden going gloriously to seed because she could never bring herself to weed anything that was trying so hard to live. Smoke from the chimney. And in the doorway, Edmund, sleeves rolled, arguing cheerfully with a goat.

*This,* said the Ember, in a voice like her own mother's, like warm bread, like every kind thing anyone had ever said to her and meant it. *This is what you want. Put down the words. Let the circle close. It's already yours. You only have to stop reaching for it.*

"It's not real," Sera said through her teeth, and kept chanting.

*It's more real than this vault. It's more real than the war. Who will know? Who will mourn one unbinding delayed by a season? The king has been bound for years — what is one more?*

"Every day is a man in a cage," she said. "I've seen his eyes. Well — his eyes' echo. Edmund saw them. Ask him."

But the cottage stayed. The smoke curled. The goat bleated. And Sera, chanting the unbinding with sweat running down her spine, understood with cold clarity that the Ember had found the exact shape of her heart and was standing on it.

She did the only thing she could. She changed the chant — dropped into the second verse early, the harsh one, the one about debts and reckonings — and the cottage flickered like a candle in wind.

* * *

It went for Odo next.

Sera saw it happen — saw the big man sway at the stairhead, saw his sword arm drop. And she heard him speak, in a voice she'd never heard him use, young and cracked and desperate:

"Your Majesty — I found them — I found the traitors, I — no. No, I didn't. I warned them. I warned them and they died anyway, and it was my fault, it was my—"

"Odo." Rell's voice, sharp as a whistle. "Odo. Look at me. It's showing you the court. It's showing you the pardon that never came."

"I could have it," Odo whispered. "I could go back. I could do it right this time. He'd take me back, he'd—"

"He'd what?" Rell crossed the floor in four strides and gripped Odo's shoulder, armor and all. "You told me once, on the salt flats, that the worst day of your life was the day you realized duty and the crown weren't the same thing. Was that a lie?"

"No."

"Then it's lying now. That's not Aldric asking for you. That's the cage asking for company."

Odo's breath came ragged. Sera kept chanting, and watched, and hated that she couldn't stop to help — and then watched Odo plant his sword point-down on the stone and lean on it like a walking stick, an old man's posture, a stubborn man's posture.

"I'm not going back," he said, to the flame, to the vault, to himself. "I'm going *forward*. There's a difference. My wife taught me that." He straightened. "Show me the throne room all you like. I already know how it ends. I'm standing in it."

The vision slid off him like water off glass.

* * *

It went for Rell, and Rell went down like a felled tree.

One moment he was at his wall; the next he was on his knees, gauntleted hands pressed flat to the floor, and he was saying a name over and over — "Beatrix. Beatrix. Bea." — and the vault around him had gone soft with lamplight that wasn't there.

Sera's heart clenched. Of all of them, Rell was the one the Ember would find softest. It wouldn't show him a pardon or a cottage. It would show him her.

"She's alive in it," Rell whispered. "It's showing me — she's at a table, she's laughing, she's — she says she waited for me. She says if I let the circle close she'll—"

"She's dead, Rell." Pip's voice. Small and steady and terrible. Pip had buried a twin three provinces ago and knew the exact weight of this lie. "I know that voice. I've heard it in my own head every night since the bridge. It sounds exactly like them. It's not them. It's just the shape of the hole they left."

Rell made a sound that Sera would never describe to anyone, and would never forget.

Then he stood up. Slowly, joint by joint, like a man hauling himself out of deep water. "She told me once," he said hoarsely, "that the difference between a soldier and a man is what you do when nobody's making you. Nobody's making me." He wiped his face with the back of his gauntlet. "Keep chanting, witch."

* * *

It went for Pip last, and Sera nearly broke the circle to stop it.

Because Pip didn't fight. Pip turned toward the vision with his whole face open, and Sera saw what the Ember had found: Marlow. Whole. Unburned. Standing in the vault's false light with that lopsided grin, holding out a hand.

"Come on, Pip," the not-Marlow said. "We're leaving. Both of us. The banner too. We'll go to the coast like we planned, remember? You, me, a boat we can't sail. Put down the chalk, Sera won't mind, she'll understand—"

"Marlow's dead," Pip said.

"Am I?"

"You're dead, and it's the worst thing in the world, and I'm going to spend the rest of my life being sad about it." Pip's voice cracked down the middle. "But you're not *him*. He'd never ask me to leave them. He died *for* me. He'd haunt me if I traded them for a picture of him."

The not-Marlow smiled — and for one awful moment the smile was the Ember's smile, old and hungry and gold — and then it was gone, and the vault was a vault again, cold and iron-smelling, and Pip was standing alone with a folded banner and tears running freely down his face and his feet planted like roots.

"I'm staying," he said. "Write that down, witch. In case I forget it later. I stayed."

Sera's voice shook on the next line of the chant, and she didn't let it stop her.

* * *

Through all of it, Edmund stood at the circle's edge and watched.

Sera kept glancing at him between verses — she couldn't help it — and what she saw never changed. The visions broke over him like weather over a mountain. Once, she saw the air in front of him shimmer with something golden and pleading, saw it press against him like a cat against a closed door, and saw Edmund tilt his head and say, mildly:

"No, thank you."

The Ember flared, almost petulant.

"I mean it," Edmund told the flame conversationally. "Whatever you're selling. I had a valley for forty years. Quiet, no taxes, excellent stargazing. You're offering me things I already learned to live without. That's not a temptation, that's a refund."

The flame surged — Sera felt the whole ritual strain against it, felt the unbinding catch on the Ember's fury like a cart in mud — and then Edmund did the thing that only Edmund would do. He stepped *closer*. Right to the circle's edge. Close enough that the gold light washed over his boots.

"You're tired," he said to it. Not unkindly. "Aren't you? You've been burning in a box for years, doing a cruel job for someone crueler. I know what that's like. I stood at a gate my whole life for a lie." He crouched, so his eyes were level with the caged flame, and Sera's heart stopped — because nothing, nothing should be able to look at the Ember directly, and Edmund looked at it the way he looked at everything, plainly, without flinching. "I'm not going to hurt you. I'm going to open the cage. But I'm not going to want what you're offering, and you can't make me, and I think that's the first time anyone's ever told you no."

The Ember burned very still.

And Sera, chanting, felt the ritual *turn* — felt the unbinding find its grip at last, the old grooves lighting true, the dead language locking into place like a key in a lock a thousand years rusted shut.

"It's working," she breathed. "It's working — hold the door, all of you, it knows what we're doing now—"

Above them, far above, through miles of stone and ceremony, something began to ring. Bells? Alarms? The vault's white lattice groaned, and the Ember's pulse went from sleeping-breath to gallop, and the warmth in the air curdled into pressure, into *attention*.

Odo drew his sword. Rell drew his. Pip raised the folded banner like a spear.

And down the long stair, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of footsteps. Many. Unhurried.

The Ember pulsed once, twice — and in its light Sera saw the flame turn, deliberately, toward Edmund, and she heard it speak for the first time in a voice with words, a voice like a hearth and a cage and a woman's cold amusement all at once:

*She is coming.*

The footsteps on the stair did not hurry. They didn't need to.
