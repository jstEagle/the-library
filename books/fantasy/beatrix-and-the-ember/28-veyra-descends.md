## 28. Veyra Descends

The alarm woke her the way a hand wakes you from a dream of drowning — sudden, certain, and with your own name in its mouth.

Veyra opened her eyes in the dark of her chambers and felt it at once: the Ember, three hundred feet below her feet, singing out of tune. Someone had touched it. Someone was *touching* it.

She sat up. The Binding Day banners she'd had hung from her ceiling — crimson silk, gold thread, the twin crowns of Aldric and herself stitched side by side — stirred in a wind that had no source. Her magic did that when she was angry. It leaked. She had spent thirty years learning to keep it leashed, and thirty years being disappointed.

"Well," she said to the empty room, "isn't that rude."

She dressed without servants. She had dismissed them all an hour before dawn, which they had found strange, and which would have been stranger still if any of them had known what she knew: that by nightfall, some of them would be dead, and she preferred not to learn their faces first. A small mercy. She allowed herself perhaps four a year, and this was one.

The mirror above her washbasin showed her a woman of fifty-odd years with silver shot through her black hair and eyes the color of banked coals. She looked tired. She *was* tired. Ruling an empire you had stolen was exhausting work, whatever the ballads said, and the ballads were already saying things. She'd heard the one about the null man and the burning map. Minstrels had no respect for operational security.

She pressed two fingers to the mirror's surface, and it went soft as pond water.

"Rell," she said.

Nothing answered. Of course nothing answered. Rell had gone silent eleven days ago, somewhere on the Salt Flats, and his last report had contained the phrase *"the Ash woman is dead"* written in a hand that shook. She had read it forty times. She had burned it twice and reconstructed it twice, because she could not decide whether she wanted it destroyed or preserved, and this indecision annoyed her more than the treason itself.

"Captain Rell," she said again, to no one, "you sentimental fool."

Then she stepped into the mirror, and the palace swallowed her whole.

* * *

She came out of the glass in the antechamber above the vault stairs and immediately understood that her evening had gone poorly.

The door to the stairwell hung off its hinges — not broken, *unmade*, the iron unbraided back into ore. Two of her vault wardens lay crumpled against the far wall, breathing, which meant whoever had done it had been merciful or hurried, and both possibilities offended her. A third warden, young, wide-eyed, was pressing himself flat against the floor and babbling.

"Your Majesty — Your Majesty, there were only six of them, we had thirty—"

"Six," Veyra said.

"The big one broke Ferren's shield like — like bread, and the witch, the witch was *awful*, she turned the whole corridor to brambles, and then the null man just walked through everything, he walked right through my warding like it wasn't—" The boy stopped. He had realized, belatedly, whom he was describing, and what it meant that his warding hadn't held, and what she might do to him for it.

"Get up," Veyra said. "You're going to run to the barracks and tell them to seal the upper levels. If anyone asks why, tell them the Queen commands it and looks very frightening, which is true."

He scrambled up and fled. She watched him go with something adjacent to affection. Fear made people honest; terror made them fast. It was one of the few reliable rules left in the world.

Then she descended the stairs toward the sound of battle.

She did not hurry. Hurrying was for people who could lose. She took each step at a stately pace, feeling the wards along the walls flare and die under her passing — her own wards, keyed to her blood, and yet they flinched from her now, the way a dog flinches from an owner who has beaten it once too often. She had not beaten them. She had simply asked them to do terrible things, over and over, until they forgot how to do anything else.

There was a word for that. Several words. She had heard most of them screamed at her from prison cells.

From below came light — gold-white, stuttering, the Ember's color bleeding up the stairwell like dawn through a keyhole. And voices. She catalogued them as she descended, the way she catalogued everything: the hedge-witch chanting in Old Corvene, the disgraced guard roaring orders, the twins' quick treble answering each other across the vault mouth, the wounded traitor Rell — *there* he was, alive after all, and fighting on the wrong side, which she had known since the Salt Flats and had refused, until this moment, to feel anything about.

And beneath it all, a silence. A hole in the world's noise, shaped like a man.

The null. Edmund Yarrow. Warden of the Valley Gate.

She had been waiting for him for twenty years without knowing his name.

* * *

The vault opened before her like the inside of a struck bell.

It was a round chamber older than the palace above it, older than the kingdom, its walls carved with the names of every flame-tender since the First Kindling — names worn smooth now, half of them illegible, because even the people who built monuments to memory eventually forget to dust it. In the center rose the Ember's cage: seven rings of cold-forged iron, unmagicked, unmagickable, turning slowly around a flame that burned without heat and gave light without flickering.

The flame was screaming. Only Veyra could hear it, and only because she had taught it to scream — had bound King Aldric's living will into it strand by strand, the way you bind a hawk, the way you bind anything that used to be free. It had taken her nine years. It had cost her things she did not itemize anymore.

Around the cage stood six people and one corpse's worth of hope.

The hedge-witch — Sera, the reports said, Sera of the Bramble Road — stood at the cage's base with both hands raised, sweat pouring down her face, weaving the unbinding working thread by luminous thread. The big guard, Odo, planted himself between her and the stair with a tower shield. The twins flanked him with knives and the particular ferocity of people who have already buried someone this season. Rell stood apart, sword drawn, facing her, and his face when he saw her was the worst thing in the room: not hatred, but grief wearing hatred's clothes.

And beside the cage, one hand resting flat against the iron as though greeting an old horse, stood a tall, plain, gray-eyed man who looked — there was no kinder word for it — ordinary. Dusty boots. Mended coat. The face of a man who had spent his life doing one quiet job in one quiet place.

Edmund Yarrow turned and looked at her, and Veyra felt, for the first time in nine years, the Ember's binding *slacken* — just slightly, just at the edges, the way a knot loosens when the rope goes slack — because his null touch was drinking the current straight out of the air.

"Ah," she said pleasantly. "The gatekeeper."

"You know me." His voice was steady. Good. Steady men were harder to break, and she hated breaking things slowly.

"I have known of you since before you could walk," she said. "I have files on your grandmother. I know what valley bread tastes like, secondhand, from a courier's report. You are the last of a line of locks, Master Yarrow, bred and kept and lied to for four hundred years, and tonight you are standing in my house touching my fire." She smiled, and let a little of the coal-light rise in her eyes, because fear was a tool and dull tools wasted effort. "So let us skip the part where you pretend to be surprised that I'm here."

"Sera," Odo rumbled, without turning. "How close?"

"Close," Sera gasped. "Don't let her talk. Whatever she says — don't listen. She makes true things sound like lies and lies sound like—"

"Lies sound like truth?" Veyra finished gently. "Child, I am eighty-one years old. I have no time for complicated lies anymore. They tangle. Ask anyone I've ever ruled." She began to walk, slowly, along the curve of the wall, and the twins shifted to track her, and Odo's knuckles whitened on his shield. "Ask the grain farmers whose blight I ended. Ask the border villages I pulled out of the succession wars. Ask Aldric himself, if you can find him in there." She nodded at the screaming flame. "He'll tell you. He begged me to take the crown. He was drowning in a kingdom and I offered him a raft, and the raft happened to be steered by me. History will sort out whether that was tyranny. I won't live to see its verdict, so I've stopped worrying."

"You killed Marlow," Pip said. Small voice. Enormous weight.

"I have never met Marlow," Veyra said, "and I am sorry for your loss, and I did not order the bridge ambush, and you will never believe any of those three sentences in that order, so let us move on." She stopped walking. She let her power gather — not to strike, not yet, but to be *seen*, the pressure of it filling the vault like water rising, and she watched each of them feel it. The twins swayed. Odo set his feet. Sera's chant faltered and recovered.

Only the null man stood unchanged. The pressure slid off him like rain off glass. She had imagined this moment for months, and it was better than she'd imagined: standing in front of a hole in the world and watching her own storm disappear into it.

"There it is," she said softly. "Do you know what you are, Edmund Yarrow? Do you actually know? Or did they send you out with a map and a swear-word and no explanation, the way they always do?"

"He knows enough," Sera said sharply.

"He knows nothing," Veyra said. "That is the entire design. That has always been the entire design." She turned to Edmund fully, and she dropped the performance — the coal eyes, the rising pressure, the queen's cadence — and spoke to him the way one farmer speaks to another across a fence. It was, she reflected, the most honest she had been in a decade. "Warden. Ask yourself one question, and answer it honestly, because your life depends on the honesty and not on the answer. In all your years at that gate — what exactly were you guarding against?"

Edmund's brow furrowed. "Magic. The valley's sealed so magic can't—"

"Can't what? Follow whom? Harm what?" She spread her hands. "There is nothing in that valley, Warden. No prisoner. No horror. No ancient evil sleeping under the lake, whatever the founding stones say. I have looked. I looked for eleven years, early in my reign, because I too believed the stories — I sent diviners, delvers, blood-hounds, and they found sheep, and barley, and your family's graves. There is nothing behind that gate but peace." She let that sit. "Peace purchased with a lie. Because a lie keeps wardens loyal. Because a man will guard a prison forever, but a man told he's guarding nothing will ask why he's guarding at all — and then he'll open the gate himself, and the valley ends, and the wars come in, and the mages come in, and everything your line bled for drowns in a fortnight."

"That's not—" Odo started.

"It is," Veyra said, "and the proof is standing in front of you holding a burning map. Who gave him that map? Beatrix Ash. Who sent Beatrix Ash? The free cities' conspiracy. Why? To use a magicless man as a walking siege-ladder against my vault. They didn't recruit him because they trusted him. They recruited him because he's *immune*. Because to them he isn't a man, he's a tool that happens to breathe." She watched Edmund's face do something small and terrible. "They told you that you were chosen, Warden. Chosen implies someone thought you mattered. What they mean is that you're useful, and those are different words, and I am the only person in this room who will ever say so to your face."

The vault was very quiet. Even the Ember's scream seemed to lean in.

Sera's voice cracked across the silence like a whip. "Finish the ritual, Edmund. Don't listen. She's good at this — she's had lifetimes of practice making chains sound like kindness—"

"And you've had weeks of practice making a weapon sound like a calling," Veyra said, without heat. "Hedge-witch. I know what the ford-witch told you. I know what you haven't told him." She saw Sera go white, and felt no triumph at all, which was the trouble with truths: they cut everyone in reach. "Yes. I know that too. I know most things. It's the loneliest talent in the world and I would give it away tomorrow if anyone could carry it."

"Why are you talking?" Rell said suddenly. His voice was raw. He hadn't looked away from her since she entered. "Why are you *talking*, Veyra? You don't talk. You arrive and things end. What are you waiting for?"

And there it was. The question underneath the evening.

Veyra looked at Captain Rell — at the boy she'd commissioned, the soldier she'd promoted, the lover her enforcer had been to Beatrix Ash in the years before either of them chose their sides — and she told the truth, because she was tired, and because the hour was late, and because lies had gotten heavy.

"I'm waiting," she said, "because if I destroy you all, the binding holds, and Aldric stays mine, and the empire stays whole, and I win — and I have learned, over nine long years, exactly what winning costs, and I am no longer sure I can afford it. And if I let you finish, the king wakes, and the empire fractures, and the provinces burn each other for a decade, and the thing I built dies screaming. So yes, Captain. I'm talking. Talking is what you do when every other road is bad." She laughed, short and bitter. "Your histories will never write that down. Tyrants aren't permitted doubts. But I am standing in front of the only man in the world my magic cannot touch, asking him to consider that maybe — *maybe* — the lock he guarded his whole life was keeping out something worse than me."

"Which is what?" Edmund said quietly.

"Everything," Veyra said. "Every ambition I've chained. Every province that wants its neighbor's fields. Every mage-king waiting beyond the mountains for the empire to crack. You think I'm the monster, Warden. I am the *fence*. And fences are hated by everything on both sides of them."

Behind her, Sera's chant climbed to its final movement — he heard it, they all heard it, the working gathering itself like a wave deciding to fall — and Veyra did not move to stop it. She stood with her hands open at her sides and watched Edmund Yarrow watch her, and she put every ounce of her terrible sincerity into the last thing she said:

"They built your valley on a lie, they bred your bloodline for a purpose they hid from you, they handed you a dead woman's map and called it destiny, and the woman you love is planning to die for you without telling you. I am the villain of this story, Edmund Yarrow. Ask yourself, before that wave falls — why does everyone else's version of saving the world require *you* to be sacrificed?"

The working crested. The Ember roared. The cage-rings shuddered —

— and Edmund Yarrow, the null, the lock, the last warden of the Valley Gate, took his hand off the iron and stepped back.

The unbinding collapsed like a breath let go.

Sera cried out — a sound Veyra would remember longer than she wanted to. Odo turned, stricken. And in the ringing dark of the vault, with her armies sealing the levels above and her oldest soldier pointing a sword at her heart, Veyra looked at the faltering company and said, almost kindly:

"Now. Shall we talk about what happens next — or would you prefer I let the fire answer for me?"

The Ember's light guttered, and somewhere far above, the palace doors began to bang.
