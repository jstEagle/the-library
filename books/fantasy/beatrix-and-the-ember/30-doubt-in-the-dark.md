## 30. Doubt in the Dark

The under-city smelled of wet stone and old candles and, faintly, of someone's dinner — which was cruel, because Odo hadn't eaten since the vault.

He sat with his back against a wall that had probably been a temple once, in a cellar that had definitely been a smuggler's cache more recently, judging by the crates. The company had fled down through the under-city for hours after Rell died holding the stair, following Marlow's old routes — the twins had known these tunnels the way other people knew their own names — until Pip found them this hollow and refused to go one step further.

Nobody argued. That was the worst part. A company that didn't argue was a company that had stopped believing there was anywhere to argue *to*.

Across the cellar, Edmund sat apart from everyone, which was also new, and also wrong. He sat the way he used to sit at his gate, Odo realized. Shoulders drawn in. Hands quiet in his lap. A man guarding something that wasn't there anymore.

Sera was wrapped in her own cloak by the cold-lamp, not sleeping. Her hands were burned from the ritual — the Ember had fought her like a living thing fights, and it had left its marks along her forearms in pale weals that no salve would touch. She kept flexing her fingers as if checking they still answered to her.

Pip hadn't spoken since the stair. Not since Rell went down under Veyra's fire with his sword up and his mouth open around some last joke none of them would ever hear. Pip sat against the far wall with his knees up and his arms around them, making himself small, and every few minutes his eyes went to the tunnel mouth as if he expected Rell to come limping out of it, complaining about the stairs, asking what everyone was crying about.

Odo knew that look. He'd worn it himself, twenty years ago, when King Aldric's father died and Odo had stood guard outside the door expecting the old man to walk through it any moment because kings didn't die, they just went somewhere else for a while.

Kings died. Captains died. And sometimes the plan died with them, and what you were left holding was a magicless man who wouldn't stop apologizing.

"I'm sorry," Edmund said, to no one, again. It was maybe the sixth time. "I froze. She said — what she said about the gate, about my family, and I just—"

"Edmund." Odo kept his voice level, the parade-ground voice, the one that could hold a line of green soldiers steady while the arrows came down. "If you apologize one more time I'm going to make you do push-ups, and I don't care that there's no floor drill instructor here to appreciate it."

That got him a flicker. Barely a flicker. But flickers were how fires started; he'd learned that from Beatrix, secondhand, through Sera's stories.

"She lied to me," Edmund said. "Or she told the truth. I don't know which is worse. My whole life, Odo. My whole life was built on a door, and now I don't know if the door was keeping something *out* or keeping us *in*."

* * *

They ate eventually. Sera produced hardtack and dried plums from her pack with the grim competence of a woman who had been feeding outlaws through disasters for a decade, and food helped the way food always did — not much, but measurably.

It was Pip who broke first, which surprised Odo, because he'd have bet silver on Sera or even Edmund. Pip set down his biscuit half-eaten and said, in a voice scraped raw: "Rell was going to teach me the low guard. He promised. He said after the capital, when this was done, he'd show me how to fight left-handed so nobody could read me."

Nobody said anything. There was nothing to say that wasn't a lie, and lies were heavier than silence.

"He let us go," Pip went on, angrier now, anger being easier than grief. "At the ford, he had us dead to rights and he let us go, and then he came back anyway, and then he—" His voice cracked clean through. "Why do people keep *doing that*. Dying for us. Like we're worth it."

"You are," Odo said simply.

"You don't know that."

"No," Odo agreed. "I don't *know* it. I've decided it. There's a difference, and the difference is everything. Knowing is for gods and cowards. Deciding is for soldiers."

Pip stared at him. Then, slowly, he picked the biscuit back up and finished it, which in the language of grieving nineteen-year-olds was practically a treaty signing.

Edmund watched all this from his corner, and Odo saw something move behind his eyes that Odo didn't like at all. It was the look of a man doing arithmetic he shouldn't be doing. Counting costs. Tallying names.

"Don't," Odo said quietly, when the others had drifted into exhausted half-sleep.

"Don't what?"

"Don't start deciding we're not worth it either. You've got the same face Pip had."

Edmund looked down at his hands. "She showed me things, Odo. In the vault. Veyra. She said the gate was built to imprison something worse than her. She said my family — generations of us — we weren't guardians, we were *locks*. Made. Bred. Chosen before we could choose anything." His laugh had no humor in it whatsoever. "And the worst part isn't that it might be true. The worst part is that it explains everything. Why the valley has no magic. Why nothing ever came through the gate in three hundred years. Why I never fit anywhere except standing in front of a door."

"And if it's true?"

"Then I'm not a man who chose his duty. I'm a hinge someone else hung."

Odo chewed on that for a while, because it deserved chewing. Around them the under-city dripped and settled, and somewhere far off water moved through channels the old builders had cut, patient as anything.

"When I was twenty-two," Odo said, "I took an oath to Aldric. Kneeling, hand on the crown-sword, the whole theater of it. And I meant it. Every word. Do you know why I meant it?"

Edmund shook his head.

"Because I decided to. Nobody made me kneel. My father wanted me farming. The academy nearly rejected me — wrong build, wrong temper. Everything about my life pointed away from that throne room, and I walked into it anyway and knelt down and gave away my whole future on purpose." He leaned forward. "Now. Here's the thing, Edmund. If Veyra stood in front of me tomorrow and proved — proved beyond doubt — that the crown had arranged my whole path, bought my instructors, steered my feet — do you know what I'd say?"

"What?"

"I'd say: *and yet I knelt.* Whatever she did to the road, I'm the one who walked it. And whatever your family did to the valley, you're the one who stood at that gate for thirty years with no one watching and no one thanking you, and that was yours. She can't have it. That's the one thing a liar can't steal — what you actually did."

Edmund's jaw worked. "You sound like Beatrix."

"I'll take that as the compliment it is."

* * *

It was near what passed for morning down here — Sera counted hours by a little sand-glass she turned without looking at it, a habit so deep it survived capture, war, and the apocalypse — when Sera finally spoke the thing she'd been not-speaking since the ford.

Odo saw it coming. He'd seen her circle it twice already, testing the ice. Now she set down her cup, folded her burned hands in her lap, and said, "There's something you all need to hear. And I need you to let me say all of it before anyone argues."

The cellar went still.

"At the river ford," she said, "the witch read my cards. Properly read them — not the market-fortune nonsense, the real thing, the kind that leaves marks. She told me two lines. The first: *you will save the man you love.*"

Her eyes went to Edmund. Edmund went very carefully blank, the way men do when a feeling arrives too large for the room.

"The second line," Sera said, steady as a surgeon, "was: *by losing yourself.*"

The words dropped into the dark like stones into a well. Odo heard them land all the way down.

"That's why the unbinding failed," she went on, before anyone could speak. "Not because of Veyra. Not only. The ritual needs a vessel willing to take the Ember when it dies — a living vessel, or it tears loose and takes the king with it. I knew that going in. I thought I could manage both. Hold the door open for the flame and stay standing behind it." She smiled, and it was the saddest thing Odo had seen since Marlow's banner went up. "The witch knew better than I did. You can't save him halfway. The flame takes what it takes."

"No," Edmund said.

"Edmund—"

"*No.*" He was on his feet, and Odo realized he'd never heard Edmund raise his voice. Thirty years alone in a valley will teach a man to keep his voice down; apparently there was a limit. "You don't get to trade yourself. Not for me. Not for anyone. We lost Beatrix carrying this thing. We lost Marlow. We lost Rell on those stairs ten hours ago, and I am not — I will *not* stand in another cellar and listen to the next name on the list be offered up like it's noble. It's not noble. It's just the next grave."

"It's my choice," Sera said quietly.

"It's a *bad* choice."

"Most true ones are."

Odo watched them across the cold-lamp light — the hedge-witch with her burned hands and her terrible clarity, and the gatekeeper with his whole body shaking, and between them the shape of the thing neither of them would say outright, because saying it plainly would make it too heavy to carry.

"There has to be another way," Edmund said. "There's always another way. We just haven't found it yet."

"Sometimes there isn't."

"Then we find it anyway. That's the job. That's the whole *job*, Sera. I spent thirty years keeping a door shut on the theory that what was behind it mattered more than my comfort. Fine. New theory: your life matters more than the tidy ending. I'll hold the Ember myself. It doesn't burn me. Let it ground through me until it gutters out — I'm a null, I'm a *hinge*, remember? Use me."

"You can't bind a king's will with a null, Edmund, the ritual needs a living conduit with—"

"Then we find one. Then we invent one. Then we ask the Ember nicely." His voice cracked and he pushed through it. "I have buried enough of this company in my head in the last day to last a lifetime. I'm not burying you. I forbid it."

"You *forbid* it?" For the first time, something like a real smile touched Sera's mouth, tired and crooked. "On whose authority, gatekeeper?"

"On mine. I'm the only person alive who can touch the damn thing. As far as I'm concerned that makes me the ranking expert on Embers, and the expert says no."

Pip, from the wall, in a hoarse whisper: "He's got you there."

"Pip."

"What? He does."

* * *

Later — much later, when the sand-glass had run out twice and Sera had finally, finally slept, curled against the wall with her burned hands tucked against her chest like a bird with folded wings — Odo sat beside Edmund in the dark and spoke low, so as not to wake anyone.

"You can't forbid her, you know."

"I know."

"She'll do it the moment the chance comes. She's been walking toward that prophecy since the ford. Maybe longer. Maybe since Beatrix died and she decided love was a debt you pay in full."

"I know that too." Edmund rubbed his eyes. "But if I let her believe I accept it, then I've already lost her. So I'll argue. Every hour of every day until we reach the throne room, I'll argue. Maybe arguing is its own kind of holding the door."

Odo huffed. "That's the most warden thing you've ever said."

"Thank you."

"It wasn't entirely a compliment."

"It absolutely was."

They sat in the dripping dark a while longer. Then Edmund said, very quietly, "Odo. When I touched the Ember in the vault. Before everything went wrong. I heard him. The king. Screaming. Not words — *worse* than words. Like a man shouting through a wall for years and years until his voice is gone and he's just hitting the stone." He paused. "What if he's past saving? What if we tear the binding out and there's nothing left of Aldric but the hole it filled?"

Odo thought of the sealed order they'd intercepted, written in the king's own cipher, in the king's own hand — *under compulsion*, but written. Some thread of him had held the pen. Some thread of him had hidden messages inside the compulsion like knots tied in a rope while someone else pulled it.

"Then we save what's left," Odo said. "That's the oath. Not to the man he was. To the man he's still trying to be."

Before Edmund could answer, Pip sat bolt upright against the far wall.

Everyone woke at once — years of outlawry had trained them to that, sleep shallow as a dog's. Pip's eyes were wide and fixed on the tunnel mouth, and his hand was on Marlow's knife, the one he'd carried since the bridge.

"Do you hear that?" he whispered.

Odo listened. At first: nothing. Dripping. Water in the old channels. Then, beneath it, threading up through the stone like smoke through a floorboard—

A voice. Faint, ragged, impossibly far and impossibly close at once, as if the walls themselves had grown a throat. It wasn't speaking to all of them. Odo felt that with certainty. It was speaking to one person, and every face in the cellar turned toward Edmund.

Edmund had gone white as salt.

"It knows my name," he breathed. "It's calling me *Gatekeeper*."
