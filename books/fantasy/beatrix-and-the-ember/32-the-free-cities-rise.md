## 32. The Free Cities Rise

Pip had decided, somewhere around the third day of walking with his brother's ashes on his back, that grief was a lot like a badly packed saddlebag. It didn't matter how carefully you arranged it at the start. By noon it had shifted, and something hard was digging into your hip, and you had to stop and unpack the whole miserable thing and repack it, and it was never, ever the same twice.

"You're brooding," said Marlow.

"I'm walking," Pip said. "Brooding is when you stop."

"You stopped."

"I stopped to *look* at the horizon. That's scouting. That's a twin duty."

The ghost of his brother sat on a milestone beside the road, swinging his legs the way he used to when they were small and waiting out a rainstorm under the fishmonger's awning in Correnvale. Death had not improved Marlow's posture, but it had done wonders for his punctuality. He was always exactly where Pip's thoughts went when they went looking for him.

"You're talking to nobody again," said Sera, falling into step beside him. Her left side was still bound where the witch's ford magic had pulled at her, and she walked with a stick she'd named — of all things — *Duke*. "The twins used to do this thing where they'd have entire conversations with just their eyebrows. Now you're doing it with empty air. It's very unsettling."

"He's here," Pip said. "Sort of."

Sera glanced at the milestone. She couldn't see Marlow — nobody could, which Pip had decided was the single worst part of the whole arrangement, worse even than the ashes, worse than the banner. But she touched the milestone anyway, the way you'd touch a friend's shoulder.

"Tell him the road's long and the company's short one set of terrible jokes."

"He heard you," Pip said. "He's making a face."

"He's *always* making a face," said Odo from up front, without turning around. The old guard had a way of hearing everything, which Pip suspected was less a skill and more a habit of twenty years spent listening for assassins. "I served with a man who claimed his dead brother followed him. Turned out to be a parrot. Very convincing parrot."

"Was it?" said Rell.

"Screamed 'treachery' at the right moments. Yes."

Pip laughed — a short, startled sound, like a hiccup with ambition. It was the first laugh in three days, and it hurt going down, and it helped anyway. That was the thing about the company. They carried your grief for you when your back was full of ashes. Not all of it. Just enough that you could keep walking.

The ashes themselves rode in an urn Sera had charmed to stay warm. "Not warm-warm," she'd explained, "just *remembering* warm. Like bread that's been out of the oven an hour." Pip had cried at that, and then Marlow's ghost had said, *she's been practicing that line for days, I watched her*, and Pip had cried at *that*, and Sera had pretended very hard to be interested in the fire.

The banner rode on top of the urn. It was Marlow's — the one he'd painted himself, a lopsided sun over a bridge, the sigil of the Correnvale ferry-brigade he'd been born into and too stubborn to leave. It had a scorch mark across one corner from Hollow Bridge, and Pip had decided it was staying exactly like that. Some repairs were a kind of lying.

*You should fly it higher,* Marlow said, watching him adjust the strap. *People can't read a banner that drags.*

"People aren't supposed to read it. It's not an advertisement."

*Everything's an advertisement if you're brave enough.*

"That doesn't even make sense."

*Neither does carrying your dead brother up a mountain, and yet here we both are.*

Pip had no answer for that, so he kept walking, and the ghost kept pace, and the road kept unspooling north toward the capital like a thread pulled from a coat.

* *

They came down out of the hills on the fifth morning and found the world had changed color.

Not literally — though with Sera along, Pip had learned never to rule it out. But the refugee road, which for weeks had been a grey ribbon of trudging silence, was *loud*. Fires burned in proper rings instead of scattered huddles. Someone had strung bunting between two wagons, actual bunting, red and gold, the colors of the old king. A woman was teaching a crowd of children a marching song, and the children were singing it badly and at full volume, which Pip supposed was the traditional way.

And everywhere, everywhere, the same words, passed mouth to mouth like a cup of water:

*The king lives.*

"Where did it start?" Edmund asked, standing at the road's edge with the wary look of a man who has spent his life in a quiet valley and does not trust crowds on principle. He'd been like that since the under-city — since Aldric's whisper had reached him in the dark and re-armed them all with the truth. But the wariness had a new edge to it now. Purpose, Pip thought, looked a lot like fear from the outside.

"A tavern in Duncar," said a carter, only too happy to be asked. He had the build of a man who'd spent his life moving other people's furniture and the eyes of a man who'd recently decided to stop. "A guard — one of the palace ones, turned rebel — he stood up on a table and read out a sealed order in the king's own cipher. Said the king was alive, said he was bound, said there were folk walking to free him." The carter looked at Odo, then at Rell, and his voice dropped to something almost reverent. "Said the king's own guard was among the free ones."

Odo went very still. Rell, beside him, went very still in a different way — the way of a man hearing his own crimes described as virtues.

"Well," said Odo at last, gruffly. "Someone's been gossiping."

"Someone's been *hoping*," said Sera.

The carter leaned in. "It's true, then? The king lives?"

"It's true," said Edmund, and something in the flat certainty of it — the null certainty, the one thing in the world magic couldn't bend — made the carter's eyes shine. Pip had watched Edmund do this all down the long road. People believed him the way they believed stone. You couldn't charm a stone. You could only stand on it.

*Look at him,* Marlow said. *Our Edmund. All he needed was one continent and a war.*

*He needed us,* Pip said.

*Well, obviously. But the continent helped.*

* *

By midday they'd stopped being five travelers on a road and started being a *point*.

It happened without anyone deciding it. A woman with a burned farmstead and a hatchet asked Sera if it was true the sorcerer-queen could be beaten. Sera said, "We're going to try," which was honest and apparently more inspiring than any speech. Then a boy of maybe twelve asked Pip if the banner meant they were an army, and Pip — who had opened his mouth to say *no, we're five idiots and a ghost* — heard himself say:

"It means Correnvale remembers."

The boy looked at the lopsided sun over the bridge. Then he ran back to his family, and by evening there were eleven suns-over-bridges painted on shields, doors, a wheelbarrow, and one extremely committed donkey.

"They've made it a *standard*," Odo said that night, watching the fires. He sounded like a man watching weather turn. "In twenty years of soldiering I've seen standards raised by kings, by generals, by one very confused bishop. I have never seen one raised by a donkey."

"The donkey's very motivated," Pip said.

*He would have loved this,* Marlow said quietly, from beside the fire. *The donkey especially.*

Pip didn't answer. He'd gotten good at not answering, which was its own kind of conversation.

Because here was the thing Pip hadn't told anyone: the ghost was getting fainter. Not all at once — Marlow didn't do anything all at once, he'd taken four years to finish growing — but in the way a candle goes down when you forget to trim it. His edges had gone soft. Some mornings Pip had to squint to find him. And some mornings — this was the part that scared him — some mornings Pip woke up and *hadn't looked*, hadn't reached, and the silence where his brother should have been had felt almost like peace.

He'd hated himself for that. Then Marlow had said, *good*, and Pip had said, *what*, and Marlow had said, *that's what healing looks like from in here, you idiot, it looks like betrayal, keep going.*

"You're doing the eyebrow thing at nobody again," said Rell, settling down beside him with the careful motions of a man whose ribs were still knitting. Rell sat with the company now the way a rescued animal sits by a fire — close enough for the warmth, ready to bolt at the first raised voice. But he'd been sitting closer each week, and tonight he sat close, and he looked at the banner where Pip had laid it across his knees. "Correnvale ferry-brigade," he read. "You're a long way from your river."

"It's my brother's. He carried it at Hollow Bridge." Pip's throat did a thing. He made it stop. "He was going to take it home after the war. He had plans. Stupid plans. He was going to open a pie shop."

"A pie shop."

"With a bridge theme. Bridge-shaped pies. He'd already named six of them."

Rell was quiet a moment. "What were they called?"

"None of your business," said Pip, and then, because grief had been teaching him all month that secrets are just sorrows with their coats on: "*Drawbridge*. It was a pie with a flap."

Something cracked in Rell's face — not a break, more like ice going out of a river. He laughed, one rough bark of it, and put his hand briefly on Pip's shoulder, and Pip let it stay.

*Tell him the other five,* Marlow said.

"He wants to know the other names," Pip said.

"Absolutely not," said Rell.

"*Portcullis. The Bascule. Tower Special—*"

"Stop."

"*The Toll Pie.*"

"I said *stop*—" But Rell was laughing again, properly now, and Odo was looking over with an expression of profound suspicion, and Sera was smiling into the fire like a woman who'd brewed something and wasn't saying what, and Pip sat in the middle of it with his brother's ghost warm against his side and thought: *oh. So that's what he meant. Carry it, and it turns into this.*

* *

The road grew a spine on the third day.

That was how Pip thought of it afterward — one morning there were forty travelers, and by the next noon there were four hundred, and by the third dawn the road itself seemed to have grown a backbone of banners and wagons and smiths' carts and one, at this point inevitable, additional donkey. Word had gone ahead of them like spilled ink. The free cities — Correnvale, Duncar, little Ferrow with its bell-tower, the fishing towns of the Weep — they were rising. Not because anyone had ordered it. Because a story was walking north with a lopsided sun on its chest, and the story had grown legs.

The trouble with stories growing legs, as Odo pointed out with increasing frequency, is that they grow mouths too.

"They're not soldiers," he said on the fourth evening, standing on a rise, watching four hundred campfires bloom across the valley like a rash of small brave suns. "Farmers, ferrymen, a suspicious number of bakers—"

"Marlow would've fit right in," Pip said.

"—and they're going to walk into the teeth of Veyra's legions because a pie joke made them feel brave." Odo rubbed his face. "I've seen what her riders do to brave. I've *been* what her riders do to brave."

"Then teach them," said Edmund.

Everyone turned. Edmund didn't speak often — he spent words the way misers spent coins — but when he did, the whole company had learned to shut up and listen. He was looking out at the fires, and his face had that stone-calm thing it did, the null-thing, the thing that made even Rell straighten up.

"They don't need to be soldiers," Edmund said. "They need to be a road. We need to get to the capital, and everything Veyra sends to stop us has to wade through *this*." He gestured at the fires, the bunting, the donkeys. "She can't fight four hundred hopes and keep her armies on the walls. Every rider she pulls off the palace to chase farmers is a rider not standing between us and the vault."

"He's right," said Rell slowly. "She'll have to respond. She *hates* disorder — it's the one thing her magic can't simply rewrite. Order is the whole architecture of her." He paused. "I know how she thinks. I was the architecture."

"Then we'll be the earthquake," said Sera.

*Ooh,* said Marlow. *Write that down. She should put that on the banner.*

*There's no room. The donkey took the last spot.*

* *

It was Pip who taught them to march.

He hadn't planned it. The ferry-brigade of Correnvale had a cadence — you rowed to it, you hauled to it, you carried your drowned home to it — and on the fifth morning, with the column stretching back over three hills and the mood gone grey and heavy as wet wool, Pip had simply started the count under his breath. *Haul-two-three-four. Haul-two-three-four.* The woman with the hatchet picked it up. Then the carter. Then a hundred voices, then four hundred, the beat rolling down the column like something alive, and the grey mood cracked open into rhythm, and rhythm, Pip had learned on the river, is just hope with somewhere to be.

Odo watched from the rise with his arms folded. "Where did he learn that?"

"Correnvale," said Sera. "Ferry-brigade. They sing when they carry things."

"Carry what?"

"Everything," said Pip, coming up the hill with the banner high on its new pole. "That's rather the whole job."

He'd stopped dragging the banner. He'd raised it high, the way Marlow kept telling him to, and the lopsided sun over the bridge caught the morning light and threw it down the column, and four hundred people looked up at it as they marched. Some of them had started saluting it. Pip had decided not to stop them, on the grounds that Marlow would have been insufferable about it for eternity, and eternity was exactly the timescale he had available.

*You're good at this,* Marlow said, walking — not sitting, walking now, keeping pace at Pip's shoulder like he used to on the river road.

"I'm counting to four."

*You're carrying four hundred people, you fraud. Same thing. You learned it from me.*

"You learned it from the fishmonger's drum."

*I learned it from being your brother, which is a full-time position with no benefits.* The ghost was quiet a moment, and his faintness showed — his outline went soft as smoke, and Pip's chest clenched around it. *Pip. When we get there. The capital. It's going to be loud and terrible and you're going to want to hand the banner to someone and go be a soldier instead of a sign.*

"So?"

*So don't. Signs matter. I'd know. I've been one for a month.* He grinned, and the grin was Marlow all over, crooked and certain. *Besides. Somebody has to teach the bakers to march.*

* *

They saw the capital on the ninth day.

It rose out of the plain like a black tooth in a grey gum — the spires, the walls, and above it all the palace, and around the palace a light that had no business existing, a slow red pulse like a heartbeat seen through skin. The Ember. Even at this distance Pip could feel it pressing on the air, and he watched Edmund feel it too, watched the null man go still as stone in a current.

"Tomorrow," Odo said quietly. "The rebel guards make contact at the river gate at dawn. Rell knows the tunnels. We go in under the ceremony, we go in quiet, and this—" he gestured at the vast, breathing camp behind them, the four hundred and still growing, the fires like fallen stars, "—this gives us the noise to hide in."

"Veyra will see us coming," Sera said.

"Veyra will see *everything* coming," said Rell. "That's the point. She can't be everywhere. Tonight, she has to choose what to look at." He looked at Pip, and at the banner, and something old and tired and almost kind moved behind his eyes. "Make her look at the sun over the bridge."

That night the camp sang. Not the marching count — real songs, old ones, songs from the free cities with the sad verses left in, because that was the fashion of grief in the Weep and the Ferrow: you sang the sad verses loudest. Pip sat at the biggest fire with the company, with the urn warm against his ribs and the banner wrapped around his shoulders like a cloak, and he sang the sad verses loudest of anyone.

*Hey,* Marlow said, when the songs wound down and the fires burned low. *Pip. Look at me.*

He did. The ghost was faint as breath on glass now, and he was smiling.

*Tomorrow you walk into the worst place in the world, and you're going to be scared, and that's correct, fear's just your body counting the cost. But listen. Whatever happens down there — whatever they did to the king, whatever the sorcerer-queen throws at you — you carry it. That's the family trade now. You, me, the ferry-brigade, that ridiculous donkey. We carry things. It's what we're for.*

"I know," Pip said, and his voice came out steady, which surprised him. "Haul-two-three-four."

*Haul-two-three-four,* Marlow agreed, and faded like a lamp turned down, not out — Pip could still feel him, a warmth at his shoulder, an ember in the lantern of him — and Pip pulled the banner tighter and looked east toward the black tooth of the capital, where the red pulse beat and beat, and somewhere beneath it, a king waited in the dark to be carried home.

At dawn, the horns of Veyra's outriders sounded on the northern road — early, too early, a full day ahead of any plan — and Odo came running through the camp shouting for the company to arm, and Pip stood, and slung the urn across his back, and raised the banner high.

"Haul-two-three-four," he said, to four hundred waking fires. "Up. We're carrying each other."

And the free cities rose.
