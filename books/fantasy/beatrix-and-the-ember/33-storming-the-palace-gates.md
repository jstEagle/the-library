## 33. Storming the Palace Gates

Odo had stood in battle lines before. He had stood in them for eleven years, in the king's crimson, with a shield on his arm and a captain's voice in his ear, and he had believed — right up until the day he didn't — that a battle line was the most honest thing in the world. Two rows of people, all their arguments settled down to one simple question: which side of the line are you on?

He had forgotten, in the years since, how much lying a battle line could hold.

Dawn came grey over the capital, and the rebel host came with it. Odo stood in the third rank of the Correnvale column, a borrowed shield on his arm and a borrowed sword at his hip, and looked down the long avenue at the palace gates — black iron, forty feet high, hung with Binding Day banners that Veyra's people had not yet thought to take down. Above the walls, the morning fog glowed faintly gold. The Ember, burning in its vault, bleeding its light up through the stones of the whole city like a bruise seen through skin.

"Odo." Sera appeared at his elbow, staff in hand, her hair tied back with a strip of red cloth that Pip had insisted was lucky. "You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The face. The old-soldier face. The one where you look at a wall of spears and start doing arithmetic." She bumped her shoulder against his. "Stop doing arithmetic. It never helped anyone."

"It helped me survive eleven years of it."

"Yes, and look how well that went." But she said it gently, and her hand found his forearm and squeezed once, and that was the whole of the conversation, because there wasn't time for more. Horns were sounding along the rebel line — three notes, then two, the signal Marlow's banner had taught the free cities, the signal that meant *the king may live, the king may live, come and see.*

Odo looked down the rank and found his people. Edmund, three ranks ahead, wearing a pilgrim's grey that had long since stopped fooling anyone, his jaw set in that particular way he had — not brave, exactly, but *unwilling to leave*, which Odo had come to think was braver. Pip beside him, carrying Marlow's banner wrapped tight around his shoulders like a cloak, the ash-grey cloth with its stitched sparrow. Rell at the far end of the line, in no colors at all, his old guard's sword bare in his hand, standing where everyone could see him and no one would stand beside him — except that today, three rebel soldiers had drifted over to his end of the rank, because Rell had spent the last week teaching their sergeants every patrol route in the palace district, and soldiers know the worth of a man who gives them the truth of a wall before they have to die against it.

And there, at the center, the company. The broken road, all of it, gathered into one line.

"Remember," Odo called down the rank, because someone had to, and he had a captain's voice whether he wanted it or not. "We don't hold the avenue. We don't take the walls. We go *through* — fast and ugly — and when Edmund runs, we make sure nothing runs with him. Everything else is noise."

"And the noise?" Pip asked, with a grin that didn't quite reach his eyes.

"The noise," Odo said, "is ours."

The horns sounded again, and the line moved.

* * *

The first hundred yards were the worst, because nothing happened.

Odo had learned that in his first campaign, aged nineteen: the terrible quiet of the last stretch of ground before two armies meet, when your boots make the only sound in the world and you have time to think about your mother, your debts, the letter you never sent. He watched the young rebels around him start to pray, or swear, or both, and he did the old captain's trick of counting their breathing — four in, four out — until the walls above them bloomed with crossbows and the quiet ended all at once, like a door slamming.

"Bolts!" he roared, and the rank went to its knees behind the pavises the Correnvale engineers had built, and the sky filled with the sound of iron on wood. Then the horns, then the ladders, then the screaming, and the battle was a real thing at last, with weight and smell and a terrible appetite.

The avenue was a killing ground and Veyra's mages had made it worse. Odo felt it before he saw it — a wrongness in the air, a pressure behind the eyes — and then the cobbles ahead of the column *moved*, stone unknitting itself into grasping hands, and the rebel line buckled as men went down into the cobble-grip.

"Sera!" he bellowed.

"I see it!" She was already moving, staff spinning, and Odo watched her work the way he had watched her work a dozen times on the broken road — except that now he knew what the witch at the ford had told her, and he could not un-know it, and every spell she cast made his chest hurt. She sang the stones back to sleep. The cobble-hands went slack and became cobbles again, and men crawled out of them, and Sera staggered a half-step, caught herself, and grinned at Odo across the smoke like this was all a fine game.

*You will save the one you love by losing yourself.* Odo had made her tell him, weeks ago, in the dark under the ruined city. He had made her say the words out loud. And then he had said: *Then we make sure you're never the one who has to save him.* And she had agreed. And they had both known, in the way soldiers know things, that agreements like that are written in water.

The gates were the next problem. Forty feet of black iron, and behind them the sound of the palace garrison forming up. The rebels brought up the ram — a felled bell-tower beam on chains, forty men to a side — and Odo took his place at its head because that was where a captain belonged, and they swung it, and the gates rang like the world's saddest bell, and swung again, and again, and on the fourth stroke the iron split along a seam Rell had promised was there, and the morning came in through the crack like a blade.

"Through!" Odo screamed. "Through, through, *through* —"

The gates burst. The battle poured itself into the gap. And Odo stopped being a narrator of anything and became, for a long grey while, only a shield and a sword and a voice, doing the oldest arithmetic there is: *him, me; him, me; left, blocked; right, dead; forward, forward, forward.*

* * *

They broke the inner court at mid-morning.

It cost them. Odo would remember the cost all his life — the twins' banner-bearers who carried Marlow's sparrow up the steps and did not come down; the Correnvale sergeant who had argued with him about rations a week ago and died holding the fountain so the wounded could reach the water; the boy, no older than Pip, who had asked Odo that morning whether the king was really alive and had been the first man over the wall. The court was a churn of crimson and grey, of rebel spears and guard halberds, and above it all the mages worked from the colonnade — Veyra's hunters, the ones with the silver collars, throwing fire that burned blue and cold and did not spread but simply *unmade* what it touched.

And in the middle of it, Edmund ran.

That was the plan. That had always been the plan, from the moment Rell had drawn the palace's bones in the dirt: the company makes noise, the rebels make noise, and Edmund — the one man in the world whose footsteps leave no ripple in the magic — goes quiet through the middle of it all, down the processional way, to the throne room, to the Ember. *Carry it, don't wield it,* Beatrix had said, and Edmund had carried that sentence for a year the way he carried everything: silently, stubbornly, like a man bringing water to a field that isn't his.

Odo saw him break from cover behind the fountain — grey-clad, unremarkable, moving with the flat unhurried speed of a man who has decided and will not be undeceived. Two of Veyra's hunters saw him too. Odo saw the silver collars turn, saw the blue fire begin to gather in their hands, and understood, with a cold drop in his stomach, that the plan had a flaw they had all agreed not to say out loud: the hunters couldn't *sense* Edmund, but they could still *see* him, and a man who is invisible to magic is only invisible to half the world.

"Sera —" he started.

She was already moving.

Later — in the weeks after, in the long nights of the aftermath — Odo would take that moment apart a hundred times and never find the seam where duty ended and love began. She didn't shout a warning. She didn't look at Edmund at all. She simply planted her staff on the fountain's rim and did the largest working Odo had ever seen her do — larger than the ford, larger than the salt flats, larger than anything — and the whole colonnade *went dark*. Not shadow-dark. *Silence*-dark. She pulled the magic out of the air itself, out of the hunters' collars, out of the blue fire, out of the wards on the throne room doors, folding it all into herself the way a woman folds a storm into a jar, and for three full seconds the palace's heart was a place where nothing enchanted could live.

Three seconds. Long enough for Edmund to cross the open ground untouched. Long enough for the hunters' fire to gutter and die in their hands. Long enough.

It was not long enough for Sera.

Odo reached her as the silence broke and the battle's noise came crashing back. She was on her knees by the fountain, and the red cloth had fallen from her hair, and she was *grey* — not pale, grey, like ash, like the province they had crossed, like something with the color drawn out of it. Her staff was still in her hand. Her eyes were still on the throne room doors, which were, he saw with a strange distant relief, already closed behind Edmund.

"Stubborn," she said, when she saw him. Her voice was thin as paper. "He actually went. He actually — did you see? He just *went*, like a man crossing a road."

"I saw." Odo got an arm under her and lifted, and she weighed nothing, which frightened him more than the blood at the corner of her mouth. "Sera. Sera, look at me. What did you take into yourself?"

"Everything that was in the way." She tried to smile. It came out crooked. "It's all right. It's only borrowed. I'll give it back."

"That's not how it works and you know it's not how it works."

"I know how it works, Odo." And for a moment her eyes cleared and were the old eyes, the sharp ones, the ones that had read the map's wanting and the witch's warning and Edmund's bloodline and every other truth the company had stumbled over. "I knew what the ford meant. I've known since the ford. And I'm telling you —" her hand closed on his collar, weak as a child's, fierce as a vice — "I'm telling you it's not finished. Whatever this is, whatever I've started — it isn't the losing. The losing comes *later*. Do you understand me? There's still a door. There's still —"

The doors of the throne room boomed.

Once. Twice. The whole court stopped fighting to look — rebels and royal guards alike, hundreds of faces turning, because the sound was wrong, was *deep*, was the sound of something vast being struck from the inside. A third boom, and hairline cracks of gold light spread across the great doors like frost across a window, and through the cracks Odo felt it: the Ember, unshielded, screaming its light into the world.

And above the palace, the fog that had glowed all morning began, very slowly, to rise.

"Odo." Sera's grip on his collar tightened. Her grey face had gone strange and calm. "Get me up. I have to be standing for this."

"You can't even —"

"Get me *up*." 

He got her up. He put her arm over his shoulders and her staff under her hand, and together — the disgraced guard and the hedge-witch, the two people in the world who best knew what was about to be asked of the man behind those doors — they turned to face the throne room as the gold light widened, and the doors began, slowly, to open.

And through the widening gap, Odo heard a voice he had not heard in eleven years. A voice worn thin and rusted, a voice that had been used as a puppet's string for a decade and a half, but a voice he would have known in his grave, in his ashes, at the end of the world.

King Aldric's voice, and it was his own, and it said:

"*Who is holding my flame?*"
