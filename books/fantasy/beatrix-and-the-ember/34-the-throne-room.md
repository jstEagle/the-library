## 34. The Throne Room

The last door between Edmund and the throne room was made of gold, which struck him as a poor choice of material for a war.

Gold dents. Gold melts. Gold is heavy enough that it takes six men to swing it shut, and by the time you've managed that, whatever you were trying to keep out has already come in through the window. Sera would have warded it. Odo would have braced it with an oak beam. Pip would have simply asked why anyone would build a door out of the one metal everyone wants to steal.

Edmund thought about all of this because thinking about doors was easier than thinking about what waited on the other side.

He stood in a corridor high in the palace of Veyra's capital, alone except for the dead. Not literally — though there were plenty of those too, fallen rebels and royal guards alike, sprawled along the marble like dropped marionettes — but figuratively, in the way that mattered. Beatrix was dead. Marlow was dead. Rell had died on a stairwell two nights ago holding back half a legion so the rest of them could run, and his last words had been a joke about how he'd always wanted to die somewhere with good acoustics. And Sera —

Sera was three floors down, bleeding from a wound across her ribs that Odo had bound with strips of his own cloak and more desperation than skill, insisting through gritted teeth that she could still work the ritual if Edmund could just get to the Ember. *Go,* she'd said. *You're the only one it can't lie to.*

So here he was. Alone at a golden door, listening to the battle rage far below him — the crash of the free cities' assault against the palace walls, the shriek of magic meeting the strange null silence that clung to him like a second skin — and thinking about metallurgy.

"You're stalling," he told himself quietly.

He was. He knew he was. But a man who had spent thirty years tending a gate in a valley without magic had learned a great deal about the proper way to approach a threshold, and the proper way was never quickly. You checked the hinges. You checked your hands. You checked whether you were the same person who would walk back out again, because thresholds changed people; that was rather the whole point of them.

His hands were steady. That surprised him. They should have been shaking. He was a gatekeeper's son with a sword he barely knew how to hold, standing in the heart of the most dangerous building on the continent, about to face a sorcerer-queen who had bent a king to her will for seven years. Every rational part of him should have been screaming.

Instead there was just a strange, settled quiet, like the valley before snowfall.

*Carry it, don't wield it.* Beatrix's voice, in his memory, in that last ragged breath on his floor. He hadn't understood it then. He wasn't sure he understood it now. But he had carried her map across a continent, through ashfall provinces and salt flats and a mountain that shouted names, and he had learned this much: carrying was its own kind of strength. You didn't have to be mighty. You just had to not put it down.

Edmund put his shoulder to the golden door and pushed.

* * *

The throne room was larger than any space had a right to be.

Pillars rose into darkness overhead, each one carved — he realized with a lurch — in the shape of a warden. Generations of them, or figures meant to look like wardens: stern men and women with keys at their belts, their stone eyes fixed on the throne as if even in death they were keeping watch. His ancestors, maybe. Or their likenesses, stolen and repurposed, set here to decorate the ambition of a woman who had torn a kingdom apart.

Between the pillars, the floor was black marble polished to a mirror shine, and in it Edmund saw himself reflected a dozen times over — a broad, plain man in scorched pilgrim's robes, mud to the knees, blood on his sleeve that wasn't his, carrying nothing but a sword he didn't trust and a heat-marked map folded against his heart.

At the far end of the hall, on a dais of white stone, sat the throne.

And in the throne sat two people wearing one body.

That was the only way Edmund could describe it, and he knew even as he thought it that no one else would understand. To any other eye, King Aldric sat upon his throne — tall, golden-crowned, robed in the deep blue of the old dynasty, his face composed and kingly. But Edmund was null. Magic slid off him like rain off glass, and what slid off left traces, the way water leaves a mark on chalk. And what he saw, in that terrible clear sight of his, was Aldric's body moving like a puppet whose strings ran up into the air and vanished — and wrapped around the king like armor plate, like a second skin of light and shadow, the shape of a woman.

Veyra did not sit beside her king. She wore him.

"Gatekeeper."

Her voice came from everywhere and nowhere, from the pillars and the floor and the crown itself, and also — this was the worst part — from Aldric's own mouth, the king's lips shaping her words while his eyes stared straight ahead at nothing. "You came farther than I expected. I confess I underestimated you. It's a mistake I rarely make twice."

Edmund walked forward. Slowly. Checking his hinges.

"I'm not here to talk," he said.

"No," she agreed, and something in the vast dark of the room seemed to amuse her, a ripple of cold laughter through the stone. "You're here to destroy my Ember. Yes. I know. I have known since the day you stepped through your little gate, since the moment your magicless foot touched soil that remembers every spell ever cast upon it. Do you know what you are, Edmund Yarrow? You are a hole in my hearing. A blind spot walking. For months I have reached across this continent and felt everything — every hedge-witch's whisper, every soldier's prayer, every spark of the free cities' rebellion — and in the middle of it all, a silence shaped like a man." The puppet-king's head tilted, just slightly. "It has been very irritating."

"Glad to hear it," said Edmund. "I'd hate to think all that walking was for nothing."

There was a pause. Then, from the shadows near the eastern pillars, a sound Edmund had not expected: a short, sharp laugh — human, female, genuinely startled. It echoed oddly, as if the room itself had laughed and Veyra was merely borrowing the echo.

"You're funny," she said. "Beatrix Ash was funny too, at the end. Did you know that? She stood in a river ford with an arrow in her lung and told my hunters their mothers would be ashamed of them. She was nearly right about which ones." The throne creaked as the king's body shifted forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the movement was so natural, so tiredly human, that Edmund's chest ached. "I offered her a place at my side once. She declined. Loudly. With fire."

"She was good at loud," Edmund said quietly.

"Yes." Something almost soft moved through the vast voice. Then it hardened, the way frost hardens over a pond overnight — slowly, completely, irreversibly. "But we are not here to trade anecdotes about the dead. We are here because you are twenty paces from my Ember, and I would prefer not to kill you. Killing you would be wasteful. You are the last of your line, Gatekeeper, and your line was expensive."

Edmund stopped walking. Twenty paces. Behind the throne, half-hidden in the shadow of the tallest pillar, he could see it now: a cage of black iron, tall as three men, and inside it a flame that burned without fuel, gold-white and pulsing, slow and rhythmic as a heartbeat. As he watched, the flame flared — and the king's shoulders bowed, just slightly, like a man under a weight only he could feel.

*The Ember binds Aldric to Veyra's will.* Sera's voice, around a fire many miles and a lifetime ago. *Destroy it wrongly and the king dies with it.*

"I know what my line was for," Edmund said. "The mountain told me. The books told me. Your hunters told me, right before Odo broke their noses. Wardens. Living locks. Bred magicless on purpose so we could hold things that would burn anyone else." He looked up at the pillar-carved wardens, their stone eyes watching. "You decorated your throne room with us. That's either a tribute or a trophy, and I'm guessing trophy."

"Clever," said Veyra. "And wasted on a farmhand. Here is what neither the mountain nor your witch told you, because they don't know it." The king rose from the throne — smoothly, terribly, the whole body moving with a grace that belonged to someone else — and began to descend the steps of the dais toward Edmund. "Your valley was not built to keep the world out, Gatekeeper. It was built to keep something *in*. The oldest prison in the world sits beneath your tidy little fields, and your ancestors were its lock, and the Ember—"

"The Ember is yours," Edmund said. "I know. You wear the king with it. I've seen it. I see it right now."

He had meant it as defiance. What happened instead was worse: the descending figure stopped, and the king's face — Aldric's face, weathered and honest and hollowed out — turned fully toward Edmund for the first time, and behind the empty eyes something flickered. Brief. Desperate. Like a man trapped underwater seeing light on the surface.

Then it was gone, smoothed away, and Veyra's amusement flooded back into the borrowed features like water filling a footprint.

"You see what I allow you to see," she said. "Now. Let us speak plainly, you and I, because I find I am tired of speaking through puppets." She spread the king's hands — a gesture of open reasonableness, horribly practiced. "You cannot destroy the Ember. Oh, you can touch it — you are perhaps the only living soul who can — but touch is not destruction, and the moment you try, my enforcers will take the hall back, and your witch will die on the stairs, and your big loyal guard will die beside her, and the boy will die last because I know how much you've all grown to love the boy. Whereas if you walk away—"

"I don't walk away," Edmund said. "That's sort of the whole story."

"*Listen to me.*" The temperature of the room dropped. Frost feathered across the black marble at the king's feet. "I am offering you your life back, Edmund Yarrow. Not merely your survival — your *life*. The valley. The gate. The quiet. No more war, no more marching, no more burying friends in shallow graves along bad roads. Go home. Tend your wards. Grow your beans." A pause, and then, with something dangerously close to gentleness: "I will even reopen the road south, so travelers can pass through in summer. You'll never be lonely again. Isn't that what you wanted, all those years at your gate? Someone to talk to?"

And that — that was the knife of it. Not the threat. The offer.

Because she was right. Gods help him, she was right. Thirty years he had stood at that gate, sweeping the same stones, mending the same fence, talking to the same four mountains, and every night he had lain awake in his narrow bed and wanted exactly this: a world that let him stay. A world that asked nothing of him but the small faithful work of his hands. He had crossed a continent and lost friends and found a family and fallen in love, and some stubborn buried corner of him still ached for the valley the way an old scar aches before rain.

Veyra watched him feel all of this. He could tell she was watching — the king's head cocked slightly, patient as a spider.

"There it is," she murmured. "I thought so. You want to say yes. Good. Wanting isn't weakness, Gatekeeper. Wanting is honesty. Say yes, and mean it, and I will let your friends live. All of them. The witch, the guard, the boy. You have my word — and my word, whatever else may be said of me, has never been broken."

"That's true, actually," Edmund said. "Odo mentioned that. Said it was the worst thing about you. Everyone knows exactly where they stand."

"And yet here you stand, hesitating."

"I'm not hesitating." Edmund took a breath, and found, to his mild surprise, that he was smiling. It felt strange on his face, like a coat borrowed from a bigger man. "I'm checking the hinges."

"...I beg your pardon?"

"It's a gatekeeper thing. Before you open a door, you check the hinges. Because a door is a promise, see. A door says: this way is safe, this way is honest, what's on the other side won't hurt you. And when somebody offers me a door—" he nodded at the king's open hands, at the whole vast gilded lie of the room, "—the first thing I check is whether the hinges are real."

He started walking again. Ten paces now. Eight.

"Edmund Yarrow." Her voice lost its silk entirely, and underneath it was something old and cold and enormous, the sound of a glacier calving. "I am offering you mercy. There is no second offer."

"Yeah," Edmund said. "See, that's the other thing about doors." Six paces. The Ember's heartbeat filled the air now, a pulse he felt in his teeth, in the map pressed warm against his chest. "A real door opens both ways. Yours only opens out. You want me back in that valley because a locked valley suits *you* — because the last of the wardens, tucked away behind wards, means nobody's left who can hold what you're hoarding. This isn't mercy. It's housekeeping."

Five paces. Four.

The king moved faster than anything that size had a right to move — a blur of blue robe and gold crown, a hand closing around Edmund's throat and lifting him clean off the floor, and the terrible thing, the thing Edmund would remember all his life, was that the grip was gentle. Precise. The grip of someone who needed him alive and resented every second of it.

"Do you know what I did to the last man who refused me?" Veyra whispered through Aldric's mouth, her face inches from his. "I wore him. For eleven years. I am wearing his king right now. I can wear you, Gatekeeper. Your emptiness protects you from spells, but flesh is flesh, and I have worn flesh for a very long time."

Behind her eyes — behind Aldric's eyes — the flicker came again. Stronger this time. And with it, faint as a voice through a wall, something reached for Edmund. Not words. Not yet. Just a pressure, a presence, pressing against the silence of him like a face against a window.

*Help me,* it did not say, because it had no voice left. But Edmund heard it anyway.

"I believe you," he rasped, feet dangling, stars gathering at the edge of his vision. "About the wearing. About all of it. You're very convincing — it's clearly your best spell." His hand crept, trembling, toward the fold of his shirt. Toward the map. Toward the warmth that had been riding over his heart since a dying woman bled out on his kitchen floor. "But you made one mistake."

"Enlighten me," said Veyra.

"You offered me my old life back." Edmund smiled through the choking, and this time the smile fit. "As if I'd want it. As if thirty years of talking to mountains was the best I could hope for. I have a family now. A loud, broken, impossible family — a witch who talks to plants, a guard who cries at funerals and denies it, a boy who named a mule after his dead brother. And a woman down three flights of stairs who I—" his voice cracked, and he let it, "—who I intend to grow old and ridiculous with. You can't offer me that valley. I already gave it up. I gave it up the day a stranger bled on my floor and I chose to carry her map instead of sweeping my step."

The map came out of his shirt, blazing, gold-white as the Ember itself, and Veyra recoiled — actually recoiled, the puppet-king staggering back a full step, and in that instant of loosened grip Edmund dropped, hit the marble, rolled, and came up running for the iron cage with the burning map held out before him like a lantern.

"BEATRIX ASH SENDS HER REGARDS!" he shouted, which was not strictly accurate, since she was dead, but felt important to say.

The Ember flared. The whole cage lit like a second dawn. And deep beneath the throne room, in the vaults and tunnels below, Edmund heard — felt — a sound like a great bell being struck: Sera, beginning the ritual, the unbinding, the thread that ran from the flame to the king beginning at last to fray.

Veyra screamed. It was not a human sound. The pillars shook. The carved wardens' faces seemed to twist. And the sorcerer-queen turned from Edmund entirely, pouring herself down through the floor in a torrent of shadow to snuff out a hedge-witch three stories below—

—which was, Edmund understood dimly, exactly what Sera had planned for, and exactly why she'd sent him up here alone.

Which left him, twenty paces became none, alone with a caged god-flame, a collapsing ritual, and a king who was looking at him with eyes suddenly, desperately alive.

"Well," said Edmund Yarrow, gatekeeper, keeper of doors, ordinary man, reaching out with the only hands in the world that wouldn't burn. "Let's see what you're made of."

He touched the Ember.

And the Ember touched him back.
