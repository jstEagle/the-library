## 35. What Beatrix Would Do

The throne room of Aldric the Beloved had been built to make a person feel small.

It succeeded. Edmund stood at the far end of it — small, ordinary, swordless, and bleeding from a cut along his forearm he didn't remember earning — and felt very much like a mouse that had wandered into a cathedral during a sermon about the sins of mice.

Veyra stood before the throne, and King Aldric knelt beside her like a coat she'd hung up.

That was the image that stuck in Edmund's mind afterward, more than the light, more than the heat: not the sorcerer-queen wreathed in the Ember's stolen fire, but the king beside her. A big man, broad through the shoulders, gray at the temples, dressed in ceremonial white gone yellow with years. He knelt the way furniture stands. His eyes were open. They looked at nothing. The crown on his head glowed faintly, pulsing in time with the caged flame above Veyra's palm, and every pulse made his fingers twitch against the marble floor, as if some part of him was still trying to crawl out from under his own skin.

"Edmund Yarrow," Veyra said. She said his name the way Sera read recipes — with interest, but no urgency. "The gatekeeper. Do you know how long I've been aware of you? Years. A null spot walking across my maps like a hole in a sock. I could have killed you a dozen times over."

"No, you couldn't," Edmund said. It came out steadier than he felt. "Your hunters tried. Their spells kept sliding off." He wiped his sweating palm on his trouser leg. "Pip says it's like watching rain hit a window. Very satisfying, apparently."

Something moved across her face. It might have been the ghost of amusement. "And yet here you are, at the end of the world, holding nothing."

"Actually," Edmund said, "I'm holding this."

He lifted the Ember.

He'd taken it from its cage back in the vault — or rather, he had been the only one who *could* take it, because it burned everyone else, because his bloodline had been bred for exactly this without anyone telling him, which he intended to raise with somebody once the world stopped ending. It sat in his hands now like a heart torn out still beating: a fist-sized flame, gold-white at its core, red at its edges, warm but never burning him. Through the null silence of his own body he could hear it — faintly, horribly — the screaming of the man kneeling by the throne. Aldric's will, tangled thread by thread into the flame, screaming to come home.

"You cannot destroy it," Veyra said. Her voice hardened. "Sera Ashlin told you, or she worked it out, or your dead courier wrote it in her map. Destroy the Ember wrongly and the binding tears through the king instead. You would trade a tyrant's puppet for a corpse and a succession war. Is that your justice, gatekeeper? Shall I let you?"

"I'm not going to destroy it," Edmund said.

Behind him — far behind him, down the shattered entry hall, past the barricade of overturned statues where Odo and Rell and Pip were holding the line against her guards — came the sound of steel and shouting, and one high, clear voice yelling something about *left side, watch the left side, Marlow would've had the left side.* Pip. Still fighting. Still here.

"I'm going to do what Beatrix told me," Edmund said, and took one step forward.

*Carry it,* she had said, dying on his floor a continent ago, pressing the burning map into his hands with the last of her strength. *Don't wield it. Whatever you do, whatever it offers you — carry it.*

At the time, half-dead of blood loss, she had sounded less like a prophet and more like a woman giving instructions for returning a library book. He had resented it. He had spent months resenting it, quietly, while the map dragged him across the world: *carry it, carry it*, as if he were a mule, as if his whole purpose were to be a container.

Standing in the throne room, with the Ember humming in his hands and Veyra's power rolling off her like heat off a forge, Edmund finally understood.

She hadn't been diminishing him. She'd been freeing him.

"Don't take another step," Veyra said.

He took another step.

She raised her hand and the world went white.

* * *

Later — much later, when he could bear to describe it — Edmund would tell them that being struck by Veyra's full power felt like standing in a river and discovering the river was made of fists.

The bolt of it crossed the throne room faster than thought. It should have unmade him. It had unmade walls, armies, cities; the ruined streets of the City That Remembers still wept their ghosts over it. It crossed the distance between them in the space of a heartbeat, carrying enough woven death to end everything Edmund Yarrow had ever been —

— and slid off him like rain off glass.

Not around him. Not deflected. It simply ceased, at the boundary of his skin, the way sound ceases in the valley of his birth. The magic touched him and found nothing to hold onto. No spark to twist, no breath to stop, no fear-shaped hook to catch. Just a man. Just an ordinary, stubborn, magicless man, standing there with his boots slightly apart because Odo had drilled him on stance for weeks and it turned out Odo was right about everything.

The bolt broke apart into embers that rained down around him and guttered on the marble.

"Oh," Edmund said. Then, because his mouth had always run ahead of his courage: "Is that all?"

It was not all. But it was a start.

Veyra stared at him. For the first time since he'd seen her — in visions, in wanted-posters, in the nightmares the whole company shared — she looked genuinely uncertain. Power flickered and reformed around her fingers. "What are you?" she demanded.

"Edmund Yarrow," he said. "Keeper of the western gate. Well — former keeper. I resigned. It was a whole thing; there was a vote."

He walked toward her.

She threw everything at him. Fire that came sideways out of empty air. Chains of light rising through cracks in the floor. A whisper that poured into the room like fog, speaking in the voices of everyone he loved — Sera calling his name, Beatrix dying again, his father's voice from twenty years gone saying *you'll die out there, boy, the valley is your place, the valley is your only place.* Each spell struck his silence and unraveled. The whisper reached his ears and simply stopped mattering, because magic could not make him believe anything; he had to do that himself, the slow human way.

The chains shattered. The fire died. The voices thinned into nothing.

Only the last one lingered, because it wasn't magic at all — it was just his own tired doubt wearing a borrowed shape, and he answered it the way he'd learned to answer everything on the long road west.

"The valley was my place," he said aloud. "Then someone died on my floor and asked me to walk. So I walked."

* * *

Ten paces from the throne, the Ember began to sing.

Or perhaps *sing* was too kind. It hummed, deep and urgent, and through his null-still hands Edmund felt what it actually was: a current. A great binding circuit running from the crown on Aldric's head into the flame in his palms, round and round, the king's will captured and fed back to him broken, year after year, decade after decade, until the strongest man in the kingdom knelt like a hat stand.

Veyra's power ran on the same wire. Every spell she'd thrown, every hunter she'd sent, every ward on this palace — all of it drew from the Ember's stolen loop. All of it passed through the flame.

All of it, therefore, passed through Edmund's hands.

He stopped walking. He looked down at the little fire, screaming and singing at once, and he understood the final thing — the thing Beatrix had known on his floor, the thing she'd spent her whole life and her whole company to teach an ordinary man.

You couldn't fight this. Fighting it meant wielding power against power, and he had none, and even if he had, power answering power just made a bigger fire. Sera couldn't unbind it under this pressure; he'd watched her try and fail in the vault while Rell died holding the stair. There was no clever ritual left, no secret word, no chosen-one stroke of destiny.

There was only a gatekeeper, and a gate.

"That's what you built us for," Edmund whispered — to the Ember, to his unknown great-great-grandmothers, to the lie of a quiet valley bought with generations of breeding. "Not to lock things in. To hold the door."

He planted his feet the way Odo taught him. He raised the Ember in both hands, level with his chest, the way he'd carried water buckets and gate-bars and Beatrix's body when no one else could bear the weight. And then he did the single bravest and least dramatic thing he had ever done in his life.

He held on.

* * *

The current hit him like the storm that had brought Beatrix through the wards — sudden, total, howling.

Every thread of binding in the room snapped taut and ran toward him, hunting ground, hunting purchase, hunting anything it could seize and turn. It found his body. It found his bones, his blood, his aching knees and his scarred palms and his heart hammering like Pip's laugh. Ordinary flesh. Utterly ordinary. And utterly, magnificently unavailable to it.

Because binding was a magic act, and there was nothing in Edmund Yarrow for magic to hold.

The current poured into him and stopped. Poured in and pooled. Poured in and — grounding, grounding, the way lightning grounds through a rod, the way rain slides off glass — dissipated into plain meat and plain bone and plain, stubborn, unremarkable life. The scream of the king's stolen will poured through him and lost its edges. The loop that had circled Aldric for thirty years now ran crown-to-flame-to-Edmund and simply *ended*, over and over, a thousand times a second, each cycle fraying another thread of Veyra's great woven working.

Her power began to unravel.

He felt it happen — not as spectacle but as weather. The pressure in the room eased. The light around Veyra's hands stuttered like a lamp low on oil. Down the entry hall, the enemy guards' enchanted blades dimmed to plain steel; he heard Rell bellow something incredulous and joyful, heard steel ring on steel with new equality. The wards sewn through the palace flickered out one by one, floor by floor, a city of lights going dark.

"It doesn't hurt me," Edmund said, wondering. He hadn't meant to say it aloud. "It can't hurt me. It can't hurt *anything* through me. Do you understand? I'm not a weapon. I'm a door that closes."

"Stop." For the first time, Veyra's voice cracked. She flung another bolt; it died against his chest like spray on a seawall. She flung another. Another. Each one fed the unraveling faster, because each one ran through the same broken circuit, and every spell that grounded harmlessly through a middle-aged man who missed his garden tore another seam out of her thirty-year tapestry. "STOP. I built a better world! Your precious free cities burn each other the moment my hand lifts — I have held the whole mad continent in one piece —"

"With a king on a leash," Edmund said. His arms trembled. The weight of the flame was nothing, and the weight of everything pouring through it was everything, and he held anyway. "You didn't build peace. You built a very large lock. I know locks. Locks aren't peace. Peace is people choosing to stay."

"You're *nothing*," she snarled, and there it was at last — not the queen, not the sorcerer, just a woman watching her life's work come apart in the hands of a gatekeeper. "A null. A hole. The world's leftover."

"Yes," Edmund agreed pleasantly. "Funny old world, isn't it."

The crown cracked.

They both heard it — a sound like ice letting go of a lake in spring, one sharp note, then another. The glow bled out of it in threads of gold, and those threads ran down, off the metal, into the kneeling man, and the man breathed.

Aldric's hands flattened against the marble. His shoulders rose. Slowly, joint by joint, like a man waking from a fever of thirty years, the King of the Nine Provinces lifted his head — and looked at Veyra with eyes that were, at long and terrible last, entirely his own.

Edmund would remember her face in that moment for the rest of his life. Not rage. Recognition. She had known, somewhere deep in the cellar of herself, that this day existed, and had spent three decades building towers against it, and the towers had come apart anyway, in the hands of a man who tended gates.

The Ember's screaming faded to a whisper, then to warmth, then to something almost like rest. In his hands it dimmed to ember-true coals — dying now, Sera had said, needing a vessel or it would take the king down with it. Dying, and still beating.

But that was the next minute's problem. That was Sera's problem, and he could already see her at the edge of the ruined doors — pale, limping, hair loose, alive, alive, *alive* — pushing past Odo toward him with the prophecy in her eyes and her mind already made up, and Edmund's heart dropped like a stone through his unraveling triumph, because he knew that look, he'd forbidden that look—

Later. One disaster at a time.

King Aldric rose from the marble floor.

He rose the way mountains attend to getting up: slowly, enormously, with the whole room adjusting around him. Thirty years of stolen will had not softened him; it seemed, if anything, to have compressed him into something denser. He looked at the Ember in Edmund's hands. He looked at Edmund. And then he looked at Veyra, and spoke for the first time in Edmund's presence, and his voice was quiet enough that Edmund had to strain to hear it over the ringing in his ears.

"Take your hands off my kingdom," Aldric said.

Veyra backed away one step. Two. Behind her, the great doors stood open, and framed in them were Odo and Rell and Pip, battered and bloodied and grinning like idiots, and beyond them, filling the entry hall and spilling down the palace steps into a courtyard full of torches and banners and the roaring of a city that had just watched the crown go dark — the free cities, arrived at last.

Sera reached Edmund's side. Her hand found his elbow. Her other hand was already reaching, gently, inevitably, toward the dying flame.

"Sera," Edmund started, "don't—"

"There's no time," she said softly. "Let me."

And the Ember, cooling, fading, choosing — pulsed once, twice, like a heart making up its mind.

End of Chapter 35.
