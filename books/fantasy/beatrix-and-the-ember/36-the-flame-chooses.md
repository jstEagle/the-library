## 36. The Flame Chooses

Sera had always believed she would know the moment of her death the way a hedge-witch knows rain coming — a pressure behind the eyes, a taste of iron on the wind, some small domestic omen. A candle guttering. A kettle singing flat.

Instead it came as arithmetic.

She stood in the ruined throne room of the capital, in the wreckage of Veyra's unraveling power, doing sums she did not want to finish. The Ember hung in the air before Edmund Yarrow — no longer caged, no longer bound to its vault, held in the cupped hands of a man who could hold anything because nothing could hold him. It was dying. She could see it the way she'd once seen fever in a child's face: the light flickering at frequencies that meant *not long now*, the pulse of it stuttering like a heart deciding whether to bother.

And when it died, it would not simply go out. Sera had read the old texts by candlelight in Correnvale, had translated the marginalia of monks who'd studied binding-magic before it was outlawed twice over. A living flame did not extinguish. A living flame *released*. Everything it held — every stolen year of King Aldric's will, every thread of compulsion woven through him for seven years — would snap back into the king all at once, like a bowstring cut at full draw.

Seven years of another mind wearing yours. Returning in a single heartbeat.

The king would die of it. Or worse — whatever worse was, when you were a king mid-battle with your rescuer's armies storming your own palace around you.

"Edmund," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "Put it down."

"I can't." He wasn't looking at her. He was looking at the flame, and his face — that plain, stubborn, gatekeeper's face she had come to love with the slow inevitability of seasons — was wet with tears he didn't seem to notice. "It's screaming, Sera. I can hear him. I've been hearing him since the vault. If I let go—"

"You're not letting go. You're handing it over." She crossed the broken marble toward him, stepping over a shard of what had been a window tall enough to hang a sky in. Veyra's soldiers had fled or fallen; somewhere above them, the battle still roared through the palace corridors, but here there was only the two of them and the fire between. "That's what you do, remember? Carry it, don't wield it. Well, my love. It's time to set down the parcel."

He turned to her then, and she watched him understand.

She watched it land on him the way grief lands — not all at once but in pieces, each one heavier than the last. His mouth opened. Closed. Opened again.

"No," he said.

"Yes."

"Sera—"

"Edmund Yarrow, you have argued with me across half a continent and lost every single time, and you are not going to win this one either." She smiled at him. It cost her more than any spell she'd ever cast, and she spent it anyway, because he needed to see her unafraid, and because — this was the secret she'd carried since the ford, since the witch with the river-grey eyes had taken her hands and spoken her future into the space between them — because she *was* unafraid. Had been, ever since she understood what the prophecy actually purchased. Fear is for futures you don't choose. This one she chose. There was a strange, clean comfort in that, like a well-made knot.

The witch's words rose in her memory, plain as the day they'd been spoken:

*You will save the man you love by losing yourself. The river gives nothing back, daughter, but oh — what you buy with it.*

She had hidden it from them. From Edmund most of all. She had lain awake beside him on the road telling herself she'd find another way, a loophole, a third option, because surely magic — which had rules for everything, which taxed every gift with a price and posted the price in advance — surely magic would allow an exception for someone who only wanted a small life afterward. A garden. His hand in hers. Mornings.

But magic does not make exceptions. Magic makes *offers*. And here, at the end of everything, the offer stood in front of her wearing the shape of a dying flame and a king who deserved to live.

* * *

"It doesn't have to be you." Edmund's voice cracked on the last word. Behind him, Odo was hauling himself up from where Rell's last stand had thrown him against the dais — bloodied, limping, alive, his face already reading the room the way old soldiers read rooms. Pip stood frozen in the doorway with Marlow's banner wrapped around his shoulders like a cloak. "There has to be another vessel. Someone else. Anyone else."

"There isn't." She kept her voice gentle. "It has to be someone who can carry living fire without being consumed, and it has to be someone with enough craft to hold the unbinding while it settles. That's a list of perhaps four people on this continent, and three of them are standing in this room bleeding." She glanced at Odo. "No offense."

"None taken," Odo said hoarsely. "I'd make a terrible lantern."

"And the fourth?"

"The fourth is a king currently occupied," Sera said. "Edmund. Listen to me. You can't take it — you know you can't. You'd ground it, smother it, and Aldric with it. Pip—" She caught the boy's eye and shook her head gently before he could speak. "You're not a vessel, sweetheart. You're a banner-bearer. Different work. Equally important."

Pip's jaw worked. He looked, in that moment, so much like Marlow that Sera's chest ached. "There's always something else," he said, but it wasn't argument. It was prayer.

"There isn't," she said softly. "There's just what's chosen and what isn't."

She reached out and touched the Ember.

Even through Edmund's null-dampening field — even held in the strange silence of him, where magic slid off like rain off glass — she felt it. Heat without temperature. A will vaster than hers pressing curiously at the edges of her mind, the way a great dog noses at a stranger's hand. It had been caged so long. It had been *used* so long. Underneath the compulsion-work, underneath Veyra's chains, it was just what it had always been: fire that wanted to burn for something.

"Well," Sera said, and heard herself laugh — a small, surprised sound, entirely genuine. "Hello to you too."

"Sera." Edmund's hand closed around her wrist. Not pulling her away. Just holding on, the way you hold onto a door in a gale. "Tell me there's another way. Lie to me if you have to. Just say it."

She turned her hand in his grip until their fingers laced together. His palm was cool — null-flesh, magicless, the strangest and safest thing she had ever loved.

"When we were on the road," she said, "after Hollow Bridge, after we buried Marlow — do you remember what you said to Pip? When he wanted to carry the body alone?"

"He remembers," Pip whispered from the doorway.

"You said: *grief shared is still grief, but it's grief you can stand under.*" She squeezed his fingers. "This is the same thing, my love. Only inverted. This is a saving that can't be shared. Some weights only one person can lift, and the whole cruel joke of it is that lifting them is the easiest part."

"That's not a lie," Edmund said desperately. "That's not — you promised you'd never—"

"I never promised you forever." Her voice gentled further. "I promised you the road. And the road, Edmund. The road was *everything*. I was a hedge-witch selling wart-charms in villages that burned behind me, and you gave me a company, and a cause, and a hand to hold in the dark stretches, and if you think I would trade one hour of that for fifty years of safety, then you haven't been paying attention, and I know for a fact you have, because you noticed when I changed my hair ribbon in Ashfall province and pretended you hadn't."

A sound escaped him that was half laugh, half sob. "Green," he said. "It was green. I liked it."

"I know you did." She rose on her toes and kissed him — soft, unhurried, taking her time about it, because if this was the last kiss she got, she intended it to be a good one, and because somewhere in the back of her mind the witch's voice murmured *the river gives nothing back*, and Sera had decided, long ago on the road, that the witch was wrong. The river gave her this. The river gave her him. Nothing given was ever taken back; it only changed hands.

When she drew away, she did not let go of his hand.

"Hold the Ember steady," she told him. "Don't ground it, don't fight it. Just be the door you've always been, and I'll walk through."

"Sera—"

"Say yes," she said. "I need to hear it. I'm about to do the bravest thing anyone's ever done in this room, and I would like permission first. It's only polite."

He made a noise like a man breaking. But he nodded. Once.

"Yes," he said. "Gods help me. Yes."

* * *

Later — in whatever later amounted to, in the fading country she was already beginning to walk — Sera would try to describe what taking in the Ember felt like, and she would fail, and she would keep trying anyway, because that was her nature.

It was swallowing the sun and the sun swallowing her back. It was every hearth she'd ever warmed herself at, arriving at once. It was Aldric's seven stolen years pouring through her like floodwater through a breached dike, and her craft catching them, sorting them, unbinding thread by thread — the compulsion-work dissolving under her hands the way frost dissolves under a palm, and beneath it, a mind. A tired, dignified, furious mind, blinking awake after seven years in the dark.

*Who,* the king's presence asked her, in a voice like a bell rung underwater.

*A friend,* Sera answered. *Go home, Your Majesty. Your kingdom's been keeping your seat warm.*

The Ember settled into her the way a bird settles onto a branch — a flutter, a folding, a stillness. And then the fading began.

She had expected pain. What came instead was quiet. A softening at the edges of things, as though the world were a painting and someone had begun, very gently, to thin the paint. Colors went first — the gold of the wrecked throne room dimming to amber, amber to honey, honey to the color of old paper. Sounds followed, stretching and sweetening, the distant battle becoming something like surf.

What remained sharp — what refused to fade — was Edmund's face.

He had caught her before she fell. Of course he had. He was holding her the way he'd carried Marlow, the way he'd carried the map, the way he carried everything that mattered: carefully, completely, as if weight were a form of devotion.

"You knew," he said. His voice was wrecked. "At the ford. The witch told you, and you knew, and you let me—"

"Fall in love with you?" Sera smiled. The smile felt far away even as she made it. "Yes. Guilty. Sentence me to a valley."

"Don't." His forehead came down against hers. "Don't joke. Don't—"

"Edmund." She found his hand and pressed it flat over her heart, where the new warmth lived, banked and bright. "Feel that? That's not me leaving. That's me staying. Every hearth you'll ever sit beside. Every candle you light. I'm going to be the most annoying ghost in the history of haunting — I'll flicker when you say something foolish, and I will flicker *often*."

"Sera." Odo knelt beside them, and his voice — gruff, steady Odo, who had held the line at Hollow Bridge and wept only once in all the miles — was shaking. "Sera, you magnificent, impossible woman."

"Odo." She turned her head with effort. The world was very soft now. Very kind. "The king. Watch the king. And watch Edmund — he forgets to eat when he's sad. Both of you. Feed him. He pretends he isn't fond of stewed pears, and he is *lying*."

"I don't—" Edmund began.

"You are," said Pip, kneeling on her other side, Marlow's banner pooling around him, tears cutting tracks through the ash on his face. "He absolutely is."

"Pip." She reached for him and he took her hand and held it against his cheek. "Sweetheart. You found your voice on the road. Don't you dare lose it now. The free cities marched behind a boy with a banner. They'll want to know what he says next."

"I don't know what I say next," Pip whispered.

"Neither did I, at your age." Her breath was getting shorter. She noticed it the way you notice weather in another country — distantly, without alarm. "That's rather the point of the whole business."

The doors of the throne room boomed — once, twice — and then gave, and through them poured the sound of the world: boots, voices, trumpets, the free cities arriving in a tide of steel and torchlight, and above it all, rising on his own feet for the first time in seven years, a king.

Sera saw none of it clearly. She saw Edmund.

He was saying her name. Over and over, quietly, like a ward, like a prayer, like a man building a wall against the dark with nothing but syllables. She lifted her hand — heavier now, so heavy — and laid it against his cheek.

"Listen," she whispered. "Last lesson. No charge."

"Anything."

"The gate you guarded your whole life — you thought it kept the world out." Her vision was honey and candlelight and his face, his dear plain stubborn face, the best thing she had ever seen. "It was keeping you *in*. And look at you. Look what you walked out and became. So when they offer you a gate again — and they will, men like you always end up keeping doors — build one that opens both ways."

"I will," he said. "I swear it."

"And Edmund?" The fading was nearly complete now. She could feel the Ember humming contentedly in the place where her heartbeat used to lead, and beyond it, waiting with the patience of all fires, a kind of dawn she did not have words for and was no longer afraid of. "The green ribbon. In the bottom of my pack. I never wore it because it wasn't special. Wear it anyway. Be ridiculous. Live loudly enough for both of us, and I promise you — I *swear* you — I'll flicker at every single one of your bad jokes."

"They're not bad," Edmund said, crying and laughing at once, holding her as the light in the room changed, as trumpets sounded, as a king's voice rang out over the assembled hosts of the free cities calling a name — *Veyra* — in a tone that made the very stones listen.

"They're terrible," Sera said fondly. "I've loved every one."

And then — gently, the way a candle goes out when its work is done, the way a door closes on a warm room to keep the warmth in — she let go.

The last thing she felt was his arms. The last thing she thought was: *the witch was wrong. The river gave everything back. It just gave it to everyone else.*

The Ember-light in the throne room pulsed once, twice — soft, steady, alive.

And in Edmund Yarrow's fist, clutched tight against a heart that would never quite stop listening for her, a scrap of green ribbon glowed faintly, and did not go out.
