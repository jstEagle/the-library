## 37. The Fall of Veyra

Odo had stood in a great many throne rooms in his life, and he had hated nearly all of them. There was something about the geometry of power — the dais lifting one person above everyone else, the guards arranged like furniture, the way your own voice came back to you smaller than it went out — that had always made his shoulders crawl. He'd spent six years as a royal guard learning to stand at attention in rooms like this one, and three more as an exile learning to hate what those rooms did to people.

But he had never seen a throne room like this.

The doors had been thrown open to the dawn. The great hall of Aldric's palace — Veyra's palace, though Odo's heart refused the possessive — was filled not with courtiers and censors but with soldiers, rebels, free-city militia, and ordinary citizens who had climbed over their own barricades when the news ran through the capital like fire through dry grass: *the king lives. The binding is broken.*

They crowded the gallery. They lined the walls. They stood among the shattered remains of Veyra's honor guard, who had mostly surrendered once the sorcery holding their spines straight simply — stopped. Odo watched one of them now, a boy of maybe nineteen, sitting on the steps with his helmet off, weeping quietly and without shame, the way a man weeps when a hand he didn't know was gripping him finally lets go.

And in the center of it all, King Aldric of the Nine Valleys was on his knees.

He had been kneeling since they'd carried him up from the under-vaults. The unbinding had left him hollowed, gray-skinned, trembling like a man just pulled from deep water. Sera had wanted him kept below, but Aldric had insisted — had *begged*, actually, which Odo found more alarming than anything else — on coming up before the people. So they'd propped him against the foot of his own throne, wrapped in a soldier's cloak because no clean royal robes could be found, and there he knelt while the hall slowly filled with everyone he'd failed.

Odo stood near the dais steps, one of perhaps four people in the room the king could look at without flinching. To his left, Rell — Captain Rell, traitor to Veyra, dead man walking these past weeks who had somehow, absurdly, survived to see this morning — held himself in parade rest so rigid that Odo suspected if anyone touched him he'd shatter. Behind them, Edmund sat on the bottom step with Sera's head on his shoulder, both of them gray-faced and singed and alive, which after the last two days still struck Odo as a small miracle he intended to be grateful for at least until noon. Pip was somewhere in the crowd with Marlow's banner, refusing all suggestions to sit down.

Veyra knelt too.

That was the thing Odo couldn't stop looking at. They'd bound her hands with iron manacles stripped from her own dungeon stores — plain iron, nothing enchanted, because Edmund had touched them first to make sure. She knelt twenty paces from the king she had worn like a glove for nine years, and she knelt *well*, spine straight, chin level, silver hair loose down her back like a woman posing for a portrait rather than awaiting judgment. Her eyes moved across the hall with a terrible calm, cataloguing. Odo had interrogated enemy officers during the border war; he knew the look of someone taking notes for later.

"Get up," someone shouted from the crowd. "Make her kneel properly!"

"She *is* kneeling properly," another voice answered, ragged with grief. "My daughter knelt like that. In this room. Before her."

The shout died. The hall settled into a silence so complete Odo could hear the dawn wind moving the banners.

Aldric raised his head.

* * *

Odo had served Aldric for four years before the Binding Day when everything changed. He remembered a king who was clever but cautious, kind but slow, a man who weighed every decision so long the weight of deciding sometimes became the decision. He remembered a voice that was always measured, always a half-step behind events.

The voice that filled the hall now was none of those things.

"I know what you have come to see," Aldric said, and the acoustics of the old throne room — built by kings who wanted every whisper heard — carried it to the farthest corners. "You have come to see her burn. Some of you have come to see me do it." A pause. "I do not blame you. For nine years I watched through eyes that were not mine as she ruled in my name. I felt my own hands sign her orders. I heard my own voice speak her sentences. And beneath all of it, in the small locked room where she kept what remained of me, I screamed and screamed, and no one heard."

In the gallery, a woman began to sob. Others joined her. Nobody told them to stop.

"So believe me when I say: there is no punishment this hall could devise for Veyra that I have not already imagined in detail, at night, for nine years." His voice dropped, and the whole hall leaned toward him. "And that is precisely why I will not do it."

Murmurs. Anger in the murmur. Odo understood it. He felt it too — a hot, simple wanting in his chest that said *she took Marlow, she took Rell's whole life, she took nine years of this good man, burn her.* He'd spent his exile learning that justice and vengeance were different animals, and he'd spent the last hour discovering that knowing the difference didn't make the wanting go away.

"Aldric," Odo said quietly, at the king's gesture, and stepped forward to read from the scroll they had taken from Veyra's private vault — the tally Odo himself had spent three sleepless nights compiling from captured orders, cipher letters, and the testimony of freed prisoners. His voice was a guard's voice, flat and precise, because that was the only way he could get through it.

"For the suppression of Correnvale: four hundred dead, the council dissolved, the archives burned. For the clearing of the Ashfall provinces: eleven villages depopulated by decree, their people marched north as labor. For the mage-hunts: two hundred and sixteen executed without trial, their families bound by oath-silence." He read on. The names went on. It took the better part of an hour, and Odo did not hurry it, because he had learned in the last months that names were the whole point — that Beatrix had died pressing a map into a stranger's hands because she believed names mattered, and Marlow had died shielding his twin because he believed it too.

Through all of it, Veyra watched the king.

When Odo finished, Aldric rose. It cost him visibly — Rell started forward and was waved off — but he rose, and stood, and looked down at the woman who had worn him.

"You were my friend," Aldric said.

It was not what anyone expected. The hall stilled.

"When my father died, you were the first person in the palace who spoke to me as though I were a person and not a coronation. You taught me the river roads. You argued with me about grain tariffs until sunrise, and you were usually right." Aldric's voice cracked and he let it. "I have spent nine years trying to understand how the woman who did those things became the woman who did *these*. I understand now that you never thought of it as theft. You thought of it as rescue. You saw a kingdom drowning in its own gentleness, and you decided you would save it by force, and you decided that my consent — everyone's consent — was a price worth paying."

"I was right," Veyra said.

Her voice was the first sound she'd made all morning, and the hall flinched at it — low, unhurried, almost gentle. "The free cities are marching *today*, Aldric, because you exist to march them. The borders are secure. The plague roads are built. Give me ten years and no child in this kingdom goes hungry." She turned her head, and her gaze found Edmund on the step, then Odo, then Rell, lingering longest on Rell with something in it that Odo chose not to name. "You will rule better broken than you ever ruled whole. That is not cruelty. That is arithmetic."

"Arithmetic," Aldric repeated softly. "Yes. That is exactly the word. Everything counted, nothing weighed." He descended one step, so that for the first time they were nearly level. "Here is mine, then. I cannot execute you. Not because you don't deserve it — the hall can decide your desserts without my help — but because if I kill you, I kill the argument. I make you a martyr to everyone who ever thought kindness was weakness, and in thirty years some clever angry child finds your story and does it all again, better. No." He shook his head slowly. "I sentence you to be *uninteresting*."

A ripple of confusion through the hall.

"There is a valley," Aldric said, louder now, addressing the crowd, "in the eastern hills, where magic has never reached. My wardens kept a gate there for seven generations. Nothing enchanted can cross it. No scrying can find it. No spell can follow." He paused, and Odo saw, unexpectedly, Edmund lift his head. "Veyra of the Ember Throne. Your sentence is exile to that valley, there to live out your natural life among people who owe you nothing, governed by nothing but ordinary law, in a land where you have no more power than any farmer's wife. You will grow old. You will grow *slow*. You will watch seasons come and do nothing remarkable about them. And the kingdom will heal around you without ever thinking of you at all."

"That's it?" Someone shouted it from the back — a big rebel sergeant, blood still crusted at his temple. "She kills hundreds and we give her a *farm*?"

"No," said a new voice.

Edmund got to his feet. He looked terrible — hollow-eyed, his hands bandaged where the Ember's passage had scorched even his null skin — and when he spoke, his voice carried in that hushed way it had, the quietness that made everyone lean in.

"It's worse than you think," Edmund said. "I lived there. Thirty-one years." He looked at Veyra, and Odo watched her look back, and something passed between them — the usurper and the null, the woman who had lied about the gate and the man who had guarded it. "There's nothing wrong with the valley. That's the thing. The bread is good. The neighbors help with the harvest. It's peaceful, and kind, and utterly, permanently *small*. You'll be happy there, some days. That's the worst part. You'll catch yourself being content, and then you'll remember what you gave up to be content, and it will start all over again." He shrugged one shoulder, tired. "I spent my whole life trying to get out. You're going to spend the rest of yours wishing you could matter. It's not mercy. Trust me."

For the first time all morning, Veyra's composure flickered. Just at the edges. Just enough.

"The gate is open now," Aldric said gently. "People walk through freely, both ways, by choice. You will not have that choice. The valley keeps its peace, and you will keep it company." He gestured to the guards. "Take her to the eastern road. See that she wants for nothing. That's an order."

As they lifted her upright, Veyra paused beside Edmund. She studied him the way she'd studied the hall earlier — cataloguing — and then, quietly, so only their end of the room heard:

"The lie kept you loyal for thirty years, warden. Remember that the next time a king tells you a comforting truth."

Then she walked out of the throne room with her spine straight and her chin level, and history closed behind her like water.

* * *

The hall emptied slowly, the way a fever breaks.

Odo found himself on the dais steps with Rell, neither of them saying much. This had become, over the past weeks, one of the pleasures of his life: silence with Rell required no maintenance. Below them, workers were already sweeping glass from the floor. Somewhere a bell was ringing — not alarm, celebration — and would probably not stop ringing until somebody ordered it to, and nobody was going to order it to.

"You knew he wouldn't execute her," Rell said finally. It wasn't quite a question.

"I hoped," Odo said. "Hoping's braver than knowing." He rolled his shoulder, which had been dislocated two days ago and had opinions about it. "Beatrix would've wanted her dead, mind. Beatrix would've done it herself and slept fine."

"And you?"

Odo considered. "I wanted it. All morning I wanted it. But I've been the man following orders I shouldn't have, and I've been the man paying for other men's orders, and I've noticed that killing the person at the top doesn't actually fix either one of us. It just clears the stage." He nodded toward the door Veyra had gone through. "Making her live small — making her *ordinary* — that's not mercy. That's the sentence fitting the crime. She stole a kingdom's right to choose. Now she gets a life without choices. It rhymes."

Rell made a sound that might have been a laugh. "You've become a philosopher in your old age."

"I've become exhausted in my old age. Philosophy's cheaper than brandy."

Down on the floor, Aldric was doing something Odo had never once seen a monarch do: he was going person to person among the wounded, kneeling by each pallet, listening. Really listening — you could tell by the way people's faces changed, the surprise of it. Nine years of stolen will, and the first thing the man did with his own was give it away by the handful.

"He'll need advisors," Rell said. "Good ones. Ones who'll argue."

"Mm."

"Guards, too. Real ones. Ones who've learned what happens when you don't ask questions." Rell's jaw worked. "I'm saying I'd stay, Odo. Rebuild the shield-watch. Somebody has to make sure the next Veyra trips on the first stair."

Odo looked at him for a long moment. "Somebody has to," he agreed. "And I'm saying I'd come with you, at least partway. Old habits." He smiled, and it surprised him by not hurting. "Though I confess I've gotten used to traveling with a ridiculous family, and I haven't finished negotiating the terms of my release."

Speaking of which —

He found most of them on the palace terrace, where the morning had warmed into one of those impossible post-battle mornings, gold and rinsed-clean, as though the sky itself had been bound and newly unbound. Pip sat on the balustrade with Marlow's banner across his knees, talking with two Correnvale soldiers, and — Odo noted, with a grandfatherly pang — laughing. Actually laughing, mid-sentence, the first true laughter since the bridge. Grief didn't leave. But it made room.

Edmund and Sera stood at the far rail, her head against his shoulder, watching the city. Watching the free-city banners coming up the processional road, rank on rank, and the crowds lining the streets, and the small figures of children running along the rooftops.

Odo joined them and followed their gaze.

"What now?" he asked, because someone had to ask it, and he'd long ago accepted the role of the man who asked things.

"Now," Edmund said slowly, "the king rules, the cities argue, the world does what it does." He flexed his bandaged hands. "And there's a gate in the eastern hills with no keeper, and a road I've never walked, and—" he glanced down at Sera, and his voice did the thing it did, went soft and certain at once— "and apparently I don't have to decide alone anymore. Which is still strange. Ask me again in a year."

"In a year," Sera murmured, and there was something in the way she said it — light as ash, quick as a door closing — that Odo heard and chose, for the moment, not to hear. She was very pale under the wind-flush. She had been pale since the vault, and she had laughed off every question about it with a hedge-witch's fluency.

But Odo had been a guard for a king whose smile was a lie written on his face by someone else. He knew what it looked like when someone wore a pleasant expression over a locked room.

He watched her lean into Edmund's side and say something that made Edmund laugh, and he watched her hand, hanging at her side, close slowly into a fist — and open again before Edmund could turn.

Below, the bells rang on. The banners climbed the hill. The world, astonishingly, went on.

"Sera," Odo said lightly, "walk with an old man a moment. I want your opinion on something."

She looked up. Smiled. And behind the smile, faint as embers under ash, Odo saw the thing she was hiding — and understood, with cold certainty, exactly how the next chapter of this story would hurt.
