## 38. Embers and Ashes

The throne room smelled of smoke and lilies, which Edmund would think, later, was exactly the sort of detail Sera would have found funny. All that power, all that terror, and at the end of it the air tasted like a funeral in a wealthy man's parlor.

He did not stay for the speeches.

He carried her down the long hall while the marble still steamed underfoot, past banners that had hung loyal to Veyra an hour ago and now — with the fluid honesty of cloth — bore Aldric's stag again. Nobody stopped him. Word had already run through the palace like water through a cracked floor: the null had done something, the flame was dying, the witch was dying, get out of the way.

Sera weighed almost nothing. That was the cruelty of it. She had spent weeks telling him she ate like a bird because hedge-witches ran on spite rather than bread, and he'd laughed, and now her head against his shoulder was light as a bundle of dried herbs.

"Put me down somewhere with a view," she said, in a voice like paper settling. "If I'm going to do this dramatically, I want a window."

"There's a balcony," he managed.

"Splendid. Balconies are very dignified." Her eyes were already ember-lit, faint gold coals behind the iris, and when she blinked the light guttered. "And Edmund? Don't do the face."

"What face?"

"The one where you've decided this is your fault. You have a whole library of faces and that's the worst one."

* * *

The balcony looked east over the capital, over the smoking quarters of Veyra's barracks and the rooftops where people were already climbing out to see what the silence meant. Somewhere below, the free cities' army was flowing through gates that had been opened from inside. Pip's banner — Marlow's banner — was a small gray smudge among the columns, held high, held steady.

Edmund sat with his back against the balustrade and cradled her against his chest, and the light in her came and went like breathing.

"It doesn't hurt," she said, before he could ask. "That's the thing nobody tells you. I expected fire. It feels more like being a rumor. Like I'm becoming a story about myself." She smiled at the horizon. "I always wanted to be a good story."

"You're the best one I know."

"Flatterer. You're a warden. Wardens don't flatter, they *assess*." She coughed, and the cough glowed. "Assess me, then. Go on. You're good at watching things. Watch me and tell me what you see."

So he did what she asked, because he could refuse her nothing and because it was easier than weeping. He watched her the way he had watched the valley for thirty years, with the patience of a man who had learned that attention was a form of love.

"I see a woman who lied to a witch to learn her own future," he said quietly, "and then walked into it anyway, with her eyes open, because she'd decided other people were worth more than her fear. I see someone who fed strangers before she fed herself. Who taught a boy to read and a guardsman to forgive himself and a gatekeeper to want things." His voice went rough. "I see the person I built a whole life of walls without ever knowing I was waiting for."

Sera was quiet a moment. The ember-light pulsed, steadied.

"Well," she said at last, thickly. "That'll do."

"Will it?"

"It'll have to. I only budgeted enough self for one more speech, and you've used most of it up being decent at me." She turned her head so she could see him, and the gold in her eyes had softened to something like hearth-light, like the lamp in a window over a long road. "Listen. The Ember has to go out — that's not negotiable, it's mostly gone already, it's just deciding how. But flames don't like ending cold. So it's leaving me a little. A coal. Enough to keep one lantern lit, if somebody thinks to catch it."

"Catch it how?"

"You'll know. You're terrible at magic and wonderful at keeping things alive. Between those two failings, you'll manage." Her hand found his, small and cooling, and pressed his fingers closed around something he couldn't yet see. "Don't waste it mourning me, though. Mourning's fine, mind. Wallow a bit. Have a good cry on somebody's shoulder — Odo cries beautifully, he'll deny it, but he does. Just don't build the wall back up. Promise me the wall stays down."

"The wall's gone," Edmund said. "You burned it. You and Beatrix between you, you burned the whole thing down."

"Good." She closed her eyes. Opened them. "Good. Then I've done my work. Everything after this is just—" the light flickered, "—just scenery."

"Sera—"

"Hush. Let a woman finish." She took a breath that seemed to draw the evening in with it, the last rose-gold of the sunset, the first stars. "I loved you, Edmund Yarrow. Quietly, badly, in the middle of everything, and I wouldn't change a single hour of it. When you get old — and you will, you're built to last, stubborn as oak — I want you to remember that somebody loved you not in spite of your ordinariness but *because* of it. Because you were the realest thing in a world full of spells."

The ember-light rose once, gently, like a sigh given light.

And then it settled into her skin and stayed there, faintly warm, faintly gold, and Sera Ash — hedge-witch, liar, prophet, cook of miraculous stews from nothing — faded the way a candle goes out when the morning comes: so gradually that you couldn't name the moment, only notice, afterward, that the dark had gotten softer instead of harder.

She died smiling. Of course she did.

* * *

He didn't know how long he sat there. Long enough that the stars wheeled. Long enough that his legs went numb beneath him and he found he didn't care, because numbness was a mercy and he intended to hoard every mercy the night offered.

King Aldric found him there near dawn.

Edmund heard him come — or rather, didn't hear him, which for a king in armor was its own statement — and looked up to find a tall man standing at the balustrade with the careful posture of someone who had recently been a puppet and was still learning where his own limbs attached. His crown was off. He held it in one hand like a hat he wasn't sure belonged to him anymore.

His eyes were his own now. That was the first thing Edmund checked. Empty no longer; they were gray and tired and human, and wet at the edges.

"You're the warden," Aldric said.

"Was."

"The whole court is telling me what happened. Half of them say a soldier broke the vault. Half say a ghost. Odo — my Odo, small boy the last I saw him, grown into a mountain — says it was a gatekeeper who couldn't be burned." The king lowered himself, joints cracking, to sit against the balustrade beside him, an unkingly sprawl that made Edmund like him immediately. "None of them mention the witch."

"Everyone mentions the witch," Edmund said. "They just don't know they're mentioning her."

Aldric nodded slowly, and for a while they sat in the kind of silence that only two men who have both been prisoners can share — one in chains of iron he could see, one in a valley he couldn't leave and hadn't understood until it was too late that he'd never once asked why.

"She felt it," Aldric said finally. "When the binding broke. Do you understand what that was like? Twenty years of being a passenger in my own skull, and then suddenly — *me*. Just me. Terrifying. I sat down on the floor of the throne room and cried like a child, and your friends stood around pretending to inspect the architecture." He huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Rell stayed close. In case I needed guarding from myself, I think. Loyal to the last, that man. To the wrong queen for twenty years, and then, in one night, to the right thing."

"He paid for it."

"They all paid. That's the arithmetic of tyrants — everyone else pays." Aldric turned the crown over in his hands. Stag-worked gold, and where Veyra's ember had lived in its band there was now only dull metal, dead as a coin. "Yarrow. I'm told your family kept a gate in the Sunken Vale for nine generations."

"We did."

"And I'm told the reason was a lie. That there was nothing behind it but quiet, bought with a story, so that wardens would keep warding." The king's jaw tightened. "That lie was told by my grandmother's grandmother's court. I found the original decree in Veyra's archives, three days after I could finally read again. Your whole line — bred hollow on purpose, seeded into that valley like a lock planted in a door — for nothing. For *nothing*, Yarrow. There was no horror behind your gate. There never was."

"I know," Edmund said. "The Ember told me. In the vault."

"And yet here you sit, having carried its map across a continent, having walked through fire that unmakes archmages, and I have to ask —" Aldric's voice frayed, "— why? If the gate was a lie, why defend it? Why defend *us*?"

Edmund looked out at the city waking. Smoke still rose in threads. Somewhere a baker, madman, had lit his ovens, and the smell of bread was drifting up past the lilies, and that — that absurd, ordinary smell — was the whole answer, and it took him a moment to find words big enough to be worthy of it.

"Because the lie was a lie," he said. "But the peace was real. People lived whole lives in that valley. Children grew up. My mother grew onions. It doesn't matter that the wall was built on a falsehood — what mattered was what it sheltered." He swallowed. "Beatrix said something once. Carry it, don't wield it. I think she meant the map, but I think she meant everything. Duty isn't the wall, sir. Duty is what you carry *to* the wall."

Aldric was silent for a long moment. Then he set the dead crown down on the stone between them, deliberately, like a man setting down a weight.

"Ask me for something," he said. "Anything. Lands. Titles. The vale itself, chartered free and clear. I am told by three councils and one extremely opinionated twelve-year-old with a banner that I owe you a kingdom, and I have discovered that I intensely enjoy being able to mean the things I say, so please — let me mean this."

And Edmund, who had spent thirty years wanting nothing, found — to his mild astonishment — that he wanted something after all. Several somethings. They'd been accumulating all this time, quietly, like snow.

"I want the gate rebuilt," he said.

Aldric blinked. "The gate."

"Not as a prison. Not as a lock. The valley shouldn't be sealed — that was the lie, and lies make bad masonry. But the wards are real, the stones are real, the *work* is real, and there are people who need doors, your majesty. Exiles. Runaways. Folk the magic world has chewed up — like Sera's family, like Rell, like half the company. A place they can walk into *on purpose*. Not driven there. Not sentenced there. Choosing it." He heard his own voice steady, and realized this was the first speech of his life and it had been forty years in the drafting. "A gate with the bar on the outside, so to speak. Where the keeper holds the door open and asks only one question: *do you mean to enter?*"

The king stared at him. And then he laughed — a rusty, broken-hinged sound, the laugh of a man whose laughter had been locked up for twenty years and was discovering it still worked.

"A door that people choose," Aldric said. "Of all the things in my kingdom — ships, gold, my undying gratitude — the man who toppled a sorcerer-queen asks me for a *gatehouse*." He wiped his eyes. "It's perfect. It's absolutely perfect, and I suspect a certain witch put you up to it, and I wish to heaven I could thank her myself."

"She'd have charged you for the consultation."

"No doubt." The king rose, joints cracking again, and this time he offered Edmund his hand and pulled him up like any two tired men at the end of any long night. "Stone and masons and charter, then. Whatever the vale needs. And Yarrow —" his grip tightened briefly, "— when it's built, I should like to walk through it once. As a guest. Kings rarely get to knock."

* * *

They buried her three days later on a hill above the capital, because Sera had left instructions — of course she had, folded into Odo's pack years ago, dated and witnessed, the scheming darling — and the instructions specified: high ground, facing west, *near roads, so I can eavesdrop on travelers*.

Pip read the words, because his voice had been found on the march and refused to be lost again. Odo stood like a mountain and cried beautifully and denied it through his teeth. Rell wore no colors at all, just plain wool, and laid his old captain's insignia on the grave beside a sprig of rivermint. Marlow's ashes, in their second urn, were buried alongside, at Pip's flat insistence: "They hated being apart. Even dead, especially dead."

And Edmund, when the others had drifted back down toward the road and its noise, knelt and pressed his palm to the turned earth, and felt it.

Warmth. Faint as a banked coal. Exactly where she had closed his fingers around something invisible on a balcony at dusk.

*Well,* her voice said, not in his ears but in some quieter room of him, *told you you'd manage.*

He laughed until he cried, and cried until he laughed, kneeling on a hillside above four roads, holding the last ember of Sera Ash in the cup of his hand — and it did not burn, because he was Edmund Yarrow, who could not be burned, and she had known that too, clever thing, she had counted on it all along.

Below, the company waited by the crossroads with horses and supplies and a charter signed by a grateful king, and beyond the western hills, a long road home to a valley that was about to learn what it had really been all along.

Not a prison. A door.

"All right," Edmund said aloud, to the grave, to the coal, to himself. He stood, brushed off his knees, and started down the hill toward his family. "Let's go build it."

Behind him, faint and warm against his palm, the ember pulsed — once, twice — like laughter.
