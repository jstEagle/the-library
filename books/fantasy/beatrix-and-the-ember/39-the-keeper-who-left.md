## 39. The Keeper Who Left

The gate had never looked like this.

Edmund stood on the valley side of it in the first week of spring, hands in his pockets, and tried to decide whether he approved. The iron doors that had been sealed since before his grandfather's grandfather were now propped open with two wedges of mountain oak, and between them hung a new sign, painted by Pip himself in letters that leaned like drunk soldiers:

**THE VALLEY OF PEACE — ALL WHO COME IN KINDNESS WELCOME. ALL WHO COME OTHERWISE: THE KEEPER IS ARMED AND IRRITABLE.**

"You misspelled 'irritable,'" Edmund said.

"I did not," said Pip, who was up a ladder rehanging the sign for the fourth time because he kept finding something wrong with it. "I spelled it *better*. It's got an extra R in it for menace."

"That's not how menace works."

"You guarded a door for thirty years and never once let anyone through it. What do you know about how anything works?"

Edmund considered this. "Fair."

The ladder wobbled. Pip swore, caught the top rung, and came down with the dignity of a man pretending he'd meant to do that. He was twenty-two now, or thereabouts — nobody had ever known the twins' true birthday, and Marlow had claimed they were born on two different days out of sheer contrariness — and he had filled out in the year since the war ended. Not taller. Just *more*. His voice had settled. His silences, which after Marlow's death had been so deep you could fall into them, had grown shallower, and he'd learned to fill what remained with talk instead of recklessness, which everyone agreed was an improvement.

He stopped at the bottom of the ladder and looked at the open gate the way Edmund had once looked at it closed: like it was the whole world.

"It feels strange," Pip said. "Leaving it open."

"You'll get used to it."

"That's what frightens me." Pip turned the sign over in his hands, checking the paint. "My whole life I've been breaking into places. Locked doors, sealed vaults, one memorable window in the palace that Odo still won't discuss. And now you're handing me a door whose entire job is being open, and telling me to guard it." He glanced sideways. "What if I'm bad at it?"

"Then you'll be bad at it honestly," Edmund said, "which is more than most keepers can say."

The truth — the thing Aldric had whispered to him through the Ember's silence, the thing Veyra had never known because she'd never bothered to ask — was that the gate had never held back any horror. No ancient prisoner. No door behind the world. The valley's peace was real; it simply wasn't magic. It was the absence of magic, bought generations ago by wardens who told their sons a lie to keep them loyal, because a man will guard a prison forever but might grow curious about a garden. Edmund had spent a winter furious about it. Then he'd spent another winter deciding what to do with it, and what he'd decided was this: the lie had kept people safe, but the truth could keep them free, and freedom was worth the risk of curiosity.

So the gate stood open. The wards were lowered to nothing more than a courtesy — a bell that rang when someone crossed with ill intent, tuned by Sera before her strength began to fade, though she'd insisted on finishing it even when her hands shook. Travelers came through now. Farmers from the eastern provinces, fleeing nothing, wanting only good soil and quiet. A family of stone-masons. Last month, a mage — the first magic ever to enter the valley, sliding off its borders like rain off glass, exactly as it always slid off Edmund — a young woman who wanted to study why the valley was empty and had been politely informed by Pip that she could study it from the outside until she learned some manners.

"She'll be back," Pip said now, with satisfaction. "They always come back when you're rude to them properly. It builds character."

* * *

The last of the packing took three days, which embarrassed Edmund, because he owned almost nothing.

It was Sera who pointed out the problem. She sat on his bed — his narrow keeper's bed, the same one he'd slept in for thirty years — surrounded by the contents of his life arranged in neat squares, and she was laughing at him. Gently. But laughing.

"Edmund. Sweetheart. You have four shirts, one coat, a whetstone, and forty-one books on gate maintenance."

"Forty-three."

"*Forty-three books on gate maintenance.*"

"They're different editions."

"They are the same book wearing different hats." She picked up the topmost volume and fanned through it. "You are moving into a house with five other people. There is no room for forty-three hats."

He sat down beside her. Outside the window, spring was doing its annual work on the valley — the orchards in blossom, the river loud with meltwater, the mountains still holding snow on their shoulders like old men reluctant to take off their coats. He had looked at this view every morning of his life. He found, examining it now, that he could look at it without it looking back. That was new. That was the whole difference, maybe, between a cell and a home: whether the view required something of you.

"I don't know how to want things," he said. It came out plainer than he'd intended. Most true things did.

Sera set down the book. Her hair had gone nearly white in the year since the Ember — the flame had burned slow in her, patient, but it burned — and her face had thinned around the cheekbones until sometimes, in certain light, she seemed lit from within, which she was. But her eyes hadn't changed at all. They were still the eyes of a hedge-witch who had swindled three tax collectors and healed a village for free in the same afternoon.

"Yes, you do," she said. "You just spent thirty years practicing wanting small things, and now the big ones feel greedy. So we'll practice." She counted on her fingers. "One: you want a house with a kitchen big enough for all of us, because Odo cooks like he's feeding a garrison and takes up counter space like a siege engine. Two: you want a room with a window facing east, because you've earned sunrises. Three—" she tapped his chest, right over the heart, "—you already have everything else, so stop hoarding books about doors and come live with us."

"The map comes too," Edmund said.

"Obviously the map comes." She said it the way other people said *obviously the sun rises*, and he loved her for it, and the love still startled him every time, arriving like weather.

Beatrix's map hung above the hearth in the keeper's cottage, framed in mountain oak by a carpenter who had refused payment and asked instead to shake the hand of "the man who carried it." The heat-marks had faded to a soft amber, the burning roads gone cool, but on certain evenings — Sera swore it was true, and Edmund had stopped doubting her about such things — the lines seemed to glow faintly, as if somewhere under the ink the fire remembered where it had been going. They had followed it across a continent. It had cost them Marlow, and Rell, and Beatrix herself before any of it began. And it had given them each other, which was the sort of arithmetic Edmund had never been able to make balance and had finally stopped trying to.

They took it down together, wrapped it in wool, and packed it flat between two of the forty-three books, which felt appropriately irreverent and appropriate anyway.

* * *

On their last morning, the valley turned out to see them off, which mortified Edmund completely.

There weren't many of them — the valley had never held many people — but they lined the road to the gate in their work clothes with their children, and old Marta from the mill pressed a sack of flour into his arms and said, "For the road, though I hear you're only going as far as Correnvale, which hardly counts as a road," and the blacksmith's boy asked Pip if it was true he'd stolen the crown jewels, and Pip said, "No comment," which everyone correctly understood as *yes*.

Odo waited at the gate itself, arms crossed, wearing his old royal guard cloak with the insignia torn off and, at Sera's insistence, a new one stitched on: a simple door, open. He'd fought beside King Aldric for fifteen years and against him for one, and served under Veyra's compulsion for three, and when the unbinding came he had wept in front of two armies, and then punched Captain Rell — the late, lamented, impossible Rell — on the shoulder hard enough to bruise, which among soldiers of their vintage was roughly equivalent to a eulogy.

"You're sure about this?" Odo said quietly, while Pip said goodbye to the blacksmith's boy with elaborate coolness. "The valley. Leaving it. You can change your mind. Nobody would think less—"

"Everyone would think less of me. You'd think least of all."

"I would," Odo agreed. "I'd be insufferable about it for years." He put a hand on Edmund's shoulder, brief and heavy. "Your father kept this gate. His father before him. And every one of them died without ever seeing what was on the other side of their own door." He nodded at the open gates, at the road beyond winding down toward the lowlands, green and enormous. "You get to see it. Make it count for all of them."

Edmund looked back once — just once, at the cottage with its empty hooks and swept floor, at the orchard he'd planted the year his father died, at the whole green silence of the place that had been his entire world. It didn't ache the way he'd expected. It ached the way a well-worn coat aches when you finally give it away: with gratitude, mostly, and a little guilt, and the knowledge that it had done its work and deserved rest.

Then he walked through the gate, and the world was on the other side of it, and he did not look back again.

* * *

The house in Correnvale had been Marlow's idea, in a way.

He'd left money — twins kept accounts nobody knew about, it turned out; Marlow had been skimming a percentage off every job for eleven years and hiding it in six banks under four false names, and Pip had found the last of it in a hollow boot heel and cried for an hour and then laughed for two — and in his final letter, unsent, found folded inside his banner, he'd written: *when this is done, get a house with a bad staircase. Bad staircases mean stories. Good houses are boring.*

The house they bought had a staircase that creaked in seven distinct notes, which Odo had transcribed and claimed was a folk song from his grandmother's village, and which Sera had identified, definitively, as a folk song from nowhere, invented by Odo's grandmother specifically to win arguments. The kitchen was large enough for a garrison cook. Edmund's room faced east.

Pip had the room nearest the door, because he still woke at every sound, and because he liked being the one who answered knocks. Some nights Edmund heard him talking to Marlow — not a séance, nothing so dramatic, just the low running commentary of a twin who had lost half of himself and learned to carry the conversation for both: *you'd hate this soup, you'd love this horse, wait till you hear what Odo did.* It had frightened Edmund the first time he overheard it. Now it sounded like what it was: a man keeping a lamp lit in a window.

And on the mantel, in the main room, sat the lantern.

Sera had made it herself in the weeks when her hands still obeyed her — plain iron, unremarkable, the kind of lantern sold in every market in the free cities. But inside it, where a candle should be, there was only a sliver of ember-glass, cooled from the heart of the flame she carried, and it glowed. It had glowed for a year. It would glow, the mages said, for as long as Sera lived, and perhaps — they said this part carefully, watching her face — a little longer.

She didn't like to talk about the fading. She made jokes instead, terrible ones, about finally having an excuse to be mysterious, about haunting Edmund specifically because he was easiest to haunt. But some nights he woke and found her standing at the mantel, holding her own wrist, watching the little light burn, and those nights he got up and stood beside her and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the light wasn't already saying: *I am here. I am here. I am here.*

Tonight was one of the good nights. All seven staircase-notes announced Odo coming down, followed by the smell of a stew built for twelve being eaten by six, and Pip arrived breathless from the door with news, because Pip always arrived breathless with news.

"A rider from the capital. From the king's own household." He dropped onto a bench and helped himself to bread with the entitlement of family. "Aldric's calling a gathering. All the free cities. Everyone who stood at the gates, everyone who sheltered us, everyone who—" he waved the bread vaguely, "—did war things. Honors. Ceremonies. Odo, they want to give you your commission back."

Odo didn't look up from the pot. "Tell them I have a commission. It involves onions."

"They want to give *me* a medal, Edmund. A medal. For carrying a body down a mountain." His voice did the thing it did sometimes, going bright and brittle at the edges. "Marlow should get the medal. Marlow did the dying."

"Marlow," said Sera, passing behind him and resting her chin briefly on his head, "would have demanded two medals and sold the second one."

Pip laughed — wetly, but he laughed — and the brittleness passed, as it always passed now, faster than it used to.

"And you," Pip said, pointing the bread at Edmund. "The king wants to see you. Personally. Says he owes you a debt he can't name in a letter."

Edmund looked at the stew. At the table with its mismatched chairs. At the map, framed and glowing faint amber in the lamplight, hanging on the wall of a house full of people. At the lantern on the mantel, burning its small stubborn fire.

"Tell the king I'll come," he said. "But tell him he doesn't owe me anything. Tell him I was paid in full on the road." He paused. "And tell him if he tries to give me land, I'm giving it to Pip."

"You absolutely are not—"

"Land, Pip. You'd be a *landowner.*"

The argument that followed lasted through dinner and well past it, and it was, Edmund thought, sitting back with his hands laced over his belly while Sera argued his side purely for the pleasure of contradiction, the finest sound a house could hold.

Later, when the others had gone up — note by creaking note, the whole crooked song of it — Edmund stood alone at the mantel with the lantern. The ember-light moved on his face, warm as a hand.

"All right, Beatrix," he said quietly. "We made it home. Both definitions."

The light flickered — once, twice — and steadied, and if he chose to believe that was an answer, well. Thirty years of guarding a door had taught him patience, and one year of living had taught him hope, and between the two of them he could afford to believe almost anything.

From upstairs, faintly, came Pip's voice, mid-story, telling Marlow about the medal he was definitely going to refuse.

Edmund smiled, banked the lamp, and went up the singing stairs to bed.

In the morning there would be a road, and a king, and whatever came next. But that was morning's business. Tonight the house held, the light held, and the keeper — the one who had left, the one who had finally arrived — slept without listening for anything at the door.
