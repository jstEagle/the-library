## 40. Chapter 40

The road to the valley had never been so crowded.

Edmund Yarrow stood at the old warden's post — his father's post, and his grandfather's before him, though neither of them would recognize it now — and watched the line of travelers wind down from the northern ridge. There were merchants with wagons squeaking under cloth-covered goods. Pilgrims in seven colors of patched wool. A troupe of players arguing loudly about whether the wolf in their famous play should die in act three or live to menace the village again in act four. A woman carrying a cage of doves. Three children racing each other along the verge, shrieking, while their mother called out warnings nobody obeyed.

A year ago, every one of them would have turned back at the ridgeline. The wards had seen to that — a low dread that settled into the bones of anyone who approached the valley with intent to enter, turning them gently, persistently, like a shepherd dog that had never once barked but never once failed. Now the wards were gone, or rather, they were something else. Pip called them the Threshold Questions. Anyone who came to the gate was asked three things, and if they answered honestly, they walked through.

*Do you come in peace? What do you carry? What do you leave behind?*

The third question was Pip's addition. Edmund had argued against it — too poetic, he'd said, people will lie — and Pip had looked at him with Marlow's exact raised eyebrow, the one that still stopped Edmund's heart for half a beat, and said, "Everyone lies about what they carry, Ed. Nobody lies about what they're leaving behind. Not really."

He had been right, of course. He usually was, these days, which Edmund suspected was less wisdom than grief wearing wisdom's coat, but it worked all the same.

"Next!" Pip called from the gate itself, where he sat on a stool entirely too grand for a man of twenty-two, boots propped on the old locking bar that Edmund had repurposed as a footrest. The bar had not locked anything in a year. It made an excellent footrest. Edmund liked to think his grandfather rolled in his grave about it, and liked further to think that, if he'd met the man, he'd have won him around.

The dove woman stepped up. She was maybe fifty, sun-brown, with the calloused hands of someone who made her living by things that flew.

"Purpose?"

"Selling birds at the spring market in Harrowdale. They say there hasn't been one in years, on account of the wards."

"There hasn't."

"Well," she said, "someone's got to restock the sky."

Pip grinned. "What do you carry?"

"Doves. Feed. My grandmother's ring, which I'm not selling no matter what Harrowdale offers." She patted the cage. "And a grudge against a man named Fennick, but it's small and I've mostly fed it to the birds."

"What do you leave behind?"

The woman was quiet a moment. "The road," she said finally. "Eleven years of it. My daughter's buried in Correnvale and I kept walking because stopping meant knowing where I was relative to her grave. This is the farthest point from that city I could find." She shrugged, embarrassed. "It's time to put down roots somewhere the ground doesn't remind me."

Pip wrote something in his ledger — he recorded everything now, names and answers, a book of thresholds — and waved her through. "Harrowdale's a day south. Good soil. Ask for Tam Broadfoot about land; he'll haggle but he'll feed you first."

She went through the gate leading her doves, and Edmund watched until she passed the old boundary stones, half-expecting the air to shiver where magic met its absence, the way it always did, the way it still did, a faint pressure like a held breath released. The doves didn't seem to mind. Nothing did, anymore.

"She fed her grudge to the doves," Pip said, appearing at Edmund's elbow. "That's going in my memoirs."

"You're not writing memoirs. You're twenty-two and you can't sit through Odo's stories without falling asleep."

"Odo's stories are *long*, Ed. There's a difference between endurance and quality." Pip leaned on the doorframe. "You're doing the thing again."

"What thing."

"The staring-at-the-road-like-it-owes-you-money thing." Pip studied him. "Big day tomorrow."

Tomorrow. The first anniversary. They were calling it Emberday in the free cities, which Edmund found faintly embarrassing, and Unbinding Day in the capital, which he found worse, and nothing at all here in the valley, where Aldric's new decree — *let no day be made holy by my suffering* — had been taken seriously by exactly the people who'd suffered least and celebrated most.

"I know what day it is," Edmund said.

"You always say that like it's a threat."

"It is a threat. Last year you organized a bonfire and Marlow's banner caught fire and you cried."

"I did not cry. There was smoke. Smoke near eyes causes—" Pip gave up. "Fine. I cried. I cry at everything now, it's my whole personality, Marlow would be insufferable about it." But he said his brother's name easily, the way you touch a scar that's finished aching, and Edmund felt the familiar complicated warmth of it. "Go home, Ed. Sera's lantern needs lighting before dark, and you're the only one who does it right."

"There's no wrong way to light a lantern."

"There absolutely is and you know the right way, with the little ceremony and the three breaths, and if I did it you'd stand there radiating disapproval like a wet cat." Pip shooed him. "Go. I'll close the threshold."

*The threshold doesn't close,* Edmund almost said. That was the whole point. But some habits of speech were older than the new world, and he let Pip keep his.

* * *

The house sat a quarter mile from the gate, built in the first summer after, when Edmund had realized he could not sleep in the warden's post anymore and also could not bear to tear it down. So they'd built beside it instead — a proper house, two rooms and a kitchen and a porch facing west, raised by twelve pairs of hands over one loud month. Rell had done the roof, because Rell did heights the way other men did breathing. Odo had supervised, which meant he'd held the level and disagreed with everyone. The twins had dug the foundation badly and then, when the well was sunk, had carved their initials into the capstone side by side, P.M., so that Marlow would hold up the house forever. Sera had grown herbs in the mortar gaps, which made the masons furious and the house smell wonderful.

Tonight the porch held two chairs, a table, and a lantern of black iron with a handle worn bright by handling.

Edmund lit it the way he always did: three breaths. One for the road behind. One for the road ahead. One for the ones who couldn't walk either anymore. Then he opened the little door and touched flame to the wick inside — except there was no ordinary wick, and no ordinary flame. In the heart of the lantern, small as a thumbnail and steady as sunrise, burned a spark of gold that had not gone out since the night Sera Ash took the Ember into herself and began, gently, over eleven days, to leave.

They had tried everything, that first terrible week. Odo had ridden to Correnvale for healers. Rell had threatened three priests. Edmund — this still shamed him, and always would, and he'd learned to carry the shame like the map used to burn, as proof he was holding something real — Edmund had raged at her, demanded she give it back, give it *up*, find another vessel, any vessel, and Sera had taken his face in her ink-stained hands and said, "Oh, my love. You still think love is a problem with solutions."

The spark in the lantern was all that remained. Except that wasn't quite true either, and everyone knew it, and nobody said it, because saying it would make it either more or less than it was, and it was already exactly enough.

Because the lantern did things.

Small things. The milk stayed sweet a day longer near it. Wounds dressed in its light closed cleaner. Once, in the worst week of winter, when fever came through Harrowdale and took three children to death's door, Edmund had carried the lantern cottage to cottage for two nights without sleeping, and the fevers broke, every one, and Dr. Hesse of Harrowdale — a rational woman who did not believe in anything she couldn't bleed — had examined the lantern, written four pages of notes, torn them up, and asked only, "Does it need oil?" and when Edmund said no, she'd nodded slowly and said, "Then I'll prescribe rest and lantern-light, and we'll both pretend that's medicine."

The spark never spoke. It never moved. But sometimes, on certain evenings, it leaned. Toward laughter, mostly. Toward the porch, when the company gathered. Toward Edmund, when he read aloud, which he did most nights, working his way through every book the free cities' presses had begun to pour out now that Veyra's censorship had died with her power — and last month, trembling slightly, through the first printing of a novel called *Beatrix and the Ember*, by an author named J. Winterbourne whom nobody had ever met and everybody had a theory about.

He'd read the whole thing aloud to the lantern over five nights. On the fifth night, when he reached the end — *she pressed the map into his hands and said, carry it, don't wield it, and he carried it, and it did not burn him, because some things are only heavy* — the spark had flared, briefly, gold going white, and settled again, and Edmund had sat in the dark with his own heartbeat for a long time, and then laughed, wetly, at nothing, at everything, at the sheer audacity of a flame that could unbind a king but still wanted to hear how the story ended.

He set the lantern on the table now and sat in his chair, and the evening came down over the valley the way it did — slow, honeyed, the mountains going violet, woodsmoke rising from Harrowdale's chimneys beyond the southern hills, where the dove woman would arrive tomorrow and put down roots in ground that didn't remind her.

"You're early," said Odo's voice from the lane. "Good. Someone has to be, now that the twins are in charge of time."

Odo came up the path with bread under one arm and his old guard's cloak over the other, grey now at the temples, walking with the faint hitch that the palace stairs had left in him. Behind him came Rell, carrying two bottles of the cherry wine from Correnvale that he pretended not to love, and behind Rell, at the pace of someone who had stopped hurrying anywhere ever again, came King Aldric of the Nine Provinces, in a plain brown coat, carrying a fishing rod.

This had become normal. Edmund had a theory that the human capacity for *becoming normal* was the actual magic of the world, and everything else was decoration.

"Your Majesty," Edmund said, standing.

"Aldric," said the king, for the hundredth time, sitting down in the second porch chair as if it had been built for him, which in a sense it had; Pip had put a plaque on it reading RESERVED FOR TYRANTS AND ANGLERS. "I have been informed by my council that leaving the capital during treaty negotiations sets a poor precedent. I have informed my council that the precedent they should worry about is kings who forget they are men. Then I left."

"He climbed out a window," Rell said, settling onto the steps. "Second floor. I watched him do it. I'm supposed to be his protection detail and I just watched."

"You were laughing," Aldric said.

"I was. It's a flaw."

Odo distributed bread. Rell opened wine. Down the lane came the sound of Pip arriving on his mule, late, loudly apologizing to a mule that did not care, and the porch filled up the way it filled up every Emberday eve, and the lantern on the table burned its small gold steadiness into the middle of everything.

They ate. Odo told a story so long that Rell fell asleep on the steps midway through, which happened every time, and which Odo now engineered deliberately, pacing his digressions to the exact rhythm of Rell's breathing. Aldric fished the pond they'd dug behind the house and caught, as always, nothing, and pronounced the evening a triumph anyway. Pip fell asleep against the doorpost with his ledger open, and Aldric read the entries upside down and grew quiet over them — the answers people gave, the things they carried, the things they left behind — and Edmund watched the king copy one into his own pocketbook, word for word, and decided not to ask.

Near midnight, the others gone home or asleep where they sat, Edmund sat alone on the porch with the lantern.

"One year," he said to it.

The spark did not lean, exactly. But it was brighter than it had been at dusk, and it was aimed, unmistakably, at him, the way a face aims at a voice.

"I finished the book. The real one, I mean. Not the novel. The journal." He had spent the year writing it — Beatrix's story told properly, from the courier who'd chosen the road over the bed, to the map that chose him, to the company of the broken road and everything they'd broken and mended. He'd written the parts he hadn't witnessed by asking everyone, cross-checking, arguing with Pip about details, corresponding with a witch in the eastern fens who had known Sera's mother. He'd written the prophecy and the ford and the eleven days. He had written the last page last week, and could not decide how it should end, and had come to understand that this indecision was the point.

"The publisher wants an ending where you come back," he told the spark. "Somehow. Ember finding its way home, vessel refilled, very tidy, sells better." He turned his cup in his hands. "And I'll be honest, there are nights I want that ending too. Nights I'd trade a lot for one more argument about herbalism. You'd hate it, being a miracle. You'd say miracles are just work nobody's taking credit for yet."

The flame held steady. Outside the lantern's glass, the valley breathed — crickets, wind in the barley they grew now where the ward-stones used to stand, the far-off bark of Odo's ridiculous dog.

"So I ended it differently," Edmund said. "I ended it with the gate."

He described it to her, because describing things to her was the prayer he knew. The gate that stood open now, unbarred, unwarded, with its three questions and its keeper's stool and its ledger of ten thousand honest answers. The decree Aldric had signed with his own restored hand: *No door in my realm shall be sealed against its own people.* And beneath the gate, in the old vault that had held nothing but the valley's peace and the lie that bought it — Aldric's whisper had been true, in the end; the door behind the world led nowhere but here, to grass and quiet, purchased at the price of a century of loneliness — beneath the gate, they were building a school. For hedge-witches and hedge-scholars both. For magic and its absence, taught side by side, because if the year had taught Edmund anything it was that the world did not have too much of either; it had too few doors between them.

Sera would have taught there. That was the grief under everything, permanent as bedrock: she should have been the first headmistress, terrifying children with botany, and instead her spark lived in a lantern and her herbs grew wild in the mortar and the school would open in autumn with her name over the door and her methods in every lesson plan, because Odo and Rell and Pip and even the king had spent the winter assembling her scattered notes into a curriculum, arguing about her marginalia like scholars, laughing like mourners, which are often the same activity.

"They asked me to give the address," Edmund said. "At the opening. I said no. I said I'm not a speaker." He smiled at the lantern. "Then Pip pointed out that I once talked my way past an entire mage-hunter patrol by boring them about grain silos, so apparently I'm a speaker when it matters and I don't get to choose when it matters. Fine. I'll speak. I'll tell them about a woman who read futures in tea leaves and never once read her own, because some knowledge you have to earn by not wanting it."

The spark flared — softly, softly, but he saw it — and he laughed.

"All right. All right. You never could resist a good lecture."

He sat with her while the moon crossed the yard, and just before he rose to go in, he said the thing he said every Emberday eve, the thing that was not a goodbye because he refused on principle, the way you refuse to close a door you built to stay open:

"Same time next year. Bring arguments."

The spark leaned, very slightly, toward the sound of his voice.

* * *

Morning came bright, and with it came the road.

By noon there were hundreds at the gate — villagers and pilgrims, the players' troupe performing the wolf debate for a crowd of sixty, Dr. Hesse up from Harrowdale pretending she'd come to check on Pip's lingering cough, messengers from Correnvale with letters Edmund stacked unread because today was for living, not paper. Aldric stood among the crowd unrecognized in his brown coat, handing out bread like any grandfather at any fair, and only Rell noticed the three former rebels who noticed the king and said nothing, because some protections are silence.

At midday, Pip mounted the wall beside the gate — the wall was for climbing now, never for keeping out — and rang the bell that had replaced the alarm-horn, and the crowd hushed, and Edmund stood beneath him feeling like a man about to jump into water of unknown depth, which was, he supposed, the entire story of his life since the storm that brought Beatrix Ash bleeding through his wards.

"Two years ago I guarded a gate so nothing would change," Pip called, because Edmund had delegated the speaking-of-Pip's-own-address to himself, an irony he was savoring. "Now I keep a gate so everything can. Same door. Different job. The difference isn't the door." He looked down at Edmund. "It's who walks through willing. Happy Emberday! Questions are open! Mind the doves!"

The crowd laughed and surged through, answering the three questions in a ragged chorus, and the ledger-keepers — volunteers now, a dozen of them, because the book had outgrown Pip's lap — began the year's great recording of humanity's arrivals and abandonments.

Edmund stood at the threshold itself, on the line where magic slid off the world, and felt it as he always did: that faint seam in the air, cool as a riverbank. People passed through him and around him. The dove woman, arriving early, tipped her hat. A boy of six stopped dead in front of him, stared up with enormous eyes, and asked, "Are you the one from the book?"

"I'm in the book," Edmund said carefully. "So are a lot of braver people."

"My ma says you carried fire and it didn't burn you."

"It didn't," Edmund agreed. "But I'll tell you the secret, if you want it. It wasn't bravery. I just refused to put it down, and eventually refusing became a kind of holding, and holding became a kind of love, and none of those steps felt brave at the time. Mostly they felt like Tuesday."

The boy considered this with the gravity of a judge. "My da says Tuesdays are the hardest days."

"Your da," said Edmund, "is a philosopher. Go on through. Answer honest."

The boy ran to catch his mother, and Edmund watched him go, across the seam, into the valley, into the ordinary extraordinary world.

And that was when he saw her.

Not *her*. He was not a man given to cruel self-deceptions, and he had buried Sera Ash with full honors and meant to keep her buried, whatever the lantern leaned toward, whatever the publishers wanted. But at the back of the crowd, at the top of the ridge where the road came down from the north — from the capital, from the coast, from everywhere — a rider had paused. Grey cloak. Dust of hard miles. She sat her horse the way couriers sat horses, all economy, no romance, and even at that distance Edmund's heart recognized the silhouette before his mind caught up: a traveler who has stopped traveling only because she has arrived.

The rider lifted a hand. Not a wave. The gesture of someone showing an empty palm — *I carry nothing* — and then, deliberately, she reached into her coat and drew out a folded paper and held it high so the sun caught it, and Edmund's legs were moving before he had decided anything, pushing through the thinning crowd, because he knew that gesture too. He had invented it, in a novel, for a dying courier on a storm-lit night.

The rider came down the road at a walk, unhurried, and reined in at the threshold, on the far side of the seam, and pushed back her hood, and she was young — too young, brown-haired, plain-faced, wind-burned, nobody — and yet when she spoke, Edmund felt the ground tilt, because he knew the voice, he had heard it once in a storm and once in his dreams and once more, a year ago tonight, leaning out of a lantern:

"Edmund Yarrow?" the courier said, and held out the folded paper. "I've a delivery. It's marked for the Keeper of the Gate. There's no return address." Her eyes were steady and amused and entirely unfamiliar. "But the seal's odd. It looks like a spark."

Below the seal — a small gold flame, pressed in wax that seemed faintly warm — the paper bore three lines in a hand Edmund knew better than his own, a hand he had transcribed and studied and dreamed, ink-brown and slantwise and alive:

*Three questions, keeper. You know them.*

*I answered true.*

*Open the door.*

Behind him, the crowd had gone silent. Pip's hand found his shoulder. Somewhere in the valley, impossibly, a mile away, the lantern on his porch table flared white.

The courier waited at the seam, palm out, paper shining, and Edmund Yarrow — gatekeeper, carrier, ordinary man, immune to magic and nothing else — reached across the boundary of worlds to take what she was delivering.
