## 1. The Quiet Valley

Tomas had lived in Bellhollow for thirty-one years, and in all that time the sky had never once argued with him.

That was how he thought of it—how most of them thought of it, though few said so aloud. The valley sat cupped in the hands of two mountain ranges like a bowl of warm milk, and the weather came down gentle and even as bread. Rain arrived on schedule and left without tantrum. Wind was a rumor, a stirring of curtains, never a voice. Once, when he was nine, a cloud had piled itself into something dark and grumbling over the western ridge, and old Mistress Halloway had rapped her cane against her doorstep three times, and by evening it had simply... unraveled. He had not wondered then why the cloud unraveled, or who did the unraveling. In Bellhollow you learned early not to wonder at the seams of things.

He wondered at other things instead. He wondered at paper, mostly, which was a fine thing to wonder at when your work was the archive.

The archive of Bellhollow occupied the ground floor of the old tithe-house, a long room of honey-colored stone where the light fell through leaded windows in soft, dusty columns. Tomas had spent eleven years among its shelves—deeds and ledgers, wedding records and death rolls, the slow sediment of a town that prized its quiet enough to write everything down and then read none of it twice. He knew the collection the way a shepherd knows his hills. He could find any birth register blind. It was, he sometimes thought, the closest thing to magic he would ever need: the small conjuring of making lost things found.

Marguerite Villalobos did not visit the archive. That was the first thing worth knowing about her, and for a long time it was nearly all he knew.

She kept the garden behind the meetinghouse—a strip of green that shouldn't have thrived the way it did, tucked between two walls where the sun reached it only in slants. Yet her roses bloomed into November some years, and her lettuces refused to bolt in August, and the whole plot had a lushness that made passersby slow their steps without knowing why. Tomas slowed too, but he told himself it was for the roses. It took him most of a spring to admit that he came down Alder Street at half past four because that was when she knelt in the dirt with her sleeves rolled, and because watching her work was like watching someone pray in a language he didn't speak.

She was reserved the way deep water is still. She traded pleasantries with everyone and confidences with no one. When the market women gossiped about the weather—and in Bellhollow, weather gossip was the only drama anyone ever needed—Marguerite smiled and turned her watering can to another row. Her hands were earth-browned and steady. Her eyes were the color of the sky before rain, though Tomas would not have known to say that yet, because in Bellhollow the sky before rain was only gray, and gray was nothing to write home about.

The thunder began in his third spring of knowing her.

It came at dusk on a market day—a low, distant rumble from beyond the western ridge, so faint it might have been a cart on gravel. Half the square didn't even notice. But Marguerite, standing at her stall with a bundle of thyme in her arms, went white to the lips. Not startled—Tomas had seen startled, and this was something older and colder than that. She stood absolutely still, the way a rabbit stands, and her eyes fixed on the west as if she could see through the mountains to whatever was growling there. Then the sound faded, and she blinked, and laughed at some joke of the cooper's wife, and was ordinary again.

But Tomas had seen. And being who he was—an archivist, a man who believed that anything noticed should be written down—he carried the flinch home with him like an uncatalogued book, turning it over in his mind for weeks.

The second time, there was no mistaking it. A storm cell had wandered near the valley's rim, close enough that thunder rolled down the slopes in long grey waves, and Tomas happened to be walking past the garden when it started. Marguerite dropped her trowel. Actually dropped it, and pressed both hands flat against the garden wall, fingers spread, and breathed in a way that looked like counting. He crossed the street before he knew he'd decided to.

"There's no storm in Bellhollow," he said. It wasn't clever. It was just true, and somehow the true thing seemed the kindest.

She looked at him—really looked, for the first time in three years of nodding acquaintance—and something in her face shifted like a curtain moving in a closed house. "No," she agreed quietly. "There never is."

They began, after that, to talk. Nothing remarkable at first—beans and compost and the price of lamp oil—but talking with Marguerite was like opening a door into a room you'd walked past all your life. She was funny, dry-voiced and precise. She remembered everything he told her, which undid him more than beauty could have. By midsummer he was bringing her books from the archive—histories of far places, travel accounts, anything with horizons in it—and she read them the way starving people eat, quickly and carefully, licking every finger.

"Why do you never leave?" he asked her once, in September, when the light had gone gold and low across her rows.

"I did leave once," she said, weeding. "I came back." And then, when he waited: "Some people aren't allowed anywhere else, Tomas. Best to love where you're permitted."

He wanted to ask what she meant. He was learning, slowly, not to pull up a person's roots to see how they grew.

* * *

The estate of Aldous Wren came to the archive on a Tuesday in October, eleven crates of books hauled down from the dead man's house on the hill by his nephew, who wanted them gone before frost. Wren had been one of the valley's quiet hoarders—the kind of man who bought books and never lent them, whose library smelled of camphor and secrets. The nephew hinted there might be money in some of them. Tomas suspected there was mostly mold. He was right about the mold and wrong about the money, but he was wrong in a way that would crack the valley open like river ice.

He catalogued alone, evenings, after hours, because the work went faster without interruption and because the archive at night was his favorite place in the world—lamplight pooling on the reading table, the shelves breathing their old-paper breath, the whole accumulated memory of Bellhollow drowsing around him like a dog by a fire.

The seventh crate held sermons, almanacs, a water-damaged herbal. At the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, was a slim volume with no title on its spine—only a hand-tooled pattern of interlocking circles. The paper inside was a century old if it was a day, and the script was cramped, private, the hand of someone writing fast and believing every word.

It opened, at first glance, like a diary of weather. *Frost on the 9th. Rains held off till the feast day.* Ordinary. But halfway through, the entries changed character. The handwriting tightened. And on a page marked with a pressed sprig of rowan—brown now, brittle, its berries long gone to dust—Tomas read:

*In the year the covenant grows thin, an archivist will find this book, and the finding will be the beginning. His name is Tomas. He will read these words in the evening of the last warm night, and the winter that follows will not be a natural winter, and the peace of Bellhollow will stand or fall by what he does with what he knows.*

He set the book down very carefully, as if it were sleeping.

The lamp hissed. Somewhere out in the dark, a late cricket was singing, stubborn and off-season, and Tomas realized he could hear it because the night through the window was unnaturally, perfectly still—even the archive's usual settling creaks had stopped, as if the building itself were holding its breath.

His name. His own name, in a stranger's hundred-year-old hand, sitting in a crate that had arrived that morning. He checked the date against the almanac without meaning to, an archivist's reflex, and the almanac did not comfort him: tonight was forecast to be the last mild evening before the cold came down from the ridges. Tonight.

He read the entry again. And beneath it, in the same cramped script but darker ink, pressed hard enough to score the page, the diarist had added a single line:

*What they buried under the peace is not dead. Ask what became of those who carried the storm.*

Tomas sat for a long time in the yellow circle of lamplight, listening to the silence where wind should be. He thought of Marguerite's white face at the sound of thunder. He thought of Mistress Halloway's cane rapping the doorstep, of clouds that unraveled, of a sky that had never once argued with him—as if argument itself had been banished from the valley along with something else, something buried, something that was not dead.

Outside the window, above the black line of the western ridge, the first stars were coming out. And among them, faint but unmistakable, a single cloud had begun to pile itself up against the windless dark—dark, grumbling, and moving, very slowly, toward Bellhollow.
