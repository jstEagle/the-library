## 2. What the Covenant Buried

Tomas did not sleep.

The prophecy sat on his kitchen table where he had left it, its leather cover drinking the lamplight, and every time he closed his eyes he saw the ink again: *The archivist Tomas Reyes, on the eve of the harvest moon, shall open the buried book.* His own name. Tonight's date, written a hundred years before his grandmother crossed the mountains into Bellhollow. He had checked the almanac twice, then a third time, as if the moon itself might be persuaded to change its schedule.

By the time the sky grayed toward morning, he had read the passage until the words wore grooves in him.

*And the storm that was buried shall rise from the garden she has planted, and the covenant of silence shall crack like river ice, and the valley will remember what it paid for its peace.*

*The garden she has planted.*

He was walking before he fully decided to walk, boots crunching frost on the lane, past shuttered bakeries and the well where old women would gather at noon to gossip in voices calibrated to carry exactly as far as they intended. Bellhollow woke slowly and gently, the way it did everything. The way it had done everything for a hundred years. He had always found that rhythm comforting. This morning it felt like the held breath of someone trying not to be noticed.

Marguerite was already in her garden.

Of course she was. She was always there at first light, wrapped in her patched wool coat, moving down the rows with her hands in the dirt as though reading something written there. He had watched her a hundred mornings from the top of the archive steps and told himself the warmth in his chest was simple admiration—a man falling for a gardener's patience, a gardener's quiet. He had told himself a great many things about Marguerite Villalobos.

She looked up when his shadow fell across the bean rows, and something in his face made her go still. Not startled. *Braced.* He understood, with a lurch, that she had been braced since the day they met. That the stillness was not calm at all. It was a woman holding a door shut.

"You found something," she said.

"How did you know?"

"Because you walk like a man carrying a heavy tray." She rose, wiping her hands on her apron, soil clinging to the lines of her palms. "You've been walking like that since yesterday evening."

"The Harlowe estate donation," Tomas said. "I was cataloguing. There was a book at the bottom of a trunk, wrapped in oilcloth, unlisted." He heard his own voice go flat, archival, the tone he used for damaged materials. "It's a prophecy, Marguerite. Written over a century ago. It names me. It names last night. And it says—" His throat closed around the words. He made himself say them. "It says the storm that was buried shall rise from the garden she has planted."

The morning went very quiet. A crow called somewhere beyond the orchard wall and even it seemed to wait.

Marguerite did not look surprised. That was the worst part. She looked like a woman who had been expecting a knock at the door for thirty years.

"Sit down, Tomas," she said gently, and nodded at the stone bench beneath the pear tree—their bench, he realized, the bench where she had first kissed him under the June blossoms, laughing at the petals in his hair. "There are things you should know before you decide what you've found."

"I don't want to sit. I want—"

"I know what you want." Her voice didn't harden; it simply settled, like snow finding its level. "You want me to tell you it's a coincidence. An old monk's fever dream. I've wanted that for you every day since we met." She sat down herself, unfolding with the care of someone much older than her body suggested. "Sit with me. If you're going to carry this, you should carry it sitting down."

He sat. Not because he obeyed, but because his legs had quietly stopped negotiating.

* * *

"My name isn't only Marguerite," she began. "In the mountains, past the passes, I was called Villalobos-of-the-Villalobos-Line, which is a way of saying: a storm-caller born to storm-callers, eleven generations back." She said it plainly, the way one recites genealogy. "You've felt thunder make me flinch. You were kind enough never to ask."

"Bellhollow has no magic," Tomas said automatically. The catechism, learned in childhood at his mother's knee: *Bellhollow has no magic, and no magic wants us, and that is our blessing.* "The covenant—"

"The covenant doesn't remove magic." She turned to face him fully. "It forbids it. There's a difference, and the difference is a graveyard."

The words landed in his chest like stones dropped down a well.

"When I was nine years old," Marguerite said, "my mother could call lightning out of a clear sky. My aunt could sing rain into the vineyards during drought years. We kept the high valleys green. We were—" the first fracture showed in her voice, quickly mended, "—we were useful neighbors, once. Then the war came. You'll have learned it as the Border Wars, three summers of raids, both sides bleeding. What you won't have learned is how it ended."

"The covenant," he whispered.

"On a night much like tonight, a hundred years ago next spring, the valley elders met with our elders and offered peace. All the magic in Bellhollow, surrendered—all of it, theirs and ours alike, so no one would ever again have power over anyone. That's what the children learn." Her hands had knotted themselves in her apron. "What actually happened is that the valley elders came to our villages at midnight with soldiers and chains blessed by their priests, and they bound every storm-caller they could find and drove them through the high passes in winter. Eleven generations of my family, Tomas. Two hundred souls. The ones who wouldn't run were—" She stopped. Started again. "There is a field north of the pass. The valley records call it the Old Battlefield, as if armies met there. Armies never met anywhere. It was a massacre, and your valley bought its silence with my family's blood, and then wrote the receipt as a holy covenant."

Tomas discovered he had stopped breathing. He made himself inhale, and the cold air hurt.

"And you," he managed. "You stayed."

"I ran south instead of east, alone, when I was nine. I walked into Bellhollow in spring with a story about being an orphan from the coast, and no one looked too closely, because looking closely isn't something Bellhollow does." A terrible tenderness entered her face. "I chose the one place in the world where my magic would starve. Where I'd never feel it rise. Where I could be nobody." She laughed, very softly, without any humor in it. "And then a clumsy archivist knocked over my seedling cart at the autumn market and apologized for forty minutes straight, and I thought: oh no. Oh no, not this. Not here."

"Marguerite—"

"Let me finish, because there's one more thing, and then you can decide what kind of man you're going to be." She took his hand. Her fingers were cold as October. "The book you found. Read me the prophecy. All of it. Word for word."

He did, from memory, because the words were written somewhere behind his eyes now. When he reached the end—*the covenant of silence shall crack like river ice*—Marguerite closed her eyes.

"It's ours," she breathed. "It's one of ours. That cadence—that's a calling-chant, the rhythm we use to bind prophecy into paper. Someone in my line survived long enough to write it, and hid it in the valley, waiting for an archivist who would catalog honestly and love badly and dig to the bottom of every trunk." Her grip tightened. "Tomas, that book doesn't predict your evening. It predicts mine. It's saying the storm that was buried—"

"Rises from the garden she has planted," he finished. His voice sounded far away. "You. It means you."

"I spent thirty years burying it deeper every day." For the first time, fear moved openly across her features. "But magic starved isn't magic dead. And lately—" 

She never finished. Because the light changed.

Not dimmed—*changed*, silvered, gone strange. Tomas looked up and saw clouds boiling over the southern peaks where no clouds should be, black-bellied and wrong, moving against the wind. The air smelled of iron and ozone and something older, something that made the hair on his arms stand and his teeth ache. The crow beyond the orchard wall began to scream.

"Tomas," Marguerite said, and her voice had a double ring to it now, as if two women spoke in unison. "Get up."

"Why? What's—"

The first snowflake landed on the bean rows between them. Then another. Fat, deliberate flakes, falling thick and fast out of a cloudless summer sky, in the week of the harvest, when Bellhollow had not seen snow for a hundred and one years.

"It's early," Tomas said stupidly. "Winter's early."

"Winter isn't early." Marguerite stood, and the storm-caller rose inside her like a second shadow, and he could see her fighting it, see her hands trembling against her sides. "Winter is coming *for* something. The covenant is cracking, just like the book said. And everyone in this valley is about to remember exactly what my family paid for their peace—because the debt," she whispered, watching the unnatural snow bury her garden, "is being called due. And I don't know yet whether I'm the debtor or the collector."

Down in the town square, the council bell began to ring—frantic, off-rhythm, nothing like the gentle noon chime.

They were already coming for her.
