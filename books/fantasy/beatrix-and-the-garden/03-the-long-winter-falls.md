## 3. The Long Winter Falls

The summons came at dawn, folded under the door of Tomas's archive office like a knife slipped between floorboards.

*By order of the Council of Bellhollow: present yourself and all documents pertaining to the Villalobos matter before the assembly, this day, midwinter bell.*

He read it three times. Outside his window, the snow was still falling — six days now without pause, though the almanacs swore Bellhollow had never seen snow before the feast of Thawing. The valley lay white and silent as a held breath.

They knew. Someone had read his research notes — he'd left them in the council reading room, foolishly, honestly, believing truth was a thing you could set down gently and let people approach at their own pace. Instead they had come for it like scavengers, and what they'd found in his careful margins had frightened them enough to summon him by name.

By *both* their names. The second line of the summons read: *Marguerite Villalobos will appear also.*

Tomas dressed in the dark, hands shaking, and went to find her.

* * *

She was already awake when he reached her cottage above the terraced gardens, standing in her doorway with a shawl around her shoulders, watching the snow come down.

"They sent for me too," she said, before he could speak. "A boy brought it an hour ago. He wouldn't meet my eyes."

"Marguerite—"

"Don't." She turned to face him, and in the grey light he saw how tired she was, how long she had been carrying this alone before he ever found the book. "Whatever you're going to say — that you'll fix it, that you'll talk to them, that it will be all right — don't. I've stood before that council once already. I was nine years old."

The words landed like stones. "You never told me that."

"There wasn't time for everything." Her mouth curved, not quite a smile. "There's never time for everything, Tomas. That's the trick of a quiet life. You get to choose which silences you keep."

"And now?"

"Now the silence is over." She looked past him, down the hill, toward the council hall where the midwinter bell would soon ring. "I can feel it ending. I've felt it for six days, since the first snow. Something in me is—" She pressed her hand flat against her sternum, and for one terrible instant Tomas thought he heard it: a low hum beneath her voice, like wire singing before it snaps. "It remembers," she said quietly. "Even when I don't want it to."

He reached for her hand. She let him take it. Her fingers were cold as the ground.

"Then we go together," he said. "And we tell them everything. Not just what frightens them. All of it. The massacre too. What the covenant cost."

Her eyes searched his face. "You understand what that means? Once they hear the word *massacre*, there's no garden big enough to hide in. No quiet life. For either of us."

"I know."

"You're an archivist, Tomas. You love things best on shelves, where they stay put."

"I love *you*," he said, "and you were never going to fit on a shelf."

For a moment — one whole, suspended moment — she laughed, low and startled, and the hum beneath her voice harmonized with it, and the snow above the cottage seemed to swirl, just slightly, against the wind.

Then the midwinter bell rang out across the valley, and her face closed like a gate.

* * *

The council hall had never been so full.

Tomas stood at the front beside Marguerite, facing rows of neighbors he had known his whole life — the miller who bought him honey cakes as a boy; old Serafina from the dye-works; the entire elected bench of the council, seven faces arranged behind the long table like a jury carved from oak. First among them sat Magistrate Corven Hale, a broad, deliberate man whose family had held the eastern orchards for four generations. It was Hale who had read the research notes. It was Hale, everyone understood, who had called this assembly.

"Marguerite Villalobos." Hale's voice filled the hall without effort. "You stand accused of concealing your nature upon entering Bellhollow, in violation of the Covenant of Quiet, signed in the year of the Ashes. The penalty is exile. Before we proceed — does anyone speak for her?"

"I do," said Tomas.

Hale's gaze settled on him with something almost like pity. "Archivist Marchetti. Your research prompted this inquiry. You may speak, but know that the council has reviewed your notes. We are aware of the prophecy book. We are aware of its... contents."

"Then you know more than the contents of my notes," Tomas said, and his voice surprised him by holding steady. "Because my notes only begin the story. Last night I finished translating the rest of the estate ledger — the full record, not the fragments. And I'm asking every person in this hall to hear it before you vote to send this woman into the winter."

Murmuring moved through the benches like wind through wheat. Marguerite stood very still beside him.

"The covenant didn't end a war," Tomas said. "It ended a *people*. Read the ledger. In the year of the Ashes, the valley's storm-callers — forty-one souls, most of them born here, families who had farmed these hills longer than any council seat — were summoned to the northern field to sign the peace. They came unarmed. They came trusting. And while the covenant was being woven to strip magic from the border, the weaving was turned inward, on them, all at once." He let the silence sit. "Forty-one people. There is no record of a trial. There is no record of a battle. There is a record of a field, and what was buried in it, and a payment made to the masons who dug the graves."

The murmuring stopped. In its place: nothing. A hall full of people not breathing.

"That's a lie," someone said from the back — Halvard the smith, his voice cracking on the word. "That's — the covenant saved us. My grandmother told me. The war was burning the valley down—"

"The war ended," Tomas said. "And then, after it ended, when no one was fighting anymore, forty-one of our neighbors were killed and buried in an unmarked field north of the walls. Because they were dangerous. Because they might have been angry. Because forgetting was easier than justice." He turned to the council bench. "You've known. Some of you have always known. That's why my notes frightened you — not because they named Marguerite, but because they came close to naming *that*."

Magistrate Hale rose slowly. When he spoke, his voice was quieter than before, and somehow worse for it.

"Archivist. Do you know why the covenant holds?" He spread his hands. "Not because of wards or borders. Because of agreement. Every soul in this valley agreed, generation after generation, to live without magic, and in exchange we have had ninety years without fire falling from the sky. Ninety years of children who sleep through storms. You would trade that — for what? An old grief? Grief keeps, archivist. Peace does not. If the truth comes out — if the descendants of those forty-one learn what was done — there will be anger. And anger, in a storm-caller, is weather." He looked at Marguerite then, and his eyes were not cruel but afraid, which was more terrible. "We are sorry, truly. But the valley comes first. The council votes exile. Effective immediately, before the gates freeze shut."

Hands rose along the bench. Six of seven.

"No," said the seventh.

Every head turned. Old Serafina from the dye-works stood, small and straight-backed, her dyer's stains still purple on her knuckles. "My grandfather was a storm-caller," she said simply. "Nobody ever says the word in this valley, but somebody should start. I say we remember."

And the hall broke open.

* * *

What happened next, Tomas would spend the rest of his life trying to describe, and never quite manage.

Half the hall surged to its feet shouting — some crying *exile*, some crying *shame*, Halvard the smith bellowing about his grandmother's burned village while others shouted back that his grandmother hadn't been buried in a mason's field. The benches emptied into a roiling crowd. Council members argued across each other. Somewhere a chair went over. The midwinter cold poured in through doors flung wide and nobody cared.

Through it all, Marguerite did not move.

Tomas felt it before he understood it — a pressure in his ears, a taste like copper rain. The humming he'd heard in her cottage was audible now, rising out of her chest, and the air around her had begun to shimmer, faintly, the way heat rises off summer stone.

"Marguerite," he said. "Look at me. Stay with me."

"They're going to drag me to the gates," she said. Her voice was calm. That was the frightening part. Beneath the calm, something vast was turning over, waking joint by joint like a creature sleeping ninety years. "Two guards are already coming down the aisle. I can hear their boots. And Tomas — I can hear *everything else* too. Every drop of water in the snow outside. Every charge building in the clouds. It's been knocking on the inside of my ribs for six days, and I am so tired of not answering."

"Then answer it *gently*," he said. "Please. Whatever you do — they need to see it. All of it. But gently."

The guards reached her. Hands closed on her arms.

And Marguerite Villalobos closed her eyes, lifted her face toward the rafters, and answered.

The sound began low — a note below hearing, felt in the teeth, in the floorboards, in the fillings of the heart. Frost bloomed up the pillars in ferns of white. Every candle in the hall guttered sideways though no door had opened. And through the high clerestory windows, the snow stopped falling *down* and began to fall *inward*, drawn toward her like iron filings to a lodestone, until she stood in a slow-turning column of white, and the column was not empty —

— it was *full*. Faces in the snow. Forty-one faces, flickering, brief, unmistakable: a woman laughing, a boy clutching a wooden bird, an old man with his hat in his hands. The crowd's shouting died to whimpers. Halvard the smith sat down hard on a bench.

"What is it showing us?" whispered Serafina.

"It isn't showing anything," Tomas said, his throat tight. "It's *remembering*. She's letting the storm remember out loud."

The faces wheeled overhead, and the temperature dropped, and somewhere beyond the walls, beyond the frozen gates, thunder rolled across a valley that had not heard thunder in ninety years.

Marguerite opened her eyes. They were pale silver, lit from within, and fixed on Magistrate Hale.

"You wanted the valley kept safe," she said, and her voice came doubled, hers and something older underneath. "So look at what keeping it safe cost. Look at what you're about to make me do at the gates. Choose, Bellhollow. Choose *now* — because the storm and I are done waiting."

Outside, the sky went black at noon.
