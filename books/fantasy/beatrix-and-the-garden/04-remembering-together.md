## 4. Remembering Together

The gates of Bellhollow had never looked like this. Tomas had walked through them a thousand times—iron scrolled with wheat sheaves, green with summer ivy—and they had always meant home. Now they stood open onto a frozen road, and the council stood before them in a half-circle, and Marguerite stood alone on the ice-slicked stones with her hands at her sides, and the whole valley watched from the walls and the square behind him, breath steaming, silent as the snow that should not have fallen.

"Marguerite Villalobos," said Councilor Hedda Voss, her voice carrying the flat authority of someone reading from a ledger. "By the terms of the covenant, you are exiled from Bellhollow. You will pass the gates before moonrise, and you will not return."

Tomas pushed through the crowd. His lungs burned. The book was under his arm—the prophecy, the old chronicle bound in storm-grey leather, its pages soft as moth wings. He had carried it from the archive at a dead run, and now he held it up over his head like a lantern.

"Then exile all of us," he said.

Hedda turned. Around the square, faces swung toward him—neighbors, friends, the baker whose bread he bought every morning, the children who stole apples from Marguerite's garden. He saw fear there, and something else beneath it, older and sadder.

"The covenant keeps us safe," Hedda said.

"The covenant keeps us *ignorant*." His voice cracked on the word. He opened the book. "I found this in the Aldermere estate donation. A chronicle, written by a storm-caller named Ede Villalobos—Marguerite's great-great-grandmother. It records what happened the night the covenant was made. Not the story we tell our children. The true one."

He read. His voice shook, but he did not stop. He read of the war between the valley and the storm-callers who had once been its protectors. He read of the peace offered and the price demanded: every caller would surrender their magic or leave. And then he read the part no one knew—the part where the council of a century ago, fearing the callers would refuse, had not waited for their answer. He read the names. Forty-one names. Callers who had gathered at these very gates to accept exile peacefully, who were struck down before they could cross it, so that the covenant's magic would have its sacrifice and the valley would have its silence.

The wind moved through the square like a held breath released.

"You want to send her through those gates," Tomas said quietly. "She is walking to the place where her family died."

* * *

Marguerite had not moved. Snow gathered on her shoulders, on her dark hair, and Tomas saw that she was trembling—not with cold. He knew that trembling. He had seen it once, the night thunder rolled over Bellhollow out of season and she had gripped his hand hard enough to bruise, whispering, *it wants me, it always wants me.*

Now the sky above the valley churned grey-green, and the air tasted of copper and rain that hadn't fallen yet.

"Marguerite," Hedda said, and for the first time her voice wavered. "Stand down."

"I'm not doing anything," Marguerite said. Her voice was low, almost gentle. "That's the terrible thing. I haven't done anything in eleven years. And it's still here. It's always been here, waiting." She looked out at the crowd—at the people she had grown vegetables for, healed with comfrey and patience, loved without ever letting them know her fully. "You're right to be afraid of me. But you're afraid of the wrong thing."

She raised one hand—not a threat, an offering—and closed her eyes.

What came was not the storm Tomas had braced for. No lightning split the sky. Instead the clouds above the gates began, slowly, to turn like a wheel, and light gathered in them, silver and soft, and then it rained—but not water. It rained memory.

It fell across the whole square at once, and everyone it touched saw the same thing: the night a hundred years past, rendered in light. The forty-one callers standing at the gates with their bundles and their children. The council's archers on the walls. The moment the order was given. The light going out of forty-one people in the falling snow, and the covenant drinking deep, and the valley waking the next morning to a peace it did not question because questioning hurt too much.

Tomas heard weeping around him—the baker, on his knees. Hedda Voss had both hands pressed over her mouth. An old woman near the wall was saying a name, over and over, a name Tomas recognized from the chronicle: *Ede, Ede, my sister Ede.* Descendants of the dead, mourning ancestors they had never known they had.

The rain of light fell until it had shown everything, and then it stopped, and the clouds broke apart, and ordinary stars came out over Bellhollow for the first time since winter began.

Marguerite lowered her hand. She looked exhausted, translucent, but standing. She looked at the council.

"I don't want your fear," she said. "And I don't want your forgiveness. Forgiveness is for something I did. I want you to decide—who you are. That's all. That was always all."

* * *

They deliberated until dawn, in the open, in the square, because Hedda Voss refused to close the doors—"We've made enough decisions in hidden rooms," she said, and her voice was raw. Tomas sat with Marguerite on the steps of the archive, her head against his shoulder, while the valley argued itself awake around them.

He heard everything. The fear: *magic brought the war, magic will bring it back.* The grief: *they were ours, they were neighbors, we ate at their tables.* And slowly, rising like water finding its level, something else: *the covenant was written in blood we didn't know about, and a law built on a lie protects nothing.*

Near sunrise, Hedda came down the archive steps herself, old and straight-backed, and knelt in the snow before Marguerite—an act that drew a gasp from the whole square.

"The covenant will be amended," she said. "Not broken. Amended. The stripping of magic ends. The exile is revoked—for you, and for any caller who wishes to come home. And the forty-one names go on the memorial stone, where they should have been for a hundred years." She paused. "We cannot undo what was done. But we can stop pretending we didn't do it."

Marguerite looked at her for a long moment. Then she took the old woman's hands and drew her up.

"Then help me plant something," Marguerite said.

* * *

They planted the garden in spring, on the field outside the gates where the forty-one had fallen—a field the valley had left fallow for a century without ever quite knowing why. Tomas dug the first row himself, turning soil that was black and rich, and Marguerite walked behind him setting seed with hands that trembled only a little.

Storm-callers came home through the spring. First one, an old man who wept at the gate. Then families. They brought seeds of their own—flowers Tomas had no names for, vegetables that grew in colors the valley had forgotten. The amended covenant was carved into the gateposts, beside the wheat sheaves: *We remember together.*

On the evening the last row was planted, clouds gathered over the garden—not angry, just full, the way a heart gets full. Rain came down soft and warm, and where it struck the fresh-turned earth, small lights rose up like fireflies, like breath, like forty-one voices saying *thank you* and *at last* and *home.*

Tomas stood under it with Marguerite, soaked through and laughing, her hand in his.

"It's still in you," he said. "The storm."

"It was never the enemy," she said, watching the lights rise. "It was only ever waiting to be asked what it remembered."

Above them, far off beyond the mountains, thunder rolled—gentle, almost fond. And in the new-planted earth, a single seedling broke the surface overnight, impossibly fast, unfurling leaves that shimmered faintly silver whenever lightning flickered on the horizon, as if the garden itself had begun to listen to the sky.
