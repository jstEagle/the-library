## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The miller was dying the way old men die in the Hollow: slowly, loudly, and with a great deal of unfinished business.

Felix Okonkwo sat on a stool beside the bed and waited for the right moment. He had learned long ago that there was always a right moment — a breath, a loosening, a small surrender in the face — when grief came loose from its moorings and could be lifted out whole, like a stone from soft clay. Take it too early and you got fragments, splinters of feeling that cut on the way down. Take it too late and it had already gone wherever dying men's feelings go.

He watched Tam Halloway's chest rise and fall. The room smelled of flour dust and lamp oil and the sweetish rot that comes into a house when someone has been in bed too long. Outside, the mill wheel turned without grain to grind, because Tam's sons were dead and his daughter had left twenty years ago and nobody in Cindermill had needed much flour lately. The wheel turned anyway. It was the only thing in the valley that refused to forget its work.

"You're the thief," Tam said. His eyes were open now, fixed on the ceiling beam where forty years of smoke had written its black calligraphy. "They said you'd come."

"I'm a taker," Felix said. "Thief implies you didn't invite me."

Tam made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't gotten so expensive. "My girl," he said. "Maren. She went over the ridge in '61. Nobody goes over the ridge. But she did." His hand worked at the blanket. "I've carried her every day since. Every morning I wake up and she's the first thing, before my own name even. You know what that's like?"

Felix knew what it was like to wake up with nothing first. He said nothing.

"They say you can take it out. All of it. The weight." Tam turned his head, and his eyes were wet and fierce and afraid all at once. "I don't want it gone. Understand? I want it *lighter*. Can you do that? Can you take the edge off and leave me the rest?"

That was the difference between Felix and the guild men who came through twice a year with their ledgers and their brass instruments and their prices in bread and salt. The guild took everything or nothing. Felix could take a slice.

"I can take the sharp part," Felix said. "The part that wakes you at night. The rest stays. It'll feel like... a scar instead of a wound."

"And you keep it." Not a question. Everyone in the Hollow knew what takers did with what they took. A memory kept well was currency. A memory spent carelessly was just theft with extra steps.

"I keep it until someone buys it," Felix said. "Or until I give it back. That's happened twice."

"Give it back," Tam repeated, tasting the words. "Why would anyone give back a grief?"

"Because sometimes the person who paid for it turns out not to want it after all." Felix stood, flexing his fingers. His hands were good hands for the work — steady, warm, unremarkable. In the Hollow, remarkable was dangerous. "Last chance to change your mind, Tam."

"The bread first," Tam said. "I'm not a fool. My nephew's boy brings it tomorrow if we strike the bargain tonight. Rye. Fresh. And a jar of the honey from the hives on the south slope, the ones that survived the blight."

"Rye and honey," Felix agreed. "And I'll sit with you after, so you're not alone with the quiet. First hour's free."

* * *

Taking a memory was not like the stories said. There was no pulling, no tearing. Done right, it felt like lifting a hot loaf from an oven — careful, both hands, respect for the heat.

Felix knelt by the bed and pressed two fingers to Tam's temple, and Tam closed his eyes, and Felix went in.

Inside, Tam Halloway was a mill. Of course he was. Forty years of grinding had worn grooves into him so deep that his mind had taken the shape of his work: great turning rooms, floors dusted white, sacks stacked to the rafters. And in the center, in the room where the millstones lived, Maren.

She was seventeen in there forever, laughing at something her father had said, flour on her nose. Around her the grief was packed so tight it had become architecture — walls of it, beams of it, the whole structure of the man load-bearing on top of one dead girl.

Felix found the sharp part. It wasn't hard to identify; it was the part that moved. Grief that heals settles and stills. Grief that kills keeps churning, and this churned — a millstone of it, turning and turning, grinding Tam down finer every year.

He put his hands around it the way you'd cup water from a stream, and he lifted.

It came free with a sound only he could hear — a low note, like a wheel finding its rhythm. Tam gasped beneath him, arched once, and then settled, breathing easier than he had in years. Felix sat back on his heels with the grief held against his sternum, heavy and hot and alive, and waited for it to settle into him the way stolen things do.

It took longer than usual. This grief was old and dense and it did not want to be his. It pressed at him, looking for Maren-shaped places to live, and found none, because Felix Okonkwo had spent thirty-one years keeping his interior swept bare as a threshing floor.

At last it coiled itself somewhere behind his ribs and went quiet.

"There," he said aloud, because Tam's eyes had opened and were watching him with something like terror. "It's done. Try to think of her."

Tam thought. Felix watched the man's face work through it — the reaching, the flinch, and then the surprise. "She's... smaller," Tam whispered. "No. Not smaller. Farther. Like she's standing at the end of the lane instead of lying on my chest." Tears came, but they were the ordinary kind, the kind that wash rather than drown. "Gods. Gods, I can breathe."

"Sleep now," Felix said. "You'll wake tomorrow and it'll hold. If it doesn't — if the edges fray — send for me and I'll patch it. No charge for patches."

Tam caught his wrist. His grip was shockingly strong for a dying man. "What does it feel like?" he asked. "Carrying someone else's grief. What's it like in you?"

Felix considered lying. It was practically professional courtesy; clients preferred their takers to be either monsters or saints, and the truth was neither.

"It's like carrying a stranger's child," he said finally. "Heavy, and not yours, and you find yourself being careful with it anyway."

Tam held his eyes a moment longer, then let go. "Strange trade," he murmured, sinking down into the pillow. "Strange, strange trade." Within minutes he was asleep, truly asleep, the first real sleep he'd likely had since the sharp part of Maren had started grinding him.

Felix banked the lamp, let himself out, and stood a while in the mill yard listening to the useless wheel turn.

* * *

The fog was coming in off the valley walls, the way it always did at dusk, pouring down the slopes like milk into tea until the whole Hollow was one gray bowl of it. That was the point of the Hollow, of course. The fog wasn't weather. It was warding — old magic, older than anyone alive, laid down when the world outside had gone mad with kings and wars and things worse than wars. Inside the fog, magic couldn't follow. Couldn't root, couldn't burn, couldn't reach. The Hollow was the one place in the world where a man's mind belonged entirely to himself.

Which was ironic, given that the entire economy of the place ran on taking minds apart and selling the pieces.

Felix walked home through the graying lanes of Cindermill, nodding to neighbors who nodded back with the particular wariness people reserve for a man who might, at any moment, be holding a piece of them. Old Sef the cooper touched his hat brim but kept his hand near his pocket, where he kept his memories written down in a little book — names, dates, the shape of important days — so that if Felix ever took something, the book would tell him what was missing. Everyone in the Hollow kept such books. It was the closest thing they had to locks.

His own cottage sat at the end of Tanner's Row, small and neat and anonymous, the way he liked it. Inside: one chair, one table, one bed, one shelf of jars.

The jars were the honest part of the trade. Each one held a slip of paper bearing a memory's name and weight, sealed with wax, dated. *Grief of Tam Halloway, milled fine, 1 portion sharp.* Below the shelf, in a locked box under the floorboards, were the memories themselves — not in the jars, obviously; the jars were bookkeeping. The memories lived in Felix, layered and labeled, a library with one librarian and no readers.

He lit the lamp, counted nothing, checked nothing, and ate half the heel of yesterday's bread standing up, because sitting down to eat alone felt too much like a confession.

Then he did what he did most nights. He sat in the one chair and he took inventory.

Not of stock. Of self.

This was a discipline, like a merchant counting coin. *Name: Felix Okonkwo. Trade: taker. Age: thirty-one, or thereabouts — the records from the Foundling House burned in the fire year, so who could say. Mother: unknown. Father: unknown. Earliest memory:* —

And here the inventory always snagged, the way a cart snags on a rut. Because Felix Okonkwo had no earliest memory. He had a *first remembered thing*, which was different: a door, a gray door in a gray wall, and cold air, and someone's hand between his shoulder blades, pushing him through. He must have been four, maybe five. After the door: the Foundling House, the matron with her ledger, the other children, the slow discovery that he was different — that when he touched people's temples, things came away.

Before the door: nothing. A clean sweep. Other children had fragments — a smell, a voice, a warmth. Felix had a blank so smooth it didn't even itch. The guild physician had examined him once, years ago, and pronounced it *congenital erasure*, rare but documented, nothing to be done, here's your license.

Felix had never quite believed in congenital erasure. He believed in thieves. He'd simply never found a reason to dig, because digging into yourself was the one theft the guild forbade, and because — if he was honest, in the privacy of the one chair — he was afraid of what a man finds when he opens a floorboard and it isn't dust underneath.

So: inventory complete. Name, trade, age approximate, history: a door and a push. Assets: one grief, freshly milled. Liabilities: none worth naming.

Loneliness wasn't a liability. Loneliness was the workshop condition. You couldn't do clean work carrying other people's lives inside you if your own life was loud. So he kept it quiet. He kept it *swept*. He told himself this was competence, and mostly, in the lamplight, he believed it.

Mostly.

* * *

Three days later, the rye and honey arrived, and with them the whole rotten machinery of the Hollow's economy, laid out on Tam Halloway's table like a surgeon's tools.

The nephew's boy was called Denny, fourteen, all elbows and appetite, and he watched Felix with the open awe of a kid who'd decided already that takers were sorcerers. Which, outside the fog, they would have been. Inside the fog they were tradesmen.

"He slept," Denny reported, hauling the basket onto the table. "Real sleep. And this morning he ate a whole bowl of porridge and cried about my cousin Maren but it was — Uncle Tam says it was like rain on a roof instead of rain in the house." The boy frowned, working at the metaphor, pleased with it. "Is that what it's like? What you do?"

"That's what it's like," Felix said, and broke the rye loaf, and the smell of it went through him like a bell through a bell tower. Three days of trading meals for work, and the work had been thin. Bread was bread.

Denny produced the honey jar, then hesitated, then produced something else — a folded paper, soft with handling. "Uncle Tam said to give you this. Said you'd know what it means."

Felix unfolded it. It was a promissory note, guild-stamped, drawn on the memory-house at Lowbridge: *One standard grief, any origin, payable to bearer.* Worth maybe six loaves. Tam was paying him twice, which meant Tam was grateful, which meant Tam wouldn't last the month, which meant—

Which meant Felix should sell the sharp part of Maren soon, while the grief was fresh and the provenance clean. Guild houses paid better for recent work. And there would be buyers, too — there were always buyers. Half the Hollow had lost someone to the ridge, the river, the fever years. A mother's grief for a drowned son, sold to a mother whose son had died the same way: that was the cruelest kindness in the valley, and it kept everyone fed. You didn't buy a memory to *have* it. You bought it to set it down. To walk a mile with someone else's mourning so your own could rest.

Folding the note away, Felix felt the stolen thing stir behind his ribs — Tam's grief, turning in its sleep, recognizing its own name in the transaction. He pressed a hand flat to his chest until it stilled.

"You'll be a taker someday," he said to Denny, mostly to change the subject in his own head. "You've got the look."

The boy went pink. "Guild won't take valley-born. Says we're—" he dropped his voice, reverent and scandalized— "*inbred to the fog.*"

"The guild says a lot of things." Felix gathered the bread and honey into his satchel. "Mind your uncle. Patch the roof before the rains."

He was three steps down Tam's path when the feeling hit him — the one he'd learned never to ignore, the taker's instinct, the sense of a memory out of place. Except it wasn't coming from a person. It was coming from the north.

From the ridge.

He stopped. Turned. The fog hung over the valley walls as it had hung for two hundred years, thick and seamless and silent.

But underneath the silence — faint, faint, like a note heard through a wall — something was *pressing*. Something on the other side of the fog, leaning against the warding the way a drunk leans against a door. And threaded through the pressure, unmistakable to a man who made his living handling other minds: a taste of foreign memory. Old. Vast. Angry.

Felix stood in the lane with bread going stale in his bag and the hair rising on his arms, and told himself it was the grief he'd taken, settling, misfiring, nothing. Told himself the Hollow had stood two hundred years and would stand two hundred more.

Behind him, in the mill, Tam Halloway slept without dreaming for the first time in twenty years.

Ahead of him, at the far end of the valley, at the gate that no outsider had ever found — the gatekeeper's dog began to bark.
