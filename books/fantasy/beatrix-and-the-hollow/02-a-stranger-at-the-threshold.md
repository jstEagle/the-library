## 2. A Stranger at the Threshold

The Hollow had three rules, and every child learned them before they learned their letters.

One: the fog keeps what it keeps. Two: nothing from outside finds its way in. Three: if you ever see something impossible, keep walking.

I was breaking the third rule the morning the stranger came through the gate.

* * *

The gate wasn't much to look at. Two leaning stones at the mouth of the north road, older than the valley, older than the mill, older than any name anyone could remember stealing. The fog pooled around them like it was holding them up. On the far side, the road ran a hundred paces and simply stopped — not blocked, not washed out, just *stopped*, the way a sentence stops when the speaker forgets what he was saying. That was the boundary. You could stand at the last stone and look down the road and see nothing but fog, and if you walked into it you'd come out the other side of the valley without ever turning around. The Hollow folded back on itself. That was the whole trick. That was why we were safe.

I'd gone up to the gate because of the bread.

Marta Hollis had paid me the night before — a heel of black rye, still warm, wrapped in cloth that smelled of her kitchen — and I'd eaten half of it walking home and half of it in bed like a man who didn't trust his own luck. Which I didn't. Luck in the Hollow was just a theft you hadn't been caught at yet. So in the morning, with the fog still thick and the mill not yet grinding, I went up to the gate to check the road. Habit. The way a man checks his purse after a crowd brushes past him. Not because he expects it empty. Because he needs to know.

The road was empty. The road was always empty. I'd stood at that gate a hundred mornings and a hundred evenings and the road had never once held anything but fog and the memory of fog.

This morning it held a woman.

She was maybe forty paces down, past the last stone, in the place where nothing could be, and she was walking toward me with the stiff, deliberate gait of someone who has decided that falling is a problem for later. One hand pressed flat against her side. The other held a leather tube against her chest the way you'd hold a child. Dark coat, dark hair coming loose from its braid, and a face that the fog made pale as paper.

I did the math the way you do. Forty paces. The boundary stone at my back. The mill a mile down the valley, and Marta Hollis the only soul likely to hear me if I shouted, and Marta Hollis seventy years old and half-deaf in one ear.

The woman took ten more paces and crossed the boundary.

I want to be clear about what that meant, because I don't think anyone who hasn't lived behind the fog can understand it. Things didn't cross. Birds didn't cross. Rain came from inside the valley or not at all. In my whole life, in the whole history of the Hollow as anyone remembered it — and we remembered a lot, we traded in it — nothing from outside had ever come through the gate. The valley didn't hide from the world. The world simply couldn't find it. That was rule two. That was the bedrock everything else stood on.

She crossed it like it was a threshold in a house. Like it was nothing.

Then she saw me, and her face did something I've thought about since. It didn't fill with relief. It filled with *recognition* — the flat, grim look of a woman confirming a fact she'd already accepted — and then her legs went out from under her and she went down into the fog like a stone into water.

* * *

I want to say I ran to her. I didn't. I stood there for the space of three breaths, maybe four, running the rules over in my head. Impossible things keep walking. That was rule three, and rule three had kept me alive through years of taking things from people that couldn't be given back. You see a ghost in the fog, you keep walking. You hear the mill grinding at midnight when the miller's been dead a season, you keep walking. The Hollow was full of edges, and the people who looked over edges fell off them.

But she wasn't a ghost. Ghosts don't bleed. I could see it now that she was down — a dark spread on the fog-damp stones beneath her, black in the grey light, and growing. And ghosts don't carry leather tubes with wax seals the size of a fist, stamped with a sigil I didn't know, which meant it wasn't ours, which meant it was from *outside*, which meant—

Which meant the world had found us.

I ran.

She was heavier than she looked, or I was weaker than I thought, or both. I got an arm under her shoulders and her blood came off on my sleeve, warm through the wool, and her eyes opened — grey eyes, sharp even half-gone, the eyes of someone who'd been making hard decisions for a long time — and she looked at my face like she was checking it against something.

"You're him," she said.

"I'm nobody," I said. "Who sent you?"

That got a sound out of her that might have been a laugh if laughs cost less. "Nobody sent me. I sent me." Her hand came up — slow, shaking — and closed on the front of my shirt, and pulled, and I let her pull me down close because her grip was the only thing keeping the rest of her together. Her breath was hot against my ear and it smelled of iron and something else, something faintly sweet and wrong, like fruit going off.

"Listen. Listen to me, thief."

I hadn't told her I was a thief. I hadn't told her anything. My blood went cold and slow.

"I know what you are," she whispered. "I've been looking for this valley for eleven days and the fog wouldn't let me in, wouldn't let me in, and then last night it *tore*, just for a moment, like a seam splitting — and I heard the mill. I heard a mill turning. And I thought, there will be someone there who takes things. There's always someone who takes things. Every place has one." Her eyes found mine again, and there was no accusation in them, which was worse. "I need you to take something."

"I don't know what you think I—"

"The wound won't close." She said it flatly, the way you'd report the weather. She let go of my shirt and pulled her coat open with the hand that still worked, and I looked, because there was no not looking.

Someone had cut her. Low on the left side, a long clean stroke, the kind of wound that kills slow and knows it. Except it was worse than that. The edges of the cut were *grey*. Not bloodless — they bled, God, they bled — but grey the way old meat is grey, the way the fog is grey, the skin around it gone the color of ash and creeping outward in fine filaments like frost on a window. The wound wasn't healing and it wasn't festering. It was *unmaking* her, a finger's width at a time.

I'd seen wounds in the Hollow. I'd seen fever, and broken bones, and old Marta's cough that would take her before winter was out. I'd never seen anything that looked like the fog had learned to cut.

"What did this?" I heard myself ask.

"A man in a grey coat." She smiled, and it was the worst thing I'd seen that morning, and I had already seen a wound that ate. "He had a sigil on his breast. A tower, and a hand coming out of the top of it. He said—" and here her voice did something strange, went distant, like she was quoting a lesson learned by heart, "—he said that what is hidden must be surrendered, and what is surrendered must be burned, and that mercy is a debt the crown no longer honors. And then he cut me and let me go, because a wound like his doesn't need to kill. It just needs to follow."

I looked at her side. The grey filaments had crept another finger's width while she talked. I swear it. I watched it happen.

"Take it," she said. "Before it takes me whole."

* * *

Here's what I knew, crouched in the fog at the gate of the only world I had, holding a dying stranger: that I should keep walking. That rule three existed for a reason. That whatever she carried had brought a man in a grey coat to the edge of our valley, and men like that didn't come alone, and the Hollow's whole defense was that the world couldn't find us — and she had just walked through a door that had been shut since before anyone's memory, which meant the door was broken, which meant everything the rules protected was already in the process of not being protected anymore.

And here's what I did anyway, and I still can't fully tell you why, and if you'd asked me that morning I'd have told you it was the map. The sealed tube, the sigil I didn't know, the thing worth chasing a woman across a broken boundary for. A thief sees a prize and his hands move before his head votes. That's the honest answer and it's the coward's answer both.

Because the other answer is that she said *take it*, and I have spent my whole life being the man people say that to, and no one had ever said it bleeding, and no one had ever said it like it was the last thing they'd ever trust anyone with.

"Can you walk?" I asked.

"Can you carry?"

"I carried a grief out of a dying man's chest two nights ago. I can carry you a mile."

Something moved behind her eyes at that — not fear, not quite. Recalculation. The look of a gambler who'd bet on a horse and was watching it run. "A thief and a taker both," she murmured. "Good. Good." And she fainted, which I decided to take as a vote of confidence.

I got her up across my shoulders. The leather tube she would not release — even unconscious, even grey to the jaw, her arm was locked around it like a root around a stone, and I stopped trying to take it after the second attempt. Fine. She could keep her secrets warm a while longer.

I carried her down the valley road, past the leaning stones, past the fog-line where the world went from grey to merely overcast, and the whole way I kept thinking: the seam tore. She heard the mill through it. The fog let her in.

The fog never lets anything in.

The fog had never had a reason to. And I found myself walking faster, not because of the weight on my shoulders, but because of a question that had gotten into me like a splinter and wouldn't come out:

If the fog could tear for her —

What else was on the other side of it, looking for the tear?

* * *

I laid her in Marta Hollis's back room, because Marta owed me, or I owed Marta, or the ledger between us had gotten so tangled that neither of us could say which, and Marta took one look at the woman's side and went white and started boiling water without asking a single question, which is a kind of wisdom you can't steal, you can only live into.

I sat by the bed through the afternoon while the grey crept. Marta packed the wound with moss and comfrey and it did nothing. I held a poultice of bread-and-mold, the old field trick, and it did nothing. The filaments spread. By dusk they'd reached her collarbone, and her breath had gone shallow and quick, and her fever had a smell to it — that same sweet-wrong smell, fruit going off, the fog's smell.

Toward evening she woke, and her hand found the leather tube, and then found my wrist.

"Thief," she said. Her voice was a thread now. "What's your name?"

"Felix."

"Felix." She said it like she was testing whether it would hold weight. "I'm Beatrix. I was a courier, before. Before I was this." A ghost of a smile. "I have something in my keeping that isn't mine. I've carried it for six years and I was told — I was *told* — to bring it to the Hollow if all else failed, because the Hollow forgets, and what the Hollow forgets can't be found." Her fingers tightened on my wrist, hard, impossibly hard, the grip of a woman spending her last strength like a miser's last coin. "But the Hollow is forgetting how to hide, Felix. I felt it tear. And the man in the grey coat is not the only one. There are more. There are always more."

"Beatrix—"

"When I die," she said, "it will try to scatter. Memories do, when their keeper goes. It will come apart like smoke unless someone takes it whole, and holds it, and keeps it whole." Her grey eyes held mine, and I saw that she was not begging. She was *instructing*, the way you instruct someone in a duty you believe they were born to. "You take things. So take this. Take it and keep it and carry it out of this valley when the time comes, because the time is coming, Felix, it is already coming, I could hear the riders on the ridge last night—"

"Riders," I said. "On which ridge?"

She didn't answer. Her eyes had gone past me, to the window, where the last light was failing — and her face changed, and not from the fever.

"Felix," she whispered. "What is that light?"

I turned.

Down the valley, far off, past the mill, past the south fields, on the black line of the southern ridge where no road ran and no one lived — there was a glow. Orange. Steady. The kind of steady that fire gets when someone is feeding it on purpose.

And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, carried on a wind that shouldn't have been able to cross the boundary — a sound like singing. Many voices. Chanting.

The fog along the southern ridge was beginning to thin.
