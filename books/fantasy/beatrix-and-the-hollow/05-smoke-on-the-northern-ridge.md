## 5. Smoke on the Northern Ridge

The Hollow woke him three days after Beatrix died, and it woke him angry.

Felix came up out of a dream he couldn't remember — couldn't *keep*, was more like it — with his heart hammering and the taste of ash on his tongue. For a moment he lay in his narrow bed listening to the valley breathe, the way he'd listened to it all his life: the mill wheel turning down by the stream, old Marta's rooster doing its broken-crow impression, the fog pressing soft against every window like it wanted to be let in.

Then he heard the screaming.

Not fear-screaming. Wonder-screaming. Which in the Hollow was worse.

He dressed in the dark and went out into the street, and that was when he saw the light coming off the northern ridge. It wasn't firelight. Firelight flickered. This *moved* — slow ribbons of blue-white rising off the tree line like heat shimmer standing up wrong, curling against the underside of the fog and making it glow from within. The whole northern sky had gone the color of a bruise healing.

People were gathered in the square in their nightclothes, faces tilted up, mouths open. Old Tam the cooper was pointing with a hand that shook. His wife held a candle she'd forgotten to light.

"It's coming through," someone said. "It's coming *through*."

Magic couldn't enter the Hollow. That was the first truth every child learned, before they learned their letters. The valley sat under a warding older than anyone could name, a silence laid over the world like a lid on a pot, and nothing that burned or sang or whispered could cross it. That was why the outside world forgot they existed. That was why people like Felix — people who needed to disappear — came here and stayed.

And that was why the ribbons of light crawling down the ridge meant the lid was cracking.

Felix stood at the edge of the crowd and felt the second thing, the thing nobody else had noticed yet, and his stomach turned to stone.

The memory was awake inside him.

Beatrix's ghost-bound secret had been quiet since he'd taken it — a weight behind his breastbone, a locked room he'd learned to walk past without looking at the door. But now it stirred, turning over in its sleep like a dog scenting another dog, and along his left palm the burned map-lines *itched*. He looked down. In the blue-white light from the ridge, the faint tracery of roads and rivers across his hand seemed almost luminous, as if the map were trying to answer whatever was leaking down off the trees.

*Don't,* he told it. *Lie still.*

But he could feel it pulling, faint as a fisherman's line, toward the north. Toward whatever was coming through.

"Felix."

Marta stood beside him. She was seventy if she was a day, and she'd known him since he was a foundling with no name worth keeping, and she was looking at him the way you look at meat gone off. Not with hatred yet. With suspicion working up the courage to become hatred.

"You're feeling it," she said. "Aren't you."

It wasn't a question, so he didn't answer it. Around them, heads were turning. The crowd's attention slid toward him the way water finds a crack.

"I don't know what you mean," he said, which was true in the way all his best lies were true — technically, uselessly.

"You've been strange since that woman died at your door." Marta's voice carried. She'd spent fifty years being heard across a market garden; her voice knew how to carry. "Three days you haven't come out. And now the sky's burning and I can *smell* it on you, boy. Something foreign. Something that doesn't belong in this valley."

"That's not—" He stopped. Swallowed. Because here was the terrible thing: he could smell it too. Beatrix's memory had a scent, now that the warding was thinning enough for such things to have scents — juniper and horse sweat and something underneath like struck flint. Her. All her. The secret wearing her smell like a coat.

"Something got in," said Tam, low. "When she crossed the threshold. Whatever killed the warding came in with her."

"She was dying," Felix said. "She was a courier. She—"

"She brought this." Marta pointed up at the bruised, glowing sky, and the crowd murmured agreement like a hive waking. "And you're carrying whatever she handed you. Don't think we don't see it. You've always been able to hide things from us, thief. Not anymore."

*Thief.* He'd been called worse in the Hollow, and often, but never in front of everyone, and never while the world was ending overhead. He watched the word land on the crowd and take root, and he understood with cold clarity that he was watching his life end in real time. Forty years of trading fairly, of taking only what was offered, of paying for bread with grief honestly stolen — none of it mattered. Fear didn't audit the ledger. Fear just burned the books.

* * *

By noon the smoke arrived, and with it certainty.

It came rolling over the northern ridge in a grey wall, and beneath it, when the wind shifted, they could see them: figures on the ridgeline, small with distance, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who had all the time in the world because nothing ahead of them could hurt them. They wore no colors Felix could distinguish. They didn't need to. Everything about them — the spacing, the pace, the way they moved like fingers spread across a page — said *inquisitors*.

Behind his ribs, Beatrix's memory slammed itself against its cage hard enough to make him gasp.

*Easy,* he thought at it, absurdly, as if reasoning with it. *They'll not get me.*

But the memory wasn't cowering. It was *reaching*. Toward the ridge, toward the riders, and — this was the part that made his hands shake — part of it was reaching toward the cracks in the warding with something that felt horribly like recognition. Like homesickness.

The council met in the millhouse at midday, and they didn't invite Felix. He went anyway, stood outside in the yard among the milling crowd while voices rose inside, and listened to his exile being arranged one sentence at a time.

"—can't stay, not with that in him—"

"—the warding held for centuries until *she* came, and she came to *him*—"

"—maybe that's what it wants, maybe he drew it, you know what he is, you know what he does—"

That last voice was Tam's, and it was the one that finished it, because Tam was respected, Tam was fair, Tam had bought back a memory of his drowned son from Felix two winters ago at half price and wept on his doorstep in gratitude. If Tam believed Felix had drawn the inquisitors down on them, then everyone would believe it, and the worst part — the part Felix would carry out of the valley along with everything else — was that Tam might even be right. Things were drawn to secrets. Maybe the Hollow's silence had finally cracked under the weight of the one he was holding.

Maybe he hadn't been safe all these years. Maybe he'd just been a fuse waiting for someone to bring the match.

The council emerged an hour later. Elder Prue, who was older than the millwheel and twice as unyielding, walked to the yard's gate where Felix waited, and the crowd parted around her like she was a blade.

"You'll go," she said. No preamble. She was too old to waste words on him.

"Prue." He used her name without the honorific because he was angry, and because anger was easier than begging. "I've lived here my whole life. I've never harmed this valley."

"You're harming it now." She nodded upward, at the bruised sky, at the smoke. "Every hour you stand under our fog with that thing in your chest, the cracks widen. You can feel it. Don't lie to me, boy — I watched you flinch in the square this morning like something kicked you. It wants out, and it wants *outward*, and outward is through us."

He couldn't argue. That was the cruelty of it. He *could* feel it — Beatrix's secret straining toward the breaches like water toward holes in a dam — and if he could feel it, then everyone in the valley whose blood ran thin enough to sense magic at all was probably lying awake hating him for it.

"The woman," Prue said, quieter. "Before you go. What did she give you?"

"A burden," Felix said. "That's all I'll say."

Prue studied him for a long moment, and something old and tired moved behind her eyes. "Your mother said nearly the same thing, once. Word for word."

His whole body went still. "What did you say?"

"Nothing." But her gaze flicked away, just slightly, just once, and Felix — who had made a life's study of what people hid — saw it plain as a brand. "Go north by the sheep track. It avoids the ridge road. And Felix — don't come back. If the warding fails entirely, the Hollow won't be here to come back to. And if it holds—" She almost smiled. Almost. "Well. Then you'll be dead somewhere interesting, and we'll have peace either way."

*Your mother said nearly the same thing.*

He walked away from the gate with that sentence lodged in him like a splinter too deep for tweezers, and he didn't realize until he was halfway up the sheep track, valley fog closing behind him, that he wasn't sure whether he was fleeing the inquisitors or the question.

Both. He was fleeing both. He'd always been good at running from two things at once. It was standing still that he'd never managed.

* * *

The sheep track climbed through fog so thick it muffled even his own footsteps, and that should have been a comfort — the Hollow wrapping him up one last time, hiding him as it had hidden him all his life — but instead every step felt like walking deeper into a wound.

Because the fog was thinning. He could feel that too, now. Twenty years of taking memories had given him fine instruments where other people had instincts, and those instruments were all screaming. The warding wasn't a wall around him anymore; it was a roof with holes in it, and through the holes came *weather*. Not rain. Weather of another kind entirely — pressure changes, tides of something that pressed against his skin and made the hair on his arms rise and made his teeth ache in their roots. Magic, leaking into a place built to exclude it, and finding him the way lightning finds the tallest tree.

Twice on the climb, Beatrix's memory broke loose of its moorings and flooded him.

The first time it was small: a flash of a road he'd never walked, white dust and olive trees, a woman's laugh he didn't know. Hers. Younger. He staggered against a rock and gripped it until the flash passed, breathing hard, fog beading cold on his face.

The second time was worse, because it came with the sound of the riders below.

He'd paused in a fold of the hillside to listen — hooves, many, on the ridge road, moving fast — and in the moment his concentration broke, the memory surged up and *showed* him. Not a road this time. A hall. Banners burning on the walls, and a man in white and gold kneeling before another man in black, and the word that hung in the air of that hall like smoke: *traitor*. He felt the watcher's rage — Beatrix's inherited rage, borrowed rage, rage two generations old — and it was so vast and so clean that for one horrifying second it felt like his own, and he understood that he was one leaky crack away from becoming nothing but a glove this memory wore.

"No," he said aloud, to himself, to it. "You're cargo. I'm the courier. That's the whole arrangement."

The memory subsided, sullen, and below him on the ridge road the hoofbeats passed — and then, horribly, slowed.

He flattened himself against the hillside. Through the fog he made out shapes: six riders, halters jingling, and at their head a figure in grey who sat very still, face tilted upward toward the valley, toward the thinning fog, like a person listening to a conversation through a wall.

Then the grey figure raised one hand, and the fog *moved*. Recoiled. Peeled back from the riders like a curtain drawn by a servant, and Felix's skin went ice-cold, because the warding was supposed to make that impossible, and this woman had parted it the way you'd part tall grass.

She couldn't see him. The track was blind-folded in rock and heather and he'd spent a lifetime learning how not to be perceived. But her head tracked slowly across the hillside anyway, deliberate as a lighthouse beam, and when it passed over the place where he lay, the map on his palm burned hot enough to make him bite his fist.

*She knows something's here,* he thought. *She doesn't know what. Yet.*

The hand lowered. The fog flowed back. The riders moved on southward along the ridge road — not into the valley, he realized with a chill that sank all the way to the marrow. Along its rim. Circling. Herding.

She wasn't trying to catch him in the Hollow. She was going to be waiting everywhere the Hollow wasn't.

* * *

Near dusk, Felix reached the boundary.

Everyone in the valley knew where it was without ever being taught — a line in the heather where the fog simply stopped, rolling up against empty air like water against glass. Beyond it, in the last flat light, he could see the Bright Country: hills the color of copper, a river running gold, forests with actual shadows under them instead of fog. Enormous. Detailed. Real in a way the Hollow had never let anything be real.

He stood at the line with his toes an inch from crossing and made himself look back, one time, the way you look back at a house you've just sold.

The valley lay below in its shroud, blue-grey and soft, the mill wheel turning, lamplight blooming in the square where his whole life had happened. It looked exactly as it had every evening of his forty years, and for the first time he could see it clearly — from outside, so to speak — and what he saw was a beautiful room with the windows painted shut.

They'd been right to send him away. That was the bitter mouthful of it. Marta and Tam and old Prue, with their suspicion and their fear and their torches barely lit — they'd been right in substance if not in kindness. He *was* carrying something that didn't belong here. The cracks had followed him the way cracks follow weight. And they'd also, in their panic, handed him the only gift he'd ever gotten from the valley: permission to stop pretending it had ever held him.

*Your mother said nearly the same thing.*

He turned that over one last time, the splinter, the question. Then he put it away in the same inner pocket where he kept everything he couldn't afford to feel yet, and he stepped across the line.

The world hit him like a wave breaking over a drowning man.

Color first — real color, saturated, violent, the copper hills and the gold river and a sky overhead that went on *up*, no ceiling of fog, just depth after depth of evening blue with actual stars starting in it, and he stood there with his mouth open like a fool because he had never in his life seen a star. Sound next: wind with texture in it, birdsong from three different directions, the river talking to its stones. Smell — pine sap, wet earth, distant woodsmoke, a hundred things at once, and his senses drank and drank and couldn't stop drinking.

And underneath all of it, stirring like leaves in that new wind: his own stolen memories, the ones he'd taken over twenty years and never fully digested, waking up in the bright air and rustling through him, restless, alive, *loud*.

Along his palm, the map pulsed. Behind his ribs, Beatrix's secret turned its face toward the open country and pulled, gentle but insistent, like a hand in the dark. North, it said. It didn't use words, but it said it. *North.*

"Not yet," Felix told it, and started downhill toward the river and the gold light and whatever was waiting there, and behind him the fog rolled against the boundary like a tide against a seawall, hiding the valley, hiding the riders, hiding everything except the road ahead.

Somewhere south of the ridge, a grey rider turned her horse. But he didn't see that. All he saw was the river, and a campfire on its bank, and the silhouette of a woman sitting beside it who was watching him long before he knew she existed.
