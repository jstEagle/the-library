## 6. Out Into the Bright Country

The boundary of the Hollow was not a wall. It was a seam in the air, a place where the fog simply stopped, as if someone had drawn a line with a knife and the world on the other side had refused to bleed across it.

Felix had lived within sight of that line his whole life. Every child in the valley learned it early: you could walk to the edge, press your hand into the haze where it thinned to nothing, and feel a pressure like glass against your palm — but you could not pass through. The Hollow kept its people the way a jar keeps fireflies. Not cruelly. Just completely.

He had never wanted out. That was the strange truth he only understood now, standing at the seam at dawn with Beatrix's memory coiled behind his eyes like a sleeping snake, the map burned into his left palm in lines of silvered scar tissue that ached when he flexed his fingers. He had never wanted out because he had never known there was an out to want. The Hollow gave you fog, bread, grief to steal, and a life small enough to hold in one hand. What else was there?

Now he knew. Now there was smoke on the northern ridge, and inquisitors with white brands on their cheeks, and neighbors who had looked at him across the miller's yard with eyes gone flat and afraid, sensing something foreign curled inside him like a second tenant. *Witch-thief,* someone had whispered. *He's carrying it. He's carrying her secret.* The warding was cracking. Magic was seeping through the fissures like water through a dam, and everything the Hollow had been built to keep out was coming in.

So: out.

He stood at the seam as the sun came up behind the eastern crags, and for the first time in his life he watched the light hit the boundary from the outside-in. It did something impossible. The light bent. It pooled along the invisible wall like rain along a windowpane, gold and thick, and where it touched the fog the fog turned briefly luminous before dying into gray again. He had seen this every morning of his life and had never once wondered what it meant. It meant there was another side. It meant the sun over the Hollow was a reflection, a copy, a candle held up to a window while the real fire burned somewhere else.

His heart was doing something it had never done. It wasn't fear exactly. It was closer to the feeling he got when he took a memory from someone — that lurch, that falling-through-the-floor sensation, the moment when another person's grief opened inside your chest like a door you hadn't known was there. Except this time the door was his own. This time what waited on the other side was not someone else's loss.

It was everything he'd been stolen from.

He pressed his scarred palm flat against the seam. The silver lines of the map warmed against the barrier, and the barrier — which had held against seventy years of desperate hands, exiled lovers, children dared by their friends to touch the edge and run back screaming — yielded. Not broke. *Yielded*, the way a lock yields to its own key. The fog parted around his hand like water around a prow, and he stepped forward into the pressure, into the resistance, into the moment where the whole world pushed back against his skin—

—and then it didn't anymore.

* * *

The first thing was the color.

He had known colors the way a blind man knows words for colors. Green. Blue. Red. Names attached to the muted spectrum the fog allowed through, grays wearing labels, a whole valley of people agreeing to pretend. But this — the meadow sloping away from the boundary was green the way a wound is red, saturated, obscene, so vivid his eyes physically hurt and watered and he had to shut them and open them again, twice, three times, teaching himself to look the way an infant learns. The grass moved. Wind — real wind, wind with texture and temperature and intent, not the dead recycled breath of the valley — came down off the hills and combed through it, and each blade caught the light differently, ten thousand small greens shifting like a living thing, and Felix stood at the edge of the Hollow with his mouth open and felt his knees go soft.

The second thing was the noise. Birds — dozens of them, layered, calling over each other from trees he had no names for. Insects ticking in the grass. A stream somewhere below, running loud and bright over stones. And underneath all of it a low continuous hum that resolved, after a minute of stunned listening, into distance: roads, carts, voices, a whole country going about its morning without any idea that a man had just stepped out of a valley that officially did not exist.

The third thing was worse than both.

It happened slowly, then all at once. His own memories began to stir.

He had spent nineteen years stealing from other people because his own shelves were nearly bare. Everyone in the Hollow knew the type — the thieves were born light, mostly empty, and the guild had found them young and trained them young, and Felix had always assumed his emptiness was natural, a flaw of make, like being born short or tone-deaf. He had made his peace with it. You could build a life on very little if the little was solid.

But now, outside the warding, outside the fog that pressed down on the mind as much as the sky, things were surfacing. Fragments. Warmths. A smell — cut grass, no, hay, hay drying in a loft, and dust motes swimming in a slant of light, and someone's voice humming, low and off-key, a woman's voice. A hand holding his, larger than his own, rough-palmed, walking him somewhere at dusk. The taste of something sweet he couldn't name — honey? — smeared on bread. A word spoken to him, repeated, a name maybe, or half a name, sitting just beyond reach like a fish under ice.

They weren't memories, exactly. They were the *holes* where memories had been, and the holes had shapes, and the shapes were suddenly, unbearably vivid. It was like pressing your tongue into the socket of a tooth knocked out years ago and finding that the socket still remembered the tooth perfectly.

Felix sat down hard in the grass. The green soaked cold through his trousers. He put his scarred palm against the ground and breathed, and the fragments kept rising, and he let them, because he didn't know how to stop them, and after a while they settled into a slow tide instead of a flood, coming and going, teasing him.

"Someone took them," he said aloud, to no one. "Someone took them and I let them."

Beatrix's memory shifted behind his eyes. He felt it move the way you feel a stone shift in a riverbed — a settling, a readjustment, the bound loyalty of a dead courier testing its new housing. For a moment he saw through borrowed eyes again: a woman on a horse, riding hard through country like this, green and vast and dangerous, carrying a secret worth more than her life. Then it sank again, quiet, patient. It would wait. It had waited through her death. It could wait through his panic.

He got up. He walked.

* * *

The country east of the Hollow rolled down through meadows into farmland, and the farmland into a river valley, and by midday Felix had learned three things about the bright country. One: distances lied here. In the Hollow, where fog ate everything past fifty paces, you measured the world in steps. Here the eye said *that hill is close* and an hour later you were still walking toward it. Two: people looked at him. In the valley, everyone knew everyone, and strangers were a category that did not exist; here, faces passed him on a rutted road — a drover, two women carrying baskets, a boy leading a mule piled with turnips — and every single one looked at him, some with curiosity, some with the flat assessment of people who lived close enough to the border to know that odd travelers sometimes came out of the fog, and none of them with kindness he could trust. Three: he was visibly wrong. Something about him read as off — the way he flinched at loud sounds, the way he stared at ordinary things, the brand on the back of his right hand, old and faded, the thief's mark, five-pointed, that the guild burned into its foundlings so they could always be found.

A farmwife offered him bread and cheese at her gate and asked where he'd come from, and when he said *the Hollow* she crossed herself with a gesture he didn't recognize and told him the fog-country was a story mothers used to frighten children, and that no living person came out of it, and then she looked at his face a moment too long and took the basket back inside and barred the door.

By late afternoon he reached the river. It was wide and brown-green and busy — barges, skiffs, a ferry, nets drying on racks, a town strung along the far bank with smoke rising from a hundred chimneys. Signs everywhere, letters everywhere, and Felix could read, barely, the way the guild taught counting and script to children it expected to keep ledgers, but the sheer volume of written words on every surface made the world feel like it was shouting at him.

He needed passage. He needed distance between himself and the boundary before the inquisitors' outriders fanned out along it, and he had almost nothing to pay with — a few coins hoarded against a day he'd never imagined, a knife, and the one commodity the Hollow had taught him to carry: other people's memories, sealed in wax-and-iron vials in the lining of his coat. Griefs, mostly. A first love. A dead child's laughter. Things he'd taken from sellers who wanted the weight gone and buyers who wanted the warmth. On this side of the seam, he suspected, such trade existed too — everything existed here — but he didn't know its markets or its rules, and flashing stolen minds to strangers on a riverbank seemed like a fast way to die.

What he had instead was silence. Or rather, the appearance of it. He had come out of a place no one was supposed to be able to leave, carrying a secret whose value he could feel in his own skull like a coal that never cooled, and he had not said one word about any of it. Silence, he was beginning to understand, was currency here too. Perhaps the oldest currency of all.

At the ferry landing, among the barges, one vessel sat apart. Black-hulled, low, patched with fresh timber in three places, its sails furled and its deck cluttered with crates that had been rearranged recently and hastily, the way cargo gets rearranged when someone expects to run. Painted on its stern, in flaking gilt: *Kestrel*. At its rail stood a woman with iron-gray hair cropped short, sleeves rolled, hands stained with tar, arguing with a man about a rope knot in a voice that suggested she was winning.

Felix watched her for ten minutes before he approached, because watching was the first thing the guild had ever taught him. She moved like someone who owned every inch of the deck and resented having to share it with the rest of the world. She counted things constantly — crates, ropes, seconds between her crew's questions and her answers — and the counting had a hardness to it that Felix recognized, because he counted too, always, everyone he'd grown up with did; counting was what hollow people did instead of remembering.

When the argument ended and the man slunk off down the gangplank, Felix walked up and said the only useful thing he knew how to say.

"I need passage, captain. Downriver. Far downriver."

She looked at him the way a moneylender looks at a borrower. "Passage costs."

"I can pay little. But I can pay in a thing rarer than coin."

"Mm." She spat over the rail, expertly, without looking. "Everyone on this river says that. Rarer than coin. Worth more than gold. You're the fourth today." Her eyes were pale gray, almost colorless, and they did not soften. "What are you?"

That question. From the farmwife it had been curiosity. From Odile Grey — though he didn't know her name yet — it was triage. She was deciding, right now, whether he was cargo, ballast, or trouble.

"A thief," Felix said, because lying to this woman seemed like drowning in a teacup. "From the Hollow."

"The fog-country." Not disbelief. Interest, filed away. "No one comes out of the fog-country."

"I did. Today. Through the seam on the northern march."

Something shifted in her stance — a fractional straightening, weight moving from heel to ball of foot, the posture of a woman recalculating. "Through the seam," she repeated. "Alone?"

"Alone."

"And what are you carrying that the fog let you take out?"

And here Felix made the decision that would shape everything after. He thought about telling her nothing. He thought about telling her everything. He did neither. He told her a shaped thing, a true-shaped lie, the kind of trade the Hollow had trained him for since childhood.

"A debt," he said. "I'm carrying a debt owed to a dead woman, and the payment is delivery, and the delivery is to a place I'll know when I see it. I can't say more. Saying more gets people killed, and I've already buried one person this month."

Odile Grey — he learned her name within the hour, from a crewman who talked too freely around strangers — considered this. She had the particular stillness of people who have survived by letting others talk first. She circled him once on the deck, not touching, reading him the way he read others: gait, hands, eyes, the brand. She stopped behind him longer than anywhere else. He felt her looking at the thief's mark and said nothing, and she said nothing, and both silences were loud.

"You're marked," she said finally, coming back around to face him. "Guild-trained. Which guild?"

"The one that doesn't advertise."

"They all say that." She folded her arms. "Here's what I know, fog-rat. There are riders on the north road — white-branded, king's men, inquisitors' outriders — asking questions at every landing since yesterday. Questions about a courier. A woman. Dead or alive, they don't care which. Questions about what she carried." She let that hang in the air between them for three full seconds, and Felix kept his face still, and keeping his face still cost him more than anything he'd done since crossing the seam. "So either you're what they're hunting, or you're bait they've dressed up and pointed downriver, or you're nobody, and I've met enough nobodies to know they don't cross impossible borders alone. Whichever you are, you're interesting. Interesting is expensive."

"How expensive?"

"Your silence. Mine, I mean. You buy my silence about what you are and what I think you're carrying, and in exchange you work. Lines, cargo, bilge — whatever my bosun puts in your hands. And you keep your vials and your secrets stowed and tell me nothing unless I ask, and when I ask, you decide each time whether lying to me is worth it." She smiled for the first time, thin and dry as a paper cut. "I'll warn you once: I've caught better liars than you'll ever be, and I fed them to the river."

"Fair terms," Felix said.

"They're not fair. They're mine." She jerked her chin toward the deck. "There's a pallet under the foredeck. Bosun's named Tallow. Do what he says and we'll get along."

He worked that evening and all the next day as the Kestrel ran south with the current, and the country kept assaulting him — cliffs the color of rust, towns stacked up their banks like nests, night falling with actual stars, thousands of them, a spill of diamonds across black velvet, and Felix standing at the rail after his shift with his neck craned back and his eyes wet, because the Hollow's sky had held four stars, the same four, every night of his life, and he had loved them, and he had not known they were a cage with pinpricks punched in the lid.

The fragments kept surfacing. The hayloft. The humming. The large rough hand. Each time one rose, he gripped the rail and rode it like a wave and let it sink, and each time it sank a little slower, a little less willing, as if whatever part of him still remembered was learning that it was safe now, that the fog couldn't follow him here, that it could come up and stay up if it wanted.

On the third night, at a fueling stop, a rider came down to the landing. White brand on the cheek. King's colors. He walked the length of the dock asking his questions in a bored official voice — *seen a courier, seen a stranger out of the fog-country, seen anything unusual* — and Odile answered him with cheerful, useless, immaculate lies, laughing about fog-wives' tales, and the rider believed her, or pretended to, and rode on.

That night she came to where Felix sat mending net and crouched next to him, uninvited, close enough that he could smell tar and pipe smoke.

"White-brand asked about a courier," she said quietly. "You knew that before I did. I watched your hands. Mending knots, steady as a surgeon, until he mentioned her. Then your left hand closed." She nodded at the scarred palm, the silver map-lines faintly visible in the lantern light. "That's the tell, fog-rat. That's the tell that'll kill you."

Felix said nothing. She waited. He said nothing longer.

"Good," she said finally, standing. "Keep practicing." And then, at the ladder, pausing with one foot on the bottom rung, she added the thing she'd clearly climbed down here to say, the real cargo beneath the warning:

"There's a market two days south. Carrowgate. If you're selling what I think you might be selling, you'll want to do it there — and you'll want someone beside you who knows which buyers are brokers and which are inquisitors wearing broker's coats." She looked at him levelly. "I'm for hire, fog-rat. Think about it."

Then she was gone up the ladder, and Felix sat alone with the net in his lap and the coal burning behind his eyes, and the stars wheeling overhead, and the fragments rising — the hay, the humming, the hand — and for the first time he let one of them stay.

Just for a moment. Just the smallest one: the taste of honey on bread, and a voice saying a word, half a name, warm as a lit room.

He held it the way you hold a bird that has flown back to a broken cage. And then, because holding things was the one skill he had never been allowed to keep, he let it go — and felt it fly a little farther away.

Two days to Carrowgate. And someone else, he was beginning to suspect, was already shopping there for the same secret beating in his chest.
