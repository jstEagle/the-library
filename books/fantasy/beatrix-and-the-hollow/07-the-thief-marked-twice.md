## 7. The Thief Marked Twice

Odile Grey had a rule about passengers: never take one who looked relieved to see you.

Relief meant pursuit. Pursuit meant complications, and complications meant either money or corpses, and she had buried enough of both to be particular about which. So when the lean man with river-mud on his boots came down the shingle at Greywater Crossing — moving quick and low, like a man who'd learned that standing upright got you noticed — she watched him from the deck of the *Magpie's Debt* and made her living by counting his sins before he ever opened his mouth.

One: he traveled light. One bag, no bedroll. Men who traveled light were either desperate or professional, and the ones who were both were the most dangerous kind.

Two: he kept his left hand closed. Not clenched — closed, casual, the way a card player holds a losing hand he intends to bluff. She'd captained river runners through three border wars. Nobody protected an empty fist.

Three: he didn't haggle.

"Greywater to Carrowgate," he said, when she came down the gangplank to meet him. "Quiet berth. No questions. I can pay."

He could. The coin was good silver, unworn, the kind that hadn't passed through enough hands to leave a trail — which was its own small confession. But it was the hand that decided her. When he counted out the passage fee, his fingers uncurled just far enough, and Odile Grey saw what he'd been hiding against his palm.

A brand. Old, puckered, deliberate. A thief's mark — the guild sigil of the Hollow roads, the one they pressed into the flesh of anyone caught lifting in the memory-towns beyond the fens. She'd seen it twice before. Once on a man she'd sold to the inquisitors for the bounty, and once on a woman whose mind they'd emptied so thoroughly on the scaffold steps that she'd smiled at the crowd.

And beside it — newer, raw, the skin still angry and weeping at the edges — burned a second mark. A circle crossed by a single vertical line. The seal of the Inquisitorial Office of the Usurper's Throne.

Odile did not let her face change. That was the first thing they taught you on the rivers, before knots, before navigation, before anything: your face is cargo too, and you decide what it carries.

"Cabin aft," she said. "Meals at second bell. You don't come on deck after dark unless I call for you."

"That's it? No questions?"

"I said no questions, didn't I." She turned back up the gangplank. "Welcome aboard the *Magpie's Debt*. Try to still be alive when we dock."

* * *

She told herself she'd take him because the coin was good. She told herself that all evening, over supper in her cabin, going through the ledgers she kept more carefully than any manifest — debts owed her, favors banked, names worth more than gold in certain ports.

The truth was less comfortable and considerably more interesting.

The truth was that a man wearing both a thief's brand *and* an inquisitor's seal was a walking contradiction, and Odile Grey had built a life on the simple principle that contradictions were where the money lived. You couldn't get the guild mark without having been caught by the guild. You couldn't get the inquisitor's seal without having been *marked* by the Throne's own hunters — and not executed, not questioned and released, but branded. That was new. That was very new. In fifteen years of running contraband past the Throne's checkpoints, she had never once seen their sigil put on a man's skin as anything other than a death sentence deferred.

Which meant somebody in the Inquisitorial Office wanted this one found again. And somebody else — the guild, presumably — wanted him first, or wanted whatever he was carrying.

Felix. He'd given the name Felix when the bosun took his fee, flat as a coin tossed on a table. It fit him about as well as her own name fit her, which was to say well enough for strangers.

He ate alone at second bell, as instructed, sitting in the galley doorway where he could see both hatches. Habit. She noted it. He thanked the cook quietly, ate everything set in front of him without comment or pleasure, and asked only one question the whole meal — whether the river traffic ahead was patrolled or merely tolled. Merely tolled, the cook told him, and Odile saw something in the man's shoulders unknot by a fraction, and then retie itself tighter, because whatever waited in Carrowgate was worse than patrols.

She sent for her quartermaster, Brann, that night. Brann was sixty, missing two fingers, and had once smuggled a bishop out of a besieged city inside a shipment of prayer books, which told you most of what you needed to know about bishops and slightly more than you needed to know about Brann.

"The passenger," she said. "Watch him."

"Thief," Brann said. It wasn't a question; he'd seen the hand too. "Guild-marked, southern chapter. And that other burn—"

"Inquisitor's seal."

Brann was quiet a moment. The lamp swung with the river's slow roll, dragging shadows across his seamed face. "You're thinking bounty."

"I'm thinking value."

"There's a difference?"

"There always is." Odile turned the ledger shut under her palm. "A bounty's a number. Value's a story. Bounties get paid once. Stories pay forever."

"And if his story gets us killed?"

"Then I'll have misread it." She looked up at him. "You've sailed with me eleven years, Brann. How often have I misread one?"

"Twice," Brann said. "The second time cost us the mizzen."

"The mizzen grew back. Watch him."

* * *

She watched him herself, of course. She always watched the interesting ones personally; delegation was for cargo that couldn't lie.

On the third day out, running the narrows below Hallowford, she found her chance. The narrows required every hand on deck and every line singing, and in the controlled chaos of threading a loaded barge between stone teeth, nobody notices one extra body working — especially a body that moves like Felix moved. He didn't wait to be ordered. He read the sail, read the water, put his shoulder into the sweep bar exactly when the sweep needed weight, and said nothing the entire passage except one clipped warning about a deadfall current that Brann confirmed thirty seconds later by feel.

Afterward, dripping spray, coiling lines while the crew laughed and swore at each other in the aftermath-joy of a run survived, he stood apart. They all pretended not to notice. Crews always pretend not to notice the marked man; superstition is cheaper than sympathy.

Odile brought him tea. It was bad tea, from the galley's dregs, but it was an offering, and offerings open mouths faster than questions.

"You've worked a deck before," she said.

"A long time ago."

"Where?"

He wrapped both hands around the cup. His left palm she noticed — the brands — he kept angled toward his own body even now, even holding tea, some old reflex worn deep as the scar tissue. "Doesn't matter."

"Everything matters. That's the dirty secret nobody tells you." She sipped her own cup and watched the far bank slide past, all hazel and haze. "I knew a man with your hands once."

Something in him went very still. Not fear exactly. Recognition — the flinch of a man hearing his own sentence read.

"He ran the memory roads south of the fens," she went on, conversational, mild. "Guild-thief, guild-marked, good at his work. They caught him eventually. The Throne caught him." She let the pause do its work. "They didn't hang him. Do you know why?"

"No," Felix said, and his voice was careful as a man crossing ice.

"Because by the time they caught him, he'd already been caught by someone worse. There are things in this world, thief, that make the guild look like children trading marbles. And the Throne has spent twenty years learning what those things want and how to stand between them and everyone else. A marked man who survives the marking isn't a criminal to them." She finally looked at him directly. "He's evidence."

The river slapped the hull. Somewhere behind them the cook dropped a pot and cursed with real feeling.

"What do you want from me?" Felix asked quietly.

"Nothing yet. That's the point of watching." She drained her tea. "When I know what you're carrying, I'll know whether you're worth more alive than sold. Until then—" she shrugged, "—you work your passage, you keep off my deck after dark, and you don't lie to me badly enough to insult us both."

"And when you decide?"

"Then you'll wish I'd been the sort of captain who decides fast." She took his empty cup. "Get some sleep. Carrowgate in two days, and the city eats men who arrive tired."

* * *

That night she dreamed about the scaffold, which annoyed her, because she had trained herself out of dreaming about the scaffold years ago.

It was the woman this time. The second marked one, the one whose mind they'd emptied on the steps while the crowd sang the Usurper's hymn of cleansing. Odile had stood in that crowd because standing in crowds was how you stayed invisible and because some stubborn animal part of her had refused to look away. The woman had smiled. That was the part the years hadn't scoured out — the smile, serene and empty, on a face that three weeks earlier had begged Odile for passage and wept over a locket containing nothing, because the memory inside the locket had already been taken, and the locket was just a little brass box full of absence.

Odile had taken the job anyway. Cheap jobs for desperate people — that was how she'd started, before she learned that cheap jobs get you killed and expensive ones get you hunted. She'd failed. The riders had taken the woman at the ford, and Odile had run, because running was the other thing she was good at, and the woman had gone to the scaffold smiling an empty smile, and somewhere in the Throne's ledgers there was a line recording the disposition of her memories, itemized like confiscated cargo.

Sable Vane signed those ledgers. Odile knew that now. Had known it for six years, turning it over the way you turn a stone you can't put down. The Inquisitor Vane, cold as ledger-paper, who audited the memory-courts across half the realm.

And now a man sat in her aft cabin wearing Vane's own seal burned into his skin beside a thief's brand, fleeing toward Carrowgate like a man carrying something that both those worlds would burn cities to recover.

Coincidence was for priests. Odile believed in currents, and this current ran straight through her ship.

She went up on deck in the grey hour before dawn, because sleep was done with her, and found Felix there already — breaking her rule, standing at the stern rail with his hands flat on the wood, looking east toward the brightening horizon where Carrowgate's beacon-fires would soon be visible.

"This is the part where you throw me overboard," he said, without turning.

"It's the part where I ask what you're carrying."

"You won't believe me."

"Try me. I've carried stolen bishoprics, forged royal seals, and one actual ghost, though that last one was a misunderstanding about cargo manifests."

He almost laughed. It died young. He turned, and in the pre-dawn grey his face looked older and more exhausted than a man his age should wear, and his left hand came up — deliberately, this time — and opened.

The brands caught the faint light. Thief and inquisitor, twin wounds on one palm, close enough together to touch.

"The first one I earned," he said. "The second one I don't remember getting. I woke up with it after a woman died in my arms in the Hollow, a woman who shouldn't have been able to find the valley at all, carrying a map that burned itself into my hand along with—" he stopped. Started again. "Along with something that isn't mine. Something she trusted me to hold. And there are people shopping in Carrowgate for what I'm holding, and an inquisitor named Vane riding my shadow, and I need to reach the free cities before either of them does. That's what I'm carrying, Captain. That's all I'll say. Ask me more and I'll lie, and you told me not to insult us both."

Odile stood very still. The river ran. The eastern sky went from ash to tarnished gold.

*A map that burns itself into your hand.* She'd heard rumors of such things — spirit-bound memories, witness-sealed, the old rites from before the Usurper's purges, the kind of cargo that didn't go in boxes because boxes weren't loyal enough. Rumors from drunk scholars and frightened couriers, the kind of rumors she'd discounted because discounting them kept the world manageable.

But the seal on his hand was not a rumor. The seal on his hand was the Throne's own signature, and the Throne did not sign things it intended to lose.

"You understand what you're worth," she said slowly. "To Vane. To the guild. To whoever's shopping in Carrowgate. Do you understand it? Because I don't think you do. If I sold you tomorrow, I could buy a fleet."

"Yes," Felix said simply. "So could Beatrix have. She could have bought anything, right up until she gave it away instead."

"And you're telling me you'll do the same. Give away the thing that could buy your life."

"I'm telling you it isn't mine to sell."

There it was. Odile Grey had heard every variety of self-interest the river could float, every rationalization, every bargain, every lie dressed as principle, and she had long ago concluded that the species was not worth saving except in batches. But this hollow-handed thief with two worlds' marks on his palm stood at her rail at dawn and said *it isn't mine to sell* the way other men said *the sun rose*, and something in her chest — the part that still stood in imaginary crowds refusing to look away from scaffolds — sat up and paid attention.

Calculating mercy, Brann called it, when she was in a generous mood and he was feeling brave. Mercy with a ledger attached. She preferred *sound investment.*

"All right," she said. "Here's my price, and it isn't silver. When we dock, you don't disappear into Carrowgate on your own, because you'll be dead by dusk and I'll have wasted a perfectly good curiosity. You use me. My contacts, my routes, my name — it opens doors your brands will nail shut. And in exchange, when you find whoever you're looking for—" she held up a finger before he could speak, "—no, I'm not asking for the secret. I'm asking to be there when it opens. Whatever it is. Whatever it's worth. First sight, captain's privilege."

"Why?" It came out of him rough and genuine. "Why help me? You don't know me."

"No," Odile agreed, turning back toward the wheel, toward the waking ship, toward the bright country opening gold and dangerous before them. "But I knew someone like you once. And I ran."

She paused at the hatch, and did not look back, because captains who look back drown.

"So this time," she said, "I'm staying. Now get below before the crew sees you breaking my rules twice in one lifetime. We make Carrowgate by tomorrow's dusk — and thief, whatever's waiting for you there, I promise you it already knows you're coming."

Below the waterline, faint through the hull timbers, Felix's second heart — the one that wasn't his — chose that exact moment to beat.
