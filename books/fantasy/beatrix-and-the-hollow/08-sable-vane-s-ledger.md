## 8. Sable Vane's Ledger

The smuggler's name was Tam Vess, and he had lasted four hours before he started crying, which Sable Vane considered almost respectable.

She sat across the table from him in the hold of the seized river-barge, a lantern between them turned low so the shadows did the work she didn't want to do with her hands. Not yet. The lantern light made his face look older than it was — mid-thirties, river-tanned, the kind of man who smiled first and counted the cost after. He was chained at the wrists to a ring bolted into the deck beam, and the chains were the least of his problems. The worst of his problems was the ledger open on the table between them, and the fact that his name was in it.

"Read it back to me," she said.

"I already read it." His voice cracked on the second word. He swallowed. "I already told you everything."

"You told me everything you thought would keep your fingers attached. Those are different lists." She turned the ledger so it faced him. Her handwriting was small and even, the handwriting of a woman who had never once written anything she didn't mean. "Read it back. All of it. I want to hear how it sounds in your mouth."

Tam Vess looked at the page. His lips moved. She watched him arrive at the line she'd written an hour ago, in the barge's cabin, while his crew stood on the dock in irons and the river slid past black and indifferent.

*Three runs into the Hollow's margin, paid in guild script, cargo unlisted. Fourth run contracted: one passenger, inbound, no return manifest. Passenger description withheld by broker. Broker: Odile Grey.*

"Say the last line," Vane said.

He said it. His voice came out thin. "Broker: Odile Grey."

"Odile Grey," Vane repeated, as if tasting it. "You know what I like about Odile Grey, Tam?"

He shook his head. Sweat had pasted his hair to his forehead. The lantern guttered and threw his shadow long across the curved hull behind him, so that it looked like a second, larger man hunched over his shoulder, listening.

"She's consistent," Vane said. "Twelve years I've been reading her name out of other people's confessions, and in twelve years she has never once been caught carrying anything she couldn't deny. No contraband. No stolen goods. Nothing but passengers and silence. Do you know what that tells me?"

"That she's careful?"

"That she's *expensive*." Vane closed the ledger with a soft, precise sound. "Careful men get caught. Expensive men get paid. Somebody is paying Odile Grey enough to keep her name off every manifest between here and the coast, and the only cargo worth that kind of money, this season, is the one I'm looking for."

"I never carried anything like that." Tam's voice had found a desperate steadiness, the steadiness of a man reciting. "I ran cloth. Salt. Once a crate of birds, I don't even know what kind of birds—"

"Once," Vane said, "you carried a man."

The word landed. She watched it land. His face did the thing faces did — the small collapse around the eyes, the mouth still trying to hold its shape — and she knew, with the flat certainty that had made her good at this work, that she'd guessed right. There was no man. There had never been a man. But there had been something, and the something was close enough to a man that his conscience had flinched.

"Tell me about the birds," she said.

"The— what?"

"The birds. Tell me about them. Slowly. I find I think better when people tell me about birds."

He told her about the birds. They had been larks, or something like larks, crated for a merchant in Carrowgate who sang to them, and half had died in transit because the crate had been stacked under the salt, and Tam Vess had carried that guilt for two years like a stone in his boot, and she let him talk for six full minutes because every word he said about the birds made it easier for him to say the next true thing, and the next, until—

"It wasn't a man," he said, and stopped.

Vane waited. She was very good at waiting. She had once waited three days in a snowed-in waystation for a thief to finish dying of his wounds so she could take his confession from him while he still had a tongue to give it. Patience was not a virtue for her. It was a tool, and it had an edge.

"It was a box," Tam said. "A memory-box. Sealed. Guild-wax, the red kind, the kind you're not supposed to be able to buy. I was paid to take it upriver and hand it to a ferryman at the old weir, and I wasn't to look at it, and I didn't, I swear on my mother I didn't look—"

"Where is it now?"

"Gone. Handed off. That was—" He counted, lips moving. "Nine days ago. Nine, maybe ten."

Nine days ago. Vane kept her face still, but inside her chest something turned over with a slow, cold pleasure, the way a lock turns when the right key finally finds it. Nine days ago was three days before the Hollow's edge burned. Three days before the courier. Three days before a dying woman pressed a secret into the hands of a thief named Felix Okonkwo and set the whole board moving.

The pieces were on the table now. She could feel them, all of them, arranged in the dark.

* * *

Afterward, when Tam Vess had been taken away to the brig to reconsider the completeness of his confession, Vane stood alone in the hold and let herself do the thing she never did in front of witnesses.

She thought about Elior.

Not his face. She had paid good money — inquisitor's money, ugly money — to have the memory of his face taken out of her own head, back when grief was a thing she still believed could be excised like a tumor, and the surgery had worked exactly as advertised. She knew the facts of his face the way she knew the facts of a city she'd read about in a report. Brown eyes, the records said. A scar through the left eyebrow. He smiled with his whole body, someone had written, someone who had loved him less than she did and survived it better. She could recite the facts. She could not see him. That was the price of the operation, and she had paid it, and she had told herself for nine years that the price was fair.

What the surgeons couldn't take was everything else. The sound of him laughing in the guildhouse courtyard. The weight of his head on her shoulder when he fell asleep reading. The last morning, when he'd walked out through the fog to take a contract she'd begged him not to take, and she had been angry at him — genuinely, stupidly angry — about something small, a broken promise about dinner, and so the last thing she'd ever said to him was *fine, go, see if I wait up.*

She had waited up. She had waited up for nine years.

The guild had stolen his memories when he died — that was the law of their filthy trade, that a thief's takings reverted to the guild, and a thief's mind was just another strongbox. They had cracked him open like a ledger and carried out everything he'd ever taken from anyone, every grief and every first kiss and every name, and then they had sold the pieces, because everything gets sold, that was the first law of the world, and Sable Vane had learned it young and learned it twice.

Somewhere out there, in someone's head, was the memory of Elior's last morning. Of the fog. Of the contract he shouldn't have taken. She had spent nine years and a career buying back fragments — a scrap here, an echo there, a stranger in a market in Carrowgate who'd sold her, without knowing what he had, four seconds of Elior's voice saying her name. Four seconds. She kept them in a memory-box of her own, guild-wax, the red kind, and she did not open it often, because four seconds of a voice is worse than silence. Silence you can live in. Four seconds is a door that only opens a crack, forever.

The guild had done this. The guild had done this to her and to a hundred like her, and the guild had done it under the usurper's charter, and so she had joined the inquisition, because the inquisition was the only knife long enough to reach.

And now the guild's best thief was running through the bright country with a stolen secret in his head, and the secret was the heir, and the heir was the one truth in the world that could burn the guild's charter to ash along with the usurper's throne — and Sable Vane wanted it. She wanted it with a hunger that had nothing to do with duty, and she was honest enough with herself, in the dark of the barge hold, to know the difference.

If the heir lived, the old law died. If the old law died, the guild's charter died with it. And if the charter died, every stolen memory in every vault from here to the coast became contraband — seized, catalogued, *returned*. She had read the old statutes. She'd read them so many times the pages had gone soft. Restoration of stolen goods to the injured party. *The injured party.* Nine years of law school in a single phrase.

She wanted the thief alive. That was the part she could never put in a report. The usurper's order was to kill the courier and burn the secret, and she had burned plenty of valley edges in her time without losing sleep, but this thief — this Felix Okonkwo — was carrying the heir's location in his head, and a head could be opened, and what was inside a head could be *read*, and she had not spent nine years learning the guild's craft from the outside to pass up the chance to read.

She would find him. She would take him. And when she opened him up, she would find what she was looking for — and if, in the shelves of his stolen mind, she happened to find a brown-eyed boy with a scar through his eyebrow, laughing in a courtyard, then she would take that too, and she would not apologize to God or the usurper or her own cold heart.

* * *

Her lieutenant found her on deck an hour past midnight. Renn was a big, careful man, a former river-pilot who had come to the inquisition the way most of them did, sideways and with a debt behind him, and he was one of the three people alive who had seen her open the red memory-box and had never once mentioned it.

"The prisoner's sleeping," Renn said. "Or pretending to."

"He'll talk more by morning. They always do, once they've said the worst thing." Vane stood at the rail, watching the river. The fog was coming up off the water in ropes, and somewhere north, past the fog, past the hills, the Hollow sat in its bowl of forgetting like a coin at the bottom of a well. "Send a rider to Carrowgate. Quiet one. I want Odile Grey's dock watched — her boat, her crew, her wine merchant, everyone. If the thief bought passage, it was from her, and if he bought it from her, he'll come back to her eventually. People always go back to the ones who know what they are."

"And if Grey runs?"

"Then we'll know she's carrying something worth running for." Vane turned. "Renn. The box the prisoner moved — the guild-wax box, upriver to the weir. Nine days ago. Who takes delivery at the old weir?"

"Nobody, officially. The ferryman there is guild-retired. Half-blind." Renn hesitated. "You think it's connected?"

"I think there are no coincidences on a river this small." She looked back north, at the fog. "A guild-wax box moving toward the Hollow three days before a courier comes *out* of it, wounded, carrying the one secret the usurper has spent twenty years trying to drown. Someone put a piece on the board, Renn. Someone inside the guild. I want to know who's playing."

"You think the guild is moving against the usurper?"

"I think the guild moves for whoever pays, and lately I've been wondering who pays *them*." She said it lightly, but she felt the weight of it settle as it left her mouth, the shape of a suspicion she'd been carrying for months and had never said aloud. The guild had let the courier through. The guild had moved a sealed box toward the valley's edge. The guild, whose entire existence depended on the usurper's charter, had begun — quietly, in wax and river-traffic — to hedge.

Which meant someone above her was already playing this game. And Sable Vane had not survived nine years in the inquisition by being anyone's second player.

"Also," she said, "find me everything on a thief named Felix Okonkwo. Guild rolls, contracts, brands, debts. I want to know what he stole before he stole the heir."

Renn nodded and turned to go.

"Renn." He stopped. She kept her eyes on the fog. "The records will say he was born in the Hollow. They always say that about Hollow thieves. Check anyway. Check his intake date. Check what was logged when they first marked him." A pause, and then, because Renn had earned it: "Check whether the guild ever took anything *from* him."

"You think they did?"

"I think everyone in this business is carrying something that isn't theirs." She heard him walk away, boots soft on the wet deck. "The trick is finding out what it costs them to give it back."

The fog thickened. Somewhere upriver, a night bird called — a lark, maybe, or something like one — and Sable Vane stood at the rail with her ledger under her arm and her four seconds of a dead man's voice locked in red wax against her heart, and began, with the patience of nine years, to plan a theft of her own.
