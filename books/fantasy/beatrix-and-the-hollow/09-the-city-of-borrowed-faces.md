## 9. The City of Borrowed Faces

Carrowgate announced itself before Felix saw it — a smell first, salt and tar and frying onions riding a wind that had never once blown through the Hollow. Then the sound, a low animal roar of ten thousand lives overlapping, and only last the sight: white walls climbing out of a green bay, roofs tiled in blue and copper, ships stacked along the quays like knives in a drawer.

He stood on the road above the harbor gate for a long moment, letting it hit him. He'd crossed into the bright country three days ago with Odile Grey's barge, and he still hadn't gotten used to *this* — the sheer unguarded volume of the world. In the Hollow, everything was muffled. Here, everything insisted.

"You're gawking," Odile said beside him. She'd tied her black hair back under a fisherman's cap, which fooled no one; her crew gave her the wide berth of men who knew exactly who she was.

"I'm memorizing," Felix said. "It's what I do."

"Mm." She looked him over the way she did every morning since the ash roads — a quick inventory, checking he was still whole. "Rules. We dock at the Tanners' Stair because the harbormaster at the main quay sells names to anyone with coin, and I'd rather not have mine sold today. You stay within shouting distance. You don't talk to priests, tax collectors, or pretty strangers."

"What about ugly strangers?"

"They'll talk to *you*. Thief-brands travel." Her eyes flicked to his left hand, where the guild mark sat under its wrap, and then — briefly, unwillingly — to the other mark, the one burned beside it on the road out of Ashfall, the inquisitor's sigil that Vane's rider had pressed into his skin like a signature on a debt. Two brands. Two worlds hunting him. "You pawn something small, you buy food and rope and a second cloak, and you meet me at the Saltcellar by dusk. Not after dusk. Dusk is when Carrowgate starts lying about itself."

"And if I'm late?"

"Then I assume you're dead or rich," Odile said, "and either way I sail lighter without you." But she said it in the tone she'd been using lately, the one that meant the opposite of its words, and walked off toward the stair without waiting for an answer.

Felix watched her go and felt the strange new weight of being someone someone else checked on.

* * *

The memory-market opened at noon in a courtyard behind the Chandelier, a temple so old nobody remembered what it chandeliered. It was called the Courtyard of Honest Coin, which was the first lie — nothing there was honest and very little was coin. Traders sat at folding tables under striped awnings, and between them drifted the dream-sellers, men and women with silver thimbles on their fingertips who could pull a memory loose and pour it into glass vials that glowed faintly, like drowned stars.

Felix had grown up around stolen memories. The Hollow traded them like grain. But the Hollow's trade was furtive, ashamed, conducted in whispers over kitchen tables. This was commerce. There were *prices chalked on slate*. There was a child selling memories of snow to a merchant who had clearly never seen it, and the child was bored.

He found a broker with kind eyes and a ledger and sat down across from him.

"Buying or selling?"

"Selling. One memory. Small."

"Small is my favorite size." The broker laced his fingers. "What have you?"

Felix reached inward, past the second heartbeat lodged behind his ribs — Beatrix's gift, or curse, the sealed thing that had carried him this far and would kill him if it broke open wrong — and found the memory he'd chosen on the barge. A minor one. Safe. The taste of his mother's bread, or what he believed was his mother's bread; he'd stolen it from a baker in the Hollow years ago and told himself it was his. It was warm and ordinary and entirely replaceable.

"This," he said, and held it up the way the traders here did, cupping it invisibly between his palms.

The broker's eyes went distant, tasting it. "Bread. Childhood. Authentic?" He glanced up sharply, the way a jeweler checks for paste. "Not reconstructed?"

"Genuine theft," Felix said. "I was there."

"Hm. Eight marks."

"It's warm bread in winter. Twelve."

"Ten, and I don't ask why a man sells the taste of his own childhood."

Because it isn't mine, Felix almost said. Because I woke up one day in the Hollow knowing how to steal and not knowing why I was the kind of person who could. Instead he said, "Eleven and a rope."

"Eleven and half a rope." The broker drew a vial from his coat, set it on the table between them, and put a silver thimble on his finger. "This will sting less than you fear."

It stung more. It always did — the little tearing sensation, like a thread pulled out of cloth, the moment of *what was I just thinking about?* And then the memory was in the glass, glowing soft amber, and Felix was looking at it from outside: some other man's kitchen, some other woman's hands dusted with flour, and a boy at the table whose face he couldn't quite —

The broker capped the vial. "Pleasure."

Felix counted out nothing and received eleven marks and three feet of rope, and sat there a moment feeling the small new hole inside himself, the missing tooth of the mind. That was the trade. Everyone in the courtyard wore their holes differently — the merchant buying snow had a hungry look; the dream-sellers had so many holes they moved through the world like sieves.

He bought the rope, the cloak, dried fish, a wax-sealed lantern. He haggled badly because he kept getting distracted by things the Hollow had never contained: a fountain that ran wine on feast days; a wall covered in handbills, one of which read **LOST: SEVEN YEARS, REWARD**. He was reading that one, feeling an unpleasant kinship, when it happened.

A stranger's memory leaked.

* * *

It happened sometimes in the markets — a vial cracked, a thimble slipped, a seller careless — and the spilled memory hung in the air like perfume, catching anyone standing close. Felix had felt two already that day: a sailor's terror in a storm (he'd stepped back, heart slamming) and a bride's joy (worse, somehow; he'd had to sit down).

But this leak came from a man brushing past him at the handbill wall — tall, gray-cloaked, moving with the flat economy of someone paid to be unnoticed — and it wasn't spilled by accident. It was *shielded* badly, deliberately half-open, the way a fisherman drags a glittering lure.

Felix felt it anyway. Everyone nearby felt it. That was the trick of it: a lure only works on the fish that wants the bait.

The leaked memory showed a face.

A young woman's face, seen from above and at a distance, lit by candlelight in a stone room — sharp-boned, dark-haired, ordinary enough except for the eyes, which even in a stranger's recollection seemed to be looking directly at whoever remembered her. And wrapped around the face like wire, the context: *the heir, the last heir, the one the usurper fears, hidden in the north refuges, the face that must be found.*

Felix stopped breathing.

Because he knew that face.

Not from Beatrix's sealed memory — that stayed locked behind his ribs, silent as a stone in a well. He knew it from the map burned into his palm, from the fragments that leaked on bad nights, from the fevered trust of a dying courier. The candlelit face in the stranger's memory was Wren's face — no. No, he didn't know the name yet, wouldn't for days. But he knew the *face*, the same face that surfaced in his dreams wearing Beatrix's voice, saying *keep it safe, keep it safe, keep it safe.*

Someone else had seen her. Someone else was carrying the shape of her. Which meant someone else knew — or suspected, or was being fed suspicions — and had come to this market to do what Felix had done: shop for the rest.

He turned. The gray-cloaked man was eight feet away, studying the handbills with theatrical idleness. Up close Felix caught details the leak had been designed to hide: boots too good for a clerk, a sword knot worn smooth by use, and on the inside of his wrist, glimpsed as he raised a hand to the bills — a tattoo. A closed eye.

Vane's sigil. The inquisitors' mark.

Cold walked down Felix's spine and settled in his knees. He made himself breathe. Made himself look past the man at the handbills, at LOST: SEVEN YEARS, REWARD, and think like a thief instead of a rabbit. The man wasn't looking for him — couldn't be, or the trap would already have sprung. The lure had been cast for the heir's secret, drifting in the market like chum, waiting for anything carrying the true memory to bite. Waiting for a face to flicker. For breath to catch. For a marked hand to tremble.

Which meant Felix's only job was to be boring.

He yawned. He scratched his jaw. He read the bill about the lost seven years with the mild interest of a man with nowhere better to be, and he began — slowly, casually, heart hammering against the sealed thing in his chest as if it knew — to drift down the wall away from the gray-cloaked man.

Behind him, softly, the man said to no one in particular: "Strange market. Half these memories are forged, and the other half are bait."

Felix kept walking.

"The clever ones," the voice continued, conversational, pitched just loud enough, "can tell the difference. Can you tell the difference, friend?"

There were three ways out of the courtyard: the arch he'd entered by, a narrow alley behind the wine fountain, and a gap between two awnings where the dream-sellers clustered. The alley was closest. Thieves' rule: never take the route your hunter is watching you notice. So Felix took the alley, at a walk, counting his heartbeats, and the gray man did not follow — but at the mouth of the alley Felix passed another man coming in, and this one wore the closed eye too, inked openly on his throat like a man among friends.

Two of them. Casting one lure. Which meant a third somewhere watching the exits, which meant the Courtyard of Honest Coin was not a marketplace today. It was a net.

And the net had nearly caught him — not because of his brands, not because of his face, but because the usurper's people were shopping for the heir in the same aisle he was.

They weren't hunting Felix. They were hunting what he carried. And they were closer than Sable Vane had ever been.

* * *

He didn't go straight to the Saltcellar. He went the long way, twice across his own trail, down into the fish-reeking lanes below the Tanners' Stair, and only when the afternoon light had gone bronze did he let himself climb toward the tavern, working through it as he walked.

They had a fragment. A face, a rumor of northern refuges. Fragments were dangerous but findable — Carrowgate was a city of borrowed faces, and somewhere in it was a broker who had sold that gray man his piece, and brokers kept ledgers, and ledgers could be read. His own guild had taught him that much before it taught him nothing else.

Worse: if the inquisitors were casting lures in the open markets, then the usurper's court believed the heir was alive and near. The hunt had stopped being a search and become a sweep. Every day Felix spent haggling over rope was a day the net tightened around the north — around whatever refuge the map in his palm pointed to, around whoever lived there, candlelit and unaware.

*Keep it safe.* Beatrix's voice, in the fever, in the leaks. He'd thought she meant carry it. He was beginning to understand she'd meant something larger and more terrible: keep *her* safe. The girl with the eyes that looked back.

The Saltcellar smelled of brine and bad beer, and Odile was at a corner table with her boots up and a knife she was cleaning for the fourth time, which was how she waited. She read his face before he sat down.

"Trouble."

"Inquisitors in the memory-market. Two I saw, casting a leaked memory as bait — the heir's face."

Odile's knife went still. For a moment the tavern noise seemed to bend around their table. "In Carrowgate. Openly."

"Tattoos and all. They're not hiding."

"That's worse than hiding." She put the knife away and leaned forward, and Felix watched the smuggler arithmetic run behind her eyes — cargo, crew, risk, reward — and watched, again, something else arrive after the arithmetic, something that hadn't been there a month ago. "What did you do?"

"Nothing. I was boring and I left."

"Boring." She snorted. "You. What did it cost you?"

"A memory about bread." He flexed his left hand, the wrapped brand itching. "It wasn't mine anyway."

Something flickered across her face at that — the question she'd been not-asking since the ash roads, the question of what exactly this hollow-handed thief was carrying and why he flinched when anyone mentioned the royal line. She didn't ask it. She stood instead, dropping coins on the table.

"Dusk is when Carrowgate starts lying about itself," she said. "And tonight it's going to lie hard. We move tonight — north, ahead of the sweep, before those closed-eye bastards finish their shopping. Get your rope."

Felix rose. And as he did, he caught it — across the tavern, by the door, a flash of gray cloth and the glint of a tattooed wrist as someone stepped back out into the street. Not following. Reporting.

They'd found him after all. Or they'd found *her* captain, and through her the thief with two brands, and now the net knew exactly which thread to pull.

Odile saw it too. He watched her see it, watched her decide — fast, final — and she said, quietly, "Back way. Now."

Felix put his hand on the rope, on the coins, on the small amber hole where a bakery used to be, and followed her into the dark, while behind them Carrowgate began, lamp by lamp, to light itself up for the hunt.
