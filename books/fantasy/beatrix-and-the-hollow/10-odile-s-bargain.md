## 10. Odile's Bargain

Odile Grey had learned long ago that the truth came out of people the way blood came out of wounds — under pressure, in spurts, and never quite as much as you needed. So she didn't press Felix Okonkwo. Not that night, not the next morning. She fed him, paid for his bunk aboard the *Marrow*, and let him think she had no interest in him beyond his coin.

Coin was, of course, the least interesting thing about him.

She watched him now from the stern, where he stood with his hands wrapped around a mug of tea he hadn't drunk, watching Carrowgate's harbor lights smear across the black water. The city at night did things to newcomers — made them believe it was beautiful, which was the first lie it told, or safe, which was the second. Felix wasn't buying either one. Good sign. Bad men bought.

What interested her was his hands.

She'd seen the thief's brand on the left palm two days ago when he'd signed the passage contract — an old mark, guild-made, the kind they pressed into children because children's skin took ink like forgiveness. She'd said nothing. But yesterday, hauling rope, he'd flinched when the line bit his right hand, and she'd gotten her first clean look at the burn there: a sigil she knew, and wished she didn't. The triple-crescent of the Inquisitor's Office, seared into living flesh like a brand on livestock.

One man. Two marks. Hunted by the guild that raised him and the office that owned the law.

In Odile's experience, there were exactly three kinds of people who ended up carrying both those marks, and two of them were already dead.

"You're staring," Felix said, without turning around.

"I'm appraising."

"Of what?"

"Whether you're worth the trouble you're going to cause me."

He turned then, and gave her the look she'd come to expect from him — flat, careful, the look of a man counting exits. Hollow Country folk all wore it, but on him it sat differently. On him it looked less like caution and more like grief wearing caution's clothes.

"And what does your appraisal say?" he asked.

"That you're a terrible liar," Odile said, climbing the steps to join him at the rail. "Which is either a virtue or a fatal condition. I haven't decided."

"I haven't lied to you."

"No," she agreed. "You've just answered questions I didn't ask with great precision, and gone quiet every time anyone mentions the north road. You've done nothing *but* not lie to me, Okonkwo, and it's exhausting to watch."

A barge slid past below them, lanterns strung along its rails, some merchant's idea of romance. Felix watched it go with an expression she couldn't read, and Odile felt the old itch rise in her — the one that meant value, the one that had kept her crew fed through three famines and two wars by telling her when something was worth more than its asking price.

Everything about this man was worth more than his asking price.

"Here's what I know about you," she said, leaning her forearms on the rail. "You came out of the Hollow, which nobody does, carrying a wound nobody can see. You pawned a memory at the Saltmarket for enough coin to buy passage you could have paid twice over, which means you wanted the transaction, not the money. Memories are currency everywhere else, but where you come from they're the *only* currency, so you know exactly what you sold and you miss it already." She paused. "And you have the Inquisitor's mark burned into your hand, which means Sable Vane herself caught you and let you go. Vane doesn't let anything go. Which means she wants you to run somewhere. And I'd very much like to know where."

Felix set the untouched tea on the rail. For a long moment he looked at her, and she watched him do the same arithmetic she did — weighing what a secret cost against what it might buy.

"The Hollow doesn't have markets," he said finally. "It has trades. You give someone your grief, they give you bread. It seems fair until you've done it enough times that there's nothing left in you to trade." His voice stayed level, but his thumb moved over the thief's brand, back and forth, like a man worrying a scar on someone else's hand. "I don't miss anything I sold. That's rather the point of selling."

"Liar," Odile said, almost gently.

"*There's* my honesty."

They stood in silence while the tide turned beneath them. Somewhere across the harbor, the dream-market was opening for the night shift — she could see the greenish glow of it above the warehouse district, memory-traders hawking other people's sunsets and first kisses. Felix wouldn't look at it. He'd been refusing to look at it for two days, with the discipline of a man who knew exactly what looking would cost.

That, more than anything, told her he was carrying something live.

"Here's my offer," Odile said.

"I knew there'd be one."

"There always is. Listen anyway." She kept her voice low, businesslike, though the deck was nearly empty. "You want to go north. You've been pricing river passages to Thornmere without asking me, which is insulting, since I own half the boats you've been asking about. North of Thornmere is the Ashfall country, and past that, the borderlands where the old royal refuges were built before the war. Nobody goes there. Nobody who comes back goes back twice."

Felix had gone very still. It was the stillness of confirmation, and Odile felt the itch sharpen into certainty.

"I know those roads," she continued. "I've run cargo through the borderlands since before you had that brand. I can take you where you're going, past every checkpoint, past every rider Vane posts on the ash roads. Three weeks' travel instead of two months alive instead of probably dead."

"And the price?"

"The map."

The word landed between them and neither picked it up. Felix's face did something complicated — not surprise, she noticed. More like a man hearing his own fear spoken aloud in someone else's voice.

"I don't have a map," he said.

"Mm," said Odile.

"I don't know what you're—"

"Felix." She said his name the way she'd learned to say it, plain and unadorned, no warmth and no threat. "I've smuggled everything this world has ever decided to make illegal. Weapons, saints, survivors, secrets. Do you know how I tell what a man carries? Not by searching him." She tapped her own temple. "By watching where he won't look. You guard your right hand like it's a reliquary. You sleep with it closed. Last night you talked in your sleep, and you weren't dreaming — you were *reciting*. Directions. Distances. A voice that wasn't yours giving bearings to somewhere in the borderlands."

She watched the color leave his face and return, watched him master it. Brave, then. Or practiced. Possibly both.

"So here's the bargain," she said. "You give me the map — whatever form it takes, wherever it's written — and I give you the roads. My ship, my crew, my name at the checkpoints. Fair trade. A secret for a survival."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you can buy a river boat to Thornmere with what's left of your pawned memories, and I'll read about what happens to you in whichever broadsheet gets the story first." She shrugged. "I'm not threatening you, Okonkwo. Threats are for people who matter to me. You're cargo. Valuable cargo, but cargo."

Something flickered in his eyes at that — something that might have been relief. She understood it. Men like Felix could carry anything except being cared about; care was a debt they had no way to repay.

"Why?" he asked quietly. "Why would you trade three weeks and your whole crew's safety for a map to nowhere? What's in it for you?"

And there it was — the question she'd been waiting for, the honest one. Odile considered lying, found she didn't want to, and settled somewhere between.

"Twenty years ago I ran with a man who got marked the way you're marked," she said. "Guild-brand on one hand, inquisitor's seal on the other. He carried something for someone, and he wouldn't give it up, and they erased him for it. Not killed — erased. There isn't a record he lived. I remember him, and I'm apparently the only one." She looked out at the water rather than at Felix. "I've never known what he carried. Some nights I think knowing would be worth everything I have. Other nights I think I'm the only person left who remembers him at all, and if I learn what killed him, I stop being his remembrance and start being another person who used his secret."

She turned back to him. "So. Map for the roads. Yes or no."

For a long moment Felix looked at his hands — both of them, branded and burned — and Odile watched him weigh it with everything he had. She saw the moment the arithmetic finished, saw him arrive at the answer he'd clearly arrived at a hundred times already, alone, on her deck, in the dark.

"No," he said.

Just that. No performance, no apology. He met her eyes and held them, and Odile had to admit — to herself, in the privacy of her own skull — that it took more spine than most men managed.

"It's not mine to give," he went on. "It was given to me to carry, not to trade. The woman who gave it died believing I'd keep it. That's the whole of my fortune, Captain Grey. It doesn't convert."

"A man could get rich on a secret like yours."

"A man could get dead on it too. Rich is optional. Dead has been following me since the gate."

"And if Vane catches you in the borderlands without me?"

"Then I'll be dead owing you nothing," Felix said, "which is more than most people can promise their creditors."

Odile laughed before she could stop herself — a short, real laugh, the first in weeks. Gods, but he was a stubborn piece of Hollow Country oak. She'd offered him the deal straight, no knife behind it, and he'd refused it clean, and now she had a choice to make, the same choice she'd made once before, twenty years ago, standing on a different deck looking at a different marked man.

She could push. Or she could wait.

Pushing got you the secret and lost you the man. Waiting risked losing both — but sometimes, if the current was right, it delivered a man to your side who'd walk into fire for you later, not because you'd bought him but because you'd *waited*.

"All right," she said.

Felix blinked. "All right?"

"All right. No deal. Keep your map, keep your directions, keep whatever's beating inside your right hand like a second heart." She pushed off the rail and headed for the companionway, then paused at the top step. "We sail with the morning tide anyway."

"We do?"

"Cargo contracts, Okonkwo. I signed you through to Thornmere. A captain who breaks her own charter over a refused bargain isn't a captain, she's a kidnapper with paperwork." She smiled, thin and true. "You'll want to sleep. The estuary currents are ugly before dawn."

He watched her go, suspicion warring with exhaustion on his face, and Odile descended the steps feeling the itch settle into something rarer than certainty.

Because here was the thing she hadn't told him, the thing she'd keep folded away like a forged bill: she hadn't believed a word of his refusal, and she hadn't believed a word of his reason, and none of it mattered. She'd watched him refuse to sell the secret even when selling might have saved him, and that refusal told her more than any confession could have. Whatever he carried, it wasn't treasure to him. It was a trust. And men who treated trusts like trusts were the only men in this rotten world worth sailing north for.

She'd take him to the borderlands whether he asked or not. She'd watch him, and shield him when shielding was cheap, and decide later what his secret was worth — to her, to the free cities, to the ghost of a man whose name only she remembered.

Let him think she believed him. Belief was cheap. Proximity was everything.

On deck above her, she heard Felix cross to the far rail, heard him open his closed right hand — she knew the small sound of it, skin unsticking from skin — and hold it out over the dark water, as if testing whether tonight would be the night it finally burned through him entirely.

* * *

Two hours before dawn, the boy came aboard.

Odile was awake because she was always awake two hours before dawn; sleep and she had parted ways years ago with no ill feelings and no forwarding address. She was in the galley nursing the first bitter pot of tea when Tam, her bow-watch, hissed down the hatch, and she came up onto the deck in time to see a figure scramble aboard across the mooring lines — small, quick, hooded, moving like someone who'd done this exact climb a hundred times and been shot at during at least ten.

She had a knife at the intruder's throat before the hood came back, revealing a girl of maybe seventeen, sharp-faced, salt-haired, breathing hard.

"Captain Grey," the girl said, not flinching from the blade. Word traveled fast in Carrowgate; that alone told Odile plenty. "You're taking the Hollow man north."

"I'm taking cargo north," Odile said. "Who sent you?"

"Nobody." The girl's eyes were wide and furious and terrified all at once, the eyes of someone running on a fuel that doesn't last. "Nobody sent me. That's the point. Nobody sends anybody anymore, everybody's bought, everybody's watched—" She stopped herself, swallowed, started over. "There are riders on the north road already. Grey cloaks. They left last night. They know the route through the fens, the one only your crew knows. Somebody sold it."

Cold moved down Odile's spine, slow and deliberate, like water finding its level. Only four people knew the fen route. Two were asleep aboard. One was standing in front of her.

And one was currently topside on a borrowed ship in Carrowgate harbor, holding a secret everyone in the world seemed willing to kill for.

"Captain," the girl said urgently. "The grey riders — they're not hunting the Hollow man. That's what nobody understands. They're herding him. Every road south is open and every road north is closing, one by one." Her voice dropped. "Somebody wants him to run out of options. Somebody wants him to have nowhere left to turn except to *them*."

Behind Odile, the hatch opened. Felix came up on deck in his shirt, barefoot, hair flattened from sleep, and stopped dead at the sight of them.

"What," he said slowly, "is that?"

Odile looked at the girl, at her knife still wet with harbor fog, at the east going grey at the edges, at the tide already turning without her.

"That," she said, "is the sound of our departure moving up. Tam! All hands! We cast off in twenty minutes!"

She turned back to Felix, who was staring at the girl with the expression of a man discovering that the world had gotten smaller overnight.

"Get your boots on, Okonkwo," she said. "It seems we're leaving early — and it seems somebody out there already knows exactly where we're going."
