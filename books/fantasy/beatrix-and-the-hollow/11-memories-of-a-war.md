## 11. Memories of a War

The leak started on the third night out of the Bright Country, somewhere between the river crossings and the ash roads, while Odile Grey's barge crawled south under a sky the color of old tin.

Felix had learned to keep the bound memory quiet the way you learn to keep a knife from cutting your own hand — carefully, constantly, with an attention that never quite let him sleep. It sat behind his left eye like a coal wrapped in wool. Warm. Patient. Waiting for him to be tired enough, or honest enough, or stupid enough to leave a door open.

That night he was all three.

They had made camp on the barge itself, tied up in a stand of drowned willows where the river widened and slowed and pretended to be a lake. Odile posted her boy Tam on watch and went below with a bottle she didn't offer to share. The fire was small. The fog of home felt very far away, and Felix sat with his palms open toward it and let himself think — for just one unguarded breath — about Beatrix.

Her hand pressing the map into his. Her voice cracking on *please*.

The door opened.

* * *

He did not fall over, though he felt himself fall. His body stayed folded by the fire, breathing, hands slack in his lap, while the rest of him went elsewhere — went backward, which was impossible, because backward wasn't a place. Except that it was, and he was falling into it now, into someone else's backward, into eyes that were not his and a heart hammering a rhythm he'd never owned.

---

Fire.

Not the cookfire — a city burning, seen from a tower, and Felix knew the tower because the man whose eyes he wore had climbed its stairs ten thousand times. He knew the man's name before he thought to ask it: **Aldemar**, captain of the eastern gate, sworn shield to King Emrys the Third of the line of Vael, and Felix was inside that knowledge the way water is inside a wave — not watching it, being it.

Below, the capital of Vaelmark burned in the particular way cities burn when the fires are set deliberately, quarter by quarter, according to a plan. Aldemar could see the pattern from here. He had helped draw a version of it once, as a training exercise, a war game on slate.

Someone had played it for real.

*Aldemar.* A woman's voice behind him, ragged. *Aldemar, they're through the river gate.*

He turned, and Felix felt the turning in his own neck, saw the woman through Aldemar's eyes and loved her through Aldemar's heart so suddenly and so completely that Felix gasped inside the borrowed chest. She was young. She was bleeding from a cut along her jaw. She was Queen Maren of Vael, twenty-two years old, and she was holding a child — a girl, perhaps four, dark-eyed, silent with the terrible silence children find when the world breaks.

*How many?* Aldemar heard himself ask.

*All of them,* the queen said. *It isn't an army, it's a tide. And Aldemar —* her voice dropped to something worse than fear; it was disbelief — *it's Corvane. It's Corvane's banners at the river gate.*

The name landed in Aldemar's chest like a blade going into water — no resistance at first, then everything at once. Lord Corvane. Warden of the Marches. The king's closest friend. The man who had held Aldemar's son at his christening. The man who had dined in this very castle three weeks ago and laughed, actually laughed, at the king's jokes, and asked after trade routes and grain stores with the careful diligence of a good administrator.

Grain stores. Aldemar remembered that dinner now with new eyes — remembered how Corvane had asked where the granaries stood, where the garrison slept, which gates were manned thinnest during the harvest feasts. Remembered how he'd smiled.

*Get her out,* Aldemar said, and heard his own voice go flat and dead the way voices do when feeling has been amputated to save the rest of the body. *Both of them. There's a postern under the east wall. My sergeant holds it.*

*And you?*

*The gate garrison is mine. Every minute I hold them is a minute you ride.* He took the queen's shoulders, felt the child's small weight shift against her hip, and said what he did not yet know was true: *I'll follow.*

---

He did not follow. Felix knew that already — knew it the way you know the shape of a story from its silhouette — but knowing did nothing to blunt the moment when Aldemar fought his last fight and understood, in the middle of it, that he had always known.

The east courtyard. Forty men against a hundred, and Aldemar's sword arm doing what forty years of drill had trained it to do without consulting his heart. Around him his garrison died in ones and twos, and he catalogued each death with the awful bookkeeping of a commander — *Tam of the Mill, down. The Vess brothers, down. Sergeant Hara, down* — while above the courtyard the palace banner caught fire and came down like a dying bird.

And through it all, in some chamber of Aldemar's mind that even now refused to panic, a question kept repeating: *why*. Not why the betrayal — there were always reasons, ambition and grievance and the sweet rot of almost-kingdoms waiting to fall. But why now, why this way, why burn the whole city when taking the throne only required taking the palace?

He got his answer in the worst possible currency.

Corvane himself came into the courtyard near the end, unhurried, boots crunching ash, and his soldiers made a corridor for him the way water makes way for a keel. Up close he looked exactly as he had at dinner three weeks ago. That was the horror of it. Nothing had changed in his face. The face had known the whole time.

*Aldemar,* Corvane said, warm as ever. *You're making my men work.*

*Where is the king?*

*Dead since noon.* Said gently, like news of weather. *Your queen will be dead by morning unless your sergeant talks. They usually talk.* He stepped closer, and something moved behind his eyes that Aldemar had never seen there before, and would spend his final minutes understanding. *You want to know the truth of it, captain? You've earned that much. Ask your question. I can see it eating you.*

*Why burn the city? You've won. Why the whole city?*

And Corvane sighed — sighed, like a teacher disappointed by a bright student — and gave the answer that would echo down a hundred years and land, eventually, in the skull of a thief from a valley that forgets:

*Because a throne taken from a living line is a debt. A throne taken from a dead one is a fact. There must be nothing left to rally around, Aldemar. No heir, no relic, no story. I am not stealing a crown tonight. I am erasing an argument.*

He said it without pride. That was the part that curdled Aldemar's blood, standing there in his own blood, listening. Corvane spoke of erasing a royal line the way a mason speaks of leveling ground before laying a foundation. Necessary work. Regrettable, perhaps, in the abstract.

*But the child,* Aldemar said, and watched Corvane's stillness confirm everything. *You missed one.*

For the first time, the warden's warmth cracked. Just a hairline. Just enough.

*Where is the princess, captain?*

Aldemar smiled. He felt his own smile through Felix's borrowed mouth, felt the joy of it, vicious and clean, the last possession of a dying man. *Ride faster, my lord.*

They killed him there. It took a long time, and he spent most of it thinking about his son's christening, and the weight of the child in Corvane's arms, and how completely a man can know another man for thirty years without ever once seeing him.

The last thing Aldemar saw was the sky, going grey with smoke, and the last thing he thought — the thought that was the treasure, the cargo, the secret that a courier named Beatrix would someday carry across the world with a wound that wouldn't close — was not about the battle or the burning or the throne.

It was: *the princess lives, and one day, someone will have to decide whether telling the world so is mercy or murder.*

Then the tower of memory folded inward, and Felix fell back into himself.

* * *

He came back to the fire with his lungs full and his hands shaking and the taste of ash in his mouth — real ash, or remembered ash, he couldn't say. The drowned willows dripped. Tam stood his watch at the bow, bored and oblivious. Somewhere below, Odile's bottle clinked against a cup.

Felix was a thief. He had taken griefs and joys and first kisses and deathbed regrets out of other people's heads more times than he could count, and he had never once been wounded by the merchandise. Memories were goods. You weighed them, you priced them, you handed them over. What belonged to someone else's life stayed their property even after it lived briefly in yours.

This was different. This hadn't been a theft. This was a leak — the bound memory straining against whatever knot Beatrix had tied around it, spilling itself into him because his guard was down and the thing inside him wanted, needed, *insisted* on being understood.

*I know why now,* he whispered to the fire. To Beatrix, if she could hear him, wherever ghost-bound memories hear. *I know why you ran instead of hiding. Nobody hides this. This doesn't fit in a hole in the ground.*

Because he had understood it, in the space of one dead captain's lifetime. He had spent nine days thinking of the map as a secret — a location, a name, a commodity, the kind of thing you sold to buy survival. He had imagined Sable Vane wanting it because her masters wanted it, the way anyone wants anything: possession, advantage, power.

But Corvane's heirs — and there were heirs now, a dynasty five generations deep sitting uneasily on a stolen argument — they didn't want the location of the last true heir the way thieves want gold.

They wanted it the way a man wants the one witness who can undo him.

The princess had lived. Aldemar's smuggled child had grown, and her descendants had kept the line breathing somewhere in the world's cracks for a hundred years, and every year of those hundred years, the usurper's throne had rested on a lie that a single living proof could crack wide open. Not a war over territory. Not a succession dispute. An *argument* — Corvane's word, and it was precisely right. Reveal the heir and you didn't start a war. You revealed that the previous war had been founded on erasure, that every oath sworn to the Corvane line was sworn to nothing, and the whole structure — armies, oaths, provinces, the entire architecture of obedience — became a building with no foundation, waiting to see if anyone pushed.

Felix put his head between his knees and breathed.

He had carried stolen things before, but he had never carried something that could move borders. And the truly sickening realization, the one that made his stomach roll over, was this: the memory hadn't shown him the princess's face. It had shown him the queen's face, the child's dark eyes, the tower, the fire — but the bound memory's actual cargo, the part Beatrix had guarded, the part that mattered — the hiding place, the current heir — had stayed knotted tight. The leak was the context. The warning.

*Understand what you carry,* the leak said. *Understand what happens if you sell it short.*

Because that was the temptation sitting in his chest like a second, colder heart: he could still sell it. Vane had offered. Others would offer more. A location, delivered quietly, and Felix Okonkwo walked away rich and free and alive, and the argument stayed buried, and the world went on standing the way it had stood for a hundred years — on a lie, but standing.

And if he told the truth instead —

He thought of Aldemar smiling with his split lips. *Ride faster, my lord.* One man's defiance, bought at the price of everything, buying nothing but time. Time for a four-year-old to disappear into history. Time for this exact night to become necessary, a century later, on a barge in drowned willows, inside a thief who had never wanted to matter.

The fire had burned low. Felix fed it a stick and watched the sparks climb, and tried to imagine being the person the memory pointed toward — some heir, some stranger, alive right now, asleep somewhere, not knowing they were the answer to a question a dead warden had spent a kingdom to bury.

*One day, someone will have to decide whether telling the world is mercy or murder.*

Aldemar had left the decision to the future. Generous of him. Dead men made generous legators.

Above him, bare feet on wet deck. Odile emerged from the cabin, bottle in hand, hair loose, and stopped when she saw his face. Whatever she found there made her crouch slowly across the fire, studying him with the flat professional attention she usually saved for cargo manifests.

"You look," she said, "like a man who opened a letter he shouldn't have."

"Something showed me its insides."

"The thing you're carrying." Not a question. She'd guessed at it days ago; he'd seen her guessing. "It leaked?"

"How do you know that word?"

She took a pull from the bottle, and for a moment her eyes went somewhere else — somewhere with a ledger in it, a name crossed out — and came back. "I've moved cargo that whispers," she said. "Memory-freight, once, years back. It leaks when it wants something from you." She turned the bottle in her hands. "So. What does it want?"

Felix looked at the fire. He thought about lying, which was his native language, and found he was too tired to be fluent.

"To be delivered," he said. "And it wants me to understand that delivering it is going to break something. Maybe just me. Maybe everything."

Odile was quiet for a long moment. Then she stood, and the moment of strange openness closed over her like water over a stone.

"Well," she said. "Sleep fast, thief. We make the ash roads at dawn, and there are riders on them that don't ask questions twice."

She went back below. Felix lay down on hard deck with the coal behind his eye pulsing warm and patient, and stared up at a sky he couldn't steal, and did not sleep.

Somewhere south, past the drowned willows, the ash roads waited — and on them, though he could not yet know it, Sable Vane's riders were already closing like a hand.
