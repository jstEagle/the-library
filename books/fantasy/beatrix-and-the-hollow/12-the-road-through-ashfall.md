## 12. The Road Through Ashfall

**Felix**

The ash roads were named honestly, which was more than you could say for anything else in the world outside the Hollow.

For three days they had traveled a corridor of gray. The trees on either side of the old highway stood like things that had died mid-scream, their branches stripped and bleached, and the ground crunched underfoot with the fine white residue of some ancient burning that no one Odile would talk to could explain. The sky above was the color of dirty wool. Felix kept expecting the fog of home to swallow it, and it never did, and that absence sat in his chest like a pulled tooth.

"You keep looking up," Odile said.

"I keep expecting the fog."

"The fog's a hundred miles behind you, thief. What you're looking for is a ceiling." She rode ahead of him on a dun mare that had opinions about everything, including him. "Out here the sky goes up forever. Most people find it freeing."

"And you?"

"I find it full of things that can see you from a long way off."

They were nine days out of Carrowgate, four from the ford at Sennet, and Felix had stopped pretending to himself that he didn't know what was following them. The bound memory in his skull — Beatrix's secret, the heir's face and hiding place wrapped in her dying loyalty like a coal wrapped in cloth — had been restless since morning. It didn't speak. It never spoke. But it shifted against the walls he'd built around it the way a cat shifts against a door it wants opened, and this morning it had been shifting hard.

"Riders," he said.

Odile didn't ask how he knew. That was one of the things he was learning to trust about her: she didn't waste questions on answers she'd already priced. She turned in the saddle, scanned the bleached tree line behind them, and said, "How many?"

"Four. Maybe five. They've been matching our pace since the second river crossing."

"Vane's?"

"Who else rides in white ash cloaks with their faces wrapped?"

Odile swore with the fluency of a woman who had been fined for it in six ports. "I told you we should have gone by the coast road."

"The coast road is watched by the harbormaster's men, and the harbormaster sells to the usurper."

"The coast road," Odile said, "has boats. Ash roads have exactly what you see, which is nothing, which means when they come for us there will be nowhere to be nothing in." She wheeled the mare. "Move. There's a cutting ahead, old quarry road. Stone walls. If they want us, they'll have to come at us single file, and single file is a language my crew speaks."

"Your crew is two days behind us."

"Then we'll have to speak it alone."

* * *

**Odile**

The thing about Felix Okonkwo — and Odile had made a small, private study of him over nine days, the way she studied every cargo she carried — was that he lied beautifully about everything except the things that mattered.

He lied about being calm. His hands were never still. He lied about not caring what the Hollow had made him. He talked about his thefts like a man discussing carpentry, trade, nothing personal, and then some small thing — a child crying in a waystation, an old man selling ribbons — would go through him like a hook through cloth, and she'd watch him pretend it hadn't.

And he lied about the memory he carried. He thought she couldn't see it. He thought it was a sealed thing, a coin in a purse. But she'd carried contraband her whole life, and she knew what it looked like when a person was transporting something alive.

Now he rode beside her through the quarry cutting with his head turning like a bird's, and she did the arithmetic she'd been refusing to do since Carrowgate.

Vane's riders. Five, by his count. Odile had thirty years of running cargo through wars, famines, and one very memorable plague, and she knew what five riders meant. It meant the inquisitor wasn't spending her whole purse. It meant this was either a probe or an invitation to run somewhere specific. Sable Vane did not chase. Sable Vane herded.

"Left," Felix said suddenly. "There's a gap in the wall on the left. I can hear water."

"I don't hear water."

"I hear everything. It's the one thing the Hollow gave me that's worth a damn." He was already turning his horse. "It's a stream bed. It'll take us down and south, out of the cutting."

"Or into a box with water in it."

"Or out," he said. "You've been calculating since the tree line, captain. I can see it. You think she wants us somewhere. So don't go where the road goes. Go where the road doesn't."

Odile looked at him for a long moment — this hollowed-out thief with a ghost's memory beating in his skull, this man she'd bargained for out of pure mercenary interest and had, against every professional instinct she owned, stopped thinking of as cargo.

"That," she said, "is the first smart thing you've said since you refused to give me the map."

"It's the second. The first was hiring you."

"Don't flatter yourself. You didn't hire me. You outbid your own death." But she turned the mare after him, through the gap in the wall, down into the stream bed where gray water ran thin as milk over white stones, and the cutting fell away behind them, and for perhaps a quarter mile she let herself believe it had worked.

Then the arrows came out of the ash trees on both banks at once, and she understood that the stream bed was exactly where Sable Vane had wanted them all along.

* * *

**Felix**

The first arrow took Odile's man Tam through the shoulder. The second buried itself in the packhorse's neck, and the animal screamed in a way Felix would hear for the rest of his life, however long that turned out to be.

"Dismount — behind the horse, behind the *horse*—" Odile was shouting, and then the world went to pieces in the ordinary way, which was to say quickly and loudly and with a great deal of dust that was, here, ash.

Felix came off his mare and hit the stream bed shoulder-first. Water soaked through his coat, cold enough to make his ribs seize. Above the near bank, five riders in white moved along the tree line with the unhurried coordination of people who had done this many times and never once been surprised by the outcome. They weren't charging. They were closing, the way a hand closes.

The memory in his skull flared.

It wasn't pain, exactly. It was Beatrix — the fragment of Beatrix, the loyalty she'd pressed into him with her dying hands — and it was *afraid*, and its fear came up through him like floodwater, and underneath the fear was something else, something that made his hand rise before he'd decided to raise it.

The lead rider drew a bead on Odile.

Felix had stolen a hundred things. Grief, mostly. Guilt. A first kiss from a man who wanted to forget it. He had never once stolen anything in front of witnesses, and he had never once stolen anything that wasn't already being given up by the person losing it.

He reached across the stream bed with whatever the Hollow had left in him and pulled.

The rider's memory of the last hour — the ambush plan, the signal, the position of the sixth man they'd left on the ridge — came away like wet cloth tearing. The man swayed in the saddle, blinking at the trees as if he'd woken up mid-battle with no idea how he'd got there, and his arrow went into the sky.

It cost Felix more than he expected. Everything cost more than he expected, out here. The Hollow had been a thin soup of a place; the bright country was rich and heavy and every theft weighed ten times what it should. He went to one knee in the stream, gasping, and the world grayed at the edges, and through the graying he saw the sixth rider come down off the ridge behind them exactly as the stolen knowledge said he would, and saw him level a crossbow not at Odile, not at Tam, but at Felix — at the thief, the weapon, the thing that had just reached into a Vane man's head.

Odile saw it too.

He would replay the next three seconds for a long time afterward. She didn't shout a warning. She didn't tell him to move. She simply turned, stepped out from behind her horse into the open, and put herself between the crossbow and him, and the bolt went into her, high on the left side, below the collarbone, and she made a sound like a woman biting down on a coin and did not fall.

Then Tam, bleeding, roaring, put an axe into the crossbowman's horse's foreleg, and the ambush broke into the ugly close work that ambushes become, and Felix crawled through milk-colored water toward a woman who had taken a bolt for him and was standing there looking almost annoyed about it.

* * *

**Odile**

Pain, Odile had long ago concluded, was mostly a negotiation. You could let it name its price, or you could name yours first and hold the line. She held the line. She sat against the stream bank with her coat off and her shirt cut open and watched Felix's hands shake as he worked the bolt-head discussion out of her — the shaft had gone through the meat above her shoulder, clean-ish, the kind of wound that killed you in a week if you let it fester and annoyed you for a month if you didn't.

"Stop looking like that," she said. "You look like a man watching his ship sink."

"You took a crossbow bolt for me."

"I took a crossbow bolt for my investment. You are, currently, the most valuable thing on this road, and I include the horses."

"That's not why."

"Everything is why," she said. "That's the whole of it. Everything is why." But she heard her own voice and knew it hadn't come out right, hadn't come out mercenary at all, and she looked away from him at the ash trees, at the four dead or fled riders, at Tam binding his shoulder with the grim competence of a man who'd been stitched more times than a sail.

Felix tied off the last knot and sat back on his heels. His hands were still shaking. Not from the fight, she guessed. From the theft. She'd seen what he'd done to that rider — seen the man forget his own ambush in front of her eyes — and she had filed it away in the place where she filed things that changed the price of everything.

"You reached into him," she said. "Out here. In the bright country. I thought the Hollow was the only place you could do that."

"So did I."

"What did you take?"

"His orders. His last hour." Felix's voice was flat and tired. "There's a sixth man who ran. He'll report. Vane will know we went to ground instead of running for Sennet, and she'll know roughly where, and she'll adjust." He looked at Odile's bandaged shoulder, then away, out at the gray water. "Why did you do it? The real why."

Odile considered lying. She was good at lying; it was the family trade, before the other family trade. But there was something about sitting in a dead river in a dead forest with a hole in her and a thief's frightened eyes on her that made the mercenary ledger feel like a very thin book.

"When I was nineteen," she said, "I ran cargo for a man named Ivo Sarn. He was a smuggler like me and a fool like me, and he loved me in the stupid, loud way that young men love, and I let him, and I never once said it back because saying it back would have meant he was cargo I couldn't sell." The words came out level. She'd practiced them level for thirty years. "The guild stole from him. A debt-memory, a grudge he was carrying, something he couldn't pay and wouldn't set down. They took it out of him and he was never the same. Not because of what they took. Because of what it left. A shape where the thing used to be. He walked into the Sennet ford two winters later and kept walking." She looked at Felix. "I have spent thirty years telling myself the guild is just another trade. Then I met a man carrying something in his head that the whole world wants to take, and I watched an inquisitor's crossbow come up, and I discovered that somewhere along this road I stopped being able to do the arithmetic."

Felix said nothing for a long moment. The stream made its thin sound. Somewhere in the ash trees, a bird sang, the first one either of them had heard in days, absurd and bright.

"You could still sell me," he said quietly. "The map. The memory. Vane would pay more than the free cities ever will."

"Yes."

"You could do it now. I'm slow. You're faster than you look."

"Nobody's faster than I look," Odile said, and pushed herself upright, and the pain named its price and she refused to pay it, and she stood there swaying slightly with her hand pressed to her shoulder and her eyes on the southern road.

"Get Tam up," she said. "We move in ten minutes. Not to Sennet — Vane will own the ford by tomorrow. There's an old shrine road, east, through the hills. Pilgrims used it before the war. It's narrow, it's slow, and it's the one road in this country she can't ride five abreast down."

"And where does it come out?"

Odile looked at him, and for the first time since Carrowgate she told him a true thing she hadn't planned to tell.

"It comes out at the edge of the heir's country," she said. "The refuge lands. The places the old blood kept when everything else burned." She watched his face do several things at once. "You didn't know that. Good. That means when you refuse to tell me where we're going, you'll be refusing honestly, and I can trust it, and that — thief — is worth more to me than the map."

She turned and started giving orders to Tam, and behind her she heard Felix stand, and heard the silence where a man was deciding whether to be grateful or afraid, and knew he was choosing both, and thought: *good. Both is honest.*

The memory he carried had gone quiet again. But as they gathered the horses and turned east, off the ash road onto a track so old it was barely a suggestion, Felix felt it shift one last time — not afraid now. Pointing. The way a compass points.

Toward the hills. Toward home, or something like it, for someone.

He just couldn't have said whose.
