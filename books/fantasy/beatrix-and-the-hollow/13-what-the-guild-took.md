## 13. What the Guild Took

The safehouse smelled of tallow and old paper, the way all guild houses did — as if memory itself had a scent, and it was the smell of things slowly being forgotten.

Felix stood in the doorway of the archive room while Odile's man, a wiry smuggler called Finch, worked the lock with two picks and a stream of muttered profanity. Rain hammered the shutters. Somewhere below them, in the flooded cellar, the river muttered against stone.

"You're sure no one's coming," Felix said.

"I'm sure I'm being paid to say yes." Finch jiggled the picks. "Vane's riders swept the quarter at dusk. They're hunting a girl now, not you. Congratulations on becoming secondary."

The lock clicked. The door swung inward on a room that made Felix's chest tighten in a way he couldn't name — shelves floor to ceiling, each one crowded with small lead caskets, thousands of them, ranked like teeth. Each casket held a stolen life. He'd grown up around these. He'd been raised by the guild that filled them. And still, standing here, in the heart of the Carrowgate chapter house, he felt like a man walking into a temple of a god he'd stopped believing in.

"Records are in the back," Finch said. "Ledgers, indices. If your name's in there, it'll be under your guild-mark, not your name. Names get sold; marks don't."

"My mark is a thief's brand."

"Everyone in this building has a thief's brand, friend. That's what makes it a guild."

Finch left him with a lamp and a warning not to burn the place down, because Odile wanted the option of robbing it later. Then Felix was alone with the shelves, and the rain, and the slow drip of water finding its way through a ceiling that had forgotten how to hold.

He hadn't come for himself. That was the lie he'd told Odile, and the lie he'd told himself: he'd come for Beatrix. To learn what the guild knew about ghost-bound memories, about courier rites, about how a woman could carry another person's secret inside her skull for years without it eating her alive. Because his was eating him alive. The heir's memory beat behind his eyes like a second pulse, stronger every day, and last night he had dreamed in someone else's childhood — dreamed of a garden behind a palace wall, and woken up crying for parents who weren't his.

So: research. Professional necessity. A thief doing due diligence.

He found the courier registry an hour past midnight, wedged between an index of grief-thefts from the mill country and a ledger of purchased lullabies. Couriers were listed by route and cargo-class, not name. *Ghost-bound, high security, western circuit.* Beatrix's designation was there, three entries deep, her routes marked in faded ink. And beside one entry, in a different hand, a note:

*Cargo sourced from the Ashfall extraction. See primary ledger, mark 7-Black.*

Mark 7-Black. Felix knew that notation. Every guild child learned it before they learned letters. It was the classification for origin-thefts — memories taken so early they sat beneath language, beneath selfhood. The thefts that made people like him: functional, competent, quietly hollow. You couldn't steal a first memory without taking the foundation with it. The guild called the procedure *groundwork*, and they said it with the same tone masons used for foundations.

His hands were steady as he pulled the primary ledger. His hands were always steady. That was the problem with him — steadiness where there should have been feeling, competence where there should have been grief. He'd thought it was temperament. He'd thought it was the Hollow.

Mark 7-Black was a fat volume, its spine cracked white. He carried it to the table, set the lamp close, and opened it.

The entries were chronological. He turned pages through decades of groundwork — names redacted, locations coded, extraction dates logged with the bland precision of grain shipments. He wasn't looking for anything. He was just reading, the way a man reads a cemetery wall.

Then he saw the date.

Not his birthday. He didn't know his birthday. But the date matched the only anchor he had — the day the Hollow's matron had found him at the valley gate, four years old, unmarked, unclaimed, with no memory older than fog. The matron used to say he'd walked out of the mist like something the valley itself had coughed up. He'd always assumed he'd been born elsewhere, smuggled in, orphaned by the ordinary cruelties of the bright country.

The entry read:

*Extraction site: gate of the Hollow, northern approach.*
*Subject: male child, approx. four years.*
*Groundwork complete. Primary memory removed intact — condition exceptional.*
*Note: subject did not cry during procedure. Recommend retention for training.*
*Provenance flag: cargo matches royal-line signature. Refer upward.*

Felix read it twice. Three times. The words didn't change. The lamp flame leaned in the draft and the shadows on the shelf-caskets swayed like a crowd leaning in to watch.

*Subject did not cry during procedure.*

He remembered nothing of it, of course. That was the entire point of groundwork. But some part of him, some animal part beneath the theft, seemed to hear it anyway — a four-year-old sitting still while strangers reached into the oldest room of his mind and carried something out. Not crying. Being praised for it, probably. *Recommend retention for training.* They had taken his beginning and then kept him, fed him, taught him the trade, made him good at the thing that had been done to him. He had spent thirty years calling it talent.

And then the last line. The one that made the room tilt.

*Cargo matches royal-line signature. Refer upward.*

Royal line. The words he had been carrying across half the world in a stranger's memory. The secret worth armies. The face sleeping behind his eyes.

His earliest memory — the one thing that belonged to him before anything else did — bore the same signature as the heir.

"No," he said aloud, to the empty room, because saying it made it no more true and that was somehow comforting.

He sat down hard on the stool. The ledger lay open under the lamp like a wound. He tried to think the way he always thought — coldly, in steps, in options — and found the steps wouldn't form. This was the ground-of-self giving way, and he felt it literally, physically: the stool too far away, the floor at the wrong angle, his own hands resting on the table like tools belonging to someone else.

Every theft he'd ever committed, he'd justified with the arithmetic of the Hollow: take a little, give a little, everyone is poorer but no one is broken. He had never once wondered what had been taken from him, because a man who can't remember his childhood doesn't miss his childhood. That was the elegance of groundwork. The victim is the perfect witness against himself. He would have sworn, under torture, that he had never had anything to steal.

But the ledger said otherwise. The ledger said there had been a boy who didn't cry, and that what they took from him was rare enough to be flagged, valuable enough to be referred upward — up past the chapter house, up past the regional masters, up to whoever in the guild's long hierarchy had known, thirty years ago, that a royal-line memory had surfaced at the gate of a forgetful valley, and had decided to bury it inside the safest vault in the world: a child who would never know to look.

Him. He was the vault. He had always been the vault.

And somewhere — in some lead casket, in some deeper archive, in whatever dark room the guild kept the cargo marked *refer upward* — his first memory was still sitting. Intact. Condition exceptional.

Felix put his head in his hands and breathed, and the breathing didn't help. The heir's memory stirred against his fear like a second heartbeat answering the first, and for one vertiginous moment he couldn't tell which dread was whose — whether he was a man carrying a king's secret, or a king's secret wearing a man. The boundary he'd relied on all his life, the clean line between *what I stole* and *what is mine*, had just been shown to be a forgery. Drawn by the guild. Signed in his own blood, probably, in ink he couldn't remember spilling.

He laughed once, short and awful, at the symmetry of it. The great thief of the Hollow, the man who could lift a widow's grief or a miser's joy without leaving a scar — robbed blind at four years old, by his own teachers, of the only thing he'd ever truly owned. And he'd never even filed it as a loss, because you can't mourn what you can't remember missing.

Footsteps on the stair. Finch's voice, low: "Lamp's burning low, friend. We're leaving in five."

Felix closed the ledger. Then, on impulse he didn't examine, he tore out the single page — the entry with his date, his mark, his provenance — folded it small, and slid it inside his shirt, against skin. Paper evidence. The first document of his own history he had ever possessed. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

On his way out, past the ranked caskets, he paused. Thousands of stolen lives slept on those shelves, and one of them — maybe in this building, maybe in a hundred others — was a four-year-old's morning. Sunlight on a wall, perhaps. A voice. A name spoken by someone who loved him. *Condition exceptional.*

"Which one are you?" he whispered to the shelves.

None of them answered. Of course none of them answered. But as he climbed the stairs into the rain-washed dark, following Finch's lantern toward the waiting boat, Felix understood two things with total clarity.

First: Sable Vane had offered men death and men had refused her. She had offered them gold and they had lied to her. But she had never yet met a man she could offer *himself* back to — and if she ever learned what the guild had flagged in its own ledger, she would not need to threaten him at all. She would simply wait, and let him come to her.

Second: Wren was waiting for him at the safehouse with questions he'd promised to answer honestly. And tonight, for the first time since the gate of the Hollow, Felix Okonkwo did not know what the honest answer was — because the man she'd trusted to carry her truth had just discovered that his own truth had been cut out of him and sold, and that the buyer might be the very crown they were running from.

The boat rocked on black water. Somewhere behind them, in the dark archive, a lamp guttered out over an open ledger, and thirty years of a man's life quietly rearranged themselves around a single torn page.
