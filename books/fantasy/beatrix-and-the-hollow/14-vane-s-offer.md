## 14. Vane's Offer

Sable Vane had learned, over nineteen years of inquisition, that everyone could be bought. Not with gold — gold was a crude instrument, and crude instruments broke people instead of opening them. No. Everyone could be bought with the thing they had already lost. You only had to find it, hold it up to the light, and let them see how much of themselves was missing.

She had watched it work on millers and marshals, on priests who wept and soldiers who didn't. She had watched it work on herself, once, in a ledger room in the capital, when her own superior had slid a small black vial across the desk and said, *We recovered this from the guild raid at Vell. Do you want to know what's in it, or do you want to keep believing you chose to forget?*

She had chosen to know. That was the difference between her and everyone else. That was the whole of her, really: a woman who had chosen to know, and had not survived it intact.

So when the ash roads finally gave her Felix Okonkwo — cornered at a waystation shrine three days east of the burned refuge, alone because the smuggler woman had peeled off toward the river and the girl was hidden somewhere Vane would find — she did not draw steel.

She sat down across the shrine's cold hearth from him, set her lantern between them, and said, "You look tired, thief."

Felix's hand went to the knife at his belt and stopped there, not drawing it. Good. A man who didn't draw was a man doing arithmetic. She had seen the brand on his palm at the ford — the guild's mark, a closed eye — and beside it, newer, the inquisitor's sigil burned into his skin like a second sentence. Hunted by two worlds. She had made him that way, and she intended to use it.

"You killed the people at the refuge," he said. His voice was flat. She catalogued the flatness — fear wearing a mask, or anger wearing a better one.

"No," Vane said. "Someone else got there first. The usurper's own hand, moving faster than mine. You'll notice I'm the one sitting here talking instead of burning." She let that land. "I've been chasing you across three provinces, Okonkwo. Do you know why I haven't taken you?"

"Because you need the memory whole."

"Because I need the memory whole," she agreed. "But that's not why I haven't taken you. I could have taken you at the ford. I could have taken you in Ashfall with six riders and a net. I didn't, because what you carry is worth more than your corpse, and because—" she paused, and chose honesty the way other people chose a blade, carefully, edge out, "—because I know what it's like to have a hole in you shaped like something the guild took."

The lantern hissed. Outside, the ash wind moved through the dead pines with a sound like slow breathing.

"I read your file," Vane said. "Before I ever came to the Hollow. Everyone in my trade reads the Hollow files — the memory economy is the guild's oldest market and my order's oldest wound. But yours I read twice." She reached into her coat and produced a folio, oilcloth-wrapped, and set it on the hearthstone between them. She did not open it. "Felix Okonkwo, entered into the guild ledgers at age six. Extraction performed at the guildhouse in Carrowgate's lower ward. One memory. The earliest one. The extraction was logged as *complete* — which is rare, for a child that young, and it means the memory wasn't damaged. It means it's sitting in a vault right now, whole, waiting."

Felix had gone very still. Not the stillness of a man deciding whether to run. The stillness of a man who has stopped hearing the room.

"You don't remember anything before age six," Vane said, softly now, because tenderness was also an instrument and she had never been ashamed of that. "Do you. You've built a whole self out of the years after, the way a man builds a house on a burned foundation. You tell yourself it doesn't matter. You tell yourself the Hollow takes everyone's past, that's just what the valley is." She shook her head. "The valley doesn't take anything, Okonkwo. People do. And someone took yours before you were old enough to know what a yesterday was."

* * *

She watched him work through it, and she gave him time, because time was part of the price and she wanted him to feel every coin of it.

"You're lying," he said finally.

"I'm many things," Vane said. "I've lied to queens. I've lied to myself, which is harder. But I have never once lied about the ledgers. The ledgers are the only honest thing left in this empire." She opened the folio and turned it toward him. "Look."

He looked. She watched his eyes move down the page — the guild's extraction record, the closed-eye stamp, the date in a clerk's careful hand, and beneath it, in the column where the guild recorded *content recovered*, a single word that made his breath catch.

*Name.*

"They didn't take an event," Vane said. "Not a face, not a day, not a grief. They took your name. Your true one — the one your mother gave you, the one that came before Felix, before the Hollow, before the thefts. Children born on the wrong side of the old war were entered into the guild rolls under their birth names, and then the names were extracted so the children could be raised as nothing, as anyone, as *usable*. It was called blank-slate induction. It was outlawed twenty years ago and the records were supposed to be burned." She tapped the page. "This one wasn't. This one is in a vault under the guildhouse in Carrowgate, in a lead box, with a wax seal that only breaks for the person it belongs to. Guild law. Even I can't open it. Only you can — the box is keyed to the memory itself. It will only give your name back to the mouth that was born with it."

Felix's hand had drifted to the brand on his palm without his noticing. She watched him press it, the way a man presses an old wound to check if it still hurts.

"Why," he said. His voice had gone rough. "Why would the guild take a child's name."

"Because a name is a claim," Vane said. "A name says *you belong to something*. A mother. A house. A history. The guild deals in memories, and the usurper's order deals in erasure, and twenty years ago those two trades shook hands." She leaned forward. "You were born on the wrong side of the old war, Okonkwo. Whatever family you came from, they were on the losing side of the founding betrayal — the one you've been dreaming about, I'd wager, since Beatrix's memory started leaking into you. The guild took your name so you'd never be able to prove who you were. And then they sold you to the Hollow, where no one would ever come looking."

The lantern flame bent. Somewhere out in the dark, one of her riders coughed, and Felix's eyes flicked toward the sound and came back.

"You're lying," he said again, but it had no weight this time. It was a man testing a floor he already knew was rotten.

"I'm offering," Vane said. "That's different."

* * *

She laid it out plainly, because she had learned long ago that the cruelest thing you could do to a man was be clear with him.

"The heir's location. The face, the refuge, whatever Beatrix Hale pressed into your skull before she died. Give it to me, and I give you the box. Not a copy. Not a description. The box, unsealed, in your hands, and my written guarantee — countersigned by my order, which is a guarantee I have never broken — that no inquisitor touches you again. You walk away whole. You walk away *named*. You walk out of this war with the one thing you've been missing since before you could talk, and you never have to carry anyone else's secret again."

She watched him want it.

She had watched many people want many things, and she knew the anatomy of wanting the way a surgeon knows the anatomy of the body, and what she saw in Felix Okonkwo's face over the next long silence was not greed and not fear but something far more dangerous: recognition. The look of a man who has been shown the exact shape of his own absence.

"I could just refuse," he said.

"You could."

"I could run. Odile's waiting at the river. Wren is—"

"Wren is a girl who doesn't know what she is, and the usurper's hand already found the refuge before I did," Vane said. "Do you understand what that means? It means there is no hiding her anymore. The question is not whether she is found. The question is who finds her, and what happens when they do. If my order reaches her first, she lives — the free cities need her alive, the council needs her alive, even the *war* needs her alive. If the usurper's hand reaches her first, she dies in a ditch and the last true heir of the old line becomes a rumor again for another twenty years." She spread her hands. "I'm not asking you to betray her, Okonkwo. I'm asking you to choose who holds the truth. You've been holding it in your head like a second heart. It's killing you. I can see it killing you — the leaks, the dreams, the way you flinch when anyone says the word *war*. Give it to someone built to carry it."

"And you're built to carry it."

"I'm built to carry ledgers," Vane said. "And the ledger says the heir lives."

He almost laughed. She saw it start and die. "The ledger," he said. "You keep saying that word like it's a person. Like it cares."

"It's the only thing in this empire that cares," Vane said. "Everything else cares about itself."

* * *

He asked for the night. She gave it, because she had always intended to give it — a man who decided in a hurry would spend the rest of his life resenting the decision, and she needed him not to resent it. She needed him to walk into that vault on his own feet.

They gave him the shrine's back room, and a rider brought him broth he didn't eat, and Vane sat outside his door in the dark with her back to the wood, listening to him not sleep.

She knew what he was doing in there. She had done it herself, that night in the capital with the black vial sitting on her desk. You lay down. You closed your eyes. You tried to remember what it would be like to be whole, and the terrible thing — the thing no one warned you — was that you *could*. The mind is generous that way. It will build you a perfect picture of the missing room in the house of yourself, furnished with everything you've ever longed for, and you will lie in it for one whole night and feel what it is to be a person with a past, a person with a name, a person whose mother called him something soft in a language he no longer knew existed.

And in the morning you have to get up out of that picture and decide whether to buy it.

She heard him turn over. She heard him say something under his breath — not words, or words in a language that wasn't a language, the sound a man makes when he is trying a name on and doesn't have one to try.

Sable Vane closed her eyes and thought about her own vial, the one she had opened, the one that had contained a market day in Vell and a young man with flour on his hands laughing at something she'd said, a young man whose face the usurper's order had erased from every record in the empire two weeks later, along with the whole quarter he lived in. She had chosen to know, and knowing had made her this: a woman with a ledger where a heart used to be, hunting the men who made her, one memory at a time.

She had told Felix the truth about the vault. She had told him the truth about the name. She had even told him the truth about the refuge being sacked — she had no use for lies that would unravel mid-hunt.

But she had not told him everything. She had not told him that the vault in Carrowgate was three floors below the guild's memory exchange, and that the exchange had been quietly bought out, two months ago, by interests she could not name. She had not told him that the box might already be gone. She had not told him that she had come to him tonight half as a hunter and half as a gambler, betting that his name was still in that vault because she needed it to be, because if it wasn't, then the thing she was trading him was smoke, and she would have become the very thing she'd spent nineteen years hunting.

She heard him get up, once, and stand at the shuttered window a long time. Dawn was still hours off. Through the shutter-crack she could see the line of his shoulder, the hand pressed flat against the wood, and the faint ember-glow of the brand on his palm — both brands now, guild and inquisitor, thief and hunted, a man marked by everyone who had ever wanted to own him.

*Take it,* she thought at the door, the way she had once thought it at her own reflection. *Take it and be done. Be a man with a name. Let the girl and the war and the burning world be someone else's to carry. You've carried enough of other people's hearts. Take back the one thing that was ever yours.*

And then, because she was honest with herself if no one else: *And if he takes it, the heir dies, and the empire stays kneeling, and the man who erased my market day keeps his throne another fifty years — and I will have bought my vengeance with a child's name, and it will have been the easiest coin I ever spent.*

She heard him lie back down. She heard the broth go untouched. She heard, near morning, the sound of a man finally sleeping — the deep, surrendered sleep of someone who has made a decision, whatever it was.

Vane sat in the dark and waited for it, and hated how much she hoped.

* * *

At first light she opened the door herself.

Felix stood in the middle of the room, packed, pale, the folio in his hand. He had read every page of it in the night; she could tell by the way he held it, the way you hold a document you've memorized against the possibility it gets taken from you.

"You said the box is keyed to me," he said. "That it only opens for the mouth that was born with the name."

"That's guild law."

"Then here's my counteroffer." He set the folio on the bed between them, gently, the way you set down something breakable. "You take me to Carrowgate. You put the box in my hands. If it opens — if you're telling the truth — I give you the heir. My word on it, and my word is the only currency I've ever had that wasn't stolen."

Vane studied him. "And if it doesn't open?"

"Then you've lied to me, and I've lost nothing, and you've lost your only chance at the memory, because I'll have Odile's crew all over you before your riders can saddle."

It was a good bargain. It was, in fact, a better bargain than she had offered him, and she recognized the shape of it immediately: he had taken her trap and rebuilt it around her. If the box was gone — if her fear was true — he would know it the moment the seal failed, and she would have traded her position, her riders, and her hunt for a vault that held smoke.

She should refuse. She should take him now, by force, and pry the memory out with the order's instruments, and accept the damage.

But the instruments damaged what they touched, and the memory was the heir, and the heir had to live.

"Done," Sable Vane said, and shook his hand, and felt the brand-burn of his palm against hers like a signature on a contract neither of them fully understood.

He turned to the door. She watched him go, three steps toward the grey morning and the horses and the road east, toward Carrowgate, toward the vault, toward the name that might or might not still be sleeping in its lead box under the guildhouse.

Then he stopped.

"Vane," he said, without turning. "The file. The extraction record. It said the guild logged my name." His voice was very quiet. "It didn't say what it was."

"No," she said. "The clerk wrote the name in the box itself. Sealed with the memory. I've never seen it."

He stood there a moment longer, a man at the edge of his own life, and then he walked out into the ash-light, and Sable Vane followed him, and neither of them spoke again until the horses were saddled — because somewhere three provinces west, in a vault that might already be empty, the truest thing about Felix Okonkwo was waiting to find out if it still existed.
