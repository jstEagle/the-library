## 15. The Refuse

The map in my palm had gone quiet three days ago.

I didn't know what that meant. It had burned itself into my skin back in the Hollow, a lattice of fine lines like veins of ember under the flesh, and for the whole journey it had pulsed — faintly, patiently, a heartbeat not my own — whenever I turned toward the mountains. Now, as Odile's cart rattled up the last switchback through the pines, it lay still. Cold. The way a hearth goes cold when everyone inside is dead.

I told myself it meant we were close. The map only needed to guide me so far.

I told myself a lot of things on that road.

* * *

The refuge wasn't marked on any map Beatrix could have carried, because it wasn't a place so much as an absence — a fold in the mountain where the trees grew wrong, leaning away from something invisible, and the light came down at an angle that made your eyes slide off it. I'd have walked past it twice if the memory hadn't been pulling at me from inside, that second heart beating its patient instruction: *left at the split stone, down where the water goes underground.*

Odile left the cart with her cousin and came the rest of the way on foot, which I hadn't asked her to do and couldn't afford to pay for. She did it anyway, saying nothing, her hand never far from the knife at her hip. Three days since the ambush on the ash roads. Three days since she'd stepped in front of a crossbow bolt meant for me and then lied about how much it hurt. The bandage showed at her collar now, dark at the edge. She'd let me change it once and watched me the whole time like I was a lock she was learning to pick.

"You feel that?" she said as we climbed.

"No."

"Liar."

"Then why ask?"

She almost smiled. "Habit. In my trade you confirm what you already know. Makes people trust you." She ducked under a low branch. "And you're the worst liar I've ever smuggled."

The water went underground just past the split stone, exactly as the memory promised, pouring into a black seam in the rock. Beyond it the trees leaned away, and the light bent, and there it was.

A door.

Not a grand one. A door of grey timber set into the hillside, iron-banded, unremarkable except that no door should exist here, days from any road, in a fold of country that didn't want to be found. Carved above the lintel, worn nearly smooth by weather, was a sigil I knew from the leaked memory — from the war, from inside the eyes of a dying king's daughter: a wren perched on a broken crown.

My mouth went dry. "This is it."

"Then knock," Odile said. "Or don't. But decide fast. We've been followed since the ford."

* * *

The door wasn't locked. That was the first wrong thing.

It swung inward at my touch with a groan of swollen wood, and the smell hit us both — ash and old smoke and something sweeter underneath, the smell of a house that has been burning longer than the fire lasted. Odile drew her knife. I had nothing to draw. I'd never learned to hold a weapon; in the Hollow, the only thing worth stealing was carried in the head, and heads are taken bare-handed.

Inside was a hall carved into the living rock, and it had been beautiful once. I could tell because ruin has a grammar. There were the sockets where lamps had hung. There were the scorch-fans on the walls where tapestries had burned. There were shelves along one wall, hundreds of them, every book and scroll stripped out or burned in place — the charred spines still standing like teeth. Someone had been thorough. Someone had wanted this place emptied of everything except its stones.

"They came months ago," Odile said quietly. She crouched by a dead fire ring, sifted the ash between her fingers. "Wet season ash. This is old."

Months. I'd crossed half a country carrying a secret whose keepers were already ash. The memory inside me beat on, steady, indifferent to the news, and I hated it a little for that. *Left at the split stone,* it said. *Down where the water goes.* As if the person who'd hidden here were still alive behind these walls instead of scattered across them.

We searched anyway. Hope is stupid that way; it makes you check rooms you know are empty.

The dormitory cells off the main hall held bedrolls rotted to lace. A kitchen held pots fused to a stove. A small chapel — or what I took for a chapel, until I saw the crown-and-wren carved over the alcove and understood it was something older than either faith or throne — held nothing but a floor of white stone scrubbed clean, recently, by hands that had kept tending a dead place.

That stopped me. Scrubbed clean. Recently.

"Odile."

She came to the doorway, knife first. I pointed at the floor. At the absence of dust in a refuge abandoned for months. At the stack of firewood by the alcove, cut and split within the season. At the clay jug by the wall, unbroken, holding rainwater someone had fetched.

"Somebody lives here," I said.

"Somebody *hid* here," Odile corrected. "Different animal. Hiders don't sweep."

As if in answer, somewhere deeper in the hill, something fell over.

* * *

We found her in the far galleries, where the refuge tunneled back into the mountain and the lamplight of our single candle barely reached. She was backing away from us down a corridor, knocking things over as she retreated — a stool, a stack of pans, herself — a young woman in a man's coat too big for her, hair chopped short and ragged with a knife, eyes wide and white-rimmed in the candle-glow.

"Don't," she said. Her voice cracked on it. "Don't come in. I'll — there's nothing here. Take whatever you want, there's nothing here."

"There's you," Odile said.

"I'm nothing." She said it fast, automatic, a prayer worn smooth from repetition. "I'm nobody. I'm the caretaker's girl. My father was the caretaker. He went for supplies before the wet season and he hasn't come back and I'm nobody, please—"

She'd backed herself into a corner. Up close I could see how young she was — seventeen, maybe eighteen — and how long she'd been alone. The coat she wore had been cut down and re-stitched, sleeves shortened twice. There were tally marks scratched into the stone beside her, rows and rows of them, days counted out in a hand that had gotten shakier as the rows went on. Beside the tallies, drawn over and over in charcoal, the same thing: a small brown bird.

"Wren," I said.

Her head snapped up. "What?"

"The bird. You draw it everywhere." I nodded at the walls, the tallies, the charcoal wrens crowding the stone around her like a flock. "You count days and you draw wrens."

"I like them." Defensive now, chin coming up even while her hands shook. "They nest in the eaves in summer. They're not afraid of anything. They sing at hawks." She stopped, hearing herself, and something flickered across her face — confusion, quick and painful, like a person catching their reflection in a window they'd thought was a doorway. "Why am I telling you that? Who are you? How did you find this place? Nobody can find this place. Papa said—" She pressed her lips shut.

"Papa said what?" Odile asked, gentle in the way I'd learned she could be when she was working.

"Nothing. He said nothing. He said if anyone ever came, I was to say I'm nobody, I'm the caretaker's girl, I'm to be left alone." She recited it like scripture, and then her eyes came to me, and narrowed, and I watched fear turn into something sharper. "You're not soldiers. Soldiers burn. You're — what are you?"

"A thief," I said. Because lying to her suddenly felt impossible, and I didn't examine why.

"Thieves take things." She hugged the big coat around herself. "There's nothing to take."

"There's one thing." My palm throbbed. The map-lines had woken again, hot as a brand, and the second heart in my chest was hammering now, hammering, hammering, like a fist on a door. "I need to show you something, and I need you not to run."

"If I run," she said, "you can't catch me. I know all the ways out."

"I believe you." And I did. "But you won't run. Not from this."

I sat down on the cold floor, cross-legged, the way Beatrix had knelt in front of me in the Hollow with her life leaking out of her. I put my branded palm flat against the white stone.

And I opened the memory.

I'd done it deliberately only once before — in Vane's presence, as a weapon. Doing it by choice, gently, was different. It was like opening a fist you'd been clenching so long you'd forgotten the fingers were yours. The war-memory rose: the burning capital, the usurper's banners, and through it all a woman running with an infant wrapped against her chest, and the words she was whispering to the child, the same words over and over —

*They will look for a crown, little bird. So we will be birds.*

The gallery went silent. Even Odile had stopped breathing.

When I looked up, the girl hadn't moved. But her face had changed — drained, stricken, lit from inside by something she didn't have a name for. Her hand had risen without her permission and was pressed flat against her own chest, over her heart, as though she could feel what I'd shown her beating there.

"That song," she whispered. "My father sang that. When he thought I was asleep. He sang it wrong, he always got the words wrong, but that's — those are the words. Those are the *right* words." Her voice climbed. "How do you know my father's song? Who *are* you?"

"Your father didn't get the words wrong," I said softly. "He got them right, and hoped you'd never understand them."

"No." She shook her head, backing into the corner again, into her charcoal flock. "No, I'm the caretaker's girl. I'm nobody. He *told* me—"

"He told you because the truth would kill you," Odile said. "Same reason good smugglers lie about their cargo. The truth's the most dangerous thing aboard."

The girl looked at her, then at me, then down at her own hand still pressed over her heart. The tally marks ran up the wall behind her — two hundred days, three hundred, a year of counting mornings in a tomb, drawing wrens, singing a song she didn't know was a key.

"My name," she said slowly, testing each word like ice on a pond, "is Wren. My father called me that. He said — he said it was just a name. He said names were dangerous." Her breath hitched. "He said that the night before he left. He held my face and he said, *if I don't come back, little bird, remember that you chose your name yourself, and no one can take a thing you gave freely.*"

The candle guttered. Somewhere above us, wind moved through the burnt eaves, and I swear something small and brown sang into it, defiant, at hawks.

Hope arrived in that ruined gallery the way everything true arrives — broken, muddy, half-dead, refusing to lie down. She was alive. Against every odd, against fire and abandonment and a year of counted days, the last heir of the fallen line was alive, and she was standing in front of me in a stolen coat, terrified, furious, and utterly unaware that armies were already moving toward the sound of her name.

"We have to go," Odile said. "Tonight. We've been followed since the ford, and this hole won't hide her twice."

Wren looked at me. "Go where? With *thieves?*"

"With me," I said. "I was paid to deliver a secret. Turns out the secret breathes."

And that was when the dogs started, far below, in the valley where the water went underground — and with them, a voice calling up the mountainside, clear and cold and horribly familiar:

"Felix Okonkwo! I know you found her!"
