## 16. Wren

The first thing Wren had ever been told about herself was that she was no one.

Maren said it the way she said most things — flat, practical, over the rim of a soup pot. *You're no one, girl. No one's coming for you. No one cares what you become.* It should have been cruel. It was the opposite. In the nine years since Maren had pulled her half-frozen out of a ditch on the ash road, *no one* had been the safest word Wren owned. No one got asked questions. No one got taken. No one got burned.

She had built her whole life inside that word like a bird nesting in a bell.

And now a stranger with a thief's brand on his hand and a dead woman's grief in his eyes was standing in Maren's kitchen saying her name — not the name she'd been given, but a different one, an old one — and the bell was ringing.

"Wren," Felix said again. He didn't know he was doing it wrong. That was the worst part. "That's not — I mean, it is, but it's more than a name. It's a title. The wardens of the old line were called wrens because they were small and plain and they carried the king's voice in their bodies. When the line fell, the title went to the heir herself."

"I'm not a warden," Wren said. "I'm a goatherd."

"You're the heir."

The fire cracked. Outside, the goats were making their idiot evening noises, and the light through the smoke-hole was going amber, and everything was so ordinary it felt like a lie she was telling on purpose.

"I think you should leave," she said.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat on the edge of her cot in the loft and listened to the house settle and tried to make the last two days make sense.

The refuge had been sacked before Felix arrived — that much she'd gathered from the way he talked around it, careful, like a man walking on river ice. He'd come looking for a hidden heir and found a burned-out shrine in the hills and, by some accident he clearly didn't believe in himself, her. Wren, who had gone up to the shrine that morning because the goats liked the high grass and because she liked the silence of a place where no one had lived for years. Wren, who had been standing in the ruins of something old when a hollow-eyed stranger stumbled out of the treeline and looked at her like she was a door he'd been searching for his whole life.

She should have run then. Maren would have run. Maren had a whole philosophy about strangers: *the road gives you nothing it doesn't take back with interest.*

But the man had fallen down. That was the thing. He'd looked at her and then his knees had simply quit, and she'd caught his arm, and he'd said, in a voice scraped down to the boards, *"I know your face."* And then he'd wept, briefly and horribly, like a man bleeding from somewhere no bandage could reach, and she — fool, fool — had helped him home.

Now he was asleep by Maren's fire with a brand on his palm and a story in his mouth, and Wren lay in the dark doing arithmetic with her own life.

Because here was the thing she had never told anyone, the thing she kept folded up small the way Maren had taught her to fold money: she had always known she was someone's. Not Maren's. Maren loved her the way you love a debt you're glad to pay — honestly, but with a certain grimness. No. Wren had always known there was a *before*. She remembered, in fragments that came to her at the edge of sleep: a woman's hands, ringed, turning her face toward a window. A word repeated over her like a blessing or a ward. The smell of cedar and smoke and something sweeter underneath, incense maybe, or blood. And underneath all of it, a feeling — a certainty with no content — that she was *kept*. That somewhere, her existence was a fact that mattered to people who had never met her.

She'd assumed it was a dream. Children dreamed. She'd buried it so deep she'd almost convinced herself.

Almost.

* * *

In the morning, Felix was already awake, sitting where she'd left him, and he looked worse. Whatever was living in him — and she could see it now, once she knew to look, a wrongness in the way his eyes moved, like a man listening to music no one else could hear — it was working on him.

"Maren's gone to market," Wren said. "She'll be back by noon. If you're still here when she's back, she'll set the dogs on you, and she means it, and they're terrible dogs."

"I'll go." He said it simply, no argument in it, and somehow that was worse than arguing. "I just need you to look at something first. Then I'll go, and you can go back to your life, and I'll never —"

"No."

"You haven't heard —"

"No." Her voice came out harder than she meant. "That's the trick, isn't it? You show me something. A memory, a mark, a — whatever it is you're carrying. And after I've seen it, I can't unsee it. You don't get to hand me your trouble and leave. If you're going to ruin my life, you have to at least stay and watch."

He looked at her for a long moment. There was something in his face she couldn't read — recognition, maybe, or its cousin. "All right," he said. "I'll stay."

* * *

They went up to the shrine because he asked to see where she'd found him, and because Wren wanted, more than she wanted to admit, to look at the ruins with new eyes. If she was going to be ruined, she wanted to do it thoroughly.

The shrine was older than the war — Maren had said that once, dismissively, the way she said everything historical. Broken columns, a floor of fitted stone gone green with moss, and at the center a shallow basin carved with birds. Wrens, she saw now. Small plain birds, dozens of them, ringing the basin like a congregation. She had sat on that rim a hundred times eating her lunch and never once wondered what the birds meant.

"The wardens were crowned here," Felix said quietly. He kept his hands in his pockets like a man afraid of touching things. "The memory I'm carrying — Beatrix's memory, the one she gave me — it has this place in it. This exact basin. A woman kneeling here, and a child, and an old man with a staff saying words over them." He turned to her. "It has *you* in it, Wren. Not you. But a girl your age, with your face, being told who she is."

"That's not possible. I've never been here before this year. I've never been anywhere. I was a ditch baby."

"You were a hidden baby. There's a difference, and it's about nine years of someone's life spent keeping you invisible." He hesitated. Then, like a man stepping off a cliff: "I can show you. That's what the memory does, when it wants to. It shows things. I don't fully control —"

"Then don't," Wren said quickly.

But her heart had already said something else, and her body was already doing it — stepping toward him, toward the basin, toward the ring of carved birds — before her fear caught up, and by the time it caught up she was standing close enough to see the brand on his hand: a thief's mark, old and faded, and beside it something newer, rawer, a sigil she didn't recognize but her skin seemed to, a prickle along her arms like weather changing.

"I won't make you," Felix said. "I swear it. I've had things put inside me that I didn't agree to. I know what it is to have your head used as a room you didn't rent out." His voice went strange on the last part, and she understood suddenly that he wasn't talking about Beatrix's memory at all. He was talking about something older. Something taken.

"What happened to you?" she asked.

He almost smiled. It was a terrible smile. "That's a longer story, and it ends worse. Ask me someday if we're both still alive."

* * *

She decided, in the end, the way she decided everything: by watching.

Not him. Herself.

All that day she watched herself move through the ordinary machinery of her life — hauling water, mending a fence, salting meat — and she paid attention to the thing Felix had named and she had spent nine years refusing to. The feeling of being *kept*. It was there in everything. It had always been there. The reason she'd never fit in the village, the reason the other girls' gossip slid off her like rain off oilcloth, the reason Maren sometimes looked at her across the table with an expression that wasn't quite love and wasn't quite fear but was made of both. She had thought it was because she was a ditch baby, rootless, unclaimed.

But rootless things drift. She had never drifted. She had been *anchored* — to this valley, to this house, to this exact distance from the road — and she had felt the anchor her whole life without once looking down at the chain.

That evening she found Felix splitting wood for Maren, because apparently he was the kind of man who couldn't sit still while someone fed him, and she stood at the edge of the yard watching the axe rise and fall and said, "Show me."

He stopped mid-swing. "Wren —"

"You said you wouldn't make me. Fine. I'm not made. I'm asking." Her hands were shaking. She let them shake. "But I want you to understand what you're doing. If you show me and it's true, I can't go back to being no one. You said it yourself — I can't unsee it. So this isn't a gift you're giving me. It's a door you're opening, and whatever comes through it is going to come through into *my* house, *my* life, and you're going to be standing there too, and I want to know —" her voice cracked and she hated it and kept going — "I want to know if you'll still be standing there after. Because everyone who's ever kept me has eventually had a reason to stop."

The axe head rested on the chopping block. Felix looked at her across the yard, and the light was failing, and his face was doing the thing faces did when they were deciding how much truth a moment could hold.

"I was going to deliver you," he said. "That's the truth. That's the whole ugly truth. A dying woman paid me — not money, something worse — to carry a secret to the free cities, and you're the secret. I'm a thief, Wren. It's the only thing I've ever been good at. And every plan I've had since I found you ends with handing you over to someone, because that's what I do. I move valuable things from one place to another and I don't ask what happens to them after."

The words fell into the yard like stones into a well.

"But," he said, and set the axe down, and came toward her slowly, hands open, "somewhere on the ash road I stopped being able to feel the weight of the payment and started only feeling the weight of you. And I don't know what to do with that. I've never held anything I wasn't planning to sell." He stopped an arm's length away. "So I can't promise I'll still be standing there. I can only promise I want to be, which is more honesty than I've ever given anyone, and it's yours, and I hate how much it costs."

Wren looked at this hollow, branded, borrowed-hearted man, and felt the strangest thing: not fear. Relief. Because he had just told her the worst about himself, freely, before she could catch him in it, and in nine years no one — not even Maren, not even Maren — had ever handed her the truth unasked.

"Show me," she said again. "And Felix? Whatever I see — I'm deciding what it makes me. Not the memory. Not you. Me."

"I wouldn't dream of it," he said, and she believed him, and that was the most frightening thing that had happened all day.

* * *

He took her hand.

His palm was warm and rough and the brand was raised like a scar under her fingers, and for a moment nothing happened, and she almost laughed with relief —

— and then the world folded.

She was kneeling on stone. Cedar smoke. A woman's ringed hands — *her* hands, she knew them, she had known them all her life in the fragments she'd buried — turning a child's face toward a window where the light came up gold. An old man's voice saying words that were less language than weather. And through all of it, the feeling, the certainty, the thing she had carried since before memory: *you are kept. You are the last. You are the door they will come for, and we have hidden you so well that even you will not know, because the only safe heir is an heir who does not exist.*

The woman's face turned. It was Beatrix — she knew it the way you know a face in a dream, total and instant — and Beatrix looked at the child with such fierce, ruinous love that Wren's chest broke open around it, and the child looked up —

The child looked up and Wren saw her own eyes looking back at her from nine years ago, from the bottom of the well of her own buried life, and the memory said, in the old man's voice, in the woman's silence, in the carved wrens ringing the basin of the world:

*Wren. Last of the line. The valley forgets so that you may be forgotten. The valley forgets so that you may live.*

* * *

She came back to herself on her knees in the yard, and Felix was holding her shoulders, and the goats were screaming, and the first star was out.

"It's true," she said. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "It's true and it's been true my whole life and everyone knew but me."

"Not everyone. Maren, maybe. Whoever left you. Beatrix." He swallowed. "The usurper, soon, if we stay."

Wren stood up. Her legs held. That surprised her. Inside her chest, two things were beating now — her own heart, and the echo of the memory, the second heart he'd described, except it wasn't his anymore, it was hers, it had always been hers, just waiting in someone else's chest for her to come claim it.

She thought about *no one*. The word that had kept her safe. The bell she'd nested in.

She thought about the woman's ringed hands. *You are the last.*

"Then we don't stay," Wren said.

Felix went very still. "Wren —"

"They burned the refuge. They'll burn this valley next, and Maren with it, and every goat and every girl who never asked a question in her life. I spent nine years being no one, and it worked, and it's over, and I —" She stopped. Made herself finish. "I'm terrified. I have never been this frightened in my life. But I'd rather be the last of something than no one at all. So we go to the free cities. We go tonight. And Felix —"

The road beyond the gate was dark, and somewhere on it, she was suddenly, absolutely certain, something was already moving toward them. She didn't know how she knew. She knew the way she knew her own name.

"— we go *now*."
