## 17. Betrayal at the Ford

Odile Grey had sold many things in her life — contraband relics, forged passage-chits, her mother's silver when the winter got long enough, and twice, briefly and with great regret, her own dignity. She had never before sold a person.

She told herself it wasn't a person. It was a route. A line drawn on no map, a sequence of fords and fire-roads and one smuggler's cut through the ash hills that existed only because she'd spent eleven years learning where the world's eyes didn't look. Selling a route wasn't selling Felix. Felix was still free to be caught by someone else on some other road. The route was just geography.

Geography didn't have a face that went quiet when it was afraid.

"Captain." Tam's voice at her shoulder, low, in the dark of the *Kestrel's Debt*'s hold. "Riders. Three of them, maybe four, coming up the towpath. Lanterns hooded."

Of course they were hooded. Sable Vane did not travel like a woman who wanted to be seen. She traveled like weather.

Odile closed the ledger she hadn't been reading. "How far from the ford?"

"Half a mile. Maybe less. They're not hurrying."

They wouldn't hurry. Hurrying meant doubt. Vane had cornered the *Debt* at the Graywater ford the way you corner anything that has nowhere left to be — patiently, and from all sides at once. Odile had counted the lanterns on both banks before she'd let her crew drop anchor: nine. Nine hooded lights in the rain, arranged in an arc too neat to be accidental, and at the center of the arc, motionless, a woman in grey who did not carry a lantern because whatever she carried lit her from behind the eyes.

"You knew this would happen," Tam said. It wasn't an accusation. Tam didn't accuse; he observed, which was worse. "You took their coin at the last port. You said it was nothing. You said it was toll."

"It was toll," Odile said. "And now there's more toll. Go wake Marlowe and the twins. Quietly. Then come back and stand next to me looking harmless."

"I'm always harmless."

"That's why I'm paying you."

She climbed out into the rain alone. It came down soft and grey off the ash hills, the kind of rain that didn't fall so much as hang, and the river beneath the hull ran brown and quick with it. At the foot of the gangplank, waiting under a waxed cloak the color of old pewter, stood Sable Vane.

Up close she was smaller than rumor made her. That was the trick of her, Odile thought. Rumor built her into something tall and iron and terrible, and then you met her and she was a narrow woman with tired hands and a voice like a ledger being balanced, and somehow that was far worse. Terrible things you could run from. Tired things waited.

"Captain Grey." Vane didn't offer her hand. Inquisitors didn't touch if they could help it; touching created obligations. "You've been hard to find since Carrowgate."

"I've been easy to find. I've been expensive to approach."

"A distinction without a difference." Vane's gaze moved past her, up the rigging, along the deck, counting crew the way other people counted change. "You're carrying three passengers."

"I'm carrying cargo."

"The passengers are the cargo. One of them is a thief with two brands on his hand. One of them is—" and here Vane paused, and the pause had weight to it, the way a scale has weight before the weights go on, "—a girl of no particular history. And one of them is my property."

"Your property."

"The memory he carries. Beatrix's memory. It was seized by the Crown nineteen years ago. Everything it contains belongs to the throne, and everything it contains passes through whoever holds it." Vane looked up at her, rain running off the brim of her hood, and her eyes were patient as ledgers. "I am not here to threaten you, Captain. I'm here to do arithmetic."

"Arithmetic," Odile said. "My favorite kind of conversation at midnight in the rain."

"Your crew is eight. Your ship is thirty years old and insured twice over by men who would rather collect than repair. Your route through the ash hills — the one you think nobody knows — I have known about it since before your first command." Vane said it flatly, without cruelty, which made it land like a hammer anyway. "Against that: your freedom, your crew's freedom, and this." She drew something from inside her cloak. A small paper packet, dry despite the rain, tied with black thread. "Letters of passage. Real ones. Stamped by the Chancellery of the Free Cities. Every port on your route opens for you. Every debt you owe dissolves."

Odile looked at the packet and did not reach for it. Reaching was the first mistake people made with Vane. She'd seen it done, years ago, to a harbor-master in Carrowgate who'd reached and reached and kept reaching until there was nothing left of him but his signature on things.

"What happens to them?" she asked. "The passengers."

"The thief goes to the guild courts. He'll answer for the brands. Whether he hangs depends on what he stole and who wants it back." Vane tucked the packet away again. "The girl comes with me. Alive. Unharmed, as much as I can promise that. What she is doesn't concern me half as much as what she carries, and what she carries I can take without breaking her."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I burn the *Kestrel's Debt* at anchor with your crew aboard, and I do it slowly, and I spend the rest of my career hunting you across four kingdoms, and eventually I find you, because I always find people, Captain. It's the only thing I've ever been good at." Vane's voice didn't rise. It didn't need to. "But I don't want that. I want to go home after this. I've wanted that for a very long time."

*There*, Odile thought. *There it is.* Everyone had a seam. She'd built a life on finding seams — in customs officers, in rival captains, in the lockwork of banks — and here was the inquisitor's, showing plain as a split plank. Somewhere behind those ledger-balancing eyes was a person who had lost something and wanted only to stop losing things.

It should have made Odile pity her. Instead it made the arithmetic simple.

"How do I know you keep your word?"

"You don't. But I have never once broken terms I've set aloud, and every smuggler between here and the Salt Straits knows it. Ask anyone. My word is the only currency I've never counterfeited." A pause. Rain ticked against the gangplank. "You have until dawn. They're moving at first light either way. If the girl is gone from your ship when my riders reach the mill road, the letters are void and I start burning."

Odile stood in the rain and felt the shape of the decision settle over her like the wet cloak she wore: heavy, cold, fitted to her exactly.

"First light," she said. "You'll have your answer at first light."

Vane studied her for a moment — a long, weighing look, the look of someone checking a sum twice — and then inclined her head and walked back up the bank toward the hooded lanterns. She moved without hurry. She had already won. Both of them knew it.

Odile stayed on the gangplank until the rain had washed the sound of the riders' hooves clean off the night, and then she went below to lie to her crew.

* * *

The lie went like this: "We're taking the mill road at dawn, ahead of schedule, because there are riders on the north bank and I don't like the cut of their lanterns. Felix, Wren — you travel light and you travel fast, and when we hit the ford at Hollowbrook you go up the drover's path while the *Debt* makes noise downstream and draws every eye that's worth drawing. We rendezvous at the charcoal burner's clearing, third day. Anyone asks questions later, we were never carrying passengers at all."

Marlowe frowned. Tam watched her with those flat fisherman's eyes that missed nothing, and she could see him doing the sums — the riders, the timing, the fact that his captain had gone pale in a way she never went pale — and she could see him deciding, deliberately, to trust her anyway. That was the worst part. Not lying to Vane. Lying to Tam.

Felix didn't frown at all. He looked at her across the lamplit hold with those hollowed-out eyes of his, the eyes of a man who had spent a lifetime watching people trade pieces of themselves, and Odile thought, with a lurch: *he sees it. He sees exactly what I'm doing.*

He said only, "Third day. Don't be late."

"I'm never late," Odile said. "I'm expensively early or tragically absent."

Wren laughed — a small startled sound, the laugh of a girl who was learning, day by day, what it cost to matter — and Odile found she couldn't look at her. She busied herself with the cargo manifest instead, reading numbers she couldn't see, while somewhere above them the rain went on and the hooded lanterns burned on the north bank like a ring of patient teeth.

She did not sleep. She sat in her cabin with the letters of passage on the table in front of her and turned them over and over, and she thought about her crew — Tam and his daughter's debt, Marlowe with his bad lung and his dream of a farm he talked about like a woman he was courting, the twins who were twenty-two and had never known any sea but hers. Eight lives. Nine lanterns. One girl who'd spent nine years being no one and had finally, tonight, decided to be the last of something.

Arithmetic.

At some hour past middle-night, Odile Grey took out a scrap of paper and wrote, in her smallest hand, the route. The mill road, the ford at Hollowbrook, the drover's path, the charcoal burner's clearing. She sealed it, went up on deck, and gave it to a boy in an oilskin who rowed it across the brown water to the north bank without ever looking at her face.

Then she sat down on her own quarterdeck, in the rain, and waited to feel like a traitor.

What she felt instead was lighter. That was the horror of it — how little it weighed. She had expected the betrayal to sit in her chest like ballast, and instead it was like cutting loose cargo she hadn't known she was dragging. Eleven years of routes and favors and careful silence, and it all fit onto one small piece of paper, and a boy had rowed it across a river, and the world went on turning.

She was still sitting there when the sky east of the ash hills began to turn the color of an old scar.

* * *

They came at first light, exactly as promised. Four riders to the mill road, the rest holding the banks, and Sable Vane at their head riding a grey mare that matched her coat, and Odile stood at the rail of the *Kestrel's Debt* and watched the dust of them come and thought: *now. Now or never, and there is no never that I can live inside.*

The plan, such as it was, had assembled itself in her head sometime around the fourth bell without her permission — the way good plans did, arriving whole, refusing to be argued with. It was a stupid plan. It was the kind of plan that ended careers, and ships, and often captains. It was also, she realized with something close to relief, the only plan that left her able to look at Tam's flat eyes across a table for the rest of her natural life.

"Dawn stations," she called, and her crew moved, and the *Debt* cast off into the swollen river with Felix and Wren already gone up the bank in the pre-dawn dark, two shadows among shadows, carrying everything that mattered.

Vane reined in at the ford and looked at the empty deck, and then at Odile, and her face did not change at all.

"Captain."

"Inquisitor." Odile spread her hands. "You'll notice the absence of a girl."

"I notice several absences."

"Then I imagine you also notice the letters of passage never changed hands. Which means our arrangement is void, which means I owe you nothing, which means—" and here Odile Grey smiled her widest, most expensive smile, the one she used on harbormasters and customs men and other men who thought they owned the water, "—you can go straight to hell, and I will wave to you on the way down myself."

For one heartbeat, nothing. The river ran. The rain fell. Vane sat her mare and looked at her with those ledger eyes, and Odile saw — actually saw, in real time — the moment the arithmetic failed. The moment the inquisitor understood that the route on the paper was wrong, or incomplete, or baited; that a woman who sold her own dignity twice had known precisely what she was selling and had salted it.

"You sent them another way," Vane said quietly.

"I sent them the way you'd least like. It's longer. There's a bridge involved. Bridges are terrible for ambushes, I find."

Something moved behind Vane's eyes — not anger. Something older and tireder. "Why?"

Because of Marlowe's farm. Because of Tam's daughter. Because of a girl who'd spent nine years being no one. Because somewhere in the last month a thief with two brands on his hand had stood on her deck and lied to her badly and she'd let him believe she believed him, and some rotten sentimental part of her had apparently meant it.

"Bad habit of mine," Odile said. "Investing in long shots."

Vane raised one hand.

Odile was already moving. She'd been moving, in truth, since the fourth bell. The *Kestrel's Debt* swung her stern to the current with all the grace of her thirty years, and her crew — who had been told, in the smallest hours, everything except the part about the letter — put her broadside to the ford, and Marlowe touched flame to the powder train that ran from the aft magazine to the tarred spare sail stacked on the leeward deck, and the *Debt* became, for approximately ninety seconds, the brightest thing in the valley.

Fire jumped the gap between ship and bank where the oil-soaked driftwood Odile's twins had laid at low tide caught with a roar. The ford vanished behind a wall of smoke and orange light. Horses screamed and wheeled. Riders scattered, and the arc of hooded lanterns broke apart into nine separate panics, and through the heart of it Odile steered her burning ship straight down the channel at the grey mare and the narrow woman on it, forcing them off the ford, forcing them back, forcing them to choose between the water and the flames.

"Captain!" Tam, hauling at the wheel beside her, smoke in his beard, shouting over the fire. "The magazine—"

"I know!"

"You know we die?"

"I know we *might* die! There's a difference and it's mostly attitude!"

The heat came off the aft deck in waves. The *Debt* groaned the way old ships groan, deep in the bones, and Odile put her hand flat against the wheel her father had held and his mother before him and felt it shudder and thought: *thirty years. You were supposed to outlive me.* Ships never did what you asked. They did what you needed, once, if you loved them right.

A crossbow bolt took Marlowe through the shoulder and spun him into the scuppers, screaming, alive. A rider came through the shallows on a barge-pole's length of luck, and the twins met him with boathooks, and the whole grey morning turned into a single long grinding minute of noise and water and burning tar.

Through the smoke, on the north bank, Sable Vane stood beside her rearing mare and did not retreat. She looked across the fire at Odile — found her, through flame and rain, the way she found everyone, always — and Odile would swear until her dying day, which might be today, that the inquisitor almost smiled. Almost. Like a ledger closing. Like a woman recognizing another woman's arithmetic and hating that she respected the sum.

Then the aft magazine caught, properly, and the world went white and sideways.

* * *

She woke face-down in the shallows a hundred yards downstream, coughing river out of her lungs, one ear full of roaring and the other full of nothing at all. The *Kestrel's Debt* was a black skeleton midstream, mast gone, hull cracked open to the sky, burning down to the waterline with a sound like slow applause. Ash drifted on the rain. Her hands were burned. Her left side hurt in a way that meant ribs, plural.

Tam was dragging Marlowe up the bank. The twins were alive, one of them bleeding from the scalp, both of them hauling the longboat through the reeds. Eight crew. She counted twice. Eight.

On the south bank, distant, small with distance, riders were regrouping. Vane would come. Vane always came. But the ford was fouled with wreckage and fire, and the mill road was a guess, and somewhere north of here a thief and an heir were walking a route that existed nowhere on paper anymore, because the only copy of it had just burned along with everything else she owned.

Eleven years. One ship. Gone in ninety seconds.

Odile lay in the shallows and laughed until her ribs screamed, and Tam knelt over her with rain on his face, and she managed, between breaths:

"Third day. Charcoal burner's clearing. Get me a horse, get me Marlowe, and somebody find my ledger, because if I'm going to be destitute I'm going to be destitute *organized*."

"Captain," Tam said, "they'll hunt us now. All of us. There's no route left to sell."

"No," Odile agreed, spitting river water, grinning up at the grey sky like it owed her money. "Now there's only the truth. Much harder to steal."

Behind them, upstream, the wreck of the *Kestrel's Debt* settled into the ford with a long sigh of steam — and on the far bank, already mounted, already moving, Sable Vane turned her riders north.
