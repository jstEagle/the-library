## 18. The Weight of Two Truths

The mill they sheltered in had been abandoned so long that the grinding stones wore moss like a second skin, and the wheel had fallen into the river years ago — they could hear it still, knocking against the stone pilings with every turn of the current, patient as a clock nobody wound anymore.

Felix sat with his back against the cold granite and watched Wren pace.

She'd been pacing for an hour. She'd been pacing, on and off, since the ford — since Odile's ship burned on the water behind them and Sable Vane's riders wheeled away south carrying a lie Felix had paid for with the only thing he'd never thought to sell. Wren hadn't spoken much in the hours after. She'd slept a little, sitting up, her head tipping against his shoulder before she jerked awake and moved to the far side of the fire, and he understood that too. He remembered what it was to owe someone your life and not know the price yet.

But when she finally spoke, it wasn't gratitude. It wasn't fear, either, though he could hear it underneath, the way you hear the river under the ice.

"I'm going to the council," she said. "When we reach Carrowgate. I'm going to stand in front of them and tell them what I am."

Felix kept his voice level. "You don't know what you are."

"I know enough." She stopped pacing and faced him, firelight catching the stubborn line of her jaw. "I know there's a woman's memory beating inside your chest that says my blood is the old blood. I know the usurper sent inquisitors across half the world to find me before I even knew I was worth finding. I know Odile took a blade for me—" she faltered there, just a breath, "—for us, and Vane burned a ship and a crew, and every one of those people is on me now. On my name. The least I can do is carry it somewhere it counts."

"That's not how it will count," Felix said quietly. "That's how it will break."

Wren laughed — short, sharp, nothing warm in it. "You sound like the elders in the village where I grew up. Everything worth doing would break something, so we did nothing. Twenty-two years of nothing, Felix. They hid me so well I hid from myself. I didn't know why I couldn't go near the warding-chapel without it humming. I didn't know why dogs followed me and strangers stared and I always, always felt like a door left open in a house where everyone else had shut theirs." She crouched in front of him, close enough that he could see the fire doubled in her eyes. "I'm done being a door people walk past."

"It isn't about being seen." He made himself hold her gaze. "It's about what happens the moment you are. You stand in front of that council and say the word *heir*, and you think they'll cheer? The free cities have spent three hundred years building a peace out of silence. Half their merchants trade with the usurper's ports. Their militias haven't fought a war since their grandfathers' grandfathers. You're asking them to burn all of it down on the testimony of a smuggled memory and a girl who learned her own name last month."

"My name," Wren said, very softly, "is not Wren."

"No. And whoever you were born as died to the world the day someone hid you, and staying hidden has kept you alive for twenty-two years. That's not nothing. That's the whole argument."

"Then say it plainly." Her voice cracked and she hated it — he could see her hating it — and she stood and turned away so he wouldn't watch her hate it. "Say you want to hand me over like everything else you've carried. Wrap it up safe, deliver it whole, take your payment, and never once ask the package what it wants."

The words went in exactly where they'd be sharpest, because she'd aimed them well. He'd taught her too much of himself on the road. That was the trouble with telling people the truth about yourself: they learned where the soft places were.

"You know that's not what this is," he said.

"Do I?" She spun back. "I've known you two months, Felix Okonkwo, and in two months you've told me about every theft you ever committed except the one that matters. You'll pawn a stranger's grief to buy bread. You'll give Beatrix back her ghost at the gates of a city while the whole council watches, because that's clean, that's noble, everyone gets to see you do the right thing. But ask you to keep one true thing in the world — one living, breathing true thing — and you start pricing escape routes."

* * *

The fire had burned down to coals. Neither of them had fed it.

Felix found he was gripping his own wrist, the branded one, thumb pressed over the thief's mark the way he did when the bound memory stirred — and it was stirring now, Beatrix's borrowed loyalty turning over in him like something waking in a barn at night. He let go of his wrist deliberately, and put his hands flat on his knees where Wren could see them, which was its own kind of confession.

"I'm afraid," he said.

Wren's shoulders dropped half an inch. "I know."

"No, you don't. You think I'm afraid of the usurper, or of Vane, or of some war." He shook his head slowly. "Those I can carry. I've been carrying other people's worst days since I was old enough to hold still for the taking. What I'm afraid of is smaller than that and heavier." He looked at the coals because looking at her was impossible. "Everything true I have ever held, I lost. My first memory — gone before I could miss it properly, taken by my own guild, sold to someone whose face I'll never know. My mother's face — I can't tell you if I ever had it or if I invented it out of candle smoke and wanting. Beatrix — I gave her back, and it cost me whatever childhood I might have claimed, and I'd do it again, but Wren, I did it in front of a hundred witnesses precisely because I knew that afterward there'd be nothing left in me anyone could take." His laugh came out wrong. "I built my whole life into a vault with nothing inside it. Nothing to steal. Nothing to lose. And then—"

He gestured at her, helpless, the sentence dying where all his sentences about her died.

"And then you walked in," Wren said.

"On fire," Felix agreed. "Screaming about a map. Ruining everything."

She almost smiled. Almost. Then the grief under it surfaced, and she came and sat down beside him against the millstone, not touching, shoulder a hand's width from his, which was somehow worse than touching.

"So this is what it is," she said. "You love like a man guarding a treasure. You keep it dark and you keep it buried and you count the walls between it and daylight."

"Is that wrong?"

"Yes," she said, simply, and the simplicity of it knocked something loose in his chest. "Felix. I was a treasure. Twenty-two years in a root cellar of a life, guarded by people who loved me so much they erased me. Do you know what that protection felt like from inside? It felt like not existing. Your fear isn't keeping me safe. It's just the cellar again, with better manners."

"The cellar kept you breathing."

"The cellar wasn't living, and you know it, because you lived there too." She turned to face him fully. "Here's my truth, and you can steal it if you want, put it in your ledger, sell it in Carrowgate — I'd rather it be yours than no one's. I want to matter *out loud*. I want to stand in the light in Carrowgate and say my grandmothers' names, the real ones, the royal ones, so loud that the usurper hears it in his palace and chokes on his breakfast. And yes — I want the free cities to rise, and yes, that means war, and yes, people will die, and I will carry every single one of them, because the alternative is that they die anyway, one valley at a time, one burned Hollow at a time, while I stay safe and hidden and *useless*." Her voice dropped. "You of all people should understand. You spent your whole life stealing grief from the dying so the living could set it down. That's all I'm asking to do. Set it down somewhere it can be used."

Felix sat with that. Outside, the wheel knocked against the pilings. Somewhere far off, too far to be a threat yet, thunder rolled along the ridge — or artillery, his new instincts said, the bright country had taught him the difference and untaught him comfort.

"There's a version of this where you're right," he said at last. "And there's a version where I stand in that council hall and watch soldiers come through the doors, and everything I gave up my own yesterday for dies in front of me in the first ten minutes." He exhaled. "I can't tell those versions apart from here. Can you?"

"No," Wren admitted. "But I know which one I'd rather die inside of."

* * *

They didn't resolve it. Arguments that real don't resolve; they just change shape.

What happened instead was smaller. Wren reached over and took his branded hand — the thief's mark, the inquisitor's sigil beside it, the faded ghost of Beatrix's map burned into his palm like a scar of light — and turned it over and studied it as if reading it.

"All these things other people put on you," she murmured. "Every mark is somebody's claim."

"Not that one." He closed his fingers around hers. "This one I chose. Taking Beatrix's memory. Nobody paid me. Nobody forced me. First thing I ever stole that I gave away again free."

"And it undid you," she said.

"And it undid me," he agreed. "So forgive me if I'm slow to sign up for more undoing."

"Forgiven." She leaned her head against his shoulder, and this time she didn't jerk awake and pull away, and he sat very still, guarding the moment and refusing to guard it at the same time, and mostly failing at both. "But Felix — I'm still going to say it. At the council. Whatever you decide, whatever we decide — when the moment comes, I'm going to say it."

"I know," he said. "That's why I'm terrified of you."

"That's the nicest thing you've ever said to me."

He laughed despite everything, and the sound startled the pigeons out of the ruined loft above them, wings clattering into the dark like a small panic leaving.

* * *

He woke near dawn to the sound of the wheel stopping.

Not slowing — stopping. The river had gone silent, which rivers do not do, and the silence had a texture he'd learned on the road: the texture of many boots making no noise at once.

Felix was on his feet with his knife out before his eyes finished opening. Wren was already awake, already moving, her hand pressed flat against the millstone — and the stone was humming, faint and rising, the way the warding-chapel had hummed for her all those hidden years.

"Felix," she whispered. "There's magic outside. A lot of it."

He looked at the sealed door of the mill, and beyond it, in the grey not-light before dawn, shapes were settling over the ruin like a held breath — riders in the ash-grey of the usurper's house, and at their center, dismounting with the unhurried care of a woman who had all night and knew it, a figure he would have known anywhere, by silhouette alone, by the set of her shoulders and the white streak in her hair.

Except Sable Vane was supposed to be riding south with a lie in her ledger, two days hard away.

Which meant either the lie had failed —

— or it had never been hers to begin with.

"Wren," Felix breathed, backing toward the mill's broken loading door, knife low, heart hammering out a rhythm older than either of them. "How fast can you run?"
