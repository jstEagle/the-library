## 19. Vane Unmasked

Sable Vane had interrogated forty-one people in her career as an inquisitor of the Usurper's court, and she had learned one truth above all others: everyone broke at the thing they loved most.

The smuggler captain had broken at her crew. The guild fence had broken at his daughter's name. Even the miller's boy in the Hollow, years ago, a child caught carrying stolen grief across the wardline — he had broken at the memory of his mother's hands. Sable Vane did not enjoy the breaking. She had simply stopped pretending she didn't understand it.

So when they brought Felix Okonkwo into the tent at Ashfall Ford — wrists bound in null-iron, Odile Grey's blood still drying on his sleeve where he'd carried her from the burning ship — she did not begin with questions.

She began with tea.

"Sit him down," she said. "Give him something warm. He's been in the rain for two days."

Her riders exchanged glances. Inquisitors were not known for kindness, and Vane least of all; there were stories about her that she had never bothered to correct, because fear was a tool and dull tools got people killed on both sides of an interrogation. But she poured the tea herself, set it on the folding table between them, and sat across from the thief with her ledger closed beside her elbow.

Felix Okonkwo looked at the cup like it might be poisoned. Then he looked at her, and what she saw in his face was not fear.

It was recognition.

"You know me," he said.

"I know your file." She kept her voice level. "Felix Okonkwo. Hollow-born. Guild-trained thief of memories. Carrying a bound secret worth more than this whole army." She turned the ledger toward him, open to a page written in her own small, exact hand. "I've been hunting you since the valley burned."

"I know." His voice was hoarse. "You burned the miller's quarter to flush me out. I watched the smoke from the ridge."

"Three hundred and six people lived in that quarter," Vane said. "We gave them warning. Eleven didn't run fast enough." She said it flatly, the way she said everything, because the alternative was to say it any other way, and she had spent nine years learning not to do that. "I want you to understand that I keep accounts. Every entry in this book is a debt someone owes, or a debt owed to them. It's how I stay—" She paused. Chose the word carefully. "Clean."

"And what's my entry?"

"You're not in it yet." She folded her hands. "That's what tonight is about."

* * *

He drank the tea. She'd expected that; a man who had crossed half the Bright Country with an inquisitor's sigil burned into his palm and a dead woman's loyalty beating inside his skull was too tired to be proud. The null-iron cuffs kept his gift quiet — she could see him testing it, reaching inward and finding the doors shut — but the bound memory wasn't his gift. It was cargo. Null-iron slowed cargo. It did not stop it.

She knew that better than anyone in the usurper's service. It was, in fact, the only reason she had volunteered for this hunt.

"The heir," she said. "Tell me where."

"No."

"Captain Grey sold you your route. She'll sell me the same road by morning if I press her wounds hard enough. I don't need you, Okonkwo. I'm offering you a courtesy."

"She took a lance for me." His jaw tightened. "Ask her anything you want. You'll get nothing either."

"Everyone breaks at the thing they love most," Vane said. "It's the first lesson of my order. So let's find out what you love." She opened the ledger again, to a page near the back, and laid it flat. "Because here's mine."

For a long moment he just looked at the page. Then his brow creased, and she watched him read the single line she had written there in ink she'd mixed herself so no clerk could ever copy it:

*Tomas Vane. Taken under Erasure Order 114-A. Year of the Founding Betrayal, autumn.*

"That's a war record," Felix said slowly. "Those are sealed. Nobody outside the palace archive has—"

"I was nineteen," Vane said. "Tomas was twenty-two. He drove supply wagons on the ash roads before the roads had a name. We were going to be married in the spring." Her voice did not change register. That was the discipline of it, the craft: she could say these sentences the way other people recited inventory. "Then the new king issued his first great erasure. Every soldier who had served the old royal line, every witness to how the crown changed hands — their memories were pulled and burned. Not killed. *Erased.* Tomas woke up in a barracks in the capital knowing his own name and nothing else. No mother. No trade. No me."

Felix had gone very still. Thieves went still like that, she'd noticed — when the shape of a thing became clear to them, when they realized what was actually in the room.

"He came looking for me once," she continued. "A year later. He stood in my father's doorway with polite eyes and asked if I was the daughter of the house, and when I said yes, he apologized for troubling me and left." She turned the page. Beneath Tomas's name, in the same careful hand, was a list. Forty names. Sixty. A hundred and twelve. "I joined the inquisition because it was the only way into the archive. Nine years cataloguing erasure orders, cross-referencing, finding families, telling them what happened to the people they loved. Do you know what every single one of them asks me?"

Felix shook his head.

"*Was it painless?*" Vane said. "As if forgetting could be gentle. As if a man emptied out and refilled with someone else's calm is better off than a man grieving." She closed the ledger. "Your guild stole memories for coin. My king steals them for power. I have spent nine years telling myself those are different crimes. I would like very much for you to help me keep believing that."

"Why?" Felix asked. Quietly. "Why tell me this?"

"Because you're carrying a memory from before the erasures," Vane said. "Beatrix's cargo. The founding betrayal, seen from inside the old royal line. And if it's whole — if it survived the crossing intact — then it contains the one thing no archive copy ever does."

"What's that?"

"The faces," she said. "Every erased soldier has a number in the records. Orders don't preserve faces. But a living memory doesn't redact. If Tomas was anywhere near the events Beatrix witnessed — and he was, he drove wagons through the pass at Vell, the same week the betrayal happened — then he is in your head right now." Her hands, she noted distantly, had gone white-knuckled around the ledger's edge. She made them let go. "I am asking you, thief, to show me a ghost."

* * *

They moved to the command tent because the null-iron worked better over packed earth, away from the iron nails in the wagon frames. Two of her riders stood at the flap. Vane dismissed them. This, whatever it became, would not happen in front of witnesses.

Felix sat on the ground with his cuffed hands in his lap, and she knelt across from him, close enough that the binding between them — palm to palm, the old rite her order used for evidence extraction — would hold.

"If I do this," Felix said, "you let Grey and Wren walk."

"Wren." The name snagged. "The girl from the refuge."

"You already know about her."

"I know a refuge was sacked and its occupant unaccounted for. I know Captain Grey's crew was seen hauling a young woman north." Vane held his gaze. "Yes. They walk. All of them. You have my oath on the ledger."

"On the ledger," Felix repeated. "Is that worth anything, from an inquisitor?"

"It's the only thing I have that is."

He studied her for a long moment — the same look, she realized, that he must give locked doors and guarded strongboxes, measuring what a thing would cost to open and whether opening it would ruin him. Then he nodded once, and turned his bound palms up.

The rite was simple and terrible. Her thumb found the pulse point below his cuff; his fingers closed over hers; and she spoke the extraction words her order had taught her, the ones designed to pull a memory out clean, like a splinter.

But this was not a splinter. This was a heart.

The memory took her the way floodwater takes a door off its hinges.

* * *

*Autumn. The pass at Vell, before the war had a name, when it was only called the trouble in the east.*

*She is inside another body — a woman of the old court, tall, tired, riding a white mule down the switchbacks with dispatches strapped to her chest. The colors are wrong, too bright, the way memories are bright when the person remembering loved the world. Banners of the old line snap in the wind: silver stag on black. Below her, the supply column snakes through the pass — forty wagons, oxen, soldiers in mismatched armor, and drivers in canvas coats.*

*One driver looks up as she passes. Young. Dark hair falling in his eyes. He grins at her — everyone grinned at the dispatch rider; she was the closest thing to news the column ever got — and calls out something cheerful about the weather, and she laughs, and the laugh is warm in her borrowed chest, and*

*Sable Vane made a sound she had not made since she was nineteen years old.*

*The memory rolled on without mercy. The camp at dusk. Fires. The driver — Tomas, it was Tomas, younger than her Tomas, whole in a way her Tomas had never been allowed to remain — sharing bread with a boy from the lowlands, arguing amiably about dice. The dispatch rider sitting near enough to hear him laugh. Three days of ordinary human days, preserved like flies in amber: a man singing badly, a man writing a letter home and sealing it with a smudge of tallow instead of wax because he couldn't afford wax, a man saying to his friend, "When this trouble's done, I'm going south. There's a girl in the river country. Best thing that ever happened to me, and she doesn't even know it yet."*

*Then the fourth day. The banners changing in the night. The order coming down the column in a voice like a knife being drawn — the general's voice, the man who would be king by winter — and soldiers turning on soldiers, and the dispatch rider running, and the last thing the memory holds of the pass at Vell is Tomas Vane standing in front of a wounded boy with an empty bread hand raised, saying, "Now hold on, now hold on, we're all—"*

*And then white. The erasure, seen from the outside: the king's men moving through the survivors with their instruments, and Tomas's face going smooth and empty as a washed slate, and the letter home burning in a brazier with a hundred others, and a clerk entering into a ledger: Subject 114-A. Disposition: reassigned. Memory: destroyed.*

*Destroyed. Not archived. Destroyed.*

*The memory ended. Sable Vane came back to herself kneeling on the packed earth of Ashfall Ford with Felix Okonkwo's pulse hammering under her thumb and tears running down her face in two straight lines, like rain down a window, like something that had been waiting nine years for permission.*

* * *

For a while neither of them spoke. The torches crackled. Somewhere beyond the tent, a horse complained in the dark.

"He didn't suffer," Felix said at last, gently, the way you'd speak to someone holding something breakable. "In the taking. The memory shows—"

"I know what it shows." Her voice came out sanded flat. "I wrote the doctrine myself. *Erasure is a mercy. The subject feels nothing.* I've told a hundred families that." She looked down at her hands. They were shaking. She let them. There was no one here to fear and no one left to perform for. "I told myself the archive copies meant they weren't really gone. That someday, if the regime fell, we could give people back." A breath. "There are no copies. There never were. He isn't sleeping in a vault somewhere. He's *ash*, Okonkwo. The king burned him twice — once out of his head and once out of the world."

"Yes," Felix said simply. Because he was a thief, and thieves do not flinch from the weight of what's been taken; it was, she thought wildly, the kindest thing anyone had said to her in nine years.

Vane rose. Walked to the tent's edge. Stood with her back to him for a long time, looking out at the fires of her own command, at the riders who had followed her because she was the best hound the crown had, at the banner of the usurper hanging limp and wet above the command wagon.

The ledger lay open on the ground where she'd dropped it. Forty names. Sixty. A hundred and twelve. Every one of them a person, not a number. Every one of them ash.

She thought: *I built my whole self on the belief that the records were the remembrance. That as long as the orders existed, the erased existed somewhere, waiting.* And the man who had just shown her the truth was a criminal, and the girl he was protecting was the rightful heir of the line her king had butchered, and the war she had spent her life preventing by obedience was already inevitable, because you cannot build a kingdom on a hundred and twelve graves and call it peace.

"Okonkwo," she said.

"Yeah."

"When we reach Carrowgate — when you stand before the council — I will not be behind you." She turned around. Her face was dry now. Something behind it had rearranged itself, permanently, the way a landscape rearranges after a flood. "I will be beside you. With every rider I command, and every entry in this ledger, and every order I can forge in the king's name to open the gates before his army arrives." She picked up the ledger and held it against her chest like a shield, or a wound. "You wanted to know what I'd break at. Now you know. It wasn't Tomas."

"No?"

"It was finding out there was nothing left to save." She smiled, and it was the worst thing Felix Okonkwo had ever seen, and the best. "So now I'm going to spend what's left of me on making sure there's something left to *avenge*. Saddle your horse, thief. The council sits in four days, and the king's vanguard moves faster than I'd like."

Felix stood, rubbing his freed wrists. At the tent flap he paused.

"For what it's worth," he said, "he seemed like a good man. Your Tomas. He shared his bread."

Vane Vane — Sable Vane, inquisitor of the usurper's court, keeper of the ledger, hound of a dying crown — looked at the man she had hunted across half the Bright Country and found that she could not hate him, and no longer needed to.

"I know," she said. "I was going to marry him."

Then she snuffed the lamp, shouldered the ledger, and walked out into the dark to commit the largest treason of her life — and the first honest act of her career.
