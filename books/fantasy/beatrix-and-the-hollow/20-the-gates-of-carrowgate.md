## 20. The Gates of Carrowgate

The ash roads ended where the old king's highway began, and the highway was a river of refugees.

Felix had never seen so many people in one place. The Hollow had taught him to think in dozens — a market day, a funeral, the whole valley gathered to watch a barn burn. This was thousands, tens of thousands, a human flood pouring west along the road toward Carrowgate's walls: farmers with their livestock on ropes, merchants hauling carts piled high with everything they owned, soldiers in the livery of half a dozen free companies marching in loose, exhausted columns. And above it all, on the eastern horizon, the smoke.

Not chimney smoke. Not forge smoke. The smoke of a country burning.

"They're three days behind us," Vane said, reining her grey mare alongside his cart. She'd traded her inquisitor's black for plain riding leathers, but she still wore her sigil — turned upside down now, the broken circle inverted, the mark of an order repudiating itself. "Maybe two. The vanguard moves fast when they're not stopping to burn."

"Then we don't stop either," Felix said.

Wren slept in the cart bed, curled against a sack of grain like any farm girl dead tired from travel. That was the trick of her, Felix thought. He'd spent weeks carrying a memory that made her face feel like the most important thing in the world, and here she was, drooling slightly on burlap. The living heir of the Emberline. The spark that could light a war or end one. Asleep in a cart, smelling faintly of woodsmoke and horse.

Odile drove. She'd barely spoken since the ford. Her hands were bandaged where the inquisitors' chains had torn them, and every jolt of the cart made her wince and say nothing about it. Once, passing a burned-out waystation, Felix had caught her looking at him with an expression he couldn't read — something between apology and defiance, as if she were still deciding which one she owed him.

He decided not to ask. There wasn't time, and he wasn't sure he wanted the answer.

* * *

They stopped at dusk at a shrine roadhouse, one of the little way-circles the free cities maintained for travelers — a ring of standing stones, a well, a roofed shelter open on all sides. Vane's riders, forty strong now, took the perimeter without being asked. It still sat strangely with Felix, seeing men who'd hunted him across three provinces now building his fire and watering his mule. One of them, a scarred woman named Ilsa, had taken to calling him *sir*, and every time she did it, something in his chest flinched.

"You should sleep," Wren said, appearing beside him with two bowls of barley stew. She handed him one and sat close enough that their shoulders touched. "You look terrible."

"I always look terrible."

"No. Before, you looked terrible the way a knife looks terrible. Now you just look tired." She blew on her stew. "There's a difference."

He ate. Around them the riders talked low, and somewhere Odile argued with her quartermaster about rigging, even though there was no ship within fifty miles. Old habits. The fire cracked and spat, and for a few minutes Felix let himself pretend this was ordinary — a journey nearly done, a meal, a girl beside him, a night without anyone trying to kill anyone.

Then Wren said, quietly, "I can feel it. What you're carrying. Tonight it's louder than usual."

He set down the bowl. "What do you mean, louder?"

"Like a second heartbeat. Like someone humming under my skin." She pressed her hand flat against her sternum. "It knows we're close. Whatever Beatrix put inside you — it wants to come home."

"The only home it's going is the council floor," Felix said. "In front of the assembled delegates, with witnesses. That was the bargain."

"I know the bargain." She looked at him, and in the firelight her eyes were the exact amber of the memory he carried, the color that leaked into his dreams. "I'm not asking you to break it. I'm asking you to be ready for what happens after. Felix — when you give it back, you won't remember why any of this mattered. You won't remember *me*."

The words landed like cold water. He'd known it, abstractly, since the guild safehouse — since he'd learned what the rite of return demanded. A memory given back whole takes its toll whole. The bound memory of the heir would return to its rightful witness, and the price of unbinding it was everything tangled around it. His childhood. His name. Beatrix's face as she died. Wren asleep in a cart, drooling on burlap.

All of it. Everything the memory had touched.

"There has to be another way," he said.

"If there were, Vane would have found it. She's read every text your guild ever stole." Wren's voice didn't waver, and somehow that was worse than if it had. "I'm not telling you not to do it. The free cities need the truth more than you need your yesterdays. I'm telling you—" She stopped. Started again. "I'm telling you that tomorrow, before we reach the gates, I want to spend one hour with someone who knows me. Because after tomorrow, nobody will. You'll meet me like a stranger. And strangers don't get to know what you know about me."

"What do I know about you?"

"That you talk in your sleep," she said. "That you're afraid of deep water and you hide it by making jokes about drowning. That when you stole grief for a living, you never once took a happy memory, because you thought happiness belonged to people too much to be sold." Her eyes were bright and furious. "I know all of that because the memory told me, leaking into my dreams the same way it leaks into yours. We've been sharing a head for weeks, Felix. Tomorrow I lose that. So tonight, I'm not letting go."

She leaned her head against his shoulder. After a moment, stiffly, like a man handling something explosive, he let his cheek rest against her hair.

Across the fire, Vane watched them with an expression Felix couldn't name. When she caught him looking, she stood, brushed off her knees, and walked out into the dark toward the picket line, alone.

* * *

He found her at midnight, or near it, standing at the edge of the shrine circle where the land fell away toward the east. The smoke on the horizon had a glow beneath it now, orange as a banked forge. The usurper's army, lighting its campfires across a burning country.

"You should be sleeping," Vane said, without turning.

"So should you."

"Inquisitors don't sleep before battles. Bad luck." She was quiet a moment. "That's a joke. Mostly."

Felix came to stand beside her. Below them, the refugee road wound away into darkness, dotted with the small fires of people who had run out of country to run through. "Your riders," he said. "Forty of them. They know what they're riding toward?"

"They know. I made them read the ledger. All of it — the purges, the erasures, the names." Her jaw tightened. "Aldric's name was on page nine. My brother. Taken when I was fourteen. I joined the order to find out what happened to him, and instead I spent eleven years doing it to other people's brothers." She laughed, short and bitter. "You showed me his face, thief. In that stolen memory. Do you know what I did with it?"

"No."

"I memorized it. Then I burned my own copy of the ledger and started keeping a new one." She turned to look at him, and her eyes in the dark were dry and very steady. "Tomorrow, when you do what you're going to do, the council will believe you or they won't. But if they don't — if the usurper's gold buys the vote — my riders and I hold the gate square until the truth gets out anyway. Dead or alive, the story leaves Carrowgate. Understand?"

"That's a terrible plan."

"It's the only plan I have. I'm an ex-inquisitor. Terrible plans are our whole tradition." She almost smiled. Then: "One more thing. The rite of return. Have you actually read what happens to the giver?"

"Some of it."

"Read the rest." She pulled a folded paper from her jacket — pages stolen from a guild archive, dense with the old script — and pressed it into his hand. "Because if you walk into that council circle planning to survive it, you'll hesitate. And hesitation in front of thirty delegates is how secrets die. Go in knowing exactly what it costs. That's the only way thieves like you ever manage honesty — when they've priced it first."

She walked back toward the fire, leaving him alone with the pages and the glow of a burning country.

He read them by moonlight. And when he finished, he sat down hard on the cold grass and stayed there a long time, looking east, understanding at last exactly what he'd promised.

* * *

Dawn came up red behind them, and they rode.

The last twenty miles to Carrowgate were the longest of Felix's life. The road thickened with refugees until the cart could barely crawl; Vane's riders formed a wedge around them and simply pushed, parting the crowd with flat voices and drawn steel held low. People cursed them, then saw the inverted sigils and fell silent, pressing back against the verges. Word ran ahead of them like wind through wheat: *inquisitors, turned, riding escort — for what? For whom?*

By midday they could see the walls.

Carrowgate rose out of the plain like a grey cliff, vast and ancient, its towers hung with the banners of the free cities — the silver fish of Marrow, the green oak of Tarnholt, the sunburst of Callowmere. And outside the gates, a sea of tents. Refugees beyond counting, and among them, unmistakable even at distance, the black pavilions of the usurper's embassy, flying the crowned tower of House Malvane.

"They beat us to the door," Odile said grimly. "Not the army. The *argument*. Their envoys are already inside, buying votes while we've been dodging arrows on the ash roads."

"Then we go in now," Felix said. "Today. This hour."

"The council sits at sunset," Vane said. "Formal session, full assembly — the envoys requested it themselves. They want a public vote on recognizing the 'stability' of the Malvane succession." Her mouth twisted around the word. "Arrogant bastards scheduled their own coronation. Which means they've also scheduled ours."

They came down onto the great west road, and the crowd closed around them, and Felix felt it begin — a pressure in his chest, a warmth spreading under his ribs like sunlight through amber. The memory knew. Beatrix's ghost-bound secret, the face and the bloodline and the founding betrayal, all of it stirring awake inside him, straining toward the city where it was meant to be spoken.

Wren grabbed his hand across the cart bed. Her grip was iron. "It's starting, isn't it."

"It's starting."

"Then hold on," she said. "Both of us. Until the circle."

The gates of Carrowgate stood open — they always stood open; that was the point of free cities — but the gate square was packed, and at its center, barring the road to the council spire, stood a rank of house guards in Malvane black and gold. Their captain stepped forward, hand raised, voice carrying over the crowd:

"By order of the envoy of the rightful crown, no armed company enters the council precinct. Disperse, or be detained."

Vane rode forward slowly. Forty riders behind her. Forty inverted sigils catching the afternoon light.

"Captain," she called back, clear and cold, "look at these marks. Then look at your warrant. One of us is enforcing the law of the free cities today." She drew her sword — not fast, not threatening, just drawing it, letting the crowd see — "and it isn't you."

For a long moment nothing moved. The crowd held its breath. Somewhere a baby cried, and a gull screamed off the harbor walls, and Felix felt the memory in his chest beating like a drum before the charge.

Then the captain looked — really looked — at the inverted sigils, at the faces of men and women he'd surely trained beside, and something in his posture broke. He stepped aside.

"Clear the way!" someone shouted from the crowd, and the shout became ten shouts, became a roar, became the whole gate square thundering as forty riders and one battered cart plunged through, up the processional avenue, toward the council spire rising ahead of them with its doors just opening for the sunset session —

— and on the spire's highest balcony, black-clad and motionless, a figure watching them come. Not a guard. Not an envoy.

The usurper himself had come to Carrowgate.

And he was smiling.
