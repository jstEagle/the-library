## 22. A Man With No Yesterday

The morning after the rite, Felix woke in a room he did not recognize and did not panic.

That was the first strange thing. He lay on a narrow bed in a stone cell that had been made kind — a window, a basin of water, a sprig of some flowering vine someone had tucked into a jar — and he studied the ceiling the way a man studies a stranger's face, and felt nothing but a mild, almost pleasant curiosity. *Whose room is this?* And then, before the question could sharpen into fear: *Does it matter?*

It didn't. That was the gift of it. Every fear he had ever carried had been stored in memories he no longer owned.

He got up. His body knew things his mind did not — how to braid the laces of the boots by the door, which hand to favor when he caught his reflection's eye in the polished basin, how to stand so that a doorway could not be blocked behind him. The thief was still in there somewhere, folded into muscle and habit like a knife sewn into a coat lining. He flexed his right palm and found the map-scar gone, the skin smooth and pale as a fresh page.

*You gave it all away,* said a voice in him that might have been Beatrix's or might have been his own. *Every coin in the purse.*

And the purse was empty, and he was still standing.

* * *

Carrowgate had changed overnight, or perhaps it had been changing for years and only now dared show it. From the wall above the Gate of Bells — Wren had told him its name, patiently, twice, the way you tell a child or a guest — Felix watched the free cities arm.

Not the ugly arming of panic. This was something older and more deliberate: smithies running from dawn with their shutters thrown open so the whole street could hear the hammers, grain carts rolling in under banners that hadn't flown since the war, old men drilling boys in the market square with wooden staves and an air of festival. A city remembering it had a spine.

Wren stood at the center of it whether she wanted to or not. He could see her from here, down in the council yard, small and straight-backed in a borrowed coat of grey wool, surrounded by men and women three times her age who kept glancing at her the way sailors glance at weather. She had spoken to the council at dawn. He hadn't heard the words — they'd kept him well away from it, gently, the way you keep a lit lantern away from dry straw — but he'd seen the faces afterward. Nobody looked afraid anymore. That was what she'd done, whatever she'd said. She had walked into a room full of frightened powerful people and taken the fear out of them like a splinter.

The memory of her proof still lived in the world, even if it no longer lived in him. He knew that the way you know a fire has burned down to embers in another room — by warmth, not sight. Somewhere in the council archive, under seal and ward, Beatrix's spirit-witness sat restored: the heir's face, the hiding place, the founding betrayal, all of it given back whole. And wound through it, braided in where it had never belonged, was a boy's childhood — a stolen name, a mother's voice, a first snowfall — evidence now, testimony, part of the record of what the usurper's guilds had done.

His own yesterday, filed among the crimes of kings.

He found he could look at that thought without flinching too. Mostly.

"You're supposed to be resting."

Vane. She came up the wall stairs without hurry, out of armor for the first time he'd ever seen her, wearing a plain soldier's coat with the inquisitor's sigil cut away at the shoulder — not removed cleanly, but cut, the way you cut a brand off a stolen horse. Her riders were scattered along the battlements below, mixed in with Carrowgate's militia, and nobody was watching anybody with a hand near a weapon. It was the strangest peace Felix had ever stood inside.

"I don't know how," he said. "Resting. I think I used to be bad at it."

"You were terrible at it." She stopped beside him at the parapet, close enough to talk without raising her voice. "You fidgeted. You counted exits. Odile said watching you try to sit still in her cabin was like watching a cat pretend to be furniture."

"Odile talks about me?"

"Odile talks about everyone. It's how she thinks." Vane looked out at the city. Below, a column of spearmen was forming in the yard around Wren, and the morning light came off their points like a shoal of fish turning. "She'll live. The healers say the wound missed everything that matters. She says that proves nothing matters, which is how I know she's fine."

Felix nodded. There was a silence between them that wasn't comfortable exactly, but was honest, which was better.

"You came to check on me," he said.

"I came to tell you the council voted an hour ago. Full mobilization. The border lords are declaring for Wren faster than anyone predicted — two already, this morning." She paused. "There's going to be a war, Okonkwo. Maybe a short one, maybe not. But there's going to be one, and it started because a dying courier trusted a thief, and a thief chose the harder thing twice."

"Three times," Felix said. "I also refused to sell you the secret when you put a knife to my throat. That one cost me sleep I can't even remember losing."

Something moved across Vane's face — not quite a smile, but the ground where a smile might someday be rebuilt. "I know," she said. "I've read your file. All of it. Including the parts about you." She turned to face him fully then, and her eyes were the same cold grey they had always been, but the cold had thawed at the edges, like a river in late winter. "The guild records are being opened. Every safehouse, every ledger. There will be names in them. People who lost things. Some of those people are going to come looking for the man who can carry memories back."

"I can't do it again," Felix said. "The rite took everything I had. Whatever was left in me that could hold a memory and give it back — it's gone. I'm just a man now."

"Yes," Vane said. "I know. I watched you do it." For a moment she looked away, out at the spears and the banners, and her jaw worked once. "The man whose face was in that war-memory. Aldric. My—" She stopped. Started again, flatter. "The usurper ordered him erased forty years ago and I spent my life hunting thieves because I couldn't hunt the man who gave the order. Your rite gave him back to me. Not alive. Just... real. A face instead of a hole." She looked at him again. "I wanted you to know that. Before you walk out of this city thinking you gave up everything for nothing."

"It wasn't nothing," Felix said.

"No," Vane agreed. "It wasn't." She gripped his shoulder once, hard, a soldier's grip, and went back down the stairs, and Felix stood alone at the parapet and let the wind off the sea push at him like a hand testing whether he'd fall.

* * *

Wren found him at midday, sitting on the steps of the Gate of Bells eating bread and cheese that someone had pressed on him because he was, apparently, famous now — *the man who gave himself away*, they called him in the streets, and they said it the way you'd say *the man who jumped into the river for a stranger*: half awe, half pity, all wrong.

"You're leaving," she said.

It wasn't a question. She dropped onto the step beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched, and stole a piece of his cheese with the unselfconscious ease of someone who had grown up sharing food with strangers on roads. She was wearing the grey coat again. Under it, at her throat, hung a thin chain with a ring on it — the signet of the old line, dug out of a vault that morning, too big for her hand. She kept touching it like a woman touching a bruise to check it still hurt.

"The council wants to give you anything you ask for," she said. "A house. A title. They want to make you the Keeper of Records, which is very funny, if you think about it."

"I thought about it," Felix said. "For about four seconds. Then I imagined sitting in a room full of other people's memories for the rest of my life and I had to go lie down."

"So you're leaving."

He turned the bread over in his hands. "I don't know who I am, Wren. That's not self-pity, it's just — inventory. I know how to pick a lock and I know how your face looks when you're trying not to cry and I know that I loved you, past tense, because the feeling is still in my chest even though the reasons have all been handed over to an archive. Everything else is a blank page with a thief's handwriting on it." He looked at her. "I can't stand next to you while you rebuild a kingdom and be a monument to sacrifice. I'd rather go find out if there's a person under all the holes."

"And if there isn't?" she asked quietly.

"Then I'll build one," he said. "I'm told I'm good at making things out of stolen parts."

Wren laughed — a short, wet sound — and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, furious at herself for it, and then she took his face in both hands and kissed him, brief and fierce, the way you kiss someone at a departure gate. When she pulled back her eyes were bright and level.

"Then here's what happens," she said. "The war comes, and we win it, because we have to. And when it's over — a year, five, ten — I'm going to find every ledger, every safehouse, every vault the guild ever kept. The council's already sworn it. Every stolen memory gets returned, every name gets given back." She tapped his chest with one finger, right over the hollow place. "Somewhere in this world there's a record of a boy named—"

"Don't tell me," Felix said quickly.

"—a boy," she amended, smiling, "whose childhood is sitting in a box with a number on it. And when we find it, I'm going to bring it to you myself, wherever you are, and I'm going to hold it out, and you're going to get to choose. Take it back or leave it. Your name, your mother's voice, the first snowfall. All of it. That's a promise, Felix Okonkwo. And I'm told promises from queens outrank everything."

"I thought you hated being called that."

"I hate lots of things," Wren said. "I'm learning to survive it." She stood, brushed off her coat, and became, visibly, the woman the council yard needed — spine straight, chin level, the ring heavy at her throat. But at the last moment she crouched back down to his eye level, and the queen fell away and it was just Wren again, the girl from the sacked refuge who had chosen significance with her eyes open.

"Walk out the gate like you mean it," she said. "Not like you're fleeing. Everyone in this city is going to watch you go, and I want them to see a man choosing the road. So they'll know it can be done."

* * *

He left at dusk, on foot, because horses made him uneasy in a way he suspected predated his memory loss and he had decided to trust his instincts about himself even when he couldn't source them.

The road west ran along the coast toward the smaller towns — Greyharbor, the salt flats, places with no councils and no archives, where a man with no name and no yesterday could hire out for honest work or dishonest work depending on his mood. Odile had offered him a berth as far as Greyharbor; he'd declined, and she'd shrugged and pressed a purse into his hand anyway, saying, "Consider it severance for nearly getting me killed, minus what you owe me for actually getting me killed a little." The purse was heavier than severance should be. He hadn't argued. Some habits survived the erasure of everything that formed them.

At the gate, the guards knew him — of course they did, the whole city knew him — and they waved him through without a word, and then it was just the road, and the sea on his left going bronze and rose in the last light, and the wind carrying the smell of salt and woodsmoke and, faintly, from the city behind him, the ringing of the bells.

They were ringing for Wren. Or for the mobilization. Or maybe just because it was evening and bells ring in the evening. He decided it was for him. He was allowed to decide things like that now. It was one of the few advantages of having no history — every interpretation was fresh, every omen unclaimed.

He walked.

The grief was there. He wouldn't insult it by pretending otherwise. It sat in him like a stone sits in a riverbed — permanent, smoothed, shaping the water's path without stopping it. He grieved for a mother whose voice he would never hear again unless a queen kept a promise in a war-torn decade. He grieved for Beatrix, whom he remembered only as a fact rather than a face, and who had trusted him anyway, on credit, against all evidence. He grieved for the man he had apparently been — competent, careful, hollowed out and calling it freedom — because that man had died on the council floor giving everything away, and dead men deserve mourning too.

But grief, he was discovering, was just love with nowhere to live. And a man with no yesterday had an enormous amount of room.

The road bent north along the cliffs. In the last light he could see, far off, the smoke of Greyharbor rising straight and calm into a sky going slowly, magnificently dark — and above it, faint as breath on glass, a shimmer that had not been there a year ago. Magic, seeping back into the edges of the world now that the wards were broken, now that the truth was loose. The scholars said the Hollow itself was dissolving, its fog lifting mile by mile, a valley full of people waking up to discover they'd been living in a pocket. Someone would have to help them. Someone who knew how the forgetting worked from the inside.

Felix stopped on the cliff road and looked back once. Carrowgate blazed behind him, armed and awake, crowned with bells, holding a queen in a borrowed coat. Ahead of him the world went on for miles, unwritten.

He pulled his collar up against the sea wind, checked his purse, checked the road, checked the shadows out of a habit that had outlived its owner — and smiled at his own hands, empty, scarred with nothing, ready to be filled.

Then Felix Okonkwo, who had no name he could prove and no past he could spend, walked west into the gathering dark, choosing every step of it — a man with no yesterday, and, for the first time in either of his lives, no fear of tomorrow.
