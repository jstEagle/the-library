## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The poison in the king's stew tasted like burnt almonds and river stones, and Wren Sandoval knew before the spoon touched her lips.

That was the first wrong thing. Not the poison — poisons were her trade, and this one was a decent effort, a hemlock derivative cut with something vegetal to mask the bitterness. A competent attempt. The kind that killed three of every four tasters in the realm, if the gossip in the servants' hall could be believed, and killed them slowly enough that a good taster might save the king and still die herself.

No. The first wrong thing was that she *knew*. Not suspected. Not reasoned from experience, the way she'd been trained — tongue against memory, note by catalogued note. She simply knew, the way you know your own name, the way you know which hand is your left. The poison sat on her palate like an old acquaintance's voice in a dark room.

The second wrong thing came after she swallowed.

Because she did swallow. That was procedure: taste, hold, swallow, wait. Master Corren had drilled it into her eight years ago, when she was nineteen and hollow-cheeked and desperate enough for coin to volunteer her body as a shield for a man she'd never seen. *A taster who spits learns nothing,* he'd said. *A taster who spits is guessing.* So Wren swallowed everything they put in front of her — the arsenics and the nightshades, the slow metals and the quick acids — and she had vomited blood exactly once, at twenty-three, and never told anyone.

She swallowed now, standing at the great marble counter in the palace kitchens with forty cooks pretending not to watch her, and the poison went down like water.

Worse than like water. It went down like *broth* on a winter morning. Like something her body had been waiting for. She felt it settle into her stomach and spread outward, warm and familiar, and somewhere beneath her breastbone something answered it — some deep, patient part of her rising up to meet the toxin the way a host rises to greet a guest whose arrival was expected.

The whole thing took four seconds. Nobody saw. That was the third wrong thing: she had learned, without ever deciding to learn it, how to keep her face still.

"Well?" Steward Ambrose hovered at her elbow, a narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and the permanent expression of someone who expected to be blamed for weather. His eyes flicked from the stew pot to her mouth and back. "Sandoval."

"Give it a moment." Wren set the spoon down with deliberate care. Her hands were steady. Her heart was not. "There's something in the leeks."

"There's always something in the leeks," Ambrose said sourly. "Leeks are dirt and disappointment."

"Something *else* in the leeks."

Behind the counter, the undercook — a boy called Pell, fifteen, freckled, too honest for his own good — went the color of uncooked dough. He didn't run. He didn't speak. But his eyes went to the door, just once, just briefly, and Wren had spent enough years reading faces across tasting counters to know what that glance meant: he knew who had come through that kitchen, and he was afraid of them.

She filed it away. Filing things away was most of the job.

The warmth in her chest faded slowly, the way a bell's hum fades after it's struck, and Wren listened to it go with a feeling she couldn't name. Grief, maybe. Or homesickness, though homesick for *what*, for *where*? She had no home but a rented room above a cobbler's shop, no family but dead ones, no history but the one she'd built out of ledgers and pay records: orphaned at nine, workhouse, scullery, then Corren finding her during a cholera scare when half the palace staff was sick and she wasn't, and asking her, casually, whether she'd ever considered steadier work.

*Steadier.* She almost laughed. Eight years of swallowing death for a wage, and the only steady thing about it was that she kept surviving.

"Nothing," she said at last.

Ambrose blinked. "Nothing?"

"A bad batch of leeks. Slightly bitter. I'd have the pot scraped and remade from fresh stores anyway — the king's been complaining about the flavor since Tuesday, hasn't he?" This was true. The king complained about everything since Tuesday; Tuesday had been the day his mistress left court.

"You're certain?" Ambrose leaned closer, dropping his voice. "Certain-certain? Because if there's something and you pass it—"

"If there's something, I'll be dead before the king lifts his spoon, and you can tell whoever asks you that I died doing my duty, which will look very noble on your report." Wren picked up her napkin, folded it, set it beside the spoon. Her movements were smooth. Practiced. A liar's choreography, each gesture placed where it belonged. "Scrape the pot. Watch Pell."

Pell flinched like she'd struck him.

*Watch Pell,* she hadn't meant as an accusation — or had she? She watched the boy's face crumple and understood, with a taster's instinct for dosage, that she had just given him exactly as much fear as would make him silent for a week and no more. Good. Silence was safer for everyone, including him. Whoever had laced that stew hadn't used a servant as their instrument by choice; they'd used Pell because Pell was near the leeks and easy to frighten, and frightening him further would only push him toward braver, worse men.

Ambrose nodded, satisfied, and moved off to terrorize the scullery. Wren stayed at the counter.

The residue was the problem.

Procedure was clear: any suspected contaminant went into a sealed lead jar, jar went to Corren, Corren sent it to the alchemists' wing, and two weeks later a report came back that said nothing useful in language so dry it could start fires. Wren had delivered a dozen jars over eight years. She had never once wanted to keep anything.

She wanted to keep this.

The wanting frightened her more than the poison had. Because the poison was explainable — court politics explained everything in this city, and a poisoned king was merely a busy week — but the wanting was not. Her hands wanted it. Some animal part of her, the part that had risen warm beneath her breastbone when the toxin slid down her throat, was leaning toward the dregs in the bottom of the pot like a plant toward light.

*I know this,* that part of her was saying. *I have known this longer than you have known me.*

Wren Sandoval had survived her childhood by refusing to believe in anything she couldn't verify with her own senses, and her own senses were currently telling her that her own body was keeping secrets from her mind. She hated it. She reached for the small glass vial in her apron pocket — tasters carried vials, sample jars, all manner of official equipment, all stamped with the crown's seal — and she did not let herself think while she did it. Thinking would stop her. She scraped the cooled residue from the pot's inner seam with a clean knife tip, funneled it into the vial, corked it, and dropped it back into her pocket in the space of ten heartbeats.

Then she thought about what she'd done, and her mouth went dry.

Eight years of honesty. Eight years of being the one person in the palace whose reports nobody questioned, because a taster who lied about poison was a taster who'd sell the throne within the month. Her value was her incorruptibility, such as it was — the fact that she wanted nothing, owed nothing, belonged to no faction. And now here she stood with stolen evidence warming her pocket and a lie already cooling on her tongue, and the terrible thing, the thing she would chew on all night in her room above the cobbler's shop, was how *easy* it had been. How natural. As if the lying were also an inheritance.

"Sandoval." Ambrose again, at the doorway, holding his ledger. "The jar. Procedure."

"Already sealed it for Corren," she said smoothly.

"I don't see it."

"It's with the cold-larder samples. I'll send it along after the midday trays are cleared." Another lie, laid flat and neat as a card in a trick she hadn't known she was dealing. "You want the midday trays cleared, don't you? His Majesty eats in an hour."

Ambrose did want the trays cleared. Ambress always wanted the trays cleared. He grunted and vanished, and Wren stood alone at the counter with the empty stew pot and the sound of forty cooks resuming their work behind her, knives on boards, water boiling, the ordinary machinery of feeding a kingdom grinding on as if the world hadn't tilted four degrees sideways inside her chest.

She finished the shift. Of course she finished the shift. There was a venison course, and a soup of white beans, and a honeyed custard that she tasted with particular care because paranoia, once invited, does not leave politely. All of it clean. All of it ordinary. By the time the last tray went up the service stair, the warm answering presence under her breastbone had gone quiet again, and Wren could almost persuade herself she'd imagined it.

Almost. Except for the vial.

* * *

Her room above the cobbler's shop had one window, one chair, one bed, and — since tonight — one candle burning low beside a scrap of white porcelain she'd borrowed from the palace infirmary three years ago and never returned. She decanted a grain of the stolen residue onto the porcelain, added a drop of water, and did what Master Corren had taught her to do in her first month: the observation ritual. Color. Smell. Texture against glass. Reaction to heat, reaction to silver, reaction to breath held close.

Most of it told her nothing new. Hemlock base, yes. The vegetal masking agent was fennel-something, cleverly chosen because fennel already lived in the kitchen. Standard court work, priced accordingly — this wasn't cheap guild product but it wasn't masterwork either. Somewhere between those facts was a buyer with moderate funds and moderate connections, which described half the nobility and a worrying number of clergy.

It was the last test that undid her.

Corren's final step, taught with a wink: *the blood test.* Prick your thumb, touch the sample. Most toxins do nothing; a few change color in ways the alchemists pretend to understand. It was party-trick chemistry, he'd said. Nothing more.

Wren pricked her thumb on the needle she used for her sewing — she sewed badly and rarely, but owned the kit — and touched the bead of red to the thin smear of poison.

The smear drank the blood.

Not absorbed it. Not mixed with it. *Drank* it, the way dry earth drinks rain, greedily, instantly, and then — she leaned so close her nose nearly touched the porcelain — the faint gray residue flushed a deep, rich blue, darker than lapis, dark as the sky at midnight, and for one heartbeat she felt the warmth rise under her breastbone again, strong, glad, *recognizing*, and the candle flame stood perfectly still in the drafty room as if the air itself were listening.

Then it faded. Gray again. Ordinary. The candle guttered in a gust from the window seam and Wren sat back on her heels, thumb bleeding forgotten, heart slamming against her ribs.

She ran the test twice more to be sure. Both times, the same. Blood in, midnight blue out, that vast patient warmth stirring in her chest like something rolling over in its sleep.

She wrapped the vial in oilcloth and hid it inside the hollow bedpost where she kept her savings, which took her four minutes because her hands shook so badly, and then she sat on the edge of her bed in the dark and made herself think, because thinking was the one discipline she had never surrendered to anyone.

Facts. One: she was immune to poisons beyond anything training could account for. Two: whatever this specific toxin was, it didn't merely fail to kill her — it *fed* something in her, or something in her fed on it. Three: the residue reacted to her blood like metal to a lodestone. Four: her mother had died when Wren was nine, in the mountains, in the north, in Thornfell, and had told her precisely nothing about anything, ever, except *don't ask questions, little wren, questions are loud.*

Questions are loud. Wren had spent twenty-seven years being quiet.

She looked at the window. Beyond it, over the rooftops of the lower city, the sky was clear and cold and crowded with stars, the same sky she'd ignored for eight years of night shifts and early mornings. She found herself searching it anyway — north, past the palace spires, past the hills — toward mountains she barely remembered, where ward-towers older than the kingdom kept the border sealed against things the histories declined to name.

Her mother had come from there. Her mother had fled there. And tomorrow — she'd almost forgotten, it seemed suddenly like a message from another life — tomorrow she was assigned to escort a supply caravan over those very mountains, a routine posting, smugglers' roads, two weeks' pay.

Routine.

Wren lay back on her bed, fully dressed, and did not sleep. Under her breastbone, that patient warmth turned over again, restless now, like a dog hearing its master's boots on a far-off road.

Somewhere far to the north, she was almost sure she could feel it — not hear, not see, *feel* — the wards of Thornfell guttering, one by one, like candles in a long dark hall.

And she was almost sure they were guttering *toward her*.
