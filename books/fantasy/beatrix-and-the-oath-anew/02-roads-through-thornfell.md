## 2. Roads Through Thornfell

The steward's writ said *escort*, but everyone on the caravan road knew what it meant: the crown wanted eyes in the high country, and I was the cheapest pair of eyes it owned. A taster's salary buys a taster's trust.

"You've done mountain work before?" asked the caravan master, a broad woman named Ostre with forearms like ship's cable and a voice that carried over six mules without effort.

"Once," I said, which was a lie shaped like a truth. I had crossed Thornfell exactly once before, at nine years old, strapped to a stranger's back with my mother's hand pressed over my eyes so I wouldn't see whatever it was she didn't want me to remember. I remembered none of it except her palm. It smelled of lamp oil and iron filings, and for years afterward I could not pass a smithy without my throat tightening.

"Good," said Ostre, not believing me and not caring. "Keep your opinions to yourself and your knife where I can see it, and we'll get along."

We left Corvane at gray dawn with fourteen mules, nine drivers, forty crates of salt pork and quarrel-heads bound for the garrison at Highmoor, and one food-taster whose presence nobody could explain and nobody questioned, because the crown's seal was on the manifest and questions about the crown had gone out of fashion in Corvane sometime around the second failed harvest.

I rode at the rear, which suited me. Rear riders see what the road leaves behind.

The first day was ordinary country — terraced barley going gold at the edges, shepherd dogs lying flat in the heat like dropped cloaks, a shrine to some minor river-saint with coins greened in its basin. The second day the mountains took us.

Thornfell does not rise so much as assemble itself, range behind range, each one darker than the last, until the world is nothing but stone and the thin green stitches of pine holding it together. The smuggler roads — Ostre called them *the old paths* and spat after she said it — thread through all of it like veins through a wrist. They predate the kingdom. They predate, if you believe the waystone-keepers, the idea of roads. The crown has mapped them eleven times in two hundred years and the maps never match twice.

"Nobody uses these anymore," one of the younger drivers told me, proud of his knowledge. "Not since the wards went up proper. Why risk the stones when you can take the king's highway?"

"Why indeed," I said.

Because the king's highway runs through the low passes, and the low passes have been closed by rockfall three times this season, and because somebody in the quartermaster's office wanted a taster on the old paths specifically, close to the wards, and I had stopped pretending I didn't wonder why.

I felt the wards before I saw them.

That is not something I would have known how to say a week ago. But there is a thing my body does now, has done since the night in the kitchens when the poison slid down my throat and my blood simply *filed it away*, and the thing it does is notice. The back of my neck prickles. My teeth ache faintly, like the first warning of a coming cold. In Corvane I had decided it was grief, or nerves, or the residue of everything I'd swallowed professionally over seven years finally settling into a bill come due.

On the third morning, riding into the shadow of the first great ward-line, I understood that my teeth were aching because something up ahead was *hungry*.

"There." Ostre reined up beside me and pointed with her chin.

I followed her look and saw — nothing, at first. Then the air above the ridge opposite shivered, the way air shivers above a forge, and I caught it: a seam of pale light running along the ridgeline like a crack in porcelain, following the contour of the mountain for as far as I could see in either direction. A ward-line. I'd seen them from the city walls at festival time, when they were lit for ceremony — steady as masonry, blue-white and utterly still.

This one flickered.

Not evenly. Not like a torch guttering in wind. It stuttered in irregular bursts, brightening here, dying there, and where it died the darkness behind it seemed — I want to write *deeper*, but that isn't right. It seemed *interested*. Like a pupil dilating.

"Been doing that all summer," Ostre said. Her voice was carefully bored, the voice of a woman who had decided long ago which things were hers to worry about and which belonged to other people. "Ward-keepers came through in spring. Said it was seasonal. Snowmelt or some such."

"Does snowmelt usually make them flicker?"

"How should I know? I drive mules." She turned her mount downhill toward the others. "Eyes forward, taster."

But I watched the ward-line until the road bent it out of view, and my teeth ached the whole time, and twice — twice — the flickering paused entirely, the whole seam going dark for the length of a heartbeat, and both times I had the distinct and unshakeable sensation that it had paused in order to *look at me*.

* * *

The waystones began that afternoon.

Every child in the vale grows up knowing about waystones, the same way children grow up knowing about wolves — as a thing that belongs to stories and to grandmothers and not to the sensible daylight world. Standing stones, older than the wards, scattered along the old paths at roughly a day's walk apart, each one carved top to bottom in script no scholar has ever fully read. The crown's position, official for two centuries, is that the stones are monuments. Decorative. Dumb.

My mother's position, according to the single sentence of hers I remember verbatim, was different. She said: *Never pass a waystone without touching it, little wren, and never tell anyone why.*

So of course I had spent my entire adult life passing waystones without touching them, out of spite, or grief, or the simple animal stubbornness of an orphan who resents being handed rules by someone who then had the discourtesy to die. There are two waystones on the king's highway between Corvane and the coast. I have walked past them perhaps sixty times. Nothing has ever happened, which I always took as vindication.

The first stone on the smuggler road stood at a bend above a ravine, hip-high in bracken, tilted like a tired old man leaning on the wind. Its carvings were worn nearly smooth. As our column filed past it, I did what I always do — looked straight ahead, kept my hands on the reins, touched nothing.

It spoke my family name.

There is no honest way to soften that. The sound did not come from the stone's direction so much as from inside the stone, or from inside the air immediately surrounding the stone, a dry whisper like pages turning, and the sound it made was *Sandoval*. Not pronounced the way the steward pronounces it in the pay ledgers, *SAND-oh-val*, but the old way, the way my mother said it under her breath when she thought I was asleep: *San-DHO-vahl*, with a weight on the middle syllable, a falling cadence, a word meant to be said quietly in the dark.

I stopped my horse.

Behind me, a mule complained. Ahead, the column creaked onward, oblivious. The stone stood in the bracken saying nothing further, wearing its worn carvings, tilted against the wind, and I sat in the saddle with my heart going like a fist on a door and every professional instinct I owned telling me to ride on, report nothing, swallow it the way I swallow everything.

*Your body knows something your mind does not.* That was the thought, arriving in Halric Vane's dry voice though he'd never spoken it to me, though I hadn't yet met him — no, that comes later; forgive me, memory rearranges itself around what it needs — the thought arrived in my own voice, and it sounded frightened.

I did not touch the stone. That much of my mother's rule I kept, out of pure terror. But I leaned from the saddle, close enough that my breath should have fogged its surface on that cold mountain air, and I said, very quietly:

"What did you say?"

The stone said nothing more. But the ward-line on the far ridge — I could just see it over the shoulder of the mountain — flickered once, deliberately, like a wink. Or an answer.

I rode to catch up with the column and did not look back, and my eyes stung the whole way, and it took me another mile to admit that what I was feeling was homesickness. Homesickness for a house I could not picture, on a road I could not remember, among people who spoke my name in a cadence I only recognized because it lived somewhere below language, in the marrow, where my mother had hidden it along with everything else.

* * *

We made camp that night in a hanging valley the drivers called the Kettle, a bowl of ground ringed by peaks, with a cold stream running through it and just enough level space for the tents. Supper was salt pork and barley and Ostre's opinion of the weather, delivered at length to an audience of no one. The men rolled into their bedrolls early. Mountain nights come on fast and mean, and smart people meet them unconscious.

I stayed up. I told myself it was to keep watch, which was absurd — I was the least watch-capable person on that mountain — and then I told myself the truth, which was that I couldn't sleep in the presence of that flicker. From the Kettle we could see the ward-line on two separate ridges now, north and east, both of them stuttering, and between their pulses the sky above the highest peak looked wrong. Not cloudy. Not dark. Wrong, the way a room is wrong when a book has been moved on a shelf and you can't say which one.

I banked the fire and got out my kit, because hands want work when the mind won't shut up. Seven years of tasting have taught them trades: how to grind, how to strain, how to coax a residue out of cloth and charcoal. I had brought the vial from the kitchens — of course I had, I had stolen crown evidence and lied to a superior officer about it, and the fact that I felt no guilt whatsoever was its own small alarm bell. I set it on a flat stone in the firelight and looked at it again, properly, for the first time since Corvane.

The residue had dried to a film the color of old ash. Under the flame it did nothing. Poisons do things under flame — they shift color, they smoke, they crystallize into shapes a trained eye can read like handwriting. This one sat there being gray, and my teeth ached, and slowly, watching it, I noticed what I should have noticed in the kitchen with my tongue on my teeth and my pulse refusing to rise.

The ache wasn't near the vial. It was oriented.

Turn the vial north, toward the nearest ridge and its guttering light, and the ache sharpened. Turn it south and it faded to nothing. I did it four times. North, south, north, south. A compass needle made of toothache, pointing at the wards.

"That's not possible," I informed the fire.

The fire, being a fire, declined to argue. But the ward-line on the eastern ridge chose that moment to flare — brighter than I'd yet seen it, a clean hard line of white-blue stitched along the whole visible crest, beautiful, the way festival lights over Corvane were beautiful — and for the length of that flare my whole mouth lit up, warm and strange and almost sweet, like the first taste of honeyed wine, and every hair on my arms stood up, and I understood two things with total certainty and zero proof.

The wards were drinking. And they were drinking from me.

From wherever I sat, a thousand feet below them, across miles of granite and pine, something in those mountains had found the taste of Sandoval blood in the dark and was pulling on it, gently, patiently, the way a sleeping infant pulls at the breast, and had been pulling on it — the thought arrived whole and horrible — *had been pulling on it for years*. For decades. For as long as the wards had stood, maybe, if the wards had always known where the blood was. If my mother had moved to the capital not to escape anything but to be *available*. If her death, three years ago, of an illness the physicians could not name, a wasting, a fading —

No. I put that down. Some thoughts you pick up and some you leave where they lie until you're strong enough to lift them.

I corked the vial. I put it away deep in my pack, wrapped in the shirt I'd wear tomorrow, next to my skin, because apparently I was the kind of person now who slept beside stolen poisons and talked to fires. Around me the camp breathed. Above me the stars burned in their courses, steady and numerous and indifferent, and I remember thinking — I want this written down somewhere, even if nowhere reads it — I remember thinking *thank you* to them. Plainly. Like a fool. Thank you for staying lit.

Then I slept, and dreamed of a hand over my eyes smelling of lamp oil and iron, and a voice saying *not yet, little wren, not yet, but soon, and when it's soon I am so sorry.*

* * *

Morning broke gray and windless, which the drivers hated — "windless means held breath," Ostre muttered — and we climbed out of the Kettle onto the highest stretch of the old path, a shelf of road cut into the mountain's flank with the valley dropping away sheer on our left. The second waystone waited at the top of the climb.

This one was nothing like the first. It stood twice my height, unweathered, its carvings sharp as the day some forgotten hand cut them, and the script — I let myself look, since looking cost nothing — the script was not decoration. It was dense, structured, recursive, lines folding back into lines, and somewhere in the pattern recognition machinery of my brain, in the same place that tastes a poison and names it before I've decided to, something *read* it. Not the words. The grammar. The way you know a language is your own before you remember a single vocabulary word.

*Sandoval,* said the stone, in my mother's cadence, and then, softer, in a voice that was not my mother's and was not a voice at all but felt like snow beginning:

*Last of the smiths. The doors grow heavy. Come and see what your blood built.*

The column passed it. Mule after mule, driver after driver, none of them turning a head, and I sat my horse at the top of that climb shaking like a leaf in a style entirely unworthy of a crown officer, staring at a rock that had just issued me a personal invitation.

Come and see what your blood built.

I am not brave. Let the record show that. I am careful, I am observant, I have a good palate and a better poker face, and I survived seven years in the poison trade by treating every anomaly as someone else's emergency. The correct move was to ride on, deliver the salt pork, file no report, and lose myself in the city until the feeling passed.

I swung down from the horse.

Ostre called something behind me — my name, probably, the ledger version. I walked to the stone. The carvings up close had depth to them, channels worn by nothing, and in the deepest channel at the height of my hand there was a stain. Old. Rust-dark. Blood, if I had to guess, and my professional guess is worth something, and beneath it, half-filled with lichen, a smaller mark: a wren. A bird no bigger than a thumb, carved with three strokes.

My mother's hand. I know it the way I know her cadence. She drew that bird on the backs of letters, on steam-fogged windows, once in the dust of a windowsill where I found it after she died and scrubbed it away because grief is stupid.

"Tell me what to do," I whispered to the stone.

The ward-lines on both ridges flared at once — north and east simultaneously, which I later learned they never do, which the ward-keepers' manuals describe as structurally impossible — and my mouth flooded with that honey-warm ache, and the stone said, in the voice like snow beginning:

*Wait for the keeper. Wait for the night the stars go out. You will know it when you cannot see it.*

And then it said nothing ever again, not while I stood there, not while I mounted and rode past, not in any hour since.

We topped the pass at noon and started down toward Highmoor, and I rode at the rear with my teeth aching northward and my mother's wren printed on the inside of my eyelids, and behind us, above us, all along the spine of Thornfell, the wards flickered like guttering candles — and I counted the gaps between their pulses, because counting is what I do instead of panicking.

They were slowing.

Steadier intervals in the morning, longer ones by noon, and by the time the garrison towers of Highmoor rose out of the dusk ahead of us, the dark between each flare had grown long enough that I caught myself holding my breath through it, every time, waiting for the light to come back.

It always came back. That's what I told myself at the inn that night, over a cup of small beer I didn't taste. Wards are old, wards are tired, wards come back.

Two nights later, every star in the sky went out at once, and the light did not come back.
