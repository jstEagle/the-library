## 3. The Night Without Stars

The inn at Grayhollow had one good room, three bad ones, and a smell of wet wool and tallow that Wren Sandoval decided she could live with, because the alternative was sleeping in the cart with the salt pork.

She'd been on the border road nine days. The caravan master, a leathery woman named Ostrek who chewed cloves to hide the wine on her breath, had paid her passage as far as Thornfell's edge in exchange for tasting everything they ate — a cheap insurance policy, since a crown taster's tongue was worth more than any hired blade out here where the smuggler roads forked and nobody asked names twice.

Wren tasted the stew that evening out of habit more than duty. Mutton, barley, something wild and green she couldn't name, a whisper of wild garlic. Nothing. She told Ostrek it was clean and ate two bowls of it herself, which was more than she'd have done for the king's table some nights.

The common room filled up slow, the way rooms did this close to the wards. Carters mostly, a pair of wool factors arguing about tariffs, a young couple who kept their hoods up even indoors. And in the corner by the fire, an old man with a ward-keeper's faded blue sash worn like a joke he no longer remembered the punchline to, drinking small beer and staring at nothing.

Wren noticed him because of the sash. She noticed him more because of his hands, which never stopped moving — tapping the table, tracing lines on the wood grain, following patterns only he could see.

"You're staring," he said, without looking up.

"Your sash is out of fashion."

"It's been out of fashion for twenty years." He turned his cup a quarter-turn. "You've got a taster's hands. Thin wrists, careful fingers. You hold your spoon like it might lie to you."

"And you drink like a man keeping his head clear on purpose."

That got her a look. His eyes were gray and tired and missed very little. "Smart girl."

"Careful girl. It's different."

"Is it?" He smiled, and there was something under the smile that made her want to leave the room. "Ask me what I'm watching for."

"I didn't ask anything."

"No. But you will." He tapped the table once, twice, then drew a line through the air above it, slow, like closing a door. "Clear night tonight. First one in a week. You'd think that would be good news, this close to Thornfell."

"Why wouldn't it be?"

"Because the wards read the sky, girl. Old law. What's written above is written below." He finished his beer and stood, joints cracking like green wood. "If I were you, I'd sleep light."

He went up the stairs, and Wren sat by the fire a while longer, telling herself the crawling feeling at the base of her skull was just the weather coming in off the mountains.

* * *

It was not the weather.

She woke because the light through her shutters was wrong.

Not dark — dark she knew, dark was the default state of the world. This was *absent*. She lay in the bad second room listening to the silence and understood, in the way you understand a wrong note in a song you've known all your life, that the stars were gone.

She got up. Her hands were steady, which frightened her more than shaking would have. She opened the shutters and looked up at a sky like the inside of a closed eye. No moon either. No cloud, no haze — she could see the black clearly, the hard black of high altitude, the kind of night where you could count constellations down to the sixth magnitude. And there was nothing. Not dimmed. *Removed*, as if someone had reached into the heavens and taken back every point of light above the tree line, politely, completely, leaving no holes because there was nothing left to make holes in.

Downstairs, the common room was full of people who had all woken at once. Nobody screamed. That was the eerie part — thirty-odd travelers standing among the cold hearth and the overturned stools, all looking at the ceiling or the door, all silent, as if noise might give them away to whatever had done this.

Ostrek found her in the crowd. "Sandoval. You're crown service. Tell us what this is."

"I taste food," Wren said. "I don't taste skies."

"The old man does," someone said from the back. "The ward-keeper. Someone get him."

They got him. He came down the stairs moving faster than he had moved going up, and when he stepped into the yard and tilted his face to the starless black, Wren watched twenty years fall off him and land somewhere worse than age.

"Oh," he said softly. "Oh, you fools. You absolute—"

"What?" Wren said. "What is it?"

"They didn't go out." He was still looking up. His voice had gone flat and precise, the voice of a man reading from a ledger he'd hoped to burn. "Look at the edges. There's no fade, no scatter. A cloud dims. Smoke dims. This is a *severance*. Something cut the sky off from below, along the whole ward-line, at once." He lowered his head and looked at her, and in the darkness his eyes caught the last coals' glow like a cat's. "The towers are still holding. Barely. They're reading the sky through the old grammar, and the grammar just lost half its alphabet. They'll try to compensate. They'll burn themselves trying."

"How long?"

"If they're well-maintained? Days. If they're maintained the way the crown maintains things—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to. Everyone in that yard had dealt with the crown's maintenance.

The innkeeper, a broad, worried man called Dannel, pushed through with his daughter clinging to his side — a girl of maybe eight, hair loose, eyes enormous. "Mister, sir, the waystone by the mill's gone dark. My grandfather said if the stones ever went dark we should—"

"Should what?"

"Run south," the girl said.

Everyone looked at her. She said it again, calm as reciting a lesson: "Run south. Grandfather said the stones hold the mountain's coat on. If the coat comes off, run south before the mountain notices it's naked."

The old ward-keeper crouched to her level. "Your grandfather was stone-taught?"

"He talked to them. They talked back. Mama said he was mad."

"Mama was probably right," he said gently, "but mad men hear true things sometimes." He straightened, and Wren saw him doing arithmetic behind his eyes — distances, hours, the weight of a tower falling. "We have until the towers fail. When they go, everything they've held back comes through the gap in the first hour. So: we ride south, now, and we don't stop to argue about it."

Nobody argued. That was the strange mercy of total catastrophe; it simplified every decision. Within half an hour the inn was emptying — carts loaded in the dark by feel, horses saddled by touch, the wool factors abandoning an entire season's inventory because wool doesn't matter when the sky has been amputated.

Wren stood in the yard a moment longer, looking north toward the mountains she couldn't see.

There was a sound there. Far off, below hearing, below feeling — a pressure rather than a noise, like the moment before lightning strikes. The wards, compensating. Burning themselves to fill a gap the size of heaven.

Her chest hurt. She pressed her hand flat against her sternum and felt — and this made no sense, this made so little sense she filed it away in the drawer where she kept the vial of poison residue and the waystone that had spoken her family's name — she felt something answer. A warmth under the breastbone, faint as a banked coal, pulsing in time with that distant pressure.

Like recognizing like.

"Sandoval!" Ostrek barked. "Cart or horse. Choose."

"Cart," Wren said, and climbed up beside the salt pork, and did not look north again until the world ended.

* * *

It ended quietly, which was the worst part.

Near midnight — though midnight had become a courtesy title, since there was no sky to mark it — the pressure in the air stopped. Just stopped, between one breath and the next, the way a heart stops.

Then the bell.

Wren heard it in her bones before her ears caught up: a single vast tone rolling down out of the mountains, deeper than any bell cast by human hands, so low it wasn't sound so much as the memory of sound. The ground shivered. In the innyard, abandoned, the well-water trembled in rings.

The old man was already outside, standing in the middle of the road with his face tipped north, and Wren, who had come down for water, stood beside him.

"That's the first tower," he said. "Heart-tower, maybe. The keystone." His voice was very quiet. "Listen. It doesn't shatter. It *rings*. Twenty years of forged inspections and skimmed maintenance and drained bonds, and when it finally dies, it dies singing. There's a lesson in that somewhere."

A second tone joined the first, a half-step off, dissonant, agonizing. Then a third. Then the tones began to overlap and slide against each other, a chord built by a mad composer, and Wren clapped her hands over her ears but it didn't help because the sound wasn't arriving through her ears.

And beneath it — beneath it, threaded through it like a worm through fruit — came another sound. A rising hum. Vast, patient, hungry. The sound of something that had waited a very long time noticing that its cage had begun, note by note, to open.

The old man swayed. Wren caught his arm.

"The seals," he whispered. "Under Thornfell. Girl, do you understand what's under Thornfell?"

"Tell me."

"Older than the kingdom. Older than the *word* kingdom. The wards aren't walls against raiders, whatever the histories say. They're a lid." He gripped her wrist hard enough to bruise. "And the lid is coming off."

The chord broke apart into silence. One by one, the great towers of Thornfell — the wards that had stood since before the Sandoval name meant anything, the protection every child learned before learning letters — went dark.

In the sudden true quiet, Wren heard hoofbeats.

Riders, pouring through the pass like water through a cracked dam. She couldn't see them yet, but she could hear them — dozens, then scores, iron and leather and voices shouting in a language that had no business being spoken south of the border, because for two hundred years nothing from beyond Thornfell had needed a language. The wards had been the argument, and the argument was over.

"To the carts!" Ostrek's voice cracked across the yard. "South, now, MOVE!"

Chaos. Torches lit and immediately smothered — someone sensible screaming *no lights, no lights*. Horses screaming. The young couple with the hoods up vanishing into the dark on foot. Dannel the innkeeper hauling his daughter toward the last cart while his wife threw loaves and cheeses into a sack with the blind efficiency of a woman whose hands had decided before her mind did.

Wren ran for Ostrek's cart.

She never made it.

Something came out of the dark on the road — a rider, ahead of the main body, a scout moving fast and blind and armed, and Wren heard the hoofbeats too late, heard the sword-arm swing at the shape of the fleeing innkeeper, heard Dannel go down with a sound she would hear for the rest of her life.

The girl screamed. The rider wheeled toward the scream, toward the small shape frozen in the open, and Wren's body did something her mind had no part in.

Later, trying to reconstruct it, she could only assemble fragments. She was moving. She was fast — impossibly, obscenely fast, her feet finding purchase on wet cobblestones as if the stones themselves had offered it. The rider's sword came around and her hand was already inside the arc, catching his wrist, turning it, and the man simply *left the saddle*, airborne, hitting the wall of the inn with a crack that dropped him like a sack of grain. She hadn't thrown him. She was sure she hadn't thrown him. It was more that the space he occupied had briefly disagreed with him.

And her other hand — her free hand — had come up between her and the fallen man, fingers spread, and something had answered it. A light. Thin and white and structured, a lattice of it, hanging in the air like a spiderweb of dawn, and where it hung the darkness recoiled and the rider on the ground scrambled backward from it making a sound like a kicked dog.

Then it was gone. Then she was kneeling by the girl, pulling her up, checking her with a taster's hands — pulse, breath, pupils — because that much she knew how to do, that much was hers.

"I'm all right," the girl said, teeth chattering. "I'm all right. Your hand was *glowing*."

"I know," Wren said. "Don't tell anyone."

Around them, the yard was emptying fast. More riders on the road now, torches despite everything, and shouts, and somewhere Ostrek screaming her name. The old ward-keeper appeared at her elbow, took one look at the unconscious scout, one look at her face, and grabbed her arm with both hands.

"You caught a ward-thread," he breathed. "Out of the air. Bare-handed. Girl — *girl* — what is your mother's name?"

"Ilsette."

He went pale. In the torchlight, with riders flooding the pass and the greatest work of civilization ringing its own death-knell in the mountains, he went pale as bone.

"Get on the cart," he said. "Get on the cart and don't stop south of the river, and when you reach the capital, find no one, trust no one, and keep that hand of yours *hidden*." He shoved her toward Ostrek's wagon. "Do you hear me? The crown will drain you to the last drop and call it duty. I've seen the ledgers. I've *signed* the ledgers. Go!"

"Who are you?" she shouted after him, but he was already moving, limping fast up the opposite road, north, toward the towers, toward the thing every sane creature was fleeing.

The cart lurched. South. Behind them, Grayhollow's lights — the few defiant ones — began to go out one by one, and not all of them were being smothered by sensible hands.

Wren held the innkeeper's daughter against her side, the child's ear over her heart, and stared north at the black mass of Thornfell where the stars used to reflect off snowfields that no longer existed.

Under her breastbone, the banked coal burned steady and bright.

*They fed on it,* she thought, and didn't know yet where the thought came from. *All those years. All those doses. They weren't teaching my body to survive poison. They were feeding it to something.*

The cart rolled south through the starless dark, and Wren Sandoval, food-taster, liar, survivor, put her hand flat against her own sternum and felt the ember answer — patient, ancient, and awake.

Whatever had lived in her blood her whole life had just been given permission to speak.

And far behind them, in the gap where the towers had been, the thing beneath Thornfell began, softly, to sing along.
