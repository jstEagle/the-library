## 4. What the Ward-Keeper Knew

The night the stars went out, Halric Vane was drinking cheap plum wine in a room above the Saltcellar in Greyharrow, trying to decide whether the ache in his chest was guilt or just his heart finally giving out at fifty-three.

He'd been leaning on the windowsill, watching the harbor lights, when the sky simply — stopped.

Not clouded over. Not dimmed. *Stopped.* Every star from horizon to horizon, gone at once, like a hand snuffing ten thousand candles with one breath. The harbor below went quiet in a way harbors never do. Somewhere a dog began to howl and did not stop.

Halric set down his cup very carefully, because his hands had started to shake, and he knew exactly what he was looking at.

Twenty years ago he'd sat in a lamplit office beneath the Thornfell ward-towers and signed his name to ledgers that said the border wards were drawing their strength evenly from the bonded families of the vale. Evenly. A fair tithe from every hearth that carried smith-blood. He'd signed it because Commander Aldous Reeve had slid the papers across the desk with his daughter's name written in the margin of his thoughts, unspoken but perfectly legible: *Sign, Vane. Or Maren learns what happens to families who refuse the crown's arithmetic.*

So he'd signed. And the wards had gone on singing their slow song above the mountain passes, and Halric had gone into exile with ink on his hands that no amount of scrubbing ever touched.

But he had been a ward-keeper before he was a coward, and a ward-keeper's eyes never stop reading the sky. He knew what an honest ward looked like from below — a faint silver shimmer along the high ridgelines, steady as breath, fed by hundreds of small willing tributes trickling up through the bond-lines. He also knew what a *starving* ward looked like, because for three years before his exile he'd watched it happen and written reports about it that were replaced with prettier numbers.

A starving ward flickered. It guttered like a candle in wind. It died by inches, over years, and you could see it coming if anyone cared to look.

What he was seeing now, in the starless dark where the mountain's outline should have worn its silver crown — this was not starvation. This was not even collapse. This was a ledger being called due. All at once. Every debt, every siphoned year, every forged line in every book he'd ever signed, presented for payment in a single night.

"Gods," he said quietly, to the empty room. "It actually happened."

He'd spent two decades telling himself the forgery was survivable. That the wards had reserves. That the discrepancies he'd noticed — the way certain family names appeared in the tribute rolls far more often than others, the way the Sandoval entries read less like tithes and more like *extractions* — were clerical errors, or at worst petty corruption, the kind of rot any old institution carries. He'd told himself that right up until the moment the sky went black and proved him a liar in front of the entire world.

Now there was only one question left worth asking, and it wasn't *how bad*. He could calculate how bad. The towers would fall within hours; the resonance math was simple enough that he could have done it drunk, and he was, in fact, slightly drunk. The riders would come through the gap before dawn. The thing beneath Thornfell — the one the first ward-smiths had sealed at the cost of everything they had, the one whose name had been struck from every record Halric ever touched — would begin to wake.

No. The question was *who*.

Because the wards hadn't failed evenly. He'd have bet his last coin on that. When a bonded system dies, it dies in sequence, weakest links first, and the order of the dying tells you where the strength was pooled. And the strength had been pooled somewhere specific — pulled out of the general vale-families and concentrated, year after year, into a handful of names the crown kept off the true ledgers entirely. Names that appeared in the originals only as marginalia, as supply requisitions, as odd little payments routed through the kitchens.

Halric had copied those original pages once, late at night, with a forger's careful hand. He still remembered the names. One of them he had never forgotten, because it belonged to a woman he'd loved quietly and uselessly for most of his life, a woman who used to bring her daughter through the tower yards on market days and always looked at the ward-towers the way other people look at old wounds.

Sandoval.

He was saddling his horse before the wine had fully left his head.

* * *

The road east out of Greyharrow was already filling with refugees coming the other way, though nothing had fallen yet — nothing visible, anyway. Word traveled faster than cavalry when the word is *the sky is broken*. Halric rode against the current, an old man on a gray mare pushing upstream through carts and crying children and a priest standing on a milestone shouting that this was the gods' judgment on usury.

He let them flow past him and thought about Elara Sandoval.

She had died eleven years ago. He'd heard it secondhand, in a letter from a former colleague who owed him money: fever, quick, the daughter taken into crown service. *Food-taster,* the letter said, and Halric had laughed until it turned into something else, because of course. Of course the crown had taken the last of the smith-blood and put her mouth against poison for a living. There was a kind of poetry in it that only a bureaucrat could love. Keep the blood close. Keep it useful. Keep it *draining*, quietly, in ways no one would ever think to audit.

He'd wondered, over the years, whether Elara had known. Whether she'd understood what the tribute rolls really meant, what was being drawn out of her and her line. She'd been sharp enough. She'd been a ward-smith's daughter herself, back when there were still ward-smiths and not just ward-*keepers*, men like Halric who tended machinery they no longer knew how to build.

And now the machinery was dead, and the only people alive who might understand why were either in the ground or wearing chains of a much subtler make.

Near midnight he passed the first fallen waystone. It stood beside the road like a grave marker, a pillar of gray granite that should have hummed faintly under his palm — he could feel the absence before he reached it, the way you feel a missing tooth. He reined in and dismounted and laid his hand against the stone anyway, some old habit of greeting, and felt nothing. Not dead, exactly. *Drained.* Hollowed out, like a wineskin with the bottom cut away.

But here was the thing that made him stand in the cold road with his heart hammering: the hollowing wasn't uniform. The stone had been drained from the top down, the way water leaves a glass — except the pattern ran deeper than surface etching. He traced it with his fingers, reading the wear-gradients the way he'd once read ledger columns, and the story the stone told was the same story the forged books had told twenty years ago.

Concentrated extraction. A single point of draw. Someone — something — had been pulling the whole network's strength toward one node for years, and the node had finally burned through.

He knew the geometry. Any ward-keeper would. The draw-point sat at the heart-tower, the oldest spire in the Thornfell line, the one the histories called the First Oathstone. Which meant the crown hadn't just been skimming the bonded families. They'd been routing everything through the deep vaults beneath the heart-tower, feeding it all into one reservoir, and whatever they'd been keeping in that reservoir —

Halric straightened up slowly. His breath came out white in the dark.

They hadn't been hoarding it. You don't hoard ward-strength; it spoils, it wants to move, it wants to be *given*. No. They'd been spending it. Slowly, secretly, on something. Something that needed more than the honest tithe of the vale could provide, which was why the ledgers lied, which was why the Sandovals were drained nearly dry, which was why —

Which was why the sky had gone out. Whatever they'd been feeding under the mountain had finally eaten through its allowance and reached for the mains.

"You fools," he whispered, to a crown that couldn't hear him. "You magnificent, murderous fools."

His mare shifted under him, uneasy. Far behind him, to the east, the horizon pulsed once — a deep, silent flash, like lightning seen through miles of stone. Then another. Rhythmic. Patient.

The sound arrived minutes later: a bell-note so vast and low it wasn't heard so much as *felt*, rising through the road, through the mare's shoes, through the soles of Halric's boots and the bones of his legs. The first ward-tower falling. He counted the interval before the next pulse and did the arithmetic without wanting to. Six hours, perhaps seven, until the whole line was down. Until the gap stood open.

He swung back into the saddle.

* * *

He rode through the rest of the night, and guilt rode with him, and for the first time in twenty years it had somewhere to go.

That was the strange mercy of it. Guilt without action is just a disease; Halric had lived with it so long it had become part of his metabolism, something his body processed the way it processed food and sleep. He'd built his whole exile around it — the drinking, the drifting, the careful refusal to ever again touch anything that mattered. Cowardice as a discipline. He'd gotten good at it.

But now the guilt had a direction. East. Toward Thornfell. Toward whoever the wards had been secretly bonded to — because someone had to be. The network didn't run on stone and ceremony alone; it ran on blood, freely offered, generation after generation, and if the crown had corrupted that bargain then somewhere out there was a person whose veins still carried the old covenant, a person the dying wards had been feeding on in secret, and that person was the only key left that fit any lock.

Elara's daughter. It had to be. The last name on the true rolls, the final entry, the account the crown had never closed because they thought no one would ever read it.

Wren Sandoval. Food-taster. Poison-drinker. Living battery, if half his suspicions were right, and all his suspicions were right, because he'd written the cover-up himself.

He found her name in his memory the way you find a scar in the dark — by touch, by the shape of the hurt around it. He'd seen her once, years ago, through a window in the capital: a thin girl in servant gray walking across a courtyard with a tray, moving with the particular economy of someone who has learned that attention is dangerous. He'd recognized the walk. Elara had walked like that, after the tribute-tithes got heavy.

*I'm coming,* he thought, absurdly, to a woman he'd never spoken to. *I'm coming, and I can't undo what I signed, but I know where the bodies are buried. I dug half the graves.*

Dawn came gray and starless, which was wrong in a way that made his skin crawl — the sun rose, but the sky around it stayed the flat dead color of pewter, as if the light itself had lost confidence. Ahead of him the mountains stood up out of the murk, and where the ward-line should have crowned them he could see the last two towers still standing, silver threads snapping off them into nothing, guttering like the candles he'd compared them to, and going out one by one as he watched.

The mare screamed — an actual scream, ears flat — as the next bell-note rolled over them. Halric held her steady and made himself look, made himself count, made himself *witness* it, because witnessing was the least of what he owed.

Three towers left. Then two. Then, as the morning aged, one, burning silver at the summit of the pass like a lamp in a window for a traveler who would never arrive.

And then the pass itself changed. He felt it before he saw it — a pressure dropping, a held breath releasing, twenty years of forged arithmetic unwinding in a single exhalation. The last tower's light stretched, thinned, and went dark.

In the ringing silence after, Halric Vane sat his horse on the refugee road with the mountains open ahead of him and wept, briefly and without drama, the way you bleed from a wound you've been carrying since you were young enough to think it would heal on its own.

Then he wiped his face, checked the knife at his belt and the forged seal in his saddlebag — the one tool of his old treachery that might yet serve — and turned east toward Thornfell, toward the occupied passes, toward a woman who didn't know she was the last ember of an oath older than the crown.

Behind him, far to the south, he could hear the invaders' horns. Ahead, in the high country, the waystones had begun — very faintly, impossibly — to sing.

One note. Over and over. A name.

He spurred the mare forward, because he knew that name too.
