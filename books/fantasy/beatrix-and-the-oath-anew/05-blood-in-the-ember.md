## 5. Blood in the Ember

They took her at the second ford of the Ashwater, where the river ran gray with rock-flour and the reeds had gone brittle as old bone.

Wren never heard them come. One moment she was crouched at the bank, filling her waterskin, and the next there was a boot on her pack strap and a spear point resting against the back of her neck with the casual precision of a man who had done it a thousand times.

"Hands," he said. "Slow."

She raised them. The water kept running past her knees, indifferent. Three more soldiers came down through the reeds, and she took their measure the way she took the measure of every dish set before a king — quickly, completely, without letting her face show anything at all. Two were boys in men's mail, eyes red-rimmed, hands shaking on their spears. The third was older, a sergeant with a beard going to iron-gray and a burn scar crawling up one side of his throat like ivy made of flesh.

None of them wore colors she knew. That was the worst of it. Ward-soldiers had always worn the mountain sigil, the tower-and-star, stitched proud on every shoulder. These men wore it too, but scorched, half-cut away, as if they'd started tearing it off and hadn't found the courage to finish.

"You're a long way from any road," the sergeant said.

"I'm a taster," Wren said. "I got separated from my caravan."

"Taster." He said the word like he was tasting it himself, looking for poison in it. His eyes went over her — the plain traveling clothes, the soft hands that weren't quite as soft as a court lady's, the small satchel she'd been too slow to hide. "Open the bag."

She did. He went through it with a soldier's economy: bread, cheese wrapped in waxcloth, a spare knife, her mother's letter pressed flat beneath the oilcloth lining. His hand paused on the letter, on the blood-brown ink of the diagrams folded inside it, and something moved across his face — not suspicion. Recognition. It lasted less than a breath, and then it was gone, buried so deep she might have invented it.

"Diagrams," he said quietly. "Where did you get these?"

"My mother."

"Your mother was a ward-smith?"

"She was dead twenty years," Wren said. "Whatever she was, she kept it to herself."

The sergeant looked at her for a long moment. Behind him, one of the boys shifted his weight, and his spear tip wavered against the sky. "Captain will want to see this," the sergeant said finally, and it wasn't a question, and it wasn't a request, and that was how Wren Sandoval came to be marched west into the mountains with her wrists lashed to a saddle horn and the ashes of the world rising around her like weather.

* * *

The retreating column found them an hour later, or perhaps they found the column; it was hard to say who was moving toward whom. They came down a goat track in loose order, maybe forty men and women in scorched livery, driving two mule carts loaded with things wrapped in canvas that Wren, from habit, catalogued without meaning to: the long regular shapes of spear bundles, the squat heavy lines of strongboxes, and one crate, smaller than the rest, that three soldiers watched the entire time with the fixed attention of men guarding a wound.

There were wounded, too. More than the carts could hold. Some walked propped between comrades, and some were carried in blankets slung from poles, and all of them had the same look she'd begun to recognize since the night the stars died — not the blank shock of ordinary defeat but something older and stranger, the look of people who had lost not a battle but a premise.

A boy with a bandaged head fell in beside her mule as they climbed. He couldn't have been more than sixteen. "You're a prisoner?" he asked.

"That's the shape of it."

"They'll want to know if you're bonded." He said it carefully, the way you'd name a disease. "Everyone does now."

"Bonded to what?"

He looked at her like she'd asked which way was down. Then his face did something complicated — pity and fear fighting for the same ground — and he dropped back without answering.

They climbed through the afternoon into country Wren half-knew from her childhood, though she'd never traveled it awake: the high shoulders of Thornfell, black pine and gray scree, the peaks wearing their snowlines low like hoods pulled down in grief. And everywhere, everywhere, the wards were dying. She felt it before she understood what she was feeling — a thinning in the air, a silence where there should have been hum, the particular wrongness of a room after a clock stops. The waystones they passed stood dark for the first time in whatever ages they had stood at all, their carved spirals filled with ash instead of light, and when her mule brushed close to one of them Wren felt it shudder under her like a horse feeling a fly.

*Sorry*, she thought at it, absurdly. *I'm sorry.*

It steadied. Just slightly. Just enough that the boy behind her said, "Did you feel that?" in a voice like someone describing a ghost.

The sergeant heard it too. She saw him turn in the saddle and study her with new arithmetic happening behind his eyes, and after that he rode closer to her, and after that no one spoke to her at all except with their elbows.

They made camp at dusk in a hollow below a ruined watchtower, and this was where they brought her to Captain Aldric Voss.

* * *

He lay in the tower's shadow under a lean-to of spears and cloaks, and Wren understood within three steps why the whole column orbited him the way it did. He wasn't just their commander. He was their proof that retreat could still mean something — a man half-dead who refused to finish the job, propped upright against a saddle with his sword across his lap because lying flat, apparently, would be admitting something he wasn't ready to admit.

He was forty-something, gray at the temples, built like the mountains themselves had gotten tired of standing and decided to lie down. A wound ran from his hip up under his breastplate, bound in linen gone brown, and the fever had gotten into him somewhere along the march; she could see it in the wet brightness of his eyes and the fine tremor in his hands. Poison, she thought, automatically reading him the way she read everything — septic, deep, days from killing him unless someone competent intervened. Not her problem. Her problem was the rope on her wrists and the forty soldiers between her and the pass road.

"Sergeant Bray," Voss said. His voice was gravel poured slow. "What am I looking at?"

"Wanderer, Captain. Caught her at the Ashwater ford. Says she's a food-taster out of the capital." Bray held up the diagrams, unfolded, and Wren watched the captain's face while he looked at them. It was like watching a man look at the face of a drowned child. "And she stopped a waystone shaking, near the third mile. Boy swears it. I believe him."

Voss looked at the diagrams a long time. Then he looked at Wren.

"Come here," he said.

Bray cut her wrists free. She rubbed the marks and stepped forward, and up close the captain smelled of fever and woodsmoke and something else underneath, something mineral and cold, like the air off a glacier. Like the inside of a vault.

"You're a taster," he said.

"Ten years. Crown's service."

"Taste things for a living. Take poisons into your body so the king doesn't have to." His mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "And how is it you're still alive, taster? After ten years?"

"Small doses. Building tolerance. It's how it's done."

"Mm." His eyes didn't leave hers. "That's the story they tell. Here's another story." He lifted one hand — the tremor in it worse now, the fever winning by inches — and pointed at the crate she'd noticed on the mule cart. "Bring it," he told Bray.

Three soldiers carried it over. They set it down in front of her with the exaggerated care of men handling a reliquary, or a bomb. Voss nodded once, and Bray knelt and unfastened the lid.

Inside, packed in wool and ash-salt, lay a stone the size of a heart. No — Wren corrected herself — the size of a fist, if the fist belonged to something larger than a man. It was dark, nearly black, shot through with veins of dull ember-orange, and even dying it put out heat she could feel from four feet away, a warmth like standing near a hearth in another room.

"What is it?" she asked, and her voice came out smaller than she wanted.

"It's an ember," Voss said. "One of nine hundred we carried out of the towers before they fell. This one's from Heartspire itself, from the root-chapel, and it's been dying since the stars went out. We've fed it everything. Oath-words. Blood of the sworn. Prayers. It drinks it all and dims anyway." His breathing caught; he mastered it. "Every ember we save is a seed. That's what we told ourselves. Plant them in clean stone, speak the old grammar, grow new wards, hold the line somewhere. But the seeds are dying, taster. All of them. Whatever the wards drank from for a thousand years — that well's gone dry. Or someone took it."

His hand came up again, trembling, and beckoned her closer.

"So here's what happens next. You're going to touch it. And if nothing happens, Bray puts you back on the mule and points you down-mountain, and you go on being a taster until the dark eats the world. But if something happens—" He stopped. Coughed, wet and long. "If something happens, then we know the story isn't over. Do you understand me? I need to know the story isn't over."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I'll think less of you," he said simply, "and the dark wins either way, so I hardly see what you gain."

Wren looked at the ember in its bed of ash-salt. Heat rolled off it in slow pulses, like the breathing of a sleeping animal. And underneath the heat, faint as a voice through a wall, she felt it doing something she had felt only once before — on the night the stars vanished, when the inn's cellar wards had turned toward her in the dark like flowers turning toward a sun they'd been starved of.

It was reaching for her.

It had been reaching for her since she walked into camp. Maybe longer. Maybe since the night the sky went black, when every dying ward in Thornfell had felt her pass on the smuggler roads and called her name through the stones.

Her body knew something her mind did not. It always had.

Wren knelt.

"Wait—" Bray started, but Voss raised a hand, and the camp went quiet enough that she could hear the ember's pulse, faint and quickening, like a heart remembering its work.

She reached into the crate. The heat should have burned. It was the heat of a forge, of a fire left untended in a dry summer — and it did not burn. It sank into her palm instead, warm as bathwater, warm as a hand closing around hers, and the veins of orange in the black stone began to brighten.

Then to blaze.

Light poured up between her fingers like water finding cracks. The whole hollow lit amber; the ruined watchtower threw a long shadow across the camp; soldiers cried out and stumbled back, shielding their eyes, and the wounded sat up in their blankets staring at a brightness none of them had seen in five days and had given up expecting ever again. The ember pulsed against her palm in a rhythm she realized, with a lurch, matched her own heartbeat exactly — as if it had merely been waiting, all this time, for the right heart to borrow.

And in her blood, something answered. Not pain. Recognition. Like a word she'd spent her whole life mispronouncing suddenly spoken correctly in her mother's voice. The diagrams in her pack, the unfinished letter, the waystones that whispered her family name — every strange thing in her life slid sideways and clicked into a single shape, and the shape had her face.

"The smith-blood," Captain Voss whispered.

She looked up at him through the blaze. His fever-bright eyes were full of tears, and his ruined smile was the most terrible thing she had seen since the sky went dark.

"The smith-blood," he said again. "After twenty years. After everything they took." He laughed, or sobbed, or both. "You don't understand what you are, girl. You're not the crown's taster. You never were. You're the well."

* * *

They gave her a tent. Of course they did. They gave her the best tent, and hot food she tasted out of reflex, and a place by the fire, and space — everyone giving her wide, reverent, frightened space, the way you'd give it to a drawn bow.

She sat apart with the ember cradled in her lap in its open crate, glowing softly now, drowsy, fed. It warmed her knees through the wool blanket. It felt, obscenely, like a cat.

The smith-blood. The well.

*You're the well.*

She turned her hands over in the firelight. Ordinary hands. Taster's hands, scarred faintly at the fingertips from years of needle-pricks and caustic draughts. Hands that had survived hemlock and arsenic and seven different brands of assassin's alchemy without ever understanding why the training alone had never been enough — why the poisons had slid through her like rain through open earth, why her teachers had exchanged looks behind her back, why the steward had once asked, laughing, if she was part dragon.

Not training. Inheritance. Her immunity had never been earned. It had been *spent on her* — poured into her before birth, a dowry of resistance, a life's worth of ward-strength banked in an unborn child's blood. Her mother hadn't just hidden her. Her mother had *loaded* her, like a crossbow, and aimed her at a future she'd somehow seen coming.

The anger arrived first, hot and clarifying. Twenty years her mother had known — had *made* her — and said nothing, taught her nothing, left her nothing but a half-finished letter and a bag of diagrams like a riddle posed from beyond a grave. If you are reading this, the oath has found you. Well, Mother. It found me. Where were you?

But under the anger, colder and heavier, sat the other thing. The thing Voss had named with tears in his eyes.

*The well.*

Nine hundred embers in crates. Forty soldiers retreating from a fallen wall. A king in a capital she'd sworn with her tongue to protect, tasting his food every morning while the real treasury of the realm drained out through her veins without her consent. And somewhere in the dark below Thornfell, the thing the wards had sealed — waking now, patient, feeding on the silence where the light used to be.

If she was the well, then everyone who found out would want to drink.

She looked up. Sergeant Bray stood at the edge of the firelight, watching her, arms crossed. Not hostile. Something worse: hopeful. All around the camp, she could feel them — forty pairs of eyes pretending not to look at her, forty exhausted hearts quietly arranging their last faith around a stranger with glowing hands. She had spent ten years being nobody's hope. She'd been furniture. A fork. A useful mouth. Now she was a relic, and relics get carried whether they wish it or not.

"Captain wants you at first light," Bray said. "We're making for Keldrath Pass. He says—" The sergeant hesitated. "He says if the well walks, the realm has a chance. Those were his words. *If the well walks.*"

"And if the well would rather walk alone?"

Bray's face didn't change, but something behind it did — a door closing gently on a room full of fragile things. "Then the well had best do it before first light," he said, "because after first light, we guard it. That's the job now. That's been the job since the towers fell. Somebody has to carry the fire, taster. We were starting to think nobody could."

He left her with that.

Wren sat a long time in the dark, the ember pulsing gently against her knees, keeping its borrowed time with her heart. First light. Keldrath Pass. Forty soldiers and a dying captain who needed her to be the story that wasn't over.

Or the pass road south, alone, with a crate, a letter, and a lifetime of lies cracking open like river ice under her feet.

She looked at the ember. It pulsed. Patient. Certain of her.

*You're the well,* the captain had said, and meant it as salvation. But wells get drawn from. Wells don't get asked.

Wren closed her hands around the stone, feeling its heat sink into the scars on her palms, and made the only decision her body had ever known how to make.

She ran.

She went up, not down — up into the black pines with the crate hugged to her chest and the ember's light leaking between her fingers like contraband dawn, up past the picket lines where a startled sentry shouted and did not shoot, up onto the shoulder of Thornfell where the dead waystones trembled as she passed, one after another, flickering awake in her wake like lamps relit behind a runner. Behind her, the camp erupted into noise. Ahead, the pass road bent north into occupied country, toward Keldrath, toward whatever waited in the starless dark.

She didn't know what she was carrying. Only that it was hers — the first true thing she'd ever owned — and that everyone from the king to the dark beneath the mountain would come to take it.

Let them come, she thought, and ran harder, and the mountain lit her way.
