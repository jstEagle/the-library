## 8. Occupied Vale

**Wren**

The valley opened below them like a wound that had learned to pretend it was a field.

From the ridge, Ashvale should have been beautiful — terraced barley going gold down the slopes, a river stitching silver through the middle of it, smoke rising from chimneys in the fat gray threads of ordinary mornings. Wren had grown up on maps of this place. Grain country. The breadbasket of the eastern marches, her tutor had called it, back when the eastern marches were still theirs.

Now the smoke rose in too many columns and all of them were wrong. Thin and acrid where hearth-smoke was soft. She'd spent years in kitchens; she knew the smell of burning from a hundred paces, and she could tell the difference between bread and barn.

"Granaries," Halric said beside her. He said it flatly, the way a man names a thing he's already grieved.

They came down into the town near midday, when the occupiers would be drowsiest, and the first thing Wren noticed was the quiet. Not peace-quiet. Held-breath quiet. A market square with stalls open and no one buying. A woman scrubbing a doorstep who never once looked up at them as they passed, which in a healthy town would have been rudeness and here was survival. Dogs had gone somewhere else entirely.

And everywhere, the marks. Chalked on door lintels, painted on walls in a slanting script Wren didn't know: tall strokes crossed by short ones, like a fence built out of letters. Counting something. She stopped in front of one and felt the hair rise on her arms before she understood why.

"It's not writing," she said quietly. "It's a ward."

Halric's jaw tightened. "A ward written by someone who doesn't understand wards. Look at the joins. They're dead ends. It's a lock drawn by people who've only ever seen locks from the outside." He touched the chalk with one finger and pulled it back fast, as if burned. "Suppression work. Crude. They're pinning something down inside these houses."

"Pinning what?"

He didn't answer, and his silence told her he knew exactly what, and that knowing it would hurt her.

The conscription line formed in the square just past noon. Wren watched it from the shadow of a cooper's awning while Halric haggled over bread with a baker whose hands shook so badly he dropped the coin twice. Boys, mostly. Fifteen, sixteen, a few younger wearing their fathers' coats to look broader. An invader officer walked the line with a slate, tapping shoulders left and right like a man choosing horses, and behind him two soldiers carried a barrel of nails and rope.

"They're not taking fighters," Wren murmured. "Look. He skips the big farm boys. He takes the thin ones."

"Tunnelers and bearers." Halric didn't turn his head. "You don't feed men you mean to keep."

One boy in the line — freckled, ears too big for his head, a farmer's son by the mud on his knees — started to cry without making any sound about it. Just tears running while his face stayed perfectly still, trained stillness, the stillness of someone who had already learned that showing fear got you beaten faster. His mother stood at the edge of the square with a baby on her hip and did not go to him. That was the part that lodged in Wren like a fishbone. The mother knew better. Everyone in that square knew better. The whole town had become an instrument played by other people's hands.

The officer tapped the freckled boy's shoulder. The boy stepped out of line. His mother turned away so her son wouldn't see her face.

Wren's hand had closed around the ember-vial at her throat without her deciding anything. It pulsed there, faint and warm, like a second heartbeat that had opinions.

"We can't," Halric said, very low, not looking at her. "Whatever you're thinking. We are two people carrying the last light in the world through occupied territory, and every hour we spend being heroes is an hour the dark gets closer to the heart-tower."

"I'm not thinking anything."

"You're thinking with your hands. I've watched you do it since the pass road."

She made herself let go of the vial. But she kept watching the boy until the soldiers marched him toward the river gate, and she memorized his face the way she'd once memorized the taste of arsenic in wine — precisely, professionally, against a future use.

* * *

**Halric**

Halric had done ugly arithmetic his whole life. Twenty years of forged ledgers were nothing but ugly arithmetic with good handwriting. So he recognized the shape of the sum forming in his own head as they found lodging above a shut tannery: *one night here, rest the horses, cross the north ford at dawn, lose half a day.* And underneath it, in smaller figures: *the granary fires mean they're feeding an army off this valley, which means supply wagons on the north road, which means checkpoints, which means the ford is the worst crossing we could choose.* And underneath that, in figures he refused to write at all: *the boy with the freckles went east toward the river gate, and the mines are east.*

He caught himself doing what he'd always done — arranging the world so that the cost landed on strangers. It was the family trade of bureaucrats. You never signed your own name to the harm; you signed adjacent to it, in a margin, in a hand that could later be called sloppy.

Wren sat across the room sharpening a kitchen knife with long, even strokes, and he wondered if she knew she'd picked up the habit from him already, three days on the road together — the habit of turning feeling into procedure. He'd seen her do it with grief, with fear, with the way she looked at him sometimes when she thought he wasn't watching. Everything became a task. Everything became survivable.

"The child," she said, without stopping the knife. "In the lane this morning. The one with the scarred arm."

"There was no child in the lane."

"There was. Left side, past the well. Maybe six years old. Arm wrapped in boiled wool and the wool is soaked through with something yellow." She set the knife down. "It's a ward-scar, Halric. I've seen them in the ledgers you stole for me. Suppression burns. The chalk on the doors — it's pinned to children."

He had known. That was why he hadn't answered her on the wall. The invaders had worked out, faster than the crown ever had, that bond-blood ran in families, and they were marking the heirs the way the crown had marked them — except the crown had drained its heirs politely, with contracts and stipends, and these people were doing it with chalk and boiled wool.

"If you touch that child," Halric said, "every suppression ward in this town will feel it. They'll come. And they'll find us, and they'll find the ember, and everything your mother died to hide goes into a barrel of nails and rope."

"I know."

"You know, and you're putting your boots on."

She laced them without hurry. "I spent ten years tasting poison for a king," she said, "and telling myself it was service. Every morning I put my life on the scale and called the weighing duty. And then I find out the whole time, the crown was drinking from me anyway — that I was never the shield, I was the stores." She stood. "I'm done confusing arithmetic with honor, Halric. You taught me the difference. You taught it by being the bad example."

There was nothing to say to that that wasn't true, so he said nothing, and followed her out.

* * *

**Wren**

The child was in the same lane, sitting on a step outside a house whose door bore three chalk fences instead of one, as though someone had tried twice more and not believed it. A girl. Six or seven, maybe, small for it. Her left arm lay in her lap wrapped in stained linen, and she was watching the empty street with the patience of the very sick.

Up close, Wren could smell the wound under the wool — infection sweetening the strange burnt-metal stink of the scar itself. The girl looked up at her without fear. Children in occupied towns had apparently used up all their fear on things that deserved it.

"Hello," Wren said, crouching.

The girl considered her. "You're not supposed to stop."

"I know."

"Mama says nobody stops anymore. Stopping is how they take you." She said it in the toneless voice of a lesson recited. Then, after a moment, with a flicker of the child still alive under the lessons: "Are you a healer?"

Wren looked at the arm. The linen was stuck to the skin beneath. When she peeled back one corner, gently, she saw the mark — a burn in the shape of the chalked fences, sunk deep, edges the color of old iron. Suppression scarring. The ward had been written *on* the child, fed on whatever faint smith-blood she carried, and the feeding had gone septic.

Somewhere under Wren's breastbone, the ember turned over like a sleeper disturbed.

She should have thought about the checkpoint at the ford. She should have thought about Halric's face, about the vial at her throat, about twenty-two chapters' worth of reasons — no, she corrected herself wryly, *twenty-one chapters'* worth of reasons — to walk away. Instead she thought about a boy crying silently in a conscription line, and a mother who couldn't afford to watch, and she laid her bare right hand over the scarred arm because her body had decided before her mind convened, the way it always had, the way it had the night the stars went out.

Heat moved through her palm. Not pain — recognition. The ember poured down her arm and into the ruined flesh, and the ward-scar drank it the way dry ground drinks rain, greedily, gratefully, and then — this was the part she hadn't expected — it answered. The mark flared white through the girl's skin, and every chalked fence on every door in Ashvale lit up at once, a street of burning letters, and Wren felt them all *notice* her.

The girl gasped. Under Wren's hand, the angry iron color faded to pink, to clean new skin, and the fever heat left the small body like a bird leaving a branch.

"Oh," the girl said. She flexed her fingers. She looked at her arm, then at Wren, with an expression of enormous, offended wonder. "It doesn't hurt. It hasn't not-hurted since the tall men came."

"Wren." Halric's voice, low and urgent, from the mouth of the lane. "We have to go *now*. The whole grid is awake. There will be riders."

She was already standing. "Thank you," she told the girl, absurdly, and the girl said, "You bled," and pointed.

Wren looked down. Her nose was bleeding freely, a bright thread down her chin, and her right hand trembled with a fine continuous shiver, like a struck bell that wouldn't finish ringing. The ember sat quieter in its vial than it had in days — quieter, and somehow less hers, as if it had paid out a portion of itself and expected repayment on terms she hadn't read.

They went out the south end of the lane at a walk, because running drew eyes, and walking while bleeding from the nose apparently didn't. Behind them, boots. Ahead, the river gate and the north road and the ford that Halric now called impossible with his whole posture.

They cut through a yard where the granary had burned — she saw it up close then, the char and the spilled grain gone black, and a line of townsfolk passing scorched sacks hand to hand, salvaging half-ruined barley a handful at a time, silent as mourners. An old man pressed a twist of cloth into Wren's bleeding hand as she passed: bread, still warm. Nobody asked her name. In Ashvale, kindness had gone underground with everything else, passed hand to hand like contraband.

At the yard's far wall, Halric boosted her up and said, close to her ear, "The ford is dead. There's a ferryman two miles down who owes me a debt older than the war. We cross tonight or not at all."

"And if the debt's expired?"

"Then I'll forge it," he said, and for the first time in days he almost smiled.

But at the top of the wall she looked back once, down the lane where the chalk fences still glowed faintly, and saw soldiers gathered around the girl's doorway, and an officer holding up something small and pale — a scrap of linen, maybe, or a drawing made by a child who could not yet write but could certainly draw a woman with blood on her chin.

The officer looked up, scanning the rooftops, and Wren dropped flat before his gaze reached her.

"They have her description," she breathed. "Halric. They have *mine*."

Below, through the town, a horn sounded — one long note, then another, then a third, and doors began to bar all down the valley road between them and the river.

"Not at all," Halric said quietly, "may be the honest answer. Come on. We run for the water."
