## 9. The Ledger of Lies

The fire had burned down to a red eye in the grate, and Halric Vane found he could not look at it.

Twenty years of exile had taught him every trick of avoidance. He could talk around a truth the way water talked around a stone, wearing it smooth, never moving it. He had lied to inquisitors with a steady pulse. He had lied to his daughter's face with a smile. He had lied so long and so well that the lies had grown bark, grown roots, become a kind of forest he lived inside.

But Wren Sandoval sat across from him with the ember wrapped against her chest and her mother's letter folded in her jacket, and she was watching him the way she had watched the poisoned cup — not with suspicion, exactly. With patience. The patience of someone who had already decided how much time she was willing to spend.

"You said the ledgers," she said. "In the pass. You said you'd signed them, and that the signatures were the crime. I let it go because we were running." She turned her hands toward the fire, palms open, as if warming them, though the night was mild. "We're not running tonight. The checkpoint's two days behind us and the pass road is empty. So. Tell me what you signed, Halric."

He should have started with the weather. That was the old way — circle, soften, arrive sideways. Instead he heard himself say, "Do you know where the wards got their strength?"

"The smith-blood. Bonded families. My mother's line."

"That's the children's version. The true version is uglier." He rubbed his thumb along the seam of the table, an old habit, a forger's habit — feeling for the grain of a thing before he worked against it. "The wards were never fed by blood the way a fire is fed by wood. They were fed by *consent*. A willing bond, sworn freely, renewed each generation. The ward-smiths gave themselves to the stones, and the stones gave back protection. A marriage, in a way. A vow with two sides."

"And the crown broke the vow."

"The crown got *hungry*." The word came out of him with more force than he'd intended, and the fire spat. "Twenty-five years ago I was a junior keeper in the Office of Wards, third desk from the window, copying survey figures. Boring, honorable work. I was good at it. I was good at it because I was good at numbers, and I was good at numbers because numbers don't lie — they only get lied *about*." He laughed, and it was a dry, ugly sound. "I found it in the flow-columns. The annual reconciliation of ward-draw against bond-capacity. Every bonded family in the realm, listed by hold and line, with the amount of strength drawn from each per season."

He looked up at her. "There were two columns, Wren. The one that was read aloud to the council, and the one that was true."

* * *

She didn't move. That was the worst of it, he thought later — she didn't move at all. She had the stillness of her mother, Maren, who could sit through a lie for an hour before she took it apart.

"The public column showed a modest draw," Halric went on. "A tithe. The bonded families gave a portion of their strength to maintain the wards, and the crown gave them stipends and honors and the right to work their craft. Fair trade. Old trade. That column had been balanced for three hundred years." He drew a line on the table with one finger, an invisible ledger. "The second column showed the actual draw. And the actual draw wasn't a tithe. It was a *drain*. The crown had built siphons into the ward-lattice — hidden channels, cut into the deep stone where no one but keepers ever went. They were drawing from the bonded lines four times, six times, in the southern holds nine times what the old oath allowed. And they were drawing it not into the wards. Into reserve. Into vaults beneath the capital. Hoarded strength, Wren. Enough to light a city for a century. Enough to do — I never learned what. I only learned that it was being taken."

"Taken from whom?" she said. But he could see in her face that she already knew, the way you know the shape of a blow before it lands.

"The Sandovals first. Your line was the deepest well in the network — the smith-blood ran strongest in your family, and the siphons were set deepest where the water was sweetest. Your mother's hold, the one we sheltered in two nights ago, was the crown's richest vein. They'd been bleeding it for a generation before she ever understood what was happening." His voice went quiet. "She felt it as tiredness. As a fading. The way you feel a poison you've been dosed with too long — not sick, exactly. Just *less*."

Wren's hands had stopped moving. "She was a taster," she said. "Before me. She knew poisons. She would have known."

"She knew. That's what made it worse." Halric leaned forward, elbows on his knees, and made himself hold her gaze, because looking away now would be the last and worst lie. "She came to the Office of Wards when I was twenty-six years old. She had traced the draw herself — a taster's methods, dose by dose, comparing what the wards took against what the ledgers claimed. She had it all in a little book with a cracked spine. She laid it on my desk and asked me to help her make it public."

* * *

The fire had burned lower. Outside, wind moved through the pass with a sound like a long exhalation, and somewhere above them the empty sky pressed down, starless, patient.

"I want to be exact about what I did," Halric said. "Because I've spent twenty years being inexact. So here is the exact shape of it." He held up one finger. "I believed her. That's the first thing. I looked at her figures and I checked them against my own and they were right, and I knew within a day that the crown was draining the bonded lines into hidden vaults, and I *believed* her. Hold on to that, because it's the only clean thing in the story."

A second finger. "Then I went to my superior, Keeper Aldous Ren, and I told him what I'd found, because I was twenty-six and stupid and I thought the crime was a mistake — a clerical rot, something that could be cut out if the right man saw it. And Ren looked at me for a long moment, and then he did the kindest and cruelest thing anyone has ever done to me." Halric's hand closed. "He showed me his daughter's name in the second column."

"His daughter was bonded?"

"His daughter was *drained*. She'd been born to a minor bond-line, and the siphons didn't distinguish between great holds and small ones. She was twenty years old and she had the strength of a woman of seventy. She would die within the decade — this was the arithmetic Ren lived with every day. And Ren said to me, in a voice I have never stopped hearing: *'The men who built the siphons will not be unmade by a ledger. But a ledger can be unmade by them. Sign the reconciliation, Vane. Keep the numbers quiet, and I will keep your name off every list, and I will use what influence I have to slow the draw on the worst-hit lines. Slow it. Not stop it. Slow it.'*"

Halric stood, because sitting was suddenly unbearable, and walked to the window. The starless dark looked back at him, total and absolute.

"I signed it," he said to the glass. "I signed the false reconciliation. I signed the survey that certified the siphons as *maintenance channels*. And over the following six years, I signed forty-one more documents, each one a small clean lie, each one necessary, each one buying a little time — and I want you to understand the machinery of it, Wren, because the machinery is the whole horror. No one ever asked me to do evil. They asked me to do *prudence*. Sign now, fight later. Bend the record, save the line. And every signature bought a season, and every season made the next signature cheaper, until one day I looked up and I was the man who certified the draining of the Sandoval hold, and Maren Sandoval's name was in my own hand, in the column marked *capacity remaining*, and the number beside it was half what it should have been, and falling."

He turned. "She came back, near the end. After you were born. She'd found out about the vaults — the hoarded strength under the capital — and she'd stopped asking for help and started making demands. She was going to expose it all. She had a plan, and the plan was *you*." He saw Wren go very still. "She'd split the ember. Hidden it in the only vault the crown could never search. She was going to let the wards weaken, let the truth become undeniable, force the realm to reforge the oath honestly — and she asked me, one last time, to help her. And I —"

He stopped.

"You refused," Wren said.

"I reported her." The words came out of him like a tooth coming free — awful, and leaving a hole. "Not her name. I wasn't even that brave. I reported *an anomaly*. I flagged her hold for inspection, thinking — God help me — thinking I was protecting her, because an inspection would keep the Office's eyes on her, and the Office's eyes were the crown's eyes, and if the crown was watching her she'd be *careful*, and careful would keep her alive. That was the arithmetic I did. That was the lie I told myself while I wrote the report." His voice cracked, finally, the first crack in twenty years of smooth surfaces. "Three months later she was dead. A fall on the pass road, the record says. And you were taken into the crown's service as a food-taster at the age of nine, and I was exiled within the year for *falsifying ward-records* — the one crime I never actually committed, charged against me by the men whose crimes I had spent six years certifying. They needed a keeper to burn, Wren. I was the kindling. And I have spent twenty years telling myself that was justice. That it was *fair*. That the exile balanced the signatures."

* * *

The silence went on so long that the fire had time to collapse into embers.

Wren rose. She walked to the window and stood beside him, not touching, both of them looking out at the dark where the stars should have been.

"You knew," she said at last. Her voice was flat and cold and very quiet, and he had heard that voice before, on the lips of the dying captain in the pass — *the smith-blood* — and he understood that he was hearing the sound a person made when the last soft thing in their world went hard. "You knew what I was. You knew what they were doing to me. All those years in the kitchens, dosing myself, bleeding strength into the wards without knowing it — I was a *siphon*, Halric. The crown didn't just take my mother. They put me on a payroll and fed me poisons to thicken my blood and let the wards drink me dry a spoonful at a time. And you knew."

"I suspected. I confirmed it the night the towers fell — the collapse pattern, it matched the draw-figures, it matched —"

"Don't." She said it without heat, which was worse than heat. "Don't soften it. You rode toward Thornfell to find whoever the wards were bonded to, and you found me, and you've been guiding me ever since, and not once — not on the pass, not at the hold, not at the checkpoint — did you say *Wren, the throne you've served since you were nine years old has been drinking your life*. You let me grieve my mother as a stranger's tragedy. You let me be loyal."

"Yes." He made himself say it plainly. "Because I was a coward with signatures, and I became a coward with silence, and I told myself the ember mattered more than the truth, that you couldn't carry both. That was the last lie, Wren. The ledger of lies has one more line in it and the line is mine."

She turned from the window. In the ember-light her face was unreadable, and he thought: *she learned that in the kitchens too. They taught her to keep her face when the poison was working.*

"Here is what I feel," she said, in that cold, level voice. "I feel that the king owes my mother a debt that cannot be paid in honors. I feel that every ward I ever tasted for, every meal I ever cleared, was a theft I thanked them for. I feel that my whole life has been a column in someone else's ledger." She looked at her own hands, turned them over. "And underneath all of that — I feel the ember, Halric. It's been burning warmer since we entered the vale. It's been *answering* me. And I think the crown knows it's found me, because the crown has known about the smith-blood all along, and a crown that hoards strength in vaults does not let its richest vein walk out of the mountains."

The cold in her voice finally showed him the shape of what was coming, and his stomach dropped.

"They'll summon you," he said.

"They'll summon me. A royal courier, or a hunter's arrow, whichever travels faster." She folded her mother's letter and put it away, unhurried, precise. "So here is what happens now, Halric. You've given me your shame and I'm going to carry it the way I carry everything — usefully. You'll spend the road to the capital telling me every name, every figure, every signature. The whole ledger. Because if they drag me before the king, I intend to stand in his court with the true column in my head, and I intend to watch his face when he understands that the taster he poisoned for fifteen years can taste *anything*."

She pulled her jacket straight, and for a moment the ember's glow showed faintly through the cloth, over her heart, like a second heartbeat.

"And Halric — when we get there, and they offer you the chance to sign one more document to save yourself, you'll refuse. Not for me. For you. That's the penance. No more signatures."

Outside, far down the valley, a horn sounded — three notes, repeated, the signal a royal courier used to announce himself at a border post. Then, after a silence, again.

Wren smiled, and there was nothing warm in it at all.

"Ride fast," she said. "They've found me."
