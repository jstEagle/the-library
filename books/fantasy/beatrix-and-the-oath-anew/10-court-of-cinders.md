## 10. Court of Cinders

They came for her at the waystone grove, three days after the ember first spoke to her in her mother's voice.

Wren heard the horses before she saw them — too many horses, moving with the disciplined rhythm of crown cavalry, not raiders. She had time to pocket the ember, feel it pulse against her palm like a second heart, and arrange her face into something a loyal servant of the throne might wear.

The captain who dismounted at the grove's edge wore the king's colors and an expression of profound relief.

"Taster Sandoval." He said it like a password. "Thank all the stars still standing. We thought the mountains had eaten you."

"The mountains tried," Wren said. "I'm told I don't digest well."

He didn't laugh. None of them laughed. That was the first wrong thing. The second was the carriage — not a supply wagon, not a prisoner's cart, but a proper lacquered carriage with curtains drawn, sent two hundred leagues into occupied territory for one woman whose official title was *kitchen staff*.

"You're summoned," the captain said. "His Majesty's writ. We ride tonight."

Wren looked past him at the escort: twelve lances, a healer, a man in grey robes whose collar bore the stitched sigil of the Wardens' College — the first Warden she'd seen since Thornfell fell, since the towers rang like dying bells and the sky went black as a closed eye.

"Summoned," she repeated. Not arrested. Not escorted. Summoned.

She could have run. The thought arrived honestly, sitting down beside her like an old friend. The grove knew her now; the stones would hide her; Halric would find her by whatever smuggler's instinct kept him alive through twenty years of exile. But the vale behind her was burning, and the road to the capital ran through towns full of people who still believed the crown would save them, and some stubborn root of her — the same root that had made her swallow poison every morning for seven years without flinching — wanted to hear it from the king's own mouth.

What had he known. When had he known. What was she to him.

She climbed into the carriage.

* * *

The capital had changed in the six weeks since she'd last seen it, and the change smelled like tallow and fear.

Great cities always smell of something. Aurelmar in her childhood had smelled of river-mud and roasting chestnuts and the iron tang of the mint. Now it smelled of candles burning at noon because the starless nights came early and stayed late this far from Thornfell, and of smoke from too many fires banked too close together, and underneath it all, faint as a lie under a courtier's compliments, something cold and mineral that Wren recognized because she'd been tasting it in her dreams: the same note as the poison in the king's stew, the same note as the residue in her little glass vial.

Ward-ash. The city was breathing ward-ash.

They brought her through the river gate without stopping, up the processional avenue where the chestnut trees had been cut down — for sightlines, someone said, or for firewood, depending on who you asked — and into the inner keep through a door she'd never known existed, a plain iron door in the shadow of the great hall that opened onto stairs going down before they went up.

"Where are we—" she started.

"Protocol," the captain said, and wouldn't meet her eyes.

They searched her in a stone antechamber lit by lamps that burned without flame — old wards, still working, and she felt them the way she'd come to feel all wards now, as a pressure against her skin, a hand testing a door. They took her knife, her coins, the letter from her mother's workshop folded against her ribs. They did not take the ember.

The man in grey robes passed his palms over her, frowned, passed them again.

"She's clean," he said finally.

Wren almost laughed. Almost. The ember sat inside her — not in her pocket anymore; it hadn't been in her pocket since the grove, since the night it had sunk through cloth and skin like warm water and taken up residence somewhere behind her sternum — and the Warden's hands had passed directly over it, and he'd called her clean.

Either his art was dead along with everything else the old order built, or nobody down here wanted to find what she was carrying.

Or, said a quieter voice, they already know exactly what you're carrying, and the search is theater.

She was given an hour in a guest room with a barred window and food she tested out of pure reflex — clean, all of it, which somehow frightened her more than the opposite would have — and then a servant in royal livery laid out clothes on the bed. Not her kitchen greys. A gown of deep blue wool, fine but severe, the kind of thing worn by women who stood at shoulders' distance from thrones.

"They expect me to dress for it," Wren said to the empty room.

The room didn't answer, but behind her breastbone the ember turned over slowly, like a sleeper changing position, and she chose to read that as agreement.

* * *

The throne room of Aurelmar had been built to make visitors feel small, and it worked on Wren even knowing the trick of it — the forty-foot ceiling, the windows of tinted glass that turned daylight to amber, the long walk across a floor of black marble veined with white so that every step seemed to cross a fault line.

But the throne room was empty.

An usher in silver led her past the dais, past the empty throne with its hammered sunburst crown-rest, through a door behind a tapestry of the First Forging, and down.

Down, and down, and down. The stairs were old in a way the palace above wasn't — the capital had burned twice in recorded history and rebuilt itself both times in fashionable pale stone, but these steps were granite, worn concave by centuries of feet, and the walls sweated cold, and the lamps here weren't warded lamps but honest oil, guttering, human.

"How deep does it go?" she asked the usher.

He was young enough that his fear was still visible, sitting on him like an ill-fitting coat. "Nobody counts the stairs, mistress. You just walk until the King's Voice tells you to stop."

The King's Voice stopped them at a door of banded bronze, green with age except where a thousand hands had polished the handle-bar bright. Beyond it: warmth. A dry, humming, electrical sort of warmth that Wren felt in her fillings and in the ember behind her sternum, which had begun — she noticed with clinical detachment, the taster's detachment, the part of her that catalogued poisons while they killed her cell by cell — to sing.

Not audibly. Not yet. But soon, she thought, it wouldn't be only her who heard it.

The chamber beyond the door was vast and circular and wrong.

It had been a vault once, she could see that — the old order's architecture, the same joined-stone craft as the waystones, the same patient geometry. Someone had filled it. Racks of glass vessels glowed along the walls, amber and green and one sickly violet, and between the racks walked figures in Warden grey with clipboards, and in the center of the floor, arranged around a raised dais like an audience waiting for a play, sat the court.

Not all the court. But enough. Ministers with their chains of office. Generals with faces she'd seen on proclamations. The Lord Treasurer, who had signed the requisition for her kitchen salary for seven years and had never once looked at her.

And on the dais, on a plain chair that had no business looking like a throne but did, sat King Aldous III.

She'd tasted for him perhaps thirty times. He'd never spoken to her. Kings don't speak to spoons. He was older than she'd expected — everyone was, these days — with a grey beard trimmed short and eyes the color of pond ice, and when he saw her he did something no king should be able to do smoothly: he rose, and crossed the dais, and took her hands in his.

"Taster Sandoval," he said. "Wren, if I may. You have no idea what it cost me, letting them send you into those mountains blind."

Behind her sternum, the ember went very still.

"Your Majesty honors me," Wren said, and gave him the kitchen bow, the shallow one, because deep bows are for people you trust.

"Oh, I do more than honor you." He smiled, and it was a kind smile, a warm smile, the smile of a man who believes himself reasonable right down to the marrow. "I've waited half my life for you to walk into this room. Come. Look."

He led her to the nearest rack of glowing vessels, and up close she could see what was inside them: light. Not firelight, not lamplight. *Wardlight*, the amber of the border towers, the green of the waystones, suspended in fluid like insects in sap.

"Ward-ember, harvested and husbanded," the king said. "There are eleven hundred vessels in this room, Wren. When my grandfather was crowned there were four hundred thousand. Do you know why they're failing?"

"I've seen them fail," Wren said carefully. "In the mountains."

"You've seen the *symptom*. The disease is arithmetic." He tapped the glass, and the light inside shrank from his finger. "Every ward ever raised drew its strength from a bonded life. The smith-bloods. The old families who swore themselves into the stones, generation after generation, each child born carrying a spark of the family oath. It was a renewable treasury, so long as the bloodlines continued and the bond was renewed freely." His voice didn't change at all when he said *freely*. "But bloodlines thin. Families die out, or marry out, or forget. And the crown — my father, his father, and back five reigns — discovered that the bonds could be drawn upon without renewal. That the treasury could be spent faster than it was earned. Every ward in this kingdom has been running on credit for sixty years, Wren. And the creditors have come to collect."

"The collapse," she said. "Thornfell."

"Thornfell simply had nothing left. The first account to empty." He said it the way a banker says it. "The others will follow. Aurelmar's shields have perhaps two years. Less, now, with the dark spreading from the east."

Wren looked at the vessels again. At the violet ones, the sickly ones, and understood suddenly what she was looking at. Not harvested ember. Harvested *spark*. Bonded lives, drawn out drop by drop.

"Those are people," she said.

"Those are assets," the King's Voice said mildly, from somewhere behind her, and she realized the soft-spoken chancellor had followed them down. "Volunteers, mostly."

"*Mostly.*"

"Wren." The king turned her gently by the shoulder, back toward him, and his pond-ice eyes were full of something that looked, horribly, like tenderness. "Let me tell you about your mother."

The chamber went quiet. Even the humming warmth seemed to lean in.

"Liesse Sandoval, of the hold at Coldvane. Last true heir of the ward-smith line. Your family didn't just bond with the wards, girl — your family *built* them. The First Forging was smith-work. Every tower, every stone, every shield-wall from here to the sea carries your blood in its grammar. When your mother fled the capital, my father's Warden-General called it treason." He paused. "It was, of course. She stole the last great ember of the old oath rather than see it spent. Carried it into hiding. And then she did something cleverer than theft, something we only pieced together fifteen years ago, when a kitchen girl survived henbane in the king's own stew without so much as a hiccup."

Wren's mouth had gone dry. Seven years of mornings. Seven years of swallowing death and calling it a job. "You—"

"We tested you," the king said simply. "Poison is a crude instrument, but it reads the blood beautifully. Smith-blood doesn't just resist toxin, it *consumes* it, converts it, spends it the way the wards spend light. You've been dosing yourself with proof every day of your working life, and you never knew. Your mother split the oath, Wren. Hid the greater part of it in the places only a smith could find, and hid the living seed of it — " he touched his own chest, lightly, an obscene echo of where hers burned — "in the one vessel no thief would think to search. Her unborn daughter."

The ember behind her sternum blazed so hard she swayed. The king steadied her elbow, courteous as a dance partner.

"So you understand my position," he said. "I have watched you for years. I let you run to the mountains because I needed to know if you'd found the hidden portion, if the oath would answer you — and it has, hasn't it? The stones speak your name. My Wardens felt the grove light up from three provinces away." He stepped back, and his voice became, at last, what it had always been underneath the warmth: a ledger being balanced. "You are the last solvent account in this kingdom's treasury, Wren Sandoval. And I am asking you — no. I am *telling* you, with every courtesy I possess — that you will take your place among the vessels. Freely, tonight, with ceremony, if you consent. Or otherwise, in the morning, without it."

The words took a moment to arrive, the way distant thunder arrives after lightning.

"A battery," Wren said. "You want me to be a battery."

"For a year, perhaps two, and then your children—" the chancellor began.

"I have no children."

"Arrangements can be made," the king said gently. "The old families managed it. It needn't be cruel, Wren. You'll want for nothing. You'll be *saving* everything — every wall, every harvest, every child in every town between here and the sea. That's not a cage I'm offering you. It's the highest duty the crown can bestow."

And there it was, the whole shape of her life turned inside out in one sentence. Seven years of poison, and she saw now it had all been preparation — not for tasting, for *this*, for the day the crown would come to collect its quiet investment. She had loved this kingdom the way you love a house you grew up in: blindly, structurally, without ever asking who owned it. She had swallowed its poisons gladly. She had carried its ember through occupied mountains with a wound in her heart where her mother used to be.

And it had known. All of it, every step, every spoonful. She had never once been free, and it had smiled at her with pond-ice eyes and called it duty.

"No," Wren said.

The word came out small in the great round room. Small, and absolutely clean, like the first unmuddied taste after years of ash.

The king sighed, and looked genuinely sorry. "Captain," he said to the doorway, "the Taster is unwell. See her to the deep chambers. Gently."

Four guards started forward. Behind Wren's sternum the ember flared white-hot, and every vessel in the eleven-hundred-vessel rack nearest her dimmed at once — a whole wall of stolen light flinching like candle flames before a draft — and the Wardens stopped writing, and the generals stopped breathing, and the king looked at the dimming glass, and then at her, with the first honest expression she'd seen on his face.

Fascination.

"Take her down," he said softly. "Carefully. Everything depends on careful, now."

The guards' hands closed on her arms, and far above, through a hundred feet of granite, Wren felt the palace's oldest wards wake and begin to sing — and behind her sternum, her mother's ember sang back.

Somewhere in the dark of the stairwell below, she heard a door unbar itself before anyone touched it.
