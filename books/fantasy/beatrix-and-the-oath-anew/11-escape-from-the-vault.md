## 11. Escape from the Vault

The chains were the first honest thing anyone had shown me since the throne room.

They were old iron, pitted with rust that wasn't rust — ward-rust, the kind that ate light instead of metal. Each link hummed faintly against my wrists, and I understood without being told what they were for. Not to hold me. To *drain* me. The same way a taster's spoon holds just enough poison to test a cup.

I had been in the vaults three days. Maybe four. There was no window, and the lamps burned with a flame that gave no heat, so time went soft at the edges. What I knew was this: the chains drank, the dark fed, and twice a day someone came down the stairs with bread, water, and a face like an apology they hadn't gotten around to giving yet.

There were eleven others chained along the vault wall. Bond-heirs. That word still felt strange in my mouth — *heir*, like we'd inherited something worth having. Most of them were younger than me. A boy of maybe fifteen with river-country vowels. Twin sisters from the eastern passes who spoke only to each other, in a language I half-recognized from my mother's workshop notes. An old man named Corren whose hands shook so badly he couldn't hold the bread they brought him, so I broke it for him, night after night, because my hands still worked.

Mine still worked. That was the thing nobody in this room wanted to say out loud.

The chains had taken something from all of them. From the twins it had taken color; their skin had gone gray as unlit coals. From the boy it had taken his voice — not stolen, exactly, just *hollowed*, so that when he tried to speak, nothing came out but breath. From Corren it seemed to have taken most of a life. But from me it took almost nothing, and every hour that passed with my blood still burning steady under my skin was an hour the others knew about it, because you can't hide what you are from people who share your inheritance. They could feel me the way you feel a fire in a cold room.

The smith-blood. The captain at Thornfell had said it like a curse, like a key, like both.

The crown had known. The king had looked at me across that long table with eyes like ledger entries and said, *You'll be comfortable in the vaults, Mistress Sandoval,* and meant it. Meant every word. My country had spent twenty years siphoning strength out of bonded families through forged records and quiet deaths, and when the last of the wards finally fell, its answer was not repentance. Its answer was me: a living battery, installed below the palace, powering whatever scraps of protection the crown could still afford.

And Halric — Halric was somewhere ahead of the royal hunters, carrying a confession and a plan and, if I let myself believe it, part of my heart, into occupied country. Every day down here made it less likely he was still alive. Every day down here made it more certain the devouring dark beneath Thornfell would finish waking before anyone reached the ruined towers.

So when the guards came down on the fourth evening with their bread and water and their sorry faces, I was already watching their hands.

* * *

You learn things as a food-taster that no one thanks you for learning. You learn that arsenic tastes of nothing at all until it's too late. You learn that bitter almond is a mercy, because at least it warns you. And you learn the small pharmacology of the ordinary: which herbs in the kitchen garden are seasonings to a cook and dosages to a taster. Foxglove. Hemlock. The little blue flowers that grow in the cracks of the palace walls, whose seeds will stop a man's lungs if you're careless and slow his arms if you're kind.

The guards carried a waterskin between them, passing it back and forth on the long stair, and by the time they reached the bottom of the vault, the older one — Tammas, the younger called him, though neither had ever offered me a name unprompted — was rubbing his shoulder where the strap bit.

"Vault duty," the young one said. "Fourteen days of it. My feet are dead."

"Could be worse," Tammas said. "Could be the border."

"Could be dead," the boy agreed, and they both laughed, and I thought: *there*. There is the opening, the way there is always an opening, because men who have been given an unpleasant job will always, always want two things from it. Comfort, and company in the discomfort.

"I could fix those feet," I said.

They turned. Three days I had barely spoken, playing drained, playing dim, letting them see a woman worn down to usefulness — breaking bread for old Corren, mopping spilled water with her chained hands, harmless. Now I let my voice come up warm from underneath.

"The mountain folk have a liniment," I said. "Yarrow and wintergreen and a few other things. My mother's recipe." A lie wrapped in a truth; my mother had indeed written it, in her workshop, in ink I now understood to be blood. "My hands work fine. Give me the herbs off that shelf—" I nodded at the tending-station bolted to the far wall, where they kept bandages and a physician's dull cabinet for the prisoners they didn't want dying inconveniently, "—and I'll mix it while you drink your supper."

"We're not supposed to talk to them," the young one said, but without conviction.

"You're not supposed to feed them either, and here we are." I held up my chained wrists and smiled, and it cost me something to smile, and that was what made it work. Tasters survive by reading faces; I read his. He was nineteen and homesick and his feet hurt and the girl he'd left in the lowlands hadn't written. He wanted to sit down. He wanted someone to be kind to him. The vault was cold and the dark down here was the hungry kind, and I was offering him warmth in a cup.

Tammas shrugged. "Ten minutes. Then lights out."

The shelf above the tending-station held what I needed, because it always does, because the world is generous with poisons to anyone patient enough to look. Dried yarrow, real. Wintergreen oil, real. And behind the physician's jars, half-hidden, a paper twist of valerian cut with something sharper — the palace kept sedatives for prisoners who raved, and whoever stocked that shelf had never once considered that the prisoner might be a woman who had spent six years learning to identify substances by smell alone.

I crushed the yarrow honestly. I used the wintergreen honestly. The liniment I handed to Tammas was genuine and good and eased his shoulder within minutes, and he grunted with surprise and gratitude, and I felt the shape of the lie settle over me like a coat I'd been fitted for years ago.

"And for the feet," I said, "the mountain trick is a strong tea, drunk warm. Takes the ache out from inside."

I brewed it in a tin cup from the station. Valerian first, generous. Then the blue seeds, ground fine against the cup's rim with my thumb, careful, careful — not a killing dose, not a tenth of one. Just enough that in twenty minutes their legs would go heavy and their words would go slow and their excellent intentions would dissolve into a sleep deep enough to be mistaken, by anyone checking at the top of the stairs, for two guards who'd drunk too much cheap wine on a boring shift.

I handed the cup to the young one first, because he was the harder sell, and kindness goes further when it's unexpected.

He hesitated.

"Taster's honor," I said, and laughed at myself, and he laughed too, at the absurdity of it, at the famous traitor-taster vowing purity over a cup of tea, and he drank.

Tammas drank after him because men follow each other into everything, including cups.

I sat with them while it took hold. I asked the young one about the girl in the lowlands — I could tell from how he'd said nothing about her that there was one — and he told me her name was Bess and she had a laugh like a cart going downhill, and I listened the way you listen when you know you're the last person who will ever hear a story. Twenty minutes. His head nodded. Tammas said something slow and slurred about fourteen days of vault duty being criminal, and then they were down, both of them, folded against the wall like puppets with their strings cut, breathing easy, hearts steady. They'd wake in six hours with sore necks and a story nobody would believe.

The keys were on Tammas's belt. Of course they were. The crown had never once imagined its prisoners as competent; that was the whole architecture of everything it had done to us.

I picked the ring off him gently. I unlocked my own chains first because somebody had to be free to unlock the rest.

* * *

Corren wept when the shackle came off his wrist, silently, the way old men cry, without any change in his expression at all.

The boy who couldn't speak touched his own throat and looked at me with a question, and I shook my head — I didn't know if freedom would give his voice back; the draining might be permanent — and he nodded slowly, and then he did something that undid me: he put his fist to his chest, once, the way soldiers do, and pointed at me.

The twins were on their feet before their chains finished falling, gray hands gripping each other, already speaking in that fast pass-country tongue, and I caught enough of it to understand they were arguing about whether to run east toward the border or west toward the mountains, and I realized, with a lurch like a floor giving way, that they were waiting for me to decide.

Eleven people in a vault under the palace, and every eye had turned to the woman with the keys.

"No," I said, out loud, to all of us. "Don't— I'm not your captain. I poisoned two guards and stole a ring. That's the whole of my qualifications."

The twins looked at each other. One of them said something short, and the other laughed — a dry, cracked sound, the first laugh I'd heard down here — and translated anyway, though she didn't need to, because I understood her perfectly:

"She says the one who gets us out is the captain."

Leadership, it turns out, is not something anyone gives you. It's something that happens to you while you're busy doing the next necessary thing, and by the time you notice it, refusing it costs more lives than accepting it.

"Fine," I said. "West. Through the mountain roads, toward Thornfell. There are people — a man named Halric Vane, bond-heirs freed from other vaults, a grove of living waystones. If the wards are going to be rebuilt, that's where it starts, and nowhere else." I looked around the circle of hollow faces. "I won't lie to you. It's a long way, and the crown hunts us, and the thing under Thornfell is worse than the crown. Anyone who wants to take their chances east or south or in a ditch alone, no one here will think the less of you."

Nobody moved toward the door that led to the southern passages. Eleven exhausted, gray-skinned, half-hollowed people stood looking at me like I was a lit lamp in an unlit world, and I understood that this, too, was the inheritance — not just the blood, but the weight of being the one still standing.

We went up the guard stairs single file, quiet as thieves, which was fitting, because that was what the crown had made all of us.

* * *

The upper vaults were emptier than they should have been.

That was my first warning. The second was the sound of boots overhead, many boots, moving fast, and a bell ringing somewhere above in the pattern I recognized from my kitchen years as *fire in the lower quarters* — which meant either an actual fire or an excuse to clear a section of the palace, and either way it emptied the corridors for perhaps ten minutes.

Ten minutes is a lifetime, if you've spent six years learning exactly how long everything takes.

We moved through the storage levels in a loose line, Corren's arm over my shoulder, the twins flanking the boy, past racks of confiscated goods and crates stamped with the crown's thorn sigil, and we might have walked clean out of the palace and into history if the third staircase hadn't had a man on it.

He was young. Younger than Tammas. He had a spear and a helmet that didn't fit and he saw eleven ghosts climbing out of the vaults and his spear came up and his voice cracked on the word *"Halt,"* and I watched the whole future balance on the point of that spear.

I could taste the moment like I taste everything: completely, helplessly, in detail.

It was the boy — the voiceless one — who solved it. He stepped forward, past me, and pulled his shirt aside to show the drain-scars over his heart, the pale branching lines where the chains had fed, and he looked at that soldier the way only someone robbed can look at someone, and the spear wavered.

"My king's orders," the soldier whispered, but his spear was coming down.

"Your king," I said quietly, "chained children in the dark and called it protection. Step aside, son. Or don't, and be standing there when more of us come up these stairs, because we will keep coming, and I'd hate for you to be the reason the mountain stays dark."

I don't know if the words mattered. I don't know if anything I say ever matters as much as the things my blood does without asking me. But he stepped aside, back pressed to the wall, eyes shut like a child hiding from thunder, and we went past him, and at the last second he said, "The river gate. West postern. The porter drinks on watch after midnight bell," and shut his mouth hard, as if shocked at himself.

We went out through the west postern under a sky with no stars, eleven fugitives and one borrowed darkness, and the city swallowed us street by street.

* * *

We lost the river gate behind us and gained the marsh road, and the marsh road gained us nothing but miles, and by the grey hour before dawn we were hiding in a culvert while a hunter's patrol rode past close enough to count, and that was when Merrin — the elder twin — grabbed my sleeve and pointed back the way we'd come.

Smoke. Thin and straight, rising from the direction of the palace. Wrong smoke. Ward-smoke burns green at the base, my mother's diagrams had taught me, and this smoke burned green.

"They found the empty vaults," Corren said. "They'll burn what's left of the drains. Evidence." He said the word like it tasted foul.

But I was counting riders. The patrol had come from the south road, not the city, and it flew no banner I knew, and its leader wore grey, not scarlet — and grey was the color the hunters wore, the crown's long-arm men, except these had ridden *past* the city without stopping, which meant they weren't hunting us.

They were riding west. Toward Thornfell. Toward the passes. Toward a disgraced keeper of wards who had once loved my mother and was, as of four days ago, the only person alive who knew what I would need to survive what was coming.

"Halric," I breathed, and the name came out of me like something torn.

Merrin said something in pass-tongue, quick, and her sister answered, and the boy with no voice put his hand flat on the culvert stone and — I saw it plainly, in the grey pre-dawn, with stars gone from the sky — the stone *warmed* under his palm, faintly, the way a hearthstone remembers fire. Whatever the chains had taken from him, something was growing back. Something was waking up in all of them, and I suspected I knew why: the ember my mother had hidden in my unborn body was burning hotter now, closer to the mountain, calling to its kin.

"They'll reach the passes in two days," Corren said. "We can't make a day."

"Then we don't take the road," I said, and heard myself say it, and marveled a little at the stranger wearing my voice. "There's a smuggler's cut through the fells — steep, unmarked, no patrols, because no one with sense takes it. My mother's maps are still in her workshop, and her workshop is on our way."

"Your family hold," Merrin said. It wasn't a question. Word traveled among the bond-heirs; of course they knew what I was now, whose daughter I was, what the stones had called out to me on the border road a lifetime ago.

"Three days' hard walking saves us two days' riding," I said. "And if the hunters catch us in the fells, at least we'll die somewhere the crown has never bothered to learn."

Nobody argued. Eleven people followed a food-taster up out of a culvert and into the hills, west, toward the smoke-green horizon and the starless dark and whatever my mother had left buried in the ashes of our name.

Behind us the palace burned its evidence. Ahead of us the mountain waited with its mouth open.

And somewhere on a road between here and there, a man who owed the world a debt was walking toward the same ruin I was, and I prayed — for the first time since the stars went out, I prayed — that I would reach him before the hunters did, or before the dark beneath Thornfell finished opening its eye.

Because I had escaped the vaults. But the vault, I was beginning to understand, had only ever been practice.

The true prison was the oath itself, and it was waiting for us at the top of the mountain, and it intended to be paid.
