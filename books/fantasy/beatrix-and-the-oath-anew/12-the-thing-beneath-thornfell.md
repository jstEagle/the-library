## 12. The Thing Beneath Thornfell

Halric had told Wren he would scout the pass ahead while she slept. That was true, in the way most of his truths were true — technically, and only up to a point.

The fuller truth was that he needed to see it for himself. The thing beneath Thornfell. He had spent twenty years telling himself the ledgers he falsified were about grain weights and ward-draws, numbers on paper, politics dressed in ink. He had never once let himself imagine what the siphoning was *for* — what the crown bought with the strength it drained out of bonded families like hers, family by family, year by year. Numbers were safe. Numbers could be balanced.

But three nights ago he had watched the stars go out over the border inn like candles snuffed by a passing hand, and he had felt the sound of the towers falling in his teeth before he heard it in his ears, and he understood with a certainty that felt like falling: the numbers had always been a lid on something. And the lid was off.

So he rode east from the grove road while Wren slept with the ember-fragments humming under her skin, and left a note that said *scouting, back by dawn* in handwriting he hoped looked steadier than his hands felt.

* * *

The old pilgrim track to Thornfell's roots hadn't changed in twenty years. That surprised him. Everything else had — the vale towns burning behind him, the roads thick with conscript columns and refugees, the sky overhead wrong in a way his eyes kept refusing to process. He'd look up and count stars and get to four, five, then lose them because they were moving, or weren't stars at all, or weren't anything anymore. A man could go mad looking up. He kept his eyes on the track.

It climbed through black pine, past waystones that no longer spoke to him — they had gone silent decades ago, after the exile, after the bond was struck from his name. He had hated that silence once. Now he envied it. At least the stones couldn't feel what was coming through their roots.

Near midnight the trees stopped.

Not thinned. Stopped. One stride he was walking among pines; the next there was bare stone, smooth as a table, sloping down into a bowl of rock that no map he'd ever forged had shown. The air smelled of struck flint and, underneath it, faintly, of meat left too long in a cold room.

Halric crouched at the rim of the bowl and looked down into the mountain's root, and everything he had spent twenty years not imagining was there.

* * *

It was light. That was the first obscenity of it. He had expected dark — the starless dark, the devouring dark, all the words the old texts used. But what lay in the bowl of stone glowed: a pale, sourceless luminescence, the color of moonlight drained of everything that made moonlight kind. It pooled and shifted, and where it shifted, the shapes moved.

They were people.

That was his first thought, and it took him a long moment — heart slamming, breath caught — to understand it was his second thought that was correct. They were not people. They were *wearing* people. Figures of pale light walked the floor of the bowl in slow procession, and each one had a face he half-knew: a waywarden who had died on the wall at Harrow Gap. A kitchen girl from the border inn whose name he'd never learned but whose burned apron he'd stepped around two days ago. An old woman with a shawl — and his stomach dropped through the stone, because he knew that shawl, had known its owner, had sat across a table from her while she taught him the grammar of the oath he would later sell.

Maren Sandoval's mother. No. Not her. Something remembering her, the way a crow remembers a song.

"Ah," Halric said aloud, very quietly, to nobody, because saying it made it real and unreal at once. "You've been eating the dead."

Because that was the pattern, wasn't it? The wards had fed on living blood — the smith-blood, the bond-heirs, the long slow drain the crown had administered like a farmer milking stock. But wards did not exist alone. Every ward had a shadow-side, a place where what it kept out pressed against what it kept in, and pressure leaves an impression. Twenty years of ward-strength poured against this seal, and the seal had grown thin, and thin places *remember*. The dead of every battle fought near the mountain, every plague winter, every fall from the passes — it had all been soaking down into the roots like rain, and something down there had learned the shapes.

And now the wards were fallen, and it was waking, and it was dressing itself in its memories for the occasion.

He should have turned back then. He would tell himself afterward, many times, that any sane man would have. But Halric Vane had spent twenty years being precisely the wrong amount of cowardly — too much to do right, not enough to run clean — and so instead of retreating he slid down the first arm of the slope to get a closer look at the ledger. Because that was how his mind insisted on framing it. The final entry. The account the numbers had been hiding.

* * *

Closer, the light resolved into detail he did not want.

The figures were not walking aimlessly. They were walking the circuit of the old seal — he recognized the geometry, the seven-pointed weave of the Thornfell oath, except inverted, worn into the stone like a groove worn by water. Where their feet passed, the groove deepened. They were pacing out the shape of their own prison's weakness, patiently, the way a prisoner paces a cell, and every circuit wore the walls a little thinner.

And at the center of the seven points, something else.

It is difficult to write what he saw there, and Halric would spend years failing to describe it to anyone who hadn't seen it. The nearest he ever came was this: imagine a hole in the world shaped like an appetite. Not a creature. A *direction*. The pale light bent toward it the way fire bends toward a draft, and the shapes of the dead walked their circuits and fed it, willingly, the way the freed bond-heirs had offered their memories at the waystone grove — except inverted, hollowed, love turned inside out into hunger.

It was eating the light. He understood that much, watching the glow pool and dim and pool again. Whatever the stars were — and the ward-smiths had known, had built their whole grammar on it — this thing ate them. Had eaten them. The night without stars hadn't been a side effect of the wards falling. It had been the first meal. The wards hadn't been keeping armies out of Thornfell.

They'd been keeping the sky full.

His hands were shaking. He noted it distantly, the way he'd once noted discrepancies in a ledger column, and made himself keep counting, because if he stopped counting he would stop thinking, and if he stopped thinking —

One of the shapes stopped walking.

It stood at the edge of the seventh point, and it turned its face up toward the rim of the bowl, toward the shadowed rocks where Halric crouched, and the face was Maren Sandoval's mother's face, and it smiled with a mouth that had learned smiling secondhand.

"Halric Vane," it said.

The voice came from everywhere — from the stone, from the light, from inside his own skull where his own thoughts lived. It was not loud. It was patient. That was the worst of it. Armies shout. This thing had all the time in a starless night, and it spoke like a clerk reading a name off a page it had been holding for twenty years.

"The forger," it said. "We kept your signature."

And it showed him.

Not with eyes. With something older than eyes. It poured the memory into him the way you'd pour water into a cup: the scriptorium in the capital, the candle, the king's minister sliding the false ledgers across the desk, the weight of the pen, the smell of the ink — except the memory had edges the original hadn't. In the true memory, Halric had signed to save his daughter, and hated himself, and gone home drunk. In this version, the version the thing in the bowl held up to him like a mirror, he could see what he had refused to see for twenty years: that the ledgers recorded the draw-rates of the siphon, and the draw-rates were calibrated, tuned, *deliberate*, and the calibration curve bent toward exactly one outcome, and he had written his name at the bottom of the plan anyway. He had been not the fool of the crown's design. He had been its instrument. Its pen.

"You thought you were hiding theft," the shape of the dead woman said gently. "You were signing a feeding schedule."

Halric made a sound he didn't recognize as coming from his own throat.

The light began to move toward him.

Not fast. Nothing here was fast. But the figures abandoned their circuits, all seven lines of them at once, the geometry unraveling, and they turned and began to walk up the slope with the unhurried gait of mourners, and their faces were all faces he knew now — his dead wife, gods help him, they'd found her too, some border grave some winter raid — and his daughter, no, not his daughter, something wearing his daughter's eleven-year-old smile, and every one of them was looking at him with recognition, with *welcome*, and he understood that this was how it fed. Not on blood. On surrender. It opened the door of you from the inside. You walked down into the bowl yourself. You joined the circuit. That was why the wards demanded willing lives — because the thing beneath had been built, or had grown, or had simply always been, out of the exact opposite of willingness given freely. Out of everything taken.

"Halric." His daughter's shape was closest now, close enough that he could see the light did not quite manage her eyes; they were holes in the glow, hungry holes. "Come down. You've been outside so long."

He ran.

There was no dignity in it. He scrambled up the scree on hands and knees, tore his palm open on stone, heard the procession below him neither quickening nor ceasing — it didn't need to hurry; nothing that patient hurries — and the voice followed him up the slope, not shouting, never shouting, just *reading*: names. His name. Maren's name. Names of the bond-heirs she'd freed from the vaults, read out one by one like an inventory, like a ledger, like a feeding schedule, and the last name it read, just as he crossed the treeline, was Wren Sandoval's, spoken in her mother's voice, with a tenderness that was the cruelest thing he had ever heard.

Then the trees closed around him and the voice stopped, though the light still glowed faintly through the branches behind him, and the night above stayed empty of stars.

* * *

He made it three miles before his legs gave out, and sat down against a pine with his bleeding hand pressed to his chest, and shook.

A man's mind is a ward too, he thought. It has gates. It has walls. And he had just held them shut by the width of a habit — the forger's habit, the liar's habit, the lifelong refusal to believe anything at its own valuation. The thing had shown him truth and lie braided together, and the only reason he was sitting under a tree instead of walking that circuit was twenty years of practice doubting every document set in front of him, including the ones inside his own head. His shame had saved his life. There was a joke in that, and no energy left to laugh at it.

But his mind had held. Barely. Like the wards themselves — barely, and not for long.

Because here was the arithmetic he could no longer avoid, laid out clean as a ledger column: the thing beneath Thornfell was not an army. It was not the invaders riding through the broken border, terrible as they were. Armies could be fought, bargained with, outlasted. This was older than bargaining. It ate the light the world was made of, and the fallen wards had been its feedlot fence, and the crown had been fattening it for twenty years on stolen blood, and none of it — not the war, not the occupation, not the throne's whole rotten scheme — mattered next to the fact that within weeks, perhaps days, the circuits would wear through and the thing would finish waking and begin, in earnest, to eat.

Cosmic. The word arrived in his mind and stayed. All his life the stakes had been political — careers, pensions, his daughter's safety, a name kept clean or lost. Now the stake was whether the night sky continued to exist. Whether *anything* continued to exist, beyond as raw material for that patient glow.

Against which he had: a forged conscience, a torn hand, and a woman half a day west who carried the last ember of the old oath burning holes in her veins.

Wren.

She had to know. Not the sanitized version, not *the enemy holds the pass* — she had to know what waited under the mountain wearing the faces of the dead, and what the oath would truly cost, and why the old law demanded a willing life: because the alternative to a freely given life was not survival. It was the bowl. The circuit. The welcome.

And he knew — he had known since the poisoned cup, since she watched him drink with those level gray eyes — that when she understood the terms, she would try to pay them herself. She had her mother's whole stubborn architecture. She would walk down into that light before she let anyone else pay.

Which meant he had perhaps a day, riding hard, to decide how to stand between Wren Sandoval and the thing beneath Thornfell — knowing, with the certainty of a man who has finally read the whole ledger, that whatever he decided, he was going to have to sign his name to it.

Halric got up. His legs held. Behind him, through the black pines, the mountain's root glowed like a lamp turning in a dark room, and somewhere above the clouds where no stars were, the true dark waited to be filled or emptied forever.

He turned west and started running.
