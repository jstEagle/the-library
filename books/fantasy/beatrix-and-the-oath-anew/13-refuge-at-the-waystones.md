## 13. Refuge at the Waystones

The grove announced itself before we saw it.

I felt it first as a warmth in my chest — in the ember, rather, the splinter of old oath that had lived inside me since before I was born and that I had only learned to name three days ago. It turned toward something the way a compass needle turns north. Then the trees changed: the pines thinned, the air went silver, and out of the morning mist rose stones.

Not ruins. That was my first mistake, thinking them ruins. They were tall as watchtowers and slender as spears, maybe thirty of them, standing in a rough spiral on a shelf of mountain meadow, and every one of them was *lit*. Not with flame. With a slow pulse of gold beneath gray stone, like light under skin, like breath.

"Waystones," Sera said beside me. She was fourteen, the youngest of the bond-heirs I'd pulled from the vaults, and she hadn't let go of my sleeve since the escape. "My gran used to say there was a grove in the Thornfell high country where the stones still remembered. I thought it was a story."

"It's a story," said Doss, the quarryman, hoarse from three nights of running. "Everything's a story now. The sky's empty and we're following a girl with a fire in her blood up a frozen mountain."

"You're free to turn around," I said.

He didn't. None of us could. The ember's pull wasn't a suggestion; it was gravity. There were nine of us left out of the seventeen who'd come out of the king's vaults — nine drained, half-starved heirs of families whose names had been struck from the ward-ledgers so thoroughly even Halric needed his forger's memory to find this place. Nine people the crown had bled like batteries for twenty years, walking toward the last living thing our bloodline had ever built.

The stones brightened as we crossed the treeline. All thirty at once, a slow tide of gold rolling around the spiral, and I heard Sera gasp, and then I heard something worse: the stones humming. A low chord, felt in the teeth more than the ears. Recognizing us.

*Welcome home,* said nothing, in a voice that was not sound. Every bond-heir in the group stopped walking at the same instant.

"They know what we are," whispered Ilsette, the oldest of them, a gray-haired woman who had spent eleven years in the vaults and could still stand straight. "Gods help me. They know."

* * *

We made camp in the spiral's heart, because nowhere else felt safe anymore and because the cold up here would kill anyone who camped outside the stones' reach. The grove kept a temperature like late spring, and under its hum the fugitives slept — really slept — for the first time since the night without stars.

I didn't sleep. I sat with my back against the tallest stone and felt it lean back against me.

"You should rest," Halric said. He'd caught up to us two days out of the vaults, riding a stolen horse half to death, and he hadn't left my side since. In the firelight he looked older than his years and younger than his crimes, which I suppose is what guilt does to a face over time.

"I'm not tired."

"You're shaking."

"I'm listening." I put my palm flat on the stone. Beneath the gold pulse I could feel language — not words yet, but grammar. Rhythm. The way poison has a structure if you've studied it long enough; the way a lie has a shape. "They're trying to talk to me. To all of us. But they're talking to me loudest."

"Because you're carrying the—"

"Don't say it out loud. Not even here."

Halric crouched beside me. Twenty years of exile had taught him patience, and he wore it like armor, but tonight I saw the crack in it. "Wren. The ember fragments are burning you. I can see it. Your hands."

I looked down. My hands were fine. My hands were always fine. But when I closed my eyes I could feel it — heat laced through my veins like wine through water, a slow golden oxidation, the oath digesting the vessel that carried it. The grove knew it too. The nearest stone pulsed faster whenever I touched it, urgent, insistent, a doctor tapping for fluid in the lung.

*Soon,* the not-sound said. *Before it eats you. Learn.*

"How?" I asked aloud, feeling foolish.

The stone answered by taking me under.

* * *

It was not like falling asleep. Sleep has a floor. This had none.

Shared memory, I would later call it, for lack of better words — the stones opened their long recollection and poured it through the ember in my blood, and because the ember had been split from the grove's own heart-stone a generation ago, the memories came home to me like water finding an old channel. I did not watch the past. I stood inside it.

I stood inside the grove when it was young, when there were hundreds of stones instead of thirty, when ward-smiths walked the spiral barefoot in every season singing the oath's grammar into the ground — and I understood, suddenly, in one vertiginous swallow, that the wards were never walls. That was the crown's word, the crown's whole misunderstanding. Walls keep things out. The wards were a conversation. A treaty renewed nightly between the human vale and the deep dark beneath Thornfell: *we remember you, we name you, we agree together that you shall remain unmade.* An oath spoken by many hearts in harmony, held by stones that never forgot a single syllable. It required no death. It required only that enough willing hearts speak it, every dusk, forever.

That was the first lesson. The second was arithmetic: how few speakers remained, after the crown began draining bonded families into vaults. How the nightly chorus thinned to a whisper, then a stutter, then silence. How the towers fell not because the enemy broke them but because we starved them first.

I wanted to scream at the memory. The stones held me steady and moved on.

Then — her.

My mother came into the shared memory like a lamp coming into a dark room. Younger than I'd ever known her, round-bellied with me, kneeling in the center of this very spiral on the last night the stars still shone. Around her the other smiths argued, voices cracking with fear, because the ledgers had been falsified for two decades and everyone finally understood what that meant: the chorus was too thin, the towers would fail within a year, and the thing below was already tasting the seams.

And my mother held the ember.

The heart-stone of the grove had been dying, giving up its last strength to prop up the failing towers, and she had cut a fragment free with a smith's knife and a smith's grief — a live coal of the old oath, small as a plum seed, hot enough to burn bone. The others begged her to set it in a new tower. Build one more wall, they said. Buy one more decade.

She shook her head. Even in memory, even across twenty-odd years, I felt the weight of that headshake. A tower could be found. A tower could be taxed, drained, ledgered, lied about. The crown had proven it could hollow out anything built to stand apart.

So she did the one thing no ledger could reach. She laid her palm over the child not yet born, and she sang the ember through her own blood and skin and into mine, splitting it fine as frost across the smallest veins of a fetus, hiding the last free fragment of the oath inside the one person the crown would never suspect: the daughter who would grow up to taste the king's food and call herself nobody.

*I'm sorry,* my mother said, to the unborn me, in the voice I had spent twenty years failing to forget. *I wanted to give you a childhood. Instead I give you a war. Forgive me — or don't — but live, Wren. Live until the oath finds you, and then choose. No one else can choose for you. Not the crown. Not the stones. Not even me.*

The memory ended. I came back to my body kneeling in the frost with my palms bleeding where my nails had gone through.

* * *

Sera found me there. She didn't ask questions — vault children learn not to — she just sat down next to me and put her shoulder against mine and waited, the way you wait out a storm.

"She hid it in me," I said finally. "Before I was born. She made me a weapon and never told me."

"Was it a weapon?"

"It was twenty years of headaches. It was immunity I never earned. It was—" I stopped. Breathed. The grove hummed patiently around us, and the truth arranged itself the way truths do when you stop fighting them. "No. It was a choice. She said so. She gave me the choice and she took all the blame for carrying it until I was ready."

"And now you're ready."

"I don't know." I looked at my hands again. Under the skin, faint as banked coals, the gold was rising. "But I understand her. That's worse, somehow."

Sera considered this with the terrible economy of a fourteen-year-old. "My ma sold me to the crown's men for six silver and a sack of flour," she said. "I used to hate her. Then I saw what they did in the vaults, and I thought — six silver was probably everything she had. People hide love in stupid shapes." She shrugged. "Yours just happened to be shaped like you."

I laughed, and it came out wrong, half a sob, and Sera had the grace to pretend not to notice.

* * *

Halric found me at dusk, at the spiral's center, where the grove's oldest stone stood — the heart-stone's twin, scarred where the ember had been cut away. The wound in the stone still glimmered, a seam of raw gold, and when I pressed my hand to it the whole grove exhaled.

"It showed you," he said. Not a question. He'd been raised by these stones once, before his fall; he knew their habits.

"It showed me everything." I turned to face him. Firelight and stone-light mixed on his ruined, honest face. "The wards were never walls, Halric. They're a song. And the crown silenced the singers one family at a time and wrote it down as maintenance costs."

"I know."

"You *knew*. You signed—"

"I knew." He didn't flinch from it. He never flinched from it anymore, and that was the strange penance he'd built himself. "And I've been riding toward this grove for twenty years without knowing it was here, hoping someone would make me pay for it. No one ever did. So I'll tell you what the stones won't, because they'll show you gently, and you deserve it straight: the oath can't be sung by one voice, Wren. Not even yours. The old law says a single life freely given can anchor the web — one soul braided into the heart-stone, forever, so the chorus always has a root note. That's what your mother refused to do. That's why she hid the ember instead. She wouldn't feed the grove a person."

"Wouldn't she?"

"There's a grave outside this spiral," Halric said quietly. "Under the easternmost stone. A smith named Beatrix, four hundred years dead, who gave herself so the vale could sleep. Your mother stood on that grave and said *no more*, and hid the oath in the only vault the crown couldn't open." His voice cracked, just slightly, on the last words. "She was braver than I ever was. Braver than the whole rotten order."

I looked at him for a long time. The ember turned warm in my chest, and the grove waited, patient as mountains, and I understood that both of us were circling the same terrible arithmetic: one life, freely given, or the dark comes up out of the roots and drinks the sky permanently. My mother had bought me time. Time was nearly spent.

"Not yet," I said.

"No," Halric agreed. "Not yet."

* * *

I forgave my mother somewhere in the small hours, alone at the spiral's edge, watching the starless black and talking to a woman four years dead. I told her I understood. I told her about the stew, the waystone that spoke our name, the innkeeper's daughter, the vaults. I told her the choice she'd saved for me was monstrous and magnificent and that I was going to make it my own way or break every stone in this grove trying. Forgiveness, arriving twenty years too late to be spoken aloud, spoken anyway, into the cold, where maybe the stones carry voices longer than flesh does.

The grove listened. And then, near dawn, it stopped listening — every stone flaring gold at once, the hum spiking into something like an alarm.

Ilsette was already on her feet, gray head cocked toward the southern pass. "Riders," she said. "Many. Coming up the valley road fast."

I reached south with the ember's senses, the way the grove had taught me, and felt them: iron, horses, and worse — ward-cold, the drained chill of bond-blood being burned as fuel. Crown colors. The king's hunters had found the grove after all, and they weren't riding to talk.

Beside me, Halric drew his sword with the calm of a man who had already decided how this day would be paid for.

"Wren," he said. "How attached are you to the front gate?"
