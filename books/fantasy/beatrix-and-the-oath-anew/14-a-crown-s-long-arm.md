## 14. A Crown's Long Arm

### Halric

Halric smelled the hunters before he saw them — not the men themselves but the tallow of their torch-cakes, the cheap resin they used to keep pitch-flames burning in wet weather. He'd bought that same resin for twenty years at the same chandler's stall on the Street of Ledgers. Some habits of the crown outlived honor.

He held up a fist and the column stopped: Wren, the three bond-heirs who could still walk, little Maren asleep against Sef's back. Nine days out of the vault, four days down from the waystone grove, one valley left between them and the occupied pass. The children moved like people who had learned that stopping was dangerous.

"What is it?" Wren asked. Her voice stayed level. It had been leveling itself more and more since the grove, since her mother's letter, since the ember fragments in her blood had begun to show through her skin like lamplight through a curtain. She glowed faintly now when she forgot to hide it. Tonight she was hiding it well.

"Riders," he said. "Behind us, maybe an hour. Ahead of us too, if I know how Commander Ashgrave works her pincers."

"You know her."

"I funded her horses." He said it flatly, because there was no other way to say it. "Twenty years ago, when Captain Doss and I were falsifying the Thornfell ledgers, we needed someone in the Crown Guard who could lose requisition papers on command. Ashgrave was a lieutenant then. She signed away a garrison's worth of supply fraud and never once asked what she was signing away. We made each other." He looked at the dark line of the trees below the pass road. "She'll have remembered me fondly right up until the moment the king put a price on my head. Then she'll remember me as a loose end with excellent handwriting."

Wren came closer. In the starless dark — the stars were still gone; whatever the grove had promised, the sky above Thornfell remained a black lid — her face was mostly shadow and ember-light. "How long can you buy?"

"An hour. Maybe two if she wants something more than my head."

"She'll want the children."

"Yes," Halric said. "So don't let her see them."

* * *

### Wren

They tucked the bond-heirs into a hollow under the roots of a lightning-split fir — Sef first, because Sef would argue last; Maren still sleeping, which frightened Wren more than any waking fear would have. Then Wren climbed the rise with the ember burning its slow wire of heat up her forearm and watched Halric Vane walk down the switchbacks alone to meet thirty riders.

He went slowly, hands visible, the walk of a man who had rehearsed surrender his whole life and only now found a use for it.

The riders formed a crescent around him. Torches. Chainmail over royal blue. And at their center, a woman in a commander's half-plate, gray-streaked hair bound tight, dismounting with the unhurried economy of someone who had never once needed to hurry for anything.

"Ashgrave," Halric said, loud enough that Wren caught it from the rise. "You've kept your figure and lost your patience. Thirty riders to take one old forger."

"Forty," Ashgrave said. "The others are ahead of you. You taught me pincers, Vane — or did you forget? You drew the troop-movement diagrams yourself, back when your diagrams were lies." She walked a slow half-circle around him. "The king wants the girl. The heir-bonds she carries. And everyone who helped her reach the mountain."

"The children with her are dying," Halric said. "Drained near to husks by your king's vaults. Whatever you think you're delivering, you're delivering corpses."

"I deliver orders." Ashgrave's voice didn't change, and that was the terrible thing about her — Wren could hear it from forty yards, a voice sanded smooth of everything except function. "You'd know about orders, wouldn't you. You wrote them for twenty years. Ward-requisitions, bond-tithe schedules, the transfer authorizations that moved strength out of the smith families and into the capital reserves. Doss told me before they hanged him. He said you cried at the trial and lied at the ledger both."

"Doss forged worse than I did and hanged better than I deserved."

"Yes," Ashgrave said. "I've always liked that about you. Honesty at the end, like a man paying his debts before the tavern closes." She stopped circling. "Here is what I know, Vane. My orders say bring the girl alive. They say nothing about how intact. They say nothing about witnesses. If you give me trouble on this road, I will resolve it, and I will sleep afterward, because I have always slept well."

"And if I give you no trouble at all?"

"That depends entirely on what you're offering."

Halric raised his voice then, pitched to carry, and Wren understood with a cold drop in her stomach that he was pitching it for her too. "Passage. One hour's start, north through the occupied vale. In exchange, I stay. Name me traitor openly, bind me, take me back to the capital in chains. The trial will be short and the confession will be long. Every name. Every ledger. Twenty years of where the bodies and the bond-strength went. Your career survives the scandal because you brought in the man who started it all."

Silence on the road. Wind moving the dead bracken.

"Why," Ashgrave said, "would you hand me your own neck?"

"Because I owe a debt to a woman I failed," Halric said, "and her daughter, and her daughter's mother, and about nine hundred children whose names are written in ledgers you helped carry. I can't pay it standing behind them anymore. So I'll pay it standing in front of you."

Wren pressed her knuckles against her teeth.

Ashgrave took her time. Then she said, "One hour. And Vane — the girl won't make it through the vale. General Odran holds every crossing. There's a checkpoint at Millward Bridge that turns back anyone without occupation papers, and Odran's tasters sample every cup that touches his table. Your fugitives will need to be very good liars." A pause. "Or very good poisoners."

"I know someone," Halric said quietly, "who is both."

* * *

### Wren

He came back up the switchbacks with his wrists already bound in front of him, walking uphill in chains as though it were a style of walking he'd practiced. Which, Wren thought, maybe he had. Maybe every step of his exile had been rehearsal for this one climb.

"You absolute fool," she said, when the riders' torches were a smudge below.

"Yes," he agreed. "It's a family trait of mine — foolery. I passed it on to your mother, she refined it, and now you've inherited the improved strain." He sat down heavily on a root. His face was gray in the ember-light. "An hour. Less, probably. Ashgrave will count the hour precisely because precision is all she has left of her soul."

"Halric—"

"Don't." His voice cracked open, just for a syllable. "Don't waste our hour arguing. Listen instead, because I won't get to say this twice." He looked at her, and for once there was nothing performed in his face, nothing of the charming disgraced keeper, nothing of the liar's easy smile. Just an old man on a cold hillside, spending himself deliberately.

"Millward Bridge will stop you. Odran will test you. But a food-taster with papers gets close to generals, and a general's table is where maps live. You told me once your tongue survived things that should have killed you. Use it. Let the thing in your blood drink the poison for you and let Odran watch you do it — vain men love a wonder. Get the deployment maps. Get through the pass. Get to the heart-tower before the dark finishes chewing through the last ward-lines, because it will be soon now. Days. Maybe hours."

"You're coming with me."

"No." He said it gently. "I'm buying you the road, Wren. That's the trade. That's the first installment." He fumbled at his belt with bound hands and produced, awkwardly, a folded square of oilcloth. Inside was a key — iron, old, shaped like a thorn. "Heart-tower door. I stole it the night I ran, twenty years ago, because even then some part of me knew the ledgers were a door closing on people. Give it back to the tower. Tell it—" His breath caught. "Tell it the keeper is sorry. It will understand. Towers are patient in ways men aren't."

She took the key. Her hands were shaking.

"Maren and Sef and the boy—"

"Will follow you because you're the first person since their draining who made them feel strong instead of spent. Don't argue with me about that either; it's true and you know it." He stood. Down the slope, a horn sounded — Ashgrave, precise to the minute, calling her hour closed. Halric straightened his shoulders like a man putting on a coat he hated. "One more thing. When you get to the tower — and you will, you're your mother's daughter and your mother was the most stubborn woman the order ever produced — the oath will ask you for a life. It will ask politely. It will ask in her voice if it must. Don't answer alone. Promise me that much. Whatever else you do, whatever the law of the old wards says — don't answer it alone."

"Why?" The word came out raw.

"Because I answered alone once," Halric said. "In a records room, with a quill, and it cost nine hundred families everything." The horn sounded again, nearer. He began to walk downhill, backward, watching her, chains swinging. "Debts get paid, Wren. That's the whole of ward-craft. Everything else is just deciding who pays."

Then the dark took him, and the torches closed around him, and Wren stood on the hillside holding an iron key that was warm as a living thing, listening to a traitor name himself clearly for the first time in his life — loudly, so the whole column could hear, so the record could hold it:

"Halric Vane, keeper of the Third Circle, guilty as written!"

And then hoofbeats, fading north, and silence, and above the black ridge the sky stayed empty of stars while somewhere beneath the mountain something vast turned over in its sleep.

Sef emerged from the trees with Maren on his back, awake now, her small drained face turned toward the road.

"Did he go?" she asked.

"He went," Wren said. She pocketed the key. She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, once, efficiently, the way she'd been trained to clear evidence. "And we're going the other way. Millward Bridge. There's a general there who needs a taster."

* * *

### Halric

The wagon they put him in smelled of wet straw and old fear — the same straw, he suspected, that had carried the bond-heirs to the capital's vaults. Ashgrave rode alongside and did not look at him, which was her version of respect.

"You knew she'd take the deal," Halric said through the slats. "You counted on it. An hour's start, the bridge, the taster's gambit — you handed me the whole script, Ashgrave. Why?"

For a long time the commander said nothing. Hooves on frozen mud. Behind them, far off, the mountain breathed its lightless weather.

"Because Doss also said," she finally answered, "that you cried at the trial. And I've wondered for twenty years whether a man who cries like that is guilty or just early." She spurred forward. "Pray I'm right, Vane. For both our sakes, pray I'm right."

Halric leaned his head against the wagon wall and watched Thornfell's black bulk slide past, and beneath his ribs, where the old bond had once lived, something answered the mountain — faint, patient, and waiting for him at the heart-tower, exactly as he had promised it would be.

Three days, he judged. Three days before the tower called its debt due.

He hoped Wren poisoned quickly.
