## 15. The Poisoner's Art

The checkpoint at Greyford Bridge was a wound in the road, and Wren meant to walk through it wearing the enemy's own colors.

She'd spent two days watching from the treeline above the vale, learning the rhythm of the place the way she'd once learned the rhythms of royal kitchens: when the sentries changed, when the officers drank, when the general's kitchen wagon rolled up from the supply train with its brass-bound casks of wine. The invaders — the Ashen Compact, Halric called them, men who'd marched under a banner of a grey tree on white — had made their camp in the ruins of a wool-market town, and like every army since armies began, they had brought their appetites with them.

And every appetite had a door.

"You don't have to do this," Halric said beside her, low. He'd aged ten years in the last week, or maybe she was only now seeing him truly. "There are other passes."

"There aren't. You said so yourself." She checked the papers in her breast pocket one more time: a taster's warrant, inked in Compact script by a forger in the last free town, naming one *Maren Voss*, food-taster, formerly of the Duke of Callow's household. The duke was dead. That was the beauty of dead men — they couldn't contradict you. "A general who doesn't fear poison is a general who trusts his cooks. A general who trusts his cooks is a general I can reach."

"And if he makes you taste first?"

"Then I taste first." She smiled, and hated how easily it came. "I'm very good at not dying."

Halric didn't smile back. "Wren. What you're describing — three days inside that camp, poisoning a man drop by drop while he thanks you for it — that's not the work of ward-smiths. That's the work your mother died trying to bury."

"My mother died because the crown drained her family dry and called it duty." She kept her eyes on the campfires below, scattered across the valley floor like embers shaken from a hearth. "I know exactly what kind of work this is. I've done it for six years. I just used to do it for people who thought they were the good ones."

* * *

They took her at midday, as she'd planned to be taken.

She walked down the pass road alone, hands visible, warrant held high, and let the pickets find her the way a fisherman lets a trout find the hook. A sergeant with a burn-scarred jaw turned the papers over twice, looked at her travel-worn boots, then at her face.

"Taster," he said. "For who?"

"Was for the Duke of Callow. Callow's ash now. I'm looking for wages." She let the exhaustion show, the hunger, the small mercenary flatness of a woman who had survived by being useful. None of it was performance. That was the trick of it — the best lies were true things arranged badly. "I heard General Marrec eats well even on campaign. I hear he pays tasters who keep him eating."

The sergeant's mouth twitched. Word traveled even through occupied country, apparently, that General Marrec had lost two tasters to dysentery and one to a knife fight over cards, and that he'd grown paranoid about his plate without growing any thinner.

They marched her into camp between two soldiers who never learned her name, past granaries burned black and doorways chalked with conscription numbers, past a well where women drew water under a guard's bored eye. She catalogued it all the way she catalogued everything: the number of tents, the state of the horses, the placement of the watch-fires, the fact that the powder wagons sat too close to the eastern palisade and no one had corrected it. Six years of tasting had taught her that every organization was a body, and every body had its habits, its weaknesses, the places where it forgot to guard itself.

General Marrec received her in a requisitioned farmhouse, at a table still scarred with a wool-merchant's knife-marks. He was younger than she expected, broad and ruddy, with the easy confidence of a man who had won every battle he'd ever been handed. He looked at her warrant, then at her hands.

"A taster's hands," he said. "Scarred knuckles. You've handled reagents."

"Copper salts, mostly. Detection work." True. All of it true. "Your last man wasn't careful with his wash-water. It's how I'd have killed him, if I were the killing sort."

Something flickered behind his eyes — respect, or its cousin. "You're hired. You eat before me, you drink before me, and if I die, my quartermaster hangs you from the bridge. Understood?"

"Understood." She bowed her head, the picture of a woman accepting terms. *If you die,* she thought, *it will take three days, and it will look like bad luck, and no one will hang for it but the wine.*

* * *

The first day, she did nothing at all.

That was the discipline no one taught and every master knew: the first day is for watching. She tasted his breakfast — smoked river fish, bread, a honey-cake she pronounced clean after a ritual of spoon-and-silver that she performed with genuine artistry, because the theater mattered as much as the chemistry. She watched the kitchen staff. She learned which cook watered the stock, which scullery boy stole figs, which serving woman lingered near the general's chair a moment too long with an expression Wren recognized from mirrors: someone in love, or afraid, or both.

And she memorized the map.

It hung in the corner of the farmhouse office, weighted at the corners with candlesticks, and Marrec consulted it each evening with his captains while drinking the dark red wine of the southern valleys. Wren stood at her post by the sideboard, refilling cups, invisible the way servants are invisible, and read it the way she read faces. Two thousand foot along the river. Cavalry bivouacked at the mill. Supply line running back through Greyford to the coast — thin, stretched, guarded by a single company at the bridge. And here, circled in red ink with a question mark beside it, the mountain pass where a certain ember was believed to be moving.

Someone in the Compact command knew about the ember. Someone had told Marrec what to hunt.

She poured his wine that night with steady hands and a servant's lowered gaze, and thought: *I could put it all in tonight.* The concentrated extract slept in a hollow seam of her boot heel, bitter aloes masked with oil of bitter almond — no, better, she'd settled on something subtler, a compound of her own devising that tasted of nothing at all and worked like slow rot. Three days of small doses. By the fourth morning, a general in his prime would complain of a summer fever. By the fifth, his captains would be arguing about succession instead of deployment. An army is a body; cut out its head and the body stumbles.

But not tonight. Tonight she poured clean wine, and tasted it first, and met Marrec's eyes over the rim of her cup as she swallowed, and saw him decide to trust her.

That was the worst part. Not the poison. The trust.

* * *

The second day, the child changed everything.

She'd gone at dawn to draw water for the kitchen — tasters drew their own water, always, a habit that had saved her life twice — and found a boy of perhaps eight crouched behind the rain barrel, ward-scarred. She'd seen the marks before, in the vaults beneath the capital: the branching silver-white lines of a body used as a conduit until the conduits burst. A bond-heir. Drained, half-grown, discarded, and now hauling water for the army that had helped break the wards that made him possible.

He flinched from her. Of course he did. Everyone with those scars flinched from everyone.

Wren crouched, set down the buckets, and took his wrist the way she'd once taken the innkeeper's daughter's hand on the night the stars went out — without thinking, without permission, on pure reflex. Warmth moved through her palm. She felt it go, felt the old grammar of the wards speaking through her skin, knitting what had been burst. The boy stared at his own arm as the silver lines faded to pale threads.

"It doesn't hurt anymore," he whispered.

"Wren!" A cook's voice from the doorway. She stood, released him, became Maren Voss again in a single breath. But the boy was looking at her with an expression she'd carry out of this camp whether she wanted to or not.

That night she dosed the wine.

One measure, no more, stirred in with a rod of her little finger while Marrec argued with his cavalry captain about the mill. Clean, tasteless, patient. Then she lifted the cup, sipped first, met his eyes, and set it at his elbow.

"To the general's health," she said, because that was what tasters said, and because cruelty sometimes wore courtesy best.

He drank deep. He thanked her. He turned back to his map, and she stood at the sideboard reading it again, adding the day's changes to the map growing in her head: the cavalry shifting east, a new battery of ladders being built, the red circle around the pass redrawn larger. Tomorrow night, the second dose. The night after, the third, and by then she'd need to be gone, walking out through Greyford with a taster's dismissal and a head full of the Compact's whole order of battle.

She should have felt triumphant. Instead she stood in the lamplight holding a wine jug and felt the old self-loathing rise like bile: *this is what you are, this is what six years made you, a woman who poisons men who thank her.*

Then she thought of the boy's arm, whole and quiet under his sleeve. And she thought: *good.* Let it be ugly. Ugly things done cleanly were still done, and the vale full of burned granaries and chalked doorways did not care about the cleanliness of her soul.

* * *

The third night, Marrec talked to her.

Perhaps it was the wine beginning its slow work — a flush, a looseness, a general feeling among his captains that the old man was off his edge. Perhaps it was simply that she was the last person awake in the farmhouse and pouring. Either way, near midnight, with the map weighted down before him and his cup half empty, he waved her closer.

"You've got smith-blood about you," he said, conversational, terrible. "Did you know? We can smell it. The thing under Thornfell marks its own, they say. Every bond-heir we've flushed out of these mountains has that same look. Like a lamp behind the face."

Wren kept her hands perfectly still on the jug. "I'm a cook's daughter from Callow, sir."

"Mm." He smiled, and drank again, and she watched the dose do its quiet arithmetic behind his eyes. "Doesn't matter. In four days we move on the pass anyway. Whatever's carrying that ember up my mountain will be in a bag by week's end." He tapped the red circle. "Four days. Enjoy the wages, taster."

*Four days.* Her third dose wouldn't fell him in time to stop the march — she'd known that going in, had accepted it; the sickness was to sow doubt and delay the *next* offensive, to make his captains whisper. But the date — the date was worth more than his life. Halric needed it. The grove needed it. Every freed bond-heir waiting at the waystones needed it.

She finished pouring. She wished him good sleep. She walked out into the cold with the deployment burning in her memory — troop counts, timetables, the thin company at Greyford Bridge, the exact hour the column would move — and she did not look back at the farmhouse where the general sat trusting his wine.

At the palisade gate, the boy with the healed arm was waiting by the water barrels. He pressed something into her hand as she passed: a stub of chalk, stolen, warm from his pocket.

"For the road," he whispered. "In case you get lost."

She almost told him the truth. Almost told him that some nights, lost was the only honest direction. Instead she closed her fist around the chalk like it was an oath, and walked out through Greyford Bridge under the starless sky, past the thin company of guards who never looked twice at a taster's dismissed shoulders, and up into the dark toward the waystone grove where Halric waited with the others.

Behind her, in a requisitioned farmhouse, General Marrec slept the sleep of the trusted, and the poison began its third night's work in his blood — and far below the roots of Thornfell, something older than either of them turned over in its long bed, and began, patiently, to count the stars that were left.
