## 16. Stars in Her Hands

The burning started in her left hand on the third night after the grove, and by morning it had learned her name.

Wren woke with her fingers clenched around a fistful of bedroll she didn't remember grabbing, and the ember — the splinters of it lodged somewhere behind her sternum since her mother's workshop, since the vault, since the grove taught her what they were — pulsed like a second heart. Not pain, exactly. Not yet. More like a hand pressed flat against the inside of her skin, patient, asking.

*Soon*, it said, in the grammar the waystones had taught her. *Soon, or I take what I need.*

She sat up in the dark of the ruined shepherd's hut and held her hand toward the cold hearth. Light leaked between her fingers. Not firelight — starlight, the same white-silver the grove had shown her in shared memory, the color the sky had been before the devouring dark drank it. Her veins glowed faintly at the wrist, blue-white threads under skin, and when she flexed her hand the light moved with the blood.

"Beautiful," she said aloud, because no one was awake to hear her, "and completely unacceptable."

The ember had been patient for twenty years, folded small inside a sleeping child, hidden by a mother who'd split it like a loaf and tucked the pieces into her unborn daughter's blood. Wren had forgiven her mother in the grove — mostly, mostly — but forgiveness didn't change the arithmetic. The fragments were waking. The grove's oldest stone had said it plainly, in that voice like wind through hollow reeds: *A vessel is not a hearth. Carry it past the turning of the moon, and it will burn the vessel to carry itself.*

The turning of the moon was eleven days away. The heart-tower at Thornfell was nine days' hard ride.

She had four days of margin, if the ember was feeling generous.

Wren got up, dressed, and went outside to see whether the stars were still gone.

They were. The sky over the eastern peaks was a clean black nothing, a hole cut in the world, and the mountains stood against it like teeth. Somewhere down in that black, the thing beneath Thornfell was eating the dark itself, growing fat on the absence, wearing the shapes of the dead — Halric had come back from his scouting run with his hands shaking and wouldn't say more than that, wouldn't say anything, just *we have to move faster*, said it in a voice she'd only heard once before, from a captain dying in a retreat with the smith-blood on his lips.

She found Halric at the edge of the camp, awake, which she'd expected. He slept in pieces now, an hour here, an hour there, like a man rationing himself.

"You're glowing," he said, without turning around.

"I'm aware."

"From the wrist up. Like you dipped your arm in the winter sky." He did turn, then, and the look on his face was the one she hated most — the ward-keeper's look, the one that measured and calculated and arrived at numbers he didn't want to say out loud. "How bad?"

"Four days of margin. Nine days of road."

Halric was quiet for a moment. He was a big man gone lean on the road, gray at the temples, with the permanent squint of someone who'd spent his life reading light that other people couldn't see. Twenty years ago he'd signed ledgers that drained her family to feed the crown's wards, and he'd confessed it in the snow of a high pass with the honesty of a man setting down a weight he could no longer carry, and she had hated him for three days and then, somewhere on the road through the occupied vale, without noticing the exact moment, stopped.

"We make the tower in seven," he said. "We cut the rest days. We push the bond-heirs."

"They're children, Halric. Half of them can barely walk."

"They're bond-heirs. They were made for this road before they were born." He said it flatly, and then heard himself, and closed his eyes. "That was the crown's argument. I've been listening to it so long it's started using my voice."

Wren held up her glowing hand between them. "Then we don't tell them about the four days. We tell them seven, and we walk, and if the ember decides to cook me on day six, that's between me and it."

"That's not —"

"That's exactly the bargain. You taught me the grammar, Halric. A willing gift, freely given. Nobody's giving anything less willingly than me." She closed her fist and the light went out, and the darkness came back in a rush, and in it his face was very close and very tired and very afraid, and not, she thought, only for the mission.

"Come inside," she said. "You're shivering."

"I'm always shivering."

"Come inside anyway."

* * *

They made eight miles the first day and ten the second, down out of the high shepherd country and into the long gray shoulder of the Thornfell approach, where the road ran through country the invaders had stripped and moved on from. Burned waystones. Cut orchards. The bond-heirs walked in a ragged line with their hoods up, nine of them, the youngest seven years old, and every living stone they passed leaned toward Wren like flowers toward a window.

By the second evening she couldn't hide it anymore. The light had climbed past her elbow. It showed through her sleeve in the dark, a faint silver line from wrist to mid-forearm, branching, like frost growing in reverse, like a river system seen from very high up. The little one, Sef, who'd lost his whole village and followed her the way lost things follow warmth, walked beside her for an hour staring at it before he worked up the nerve.

"Does it hurt?"

"Not yet."

"My mam glowed like that. At the end. When the crown's men took too much." He said it with a child's terrible plainness. "She got cold after. All the time, cold. Then she didn't get anything anymore."

Wren stopped walking. The line of bond-heirs strung out behind her, and the wind came down off the peaks carrying the smell of snow and old ash, and she knelt in the road so she was at his eye level.

"I'm not going to get cold, Sef. I'm going to get *there*." She pointed east, to where the heart-tower's broken crown showed above the ridge line, a black comb against the starless dark. "And when I get there, I'm going to give this light back to the sky. All of it. And then the stars come home, and nobody's mother gets cold ever again. That's the whole oath. That's all it is."

"That's a lot," Sef said.

"Yes," Wren said. "It's a lot."

* * *

The grove found them on the fourth night.

Not a grove, properly — a single waystone in a hollow above the road, older than the others, its carved spiral worn nearly smooth, but alive in the way that made the air around it feel like the moment before rain. The bond-heirs felt it first and drifted toward it the way they always did, and Wren followed, and the stone spoke before she was within ten paces.

*Vessel.*

"I have a name," Wren said.

*Wren Sandoval, of the line of Beatrix.* The stone's voice was in the ground and the wind and the backs of her teeth. *Vessel of the split ember. Daughter of the one who hid it. We knew your mother's mother, and hers before her. We have watched your line carry fire for nine generations, and we tell you now what we told none of them, because none of them had a choice, and you do.*

Halric had come up beside her. She felt him go still.

*The fragments wake,* the stone said. *They know the tower now. They pull toward it, and they pull through you. In four days they will begin to take heat from your blood to feed their journey. In six they will take more than heat. You were not made to be a hearth, Wren Sandoval. You were made to be a door. And a door that carries its own fire through winter burns from the inside.*

"Then I walk faster," Wren said.

*You cannot outrun arithmetic.* A pause, and the wind in the hollow went soft, almost gentle. *But arithmetic is the old oath's grammar. You were taught in the grove that the old grammar is not the only grammar. Your mother split the ember because she refused the old grammar. You freed the drained ones because you refused it. When you reach the tower, you will refuse it again. But between here and there, vessel, you must survive being what you are — and you cannot do that alone.*

"I'm not alone," Wren said. "I have nine bond-heirs and a disgraced ward-keeper."

The stone was silent a moment. When it spoke again, she could have sworn it sounded amused. *Yes. Him. We remember Halric Vane too. He was kinder to the stones than to himself. Ask him what he carries, vessel. You have both been walking side by side for a hundred miles, each waiting for the other to speak first. We are old, and we are tired of waiting.*

The presence withdrew like a tide going out, and the hollow was just a hollow, and the wind was just wind, and Halric was standing three feet away looking like a man who'd been caught at something.

"Well," he said.

"Well," Wren said.

* * *

They took the last watch together, because they always did now, neither of them having ever actually arranged it. The bond-heirs slept in a heap under the waystone's lean, breathing in the slow unison that living stone taught sleeping children. The black sky pressed down. Wren's arm glowed to the shoulder now, and she'd stopped hiding it, because hiding it took energy she was no longer willing to spend.

"Nine generations," Halric said, into the dark. "Your family. Carrying fire. And the crown ground them down to embers to light its hallways, and I signed the ledgers that did it, and the whole time the stones *knew*." He shook his head. "They knew and they waited. They're patient in a way that would break most people."

"They're not patient. They're *long*." Wren pulled her knees up. "There's a difference. Patient people wait because they hope. Long things wait because a hundred years is a paragraph to them." She looked at him. "The stone said you were kinder to the stones than to yourself."

Halric was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. Then he laughed, once, without much humor in it.

"Twenty years ago I found the siphon-records. I did the arithmetic — how much the crown was taking, how long your family's line could survive it. I had a daughter, Wren. Maren. Eleven years old. The crown's men had already marked the Vane blood as a secondary bond, thin but usable, and I understood, sitting there with those ledgers, that the only question was whether I signed and stayed useful, or refused and got disappeared, and either way Maren ended up in a vault." He turned his hands over, looked at them like they belonged to a stranger. "So I signed. And I told myself I'd work from inside. And I falsified the inspection reports for six years, small lies, careful lies, and they bought Maren twenty years of being nobody, and then I got caught anyway, and got exiled anyway, and Maren grew up thinking her father was a forger and a coward, and the worst part — the part I've never said to anyone — is that she was *right*. Both things. A forger and a coward. And also the only reason she was alive to be right."

"You told me this," Wren said. "In the pass."

"I told you the shape of it. Not the bottom." He looked at her then, and the starless dark didn't matter; his eyes had their own weather. "The bottom is this. When I rode toward Thornfell the night the towers fell, I told myself it was guilt. Debt. A coward's arithmetic — one old man's life against a family's ledger. And then I found you, and you poisoned my cup to test me, and you drank half of it yourself first because you couldn't help being a taster even when you were testing, and I thought — *oh*. *Oh, no.* It stopped being arithmetic right there. It's been something else ever since, and I've been too much of a coward to name it, because naming it on a road where you're carrying a fire that's eating you felt like — like asking for something while the house burns."

The wind moved through the hollow. Under the stone, one of the bond-heirs murmured in her sleep.

"Say it anyway," Wren said. Her voice came out lower than she intended. "The house is always burning. That's the whole century. Say it anyway."

"I love you." He said it simply, the way you say a thing you've carried so far it's worn smooth. "Not the way the ledgers love. Not duty. Not debt. I watched you kneel in the road for a seven-year-old and promise him the stars, and I watched you drink your own poison, and I thought, *this is the bravest person I have ever met, and I am going to lose her in six days, and I will have said nothing.*" His jaw worked. "I'm forty-nine years old, Wren. I've been a liar, a coward, a forger, and a keeper. I'd like to be honest once, before the end, about the one true thing."

Wren found that her hand — the glowing one — had crossed the space between them and was resting on his, and she had no memory of moving it. The light lay across his knuckles like spilled milk.

"You're not going to lose me," she said. "I refuse the arithmetic. I've been refusing it since the grove. The old oath wants one life, freely given, and it's going to learn what my mother learned: I don't pay alone, and I don't pay the way I'm told." She turned her hand over, took his. "But since you were honest — Halric. I love you too. I have since the vale, since the child with the ward-scar, since I watched you bargain away your own name to buy us a road. I spent my whole life tasting for a crown that was draining my family, and I told myself I felt nothing, and then you came along with your forged ledgers and your shaking hands and you *felt everything*, all of it, all the time, and it was the most reckless thing I'd ever seen." She laughed, and it cracked a little in the middle. "You reckless old man. You made me want things."

"Terrible of me."

"The worst."

They sat like that, hand in hand, while the black sky ground slowly east, and the ember in her chest pulsed its patient *soon*, and for a little while neither of them did any arithmetic at all.

* * *

She woke before dawn to the light at her collarbone and a sound she hadn't heard in weeks: a star.

One star, low in the east, above the broken crown of the heart-tower. Faint. Flickering. But there — a single point of silver in the clean black nothing, and as she watched, holding her breath in the freezing dark, it pulsed.

Once. Twice.

In the same rhythm as the ember under her sternum.

Wren stood up so fast she woke Halric, and when he saw her face he was on his feet before she said a word, and she pointed east, and they stood together in the gray cold watching the sky answer the fire in her blood, call and response, call and response, like two voices finding the same song.

"It knows where we are," Wren whispered. "Or it knows where *it's* going. Halric — the ember isn't waiting for the tower. It's *pulling*."

Down in the hollow, all nine bond-heirs were sitting up, awake, their eyes reflecting a light that wasn't in the sky yet — and wouldn't be, unless Wren Sandoval got her burning body to the heart-tower before the fire finished learning her name.
