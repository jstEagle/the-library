## 17. The Oath Anew

**WREN**

The heart-tower of Thornfell had been beautiful once. She could tell from the bones of it — the way the fallen stones still curved toward a center that no longer existed, like mourners around an empty grave. Now it was just a wound in the mountain's shoulder, ringed by rubble, open to a sky that stayed black no matter how long they waited beneath it.

Wren climbed the last stretch of broken stair with Halric's hand at her elbow and the ember burning in her chest like a swallowed coal.

It had been three days since the grove warned her. Three days since the waystones had shown her her mother's face, her mother's hands, her mother's terrible arithmetic — *split the ember, hide it in the child, let the blood carry what the vaults would otherwise drain.* Three days since she'd stopped pretending the heat under her skin was fever.

She could feel it now in everything. In the way frost melted where her bare fingers touched stone. In the way the freed bond-heirs behind them flinched when she passed, not from fear but from recognition — she was becoming something their blood remembered.

"You're limping," Halric said.

"I'm climbing."

"You're limping while climbing." His grip tightened. "Sit when we reach the top. Promise me one small thing today."

She almost laughed. "You've stopped asking for small things lately. You used to ask for the whole world and pretend it was nothing."

"Maybe I'm learning economy in my old age."

The stair ended. The tower's heart opened before them — a circular chamber half-roofed with sky, its floor a mosaic of silver veins that had once carried light up through the mountain's every ward. At its center stood the oath-stone: a pillar of black glass taller than two men, cracked down the middle, dark as a closed eye.

The freed bond-heirs arranged themselves along the chamber's edge without being told. Maren, the granary-keeper's son who'd held the vault door. Old Sef, whose hands shook unless he touched living wood. The twins from the occupied vale, still wearing the ash-gray smocks the invaders had issued them. Eleven survivors of the crown's long theft, drawn here by the same pull that dragged Wren's feet forward — the ember in her blood calling to the stone like a voice calling down a well.

Wren stepped onto the mosaic floor.

The silver veins lit.

Not all at once — a slow ripple, spreading outward from her boots, guttering, catching, spreading again, like fire finding old dry wick. She heard the bond-heirs gasp. She felt it too: the tower waking beneath her, groaning back into some dim awareness after twenty years of starvation, and reaching for her the way a starving man reaches for bread.

*There you are,* the stone seemed to say. *There you are, at last.*

"It knows me," she whispered.

"It's known you your whole life," Halric said quietly. "That was always the problem."

She turned to look at him. He stood at the mosaic's edge, deliberately outside the light, his weathered face unreadable in the way it went unreadable when he was carrying something heavy and had decided she wasn't ready for the weight.

"Halric. What aren't you telling me?"

He didn't answer. And then the stone did.

* * *

**HALRIC**

The oath-stone spoke in a voice like a bell rung underwater, and Halric had heard it before — twenty years ago, in this same chamber, when the tower still lived and he'd been a young ward-keeper with clean hands and a clean conscience, standing where Wren stood now.

*THE OATH ASKS,* the stone said. *A LIFE WILLING. A LIFE GIVEN. FREELY OFFERED AND NOT TAKEN. BOUND UNTIL THE STARS RETURN OR THE GIVER IS SPENT.*

The words fell into the ruined chamber and lay there.

Wren went very still. "Bound," she repeated. "What does bound mean?"

Halric watched her do the arithmetic. He watched her arrive at it faster than he had at nineteen, because she was sharper than he'd ever been, because she'd spent years learning poisons and he'd spent those same years learning ledgers, and both were just ways of reading what killed you.

"Permanently," he said, so she wouldn't have to say it first. "It means permanently."

"No." Her voice came out flat. "No, there's another way. There has to be — my mother split the ember, she found workarounds, the whole grove taught me the grammar of the oath, there are eleven of us here—"

"There are eleven of you here," Halric agreed, "and the stone is looking at one."

Because it was. He could feel it — the tower's attention gathered on Wren like sunlight through a lens, all that starving hunger narrowed to a single point. The smith-blood. The vessel. The child the ember had ridden inside for twenty-six years, waiting for the day the wards failed and someone had to pay the old price.

"The original oath," he said, and hated how steady his voice sounded, "was sworn by Beatrix herself. One life, freely given. That's why the order bore her name — not because she founded it, but because she paid for it. Every ward on this mountain has been running on her gift for four hundred years. And when the crown started siphoning—" his jaw tightened— "when we started draining the bonded families instead of letting the oath rest, we weren't extending her sacrifice. We were counterfeiting it. You can't counterfeit a willing life. You can only borrow against it. And the debt came due the night the stars went out."

"So I die." Wren said it simply, the way she reported poison in a stew. "I stand on the stone, I give myself, the wards relight, and everyone else lives. That's the trade."

"That's the trade."

"And you brought me here to make it."

The bond-heirs had gone silent along the walls. Maren's hand had found Sef's shoulder. Nobody breathed.

Halric stepped forward, out of the shadow at the mosaic's edge, into the silver light — and the light flinched away from him, recognizing no claim in his blood, only an old man's borrowed guilt.

"No," he said. "I brought you here to stop you from making it alone."

* * *

**WREN**

She saw it in his face a half-second before he moved — the particular stillness of a man who has finished deciding — and she'd spent enough years around poisoners and liars to know that stillness when she saw it.

"Halric—"

"A life willing," he said, striding across the mosaic, his boots throwing ripples of light. "A life given. Freely offered and not taken. It doesn't say whose life, Wren. It never said whose life. Beatrix chose. My mother's generation assumed the choice would always fall to the strongest vessel, because that was tidy, and ward-smiths love tidy. But the stone doesn't ask for the strongest vessel. It asks for a willing heart." He stopped an arm's length from her, close enough that she could see his eyes were wet and he was pretending they weren't. "And mine has been willing for twenty years. Since the ledgers. Since I signed my name under theirs and knew what the ink cost. I have been walking toward this stone my entire exile. You just happened to be walking the same road."

"No." The word tore out of her. "No, you don't get to — you don't get to make this noble, you stubborn, impossible old man—"

"Wren."

"—you don't get to spend yourself like coin to settle a debt that isn't yours, I carried the ember, I *chose* to carry it, every mile of this cursed mountain—"

"Wren." His hands came up and framed her face, and his palms were warm and rough and shaking. "Look at me. Look at me and tell me you want to die, and I'll step back. Tell me the truth, taster. You're better at truth than anyone I've ever known."

And that was the cruelty of it. Because she looked at him — at the gray in his beard and the scar on his brow and the twenty years of shame he'd carried like a second spine — and the truth rose in her throat and refused to be spoken.

She didn't want to die. She wanted, for the first time since the stars went out, to *live*. To see the lowlands in spring. To teach. To sit across a table from this man for fifty years and argue about everything and touch his hand in the quiet parts. The grove had shown her her mother's ghost and she had forgiven her; she wanted years in which forgiveness could be more than memory.

But she looked at Halric offering himself, and the thought of the tower drinking him down — of the silver veins lighting with *his* life instead of hers, of the mountain saved and the man gone — was a poison she could taste on the back of her tongue, bitter as kingsfoil, and her whole body rejected it.

"Duty asks for one life," she whispered. "That's what the stone says. That's what the old law says."

"Yes."

"But I'm done paying what duty asks." She covered his hands with hers. "You taught me that on the pass road, when you drank the cup knowing what was in it. You said trust is built from mutual damage. So here's my damage, Halric Vane, laid out plain: I refuse. I refuse the trade. I refuse to buy the sky with you, and I refuse to buy it alone, and if the stone wants a willing life it can learn — as I learned in the grove, as my mother learned too late — that oaths are grown, not imposed. They can be *woven*."

The stone shuddered.

Halric frowned. "Woven?"

Behind them, Old Sef made a sound — a low, wondering hum — and when Wren glanced over, the old man's trembling hands had gone still against the wall, and the silver veins beneath his palms were glowing faintly gold.

"She's right," Sef breathed. "Gods above and below. She's right. Can't you feel it? The tower isn't hungry anymore. It's *listening*."

* * *

**HALRIC**

Later — in whatever time remained, in the hours before the devouring dark came up the mountain's roots to meet them — Halric would try to describe what happened next, and fail, and settle for the shape of it.

The oath-stone spoke again, but slower now, the bell-voice unclenching, curious.

*A LIFE WILLING,* it said. *IS THE LAW.*

"One law among many," Wren answered, and her voice rang strange in the ruined chamber, doubled somehow, as if the ember in her blood had found its own tongue. "You were written by people who only knew one kind of giving. One person, alone, forever. But I've carried eleven other hearts in these veins since the vaults. I've carried a mother's choice and a keeper's confession and a grove's memory. I am not one life, stone. I am a *weave*."

She turned to the bond-heirs along the wall.

"You don't have to," she told them. "Hear me — nobody has to. This is the one thing the crown never understood, the thing they built their whole thieving ledger on top of forgetting: a gift taken isn't a gift. If any of you walk out of this tower tonight, I will hold no grudge until my dying day."

Maren stepped off the wall first. Then Sef. Then the twins, hand in hand, and the others after them, until all eleven stood in the silver light, and the light rose to meet them like water rising to a shore.

"We were drained unwilling," Maren said simply. "Let us fill it willing. Let the crown's ledger balance itself wrong-side up."

Halric stood apart, watching Wren at the center of them — and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that he had misread everything. He had thought himself the sacrifice, the redemption, the coward's arc bending toward courage at the last. But she hadn't brought him here to be saved or to save. She'd brought him here because a weave needs a thread that remembers the old pattern, and he was the only soul alive who had read the original oath in Beatrix's own hand.

"Ward-keeper," Wren called to him, and there was a smile in it, fierce and bright as the light climbing her arms. "Your exile ends tonight. Take your place in the circle. We're going to write a new oath — and you're going to help me word it, because your grammar is better than mine."

He laughed. It came out wet and cracked and it was the truest sound he'd made in twenty years.

He crossed the mosaic and took her hand.

* * *

**WREN**

They spoke the oath together, and it was not the old oath.

The old oath had been a wall: one life set against the dark, alone, permanent, a candle burned to the socket. What they made in the ruined heart-tower was a web — eleven voices and a keeper's confession and a taster's refusal braided into the stone, each life offered freely and none of them surrendered, each heart bound to the others rather than to the tower alone. *We give together,* Wren said, and the ember in her chest flared white. *We hold together. We return together, or not at all.*

The stone drank it in. The cracks in the black glass began to glow.

And far below them, in the mountain's roots, something vast and starless felt the change and began to rise.

The floor trembled. Dust sifted from the broken roof. Through the gap in the tower's crown, the starless sky seemed to lean closer, and Wren felt the devouring dark pressing up against the mountain's bones like a tide against a door — and the door was them, now, and the door was opening.

"Halric." She gripped his hand hard. "Whatever comes up those stairs—"

"Together," he said. "Or not at all."

The heart-tower blazed like a second dawn — and the dark came anyway.
