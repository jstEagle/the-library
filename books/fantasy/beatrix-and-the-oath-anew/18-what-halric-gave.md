## 18. What Halric Gave

The heart-tower had no door anymore. The collapse had taken the archway and half the eastern face, and what remained was a broken tooth of black stone rising out of the mountain's shoulder, humming with a frequency Halric felt in his back teeth, in the old scar over his ribs, in the place behind his eyes where twenty years of forged ledgers lived.

He climbed anyway.

The steps were still there under the rubble — he could feel them the way a ward-keeper feels anything of the old order, by the ache they left in him. Each one cost more than the last. His legs had gone wooden somewhere below the treeline, and his hands were shaking, and he kept thinking, absurdly, of the ink stains that used to live in the creases of his fingers. Twenty years of signing things. He had signed away bonded families with a steady hand and good penmanship, and the crown had called it administration, and he had called it survival, and his daughter had grown up warm and fed on the strength of other people's blood.

Now the bill had come due, and the tower was waiting to collect.

*One life,* the oath demanded. *Willing. Freely given. Permanently bound.*

He knew the grammar better than anyone alive. He had copied it out a hundred times in a forger's careful hand.

At the top, the wind was a living thing. It came off the starless dark in long cold shoves, trying to peel him off the stone, and beyond the tower's broken rim the sky was wrong in a way no ledger could describe. No stars. No moon. Just a smooth black nothing pressing down on Thornfell like a lid, and beneath it, far below in the mountain's roots, something turning over in its sleep. He could feel it waking the way you feel a fever coming — a heat in the stone, a wrongness in the hum.

And below him, small against the scree, Wren Sandoval climbing toward him with the ember burning through her veins like a second heartbeat.

"No," she had said at the grove, when the waystones first showed them the terms. Not shouting. That was the terrible part. She had said it very quietly, the way she said everything that mattered, and then she had looked at him with those taster's eyes that weighed every substance set before them, and she had said, "Find another way."

There wasn't another way. There was only this: the old law, or the devouring thing, or the two of them dying stupidly together while the mountain's roots opened and the shapes of the dead came walking up out of the dark wearing faces he had failed.

He reached the summit platform and stood in the ruin of the binding chamber. The great anchor-stone was still there, cracked down its center but whole enough, veined with channels where the old oath had once run silver-bright. He knelt beside it and pressed his palm flat, and the stone read him the way a lock reads a key — slowly, suspiciously, finding the ward-keeper's training still lodged in him like an old coin in a mattress.

It would take him. He had known it would. A disgraced keeper was still a keeper; the oath did not care about ledgers, only about willingness, and God help him, he had never been more willing to do anything in his life.

Footsteps on the stairs. Fast. Furious.

"You climbed ahead of me." Wren pulled herself over the broken rim, hair whipping loose, and there was light coming off her skin now — faint, gold-white, leaking out from under her fingernails, from the soft hollow of her throat. The ember fragments, burning through her. She had maybe a day left before they consumed her entirely, and she had spent half of it climbing after him. "You absolute — you *knew* I'd follow."

"I was counting on it." His voice came out steadier than he felt. "You always were your mother's daughter. She never let me finish a farewell either."

"Don't." The word cracked in the middle. "Don't do that. Don't make this tidy. Don't stand there with your speech already written."

"It's not a speech." He turned to face her fully, and let her see all of it — the fear, the shame, the strange calm underneath, which surprised him most of all. "It's instructions. You'll want instructions. You're a taster; you follow procedure."

"Halric—"

"Listen to me." He took her hands. They were burning hot, and trembling, and he held them the way you hold something you are not allowed to keep. "When I bind, the tower will hold one night. One night of true light over Thornfell. The thing below will be pressed back down, not destroyed — do you understand? Pressed. It will spend that night learning the shape of the new oath, the way any enemy studies a wall looking for the loose stone."

"Then we find another way during the night—"

"There is no other way yet. But there will be." He squeezed her hands. "Because you're going to refuse. That's the instruction, Wren. The whole of it. When the tower asks for more than I can give — and it will, near dawn, when the thing rises hard against me — you must refuse the old law. Refuse it out loud. Refuse it with the ember in your blood and every bond-heir you freed standing behind you. The oath is old, but oaths are only grammar, and grammar can be rewritten by anyone stubborn enough to try."

"That's not how it works—"

"It's exactly how it works. I falsified records for the crown for six years. I know precisely how much of the sacred order is invention." He smiled, and meant it. "Your mother knew too. Why do you think she hid the ember inside you instead of giving herself to the tower? She was already rewriting it. She just ran out of time. You won't. You have the one thing she didn't — you have people. The freed heirs. The grove. The vale full of hearts willing to give something smaller than their lives."

Wren was crying without seeming to know it, tears cutting bright tracks through the dust on her face, and the light under her skin pulsed with each breath like a tide.

"And if I fail?" she whispered.

"Then I will have bought you one night to try, and one night is more than the crown ever gave anyone." He lifted her hands, pressed his lips to her knuckles — the gesture of a vassal, of a keeper to a smith, of a man to the daughter of the woman he had loved badly and served worse. "I spent twenty years being a coward carefully. Methodically. With excellent penmanship. Let me be brave once, badly, in a hurry. It balances nothing. But it's mine."

Below them, the mountain exhaled. The hum deepened into something with intent behind it, and along the horizon the starless dark began, very slightly, to lean inward — like a listener, like a hunger.

Halric stepped back onto the anchor-stone.

The channels lit at his heels. Silver ran up through the cracks, found his boots, climbed. It didn't hurt, at first. It felt like being read — every year, every lie, every forged signature laid open in the light and weighed, and the oath finding, beneath all of it, the one clean thread: he had come here freely, knowing the price, carrying no one's orders but his own.

"Wren." The light was at his knees now, and his voice was starting to echo strangely, as if the tower were already speaking through him. "The wine cellar at Vane Hold. Third row, behind the false bottles. Your mother's letters — all of them, even the ones about me. Read them. Forgive her out loud; she'll hear it somehow, they always do. And the girl at the inn — Maren — her mother owed me a debt, tell her it's paid. And Wren—"

"Halric—"

"—the stew poisoner in chapter one of my life was me, all along, and I am so sorry, and also the antidote is in my left saddlebag, you've been dosed since the grove, I couldn't risk the ember flaring early—"

She laughed. It was a horrible, wonderful sound, grief and fury tangled together, and she was reaching for him as the light took his chest.

"*Permanently bound,*" she said. "You said permanently. That means I can't even—"

"It means," and here his voice was mostly the tower's now, vast and silver and gentle, "that I will be in every wardstone in Thornfell until the mountains wear down. Every waystone that speaks your name will be speaking with my voice in it somewhere. You will never again walk these roads unaccompanied." The light reached his shoulders. His eyes were already stars. "I told you once I'd get you home safe. I keep my contracts. Even the forged ones."

Then he turned to face the dark, and the tower blazed, and one night of light fell across Thornfell like a struck bell made visible — every peak gilded, every pass lit, every waystone singing — and far below, the devouring thing slammed itself against the new-sealed roots and *screamed*, and the mountain held.

Wren stood alone on the summit with the wind and the light and the shape of him everywhere in the stone, and she pressed both burning hands against the anchor-stone, and through the hum she heard him, faint and clear and infuriatingly calm:

*Dawn is coming, taster. Refuse the old law. Rewrite the grammar. And Wren — don't you dare pay alone.*

Far below, in the roots of the world, the thing beneath Thornfell gathered itself for the second assault — and in the east, where dawn should have been, the darkness leaned closer, and closer, and did not break.
