## 20. Rekindling

**WREN**

The tower's heart was a wound in the world.

Wren stood at its center, on the cracked dais where her mother had once stood, and felt the ember fragments burning through her veins like swallowed stars. The grove had warned her. *Carry it much longer and it will carry you instead.* She could feel the truth of that now, a heat climbing her arms, writing itself into her blood — the old oath trying to take shape inside her, to make of her a single pillar, a single point of failure.

One life, freely given. That was the law. Halric was already at the eastern anchor-stone, his hand pressed flat against it, already half-lit from within. He had not asked her permission. He had simply begun.

"Halric." Her voice came out ragged. "Wait."

"There's no time to wait." He didn't turn around. The light was climbing his throat now, silver as starlight, and beneath it she could see the exhaustion of twenty years finally leaving him — or being taken. "The thing is rising. You felt it in the roots. Every minute we spend arguing is a minute it spends remembering how to climb."

"Then don't do this alone."

He turned then, and his face — lined, scarred, guilty for two decades over ledgers he'd been forced to sign — wore something she had never seen on it before. Peace.

"Wren. I spent my whole life paying other people's debts with other people's coin. Forged records. False oaths. I let them drain your mother dry and signed my name to it because they held my daughter's life over me." His voice cracked but held. "This is the first thing I have ever done that is entirely mine. Let me have it."

"No."

The word came out of her like a blade leaving a sheath.

She hadn't planned it. It rose from somewhere deeper than planning — from the vaults beneath the capital where bond-heirs had hung in chains like lanterns, drained to keep the king's wards bright. From a child in an occupied vale whose ward-scarred skin had smoothed under her palm. From every name in her mother's ledger, written in blood-ink by a woman who had split an ember and hidden it inside her unborn daughter rather than let the crown have all of it.

"No," Wren said again, steadier. "The law says one life. The law was written by people who only knew how to take."

Below them, through the shattered floor of the heart-tower, the mountain breathed. Something vast turned over in its sleep of centuries and began, slowly, to wake — a hunger so old it predated hunger, wearing shapes of the dead it had eaten, wearing her mother's face in its first attempts at speech.

Wren knelt and pressed both hands to the dais.

"But I know people who only know how to give."

* * *

**HALRIC**

Halric felt the tower take him and braced for the burning.

It did not come.

Instead there was — a hand. Dozens of them. Hundreds. The moment Wren refused the old terms, the ember in her chest did not flare; it *reached*, the way a struck bell reaches through still air, and Halric understood with a lurch that he was no longer the only one holding the weight.

He could feel them. All of them.

The bond-heirs from the vaults, scattered across three valleys, waking at midnight with their palms glowing. The freed soldiers who had chosen to stay at the waystone grove. The innkeeper's daughter, miles south, asleep and dreaming in a language of light. The child Wren had healed in the occupied vale, whose scarred skin now shone faintly through the shutters of a house where occupiers slept.

A web. Not a wall. Not one life nailed to a tower, but ten thousand small willing gifts, threaded together across the whole of Thornfell, each one freely offered, none of them sufficient alone, all of them sufficient together.

"Oh," Halric said softly, to no one. "Oh, you brilliant, impossible woman."

The devouring thing rose beneath them, and for the first time in the history of the mountain, the wards were everywhere and nowhere. There was no wall to breach. No single stone to crack. It reached for the pattern of power it had been sealed against for a thousand years and found — nothing solid. Only connection. Only giving.

And giving, it could not eat.

* * *

**WREN**

It came for her first.

Of course it did. She carried the largest fragment of the original ember, and hunger always goes for the fullest plate. It poured up through the broken floor as a shape wearing her mother's face, her mother's voice, saying her childhood name in the tones of a lullaby Wren barely remembered.

Wren looked at it and felt — grief. Clean, sharp, real grief. But not surrender.

"You're not her," she said. "She never once asked me for anything. She only gave."

She opened her hands.

She did not push the ember out. She *offered* it — the way her mother had offered it, splitting herself open in a hidden workshop to hide starlight inside her unborn child. The way the innkeeper's daughter offered her dreams. The way Halric was offering everything he had left. The ember flared at the offering, and the shape of her mother blew apart like smoke, and behind it the true thing showed itself: vast, cold, ancient, a hole in the world shaped exactly like wanting.

It lunged at the web.

And the web caught it.

Every willing heart in the vale lit at once. Wren saw it — actually *saw* it, through eyes made of ember-light — threads of silver running down mountainsides, across valleys, through occupied towns and hidden groves and prison cells and sickbeds, ten thousand strands of freely given strength converging not on a wall but on each other, weaving themselves into a lattice so fine and so vast that the devouring thing, reaching for it, found no edge to grip, no center to consume, no life being *taken* from anyone anywhere.

You cannot drink an ocean by swallowing thread.

The thing screamed without sound. It thrashed against connections it couldn't sever, because you cannot cut what people choose. And slowly — terribly, beautifully slowly — it began to come apart. Not destroyed. *Unmade.* Its hunger unraveled into appetite, its appetite into want, its want into ordinary need, its need into —

— night.

Just night. Ordinary, honest darkness, the kind that falls when the sun goes down, the kind that holds no hunger, keeps no dead, wears no faces.

* * *

**HALRIC**

Halric was on his knees at the anchor-stone when the first star came back.

He had expected to be gone by now. He had done the arithmetic in the tower's shadow: one life, freely given, permanently bound — he'd meant to be the payment. Instead he was still here, hollowed and blazing, one thread among thousands, held in the web by every other hand in it. Bound, yes. Permanently, yes. But not alone. Never again alone.

Above the ruined peak of Thornfell, in the patch of sky the devouring dark had chewed thinnest, a single point of light kindled.

Then another.

Then a handful, like embers stirred in a banked fire.

Then the sky broke open.

Stars came back over Thornfell the way water comes back to a dried riverbed — hesitantly, then all at once, whole constellations pouring back into positions they had held for a thousand years, the great wheel of the heavens grinding forward as if the night since its stopping had been nothing but a held breath. Down in the valleys, Halric knew, people were coming out of their houses. Soldiers on both sides of the war were looking up and forgetting, for one full minute, which side they were on.

He laughed. It hurt. He laughed anyway.

Beside him, Wren rose from the dais, smoking gently at the edges, alive, alive, alive — and above the tower the stars kept arriving, one by one, each one a small answer to a choice made together by everyone in the vale.

The last of them settled into place directly overhead, brighter than all the rest.

Old-timers in the lowlands would later swear it was new — a star that had never appeared on any chart, hanging above the heart-tower like a lamp left burning in a window.

Waiting for someone to come home.
