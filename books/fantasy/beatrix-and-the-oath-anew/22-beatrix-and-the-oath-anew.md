## 22. Beatrix and the Oath Anew

The bell in the vale rang wrong that morning, and Wren Sandoval smiled before she even opened her eyes.

She had learned every voice of the rebuilt valley the way she had once learned poisons: by small doses, by attention, by years. The mill bell said one thing. The chapel bell said another. The schoolhouse bell — hers, cast from the melted fittings of a ward-tower that had stood for nine hundred years and fallen in a single night — said *come learn*. But this ringing was lighter, quicker, a child's hand on the rope, which meant someone had climbed the belfry before lessons, which meant Tam Ashwell had broken his promise about not touching the rope for a whole season.

Which meant, in the grammar of oaths grown rather than imposed, that Tam owed her an hour of mucking the goat shed.

She dressed by feel, braided her hair without a mirror, and stepped out into a morning so clean it felt invented. Frost silvered the terraced orchards climbing the vale's western wall. Below the school, the river ran bright over stones that still hummed faintly if you pressed your palm to them — the web's oldest threads, the ones she and Halric had spun on the worst night of their lives, never quite gone quiet. Somewhere upslope a goat complained. Somewhere closer, forty children were pretending they hadn't all heard the same bell.

Wren took her time walking down. She had earned the habit of taking her time.

Ten years since the stars came back. Ten years since the devouring dark unmade itself into ordinary night above Thornfell, and the mountain's roots went quiet, and the world woke to a sky it had thought it would never see again. Ten years of letters from the capital that she answered slowly, of seasons when Halric came down from the half-bound tower with frost in his beard and tower-light in his eyes, of children arriving at her door from every bonded family left in the kingdom — some sent eagerly, some smuggled to her in the dead of night by parents who remembered what the crown had once done to children like them.

The school had started as six orphans sleeping in her mother's old workshop, which Wren had spent two years digging out of ash. It was now three long halls and an orchard of waystone saplings, because it turned out that when you taught a grove to hope, it grew.

* * *

Lessons were held in the great hall on frost mornings, around a hearth where the fire burned a shade too blue, the way it always did near living stone. Wren entered to the usual theater of innocence: forty faces angled toward their slates, spines suspiciously straight, and Tam Ashwell at the back wearing the expression of a boy who had definitely not run past the goat shed at a dead sprint.

"Good morning, the bell," Wren said.

"Good morning, Mistress Sandoval," the hall chorused, except for Tam, who said it half a beat late and half a tone guilty.

"Interesting weather we had at dawn," Wren said. "Rope-shaped weather."

Tam studied his slate with tremendous scholarship. The other children grinned into their collars. Nobody ratted him out, which was its own lesson, though Wren hadn't written it on any board.

She let it go. There would be time for goats. There was time for everything now; that was the whole miracle, the whole strange gift of the years after — that things could be slow. That an oath could be tended instead of spent.

"Slates away," she said. "Today we begin the old book."

The room changed. She felt it the way she once felt poison under her tongue — a collective tightening, a held breath. The book of the old order. The volume every bond-heir child had heard whispered about, the one the crown had kept locked in its deep vaults for two centuries while it drained their grandparents through forged ledgers and polite requisitions. Wren had taken it back herself, in a manner of speaking. She had sent the king her mother's ledger instead of accepting his medal, and she had sent along a second parcel: the old book, recovered from the ruin of the heart-tower, water-stained and half illegible, with a note that read only, *It belongs to the families. You may visit it.*

The king, to his credit or his cunning, had never tested that.

From beneath her chair she drew out the copy — her copy, hand-inked over four winters, the gaps filled with what the waystone grove had shown her, what Halric remembered, what the freed heirs carried in their shared memory. She laid it on the lectern at the front of the hall.

"It doesn't have an author's name," she said. "Does anyone know why we call it what we call it?"

Hands. Always hands first, in her school — she had beaten that into them gently, season by season. Mira Quill, small and solemn, whose ward-scar Wren had healed with a touch in an occupied vale a lifetime ago, raised hers highest.

"The lady who wrote it," Mira said. "Beatrix. She was the first."

"She was the first *keeper*," corrected Joss, older, precise, a boy who loved rules the way Tam loved ropes he wasn't supposed to pull. "The first oath-smith. She made the original binding at Thornfell."

"Did she?" Wren said, and let it hang.

Silence did the work for her. She watched them think. That was the craft of it, the part she loved more than she had ever loved anything about tasting or lying or surviving: the moment the hook set and the children leaned forward into their own minds.

"The truth is," Wren said, "nobody knows. Not me, not the archivists in the capital, not the grove itself, and the grove remembers nine hundred years. The name isn't in the oldest copies. It just — arrives. Every generation, someone starts calling the book Beatrix, and no one can say where they learned it."

"Like a ghost," someone whispered.

"Like a seed," Wren said.

She turned the great book open to its first page. Her own ink, her own careful hand, copying words that had been copied and lost and copied again across centuries of ward-smiths who fed themselves to stone so that strangers could sleep safely.

"When I was your age," she said, "I didn't know this book existed. I didn't know I existed — not really. I knew I was the king's taster. I knew my tongue worked strange, that poison sat in me like rain on oiled cloth. I thought that was training." She tapped the page. "It was inheritance. My mother split the last ember of the old oath and hid it inside me before I was born. The wards had been drinking my family's blood for generations, and she knew the bill would come due someday, and she paid it the only way left — secretly, silently, without asking me."

The hearth popped. Blue flame shivered.

"I was angry about that for a long time," Wren said plainly, because in her school they did not pretend feelings were shameful. "Angrier than I can say. She chose secrecy over safety, hers and mine both. She died before I could ask her why. Before I could forgive her." A breath. "Then one night in a waystone grove, the stones showed me her final act, and I understood: she wasn't hiding the ember from me. She was planting it in me. There's a difference between a secret and a seed. A secret is something you keep. A seed is something you trust to grow."

Mira's hand crept up again, hesitant this time. "Is that why the book is called Beatrix? Because it's a seed?"

"I think some names are seeds," Wren said. "I think someone, somewhere, a very long time ago, planted that word in the book the way my mother planted the ember in me — and every few generations, someone waters it without knowing, and it flowers again as a question. Like the one you just asked." She smiled. "And questions are how oaths start. Not commands. Not contracts signed in fear. Questions, asked freely, answered freely. That's the whole new law, children, and you already keep it better than the old order ever did."

Joss frowned, working at it the way he worked at everything. "But the old oath demanded a life. You told us. One life, willing, freely given. How can that be free?"

And there it was — the question she had waited ten years for someone young enough to ask without flinching.

"That's tomorrow's lesson," Wren said. "Bring your slates and your courage."

* * *

She found Halric at dusk, because she always found Halric at dusk, because dusk was when the tower let him go.

He came down the mountain path slowly these days, one hand trailing along the waystones as if greeting old colleagues, and in a sense he was. Half-bound was not a figure of speech. When he had stepped forward into the heart-tower's light — before Wren could speak, before she could say *not alone, you don't understand, I won't let you pay alone* — the old oath had taken exactly what it was written to take. It had taken most of him. What remained walked down to her each evening with silvered eyes and a faint ring of light under his skin when the sun touched the horizon, and every morning he climbed back up and held the line, and every season he stayed a little longer in the lowlands, and neither of them named the arithmetic of it anymore. Naming it wouldn't change it. Tending it might.

"You rang the bell," he said, settling onto the bench beside her outside the kitchen. Steam rose off two cups between them. He drank his without testing it, which after everything was either love or lunacy, and she had stopped trying to sort the difference.

"Tam rang the bell."

"Tam rang the bell," Halric agreed gravely. "And what does Tam owe?"

"Goats."

"Justice at last." He wrapped his hands around his cup. Tower-light pulsed faintly in his knuckles, keeping time with something far overhead. "You started the old book today."

"How could you possibly know that."

He tipped his head toward the sky, where the first stars were coming out — coming out *again*, every single night, still the miracle it had been the first time. "When you opened it, every waystone from here to the pass sang a note. Just one note, soft as anything. I nearly fell off the switchback." He glanced sideways at her, and the corner of his mouth did the thing it did. "What are you teaching them, Wren, that makes stone sing?"

"That names are seeds. That oaths grow. That questions come before vows." She looked into her cup. "That nobody should have to pay alone."

Halric was quiet for a while. Down in the vale, windows lit one by one, a constellation answering the one overhead. The web hummed under the frost — not the starving pull of the old wards, feeding on bonded blood in the dark, but something gentler, the thing she had woven from the willing memories of everyone she had freed: a web that gave as much as it took, that asked nothing, that linked every heart in the vale like lamps strung on one wire.

"They asked about the life-price, didn't you?" he said. "One of them. Eventually one of them always does."

"A boy named Joss. Tomorrow."

"What will you tell him?"

Wren watched the stars thicken. She thought about defiance — about the night she had refused the oath's terms, standing over Halric's fading body in the ruined heart-tower, and offered instead not one life but the shared remembering of every heir the crown had drained and she had freed. She had broken the oldest law in the book with nothing but refusal and borrowed memory, and the sky had answered. She thought about the word *Beatrix*, and whoever had planted it, and whether they had known, all those centuries ago, that the book would one day need a second meaning hidden inside its first.

"I'll tell him the truth," she said. "That the old law was written by people who couldn't imagine enough. One life, freely given — they thought that was the ceiling of generosity. They never guessed a hundred hearts might give a little, willingly, together. They never guessed the wards could be made of *company*." She reached over and took Halric's hand, tower-light and taster's callus. "I'll tell him the law was true and incomplete, which is the case with almost every law ever made. And then I'll tell him what I told you on the worst night of my life."

"Which was?"

"'Love refuses to pay alone.'" She squeezed his fingers. "You wrote it down once, you sentimental old tower. Don't look surprised."

"I'm not surprised," Halric said. "I'm counting."

"Counting what?"

"How many seeds you've planted in that hall." He nodded toward the school, where lamplit shapes moved behind frosted glass — children finishing chores, arguing about stars, sneaking extra bread. "Forty this year?"

"Forty-one. Mira's cousin arrived last week. Faint spark in her palms already, third generation."

"Forty-one seeds." He said it the way other men said *hail the king*, with weight and irony and something underneath that was neither. "And in twenty years, forty-one teachers. And in fifty—" He stopped, because the math outran the words, and they sat with it a moment, letting it grow.

Above Thornfell, whole and dark and harmless, the mountain wore its stars like a crown it had finally learned to hold up itself.

"One more thing," Wren said, as the cold deepened and the tea went lukewarm and neither of them moved to go in. "Something odd happened at the end of lessons. I put the book away, and Mira stayed behind. She asked if she could copy the first page. I said yes, thinking it was diligence." Wren laughed softly, uneasily, an old reflex from a life of noticing what shouldn't be noticed. "Halric — she'd already begun before I left the room. And her hand. Her hand on the page. It wasn't her handwriting. It was rounder. Older. The letterforms matched fragments I've seen from the first century, ink that predated the towers."

Halric set down his cup very carefully, the way he did when the tower was speaking through him and he wanted to be sure which voice came out.

"What did the page say?" he asked. "The first line. In her hand."

Wren closed her eyes and heard it again, the small scratch of a child's quill moving with nine hundred years of certainty:

"*To whoever grows: my name was never mine. Plant it.*"

The waystones, all across the darkening vale, began — very quietly, one by one, like stars — to sing.
