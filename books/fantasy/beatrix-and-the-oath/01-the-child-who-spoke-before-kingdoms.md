## 1. The Child Who Spoke Before Kingdoms

The mountain had been singing all day.

Ansel Delacroix heard it the way he heard most things now—through the soles of his boots, through the ache in his old sword-scar where the cold liked to settle like a debt collector. Wind through the pass above Thornfell made a sound like a struck blade left to ring, and after ten years of listening to it, he had almost convinced himself it meant nothing. Almost. The mountains sang when they were empty. They sang when the roads were safe and the wardens were far away and a man could walk his smuggling route with nothing heavier on his conscience than untaxed brandy.

Tonight the song had changed key.

He came down from the high trail with frost in his beard and forty pounds of contraband salt-pork on his back, and the first wrongness was the smoke. His cabin's chimney stood dead against the violet sky—no thread of warmth rising—and Ansel stopped at the treeline with the instinct of ten buried years crawling up his spine like ice water. He set down the pack. He drew no weapon, because drawing one would mean admitting what he'd been for a decade, and he was not yet ready to be that man again. Not tonight. Not in the snow.

The second wrongness was blood.

It marked his doorstep in a scatter, bright as spilled wine against the white, leading from the treeline to his door in the staggering signature of someone who had run until running stopped meaning anything. Small prints. A child's boots, half-soled, the right one dragging. And pressed into the drift beside the step, where something small had fallen and risen and fallen again—

Ansel crouched. Breath fogging. Heart doing something slow and terrible in his chest.

In the snow, written by a bleeding finger, were words in a language that had no business existing outside of graves.

*Vael. Suren. Kaeth-ir.*

Swordspeech. The oath-tongue of the First Kings, the language that had once lived only in the mouths of nine hundred sworn blades and died with every last one of them but him. *Help. Shelter. I am hunted.* He knew them the way a man knows his mother's voice—knew them in his teeth, in the scar tissue over his heart where the oath had been cut into him at nineteen years old and burned out of him at thirty-five, or so he had told himself. So he had sworn.

"Damn you," he said quietly, to the snow, to the language, to whatever god had decided his decade of silence was long enough. "Damn you twice."

Then he opened his door, because there was nowhere else the child could have gone, and there she was.

She had wedged herself into the gap between his woodstove and the wall, a small gray shape in a coat too big for her, soaked through, shivering so hard her teeth clicked like dice in a cup. Dark hair matted with blood at the temple. Maybe eight years old. Maybe younger—the winter had a way of erasing age from faces. Her eyes found him in the doorway and did not widen, did not cry out. That was the thing he would remember later, in the bad hours: she looked at him the way soldiers look at another soldier. Recognition without hope. She had seen men with swords before, and not from storybooks.

Her lips moved. No sound came out—or none that reached him over the wind—but he read the shapes of the words as easily as reading his own name carved into a tree.

*Kaeth-ir vael suren.* Shelter me. I am hunted.

"I know," Ansel said, and hated how rough his voice came out, rusted from disuse in his own tongue. "I know what you're saying."

Something flickered across her face—not relief. Something older and sadder than relief. She lifted one small hand, fingers stiff with cold, and pointed past him. Out. Toward the dark mouth of the pass.

And Ansel felt it then, the way he hadn't felt anything in ten years: the low hum beneath the world's skin, the resonance of an oath spoken nearby, still hanging in the air like the smell of lightning. Crown wardens carried oath-chains—cold iron links wound with binding-words—and somewhere up that pass, more than one chain was singing.

Three of them. Maybe four. Moving fast.

He should have put her out. That was the arithmetic of survival, the cold ledger he'd kept balanced for a decade: a mute child speaking a dead tongue, hunted by the crown itself, was worth more dead to him than alive. Wardens didn't ask questions of men found harboring fugitives. Wardens asked questions with fire.

Instead Ansel heard himself say, "Can you walk?"

She nodded. Then, honest even in terror, shook her head. Then pointed at her own legs with a grimace that was somehow, absurdly, apologetic.

"Right." He shrugged off the salt-pork pack and dropped it inside the door. He crouched, back to her, arms open behind him—an offer he hadn't made since the war, since the last time he'd carried a wounded comrade out of a burning garrison while the world came apart around his ears. "Up, then. Hold on to my hair if you have to. Everyone does eventually."

She climbed onto his back with the practiced economy of a child who had learned that being carried was often the difference between living and the other thing. She weighed almost nothing. She smelled of woodsmoke and blood and, faintly, of something else—something like a chapel, like old stone and candle tallow, a smell that raised the hair on his neck.

He banked the stove, kicked ash over the hearth-light, pulled the door shut, and went into the trees.

* * *

The smugglers' camp sat two miles down-mountain in a hollow the maps had forgotten, six shelters around a firepit sunk into the earth so its light showed only to the stars. Ansel's found family, though none of them would say the word family aloud before the brandy came out. There was Maren Oduya, who ran the routes and forgave nothing; her brother Tam, who could talk a customs officer into buying back his own horse; Old Wren, who had smuggled everything from silk to saints' relics and claimed to regret none of it; the twins, Petra and Ilse, who never spoke except to each other; and Doss, enormous and gentle, who was already lifting the pot off the fire when Ansel came out of the dark with a bleeding child on his back.

Nobody said anything for a long moment. The fire crackled. Snow hissed off the canvas overhead.

"A word," Maren said, in the tone that meant many words.

They spoke apart, by the woodpile, voices low. Ansel told it plain: child, blood, swordspeech, wardens on the pass, three or four chains singing. Maren listened with her arms crossed and her jaw set like a fortress gate, and when he finished she was quiet for the length of three breaths.

"You said the tongue," she said. "You said it plain. Swordspeech."

"You know what that means."

"I know what it cost *you*." Her eyes went to his chest, to the scar she'd never asked about and he'd never explained, the burn-mark where an oath had been cut out of him by his own hand and his own knife. "I know what the crown pays for people who speak it. I know what the crown does to people who *shelter* them." She exhaled, and the breath came out white and weary between them. "Ten years, Ansel. Ten years we've kept you safe from yourself."

"And now the crown is hunting a child through my mountains in the dead of winter," Ansel said. "Maren. She wrote *help* in the snow in a language that's been dead for a century. Whatever she is, whatever she's carrying—" He stopped, because he did not know how to finish it, because the truth was that some part of him had already decided, had decided the moment he'd read those words, and everything since was just the slow ceremony of catching up. "I'm not turning her out. If the camp votes otherwise, I'll take her deeper into the fells alone. But I'm asking. All of us, or just me."

Maren looked at him for a long time. Behind her, the child sat wrapped in Doss's spare cloak by the fire, hands cupped around a tin mug of broth she was too wary to drink, watching them all with those soldier's eyes. Tam had already given her his seat. Old Wren was humming to her, tuneless and kind. The twins had silently produced a comb and were, with great solemnity, working the ice out of her hair.

"They've already voted," Maren said at last, and her voice had gone soft in a way Ansel had heard perhaps twice in ten years. "Look at them, you great fool. Vote happened before you got her through the door."

Ansel looked. Doss was showing the girl how to hold the mug so it warmed both hands. Wren had draped a second blanket over her shoulders. Nobody had asked her name, because somebody had clearly guessed she couldn't give it.

Something in his chest that had been frozen for a decade cracked, quietly, like river ice in spring.

"She mouths the words," he said. "Even now. Watch."

And he watched. The girl's lips moved over the rim of the mug, silent, ceaseless—a prayer, a counting, a list of names maybe, the way the frightened recite what they must not forget. And Ansel read them, because he could not stop himself, because the language lived in him still like a splinter worked too deep to draw.

*Suren kaeth. Vael ir domm. Suren—*

Shelter me. Keep me. Remember me.

His blood went cold in a way the mountain had never managed.

Because those weren't a child's prayers. Those were the opening lines of the *Vigil*—the vigil-oath, the oldest rite in the swordspeech, the words spoken only over a king's body. Over a king's *true name*, spoken aloud so it would not be lost.

She wasn't asking for shelter.

She was carrying something. And whatever it was, the crown had already torn the mountains apart to take it from her—and they were not going to stop at three wardens on a pass.

"Maren," Ansel said, eyes never leaving the child, "get everyone up. We're leaving before dawn."
