## 2. What the Warden Knew

The man they brought in was wrong-shaped with cold, bent like a branch under snow, and Beatrix knew before anyone spoke that he had come to unmake her.

She watched from the loft of the safehouse, knees drawn to her chest, breath fogging the air above her. Below, firelight moved across the smugglers' faces—Ansel with his arms crossed and his jaw set like a door barred against winter; Marta the wide woman who cooked; Ferren who limped; a boy called Pip whose name she had learned by watching his mouth shape it. They had given her broth. They had not asked her for anything. This was new. Everything else, everyone else, had asked.

The warden knelt on the floorboards because his legs would no longer hold him upright. His blue-and-silver coat was stiff with frost, and one of his eyes was swollen shut where Ansel's fist—or the mountain, or the cold—had found him. He looked at her once, up through the ladder-gap, and she saw it in his face: not hatred. Hunger. The hunger of a starving man looking at bread he has been ordered not to eat.

*There she is,* his eye said. *There is the word we were sent to swallow.*

Beatrix pulled back from the gap and pressed her spine against the rough wall.

Words lived in her like fish under ice. She could feel them moving, silver-bellied, but when she reached for them they scattered. Her voice had gone out of her the night the tower burned—the night the tall men came for her grandmother and the grandmother put something into Beatrix's mouth, warm as a coal, and said *run, little keeper, run and keep it.* Since then she spoke only in the old tongue, the deep tongue, the one that made wardens flinch and horses shy. The words came out of her whole and shining and she did not always know what they meant until she saw what they did.

But she understood more than she could say. Understanding was the one thing they had never been able to take.

Below, the interrogation began without ropes or blades. That was the strange thing about these smugglers, these hard-edged people who smelled of pine tar and pipe smoke: they hurt less than the crown's men did, and got more truth.

"Who sent you?" Ansel's voice, low.

The warden laughed, a cracked sound. "You know who. You've known since Thornfell. Since you felt the winter come down early and told yourself it was weather."

"The king," Marta said flatly.

"The king." The warden said it the way a man says a prayer he no longer believes. "His Radiant Majesty. Who died six years ago this past autumn. I stood vigil at the funeral pyre. I was there."

Silence, except for the fire spitting.

"I'm a warden of the second circle," the man went on, and now his voice shook. "Do you know what we're sworn at our making? Not to the crown. To the *name*. There's a true name under every throne, older than the throne, and the realm stands on it like a keystone. We kept it. We carried pieces of it so no one man could speak it whole. And then—" his good eye closed—"then the thing in the palace started eating us. Calling us one by one to private audience. And the ones who came back down the stairs came back *empty*, smiling, with their oaths gone out of them like marrow sucked from bone. It takes the name piece by piece. It takes the memory with it. It's almost finished, do you understand? Almost whole. And when it is whole, there will be nothing left under the crown at all. Just the wearing of it. Forever."

Beatrix's hands had gone cold on her knees.

Because she knew. She knew the way she knew the taste of ash. Grandmother's mouth against her ear in the burning dark: *you are the last vessel, little one. The king gave his name into keeping before the end, all of it, trusting it to small hands. Guard it. Never give it whole. Never let them make you speak.*

The wraith wanted her because she was the keystone's shadow. Because somewhere inside her, behind the ice, behind the silence, lay the true name of a dead king—and a dead king's name, spoken whole by a living throat, was the only thing in the world that could unmake the thing that wore him.

They were going to ask her. She could feel it coming up through the floorboards like frost.

*

They argued long into the night. Beatrix stayed in the loft and listened, hugging her knees, learning them the way she learned everything—by mouths, by cadence, by the shapes anger and fear made in the air.

Marta wanted her gone by dawn. "A child," Marta said, "who carries the death of kingdoms in her head. Every road she walks becomes a battlefield. Send her over the pass to the sea-folk, wash our hands—"

"With what?" Ferren's rasp. "Wardens on both roads. You saw the signalcodes burning on the peaks. They've closed the mountains around her like a fist."

"She's a child," Marta repeated, but softer, and Beatrix heard the crack in it, the place where duty was starting to split along its grain and show something warmer underneath.

Then Ansel spoke, and the room went quiet around his voice the way snow quiets a forest.

"I heard her on my doorstep," he said. "Before she collapsed. She was saying words I haven't heard spoken aloud in ten years. Words I buried deeper than any grave I ever dug." A pause, long enough that Beatrix leaned closer over the loft edge. "Swordspeech. Oath-language. My order died keeping secrets like whatever she's carrying, and I swore I'd never touch that life again. So believe me when I tell you I have every reason to walk away." Another pause. "And I can't. She spoke in the tongue of oaths, and some part of me answered before I chose to. If we turn her out, we're not washing our hands. We're choosing the wraith. There's no clean middle anymore. There hasn't been for years—we just weren't asked to see it."

"And if sheltering her kills us?" Pip, young, frightened, brave anyway.

"Then we die a family," Ansel said, "instead of surviving as strangers."

Beatrix did not understand every word. But she understood *family*, because grandmother had used the old word for it once, tracing it in the ash: *the people who stand between you and the dark, and are glad to.*

She crept to the ladder and climbed down, and every face turned to her, and the fire popped, and no one breathed.

She could not say thank you. Thank you had drowned years ago. So she did the only thing her broken mouth allowed: she lifted her hand, palm open, and spoke one word of the deep tongue—not a word of power, just an old, small word, a hearth-word, the kind grandmothers used to tuck children in with. Warmth went through the room like sunlight through ice. The fire burned steadier. Marta's hard shoulders dropped half an inch. Even the warden, forgotten in his corner, sighed like a man setting down a load.

"What did she say?" Pip whispered.

Ansel was looking at her strangely, his scarred hand resting on his sword-hilt as though the blade itself remembered. "She said *mine*," he translated quietly. "As in—I am yours, and you are mine. It's how the old order sealed a bond. It can't be spoken falsely. The tongue won't carry it."

Marta stared at the child a long moment. Then she swore a smuggler's oath, foul and heartfelt, and stomped to the kitchen, and came back with bread and honey, and shoved it into Beatrix's hands like a woman surrendering.

"Eat," she said. "Family."

*

Later, near dawn, while the others slept, Beatrix knelt beside the captured warden. He watched her come with his one good eye, and there was terror in it now, and something else—hope, terrible hope, like a man dying of thirst hoping for rain even if the rain drowns him.

"You could end it," he whispered. "You know that. One word, whole, from your mouth. One word, and the thing in the palace comes apart like rot. But it will take everything you love to say it. Everything. The name doesn't leave empty-handed."

Beatrix looked at him. Then she touched her own chest, over her heart, and shook her head slowly—*I don't love anyone yet*—and stopped, because even as she made the gesture she saw Ansel asleep by the fire, and Marta, and Pip curled like a comma, and understood that the gesture was already a lie.

The warden read it anyway. He smiled with cracked lips.

"Ah," he breathed. "Too late already. Then listen, keeper, and listen well: there is an old rite. The unbinding. Your order wrote it before my order was ever dreamed. It lets another speak the name for you—a swordspeaker, an oath-bound throat—so the price falls on *his* memories instead of yours. If you can find one willing to forget everyone he loves—"

He stopped. His eye drifted, deliberate, toward the sleeping form by the hearth. Toward the man who had already, on a frozen doorstep, answered a call he'd sworn off ten years ago.

Outside, the unnatural winter pressed against the shutters, and far to the south, over the capital, the sky glowed faintly the color of an open mouth.
