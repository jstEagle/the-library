## 3. The Long Winter Falls

The winter did not belong to the world.

Ansel had lived thirty years in the Thornfells and knew every mood of their snows—the dry whisper of a cold snap, the wet gray slush of a thaw, the singing silence after a storm. This was none of those. This cold came from beneath the ground, from somewhere south where a hollow thing sat on a stolen throne, and it moved against them like a living will, closing passes that should have been open, sending wind to erase their tracks and then to erase them.

They traveled by night because the days were worse. In daylight the snow glowed with a pale wrong light, and in that light Beatrix would stop walking and stare south with her whole body rigid, as if something were pulling at her from behind her ribs.

"Again?" Ansel asked, kneeling beside her, brushing frost from her hood.

She nodded once. Then she signed—quick, sharp gestures she'd taught him on the second night of their flight: *It knows I'm coming. It's glad.*

He didn't tell her what he thought of that. He didn't tell anyone anything anymore. That was the discipline of the last swordspeaker: you carried your knowledge like a blade under your coat, edge inward, so it cut only you.

* * *

Maren led them through the high country, and Maren was the best guide on the smugglers' roads—she could read a snowfield the way priests read scripture. But even Maren couldn't read weather that hated them personally.

On the fourth day they crossed the shoulder of Mount Cael, and the wind found them. It didn't howl; it *spoke*, a low sibilant whispering that Ansel understood before he let himself understand it. The wraith's voice, threaded through the storm, saying words in no living tongue—and Beatrix answering it, screaming soundlessly into the white, her hands shaping refusals over and over: *No. No. No.*

They roped themselves together and walked blind for six hours, each man's hand on the shoulder of the man before, trusting the person ahead of them more than their own eyes. Ansel went last, behind Beatrix, one fist knotted in the back of her coat. Once the rope went slack between him and Tam, and he shouted into the wind until his throat tore, and then Tam's hand closed around his wrist out of the whiteness, and they stood there shaking, two men holding on to each other in the mouth of the storm.

That night they made camp in the lee of a fallen watchtower, and Maren didn't wake up.

Not dead—not yet. Just done. She'd given the mountain everything and the mountain had taken it. She sat propped against the stones with ice in her lashes, and when Ansel crouched before her she smiled with cracked lips and pressed her map into his hands.

"Follow the old king's road," she rasped. "The wardens won't. They think it's superstition." A cough, wet and long. "Superstition is just memory, Delacroix. And memory is the only weapon we've got."

They built her a cairn at dawn because the ground was too hard for digging. Beatrix laid her hand flat on the stones and spoke three words of the dead tongue, soft as snowfall. Ansel recognized the shape of them—a blessing, or a farewell, or both. He bowed his head and did not speak at all.

* * *

The oath-hall rose out of the white on the seventh day like the ribcage of some drowned god.

Ansel knew it. Of course he knew it. Every child of the order learned the Hall of Vows before they learned to hold a sword—learned its vaulted dark, its hundred pillars carved with the names of kings, its floor worn smooth by ten generations of kneeling. He had sworn his own oath here at nineteen, knee bleeding on stone, an old man's hand heavy on his crown of hair.

He'd sworn never to come back.

But the storm was a wall behind them and the capital four days ahead, and the hall's broken doors still offered shelter, and so they went in, all six of them, trailing snow like ghosts trailing grave-clothes.

Beatrix stopped in the center of the floor, where the mosaics formed a great sunburst, and turned in a slow circle. Her breath caught. She looked up at Ansel with an expression he had never seen on her face—not fear, not the wariness she wore like armor, but something older and stranger. Recognition. Grief. Homecoming.

"You know this place," he said quietly.

She nodded. Then she pointed at herself, at the floor, at the distant shadow of the throne far south beyond the mountains—and began, with the terrible patience of someone carrying a message no one wants to receive, to teach.

* * *

She taught him through the night, by firelight, in the language of the First Kings.

It was slow work. She had no paper, so she wrote in the ash at the fire's edge; she had no voice, so she carved meaning out of gesture and repetition and the fierce, frustrated light in her eyes. But Ansel had been trained since boyhood in swordspeech—the oath-tongue was the skeleton of it, the dead language dressed in ritual—and piece by piece, word by word, the rite assembled itself in his mind like a sword being forged.

The unbinding. He saw it whole by midnight: the true name spoken aloud, the wraith unmade, the oath restored to the man it had been stolen from. It was beautiful. It was clean. It was the reason she had run a hundred miles through a hunting winter rather than let the name die in her.

And then, near dawn, she taught him the price.

She wrote it in the ash first, in careful dead-tongue characters, and when he only stared she took his hand—her small fingers, chapped and blue-nailed, closing over his scarred ones—and pressed his palm against his own chest. Then she pointed at him. At the fire. At Maren's empty place by the wall. At Tam, asleep against a pillar. At herself.

Then she drew a line through it all. One stroke, deliberate, final.

*Gone,* the gesture said. *All of it. All of them.*

"The speaker forgets," Ansel said slowly, translating the old formulae in his head, feeling them settle into place with horrible rightness. "Every bond sworn in love. Every face. Every—" His voice failed. He looked down at the ash, at the little line drawn through the small figure she always used for herself, and felt the cold come inside him, the real cold, the kind no fire touches.

That was the architecture of the oath. Of course it was. The First Kings had built their magic on sacrifice the way masons build on bedrock—an unbinding must cost the binder everything he is bound to. You could not unmake a devourer of memories while holding yours safe. The spell demanded symmetry. It demanded *payment*.

He would speak the name. The king would live. And Ansel Delacroix would walk out of that throne room a stranger to everyone who had ever loved him—no, worse: loved by people he could not remember, a hollow man surrounded by ghosts who grieved him while he stood among them whole.

"Is there another way?"

Beatrix's face answered him before her hands did. She shook her head, slow, and her eyes were wet, and she reached up and touched his cheek with fingers gone gentle, and he understood that she had known longer than he had. She had known since the ruined tower, maybe. Since the rope in the storm. She had been teaching him the knife and the wound together, all along, letting him arrive at the price in his own time.

Because she knew he would pay it anyway.

Ansel sat back on his heels. Around him the hall held its silence, a hundred pillars of sleeping kings, the fire ticking down to embers. His companions slept the sleep of the spent and hunted, trusting him to keep the watch. Tam, who had pulled him out of the white. Old Wren, who cooked. The twins. Maren under her cairn on the mountain.

Ten years ago he had buried swordspeech and sworn the order's last vow: *never again*. Never again would the tongue of oaths pass his lips, because the last time it had, it had bought nothing but graves.

Now he would break that vow for the largest word it contained—and the speaking would take from him the only family he had left, the smugglers who'd made a home of him, the mute child who had bled on his doorstep and rewritten his life in a dead language.

He looked at Beatrix. She watched him back, steady, unflinching, as if to say: *I will not make this easier, and I will not make it harder. It is yours to choose.*

He chose. He had chosen, he realized, the moment she collapsed in the snow—chosen with every mile since, every word of hers he'd memorized, every night he'd kept the watch so the others could sleep. Duty had transformed itself somewhere on the road into something with teeth and tenderness, and there was no separating them now.

So he said nothing. He banked the fire, and he kissed the top of her head the way he might have kissed a daughter's, and he lay down with his back to the company and memorized them in the dark—their breathing, their small sounds, the weight of them alive and near—because in four days he would need to carry these faces into a throne room and set them down forever.

Outside, the false winter screamed against the ancient walls, and inside, Ansel kept his vigil, and did not sleep, and told no one.

At dawn they would follow the old king's road south, into the heart of the cold, where a hollow thing waited on a stolen throne wearing a dead man's face—and where a child's true name, and a swordspeaker's last oath, would end it.

One way or another.
