## 4. Speak, and Begin Again

*Beatrix*

The doors of the throne room were older than the winter. Older than the wraith. Beatrix could feel them in her teeth—the way she felt all old things, as a hum beneath the skin, a word waiting to be said.

The hall beyond was vast and blue with shadow. Snow had come in through the broken clerestory windows and lay in drifts across the marble like the sea had died there. And on the throne, the thing that wore the king's face sat perfectly still, watching them come.

It did not look like a monster. That was the worst of it. It looked tired. It looked like a man who had been sitting too long in one place, wearing his own body the way a beggar wears a stolen coat—correctly enough, if you didn't look at the seams.

Beatrix looked at the seams.

She saw the places where the face forgot itself: the left eye drifting a half-second behind the right, the hands too smooth, no lines, no scars, nothing written on them. A king's hands should be written on. Her mother had taught her that much before the wardens came—taught her that names lived in hands, in the creases where a life folded itself over and over.

Behind her, Ansel drew breath.

She knew what he meant to do. She had known since the oath-hall, since the night he read the rite by candlelight and went very quiet, the way men go quiet when they decide to bleed for something. He thought she hadn't understood. He thought the dead tongue was only sounds to her, that because her voice had been taken the words had been taken too.

But the tongue had never lived in her throat. It lived in her chest, in the hollow where the true name had been pressed into her like a seal into wax, and it understood everything.

*He will forget us,* she had signed to the smugglers, in their crude shared hand. *All of us.*

*Then we'll remember him twice as hard,* Marta had signed back, and that was the whole of the argument, and so they had walked through the wraith's winter to this blue room to let him do it.

The wraith rose from the throne. The crown did not move with it quite right—it hung a finger's width above the brow, held there by habit or by hunger—and when it spoke, its voice was every voice Beatrix had ever lost. Her mother's. The warden's. The kind soldier who had carried her through the pass before the cold took him.

"Little vessel," it said. "Give me back my name."

Ansel stepped forward, past her, his boots crunching the dead sea of snow. His sword stayed sheathed. Swordspeech was never in the blade; the blade was only courtesy.

And Beatrix reached into her chest, into the hollow, and gave him the name—not aloud, not in any way the wraith could catch, but the way it had been given to her: silently, in the old grammar, the way snow gives itself to the ground.

Ansel opened his mouth.

*Ansel*

He had sworn never to speak it again. Ten years in the Thornfell mountains trading on smugglers' roads, burying the magic under work and whiskey and the small warm noise of found family, and he had kept that oath the way you keep a wound clean—constantly, painfully, without thinking about why.

Now he stood in the throne room of a kingdom that had forgotten its own king, and the oath broke like ice underfoot, and he was almost grateful.

The words of the unbinding were not words. They were shapes the mouth made around silence. Ansel had learned them at nineteen, kneeling in an oath-hall while an old woman with frostbitten fingers traced them in the air, and he had hoped never to use them, because the old woman had also told him the price, and he had been young enough to think he would never love anyone enough to pay it.

He had been wrong about that. He had been wrong about a great many things.

The first syllable left him and the candles died. All of them, everywhere in the palace, as though the dark had been waiting just outside the glass for permission.

The second, and the snow on the floor began to rise—not fall, rise—turning in slow spirals toward the vaulted ceiling, each flake catching light that no longer had a source.

The third, and the wraith screamed.

It was not a sound. It was the absence of one, a tearing in the air, and the thing on the dais came apart the way a lie comes apart—gracefully at first, then all at once. The borrowed face slid off the skull beneath, and the skull beneath was not bone but packed ash, and the ash remembered being a thousand oaths and a thousand memories it had eaten in its long gray reign, and all of it came out of it screaming as the unbinding pulled it thread by thread into the lightless air.

Ansel kept speaking. His voice did not sound like his own. It sounded like the mountain, like the first morning of the world, like a door opening in a house that had been shut for centuries.

And with the last shape—*the fourth, the name-shape, the one Beatrix had pressed into him silent as a seal into wax*—he spoke the true name of the king.

The hall filled with it. Not loudly. The name arrived the way dawn arrives, everywhere at once, undeniable. The ash-thing shrieked once more, thin as a whistle, and then it was simply gone, and the winter outside the broken windows shuddered, softened, began—for the first time in years—to fall as honest snow.

On the steps of the dais, a man lay breathing.

He was old, and starved, and his hands were written on with ten years of captivity, and when his eyes opened they were his own. He looked at Ansel. He looked at the small mute girl standing in the ruin of the snow. And Aldric, third of that name, true king of a kingdom that had mourned him without knowing it, gathered the last of himself the way a dying fire gathers its heat, and raised one scarred hand toward Beatrix.

"In the sight of the old law," he said, and his voice cracked like river ice, "and by the name I have carried home—I name this child heir. Let no one who loved me say otherwise. Let everyone who loved me say it louder."

He smiled. It was a real smile; it had lines in it.

"Begin again," he said—to her, to Ansel, to the whole blue and falling world—and then the king was done, and the snow came down soft as forgiveness through the broken windows, and no one in the hall could speak for a long time.

*Beatrix*

He woke in spring.

Not far into it—the thaw had only begun, water ticking off the eaves of the smugglers' safehouse in the foothills—but enough. Enough green in the world to make a promise.

Marta had told her what the healers said: the mind is a road, and the unbinding swept it clean, and whatever walks it now must walk it new. Ansel remembered the mountains. He remembered the roads, the trade, the taste of whiskey, the shape of his own hands. He did not remember the child on his doorstep. He did not remember the vote in the lamplight, the ruined oath-hall, the blue throne room, the choice he had made in silence and told no one.

He did not remember her.

Beatrix sat beside his bed the first morning he woke and watched him look at her with the polite blankness of a stranger, and she did not cry. She had decided that in the throne room, in the falling snow, holding the king's hand while the light went out of it. Grief was a country she had already crossed on foot. She would not live there.

Instead she took his hand—his written-on, oath-scarred hand—and turned it palm up, and with one finger she traced a shape in it, slow, the way her mother had taught her. The first word of the dead tongue. The oldest one.

*Home.*

Ansel frowned at it. Something moved behind his eyes, far down, like a fish under winter ice.

"Do I know that?" he asked. His voice was rough with disuse. "That mark. It feels—" He stopped, shook his head, half-laughed at himself. "It feels like something I paid for."

Beatrix smiled. She traced it again, slower, and shaped it silently with her lips so he could watch, and when he still didn't understand—of course he didn't, not yet—she pointed out the window, at the melting snow, at the road below the safehouse where the smugglers were laughing at some joke she couldn't hear, at the whole enormous beginning of the world.

Then she pointed at him. At herself. At the space between.

*Home*, she wrote again on his palm. *Us. We'll start there.*

Ansel looked at the shape in his hand for a long time. Outside, the thaw went on dripping, patient as anything that means to win.

"Teach me," he said finally. "Whatever language that is. Teach me all of it."

And Beatrix—who had no voice, and had never needed one—began.
