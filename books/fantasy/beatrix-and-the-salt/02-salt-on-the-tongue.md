## 2. Salt on the Tongue

The page weighed nothing and everything.

August Winterbourne had carried ledgers heavier than a child before — the great salt-tithe rolls of the northern mines ran to forty pounds of vellum and iron clasps — but they had never carried a single sheet that felt like this, like holding a live coal wrapped in paper. It sat in their satchel against their hip the whole walk from the counting house to the Guildmaster's tower, and every step through the morning crowds of Brackenholt, August was certain someone could see it. That the mother's handwriting would glow through the leather. That the whole city would turn and look.

No one looked. The fishwives argued over herring. A boy swept gull droppings off the customs steps. The world went on being ordinary, and August carried the extraordinary alone, as they always had, as the guild had trained them to since they were eleven years old and first learned that some things are too heavy to put down.

The Guildmaster's tower stood at the heart of the counting house complex like a tooth in a jaw of grey stone. No windows on the lower floors — light was a luxury, and luxuries were entries in a ledger, and every entry demanded payment. August climbed the interior stair by memory, past the sealed archive doors with their locks of black salt-iron, up and up until the air grew dry and tasted faintly of the sea though the sea was half a mile away.

Corvane received them in the top room, where the only window in the tower looked east toward the mountains.

Guildmaster Edran Corvane was not what strangers expected. They imagined bulk, menace, a man built like his reputation. Instead he was small and neat and grey, a precise little man with precise little hands, and he sat at a plain desk doing sums in his own hand when August entered, and he did not look up for the space of ten full breaths. August knew better than to speak first. They had learned that lesson at thirteen, from a senior clerk who hadn't, and who had been reassigned to the kelp-harvest records on the fog coast within the month. Corvane's silences were instruments. He played them like a man tuning a lute.

At last he set down his pen. "Winterbourne."

"Guildmaster." August laid the satchel on the desk and drew out the page — the anomalous entry from yesterday's audit, copied faithfully in August's own careful hand, the original locked in their desk drawer beneath three seals. "During yesterday's reconciliation of the third-quarter trades, I found an entry I couldn't attribute. The hand isn't in our registry of certified scriveners. The ink is old — older than the paper it's written on, which should be impossible, or at least it should be an entry in itself, because impossible things are supposed to pass across our counters, not appear in them unbidden."

Corvane took the copy without hurry. His eyes moved down it once, twice. August watched his face and saw nothing, which was itself informative; Corvane's face was a ledger too, and every line of it balanced.

"Read it aloud," Corvane said.

August hesitated. Reading aloud made a thing real in a way ink alone did not; every clerk knew that. Words spoken became contracts in the mouth. But refusing a direct order from the Guildmaster was also an entry, and August's account could not afford it.

"'Received of B. Winterbourne,'" they read, "'one soul entire, unencumbered, in exchange for which the Guild shall release one August Winterbourne from all debt, servitude, and claim, now and forever.'" Their voice caught on the last word, a hairline fracture they hoped he didn't hear. "'Signed and witnessed beneath the mountain.'"

The room was very quiet. Through the eastern window, the mountains stood white-shouldered under the morning sun, beautiful and indifferent, the way beautiful indifferent things always are.

"And your assessment?" Corvane asked.

"The hand matches no living scrivener. I checked the registry twice. The ink analysis—" August stopped. How much to give him? Curiosity was a coin you spent at your peril in this house. "The ink predates its own paper by approximately twenty years. Which would mean the entry was written before the page existed."

"Twenty years," Corvane said softly. He turned the copy over, examined the blank reverse as if the truth might be hiding there. "And whose debt does the name Winterbourne carry in this house?"

"Mine," August said. "Eleven years of service. Six remain."

"Six," Corvane agreed. He smiled then, a small dry folding of the mouth, like a ledger closing. "You see the problem. If this entry is genuine, your debt is already paid — has been paid since before you were old enough to hold a pen. And if your debt is already paid, then everything this house has collected from you for eleven years has been collected in error." He let that sit between them like a stone dropped in still water. "And error, Winterbourne, is expensive. Someone must eat the cost. Do you understand me?"

"I believe so, Guildmaster."

"I don't think you do." Corvane rose. He was not a tall man, but when he crossed to the brazier in the corner — always lit, even in summer, though the room was warm — he seemed briefly to fill it. "I think you believe you've found a thread, and that threads lead somewhere. You're a good clerk. Good clerks believe in the integrity of the record. It's why I keep you." He held the copy over the coals. "But here is what good clerks forget: the ledger serves the guild. Not the other way round. The guild is not a set of accounts. The guild is *us*. You, me, the widow who bought back her grief, the miner who sold his luck, every soul in this city who ever needed something impossible and had nothing left to trade but themselves. We are the only wall between them and chaos. Do you know what happens to a city that can buy back its dead? To a market where grief has no price? Chaos, Winterbourne. Every prayer answered, every wound undone, every hunger satisfied — and nothing owed for any of it. The ledger is mercy. Remember that."

He released the page.

It caught quickly, greedily, the way salt-parchment does, flames running up the lines of handwriting as if they were wicks. August watched their own careful transcription burn, watched the words *now and forever* curl into black lace and come apart, and felt something in their chest come apart with them, quietly, without flame.

"You'll bring me the original," Corvane said, watching the fire. Not asking. "Today. And you'll enter in the audit log that the anomaly was investigated and resolved: clerical misfiling, no substance. Your own hand. Your certification makes it binding."

"And if I find another anomaly?" The words were out before August could weigh them — a clerk's reflex, balancing an entry before considering the cost.

Corvane turned from the brazier. For a moment his eyes were not a small precise man's eyes at all; they were old, and cold, and patient as stone under snow. "Then you will bring it to me, and I will resolve it, and we will both be content. Because hear me, Winterbourne, and mark it well: curiosity is a debt like any other. It accrues interest. Most people never see the bill until it comes due, and by then—" he spread his small neat hands— "well. By then the principal has grown beyond anything they can pay." He sat back down and took up his pen. "Send in the tithe clerk on your way out. And Winterbourne."

August paused at the door.

"Your mother," Corvane said mildly, not looking up from his sums, "was a woman who asked questions too. It cost her everything she had. Learn from her arithmetic."

* * *

August delivered the original at noon, as ordered, and watched Corvane feed it to the same brazier without reading it again, and entered the false resolution in the audit log in their own certified hand, and walked home through streets gone strange to them, every familiar corner suddenly suspect, every neighbor possibly a client, possibly a debtor, possibly someone whose luck or shadow or years had passed across August's own counters. The city had always been full of transactions. August had simply never before felt like goods in transit.

Their rooms were two floors above a chandler's shop on Tanner's Lane, narrow and clean and smelling faintly of tallow through the floorboards. August lit no lamp. They sat by the window while dusk came down over Brackenholt, and the lamps of the counting house kindled one by one across the rooftops like a constellation of debts, and they tried not to think about the words *learn from her arithmetic.*

They thought about nothing else.

B. Winterbourne. Her initials were everywhere in August's life, carved into the spine of the service contract, stamped on the deed of indenture, inked into the margin of every annual renewal August had signed since childhood without reading — because children don't read, and by the time August was old enough to read the whole document, the terms had become furniture, invisible, load-bearing. Eleven years for a debt of one soul. Whose soul? Their mother's, obviously. She had died twenty years ago, when August was four, of a fever the physicians called unremarkable. Unremarkable. As if anything in a house that traded souls could be unremarkable. As if a woman could sell her soul beneath a mountain and die of an unremarkable fever, and her child could spend eleven years paying off a debt that the sale itself had already extinguished—

Unless the sale was the forgery. Unless the debt was real. Unless—

August pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. This was the trap Corvane had built for them, wasn't it? Curiosity, compounding. Every question generating two more, interest upon interest, until the whole structure of their obedient life collapsed under the weight of what they knew. He wanted them to ask these questions. He wanted them afraid. Fearful clerks brought him anomalies instead of pursuing them; fearful clerks signed false resolutions and slept badly but slept.

*Learn from her arithmetic.*

Her arithmetic had bought something. The entry said so: *release one August Winterbourne from all debt.* If the entry was true, then their mother had spent her soul — her self, her eternity, whatever a soul was in the economy of things August had spent a lifetime pricing without ever believing in — to buy her child's freedom. And August had never been free. Six more years. Six years of a debt already paid, collected by men who burned the evidence and called it mercy.

They must have fallen asleep at the window, because the room dissolved.

* * *

The dream began with salt.

Not the salt of the counters, the fine white commodity salt that clinked in measures and glittered in lamplight, but old salt, grey salt, the salt of a sea that had long ago withdrawn and left its taste behind in the stone. August stood on a road cut through rock, and the road descended, and the walls on either side rose higher than any wall raised by human hands, veined with white crystal that caught no light and gave none back and yet somehow gleamed.

Ahead, the mountain breathed.

There was no other word for it. The dark ahead swelled and contracted in a rhythm slow as geology, and with each exhalation came a wind that smelled of deep places — iron, and cold water, and something else, something sweetish and wrong, like flowers arranged on a closed casket. With each inhalation the wind reversed, drawing inward, drawing *down*, and August felt it tug at them with a patience beyond appetite, the way the tide tugs at a thing it is content to wait for.

*You are expected,* said the mountain, in no voice at all. Or perhaps in many voices, layered, the way a chord contains notes. Or perhaps — and this was the horror that woke August's heart hammering — perhaps it spoke in one voice, a woman's voice, tired and gentle and terribly familiar, a voice August had not heard in twenty years and recognized anyway, the way you recognize your own reflection in dark glass.

*"Mama?"*

The breathing paused. In the pause, the silence was total, absolute, a held note between movements — and in that silence August understood with a clarity unavailable to waking minds that the mountain was not one thing. It was a court. It was full of lamps. And the lamps were burning low, and something down there in the mirrored dark was hungry, and something else down there was waiting, and both of them knew August's name.

*B. Winterbourne,* the mountain breathed, exhaling now, the sweetness washing over August like a wave over a sandcastle. *One soul entire. Received and kept. Received and kept. Received and—*

The rhythm broke. Stuttered. Somewhere far below, a lamp guttered — August felt it the way you feel a candle go out in a distant room, a small subtraction from the sum of light — and the mountain's breathing caught, and for one instant the vast slow lungs of it seized around a sound that might have been pain or might have been laughter, and a voice that was not the mountain's at all, thin and cracked and human, cried out from the depths:

*Who's answering the prayers?*

August woke with the taste of salt thick on their tongue, so strong they could not tell if it came from the dream or from their own tears or from the air itself, which had gone wrong overnight — too cold for midsummer, frost furring the inside corners of the window glass, the chandler's shop below silent at an hour when it should have been roaring with the day's first customers.

August sat up. Wiped their face. Tasted the salt again, deliberately, and found it did not taste like the sea.

It tasted like the counting house.

Below on Tanner's Lane, a woman stood barefoot in the frost in her nightgown, arms raised to the white sky, and she was praying — August could see her lips moving, hear the fragments that reached the window — *please, please, please, I'll give anything* — and the frost on the glass crept a finger's width further toward the center of the pane, and somewhere far beneath the city, patient as tide, something breathed in.

August reached for their boots. There was work to be done at the counting house, and for the first time in eleven years, August Winterbourne intended to go there and ask questions no one had ordered them to ask.

Let the interest come due. Let the bill fall where it fell.
