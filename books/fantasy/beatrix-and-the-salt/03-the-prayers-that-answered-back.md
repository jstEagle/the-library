## 3. The Prayers That Answered Back

The shrine of Vess had been cold for nineteen years, and Ysolde had learned to measure her faith in degrees of frost.

She kept the little temple anyway — a squat stone room wedged between a chandler's shop and a tenement that smelled perpetually of boiling cabbage — because a priestess who abandoned her god's house was a priestess with nothing at all. Not even disgrace. Disgrace, at least, was a shape. It had edges. She could hold it in the dark and know herself by it.

She had been Vess's voice once. Vess the Tide-Turner, Vess of the Quiet Hour, the god who governed the small mercies: the moment a drowning swimmer's foot finds the bottom, the letter that arrives the day before the bailiffs, the fever that breaks at dawn. A humble god. A god of almosts and just-in-times. The great gods of the pantheon had their cathedrals on the hill, their gold-roofed choirs, their processions through streets strewn with petals. Vess had a stone room that smelled of tallow and cabbage, and Ysolde had loved it with the whole of her scorched heart.

Then the prayers had stopped being answered, one by one, like lamps going out along a road, and the priesthood had scattered, and the great gods' temples had absorbed the desperate the way a sponge absorbs water — without comment, without warmth. And Ysolde had been left with a cold shrine, a dead god, and a past she did not speak of, not to the chandler, not to the tenement women who left her bread, not to the mirror.

Nineteen years of cold. She knew the shrine the way a widow knows a bed.

Which is why, on the morning the warmth came back, she knew it before she opened her eyes.

* * *

She had slept in the temple, as she did three nights in seven, on a pallet behind the altar, because the tenement room she rented had a leak that dripped directly onto her pillow and a neighbor who sang hymns to the new gods at all hours, badly. She woke in the grey hour before dawn with the peculiar, disorienting sensation of having forgotten something important — and then she realized her hands were warm.

Not her body. Her *hands*. The rest of her was chilled as always — the temple held its cold like a grudge — but her palms, resting on the blanket over her stomach, were warm as hands held over a candle. She lay still and listened.

The shrine was breathing.

Not literally. She was not a madwoman, whatever the hierarchy had said in their letter of dismissal, whatever the inquest had implied with its careful, devastating phrasing — *unfit to hold the office of intercession.* But there was a quality to the air in the room, a slow tidal swell and ebb, that had not been there the night before. The cold had not lifted. It had simply... stepped aside. Made room.

Ysolde rose without lighting a lamp. She knew the shrine's every stone in the dark; she had cleaned them for nineteen years, on her knees, because no one else would, because the work was penance and penance was the only prayer she still trusted herself to make. She crossed to the altar — a plain slab of grey granite, worn concave in the center by centuries of pilgrims' hands — and laid her palm flat upon it.

Warm. Warm as a living cheek. Warm as a hand held out in the dark.

Ysolde snatched her hand back as though bitten, and stood in the dark of her dead god's house with her heart slamming against her ribs, and felt — for the first time in nineteen years — something she had no right to feel and could not stop feeling.

Hope.

It was the hope that frightened her. Dread she could carry; dread was an old acquaintance, nearly a friend. But hope was a knife someone hands you handle-first and you take it before you think, and then you own the cutting of yourself, whatever comes.

"Who's there?" she said aloud, and her voice cracked in the middle like a dropped plate.

The warmth did not answer. But it did not *leave*, either. It held, steady as a banked fire, and Ysolde stood before her god's altar in the grey light with her palm hovering an inch above the stone, not quite touching, the way one hovers a hand over a sleeping child's brow — afraid that touching might wake it, and more afraid that it was not sleeping at all.

* * *

By full morning she had done what any sensible woman would have done, which was nothing, and what any priestess would have done, which was everything.

She lit the altar candle — real beeswax, hoarded, meant for the god's return that would never come — and she knelt, and she did not pray. She had sworn off prayer the way a drunkard swears off drink: sincerely, repeatedly, and without success. But she sat before the warm stone with her hands open in her lap and let the silence do what silence does.

It was the chandler's boy who broke it, banging open the door without knocking, the way he always did, calling back over his shoulder to his mother that he was *just going to ask the priestess, she'd know* —

He was maybe eleven, all elbows and chapped lips, and he skidded to a stop when he saw her kneeling there with the candle lit, because the candle was never lit, and children always notice the wrong things first.

"Mother says — " he began, and stopped, and started again. "Mother says the fever's broken."

"Whose fever?"

"Tam's. My brother. He was dying. The physician said yesterday, the physician said *last night*, and Mother sat up all night with him, and this morning he asked for bread." The boy's voice climbed and wobbled. "Mother says you'll know. Mother says to ask what it cost."

Ysolde felt the floor tilt very slightly, the way a deck tilts.

"What did your mother do?" she asked, carefully.

"Prayed." He said it the way children say the obvious things, with a shrug. "Not to anybody particular. She said she didn't care anymore who was listening, she'd take anybody, and she put her wedding ring on the altar — not *our* altar, she hasn't got one — she went to the crossroads shrine, the little one by the tannery, all broken-down, and she put her ring there and asked. And Tam's fever broke." His eyes were wet and shining and frightened. "But Mother's gone grey. All down one side, like — like wax running. And she can't remember Tam's name. She keeps calling him *the boy*."

The boy. The boy who stood before her was not the boy in question, and his mother had already begun to lose the difference.

Ysolde rose. Her knees ached; they always ached; she barely felt them. "Take me to her."

* * *

The crossroads shrine by the tannery was one of the old ones — pre-pantheon, pre-cathedral, a knee-high stone post carved with a face so weathered it was more suggestion than features, the kind of shrine the city had stopped maintaining and started ignoring. There were fresh offerings on it. Not flowers, not coins.

*Things.* A lock of hair. A baby tooth. A wedding ring, gone from the ring finger of the woman inside, whose hand Ysolde now held and whose skin was cold as the shrine had been hot.

The mother — Marta, the chandler's wife, a broad capable woman Ysolde had known for years, whose bread was the best in the street — sat propped in bed, grey from the left temple down through the left cheek and throat, the color of ash, of candle-smoke, of the inside of an old lamp. She looked up at Ysolde with eyes that were clear and wrong, clear as glass, and smiled.

"Priestess," she said. "You'll forgive me. I've lost the word for you. I've lost a great many words." She touched her greyed cheek with wonder rather than fear. "But it worked. That's the thing nobody tells you. It *worked*."

"What worked, Marta?"

"The asking." Her glassy eyes shone. "I asked and something answered. Something old. I could feel how old it was — like putting your hand in a river that's been running since before the city. And it took the ring, and it took — " she frowned, groping, a woman patting her pockets for keys — "it took some other things. I can't recall what. But Tam's alive. Tam's alive and asking for bread, and I'd pay it again, I'd pay it twice, do you understand? I'd — " and here her voice did something terrible, it went *grateful*, it went bright with gratitude, and Ysolde had to look away — "I'd give it everything. It only asked for a little. It was *kind*."

It was kind.

Ysolde walked home through streets that had gone strange to her overnight. She stopped at three more of the old shrines, the forgotten ones, the knee-high stones in the cracks of the city, and found the same at each: fresh offerings of hair and teeth and rings, and beside them, scratched into the stone in hands that shook — *it answered, it answered, it answered.*

The old gods were answering prayers again.

The old gods had been dead or silent for nineteen years. Ysolde knew this with the certainty of a woman who had helped bury one of them. She had stood in the great temple of Vess on the hill — before her disgrace, before everything — and watched the god's presence drain out of the sanctuary like water out of a cracked bowl, and the high priest had declared it a testing, and then a mystery, and then, when the miracles stopped coming and the tithes stopped coming, had quietly closed the doors. *The god sleeps,* they said. *The god withdraws.* The priesthood said what priesthoods always say, which is whatever keeps the tithes flowing.

But Ysolde had been in the sanctuary that day. She had felt it go. She knew the difference between a sleeping god and an empty one, the way a wife knows the difference between a husband asleep and a husband gone.

And now something old was answering prayers in her city, and it was taking payment in the only coin that mattered — memory, color, years, *self* — and the people were grateful. That was the horror of it. That was the thing she turned over all night, walking, walking, until her feet ached and the lamps were lit and lit again. The people were *grateful*. A woman had paid her son's name out of her own head and called it kindness.

Something was answering. But Ysolde had served the old gods, and she knew their grammar, and this was not it. The old gods took offerings — bread, oil, the first fruits, small symbolic deaths. They did not take *pieces of the pray-er*. A god who takes pieces of the faithful is not a god. It is a creditor.

The word arrived in her mind with the weight of a stone dropped down a well, and she stood still in the lamplight at the mouth of Saltmarket Street and heard, distantly, the bells of the counting house ringing the hour.

A creditor.

* * *

She went to the Salt Guild the next morning because there was nowhere else the thread led.

She had spent the intervening day doing what she had been trained to do, in the years when she had been someone: reading signs. The greyed cheek. The lost name. The baby tooth on the shrine — a tooth that had come, Ysolde was nearly certain, from a grown mouth, pulled or pushed, a tooth the body had already replaced once and would not replace again. Payment in kind, but never in the same coin twice. She had asked careful questions at the market, in the tone of an old woman gossiping, and found the pattern everywhere once she looked: the cooper's wife who could no longer taste salt; the ferryman who had prayed for his daughter's safe crossing and now could not remember his daughter's face, only that he *had* a daughter and loved *someone*; the young clerk who'd asked for luck at cards and aged a decade in a night, his hair gone white as salt.

And always, when she asked where the desperate had gone *before* the shrines began answering, she got the same answer, delivered in the lowered voice people use for institutions that own them: the Salt Guild. The Salt Guild lends against anything. The Salt Guild bought my mother's luck. The Salt Guild holds my neighbor's shadow. The Salt Guild — said with a shiver, said with gratitude, said the way one speaks of the sea or the taxman, forces of nature, forces of *contract*.

The old gods were answering prayers, and the prayers were being paid in souls, and the Salt Guild traded in impossible things, and Ysolde had spent nineteen years learning to follow a thread through the dark without flinching at what it was tied to.

So she went. Up Saltmarket Street in the flat white light of a morning that could not decide to be warm, past the guild's facade — grey granite, like her altar, like every grave in the city, and she noted that and hated that she'd noted it — through doors of black iron banded with bronze, into a hall that smelled of paper and cold stone and, beneath it, faintly, unmistakably, of salt.

Salt. On her tongue. In her nose. The taste of a wound, of tears, of preservation — the taste of something kept from rotting past its time.

The counting house proper was a vast room of desks arranged in ranks, clerks bent over ledgers like a congregation, and the hush in it was not the hush of work but the hush of a sickroom. At the far end rose a dais and a high desk, and behind the high desk sat a figure she would learn to know as Guildmaster Corvane, though she did not know him yet, and did not need to; she knew the type. She had served under his kind in another life, in another hierarchy. He had the stillness of a man who has never once needed to raise his voice because the architecture of the world does his shouting for him.

She did not go to him. She went to the nearest desk instead, where a clerk sat with a ledger open, and this clerk — young, sharp-faced, with ink-stained fingers and eyes that had done more arithmetic than sleeping — looked up at her with the wary flatness of a person who has learned that strangers at the desk mean trouble.

"I want to make an inquiry," Ysolde said.

"Inquiries are made at the public counter. This is the audit floor."

"I'm not here about a loan." She kept her voice low, level, the voice she had once used on the temple steps to talk the desperate down from their worst bargains. "I'm here about prayers."

Something crossed the clerk's face — quick, quickly mastered, but not quickly enough. Not confusion. *Recognition.*

"I don't know what you mean."

"I think you do." Ysolde set her hand flat on the ledger, and the clerk went rigid, and she felt it then, through her palm, through the paper — the faintest warmth, the same tidal warmth as the altar stone, as the crossroads shrine, as the greyed cheek of the chandler's wife. The ledgers were *warm*. "Something in this city has started answering prayers, and it's taking payment in memory and years and names, and your books are warm as a sickbed, and you're going to tell me what you're trading."

The clerk — August Winterbourne, though she would not learn the name until later, would not learn it until it mattered more than she could then imagine — looked at her hand on the ledger. Looked at her face. And Ysolde watched the war happen behind those arithmetic eyes: the trained denial rising against something older and truer, the loyal servant's reflex against the first crack of doubt.

"I balance entries," the clerk said at last, quietly. "I don't make them."

"Then you'll want to know who does." Ysolde leaned closer, and dropped her voice to almost nothing, and gave the clerk the only gift she had left to give, which was the truth, unwrapped: "Because I buried a god, clerk. Nineteen years ago I stood in a sanctuary and watched one die, and what's answering prayers in this city is wearing the old gods like a stolen coat. And whatever is wearing them is being *fed* — by someone, from somewhere — and every road I follow ends at this building."

She straightened. Around them the clerks bent and scratched, the hush held, the Guildmaster on his dais did not look up — or did not appear to.

"I'll find it with you or without you," Ysolde said. "But it'll go easier on your soul — what's left of it — if it's with me."

She left then, before the clerk could answer, because the answer was already in the clerk's face, and she had learned long ago that the first crack is not a thing you can argue open. You can only walk away and trust the cold to do its work.

Behind her, in the warm and salt-smelling silence of the counting house, August Winterbourne sat very still, one hand resting on a ledger that should have been cold, and did not write a single entry for a long, long time.
