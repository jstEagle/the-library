## 4. Terms of Service

The counting house of the Salt Guild smelled the way all important places smell: of tallow, cold stone, and money that had never been touched by human hands. August Winterbourne arrived at the seventh bell, as they had for eleven years, hung their coat on the peg that bore their name in faded ink, and sat down at the desk that faced the wall rather than the window — junior clerks faced walls; only the senior ones were permitted views, as if the sight of the sky might make a person careless with figures.

They opened the ledger marked *Q–through–R* and dipped their pen.

The work was simple if you did not think about it. That was the first thing they taught you in the guild's back rooms, before they ever let you near the books: *do not think about it.* You recorded that Mrs. Halvern of Cooper's Row had traded three years of her own springtime for a son's recovery from fever, and you wrote it in the trade column and the payment column, and you balanced the line with a rule straight as a horizon, and you did not think about what three years of someone's springtime looked like when it came out of them. You recorded that a cooper named Pell had sold his shadow — it had been repossessed at dawn, August understood, by men who wore grey and carried sacks that made no sound — and you noted that his credit balance stood at four marks, eleven shillings, which would not buy the shadow back but would keep the repossession men from returning for anything else. You did not think about Pell walking crooked through the market, flinching from noon.

August was very good at not thinking about it. They had practiced for eleven years, since the age of nine, when Guildmaster Corvane had set a ledger in front of them and said, *Your mother left you to us, and we honor her debts, and you will honor yours*, and August — small, motherless, already fluent in silence — had picked up the pen.

So when the door of the counting house opened at half past the eighth bell and a woman walked in wearing a priestess's collar with the sigil scratched out of it, August did what they always did: they thought about the ledger instead.

"We're closed to petitions until ten," they said. "Trades are conducted upstairs. This floor is audit only."

"I'm not here to trade." The woman's voice had gravel in it, the kind that accumulates when a person has spent years talking to people who don't listen. She crossed the counting floor without asking leave, and August noticed things about her in the way clerks notice — categorically, uselessly. Boots resoled twice. A burn scar along one wrist, old. Eyes the grey-brown of wet slate, moving over the room like she was counting exits. "I'm here to ask questions."

"Questions aren't traded here."

"Everything's traded here. That's what I've come to find out." She stopped at August's desk and put both hands flat on it, and August saw that her knuckles were scarred too, in the particular way knuckles get when a person knocks on doors for a living. Holy work. Begging work. The two were often the same. "Three nights ago, a dying woman on Candler's Lane prayed at my shrine — a dead shrine, mind, a shrine I keep out of habit because my god is dust — and the prayer was answered. Her pain lifted like smoke. I watched it go. And yesterday her daughter came to me weeping because the woman had forgotten every soul she ever loved. Every one. Pain gone, love gone. So." She leaned closer. August could smell salt and candle wax on her. "I followed the answer back to its source, and its source is this house. What is the Salt Guild trading?"

The correct answer — the trained answer — rose in August like water finding a pipe: *I'm a junior clerk. I record entries. What the entries mean is above my station.*

What they said instead, after a pause one heartbeat too long, was: "Nothing of that kind."

The priestess's eyes narrowed. "Of what kind?"

"Nothing." August kept their gaze on the ledger, on the beautiful ruled line beneath Mrs. Halvern's entry. "We deal in surety and collateral. Debts, mostly. Ordinary debts."

"Ordinary debts." She said it slowly, tasting it, the way you'd taste soup you suspected of being poisoned. "Then you won't mind showing me your books."

"Mind?" August heard their own voice go flat and precise, and recognized the tone as Corvane's, borrowed like an ill-fitting coat. "Madam, these ledgers contain the private financial affairs of every client of this guild. If I showed them to you, I'd be liable. If I didn't show them to you, I'd be liable for less. It's simple arithmetic."

For a moment he thought she would strike him. He saw the decision happen behind her eyes — see it gather in her shoulders and then, deliberately, be put down, like a heavy load set on a loading dock. Instead she straightened up and looked around the counting house again, at the rows of desks, the high dark shelves of ledgers climbing toward the ceiling like a library built by misers, the brass cage where the strongroom began.

"What's your name?" she asked.

"August Winterbourne."

"Winterbourne." Something moved across her face — not recognition, exactly. Recognition's shadow. "And do you believe in anything, August Winterbourne? Anything at all?"

"I believe in balanced columns."

"Yes," she said softly. "That's what frightens me." She turned toward the door, then stopped, hand on the frame, and spoke without looking back. "My name is Ysolde. I was a priestess of Meren, who kept the hearth-lamps, who is dead these twenty years and more. And I am telling you that whatever your guild is selling answers to prayers, and the price of those answers is the inside of a person. When you lie awake tonight — and you will — ask yourself whether you're the kind of clerk who balances columns, or the kind who empties them."

She went out. The door closed on the ordinary noise of the street, carts and gulls and someone crying fish, and the counting house settled back into its tallow-scented hush.

August looked down at the ledger.

Their hands were steady. Eleven years of training held the line, rule-straight. *Do not think about it.* They recorded a trade of six months' good fortune from a ferryman for relief of a gambling debt. They balanced the column. They did not think about it.

They thought about almost nothing else all day.

* * *

Corvane found them at dusk, as August was snuffing the lamps.

"You spoke with a visitor today." The Guildmaster's voice carried no accusation, which was how you knew there was one. Corvane was a narrow man, dry as a page, with white hair combed with mathematical exactness and hands that never gestured. He had been old when August arrived and had not aged so much as accumulated, like a ledger adding entries. "A priestess. Disgraced, I'm told. From the cult of the lamp-god."

"Meren," August said. "She asked questions. I told her nothing."

"Mm." Corvane drew off his gloves, finger by finger. In the last light through the tall windows his face looked like something excavated. "You told her nothing. But you considered telling her something. That's visible, August. In people, consideration is visible — the way hesitation is visible in handwriting. Do you know what curiosity is, in our business?"

"A debt," August said, reciting. "With interest."

"With compound interest, compounding silently, invisible on any statement until called due." Corvane replaced the gloves. "Your position is a good one. Stable. Your accounts are clean. Cleanliness is rare and valuable. I would hate to see a stranger's questions dirty them." He paused at the door. "The woman will come back. When she does, send for me. Not the wardens. Me."

When he had gone, August stood alone in the darkened counting house among eleven thousand entries and felt, for the first time in eleven years, the full weight of the phrase *do not think about it* — felt it as a thing with mass, a lid on a well, and beneath the lid, faint and rising, the sound of water.

They should have gone home. They had a room on Tanner's Court, a kettle, a bed, a life assembled entirely of habits. Instead August lit one lamp, carried it into the archive stacks, and pulled the master cross-reference from its shelf.

It was against protocol. It was, in fact, precisely the debt Corvane had just named. August knew this the way you know the stove is hot, and reached anyway.

The cross-reference was the guild's true memory — every entry duplicated in miniature, indexed by client, by commodity, by year. Most commodities were what a stranger might generously call ordinary: luck, years, health, grief, courage, the memory of a first kiss (purchased repeatedly, by one client, forty times). August ran a finger down the commodity index to *S.*

*Sight. Shadows. Silence. Sleep. Souls.*

Souls. There were sub-entries. *Souls — pledged. Souls — purchased, distressed. Souls — sold onward.*

Sold onward. August turned to the index of destinations, expecting the usual: private buyers, the Colleges, the auction houses of the capital. But the destination column held one repeated notation, in a cramped antique hand:

*To the court beneath the mountain. To the court beneath the mountain. To the court beneath the mountain.*

Eleven times.

Eleven entries across nineteen years, each dated, each witnessed — every witness mark initialled *V.C.* Vincent Corvane. Each sale recorded the same way: *One soul, provenance various, condition various, delivered by the salt road, receipt confirmed.*

Receipt confirmed. Someone below signed for them.

August's finger had gone cold. They made themselves read each entry, because reading entries was what they did, because the discipline of it was the only handrail left. A fiddler from Harrowgate, 1889. A child — *condition: unclaimed* — no name given, 1891. A soldier. A midwife. An old man who had sold his soul to pay for his granddaughter's apprenticeship, and the guild had taken the payment literally.

And then, tenth from the top, dated two years before August's birth, the entry that stopped the finger dead:

*Winterbourne, B. One soul, provenance: owner. Consideration received: see contract 114. Delivered to the court beneath the mountain. Receipt confirmed.*

B. Winterbourne.

August stood very still in the archive, and the lamp flame stood very still, and the whole building seemed to lean inward to listen.

Their mother's initial. Their mother's name reduced to a letter and a surname, filed between a fiddler and a child, in the same cramped hand, under the same destination. *Delivered.* Not pawned, not pledged — delivered, the way goods are delivered. Receipted.

*See contract 114.*

August knew contract numbers the way priests know psalms. Contract 114 lived in the strongroom, in the sealed cabinet, in the dark. Their own service-contract — the one Corvane had set before a nine-year-old — bore the number 115, inked in the corner like an afterthought. August had seen those numbers side by side for eleven years and never once asked what 114 was. You did not think about it.

But 114 was referenced here. 114 was the *consideration received*. Whatever their mother had been paid, whatever she had traded her soul for, it was written in that contract — and their eleven years of service, their entire orderly life, was chained to it somewhere below the arithmetic.

The lamp guttered. Down in the counting house a clock struck the hour, and August counted the strokes automatically, helplessly — ten, eleven — and understood that they had crossed some border tonight that appeared on no map. Ysolde's voice, or something like it, was speaking in the back of their skull: *ask yourself whether you're the kind of clerk who balances columns, or the kind who empties them.*

Eleven souls. Their mother's among them. Carried down the salt road, receipted, shelved — where? In what dark? For whom?

August closed the cross-reference with hands that were, finally, not quite steady, and stood in the aisle between the shelves with the lamp trembling in their grip. Tomorrow they would report to their desk. Tomorrow they would balance columns. But tomorrow night —

Tomorrow night they would open the strongroom cabinet, and read contract 114, and learn what their mother had bought with everything she was.

Above the city, far to the north, snow was falling in midsummer. August did not know that yet. They knew only that the ledger had shown them its other face, and that the entry in their mother's hand from the week before — the anomaly Corvane burned — and this entry, *B. Winterbourne, delivered*, were the same story's beginning and end.

And somewhere beneath the mountain, something had signed for her.
