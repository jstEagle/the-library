## 5. The Debt Inherited

The contract was kept in the deep vault, and the deep vault was kept cold.

August had descended into it perhaps a dozen times in eleven years — always with a writ, always with a witness, always with the particular hush that the guild demanded of anyone handling original instruments. The stairs went down farther than the counting house had floors above them, which August had long ago decided not to think about too carefully. The walls wept salt. Not metaphorically; the stone itself exuded a fine crystalline sweat that glittered in the lamplight like frost, and the older clerks said it was the building remembering the sea that had been here before the city, before the guild, before any of it. August had always assumed this was clerk's folklore, the kind of story people told to make a cold job feel romantic.

Tonight, with the burned page still ghosting behind their eyes — *B. Winterbourne, one soul, to the court beneath the mountain* — the sweating walls felt less like folklore and more like warning.

They carried a lamp and a writ of inspection signed by their own hand. That was the trick of it, the small ugly joke of the guild: August had authority enough to audit anything in the archive except the thing they most needed to audit, because every instrument touching the Winterbourne debt required a countersignature from a senior factor, and every senior factor reported to Corvane, and Corvane burned things.

But there were two ledgers in the Salt Guild, and only one of them was paper.

August had spent three nights reconstructing the second ledger from memory: dates of entry, lot numbers, the particular way the vault clerks shelved instruments by decade rather than by debtor name. The guild believed memory could be managed — that was what the oaths were for, that was why curiosity was called a debt with interest. But memory was the one ledger no master copy controlled. August had been balancing impossible entries for eleven years. They knew how to find a number someone wanted hidden. You didn't look where it should be. You looked for the shape of its absence.

Lot 4471 sat in the third row of the twenty-year shelf, wedged spine-inward between two estate settlements so dull that no clerk had pulled them in a generation. The absence-shape had been easy to find once August stopped looking for *Winterbourne* and started looking for a folder that broke the pattern — one finger-width thicker than its neighbors, its wax seal unbroken, its dust disturbed by nothing at all.

Unbroken seal. Twenty years.

August stood in the cold with the lamp raised and understood, in the way one understands a fall only after the ground is already rising up, that everything they were about to learn had been sitting one shelf away for their entire working life.

They broke the seal.

* * *

The instrument was short. That was the first shock. August had expected — had braced for — the sprawling legalese of a major soul-contract, the kind that ran forty pages and required two witnesses and a blood signature. Their mother's contract fit on a single leaf, front and back, in a clerk's hand so neat it looked printed.

**INSTRUMENT OF DEBT AND SERVICE**

**Debtor:** Beatrix Winterbourne, of Vell Street, salt-merchant's widow.

**Creditor:** The Honourable Company of Salt, its factors and assigns forever.

**Consideration received:** One (1) soul, whole and undivided, conveyed by the Debtor of her free will.

**Terms of repayment:** Eleven (11) years of service in the counting house of the Creditor, to be rendered by the Debtor or her lawful issue, commencing upon the Debtor's death or incapacity.

**Collateral:** The person of the Debtor's unborn child.

August read the word *unborn* four times. The lamp flame did not waver. The vault did not lurch. The world simply continued, indifferent and cold, while August stood holding a piece of paper that explained the entire architecture of their life.

Eleven years. They had served exactly eleven years. They had always been told — by the orphan-mistress, by the intake clerks, by the bland machinery of the guild's charity — that the term was a coincidence of arithmetic, an inheritance settled at some fixed sum, worked off at the standard rate. Debts came down from parents all the time in this city; that was half of what the guild was *for*. August had never once thought to ask what the original sum had been. Children don't audit their own origins. Children are grateful to be fed.

But the consideration was written right there. One soul, whole and undivided. And the terms — *to be rendered by the Debtor or her lawful issue* — meant the service had never been about repaying money or labor at all. You cannot repay a soul with years. Everyone in the trade knew that; it was the first lesson of the counting house, drilled into apprentices before they were allowed to touch a real ledger: *a soul has no equivalent*. Every other entry August had ever balanced — the borrowed luck, the sold shadows, the widow's grief — had a conversion table, however cruel. Souls alone were marked **non-transferable, non-redeemable**, and the trades that involved them were marked with the black ribbon that meant *held in perpetuity*.

Except this one. This one had been given a term. Eleven years of clerical work in exchange for a human soul — priced, valued, amortized, as if it were a shipment of salt.

Which meant the contract was wrong. Not morally wrong — the guild did moral wrong the way other businesses did shipping errors, routinely and without comment — but *arithmetically* wrong. Structurally wrong. Someone had drafted an instrument that violated the guild's own first principle, and the guild had accepted it, and August had served it, year after year, without ever asking the question that would have unraveled it in an afternoon:

*What kind of creditor accepts a payment that can't cover the debt?*

Unless the years weren't the payment. Unless the years were something else entirely — a leash, a delay, a way of keeping the collateral close until it matured. August looked again at the collateral clause and felt the floor of their life tilt gently, the way a ship tilts when you finally notice the horizon moving.

*The person of the Debtor's unborn child.*

Not the child's labor. Not the child's future earnings. The child's *person*. August had spent eleven years as interest accruing on themselves.

And the date. August made themselves read the date, though their eyes wanted to slide off it. The instrument was executed nine months before August's birth. Their mother had walked into the counting house pregnant, alone, a widow, and had sold the only thing she owned that the guild considered pricelessly liquid — and had bought, with it, nothing for herself. No annuity. No protection clause. No provision for the child beyond the barest legal existence as collateral. Whatever she'd received in exchange, it wasn't written here. The consideration ran one direction only: soul out, nothing back but a signature.

August's hands were steady. They noticed this distantly, the way one notices weather in another country. Eleven years of the guild had trained their body well: a clerk does not tremble over an entry, however terrible, until the entry is balanced. And this entry refused to balance. It refused magnificently. It refused with the clean, ringing clarity of a false coin dropped on marble.

There was a second page. August turned it with a thumb gone numb from the cold.

It was a rider, appended in a different hand — a hand August recognized with a jolt that had nothing to do with the temperature. Heavy strokes, ink pressed hard enough to emboss the paper. Guildmaster Corvane's hand, from twenty years ago, when he had been a senior factor with a reputation for closing difficult accounts.

**Rider to Instrument 4471:** At the request of the undersigned factor, the term of service herein shall be administered with discretion. The Debtor's issue shall not be informed of the nature of the Consideration, the Collateral, or this Instrument, during the term or after. Any disclosure shall constitute breach, with penalties per Schedule K.

*Shall not be informed.* Twenty years of orphanage bread and guild schooling and patient, gray, competent employment — all of it built on a rider in Corvane's hand ensuring August would never see this page. He hadn't merely held the contract. He had *designed the silence around it*, the way an architect designs a wall.

And yet here it was, unsealed, uncountersigned, shelved where a diligent auditor following the pattern of absences would inevitably find it.

August stood very still in the cold and let that last fact arrive.

Corvane had burned the ledger page. Corvane had warned them off with talk of interest. And then he had left the original instrument — the one document in the guild that could destroy him — sitting on an open shelf behind a broken seal, in a vault August had clearance to enter.

Either Corvane had grown careless in his old age.

Or August was being *shown*.

* * *

The stairs seemed longer coming up. August counted them out of habit — ninety-one, the same as always — and the counting helped, the way counting always helped, giving the mind a gutter to run in while the rest of the person reeled.

The counting house above was nearly empty at this hour, the day-shift gone, the lamps turned low, the great room full of that particular dusk-silence of desks and closed books. August crossed it toward the stair to the clerks' quarters with the instrument folded inside their coat, against their chest, where they could feel its small stiff heartbeat of paper with every step. They had a plan, loosely: their room, the door barred, the document copied twice, the copies hidden in two places, the original —

"August."

Corvane's voice, from the direction of his office, mild as a man remarking on the weather.

August stopped. For one absurd moment they considered pretending not to hear, walking on, claiming deafness tomorrow — but the guildmaster's door was already open, and he was standing in it with a candle, and Corvane did not repeat himself. That was one of his known qualities. He never repeated himself, because repetition implied doubt, and doubt was the one currency the guild did not trade in.

"In my office," he said. "You'll want to sit."

* * *

The office smelled of tallow and old vellum and, faintly, of the ash he was forever generating. Corvane poured two measures of plum brandy without asking — another known quality; he performed generosity the way other men performed threats — and set one glass in front of August, who did not touch it. The folded instrument pressed against their ribs like a second heart.

"You went to the vault tonight," Corvane said. "Writ 8834, your own signature. Lot 4471." He said it the way he said everything, in the flat tone of a man reading a column of figures. "I know because I signed the vault log behind you. I sign it behind everyone. It's the only page in this building I still keep by hand."

"You knew I'd find it." August's voice came out steadier than they felt. "The seal was unbroken. The file was—"

"Left where your particular mind would find it, yes." Corvane sat down behind his desk, and for a moment the candlelight did something unkind to his face, hollowing the cheeks, deepening the eye sockets, so that he looked briefly like a skull wearing a guildmaster's collar. Then he moved, and was himself again: sixty-ish, iron-gray, immaculate, tired in the way of men who have been awake for decades. "Sit down, August. Please."

August sat. They did not drink.

"Do you know why I burned that page?" Corvane asked. "The duplicate entry. B. Winterbourne, one soul, court beneath the mountain."

"Because it was an anomaly."

"Because it was *true*," Corvane said, "and true pages in my house have a way of growing legs." He laced his fingers on the desk. "Your mother's soul was conveyed to the court. That is not a metaphor, and it is not guild folklore, and I am going to say the next part plainly because you've earned plain speech: the court beneath the mountain holds your mother's soul, and the contract you just read means that whoever holds her debt holds *you*. Your eleven years were never a repayment. They were a custodial arrangement. We were keeping you — intact, educated, unattached — until such time as the holder of the underlying instrument chose to collect."

The brandy sat untouched between them, catching the light like a small amber lamp.

"And now," Corvane continued, "the prayers have started answering again. Which means the court is awake, and awake courts collect. So." He slid a paper across the desk. August looked down at it automatically — clerk's reflex — and saw their own name at the top of a letter of appointment. *Senior Factor. Full access. A seat at the table where the guild decides what the miraculous costs.*

"A promotion," August said.

"An advancement," Corvane corrected. "Senior factors are told things. What the court is. Why the winter is starting in midsummer. Where the souls go and what comes back in their place. You've spent eleven years balancing entries for impossible things; I'm offering you the chance to stop being furniture and start being a factor." He leaned back. "In exchange for one thing. Discretion. You found what you found tonight, and from this hour forward, you found nothing. Lot 4471 stays sealed in your memory the way it was sealed in wax. You serve, you advance, and in five years you'll be sitting where I'm sitting, deciding who learns the truth and who burns it."

"And if I refuse?"

Corvane smiled. It was the worst thing August had seen all night, worse than the word *unborn*, worse than the cold of the vault — because it was gentle.

"If you refuse," he said, "nothing happens tonight. Nothing happens tomorrow. That's the cruelty of contracts, August — they don't punish refusal. They just wait. Somewhere under that mountain there is an account with your family name on it, and the court is waking up hungry, and the only question worth asking in this whole city is whether, when it calls in its debts, you're standing behind my desk or in front of it." He tapped the appointment letter once, softly. "Think of it as choosing your shelf."

August rose. The instrument crackled faintly against their chest, and Corvane's eyes flicked to the sound — a flicker, no more — and August understood that he knew, that he had probably always known, that the broken seal and the open shelf and this whole evening might be nothing more than a man checking whether his collateral had learned to read the fine print.

"I'll consider it," August said.

"Of course," said Corvane. "Take the brandy. You'll sleep better."

August took the glass, because refusing would be information, and drank it in the doorway without tasting it. Behind them, Corvane began tidying his desk, unhurried, a man with all the time in the world — or a man who knew precisely how little time there was.

In the corridor, in the dark, August pressed their hand flat against the folded contract over their heart and made themselves think like a clerk instead of a child.

A forged term. A designed silence. An offer of complicity dressed as a career. Three entries, and none of them balanced — unless there was a fourth entry August couldn't see yet, something Corvane feared more than exposure, something that had made him leave the one damning document where a curious clerk would find it.

Outside the counting house windows, far off toward the mountain, the summer sky was doing something wrong. The stars were out, but the air had begun, very faintly, to smell of snow.

August buttoned their coat over the contract and went to find out who else in this city was asking questions about souls — because somewhere, someone had prayed to a dead god recently, and the god had answered, and answers like that left trails a good auditor could follow.
