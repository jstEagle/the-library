## 6. A God With No Name

Ysolde had learned to read silence the way other people learned to read scripture.

There was the silence of a shrine that had never held anything — the flat quiet of bare stone and cold ash, honest in its emptiness. There was the silence of a god recently dead, which was different: a hush with weight to it, like snow on a roof, like a room where someone has just stopped breathing. She had lived inside that second kind for nine years. She knew its grain, its temperature, the way it pressed against the eardrum.

And there was this — the silence that came off the beggar by the salt docks at low tide, when the wind turned and carried the brine up from the flats — and it stopped her mid-stride as though she had walked into glass.

"You feel it too," August said beside her. They had come down to the docks because August's ledgers had led them here — eleven entries of souls sold *to the court beneath the mountain*, and every one of them routed through the salt warehouses, through weights and measures and the small gray arithmetic of commerce. Ysolde had insisted on coming. The guild would not show her its books; she had thought perhaps she could read something older instead. "It's just an old man."

"No," Ysolde said. Her mouth had gone dry. The salt air tasted of copper. "No, it is very much not."

The beggar sat where the cobbles gave way to the shingle, beneath the leaning rib of an overturned skiff, wrapped in a coat so crusted with salt it might have been quarried rather than sewn. He was ancient in the way driftwood is ancient — bleached, hollowed, polished by weather into something that no longer resembled what it had grown from. His hands lay open on his knees, palms up, in the posture of receiving. Or begging. Or offering. With gods, Ysolde thought, the three gestures were always the same gesture.

He was not looking at them. He was looking out at the water with an expression she recognized with a violence that nearly dropped her to her knees.

She had seen it on her own face, once, in a temple mirror the morning after her god died. It was the look of a thing that had been listening for so long that listening had become its whole shape — and now heard nothing, and did not know how to be anything else.

"August," she said quietly. "Step back. Slowly."

"Why? What is he?"

"He isn't a *he*." She moved forward instead, her boots crunching on shell and gray stone, because nine years of disgrace had taught her one discipline above all others: you did not turn your back on a god, even a broken one. Especially a broken one. "That is an old god wearing a man the way a man wears a coat two sizes too large. And it has been wearing him for a long, long time."

The beggar's head turned. Not quickly — nothing about him was quick — but with the terrible patience of tide. His eyes were the color of the sea before a storm makes up its mind, and they were empty in a way that no human eyes are ever quite empty. Even the dying look *out*. These eyes looked nowhere. They were windows painted shut.

"Alms," he said. His voice was gravel rolled smooth by centuries. "Alms for the road."

"Road to where?" Ysolde asked, and knelt.

The question hung between them. For a moment nothing happened, and then the beggar's face did something worse than weep or rage: it *searched*. The emptiness behind his eyes churned, groping for an answer the way a drowning man grops for light on the surface of black water. His cracked lips parted. His hands turned over in his lap.

"I don't—" he began, and stopped, and his face collapsed inward like a roof giving up its rafters. "I don't remember. I don't remember the road. I don't remember—"

His hands flew to his temples. The sound he made was not a word. It was older than words, and Ysolde had heard it before, once, in the last hour of her god's life — the sound a divinity makes when it understands, all at once and forever, what it has lost.

She caught him as he swayed. He weighed almost nothing, as though most of him had already been sold off, parcel by parcel, and only this thin remainder still bothered to sit upright in the world.

"Hush," she said, and was astonished to hear how gentle her voice came out. She had thought that part of herself burned away with her ordination papers. "Hush. You're safe. Nothing is asking anything of you right now. Nothing is owed."

"*Owed,*" the god repeated, and laughed — a dreadful, rusted sound. "Everything is owed. Everything is owed and I cannot find the book." Its fingers closed around her wrist with sudden desperate strength. "I had a book. I had— I had a name. Someone took my name and now I can't find the door. I stand outside my own door and knock and knock and no one answers because the one who answers is me, and I've forgotten how."

Behind her, she heard August's breath catch. When she glanced back, the clerk had gone white as the salt crusts on the pilings.

"The ledger," August said. "In the counting house. There's an entry I couldn't reconcile — I thought it was a transcription error. *One name, sold.* No buyer listed. No price listed. Just — sold. I assumed it was shorthand for something else." Their voice had gone thin. "Gods' names have a market value?"

"Names are the oldest currency there is," Ysolde said. "Before coin, before salt, before blood. A name is the proof that a thing is itself. Sell a god's name and you haven't robbed it — you've unmade its claim on existence. It can't pray to itself. It can't answer prayers. It can't even know what it's missing." She looked back down at the creature in her arms, this vast diminished thing, and felt grief rise in her throat like floodwater. "Only the guild could broker such a sale. Only the guild would dare."

*And only the guild,* she thought, *would know what a stolen name is worth.*

Because she knew — she had always known, since the night her own god died with its name still intact and its heart broken anyway — that a nameless god was not a dead god. It was worse. It was a god locked outside its own divinity, starving in view of a feast it could no longer recognize. Dead gods went silent. Nameless gods went hungry forever.

And this one — she could feel it now, kneeling in the shingle with her arms full of salt-crusted ruin — this one was *starving*.

* * *

They got him to the boathouse at the end of the derelict quay, the one the fisherfolk had abandoned when the winter catches failed, and Ysolde built a fire in the old iron stove while August stood in the doorway watching the god the way a clerk watches an entry that refuses to balance.

The god sat where they put him, hands open on his knees again, and stared into the flames. After a while he began, very softly, to hum. It took Ysolde a long moment to place the melody, and when she did, her eyes stung: it was a liturgy. One of the old ones, from before the schisms, from before the guild. A song of offering. Gods sang it over their sacrifices; priests sang it over their gods. He was singing the office of worship to himself, because he had forgotten everything except the shape of devotion, and devotion needs somewhere to go.

"He doesn't know what he is," August said. It wasn't quite a question.

"He knows less than that. He knows that he lacks. Hunger without an object. That's what's left when you take the name away." Ysolde fed a split log to the stove. Firelight moved across the god's face and found no purchase there; it slid off like rain off oiled wood. "When my god died — the god I served, the one whose shrine you saw me weeping over if you were spying on me as thoroughly as I suspect — when he died, the first thing that went was the sound. Do you understand? Not the miracles, not the presence. The *sound*. For nine years I have walked past shrines and heard the silence of a dead god, and I know that silence the way you know your mother's voice."

Something flickered across August's face at the phrase — *your mother's voice* — some private flinch, quickly mastered. Ysolde filed it away. Everyone who came near the Salt Guild carried a wound somewhere; it was practically a condition of employment.

"And this?" August nodded at the god.

"This is the same silence," she said. "Wearing a body. That's why I stopped on the docks. Grief leaves a signature. Whoever killed my god and whoever emptied this one used the same hand — or the same knife. Or the same *ledger*." She let that last word fall into the room like a stone into a well, and watched August's jaw tighten. "Your masters have been doing more than keeping accounts, clerk."

"Don't call me that."

"It's what you are."

"It's what I was *sold as*," August snapped, and then looked stricken, as though the truth had gotten loose before they meant to free it.

The god's humming faltered. Both of them went still. Then, slowly, the ruined face turned toward August, and those painted-shut eyes opened — truly opened, for the first time — and fixed on the young clerk with a focus that made the hair on Ysolde's arms lift.

"You," the god said. "You smell of paper. You smell of the counting house. Paper and salt and — " its nostrils flared, a grotesque, animal motion — "*debt.*"

"I work for the guild," August said carefully. "I keep their ledgers."

"Ledgers." The god tasted the word. Something moved behind its eyes — not memory, but the ghost of memory, the dent left in a cushion long after the person has risen. "I signed something. Once. Didn't I. I signed something and now I can't sign anything, can't say anything, can't *be* anything—" Its hands began to shake. "Why would I sign? Who signs away their own—"

"It gets easier to imagine than you'd think," Ysolde said quietly. "Desperation writes excellent contracts."

The god looked at her then, and for a heartbeat something surfaced in its face that was neither hunger nor confusion — something startled and naked and terribly young. Recognition, maybe. Or the ache where recognition should be.

"You knew one of us," it said. "You smell of ash and grief and — him. You smell of a god. A dead one. But not dead-dead." Its head tilted. "Dead the other way."

"There's another way?"

"Gods die twice," the god said, with the flat certainty of someone reciting something they cannot remember learning. "Once when they stop, and once when they're forgotten. The first death is quiet. The second is loud. Yours went quiet." It frowned, groping. "I think I went loud. I think there was shouting. I think there was a table, and ink, and men with clean hands, and they asked me to sign and I — I was so *hungry*, even then, and they said signing would feed me, they said—"

Its voice broke apart. It put its face in its salt-white hands and made no sound at all, and somehow the silence was worse than weeping.

Ysolde rose and crossed to it and sat down beside it on the rotting fish-boxes, close enough that their shoulders touched, and did not say anything, because there was nothing in any liturgy she had ever memorized that fit this. Beside them, after a long moment, August sat down too, on the god's other side, stiff as a plank, a person who had clearly never comforted anyone in their life and was determined to try anyway. The god leaned into them both, a little, the way a cold man leans toward any fire.

*This is madness,* Ysolde thought. *Sitting shoulder to shoulder with a nameless god in a derelict boathouse, planning — what? What are we planning?*

But she knew what she was planning. She had known since the docks. Because the silence pouring off this broken creature matched note for note the silence of her dead god — and if the same hand had done both, then following this hand backward, through the ledgers, through the salt, down whatever road it had traveled, might lead her to the thing she had wanted for nine years and never dared name:

Someone to blame. Someone to make it *untrue*.

* * *

The tide came in as the light died. Through the boathouse's split boards they could hear the water working at the pilings, patient as a creditor.

The god slept — actually slept, a fitful, animal sleep, its breathing ragged — and in the firelight Ysolde studied it and tried to guess what it had been. Something of the sea, surely; the salt had chosen it, clung to it, honored it. Something of thresholds and roads, given its begging. An old one, older than the city, maybe older than the mountains inland. Gods that old did not sell themselves cheaply. Whatever the guild had paid, it had paid in the only coin that mattered.

"They'll know we found him," August said suddenly. Ysolde turned. The clerk was staring into the fire, arms wrapped around their knees. "Corvane. He burns pages, Ysolde. He burned my mother's handwriting out of a ledger rather than let me ask one question about it. If he learns we've located a god the guild bought and stripped — if he learns we've *spoken* to it—"

"Then we don't tell him."

"You don't understand what they are. The guild doesn't punish, exactly. Punishment ends. They *collect*. They wait until you owe them something — and everyone owes them something, eventually, that's the whole architecture of the place — and then they take it, with interest, and the taking is always legal. Always witnessed. Always *signed*." August's laugh came out bitter and small. "I have spent eleven years thinking I understood what debt was. Sitting next to that creature, I realize I don't understand anything. It sold its *name*, Ysolde. What do you have to be down to, to sign that contract? And what does it mean that the guild had the pen ready?"

Ysolde fed the fire another piece of driftwood. Sparks climbed toward the hole in the roof, toward the first stars.

"It means they're not brokers," she said. "It means they're harvesters. Your ledgers aren't records, August. They're *crops*."

The words settled over the boathouse. In its sleep, the god stirred, and its lips moved, shaping sounds without voice — and Ysolde leaned close, listening, and caught fragments. *Salt. Road. Door. Feed.* And once, unmistakably, twice repeated like a refrain worn smooth by endless repetition:

*"Beatrix. Beatrix."*

Her head came up. Across the fire, she saw that August had gone rigid, all the color gone from their face, staring at the sleeping god as though it had reached into their chest and taken hold.

"What did it say?" she asked carefully.

"Nothing." August's voice was a thread. "It said nothing. It's a mad old beggar talking in its sleep."

"August. It said a name."

"It said *nothing*." But August's hands were shaking as they pulled their coat tighter, and they would not meet her eyes, and Ysolde — who had spent a lifetime reading silences — read this one perfectly.

The god knew that name. And whatever it meant, August Winterbourne would rather walk back into the sea than explain it tonight.

Outside, the tide kept rising, cold and patient, and far inland — though none of them could know it yet — the first unnatural snow of the long summer's winter was beginning to gather itself above the mountain, waiting for the world below to finish owing.
