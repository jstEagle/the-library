## 7. What the Widow Bought

The entry was small enough to miss, which was how August knew it mattered.

Small entries were where the guild hid its sins. The great trades — a baron's twenty years of vigor, a merchant's borrowed luck at dice — those were written large and proud, notarized twice, sealed in wax the color of dried blood. But the little ones, the widow's mites and orphan's coppers of the soul-trade, they were tucked into the margins like shame into a pocket. Entry 4,417: *One (1) final utterance, purchased posthumously from the estate of Aldous Vey, delivered to Maren Vey, widow. Price: her singing voice, in perpetuity.*

August had balanced that column three days ago and thought nothing of it. People sold strange things to the Salt Guild. Grief itself had a going rate; August had priced it often enough. A widow buying back her husband's last words was almost tender, by guild standards. Almost human.

But this morning, running the weekly audit with cold fingers and a colder lamp, August found the companion entry. The one that should have existed and didn't.

There was no corresponding acquisition record. Somewhere in the guild's vaults below the counting house, someone had taken custody of Aldous Vey's last utterance — and the ledger did not say where it had come from. Every trade needed two hands. This one had only the taking.

August sat very still in the grey light of the counting room, listening to the scratch of the other clerks' pens, the cough of old Hollis two desks down, the eternal whisper of salt shifting in the walls. The Salt Guild's building breathed salt the way other buildings breathed dust. It got into everything: the paper, the ink, the corners of your mouth when you woke. August had lived eleven years inside that taste and had stopped noticing it the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat.

They noticed it now. It tasted like something spoiling.

* * *

Finding the client was simple. Finding the client was always simple; the guild kept meticulous records of everyone who owed it, and Maren Vey lived exactly where the ledger said she would — Candlemaker's Row, third house past the shrine of some minor saint whose name had been chiseled off during the Purification and never replaced. August told themselves it was routine follow-up. An audit discrepancy required verification of delivery. That was all. That was the language of it, and language was armor, and August had been wearing this particular suit of armor for eleven years until it fit so close to the skin they'd forgotten it was metal.

Candlemaker's Row smelled of tallow and wet wool. Midsummer, and yet the morning fog refused to burn off, hanging in the street like gauze over a wound. August had noticed the cold before — everyone had, the way you notice a limp in an old dog — but here on the Row it felt deliberate, pooled, as though the fog had chosen this street specifically to haunt.

Maren Vey opened her door before August finished knocking.

She was younger than *widow* had led August to expect — thirty perhaps, with the raw, scrubbed look of someone who cried often and recently. Her eyes went first to August's guild badge, the small pewter scale, and something in her face collapsed inward like a roof giving way.

"You've come about the voice," she said.

"I've come about—" August began the rehearsed sentence and found it wouldn't come out. "May I come in?"

The house inside was immaculate in the way of houses where there is nothing left to live for except tidiness. Every surface gleamed. A cold hearth. Two chairs, one permanently unused. On the mantel, a portrait of a heavyset man with a laugh lines carved deep around his mouth — Aldous Vey, presumably, caught mid-smile by some patient painter.

"He was a cooper," Mrs. Vey said, following August's gaze. "Forty barrels a season, and every one of them true. He talked all day while he worked. I used to tell him I'd sell his tongue to the guild myself if he didn't stop narrating the weather." She laughed — a small, broken sound with no floor beneath it. "And then he died, and I would have given anything. You understand? Anything. So I did."

"Tell me what happened," August said. "From the beginning. Please."

She sat in the used chair. She folded her hands like a child called before a schoolmistress.

"The clerk came — a kind man, or he seemed kind, they're all kind, aren't they, that's the horror of it — and he explained the terms. His last words. Whatever Aldous said at the end, preserved, delivered to me. I'd hear them as he said them, in his voice, once." Her knuckles whitened. "I paid what he asked. My singing. I sang at our wedding. I sang to him every night for nineteen years, even after he fell asleep, especially then. It seemed fair. It seemed *cheap*, God forgive me."

"And the delivery?"

"Three weeks ago. A man came with a covered jar — ceramic, sealed with wax, warm to the touch, which I thought was odd, but I was too — I wasn't thinking clearly. He set it on the table and told me to unseal it at dusk, alone, and speak my husband's name." She looked up, and her eyes were terrible with a question she'd been carrying for three weeks without anyone to give it to. "Master Winterbourne. What came out of that jar was not my husband."

The fire in the cold hearth might have crackled. It didn't. August heard only their own pulse and, faintly, absurdly, the salt shifting in the walls of this ordinary house, as if the guild were already here, already listening, already keeping accounts.

"Describe it," August said quietly.

"It knew things." She spoke fast now, the dam broken. "It knew our wedding song. It knew he burned the roast every Sunday and blamed the oven. It knew — it knew what he whispered to me the night our daughter was born, the night she didn't live, and no one knows that, no one living knows that, we never told a soul—"

"*Mrs. Vey.*"

"But it wasn't *him*." Her voice dropped to a whisper, and August leaned forward to catch it. "A voice is — you know your own husband's voice the way you know water from wine. This was his voice the way a painting is a man. All the right notes and none of the life. And underneath it — Master Winterbourne, I need you to understand this, I need someone official to understand — underneath his voice there was *another voice*. Like two people speaking through one throat. Something older. Something that was using his words like — like a coat it had stolen off a line."

"What did it say?"

Mrs. Vey's hands were shaking. "At first, the last words. What I paid for. *'Light the good candles, love, we'll waste nothing.'* His last words, he said them to me, he was joking even at the end, that was Aldous all over." A tear cut through the powder of her composure. "And then it kept going. Past the end. Past anything Aldous ever said. It started saying things in a language I didn't know, and the room went cold — colder than this cursed summer's been, cold like a cellar, like a grave — and it said one thing I understood."

August waited. Outside, somewhere down the Row, a door slammed, and both of them flinched.

"It said: *"She is nearly ripe."*" Mrs. Vey looked at August with the naked pleading of the bereaved. "What does that mean? What did I buy? What did I *pay*?"

* * *

August walked back to the counting house without feeling their feet touch the ground.

*Nearly ripe.* The phrase turned over and over like a stone in a streambed, wearing grooves. Fruit ripened. Crops ripened. And souls — souls, apparently, ripened, in whatever dark place the guild's trades drained toward. The widow had paid a singing voice and received a haunting; somewhere between the vault and the jar, Aldous Vey's last words had been *handled* by something that wore them badly, the way a thief wears a stolen ring on the wrong finger.

Back at their desk, August pulled the Vey file and read it with new eyes. Then pulled the next file. And the next.

Delivery complaints were rare — August could count on one hand the ones filed in eleven years — but they clustered. Three in the past season. A fisherman's daughter who'd bought back her father's blessing for the sea and received instead a voice that recited coordinates in its sleep-voice, coordinates in deep water, coordinates that made the harbor pilots refuse to repeat them. A dying miser who'd purchased a guarantee of peaceful death and whose corpse was found smiling with every tooth gone black. A young man who'd traded ten years of his life for one glimpse of his dead brother, and who now drew spirals on every surface, endlessly, in charcoal, in blood, in jam, and could not say why.

Each transaction clean. Each price paid in full. Each delivery *contaminated*.

August cross-referenced the vault transfer logs — the inner ledger, the one clerks saw only in fragments — and found what they had half-hoped not to find. The acquisitions weren't coming from where the front ledgers claimed. The front ledgers said the guild harvested directly: a deathbed contract here, a desperate signature there. But the vault logs showed transfers *inbound* from a single source, coded with a sigil August had seen a thousand times and never questioned because questioning was not in a clerk's job description.

The sigil of the court beneath the mountain.

Every contaminated delivery traced back to stock acquired from below. The guild wasn't just trading souls. The guild was a *middleman* — a fence, a factor, a shopfront — for whatever vast operation ran under the mountain's roots. And whatever ran down there was careless with its goods. Or worse than careless.

August thought of the widow's immaculate parlor. Thought of the portrait with the laugh lines. Nineteen years of nightly songs, traded away, and what she'd gotten back was a stranger wearing her husband's voice like a coat, murmuring harvest-language in the dark.

*Nearly ripe.*

Ripe for what?

The lamp guttered. August realized they'd been sitting in the dark for some time, the audit abandoned, the columns unbalanced — and for the first time in eleven years, August Winterbourne did not care about the balance. They cared about a woman on Candlemaker's Row who could no longer sing, sleeping alone in a cold house with a sealed jar in the cupboard, because she couldn't bring herself to throw it away. Because it was the last thing in the world that sounded like him.

That was the moment the horror stopped being abstract. Not the mountain breathing in dreams, not the page in a dead woman's hand, not even the eleven entries with B. Winterbourne among the sold. Those were wounds in the world's skin, distant, systemic. This was a widow's kitchen. This was a voice at dusk. The guild's arithmetic had finally produced a number August could not carry: one woman's grief, compounded monthly, accruing interest in the dark.

The souls weren't being mishandled.

They were being *eaten* — slowly, carefully, ripened like things in a larder — and the guild was the pantry door, and August had spent eleven years oiling its hinges.

August looked down and discovered their own hand had been moving without instruction, tracing in spilled salt on the desktop a shape they didn't recognize: a spiral. Tight, concentric, precise.

They wiped it away with their sleeve, heart hammering, and reached for the ledger of inbound transfers — because if the court beneath the mountain was the source, then the source had a supply chain, and supply chains had origins, and origins had names.

The first name in the oldest transfer book, dated twenty-two years back, written in a hand August knew better than their own:

*B. Winterbourne. One soul. Sold to the court beneath the mountain. Consideration rendered: full and final settlement of all debts outstanding against issue.*

Against *issue*. Against her child.

August sat in the salt-dark with their mother's handwriting swimming before their eyes, and understood that the widow's question — *what did I pay, what did I buy* — had been theirs all along, asked for twenty years in a language they hadn't known how to read.

Above the counting house, thunder rolled across a cloudless summer sky, and the first snowflakes of the long winter ticked against the window like fingernails.
