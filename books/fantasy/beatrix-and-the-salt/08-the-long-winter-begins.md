## 8. The Long Winter Begins

The snow began at noon on the feast of Saint Aldery's Fire, when the whole of Vhalsund should have been sweating under canvas awnings and drinking cider gone warm in its barrels.

August Winterbourne saw it first from the counting house window — a single flake drifting past the glass, fat and slow, like something falling that had forgotten how to fall. They thought at first it was ash from the tanneries. Then a second flake. Then a third, and then the sky itself seemed to change its mind about the season, going the color of dirty wool, and the snow came down in earnest over the midsummer market.

Below the window, the festival crowd did not understand what it was seeing. August watched a child hold out both hands and laugh, catching flakes on her palms, while her mother stared upward with an expression August had last seen on the faces of people reading ledgers — the particular stillness of someone doing arithmetic they do not want to finish. The mother snatched the child inside. Other families followed, one by one, the way birds go quiet before a storm, until within the hour the square stood empty and white and the bunting for Saint Aldery's Day hung sodden between the lampposts, its red faded to the color of old wounds.

"Close the shutters," Corvane said behind them. He had not raised his voice. He never needed to. "And bring me the weather tallies. All of them."

"The weather tallies?" August turned. "We don't keep weather tallies."

"We keep tallies of everything." Corvane was already at his desk, drawing a fresh sheet toward himself with the unhurried precision of a man shuffling cards he has already counted. His pen moved. Whatever he wrote, he wrote small. "Heat. Cold. Rain in dry years. Drought in wet ones. Every deviation is a transaction somewhere, August. Nothing in this world changes state without a price being paid by someone. Snow in midsummer means someone bought warmth." His pen paused. "Or someone is taking it."

* * *

Ysolde felt the cold arrive in her bones before she saw a single flake, because her bones were where her god used to live, and dead gods leave drafts.

She was in the shrine district — the poor quarter's shrines, the little alcoves squeezed between chandlers' shops and tenement doors, where the old gods survived as carved faces worn smooth by centuries of desperate hands. She went there every morning out of habit, though the habits of prayer had left her years ago along with her ordination, her name in the temple rolls, and any right she had to call herself a priestess. She went because the shrines were cold stone and quiet, and quiet was the only honest thing left in her life.

Except lately they hadn't been quiet. Lately they'd been warm.

She pressed her palm against the alcove of Vessia-of-the-Threshold now, the goddess of doorways and departures, whose face had been rubbed featureless by generations of travelers asking safe passage. The stone was warm. Not sun-warm — there was no sun today, only that wool-colored sky — but warm the way a body is warm, warm from the inside, as if something behind the stone had turned over in its sleep and exhaled.

Three days ago, a fishwife had prayed here. Ysolde knew because she'd seen her do it — a broad-backed woman with salt-cracked hands, kneeling in the slush of the alley, whispering into the alcove: *let my boy's fever break, let my boy's fever break, I'll give anything.* And the fever had broken. By evening the boy was up and eating, and the fishwife had wept in the street and told everyone who would listen that Vessia had answered, Vessia who had been silent for two hundred years, Vessia whom the temples said was dead.

The boy was eating. The boy was alive. But Ysolde had knelt where the woman had knelt, and put her hand where the woman's hands had pressed, and felt what the woman had not known to feel: that the warmth flowing out of the stone was not a gift. It was a withdrawal. Something had come *out* of the fishwife when she prayed — Ysolde had felt the shape of it, faint as a footprint — and whatever answered prayers these days was collecting.

She had been a priestess long enough to know how miracles worked. Miracles were never free. The old gods had run on sacrifice, on smoke and blood and first-fruits, on the deliberate giving-up of things. When the sacrifices stopped, the gods starved, and starved gods sleep, and sleeping gods are called dead. That was the theology they taught you before they taught you the rest. That was the first lesson.

The second lesson — the one they taught you last, if they taught you at all — was that a starving god will eat anything you leave within reach.

Ysolde drew her hand back from the warm stone. Around her, the snow thickened, settling on the shrine roofs, on the gutters, on the shoulders of the few mad or desperate souls still abroad. She watched it fall and did the arithmetic she had spent three days refusing to do.

Prayers were being answered all across the city. She'd counted eleven confirmed in six days — fevers broken, debts forgiven by strangely softened creditors, a drunkard's craving lifted clean away, a dying man granted one lucid week. Eleven miracles in six days, in a city where the old gods had not so much as twitched in two centuries. And every miracle since the third had been followed by cold. Not metaphorical cold. Actual cold, creeping outward from wherever the prayer had been made, as if each answered wish were a candle flame and something vast were drinking the heat off the top of every fire in Vhalsund.

*Something is answering,* she thought, *and it is paying for our prayers with the world's own warmth.*

Or — and this was the thought that made her stomach turn over, the thought shaped like a key — *something is answering on the gods' behalf, and billing the account.*

There was only one institution in Vhalsund that dealt in accounts. Only one place where a thing could be given and taken and owed and collected, all in immaculate handwriting, all perfectly legal.

She pulled her hood up against the snow and started walking toward the Salt Guild.

* * *

By evening, the panic had organized itself into the shapes panic always takes: queues and rumors and prices.

The bread line outside Marrowgate stretched four streets. The candle-sellers had tripled their rates by midafternoon and quintupled them by dusk, and no one rioted, because everyone understood without saying it that candles were about to matter more than coin. In the taverns, the talk ran hot despite the cold — the end of days, the wrath of the buried gods, a comet no astronomer had seen, the Tallow King's curse, the Salt Guild's doing. Always, eventually, the Salt Guild's doing. Crowds are crude accountants; when the numbers stop making sense, they look for someone holding the book.

And the Guild, meanwhile, was open late.

August worked the counter through the chaos and hated how good they were at it. A dyer mortgaging his workshop against a winter that shouldn't exist. A widow buying a year's fuel on credit at terms August had to write out twice because the interest made their hand shake. A merchant offering, in a low voice, triple rate for a vial of *borrowed luck* — the Guild's oldest product, luck drawn from unnamed debtors and decanted into paying customers — because he had a shipment on the mountain road and the passes were closing in July.

"We're not selling luck tonight," August heard themselves say. "Not at any price."

The merchant's face went through surprise, then calculation, then fear. "Then the stories are true. You know something."

"I know the roads are dangerous," August said, and rang the bell for the next client, and added another entry to the private tally growing in the margin of their mind: *another person the ledger makes me lie to.*

Corvane appeared at eight, when the last clients had gone and the lamps were burning low. He carried a ledger August had never seen — black binding, brass corners gone green with age, no title on the spine. He set it on the counter between them.

"Do you know what this is?"

"No, Guildmaster."

"It's the weather." He opened it. Page after page of entries in a dozen different hands spanning, by the dates, more than three hundred years. Frost dates. Thaw dates. Rainfall in measures August didn't recognize. And beside each entry, in a column of its own, figures — debits, credits, transfers. "Every unusual season since the Guild received its charter. Every winter that came early, every summer that failed, every storm with a signature. Weather is not an act of the gods, August. It is a balance. And balances," he said, tapping the column of figures, "can be read."

He turned pages until he reached the current date. The entry there was fresh, in his own hand, ink barely dry:

*Midsummer snowfall, unprecedented. Source: sustained draw, subterranean. Magnitude: increasing. Attribution:* — and here the ink was smudged, deliberately, August realized, as if even Corvane hesitated to write it plainly — *the court.*

"What court?" August asked.

Corvane closed the book. For a moment he looked at August with an expression they couldn't read — and then they could, and wished they couldn't. It was pity. Pity, and beneath it, patience, the way a farmer is patient with a lamb he has already priced.

"You've seen the anomalous entries," he said. "Your mother's hand. The soul-contracts. You've been cross-referencing after hours — don't look surprised, the lamp-oil requisitions alone would tell me. Curiosity is a debt, August. I warned you once." He straightened his cuffs. "I am not warning you again. Tomorrow morning you will find new instructions on your desk. Follow them exactly, ask nothing, and in eleven years — less, if you serve well — your account closes clean and you walk out of here a free person with a pension. That is the offer. It does not expire, but it does *narrow*."

"And if I ask anyway? What's under the mountain?"

Corvane smiled. It was the worst thing August had ever seen him do, because it was kind.

"Winter," he said, and took his book, and went home through the snow.

* * *

Ysolde reached the Salt Guild as the ninth bell rang, and found the door locked for the first time in living memory.

She stood in the falling snow looking up at the building — the pale stone, the salt-white banner hanging stiff with frost, the windows glowing amber above the street where clerks still moved behind frosted glass like figures in a lantern show. Warmth in every window. She thought of the shrine stones, warm from the inside. She thought of the fishwife's boy, alive and eating. She thought of her god, whose name she still could not say aloud without her throat closing, dead two hundred years of a death no temple would explain.

*Someone is feeding the gods again,* she thought. *But gods don't eat heat. Gods eat worship. Souls. Sacrifice. So either the gods have changed their diet—*

—*or it isn't the gods answering at all. It's something wearing their voices. Something spending stolen souls like coin, and the heat of the world is the fee.*

She had followed a prayer to this door once before, six days ago, and been turned away by a clerk with a face like a closed ledger. Tonight she pressed her bare hand flat against the Guild's door, the way she'd pressed it against Vessia's stone, and waited to feel what the stone would tell her.

Nothing. Cold oak, cold iron, cold snow melting against her skin. The Guild was warded, or hollowed, or simply dead in the way institutions are dead — which was somehow worse than any of it.

But as she turned to go, she saw it: footprints in the fresh snow along the Guild's eastern wall, leading from the servants' gate toward the docks. One set, narrow, unhurried, recently made. And at the edge of the lamplight, sitting in the lee of the wall where the snow seemed oddly reluctant to settle, a beggar she had passed a hundred times and never once seen — an old figure in gray rags, hands out, face shadowed, with a hunger coming off him like heat off a forge.

No. Like *cold* off a forge. She could never get the direction right, with him. Her senses insisted he was burning, and her skin insisted he was freezing, and both were true, and she had learned enough in the last six days to know what that meant.

She had walked past a starving god every day for a month.

Ysolde looked back once at the lit windows of the Salt Guild — at the shadow of a clerk crossing behind the frost, bent over some endless accounting — and then she crossed the street through the deepening snow, and knelt in the drifts before the beggar, and said, in the formal tongue of an order that had cast her out:

"Hungry one. Who fed you last?"

The beggar's head came up slowly. Under the hood, his eyes were the color of the sky — that same dirty wool, that same undecided gray — and when he spoke, his voice was the sound a shrine makes when no one has prayed in it for two hundred years.

"I don't remember," he said. "I don't remember anything. Except that I'm owed." His empty hands trembled. "Everything else burned. But I know I'm owed."

Behind them, across the city, the snow kept falling, and fell harder, and somewhere beneath it all — beneath the streets, the cellars, the old salt tunnels, the roots of the mountain itself — something vast turned over in its sleep, digesting, and drew another degree of warmth out of the world.

* * *

August did not sleep.

They sat at their desk with a single candle — the Guild's candles, the Guild's light, paid for out of the debt they were beginning to suspect was not what it seemed — and the master index open before them, and they read the eleven entries again. *Sold to the court beneath the mountain.* Eleven names. Eleven dates spanning forty years. And the twelfth line, the one that had stopped their heart the first time and stopped it again now:

*B. Winterbourne. One soul. Consideration received: full and final settlement of all obligations, minor debtor A. Winterbourne, in perpetuity.*

They had read it a dozen ways. They would read it a dozen more. *Minor debtor* — that was August, entered before August could walk. *In perpetuity.* And the consideration, the payment, the price — one soul, B. Winterbourne's own.

Their mother had sold her soul. To buy *them* out. And instead of freedom, August had gotten eleven years of servitude, renewed and renewed, a debt that behaved like a debt but was signed like a gift.

Outside, the snow came down without pause, burying the midsummer, burying Saint Aldery's festival, burying the city's certainties one soft inch at a time. August watched it through the dark glass and understood, finally, the shape of the thing they were inside of. The guild was feeding the mountain. The mountain was answering prayers with stolen souls. The world was paying the heating bill. And somewhere in the middle of it all was a line in a ledger with their mother's name, and a Guildmaster who smiled with pity when asked what lived below.

Corvane had said: *follow the instructions, ask nothing, and in eleven years you walk free.*

August looked at the candle. At the snow. At the twelfth line.

Free. As if anything in this building had ever meant *free*.

They blew out the candle, and in the dark, very quietly, they began to plan a theft.
