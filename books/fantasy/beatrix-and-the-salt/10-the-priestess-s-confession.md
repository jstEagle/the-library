## 10. The Priestess's Confession

The fire had burned down to its bones by the time Ysolde finally spoke.

They had made camp in the lee of a fallen waystone, one of the old markers that had once lined the pilgrim road to the mountain before the guild bought the road and closed it. The stone was cracked through its heart, its carved prayers worn smooth by centuries of touching hands, and Ysolde had chosen it deliberately — not for shelter, though the wind came sharp off the salt flats now even in what should have been high summer, but because she wanted something holy near her when she said it. Even a broken holy thing. Even a dead god's road.

Above them, the sky was wrong. It had been wrong for six days. The stars sat in their places like coins nailed to black wood, cold and fixed and far too bright, and the midsummer moon rose thin as a paring of ice. Snow hissed against the waystone in small spiteful gusts, melting on August's shoulders as they sat across the fire with the stolen ledger bound against their chest like a child they did not trust themselves to hold properly.

The nameless god sat between them. It wore its beggar's shape still — the grey robe, the ruined hands — but out here, away from the city, away from all the things it might have been, its outline had begun to soften at the edges, as if the world were having trouble remembering where it ended. Every few minutes it would turn its head toward the mountain, northward, the way a dog turns toward home or a wound turns toward salt.

Ysolde watched the snow gather in the hollows of its face and understood, with the terrible clarity that comes only at the end of long silence, that she could not walk another mile carrying what she carried.

"August," she said. "I have to tell you something, and you are going to wish you'd left me at the docks."

August looked up. In the firelight their face was all angles and shadow, a young face trying very hard to be an old one, and Ysolde thought: *they learned that expression in a counting house. They learned to wear gravity the way I once wore vestments.* Twenty years in the guild's service and the guild had taught them nothing so well as how to keep a still face while the ground moved underneath.

"I already wish I'd stayed at my desk," August said quietly. "Tell me anyway."

* * *

She began, as all her stories began, with the shrine.

"You know what I was. You've called me a priestess often enough, always with that careful flat tone you use when you're entering something into your internal ledger — *priestess, disgraced, asset questionable.* But you don't know what I served, because I never told you. Not truly. I told you I kept the old gods' shrines in the Lantern Quarter. I told you my god died of neglect, that the city forgot it, that faith thinned out like paint and there was nothing anyone could have done." She turned her hands over, palms up, and studied them. They were ordinary hands. They had been ordinary hands when they held the knife-shaped censer and the bowl of offering salt. That was the thing no one ever believed about horror: it was done almost entirely with ordinary hands.

"That was a true story with a false heart in it," she said. "My god didn't die of neglect. My god died of shame. And I am the reason it had shame to die of."

The nameless god turned from the mountain. Its eyes — pale, unfixed, the eyes of someone reading a page that had been torn out — rested on her with something that was not recognition but was adjacent to it, the way hunger is adjacent to love.

"The court beneath the mountain is not new," Ysolde said. "Everyone thinks it woke last spring, when the prayers started being answered. Everyone thinks this winter is the beginning of something. But the court is older than the guild. Older than the city. And it has always had priests."

August had gone very still. Ysolde had learned to read that stillness over months of shared danger; it was not calm. It was the stillness of someone bracing a door against pressure from the other side.

"I was ordained at nineteen," she went on, because if she stopped now she would never start again, "into an order that had no public name. We were called the Undercrofts, when we were called anything. We wore grey — you'll notice I still wear grey, habit goes deeper than repentance — and our work was simple. We tended the deep shrines, the ones cut into the roots of the hills, and we made the offerings, and we kept the accounts." She laughed, and it came out wrong, a dry cracked sound. "That's what no one understands, August. Everything in this world comes down to accounts. Your guild didn't invent the trade in souls. It inherited it. Someone was keeping ledgers for the mountain long before Corvane learned to write."

"And you were one of the keepers."

"One of the writers." She corrected him gently, because precision mattered, because precision was the last clean thing she owned. "There's a difference. A keeper guards the book. A writer puts things into it. I wrote names. Do you understand what I'm telling you? For nine years, when the court required feeding, I wrote the names of the offered into the black ledger, and beneath each name I wrote the word *freely,* whether or not it was, and my hand was steady every single time."

The fire popped. Somewhere off in the dark, snow slid from a branch with a sound like a sigh.

"What did the court give back?" August asked. Of course that was their question. Not *how could you.* Not *what did it feel like.* Their question was about the terms of exchange. Ysolde found she loved them a little for it, and hated herself for loving them, because she was about to hand them one more reason never to trust anyone.

"Miracles," she said. "Small ones. Reliable ones. A dry well filled. A fever broken. A ship that should have sunk coming home heavy with fish. The old gods answered prayers before the guild ever sold a gram of borrowed luck, and everyone forgets this, but the answering was never free — it was only ever unpriced. The court took its payment quietly, in kind, and we priests kept the books balanced so no one above ground ever had to see the arithmetic." She drew a breath that hurt. "And then, in my ninth year, the offerings stopped being enough. The court's hunger changed. It wanted not just the given but the given-up. It wanted innocence. It wanted weight. The Regent — whatever wears the Regent now, whatever sits under the mountain in the dark and calls itself courteous — sent up word through the deep shrines that the next offering would be chosen differently. Not a volunteer. Not a debtor. A child."

August's breath went out slowly through their nose. The ledger rose and fell against their chest.

"They gave me the list," Ysolde said. "Three names. I knew two of the families. The third name—" Her voice caught and she let it catch; she had decided, somewhere in the last mile of walking, that she would not perform this confession smooth. She would let it bleed where it bled. "The third name was a girl of six from the tanners' row. I had blessed her at her birth. I had written her name in a book of blessings with my own hand, the same hand, do you see, the same hand that wrote her into the black ledger twenty pages later. I remember looking at both entries. I remember thinking that the two books could not both be mine."

"What did you do?"

"I ran." She said it flatly, without ornament, because there was no ornament that would survive contact with the truth. "I did not warn the families — that was the first cowardice, because warning them meant exposure, and exposure meant the order would simply choose another writer with a steadier hand. I did not go back for my god. That was the second. I walked out of the Undercrofts at dusk with the clothes I wore and eleven silver pieces from the offering box, which I told myself I was borrowing, and I went down the mountain and I never looked back until the looking back was all I did."

"And your god—"

"Died of shame." She said it the way she had practiced saying it, alone, to ceilings, for twenty years: as fact, not grief, because if she opened the grief the fact would drown in it. "You asked me once what it means for a god to die of shame, and I gave you some answer about theology. Here is the real one. A god of the old kind is not a person, August. It is a covenant with legs. It is the sum of every promise made at its shrines, and when its priests break faith at the root of it, the god feels the breaking in its own body. My god felt what was done in its name under the mountain — felt the child's terror come up through the stone like cold water — and it could not stop it, because I had run, because its voice below was silenced, because the covenant was broken from the inside. So it lay down in its shrine and stopped. The Lantern Quarter shrine went cold in a single night. I know because I was living three streets away by then, under another name, and I felt it go the way you feel a tooth die. I went to the shrine and put my hand on the altar stone and it was warm for exactly one more hour and then it wasn't, and that hour was the cruelest gift I have ever received. It waited for me. It waited to see if I would come back and undo it, and I came too late, and it died with my failure in its mouth."

Silence took the camp. The nameless god reached out — slowly, the way one reaches toward a bird — and laid its ruined fingers on her wrist. It had no words to give; it had sold or lost them all. But its touch said *I know this shape of silence,* and Ysolde wept without dignity, briefly, and wiped her face with her sleeve, and was done.

* * *

August did not speak for a long time.

Ysolde watched them do it — watch them think, rather — and she recognized the process because she had seen it before, in council chambers of a different kind. August's eyes had gone slightly unfocused, tracking lines of implication the way other people tracked birds. Somewhere behind that still face, entries were being re-written. She knew which ones. *Ally, priestess, keeper of forgotten rites* — those columns were being struck through, and beneath them, in a newer ink, someone was writing *servant of the court, fugitive, liar.*

She let them think. She had made her peace, walking here, with the possibility that the verdict would go against her. That was the strange liberty of confession: once you had handed over the truth, you could carry nothing heavier than the waiting.

Finally August said, "The child."

"She was taken anyway," Ysolde said. "Another writer wrote the names after I fled. Or perhaps the order chose from the first list regardless. I went back to tanners' row a year later, disguised, and the family was gone. Gone could mean many things, in a city like ours. I have spent twenty years letting it mean all of them."

"And the other two names?"

"Also gone." August closed their eyes. When they opened them again there was something in them Ysolde had not seen before — not judgment, exactly. Something colder and more careful. Arithmetic.

"So the court has been fed children," August said. "Within your lifetime. By an order you served. And the guild's ledgers — the ones I balanced for eleven years, the ones I found my mother's name in — feed it still. And Corvane stands between the two, taking profit from the top of the flow." They turned the stolen ledger over in their hands without opening it, a gesture that had become habitual, a card-player's touch. "Which means that when we reach the court, Ysolde, you will be able to read its rituals. You will know which gates open to which words. You will be worth more inside that mountain than ten of me."

"Yes."

"And you did not tell me any of this until the night before we descend."

"No."

"Why now?"

It was the question she had prepared for least, because it was the only honest one, and honest questions have a way of finding the load-bearing walls. Ysolde looked at the fire instead of at August, and the nameless god's fingers were still resting light on her wrist, and she thought about lying — actually thought about it, felt the old reflex rise, the priest's instinct to manage what people knew — and set it down.

"Because at the toll gate," she said, "it will demand a memory from each of us. And if the toll takes what it usually takes, it will take the memory we are leaning on hardest — that is how these things work, they take from where the weight is. And if it takes from me, it may take my guilt. It may take the memory of the girl's name in two books. And then I will walk into that court feeling trustworthy, August, and I will not be trustworthy, I will simply be *unburdened,* and you will be relying on the conscience of a woman who cannot remember why she ever doubted herself." She lifted her chin. "So you deserve to carry the knowledge while I still can't. If the toll strips me, you'll know what's walking beside you. A woman whose worst act has been taken from her, and who therefore cannot vouch for herself at all."

August stared at her. And then — slowly, like ice shifting in a river — something in their posture changed. Not softening. Recalculation.

"My mother sold a soul," August said at last. "Or had one sold, or was made to sign something — I don't know yet which, and the ledger won't tell me, because the ledger only records the trades, never the reasons. Eleven years I've worked for a debt I never examined, because examining it felt like betrayal. You served a court you fled, and you never told me, because telling it felt like exile." They gave a short, bleak laugh. "We are both people who let the books stand unbalanced because balancing them cost too much. At least we're consistent."

"Is that absolution?"

"No." August said it plainly, without cruelty. "It's not mine to give. But I'll tell you what I have, Ysolde, since I'm apparently the accountant of this company. Loyalty that has never been tested proves nothing. Yours was tested and failed — that's a fact, and I'll carry it. But it failed in one direction, and the direction matters. You ran *from* the feeding. You've spent twenty years running from it. Everything you've done since — the shrines, the old prayers, coming to my counting house with smoke on your breath and accusations in your mouth — everything points the same way. You are not loyal to the court. You are loyal to the thing you failed to save. And I think that loyalty is worth more than a clean record, because a clean record only means nobody ever asked." They paused. "But I want one promise before the gate."

"Name it."

"When we stand in front of the Regent — whatever it's wearing, whoever's face it has — if it offers you your god back, its soul-lamp, its warmth, everything it took — I need to hear you say no out loud. Not for me. For you. So that afterward, whatever happens, there's an entry in the record written in your hand that says *refused.*"

Ysolde's throat closed. Because August, of course, could not have known how precisely they had named her nightmare — that the court knew her price and had always known it, and that the fear was not death but *bargaining.* She looked at the nameless god, sitting patient in the snow with its torn-out pages and its terrible hunger, and she thought: *if they offer it back to me, will I say no? Will I?*

"I promise," she said, and her voice held.

August nodded once, banker-fashion, transaction recorded. Then they tilted their head north, toward the mountain, and Ysolde followed their gaze and felt her stomach drop.

The peak was glowing.

Not with fire-light, not with dawn — there would be no dawn for weeks yet, the almanacs said, if the almanacs meant anything anymore. The mountain's summit shone with a low gold luminescence, steady as lamplight through horn, and as she watched, the glow pulsed once, softly, like the beating of a heart behind stone. Like the hall of some great house putting on lights for expected guests.

The nameless god rose to its feet. It was looking at the glow with its empty pale eyes, and its mouth was moving, shaping soundlessly over and over the same abandoned syllable, and around its feet, unnoticed, the falling snow had stopped — had hung suspended in the air, a thousand small white points of light, turning slowly, like a congregation waiting to be told where to kneel.

"It knows we're coming," Ysolde whispered.

The god turned to her, and though it had no voice at all, she heard it plainly, the way she had once heard her own god in the silence of the shrine:

*Then we shouldn't keep it waiting.*
