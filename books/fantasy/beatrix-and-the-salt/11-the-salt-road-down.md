## 11. The Salt Road Down

The old salt road began where the new one ended — a gate of black timber, long since rotted to lacework, and beyond it a track of pale stone that ran down into the mountain's throat like a vein of scar tissue.

August stood at the threshold with the master ledger wrapped in oilcloth against their chest and felt, for the first time in eleven years, the true weight of what they carried. Not paper. Paper was nothing; August had carried paper since they were nine years old. What they carried was every entry in it, every soul sold under false contract, every widow's grief and child's luck and borrowed year — and beneath all of that, one name written in a dead woman's hand: *B. Winterbourne*.

"You're hesitating," Ysolde said behind them. She had her hood up against a cold that should not have existed in midsummer, and her voice came out flat and practical, the voice of a woman who had made this kind of decision before and regretted it only afterward, at leisure.

"I'm auditing," August said. "It's different."

The nameless god said nothing, because the nameless god never said anything. It stood between them in its beggar's rags, gray as ash, gray as salt, and looked down the road with an expression August had come to know over three days of terrible companionship: hunger without object, longing without memory. It wanted something. It did not know what. It only knew that the wanting pointed downward, like a compass needle made of ache.

"Ledger-keeper," Ysolde said, when August still didn't move. "The snow is coming."

She was right. Above the tree line, above even the ruined gate, the sky had gone the color of pewter, and the air had a taste to it that August knew intimately from ten thousand entries in ten thousand ledgers: the taste of things being taken. The court beneath the mountain was feeding. Every answered prayer drew heat out of the world, and the world grew colder by the hour, and somewhere below them the Regent sat in its mirrored hall wearing a dead woman's face and digesting a summer.

August stepped through the gate.

* * *

The first mile was easy, which August distrusted immediately. Easy roads were how ledgers got you — a small entry here, a reasonable term there, and before you understood what you'd signed you owed eleven years. The salt road descended in long switchbacks through a forest of white pines, and the stone underfoot was worn smooth in two parallel grooves, as if generations of feet had walked it in single file, each walker stepping exactly where the last had stepped.

"The salt caravans," Ysolde said, reading August's glance. "Before the guild. They say the old salt came up out of the mountain already blessed — that a pinch of it could keep a corpse sweet for a year." She crouched, touched the stone, rubbed her fingers together. "This isn't caravan wear, though. Look at the width. Children walked here too. And animals that didn't want to."

August looked. The grooves were narrow. And here and there along their edges, scratched deep into the stone, were marks that might have been fingernails.

They walked on.

By the second mile the cold had stopped being weather and become a presence. It came up out of the mountain in slow breaths, and each breath carried a smell August couldn't place at first — not rot, not stone, but something older and more domestic. Candle smoke. Old incense. The smell of a shrine that has been burning a very long time in a room with no windows.

Ysolde smelled it too. August watched her stop mid-stride, watch her hand go to the broken symbol at her throat — she wore the shattered sigil of her dead god on a leather cord, and touched it the way other people touched wounds.

"It's warm," she said. "Down there. Something is burning."

"Lamps," August said. "The souls are kept as lamps. I saw the entries." They had spent a night cross-referencing before they fled, and the memory of those pages still sat in their stomach like swallowed ice: *one soul, gradeless, to the court beneath the mountain, in exchange for — * and then the exchanges, always generous, always impossible. Luck. Love returned. Rain in drought years. Eleven entries in the master ledger alone, and the subsidiary books suggested hundreds more.

The nameless god turned its head toward the smell of candle smoke the way a dog turns toward meat, and made a sound August had not heard it make before — a low, cracked note, almost a word, almost a name trying to remember itself.

"We follow that," Ysolde said quietly. "Its hunger. It knows the way better than any map."

"It doesn't know anything," August said. "That's the whole tragedy of it."

"No." Ysolde shook her head, and there was something fierce in her face, something August was beginning to understand was the engine underneath all her ruin. "It doesn't remember. That's different. A body remembers what a mind forgets. It walked this road once, August. Whatever it was, whatever name it sold — it walked down here under its own power, and some part of it has been trying to walk back ever since. We're not following its hunger. We're following its homesickness."

The god looked at her. And though it had no name and no words, August would have sworn before any ledger in the world that it understood her perfectly.

* * *

They camped that night in the shell of a waystation, a beehive of dry-stacked stone built into the mountainside, and August took out the little book they had begun keeping — not the guild's ledger, but their own, bought with the last coins in their pocket from a peddler at the gate who had charged them double and wished them dead, or so his eyes said.

The book had no title yet. August gave it one now, writing carefully in the light of a fire Ysolde coaxed from pine knots that burned green and reluctant in the cold:

*A Ledger of What We Are Willing to Lose.*

"What is that?" Ysolde asked. She sat across the fire with her back to the wall and her boots off, and she was rubbing her feet with the grim efficiency of someone who intended to walk until walking killed her.

"An audit," August said. "Of us. Corvane taught me that every contract fails eventually because nobody writes down what they're actually risking. They write the terms. They never write the cost." August turned to a fresh page. "So we write the cost. Now, while we can still see it clearly. If we wait until we're standing in front of the Regent, we'll promise anything. This way—" August tapped the page, "—this way we've already promised, and we can hold each other to it."

Ysolde was quiet for a moment. Firelight moved on her face. "You learned cruelty in that counting house," she said at last, "even if you learned it by accident."

"I learned honesty," August said. "Cruelty was the house style."

She held out her hand for the book. August passed it over, and watched her read the heading twice, and watched something in her shoulders change — a settling, like a load being shifted to a stronger strap.

"Me first, then," she said. She took the pencil stub August offered and wrote, slowly, in a hand that had been trained in some seminary and then spent twenty years forgetting it: *I am willing to lose my god's forgiveness. I've already lost it once. Losing it again won't kill me.* She paused, then added, smaller: *I am willing to lose my life. I am not willing to lose another child. Write that down as a term, not a cost. If it comes to feeding anyone to that mountain, I will burn the whole enterprise down around us, contracts included.*

"That's not how ledgers work," August said gently. "Terms are what you give. Costs are what you pay."

"Then let the mountain learn the difference," Ysolde said, and handed the book back, and dared August with her eyes to argue.

August didn't argue. August wrote.

Then it was their own turn, and the pencil felt heavier than it had a moment ago. August thought about what they were willing to lose and found the honest answer was nearly everything, and found also that nearly everything was not quite everything, and that the difference had a shape they recognized.

*I am willing to lose my freedom,* they wrote. *I inherited it from a woman who bought it with her soul, and I have never trusted it. I am willing to lose my name in the book of the living — I think. I am willing to lose the guild, gladly, and Corvane with it, and the eleven years, which were never mine to begin with.* The pencil hovered. *What I am not willing to lose:* and here August stopped, because the next part was either a lie or a confession, and they had spent eleven years learning to tell the difference.

They wrote it anyway. *What I am not willing to lose is the person across the fire, whom I have known four days, and the god beside her, whom I have known three, and I do not know what that says about me except that I was owned for eleven years and these are the first things I have ever chosen.*

They did not read it aloud. But Ysolde was watching their face while they wrote, and she was a priestess, disgraced or otherwise, and priests are trained to read people the way accountants read columns. When August closed the book she said nothing, but she fed the fire another knot of pine, and the warmth that reached August across the stones was warmer than the wood alone could account for.

The nameless god slept sitting upright, facing downhill, toward the smell of candles.

* * *

On the third day the trees gave out.

It happened between one switchback and the next — one step among living pines, the next among their skeletons, a forest of gray snags standing in drifts of snow that fell upward sometimes, drawn back toward the peak by the mountain's slow inhale. The cold here had teeth. It found the gap between glove and sleeve, the seam of boot and sole, the thin skin at the throat, and it worried at all of them patiently, the way water worries stone.

August checked their own ledger against reality and did not like the arithmetic. Their fingers were going clumsy. Ysolde's lips had gone the color of slate. Only the god seemed untouched, walking barefoot over snow that did not melt beneath it and did not seem to touch it either, leaving prints that filled in behind it with a soft hiss, as if the mountain were erasing its passage.

"Talk," Ysolde said, when August stumbled for the third time. "Counting-house talk. Anything. Cold kills the mind before the body, and your mind is the only weapon we're carrying that works down here."

So August talked. August told her about the counting house — about the smell of tallow and rock salt, about the great iron-bound books chained to their desks like prisoners, about old Master Quill, who had audited the guild for forty years and died at his desk with a pencil through his heart and an entry half-finished, and how Corvane had simply hired a new auditor and finished the entry himself, in Quill's hand, because the client was waiting.

"He forged a dead man's handwriting," Ysolde said.

"He forged a dead man's handwriting beautifully," August said. "That was the lesson. That was the whole apprenticeship. The guild doesn't trade in luck or shadows or years. It trades in the belief that the books are true. Everything else is inventory."

"And you were good at it."

"I was the best they'd had in a generation." August heard their own voice go flat and strange. "Corvane told me so, the night he offered me the promotion. He said I had the two necessary talents: precision, and the ability to look at a human life and see only a number. He said most people could only manage one."

Ysolde walked in silence for a dozen steps. The snow squeaked under them like new leather.

"My god saw numbers too," she said finally. "Every prayer is a number, when you're the one answering. So many voices, so much need, and only so much grace to spend. My god used to say the miracle wasn't the answering. The miracle was choosing which prayers to fail." Her hand went to the broken sigil again. "And then the guild started offering it a better exchange rate."

August stopped walking. "Is that what happened? Your god — it sold itself?"

"I don't know." Ysolde's voice cracked on the second word, and she mastered it, and continued. "One winter it stopped answering. One winter the shrine went cold, and the silence came, and everyone said the old gods were dying naturally, the way gods do. But I served the court's priest caste before that — I told you. I know what their silence sounds like. It doesn't sound like death. It sounds like a mouth being held shut." She looked down the road, into the gray. "Whatever sits under this mountain didn't kill the old gods, August. It *bought* them. And I have been walking toward the thing that bought mine for twenty years, telling myself it was penance."

The nameless god, ahead of them on the road, had stopped. It stood at the lip of a descent so steep the road became stairs — stairs cut into the living rock, wide enough for one, descending into a dark that the noon sun could not reach. From far below, faint as memory, came the smell of candle smoke, and beneath it now something else: a sound like many voices speaking very politely at once.

The god turned back and looked at them — at August, specifically, with eyes the color of unwritten paper — and it lifted one gray hand and pointed down the stairs, and then it pointed at August's chest, at the oilcloth-wrapped ledger, and its mouth worked silently around a word it did not have.

"It wants the book," August said.

"It wants what's *in* the book," Ysolde corrected. "Or in you. Or both." She pulled her hood tighter. "Well, ledger-keeper. Last chance to audit. Once we take those stairs, everything above us stops existing — the guild, the city, the summer, all of it. Are your entries balanced?"

August opened their own small book one more time, there at the top of the stairs, with the cold chewing through their gloves and the polite voices rising from the dark like smoke. They read what they had written by the fire. They read Ysolde's terms. They thought about a woman named B. Winterbourne, whose full name they had never known, whose handwriting they had inherited without knowing they had inherited it, and who had signed away her soul in a room very much like the one waiting below.

"No," August said. "But they're honest. There's a difference."

They put the book away, and followed the god's hunger down.

Behind them, at the top of the stairs, the first true snow of the long winter began to fall — and ahead, far below, something in the mirrored hall turned its borrowed face toward the door, and smiled, and lit one more lamp.
