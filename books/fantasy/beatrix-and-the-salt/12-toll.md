## 12. Toll

The salt road did not descend so much as it *descended into* — that was the distinction Ysolde came to understand somewhere past the second shrine, when the walls of the ravine closed in and the sky shrank to a river of grey light overhead. They were not walking down a road. They were being swallowed, and the mountain was taking its time about it, the way old things took their time with everything: eating, dying, remembering.

She walked last. It was not humility. It was vigilance. Twenty years of watching shrines for signs of life had taught her to watch backs as well as faces, and she did not trust the way the nameless god moved ahead of her — quick, eager, hungry in its bones — like a dog pulling toward a door it half-remembered being beaten behind.

August walked between them, and August had stopped talking.

That was the thing Ysolde had learned about the ledger-keeper in their weeks of flight from the city: silence on August was not emptiness but pressure. When they went quiet, it meant they were writing something down inside themselves, some entry in that inner book they carried the way other people carried knives. Ysolde had seen them do it at the widow's house, at the burned shrine, at every crossroads where the snow fell wrong. Quiet August was August doing arithmetic. And she had learned — painfully, at the toll of her own confession — that she would rather have their arithmetic beside her than against her.

The god stopped so suddenly that August nearly walked through it.

"Here," the god said.

Ysolde looked past its shoulder and felt her stomach drop through the floor of the world.

The gate was not built. That was her first coherent thought, and her second was that no one could have built it, because building implied materials, and materials implied a maker, and nothing that made *this* should be permitted to continue existing. The gate was a seam — a place where the rock of the ravine simply disagreed with itself, where black stone met black stone along a line too straight and too tall, forty feet if it was one, and along that line the air itself was different. Thicker. Sweeter. It smelled like the incense Ysolde's mother had burned when Ysolde was small enough to still believe in the goodness of smoke, and underneath the sweetness ran something else, something like the inside of a sealed room, like breath that had been held for centuries.

Carved into the stone above the seam — or growing there; she could not tell which, and the not-telling made her teeth ache — was a single symbol: a circle, open at the bottom, like a mouth.

"It's warm," August said.

It was. Ysolde felt it now that they had stopped moving — heat radiating off the gate in slow pulses, like the flank of a sleeping animal, and she understood with a lurch why the world above was freezing. The warmth had to come from somewhere. Heat did not vanish. It was only ever borrowed, and everything borrowed in this age of the world was owed.

"The first gate," the god said. Its voice had changed since they'd entered the ravine. Below the city it had been a rasp, a beggar's voice, cracked and apologetic. Down here it resonated. Not louder — deeper, the way a bell rung underwater was deeper. "There are three. Then the hall."

"You've been here before," Ysolde said.

The god turned its head. In the grey light its face was difficult — features present but unwilling to commit, a face sketched by someone who kept erasing and redrawing. "I don't remember," it said, and then, quieter, with the terrible honesty that was the only inheritance left to it: "I remember *not remembering*. There is a shape where the memory was. I keep reaching for it and my hand goes through."

Ysolde knew that feeling. She had spent twenty years living inside the shape of a dead god, tending shrines to something that could no longer answer, and every dawn she reached for the presence that had once filled her like water fills a cup, and every dawn her hand went through. She had thought the kinship between herself and this creature was grief alone. Standing before the gate, feeling the sweetness thicken in her throat, she understood it was more than grief. It was recognition.

"Whatever it asks," August said, "we don't—"

The gate spoke.

Ysolde would spend the rest of her life failing to describe the voice, and she was a woman who had once made her living describing gods to congregations. It did not come from the stone, or from beyond the stone, or from anywhere the ear could locate. It arrived the way meaning arrives — already inside her, wearing her own understanding like a stolen coat.

***TOLL.***

One word. It was not loud. Loudness would have been merciful; loudness could be endured by clenching. This was intimate. It was the voice of something reading aloud from a page it found tedious, and the page was them.

August's hand went to the satchel of coins at their hip — guild reflex, the instinct of a person who had spent eleven years believing every door opened to payment — and the gate said, with what Ysolde could almost have sworn was amusement:

***Not coin. Coin is a promise about value, and you have not yet learned what is valuable here. TOLL: one memory each. Freely chosen. Freely given. The gate does not take. The gate receives.***

"That's not—" August started.

***Freely given,*** the gate repeated, and the sweetness in the air curdled, and Ysolde felt the temperature of the passage fall ten degrees in a heartbeat, and she stepped forward before she could think better of it, before the thinking part of her — the part that had survived twenty years of disgrace by never volunteering, never standing forward, never again being the one the eyes went to — could be overruled by anything as stupid as courage.

"We understand," she said. "We pay."

* * *

They made camp in the mouth of the ravine rather than pay in the gate's shadow. It had been August's suggestion, delivered in that flat bookkeeping voice: *terms should be read away from the counter.* The god had curled against the rock like an animal and gone silent. August sat with the master ledger open on their knees, though Ysolde noticed they weren't reading it. They were holding it. The way you hold a rail.

"I'll go first," Ysolde said.

"No." August didn't look up. "You always go first. At the shrine, at the docks, at the ravine. You stand in front of things and let them hit you. I've been watching."

"That's what front ranks are for."

"That's what people who hate themselves are for," August said, "and we need you, so stop it."

The words landed harder than the gate's voice had. Ysolde opened her mouth to argue and found nothing in her mouth but truth, which was inconvenient, and after a moment she closed it again and sat down heavily on the cold stone and laughed — a short, ugly sound she hadn't made in years.

"When did you get sharp?" she asked.

"Inherited trait," August said. "My mother kept ledgers for liars. You learn to find the true line in any column or you die of it."

The nameless god stirred. "I will pay first," it said, and there was something in its voice Ysolde had not heard before. Resolve? No. She had heard resolve, in soldiers, in martyrs, in the faces of congregations at the good deaths. This was different. This was *longing.*

"Why?" August asked sharply.

"Because I have so little," the god said, "that giving feels like wealth. Because whatever it takes, I will lose less than either of you. And because—" it hesitated, and its unfinished face worked through an expression Ysolde could not name, "—because if it takes the shape where my name was, maybe the ache stops. Maybe I walk out of there whole. Have you considered that? Have you considered that the toll might be a mercy?"

"Yes," Ysolde said quietly. "And I've considered that it might be a lie. Gates don't offer mercy. Doors offer passage. Everything else is marketing."

The god smiled — or performed the motion its face suggested smiling might look like — and stood, and walked back toward the seam without waiting for them, because of course it did, because it was a god and gods were constitutionally incapable of being argued with by their own former priests.

They followed anyway. She was still its priestess, whatever her papers said. Some contracts outlived their signatories.

* * *

The god paid first, and Ysolde watched, and she would carry the watching the way she carried everything — folded tight, next to the bone.

It stood before the seam and the gate said ***NAME***, only that, only the one word, and Ysolde's heart broke cleanly through the middle, because the gate was asking the one thing the creature did not have, and for one vertiginous moment she thought the whole journey would end here, at a locked door, unpaid.

But the god reached up — and Ysolde saw it clearly, saw it the way you see lightning, all at once and forever — and pressed both hands to the place where the shape lived. The hollow. The negative space behind its breastbone where a name had been filed away and sold.

"I don't have a name," it told the gate, gently, the way you correct a child. "But I have the *wanting* of one. I have carried it every day since I woke on your doorstep with nothing in my hands. Take the wanting. It's the heaviest thing I own."

The gate was silent for a long moment. Long enough for Ysolde to count three of her own heartbeats and despise each one.

***RECEIVED,*** it said.

And the god screamed.

Not loudly. That was the horror of it — the scream was small, human-sized, the scream of someone burning a finger on a kettle, and it collapsed to its knees with its hands pressed to its face, and Ysolde lunged forward and caught it, and it was lighter than it should have been, the way bread is lighter after the moisture has been drawn out of it. It shook in her arms. Its face had committed, finally, to a single expression, and the expression was weeping.

"What did it take?" August demanded of the dark. "*What did you take from it?*"

The gate did not answer gates' questions.

The god wept without knowing why. Ysolde held it and felt the sobs move through it like weather through an empty house, and slowly she understood what she was holding, and the understanding was worse than the scream. It had paid the wanting of its name. The hollow was still there — she could feel it under her palm, that absence with weight — but the ache around it was gone. Smoothed over. Healed.

It would never reach for the name again. It would never feel the lack. It would walk out of the mountain whole, exactly as promised — whole the way a statue is whole, complete the way an empty bowl is complete.

"It took my grief," the god whispered against her shoulder. Confused. Almost cheerful. "Why am I crying? I feel *fine.* Why can't I stop crying?"

"Because the tears know what you don't," Ysolde said, and her voice did not break, because she had spent twenty years training it not to, and this — this moment, holding a wounded god in a frozen ravine — was precisely what all that training had been for. "Hush now. The body remembers what the toll takes. Let it remember however it needs to."

* * *

August paid second.

Ysolde tried to talk them out of it afterward — out of the choice itself, though it was already made, though the memory was already gone — and later she would realize this was foolishness, that grief arrives whenever it pleases and arguing with its timing is a fool's errand. But at the time she tried, because August had gone pale as the snowfall outside the ravine, and had said, in that ledger-flat voice, "Mine is easy to identify. There's only one it could want," and had walked to the gate before she could finish saying *wait.*

***THE LEDGER-KEEPER,*** the gate said. ***TOLL.***

"My mother," August said.

Ysolde saw their hands at their sides. Open. Deliberately open, the fingers uncurled, the way you open your hands to show you hold no weapon — or the way you open them to let something fly free.

"I have twenty years of her," August said to the gate, and to the dark, and to no one, "and most of them aren't real anymore. I've rebuilt her so many times the rebuilds have rebuilds. I don't know which memories are hers and which are mine wearing her face. So take the ones I'm sure of. Take the true ones. I want — " and here the flat voice bent, just slightly, the way a beam bends before it decides whether to snap, " — I want to owe her *nothing* when we get where we're going. Whatever she did, whatever she sold, whatever I am to her — debt or daughter or line item — I want to stand in front of it with clean hands."

***SPECIFY,*** the gate said.

"Her hands," August said. "I remember her hands. Kneading bread. Turning ledger pages. One scar across the left palm, silver, like a fish's line. Every true memory I have of my mother includes her hands. Take the hands. Leave me the rest."

***RECEIVED.***

August did not scream. That was somehow worse. August simply swayed — one degree, two — like a candle flame in a draft, and then straightened, and stood there for a long moment with their open hands slowly closing, and Ysolde watched them search inwardly for something and find the shelf where it had been, dusted and orderly and empty.

When they turned around, their face was composed. Their eyes were wet.

"I can't see her hands," they said, conversational, terrible. "I know I loved her. I can feel the shape of loving her. But every picture I have of her starts at the wrists."

"August—"

"Don't." They wiped their eyes with the heel of one palm, briskly, the gesture of someone shutting a ledger at the end of a long day. "It's paid. Paid debts don't get mourned, that's the first rule of the counting house. We have two more gates and a court and I need—" their voice did bend then, briefly, before they mastered it, "—I need to go before I try to buy it back. Does the gate make change?"

***THE GATE DOES NOT MAKE CHANGE,*** said the dark.

"Then we go," August said, and walked toward the seam, and Ysolde followed, carrying the god, who had gone quiet and pliant as a child, whose tears had finally slowed, who murmured once, dreamily, "It doesn't hurt anymore," and Ysolde thought: *no. Nothing will ever hurt you again. God help us both, that is exactly what I'm afraid of.*

* * *

Her turn.

She had known, walking down the salt road, what hers would be. There was no rebuilding, no negotiation, no clever clause. The gate would ask and she would answer, because that was what she had always done, since she was a girl kneeling at her god's altar with her palms up and her whole life in her mouth: *take it, take it, take it.*

But standing before the seam, feeling its warmth pulse against her face like the breathing of something vast, she hesitated. Not from cowardice. From inventory. She was fifty-one years old and disgraced twice over and she had so few things left that were truly hers, and she wanted, just for a moment, to hold each one up to the light and say goodbye properly.

***PRIESTESS,*** the gate said. ***TOLL.***

"I know," Ysolde said.

She thought of the obvious choices. Her ordination day — no, too useful; she needed the rites, needed the old words for what waited below. Her mother's kitchen — no, too small; she'd sooner lose a limb. Her god's death — no. Never. That grief was structural. Remove it and the whole cathedral of her came down.

Then she understood, with a cold sliding sensation, that none of these were what the gate wanted, because the gate had already shown her its appetite. It had taken the god's *wanting*. It had taken August's certainty. It did not collect memories. It collected *load-bearing* ones.

***CHOOSE,*** the gate said, and beneath the word, patient as bedrock: ***OR BE CHOSEN FOR.***

Ysolde closed her eyes.

"There's a morning," she said, and her voice came out steady, which surprised her, "the morning after my god died. I woke in the shrine at Lowbridge and for one moment — one breath, before waking finished — the god was alive again. Not remembered. *Alive.* I felt its attention on me like sunlight. I heard it say my name — my true name, the one from my ordination, that no one else knows. It said 'Ysolde, it wasn't your fault.' And then I woke up, and it was dead, and it had been dead for nine years, and I have spent twenty years telling myself that moment was a dream."

She opened her eyes.

"That's what you want. Not the grief. The *forgiveness*. Because as long as I keep it, I can survive anything down there. Take it."

The gate said nothing. The warmth pulsed. Somewhere behind her, August made a small sound — protest, or grief, or both.

***RECEIVED,***

and the world went white at the edges, and Ysolde staggered, and did not fall, because falling would mean the others carrying her and she had done quite enough of being carried. She stood. She breathed. She inventoried.

The morning was still there — she remembered waking in the shrine at Lowbridge, remembered the cold stones, the dead altar. But the center of it was gone. Scooped out. She remembered *believing* it had been a dream the way you remember reading about a country you've never visited. The sunlight-touch, the voice, the words *it wasn't your fault* — she could recite them, now, like a story. Like someone else's story. A woman in a shrine, a dead god, a dream. Very sad. What happened next?

Something hot moved down her face. She touched her cheek and found it wet and did not know why, and standing there, weeping without cause in the mouth of the mountain, she finally understood completely what the god had felt in her arms — the body keeping faith when the mind had been robbed of its reasons.

"Well," she said thickly, to the gate, to August, to whatever gods remained to witness, "that's that. Two more gates. Let's see what the rest costs."

August took her arm. She let them. The god walked between them now, unburdened, light as paper, humming something it did not recognize — and Ysolde froze, because she recognized it. Four notes. An opening invocation. The first prayer she had ever learned.

The gate's warmth breathed against her back as they passed through the seam, into the dark on the other side, and the last thing she heard from it — soft, satisfied, almost tender, the voice of a merchant watching honest customers leave his shop —

***THE SECOND GATE AWAITS. ITS PRICE IS HIGHER.***

Behind them, faintly, in the direction of the world above, Ysolde thought she heard footsteps on the salt road. Measured. Unhurried. The steps of someone who knew exactly where he was going, and had been going there for a very long time.

She did not look back. Some doors, once priced, are best not reopened.
