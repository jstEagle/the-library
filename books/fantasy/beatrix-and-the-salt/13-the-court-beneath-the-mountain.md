## 13. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The salt road did not end so much as give up pretending to be a road.

One moment August was walking on stone worn smooth by centuries of cart-wheels and bare feet, the grooves still faintly glittering with mineral crust; the next, the passage simply opened, like a throat deciding to swallow. The dark ahead was not the honest dark of underground — the kind that waits politely for a lantern — but something textured, breathing, aware of the light they carried and unimpressed by it.

Ysolde stopped first. She had gone very pale in the days since the toll-gate, and her hands had not stopped trembling, though she had learned to hide it well enough when she thought no one was watching. August watched anyway. It seemed important now, at the end of all things, to know exactly what courage looked like when it cost its owner everything.

"The hall is ahead," Ysolde said. Her voice came out flat, stripped of the priestess's cadence she sometimes fell into. "I remember it from — " She stopped. Swallowed. "From before."

"Before you fled," August said.

"Before I ran," she corrected, with the precision of someone who had spent years sharpening that distinction against her own guilt.

The nameless god said nothing. It had said almost nothing since the gate where it had paid away its name a second time and come out weeping without understanding why. It walked between them now like an old man between his children, gaunt and gray, its hunger so vast and so quiet that August could feel it through their boots, a pull in the floor of the world. Once, crossing a bridge over nothing, it had paused and pressed two fingers against August's wrist, as if taking a pulse. *Almost,* its gesture had said. *You are almost what you were owed.*

Now it lifted its head toward the darkness and sniffed, delicately, the way a hound scents rain.

"Lamps," it rasped. "So many lamps."

* * *

They went in without ceremony, because there was no door to knock upon and no threshold worth respecting. The passage widened, then widened again, and then the walls fell away entirely and August understood that they had entered a space built to make cathedrals feel like cupboards.

The Court Beneath the Mountain was a hall of mirrors.

Not mirrors of glass — August's ledger-trained mind catalogued that immediately, professionally, the way it catalogued everything, because cataloguing was the last wall between them and screaming. These were mirrors of black salt, grown in great vertical panes from the floor to a ceiling lost somewhere above the lamplight, each pane polished to a depth that made the eye slide off it. And in every pane, instead of a reflection, there burned a flame.

Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Flames of every color fire had ever been ashamed to be: blue-white and bruise-violet, green as pond ice, gold, silver, one far off in the deep distance that burned the color of a summer afternoon. They hung in their salt frames like moths pinned to a collector's board, each one burning steadily, patiently, without fuel, without smoke. The heat of them did not warm the air. Instead August felt the cold deepen as they walked, as though the flames drank warmth rather than gave it, as though every flicker in this place was subtracted from somewhere else — from midsummer fields, from hearth-coals, from the blood of the living.

"They're souls," Ysolde whispered. She had both hands pressed over her mouth, but the words escaped between her fingers. "August. They kept them *lit.*"

August knew. August had known since chapter eleven entries ago, since the widow who had bought back her husband's last words and received a stranger's voice instead, since Corvane's smooth face over the burning page. Knowing was different from standing beneath ten thousand stolen dead and hearing them burn.

Because they weren't silent. That was the horror August hadn't prepared for. Up close, each lamp made a sound — faint, continuous, intimate. A hum, a murmur, a half-remembered song. One lamp near August's shoulder flickered in rhythms like breathing. Another, deeper in, pulsed with something that might have been laughter, if laughter could be preserved in amber and slowly starved. The old gods' stolen worshippers, the answered prayers, the sold shadows and borrowed luck and second chances — all of it was here, all of it *shining*, and the whole vast hall was filled with a sound like a city heard through a wall, murmuring in its sleep.

August did what August always did. They reached for the ledger in their mind and began to count.

It steadied them. Flame by flame, pane by pane, they moved down the endless rows, and the counting became a prayer of its own — the only kind August had ever been taught. *One soul, account current. One soul, held in trust. One soul, held in perpetuity. One soul —*

They stopped.

Ahead, at the center of the hall, where all the mirrored aisles converged like the spokes of some inverted wheel, stood a dais of clear salt, and upon the dais burned the largest lamp August had yet seen. It was white — not the white of bone or salt but the white of paper before anything is written on it — and it burned with a steadiness that hurt to look at, and around it the air itself seemed to hold its breath.

And beside the dais, waiting, stood a figure in gray robes.

The figure turned as they approached, unhurried, courteous, the way a host turns when guests are finally announced. And August's heart — which had survived Corvane, the toll, the descent, the cold — stopped anyway.

The Regent wore August's mother's face.

Not an approximation. Not the blurred likeness of a portrait or a memory going soft at the edges. It was her face exactly: the strong jaw, the crooked left eyebrow that August had inherited and never known the source of until this moment, the small scar above the lip. Twenty years older than the mother in August's one surviving memory of her, aged as she would have aged, *should* have aged, had she lived to watch her child grow tall in someone else's debt.

"You must be Winterbourne," the Regent said.

Its voice was her voice too. Warm. Dry at the edges. The voice of someone who read aloud badly on purpose, to make children laugh at her.

"I've been expecting you for twenty years," it said. "Do come closer. The lamps make the hall cold, I'm afraid — we do our best with what we have." It smiled with her mouth, and behind the smile August saw, just briefly, at the very back of the eyes — those were her eyes too, hazel going gray — something else looking out. Something patient and enormous and *hungry*, wearing her face the way a hand wears a glove.

Ysolde's hand closed hard on August's arm. The god made a low sound in its chest, hunger answering hunger, and took one step forward, and stopped, because August had not moved.

"You knew my mother," August said. Their voice came out level. They would think about that later, with wonder — that they had gotten the words out level, in her voice's presence, under that smile.

"I *am* acquainted with her," the Regent said gently. "In the way the court is acquainted with everyone whose accounts pass through our hands. Beatrix Winterbourne. An unusual client. An unusual contract." It tilted her head, and the lamplight of ten thousand souls slid across her cheekbones. "But then, you know that already, don't you? You found her entry. Eleven souls sold below, and hers among them — B. Winterbourne. You counted your way here, little auditor. I respect that. The court has always respected bookkeeping."

"What is this place?" Ysolde demanded, stepping forward, and her voice cracked like a whip across the stillness. "What have you done with them?" She flung her arm wide at the burning panes. "These are people. These are *gods*. My god is here somewhere, isn't he? Burning in your wall?"

The Regent regarded her with mild interest, the way one regards weather.

"Your god," it said, "is accounted for." And it turned back to August as though she had asked about the price of bread.

August's mind was doing something terrible and quiet. It was noticing details. It was noting that the Regent did not breathe — or rather, that it breathed in imitation, a slow theatrical rise and fall of her chest, timed perfectly, *slightly too slow*, the way a musician keeps time for someone who cannot hear music. It was noting that the lamps dimmed, all together, almost imperceptibly, whenever the Regent spoke, as if the hall itself leaned in to listen. It was noting that in every black mirror they passed, their own reflection had appeared correctly — except here, at the center, where the panes showed August nothing at all.

"We've come," August said, "for B. Winterbourne's soul."

"I know," said the Regent pleasantly. "That is why you were permitted to come. Debts must be honored, August. Even here. *Especially* here." It gestured with her hand — her hand, ink-stained at the middle finger, the way August's own was stained — toward the great white lamp upon the dais. "Would you like to see the account?"

August looked at the white flame. It burned without flickering, and the longer they looked, the more certain they became, with a certainty that bypassed the mind entirely and lodged somewhere behind the ribs, that the lamp was looking back. That it knew them. That somewhere inside that steady white light was the woman who had signed her name in a strange hand in a guild ledger twenty years ago, and who had been burning here, patient and bright, ever since.

"Yes," August said.

"Then we should discuss terms," said the thing wearing Beatrix Winterbourne's face, and folded her hands courteously at her waist. "Sit. All of you. The court keeps excellent records, and I think—" the smile deepened, warm and terrible, "—you will find the original contract illuminating. There are clauses in it, dear child, that even your Guildmaster does not know exist. And one of them concerns *you*."

Behind August, the nameless god made a sound like a mountain settling — grief, or warning, or both.

The white lamp flickered. Just once.

And all around the hall, in ten thousand panes of black salt, ten thousand stolen souls turned — slowly, silently, all together — to look at August Winterbourne, who had finally arrived to audit the books of the dead.
