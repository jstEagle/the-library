## 14. The Mother's Ledger

The Regent did not walk so much as arrive, the way a decision arrives, the way a debt comes due. One moment it stood at the far end of the mirrored hall among the hanging lamps of soul-light; the next it was beside August, close enough that August could see that the face was not quite their mother's face — that the skin was too smooth where grief should have carved lines, that the eyes held the flat patient shine of salt water at rest, and that when the mouth smiled it smiled with someone else's memory of how Beatrix Winterbourne smiled.

"You keep looking at my hands," the Regent said. Its voice was warm. It was the worst thing about it, that warmth — the exact register of a voice August had spent twenty years failing to remember, reconstructed now from nothing, from rumor, from the shape of an absence. "You're wondering if they're hers. They aren't. Nothing here is anyone's, August. That is rather the point of the place."

August's hands were shaking. They noticed this the way an accountant notices an error in a column — distantly, professionally, with the understanding that the error would have to be addressed eventually, and that everything else must be set aside until it was. Ysolde stood at their left shoulder. Behind them, at the mouth of the hall, the nameless god crouched in the dark like a dog that had once been kicked in this room and had never stopped expecting it. Its hunger filled the corridor behind them, a pressure August felt against the back of the skull, patient and enormous.

"You said you'd show me the truth," August said. Their voice came out level. Eleven years of reading numbers aloud in a cold room will teach a person that. "Show me."

"Oh, I intend to do better than show you." The Regent turned, and the lamps turned with it — every soul in the mirrored hall swiveling gently on their chains of light, hundreds of small flames leaning like grass toward wind. "I intend to *read* to you. The court respects very few things, August Winterbourne, but we respect a ledger. Yours has been waiting a long time for a proper reading. Come."

* * *

They followed it down a corridor that had not been in the hall a moment before, which August was learning was simply how the mountain did arithmetic — it moved space the way a clerk moved decimal points, without apology and without explanation. The walls here were salt, but old salt, pressed into stone, veined with grey and rust-red, and set into the walls at intervals were alcoves, and in the alcoves were books.

Not lamps. Books.

"Every contract ever signed beneath the mountain," the Regent said, trailing one too-smooth hand along the spines without touching them. "Every soul, every year, every borrowed luck and purchased grief. Your guild keeps copies, of course. Corvane keeps *very* good copies. But the originals live here, where the signatories can't burn them." It paused at a shelf no different from any other and drew out a slim volume bound in grey leather gone soft with age. "Ah. Here we are."

August knew the binding before they knew the title. They had spent eleven years with guild ledgers, had learned the tanners' marks the way other children learned constellations. This was guild work. Guild leather. Guild thread. And on the spine, in the small cramped hand that the guild's clerks used for names:

**WINTERBOURNE, B. — CONTRACT OF SOUL — ORIGINAL.**

"Sit," the Regent said.

There was no chair. There was a ledge of salt stone, and August sat on it because their legs had made the decision without consulting them. Ysolde remained standing, arms folded, her priestess's collar — the burnt one, the disgraced one — a black crescent at her throat. She said nothing. She was watching August the way she watched shrines: for the moment the warmth came or didn't.

The Regent opened the book.

"Standard instrument," it said, in the tone of a clerk reciting, and August realized with a lurch that it was doing this deliberately, that it had chosen the guild's own cadence, the dry liturgy of terms and parties and consideration, because it knew August would trust that cadence more than any plain truth. "*Let it be known that Beatrix Winterbourne, of sound mind and indebted circumstance, does hereby sell, assign, and convey unto the Court Beneath the Mountain one soul entire, together with all appurtenances—*"

"Stop." August's voice cracked on the word. "Stop. That's not — my contract is with the guild. Eleven years of service. I've read my own contract. I've *balanced* my own contract. It's a service debt, it's—"

"Your contract," the Regent said gently, "is a forgery."

The word went through August like a ledger-knife through a page. Clean. Total. Everything above the cut suddenly loose.

"Look at the date," the Regent said, and turned the book around.

August looked. They could not have not looked. The date was twenty-two years old. Two years before August's eleventh birthday. Two years before the guild's clerks had come to the house on Tanner's Row with a document and a pen and the news that August's mother's debts had passed to her child, as debts did, as the guild said debts must, as August had built a whole life on the fairness of — because the alternative to believing it was fair was believing the world was simply cruel, and August had never had the strength for that.

Two years before August was born.

"That's impossible," August said. "I wasn't—"

"You weren't born. Yes." The Regent's borrowed smile widened by a degree. It was not cruel, precisely. It was *precise*, which was worse. "And yet here is your mother, two years before your birth, signing away the only thing she had of any value at all. Do you know what she purchased with it? The consideration clause is quite specific. Guild draftsmen were thorough even then." It turned the page. "Read it yourself. I think you should read it yourself."

August read it themselves.

*In consideration of the soul herein conveyed, the Court agrees to discharge, cancel, and render void and of no effect any and all obligations, debts, or contracts of service now existing or hereafter claimed against any issue of the said Beatrix Winterbourne, and further agrees that no party, guild, or power shall hold claim upon the said issue, their labor, their years, or their name.*

*Any issue.*

August's hands were shaking again, and this time the accountant in them had nothing to say, because this was not an error in a column. This was the column itself, burning.

"She bought your freedom," the Regent said. "Before you existed. Before she knew your name, your face, whether you'd have her hands or her temper or her terrible handwriting. She sold the only thing the court would take and bought a clause, and the clause is iron, August. The court honors its contracts. It is the one thing we do honor. You have been free since before you drew breath."

"Then what—" August heard their own voice from very far away. "Then what have I been *doing*? Eleven years. Eleven years in the counting house. The ledgers, the audits, the cold—"

"A forgery," the Regent repeated. It said it the way a physician repeats a diagnosis to a family that hasn't understood. "Drawn up after your birth by a guild clerk with a fine hand and no conscience. A service debt of eleven years, secured against — nothing. Against a soul that was never the guild's to hold. Your mother's contract named the court as creditor. Corvane knew. Corvane has always known. He keeps the original here, under seal, because the one thing a forger fears is the genuine article." The Regent tapped the grey leather cover once, with one finger, softly, like a knock on a door. "He has never destroyed it because he cannot. Court instruments are indestructible by their terms. So he sealed it, and he lied, and he let a child work eleven years against a debt that had been paid before the child could walk."

* * *

The silence that followed was not empty. It was full of arithmetic.

August sat on the salt ledge and did what August had always done, which was count, because counting was the only prayer they had ever been taught. Eleven years. Four thousand and some days. The winters in the counting house when the ink froze in the well and August warmed it against their skin rather than ask Corvane for the good ink. The birthdays worked through. The mother's grave visited twice a year, on the anniversary of her death and on the anniversary of the contract — August had never known why they kept that second date, only that it felt like an obligation, like a payment due, and now they knew, and knowing was a knife turning slowly in the place where a self used to be.

Every year served had been theft.

Not the guild's theft of August. That too. But first, before that, Corvane's theft of Beatrix — the theft of her sacrifice, her one great purchase, spent and spent and spent and never once honored. She had bought her child's freedom with everything she was, and Corvane had taken the receipt and filed it under seal and charged the child rent on a house the child already owned.

"I balanced his books," August said. Their voice did not sound like their voice. "For eleven years. I balanced the books of the man who—"

"Yes," the Regent said.

"I audited widows. I recorded a woman who sold her shadow. I entered a child's luck in the trade column and I *balanced the line* because the line had to balance, that was the whole of the law, the line must balance—"

"August." Ysolde's hand on their shoulder. Warm. Human. The first warm thing in the mountain. "Breathe."

"I am breathing." They were. It hurt. "I'm breathing and I'm counting and I can't stop counting, Ysolde, because if I stop counting then I have to feel it, and if I feel it I will—" They did not finish. The sentence had no ending that was not a scream, and screaming in front of the Regent felt like handing it a coin.

So they felt it silently, which is the guild way, the counting-house way, grief entered in the proper column and the column closed. And what they felt, beneath the grief, rising through it like gas through water, was rage. Not the hot kind. The cold kind. The kind that balances. The kind that keeps books.

* * *

"Why," August said at last. Their voice had come back wrong — flattened, clerical, dangerous. "Why show me this. You feed on souls. You keep people as lamps. Don't mistake me for someone who thinks you've done this out of kindness."

The Regent closed the ledger with care, the way one closes a book one has read many times.

"Three reasons," it said. "The first is that your mother's contract is the finest instrument ever signed in this hall, and I have kept it twenty-two years the way a miser keeps a coin he cannot spend. It pleases me to have it read aloud at last. Consider that my vanity."

"And the second?"

"The second is that the guild has grown greedy beyond its terms. Corvane sends me souls, yes — but he has begun to *skim*. Souls sold to him under false pretense, souls mishandled, souls that are not his to sell. Your widow's husband, for one. The court does not eat spoiled meat, August. Your guild has been passing it off as fresh for years, and I am owed an accounting." The flat salt-water eyes fixed on August, and for one moment something moved behind them, something old and vast and hungry in a way that had nothing to do with food. "And the third reason is that you are the only living signatory of the instrument that can make him pay for it."

August stood. Their legs held. Somewhere behind them in the dark, the nameless god made a sound — low, subterranean, less a growl than a settling, the sound of a mountain deciding which way to lean.

"My mother," August said. "The real one. Her soul. It's here. In this hall."

The Regent was quiet for a moment. When it answered, the borrowed warmth had gone out of its voice, and what remained was almost — almost — respectful.

"Every soul sold to the court burns as a lamp," it said. "Yours is the third lamp on the left as you enter the hall. It has burned twenty-two years without flickering. It is, if you will forgive the professional observation, the finest flame I own." It extended the grey ledger to August, handle first, the way one extends a knife. "Take it. It was always yours — hers, then yours. And when you go up out of my mountain to do whatever it is you are going to do to Corvane, know this, August Winterbourne: the court will not stand in your way. The court would *like* to watch."

August took the ledger. It was lighter than it should have been. It was warm, the way a hand is warm.

They turned to go, and stopped, because Ysolde had not moved, and because the Regent spoke once more, and its voice had found the warmth again, and this time the warmth was aimed like a blade.

"One more thing, and then you may hate me freely. Your mother did not sign that contract in despair, August. I was here. I watched her sign. She was laughing." The Regent's smile was Beatrix's smile now, exactly, terribly, the one August had spent two decades failing to remember and would now never be able to forget. "She said: *He'll never even know he was free. That's how I'll know it worked.*"

The lamps leaned toward them like grass toward wind as they walked out.

And in the dark of the corridor, holding their mother's soul-contract against their chest like a coal, August began — silently, in the old clerical cadence, the only prayer they knew — to draft Corvane's reckoning.
