## 15. What Ysolde Owes

The lamp hung from a hook of black salt, and it burned with a light Ysolde knew better than her own heartbeat.

She had spent eleven years as a priestess of the Drowned Star, and most of those years she had spent tending its light — the great blue-white flame in the temple on the headland, the one that sailors steered by and widows prayed to and children were told would outlast the sea itself. She knew the particular quality of that radiance, the way it made shadows fall *toward* it instead of away, the way it hummed at a pitch just below hearing, like a note held by a throat that never tired.

The lamp before her hummed that note.

It was small — no larger than a fist — and it hung in the mirrored hall of the court beneath the mountain among hundreds of its kind, souls strung up like lanterns at a festival, each one burning with the light of something that had once been loved. But this one was different. This one was *hers*, in the way that a wound is yours, in the way that a grief is yours. She had felt its death from forty miles away. She had felt the silence open in her chest where its presence had been, and she had carried that silence ever since, the way an amputee carries the ghost of a limb.

"Ysolde." The Regent's voice came from the thing wearing August's mother's face, and it was gentle, and it was courteous, and it made Ysolde's skin crawl the way silk makes a burn crawl. "You may touch it, if you like."

Ysolde did not move. She stood in the middle of that terrible mirrored hall with her hands clasped behind her back the way she had once stood before her own altar, and she made herself look at the lamp instead of the face, because looking at the face was a kind of surrender she was not yet ready to commit.

"It's warm," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "The shrine was warm. That's how I knew. A dead god's shrine should be cold, and mine was warm, and I thought — I let myself think it was a miracle."

"It was a miracle," the Regent said. "All our works are miracles. That is the trouble with them."

* * *

The court beneath the mountain was a place of reflections. Every surface — the walls of veined grey stone, the floor of polished salt, the great arched ceiling lost in darkness above — every surface gave back what stood before it, but not quite truly. The reflections lagged. They hesitated. And sometimes, Ysolde had noticed since their arrival, they did things the originals had not yet done: a reflection of August that turned its head a heartbeat before August did; a reflection of the nameless god that knelt when the god itself was still standing, as though the mirror remembered something the flesh had forgotten.

She had not slept since they came down the salt road. None of them had. Sleep, in this place, felt like an invitation.

The Regent had received them with the courtesy of a host at a dinner party, which was somehow worse than chains would have been. It had shown them the hall of lamps. It had explained, in the patient tone of a teacher addressing a promising student, the arithmetic by which the world above was now freezing — the prayers answered, the heat drawn up through the old channels of faith, the winter as digestion, the mountain as a belly that had finally been fed enough to grow ambitious. It had not threatened them. It had not needed to. It had simply shown them the lamps, and let them do the arithmetic of their own hearts.

And now it had brought Ysolde here, to this hook of black salt, to this lamp, and it was watching her with her dead friend's mother's face, and it said, "You may touch it."

Ysolde looked at August. August stood near the hall's entrance, arms crossed tight, jaw set, and behind their eyes she could see the ledger turning — she had learned to see it, that invisible abacus clicking behind everything they did. August was calculating. August was always calculating. It was how they survived, and Ysolde loved them for it and feared it in the same breath, because a person who calculates can always find a reason, and the court dealt in reasons the way other markets dealt in grain.

The nameless god stood at August's shoulder, wrapped in its beggar's rags, its face a smooth grey blankness where features should have been. It had been quiet since the toll gate. It had been quiet since it came out of that dark archway weeping without knowing why, and Ysolde had put her arm around it, and it had leaned into her like a child, and neither of them had spoken of it since.

"Touching it won't restore it," Ysolde said. "You're not offering me a resurrection. You're offering me a lamp."

"I am offering you a choice," the Regent said. "The lamp is yours by right — your god's soul, taken in payment for prayers it did not answer, held here against its will as yours was held against yours. I am offering to return what was stolen. All I ask in exchange is what you have already carried this far and could so easily set down." It gestured, with a hand that had Beatrix Winterbourne's knuckles, at August. "And the hungry one. The nameless thing. Two souls, freely given, and you walk out of this mountain with your god burning in your arms, and you may carry it back to your city and set it in its shrine, and every prayer you speak for the rest of your life will be answered by something that knows your voice."

The hall was very quiet. The lamps hummed their subterranean chord. Somewhere in the dark above, water dripped in a rhythm like a slow clock.

"You know what I did," Ysolde said. "You know why I fled the temple. You've been reading me since I came down that road."

"I know everything that was ever given to us," the Regent said. "Including confessions. Including the memory of a child on an altar, and the sound of your own feet running."

Ysolde closed her eyes.

* * *

Here was the shape of it, the shape she had carried up out of the temple district eleven years ago and had never once set down, not in all the years of drinking and vagrancy and half-mad devotion to a god already dead:

The court had priests once. Not the old gods' priests — the other kind, the kind that served the deep places, the kind that kept the channels open between the prayers above and the hunger below. Ysolde had been one of them before she had ever been anything else. Raised to it. Chosen for it. Taught the old rites in a cellar under a fishmonger's shop, taught that the gods above were lamps and the thing below was the oil, and that a priest's sacred work was the pouring.

She had poured for years. Luck, here. A second chance, there. Small things, mostly. She had told herself they were small things. A gambler's fortune. A coward's courage. The years of a life no one would miss.

And then one autumn they had brought her a child.

She could still see the cellar. She could still see the chalk circle, and the bowl of salt, and the child — a boy, seven years old, sold by a father who owed more than he could pay — standing in the circle with his arms at his sides because he had been told that stillness was holy. She could still see the knife. She could still see her own hands, steady on the haft, because they had trained her hands to be steady, because steadiness was the first virtue of the pouring kind.

And she could still see the moment she did not do it.

She had never been able to explain it, even to herself. The knife had been raised. The words had been in her mouth. And then something had moved in her — not courage, she had never believed it was courage, courage would have been cleaner — something more like a door swinging open onto a room she had not known was in the house. And she had looked at the child, and the child had looked at her, and he had not been afraid. That was the unbearable thing. He had trusted her. He had been told that what was about to happen was holy, and he had believed it, and he had stood there in his little stillness offering himself up to her knife with the perfect faith of the instructed.

And her god — her god had been there. She had not known it then. She had thought the warmth in the cellar was the lamps. But she understood it now, standing in this hall of lamps, understanding it with the terrible clarity that this place granted whether you wanted it or not: her god had been there, and it had seen her raise the knife, and it had seen her lower it, and it had loved her for the lowering.

And then it had watched what the court did next. The reprisal. The slow stripping of her rank, her name, her temple, her god's own worshippers funneled away to shrines that fed the channels. Her god had watched the machinery of the deep places grind down everything it loved, and it had known that the machinery existed because of prayers — because of *faith*, the very thing that was its body and its bread — and it had understood, in the way gods understand things, slowly and completely and too late, what it had been feeding on all along.

It had died of shame. Ysolde was sure of that. Gods did not die of wounds; they died of what they knew. Her god had looked at what its worship built, and it had put out its own light, and the silence that followed had been the silence she had carried ever since — the same silence, she realized now, the same exact silence, that lived in the face of the nameless god standing at August's shoulder.

That was why she had recognized it on the docks. Not the hunger. The shame.

* * *

"You could have it back," the Regent said, and its voice was Beatrix Winterbourne's voice, warm and reasonable, the voice of a mother explaining the world to a child. "Not a memory of it. Not an echo. *It.* The Drowned Star, whole, burning, yours. You have served faith your entire life, Ysolde of the headland temple. You have given it everything — your childhood, your hands, your name, your god. And what did faith ever give you back? A cellar. A knife. A child's trusting face. We are not offering you a bargain. We are offering you a *refund*."

The lamp hummed. The light fell on Ysolde's hands, and she looked at them — her priest's hands, her steady hands, the hands that had held the knife and had not dropped it and had not used it either, the hands that had spent eleven years doing nothing holy at all, mending nets on the docks, pouring cheap wine into the sea at dusk because it was the only rite she could still afford.

And the truth, the ugly truth, the truth she would carry out of this hall or die in it: she wanted it. She wanted it the way the drowning want air. She wanted to carry her god home. She wanted to set it on the headland and watch it burn blue-white over the water and hear the sailors' voices change when they spoke of the shrine — *warm again, alive again, answered again* — and she wanted to kneel in front of it and be, for one hour, one hour of her whole ruined life, a priestess in truth.

And all it would cost was August.

August, who had come down a mountain for a mother they barely remembered. August, whose entire life had been collateral and whose loyalty had been a forgery and who was standing right now in this hall of stolen light with their arms crossed and their jaw set, calculating, always calculating, because calculation was the only inheritance they'd been left. August, who had held the lantern for her on the salt road when her hands were too cold to hold it herself, and had said nothing about it, because August did not do kindness as performance; August did kindness as bookkeeping, quiet entries in a ledger no one else would ever audit.

And the nameless god. The hungry one. The thing that had sold its own name to keep its hunger fed, that had forgotten everything except the wanting, that had leaned into her arm at the toll gate and wept without knowing why — and that was looking at her now, featureless face tilted, and she would have sworn on any altar, dead or living, that it was looking at her the way the child in the cellar had looked at her. With trust. With that perfect, unbearable, instructed trust.

"Ysolde." The Regent's voice, softer now. It had moved closer. She could smell it — salt and candle-wax and, beneath, something older, something like the inside of a stone. "You have already failed once. You stood before a child and you could not do the holy work, and your god died for your failure. Do not fail twice. Do not let sentiment —"

"It wasn't failure," Ysolde said.

The hall went still. Even the lamps seemed to hush.

She turned to face the Regent fully, and she made herself look at the face — Beatrix Winterbourne's face, August's mother's face, kind and terrible — and she said it again, louder, the way you say a thing you have waited eleven years to say.

"It wasn't failure. Lowering the knife was the only holy thing I ever did. And if my god died of shame, it wasn't shame for me. It was shame for *you*. For everything you are. For this hall. For all this light that you stole." Her voice cracked and she let it crack. "You want me to trade two souls for one lamp. But I know what your lamps are. I've seen what you do with what you're given. You gave a widow her husband's last words and what came out of her husband's mouth wasn't him. You don't return souls. You *wear* them. And I will not hand you August Winterbourne and a starving god and call it a refund."

The Regent regarded her. The courtesy did not leave its face, but something beneath the courtesy shifted, the way water shifts when something large turns over far below.

"You would rather remain empty," it said.

"I would rather remain *mine*."

* * *

It was then that the nameless god moved.

It had stood silent through all of it, through the offer and the refusal, through the Regent's soft voice and Ysolde's cracking one, and now it came forward, past August, past the Regent, its rags whispering on the polished salt, until it stood before Ysolde in the blue-white light of the lamp that had been her god's soul.

And it reached up, with a hand grey and smooth as riverstone, and it touched her forehead.

The hall vanished.

She was in the cellar. She was in the cellar, and the knife was in her hands, and the child was in the circle, and the warmth was in the air — and this time she could *see* it, the way she had not been able to see it then, the way only a god's memory could show it: her god, the Drowned Star, present in the room, vast and blue and grieving, filling the cellar the way the sea fills a wreck. It had come for her. It had come down into the deep places for her, drawn by her terror, and it had watched her raise the knife with its whole burning heart in its mouth.

And when she lowered it — when her hands had come down empty and shaking and the child had run and the reprisals had begun — her god had done the thing she had never known about, the thing that had happened in the dark between its death and her flight, the thing that no one had ever told her because no one had survived to tell it.

It had forgiven her.

Not the way mortals forgive, half-owing and half-forgetting. It had forgiven her the way only gods forgive, completely, structurally, written into the deep grammar of the world: *this one is clean, this one is mine, this one lowered the knife and the lowering was holy.* It had spoken her name into its own dying light and made the forgiveness the last thing it did, the final word of a god, and the word had been her.

And then it had died, and the forgiveness had gone with it, into the dark, into the channels, down into the court — and the court had kept it, the way the court kept everything, strung up among its lamps, a soul's last word hanging in the dark for no one to read.

Until now. Until the nameless god — who had been at the toll gate, who had paid a memory it could not remember paying, who had walked beside her for a hundred miles of cold — reached into the place where that memory had been kept and gave it back to her, whole, the way you return a thing that was never yours to hold.

Ysolde came back to herself on her knees on the salt floor, and she was weeping, and she did not care, and she did not try to stop.

The nameless god knelt in front of her. It had no face to be gentle with, and it was gentle anyway. And she understood — with the clarity this place granted whether you wanted it or not — that it had known her. All this time. Beneath the hunger and the namelessness, in whatever cellar of itself it still kept, it had known her, the way her god had known her, because it was what remained of that knowing — or because hunger remembers love even when everything else is sold. It had walked beside her not because she fed it. It had walked beside her because it was still, in whatever way a god can be, *hers*.

"You gave it back," she whispered. "You gave it back to me. That was yours. You paid for that at the gate."

The god made no sound. It had no mouth to make sound with. But it took her hand — her steady priest's hands, her ruined, holy hands — and it pressed them against its grey blank face, and it held them there, and that was answer enough.

Ysolde looked up. August was staring, and behind their eyes the abacus had stopped. For the first time since Ysolde had known them, August Winterbourne's face held no calculation at all — only something raw and unguarded and very like hope, and very like fear of that hope.

And the Regent was watching too. Beatrix Winterbourne's face had gone very still, and in the stillness Ysolde saw something she had not seen before, something that made the cold of the long winter seem warm: the Regent was *afraid*.

Because the nameless god had just done a thing the court could not do. It had given something back. Freely. Without ledger, without toll, without arithmetic. It had made a gift, and gifts were the one currency the court had never learned to count.

"Regent," Ysolde said, rising, wiping her face with the back of her hand, her voice steadier than it had been in eleven years. "You should know something. You offered me a lamp, and I refused you, and I want you to understand why. It isn't because I don't want my god back. It's because I finally understand that my god is not in the lamp."

She put her hand on the nameless god's shoulder.

"It's here. It's been here the whole way down. And it just taught me the one thing you've never learned." She smiled, and it was not a kind smile. "Some things can't be traded. They can only be given."

The Regent's face did not move. But all around the mirrored hall, in their hundreds, the soul-lamps flickered — every one of them, all at once, like a held breath — and far above, through a thousand feet of mountain, Ysolde heard a sound that did not belong to the deep places.

Footsteps on the salt road. Measured. Unhurried. Descending.

And beside her, August went white as the salt beneath their feet, and said, in a voice like a ledger closing:

"That's Corvane."
