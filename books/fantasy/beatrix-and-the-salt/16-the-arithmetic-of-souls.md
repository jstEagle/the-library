## 16. The Arithmetic of Souls

The lamps did not flicker. That was the first thing August understood about the court beneath the mountain: nothing here flickered. The soul-lamps in their mirrored niches burned with the steady, patient light of things that had been taken out of time, and the Regent stood among them wearing Beatrix Winterbourne's face the way a magistrate wears robes — correctly, formally, without warmth.

August's hands were shaking. They made themselves look at them, catalogued the tremor the way they would catalogue any discrepancy in a ledger, and found that naming it steadied it. Fear, entered honestly, lost half its power. That was the first lesson of the counting house, learned at nine years old with cold fingers and a slate: *write down what is true, and the truth becomes a thing you can hold.*

"Mother," Ysolde said beside them, low and dangerous.

"Not quite," the Regent said. Its voice was Beatrix's voice pitched down into something older, the way a bell's note continues below what the ear can catch. "The face is on loan. The woman is elsewhere." It turned its head — her head — and regarded August with eyes the color of lamp-oil. "You've come a long way to argue bookkeeping, little auditor."

"That's exactly what I've come to do," August said.

* * *

They had rehearsed nothing on the road down. There had been no time, and anyway August had never been able to rehearse for this, because this — standing in a mirrored hall beneath a mountain, negotiating with a thing that wore their mother's stolen face — was not a situation any of the guild's manuals covered. But eleven years of service had taught August one discipline above all others, and it was this: when the world became impossible, return to the page. Return to what could be counted.

So August counted.

"You hold," they said, walking slowly along the line of lamps, letting their voice take on the flat cadence of an audit read aloud, "one hundred and forty-three souls under active contract. Ninety-one were sold willingly — desperate people, grieving people, but willing, and your claim on those I do not contest tonight." A lie of scope, not substance; one war at a time. "Fifty-two were taken under contracts of questionable provenance. And one—" they stopped, drew breath, "—was taken under no contract at all."

The Regent smiled with August's mother's mouth. "You mean yours."

"I mean my mother's."

"Ah." The Regent drifted toward a dais of black salt at the hall's center, and the mirrors tilted to follow it, hungry for reflection. "Then let us speak precisely, since precision is your faith. Your mother, Beatrix Winterbourne, sold her soul to this court twenty-two years ago. Consideration paid in full: one child, freed of all obligation to the Salt Guild. The instrument is valid. The signature authentic. I have checked it more times than you have, I promise you."

"Then you already know what's wrong with it," August said.

For the first time, the Regent went still. Not the stillness of a person listening — the stillness of a machine encountering an unexpected input. Somewhere behind August, the nameless god made a sound like wind through a ribcage.

"The contract was never between the parties it names," August said. "My mother contracted with the Salt Guild. Corvane holds her signature. But the soul itself—" they pointed at a lamp three niches from the dais, a small stubborn flame burning gold instead of white, "—was delivered here. To you. The guild took payment from me for eleven years; you took possession of the collateral. Two claims, one asset, and only one instrument binding them." They pulled the folded pages from their coat — the master ledger leaves they had stolen, Corvane's clause invoked and annotated in their own hand. "In ledger law, which your court honors above all things, because ledger law is older than either of us—"

"Older than every god present," the Regent agreed, silkily.

"—a soul sold under false or forged contract reverts to the *true debtor*. Not the holder of paper. The party who actually gave value." August laid the pages on the dais, one by one, like cards. "Corvane never owned my mother's soul. He forged her debt to keep me bound, and he sold her soul to feed his masters. He gave nothing. Therefore he transferred nothing. Therefore your title is broken at the root, and the asset must revert."

The silence that followed had texture. It pressed against August's ears like deep water. In the mirrored walls, fifty-two reflections of the Regent turned in unison to look at fifty-two reflections of August, and every one of those faces wore an expression August had never seen on their mother's living memory and never wanted to see again: *recalculation.*

"And the true debtor," the Regent said at last, softly, "would be the party who paid consideration. Who paid value for that soul, little auditor?"

"My mother paid," August said. "She paid with herself. She is the injured party and the rightful claimant. Under ledger law, the reclaimed asset returns to—"

"—to the claimant," the Regent finished. "Who must sign the instrument of reclamation." It lifted Beatrix's hands, spread them wide, palms up. Elegant. Empty. "Signatures require hands. Hands require the living, or something that was never dead. Tell me, child of the ledgers — where is your mother's hand?"

And there it was. The knife's edge, arrived exactly as August had known it would, because August had done the arithmetic on the salt road, alone, in the dark, over and over, hoping each pass would find a different sum.

Ghosts cannot sign. That was the first law of the ledger, older even than reclamation. The dead could be written about but never *with*; ink required blood that moved. Every escape August had built on the road down — every clause, every loophole, every elegant chain of title — ended here, at a door that opened onto nothing. Their mother could reclaim her own soul and had no hand left to sign with. August could sign anything, and had no standing to sign it. The law that might save them was the same law that locked the door.

"You knew," August said. "That's why you're being courteous. You let me build the whole argument so I'd arrive at the end of it myself."

"I admire good work," the Regent said gently. "I gave you the flaw because it is real. Ledger law binds me as surely as it binds Corvane, and I could not simply refuse you. But the law closes every door it opens — that is its nature, and it is also why I love it." It stepped close, close enough that August could see that the pores of the borrowed skin were faintly crystalline, salt blooming beneath the surface like frost in a windowpane. "There is one remedy, of course. You know it already. You've known it since the gate."

*A memory each,* the toll-keeper had demanded. And before that, other tolls, and after these, worse ones. Everything in this place was bought, and everything cost.

"A living signer can stand surety for a dead one," August said quietly. "Proximate signature. It's in the third appendix. It's almost never used, because—"

"Because the surety assumes the obligation entire," the Regent completed, and its voice — *her* voice — softened into something horribly like tenderness. "If you sign for your mother, then the debt of the signature is yours. Reclaim her soul, yes. Free her. But the bond of surety means whatever she owed the ledger, you owe forever. Her account becomes your account. Her place in the book of the living becomes—" it paused, savoring, "—contested."

Ysolde moved. August heard her staff scrape stone, heard her begin to speak — *August, don't* — and raised one hand without turning. Wait. Let me finish the arithmetic.

Because that was the thing about arithmetic. It didn't care what you wanted. It only cared what was true.

Eleven years of service, already rendered, against a forged debt: void, recoverable, perhaps reclaimable in damages. One soul, wrongfully held: restorable by proximate signature. One signature, priced at: everything August was. Their freedom — the freedom their mother had bankrupted herself to purchase — surrendered back to the ledger the moment August's pen touched the page. Their name in the book of the living, marked *contested*, held in escrow by a court that ate souls, until some future audit decided what remained.

She had bought me out, August thought. And the only way to make her purchase real is to give myself back.

It was such a clean equation. That was the obscenity of it. Grief was messy; love was messy; but the ledger reduced all of it to numbers that balanced with terrible elegance, and somewhere in the mirrored dark, something was waiting for August to appreciate that elegance and sign.

"There's a third term," said the god.

* * *

August had almost forgotten it — the nameless thing that had followed them down the salt road on legs it barely remembered how to use, weeping at the toll gate without knowing why. Now it came forward into the lamplight, and the lamps leaned toward it. All of them. Every flame in a hundred niches bent, just slightly, like grass toward weather.

The god looked at August with eyes that held nothing and everything — hunger without object, grief without source, the shape of a name with the name scratched out.

"You said the true debtor must sign," the god said. Its voice was gravel and old smoke. "You said ghosts cannot sign. That is your law?" It looked to the Regent. "*Your* law? Written before me. Binding on me. Yes. I remember — no. I remember that I *would* remember." It pressed a hand to its hollow chest. "But hear the flaw, bookkeeper, since flaws are the trade we're all in tonight. Your law says the dead cannot sign. Your law says souls sold under false contract revert to the true debtor." It turned to August. "Show me the clause, the one you named. Proximate signature. Read it. All of it."

August's mouth was dry. They unfolded the appendix with fingers that wanted to shake and refused permission. "'Proximate signature may be executed,'" they read, "'by the claimant, or by one who *carries the claimant's hand* — to wit, kin of blood who inherited the claimant's mark, whose signature the law cannot distinguish from the claimant's own—'"

They stopped.

The hall seemed to tilt. In the mirrors, a hundred reflections of August stopped too, pages in a hundred hands, and every one of them had arrived at the same place.

"They inherited her hand," Ysolde whispered. "August. You sign like her, don't you. You've always signed like her. Corvane used to make you practice her entries because—"

"Because the guild needed someone who could reproduce her script for the forgery," August said. The words came out very quiet. "He taught me her hand without telling me why. Twenty years ago he forged her signature with his own skill. Tonight he isn't here to forge anything." They looked down at the page, at the line waiting for a name, and saw it now for what it was: not a trap, or not only a trap. A door with two keys, and both keys were theirs.

"If you sign," the Regent said, and for the first time its voice had an edge of urgency it could not fully disguise, "the surety binds you. That clause does not vanish. Her debt becomes yours. Your name—"

"Or," August said, "I sign *as her*. Not for her. As her. Kin of blood, inherited mark — the law cannot distinguish the signatures. Which means the law cannot distinguish the signer. If my hand is legally hers, then the true debtor signs, and no surety is needed, and no debt transfers." They looked up. "Can you tell the difference, Regent? Can your perfect law? Because if it can't, then you have a problem, and the problem is me."

The lamps guttered — all of them, together, the first flicker the court had permitted in centuries. In the mirrored walls, the Regent's hundred reflections reached for each other and found only glass.

"It will cost you something," the god said softly, to August alone. "The law abhors a perfect forgery. To make your hand indistinguishable from hers, you will have to give it what is hers. Some part of you will stop being yours. The ledger will take its fee."

"I know," August said, though they hadn't known until the words were out, and knowing settled into them like cold water finding its level. "I've read enough contracts to know nothing balances for free."

They set the page on the dais. They uncapped the ink — guild ink, iron-and-salt black, carried all this way out of habit that had finally become purpose. And across the hall, the Regent wearing Beatrix's face watched her child bend over the instrument of her deliverance, and for one unguarded instant the lamp-oil eyes did something no audit could have predicted.

They looked afraid.

August thought of the widow's purchased grief. Of the lamps burning steady because they were forbidden to flicker. Of their mother, who had signed away everything with a steady hand and never told anyone the price, because telling would have made it someone else's burden.

*Write down what is true,* August told themselves, *and the truth becomes a thing you can hold.*

The pen touched the page. Somewhere far above, snow was falling on a city that had forgotten midsummer. Somewhere near, Corvane was descending, drawn by the disturbance in his books like a shark to blood in water — August could feel it, a wrongness in the ledger's deep grammar, an entry being written in the dark. There was no time. There was never any time; that was the whole trick of ledgers, the whole tyranny of them.

August wrote their mother's name.

The strokes came out of their hand as if the hand remembered — the heavy B with its doubled bowl, the crossbar of the x trailing like a signature's last exhale. *Beatrix.* The ink drank into the page and the page drank the ink and the whole hall inhaled, one vast intake of breath through ten thousand miles of stone, and every lamp in every niche turned gold at once—

—and the doors at the far end of the mirrored hall opened, and Guildmaster Corvane walked in out of the dark, stamping snow from his boots, smiling like a man arriving at his own coronation.

"Ah," he said. "I see the family resemblance is good for something at last."
