## 17. Corvane Descends

The mountain did not want Corvane. He had known that for forty years, and he had come anyway.

He felt it in the salt road itself — the way the old stones seemed to lean away from his boots, the way the dark thickened around him like a crowd parting for a man it recognized and despised. The nameless god's hunger had carved a path down through the rock, and Ysolde's faith had widened it, and August's ledger-hand had opened the gates one by one, but none of that was Corvane's doing. He came down the last mile of the road alone, carrying nothing but a lantern of guild-make — whale oil and iron, no miracles in it at all — and the weight of forty years of bookkeeping, which was heavier than anything else he could have brought.

He had dressed for the occasion. That mattered. Down here, where everything wore a face it had stolen or bought, presentation was the only honesty left. His coat was the deep grey of the Guildmaster's office, cut in the old style, silver thread at the cuffs in the pattern of tally-marks — eleven groups of eleven, the traditional number, the number of years in a standard soul-debt. His boots were polished. His hands were clean. Whatever the court beneath the mountain thought of him, it would think it of a man who had never once let an entry go unbalanced.

The road ended at a door that was not a door — a seam in the rock shaped like a closed ledger, tall as a cathedral, its edges rimed with frost that breathed. Corvane set his palm against it the way he had done once before, thirty-one years ago, when he had been a junior factor with ink still under his nails and a superior who told him, laughing, that the guild's oldest client preferred to conduct business in person.

*Knock,* the man had said. *And whatever answers, do not tell it your name. Names are currency here.*

Corvane had knocked. Something had answered. And he had told it his name, because he was twenty-three years old and ambitious, and because he had understood even then — with the clarity that would define his whole life — that currency is only worth what you spend it on.

The seam opened now without knocking. It remembered him.

Beyond it, the court unfolded in tiers of mirrored stone, and Corvane paused at the threshold to do what he always did before entering a place of business: he took inventory.

The hall was vast and cold and lit from within — lit by lamps, hundreds of them, thousands, each one a glass vessel of the kind used in the counting house above to hold measured quantities of luck or grief or borrowed time. But these vessels held souls. They hung from chains of woven shadow, they sat in niches carved like pigeonholes, they floated in slow constellations over the mirrored floor, and each one burned with a light that was not quite fire — a light that moved the way a person moves when they are trying very hard to be still. Some of the lamps were bright. Some had guttered nearly to nothing. Corvane found, examining his own response with the detachment of long practice, that he felt no horror. Horror was for people who hadn't read the ledgers. He felt something closer to professional satisfaction, tinged with the mild melancholy of a man surveying a warehouse whose stock was, item by item, irreplaceable.

In the center of the hall, at a table of black salt, three figures sat waiting. And beside the table, standing with the particular patience of something that had all the time in the world, was the Regent — wearing Beatrix Winterbourne's face.

Corvane bowed. Not deeply. A bow between colleagues.

"Guildmaster." The Regent's voice was her voice, or close enough; thirty years of practice had worn the imitation smooth. "You've come far."

"I've come to collect my property," Corvane said. "You have my apprentice."

"Your *debtor*," said one of the figures at the table, and Corvane turned to look at it properly for the first time. It was shaped like a man but proportioned wrong — too many joints in the fingers, a face like a page someone had written on and then tried to scrape clean. One of the court's clerks, then. Even hell kept books. Especially hell. "The contract specifies—"

"The contract," Corvane said pleasantly, "specifies that the debt of B. Winterbourne transfers to whoever holds the soul-contract. I hold it. I have held it for twenty years, since the forgery was executed, and I have maintained it through every audit, every inspection, every inconvenient question from the Assize of Debts. You will find my claim is in perfect order." He reached into his coat and produced a folio — black leather, silver clasp, the master copy of the guild's oldest register — and laid it on the table of black salt with a click that echoed strangely in the vastness. "I assume you keep copies."

"We keep everything," the Regent said.

"Then we understand each other. Where is August Winterbourne?"

The Regent tilted its head — her head — and behind the borrowed eyes something moved that had never been human, something patient and vast and cold as the winter it was digesting. "You're asking me to give up a debtor in good standing. On what grounds?"

"On the grounds," Corvane said, "that I am the only person in this room who has never lied to you."

That got a reaction. The clerk-thing's pen stopped scratching. Somewhere overhead, a soul-lamp flickered. The Regent regarded him for a long moment, and Corvane met its gaze the way he had learned to meet the gaze of creditors, inspectors, and priests: steadily, without apology, offering nothing that wasn't already written down.

"You fed us," the Regent said at last. "Forty years. Two hundred souls, give or take the ones you skimmed."

"One hundred and ninety-six," Corvane corrected. "And I skimmed four, yes, because a man must eat. You'll note I declared them in my private ledger, under *expenses*. I have never cheated anyone I respected."

"And yet you come to take back our newest acquisition."

"I come," Corvane said, "to make you an offer. The same offer I made you forty years ago, when I was young enough to be frightened and smart enough to hide it. The terms have simply improved."

* * *

They walked while they talked. The Regent led him along a gallery of lamps, and Corvane looked at them with the frank appraisal of a man reviewing inventory, because that was what they were, whatever the priestess upstairs would have called them. Here, a fisherman's soul, sold by his widow to pay a debt of grief — burning low, half-eaten, its light the color of a sunset over water. There, a child's, sold by no one, taken in payment of a prayer answered — a fever broken, a drowning undone, the bill presented twenty years later when the child had grown into something the court found useful. Corvane did not look away from any of them. Looking away was a luxury. He had given up luxuries the day he signed.

"Do you know why the guild has endured?" he asked. "Four hundred years. Wars, plagues, the fall of three kingdoms, the death of every god that ever competed with us. Do you know why?"

"Tell me," said the Regent, in Beatrix's voice, and he hated it, and he let the hatred pass through him and out, the way he let everything pass through him, cleanly, leaving the books balanced.

"Because we never pretended to be good." They passed a lamp that guttered as they went by, flinching from the Regent's shadow. "The temples promised salvation and delivered sermons. The kings promised justice and delivered taxes. We promised exactly what we delivered: a price, a term, a signature. Every soul I ever brought you knew what it was buying and what it cost. That is more honesty than this world has ever gotten from anyone else, and it is why, when the old gods died, the guild survived. People don't need mercy, Guildmaster's privilege excepted. People need *terms*."

"And the winter?" the Regent asked softly. "Do the people need the winter?"

"The winter," Corvane said, "is the best thing that has ever happened to us."

He meant it. That was the thing Ysolde would never understand, the thing August would refuse to see even when it stood in front of them wearing a grey coat and holding out a partnership — he *meant* it. Snow in midsummer. Prayers answered at a price no temple dared charge. The world learning, street by frozen street, that the miraculous had a market rate after all, and that the guild was the only house trading in it. For forty years the guild had sold small wonders at ruinous margins, rationing the supply, feeding the court just enough souls to keep the taps running. Now the court was feeding openly, the sky itself was collateral, and demand — Corvane had watched the order-books with something like religious feeling — demand had tripled in a fortnight.

"Fear," he said, "is the finest solvent of resistance ever discovered. A frightened city does not ask where the luck comes from. A freezing city signs anything. In six months there will be no institution in this country that does not owe us. In two years, no country." He stopped walking and turned to face the Regent fully, and for a moment — one unguarded moment, the only one he would permit himself — he let it see what was underneath the grey coat and the balanced books: a twenty-three-year-old junior factor, kneeling at this same table, telling a monster his name because he believed, with all his young and terrible clarity, that a man who owned the terms of everything owed allegiance to nothing.

"I am seventy-two years old," Corvane said. "I have no children. I have no heirs. The guild *is* my heir, and the guild is dying upstairs — you know it as well as I do. Three factors resigned this month. The Assize is asking questions. My own apprentices are stealing my ledgers and running down salt roads with disgraced priestesses." He smiled, thin and genuine. "I need a successor who understands the work. And you need a partner who can deliver the living world into your hands without another soul spent on sieges or schisms. August Winterbourne is sentimental, principled, and fatally honest — all excellent qualities in a conscience and fatal ones in a Guildmaster. But August is also, and this is the point, the finest ledger-keeper the guild has produced in two centuries. Better than me. I say that with the objectivity of a man who has audited everyone he has ever met."

"And if August refuses?"

"August won't refuse," Corvane said. "August will *hate* refusing. That's better. Hatred keeps a man sharp. Love makes them sloppy." He glanced up at the constellation of lamps, and found — he couldn't help it — the one he was looking for. It hung near the gallery's end, apart from the others, burning a color he had only seen once before: the exact amber of a woman laughing in a counting house twenty-six years ago, explaining compound interest to her infant son as a bedtime story. He had told himself for twenty years that he'd put it there. He had told himself a great many things.

"That one," the Regent said, following his gaze. "Yours, if you want it. A retirement gift."

"No," Corvane said.

"It's the mother of the apprentice you're selling."

"I know whose it is." He adjusted his cuff. "It stays on the books. Everything stays on the books. That is the entire point of me."

* * *

They found August in a side chamber off the great hall — a smaller room, walled in mirrors, where the boy sat at a table of black salt with the reclaimed contracts spread out before him and the nameless god crouched in the corner like a beggar outside a bakery, watching the door. Ysolde stood at August's shoulder, and when Corvane entered she drew herself up with the particular fury of the righteous preparing to be right at someone.

"Corvane." August's voice was steady. Good. The boy had spine; that had never been in question. "You came yourself."

"I always collect significant accounts personally." Corvane set his lantern on the table and took the room in: the mirrors, the god, the priestess, the scattered papers in August's careful hand. Preparing something. Ledger law, most likely — the boy had been reading the court's statutes, and Corvane felt a flicker of pride so inappropriate that he filed it away for later examination. "You've been busy. Reclamation claims. Clever. Did you find the flaw?"

"A soul sold under false contract reverts to the true debtor."

"Yes. And the true debtor cannot sign. Ghosts lack standing. You'll have worked that out too, which means you're currently stuck, which means—" he spread his hands, "—you're ready to listen."

"I'm ready to watch you explain yourself," August said. "Twenty years, Corvane. My whole life. Was any of it real? The debt, the service, the—"

"The debt was forged, yes. Your mother paid it in full the day she signed with the court. Her soul for your freedom — a clean trade, properly witnessed, and I honored it to the letter. You were free, August. Free to walk out of the counting house at nineteen and never come back." Corvane let that land. He watched it land, watched the boy's certainty wobble on its foundations, because the truth was a tool like any other and he had never been squeamish about tools. "You stayed. You balanced books for impossible things for eleven years because you were *good at it*, and don't pretend otherwise. I didn't build your life. I built the door. You're the one who kept choosing the room."

"You kept the lie alive," Ysolde said. "You fed this place for forty years. You fed it *children*."

"I fed it debtors," Corvane said. "Every one of whom signed. Every one of whom knew the price. And every year I fed it fewer than the temples would have wasted in a single bad harvest, because I rationed, madam, I *rationed* — the way a physician rations morphine. This court eats regardless. It ate before the guild existed, back when the gods themselves ran the deliveries. All I ever did was stand between it and the world with a ledger in my hand and negotiate the portion size." He turned back to August. "That is the job. That has always been the job. And now the portion size is about to become the whole meal, unless someone competent sits at this table and manages the terms. I'm old. I'm offering you the seat."

He opened the folio. Inside lay a single sheet, drafted in his own hand, in the guild's formal script: articles of partnership, succession, succession of succession. He had written it on the road down, by lantern light, with fingers too cold to hold the pen well, and it was the finest document he had ever composed, and he knew, with the same terrible clarity he had carried since he was twenty-three, that it was also a confession — that every clause was a wall built against the day he died, and that walls were all he had ever built, and that he had mistaken this, for forty years, for a life's work.

"Sign," he said, "and the winter becomes a business. Manageable. Priced. Souls stop being eaten and start being *amortized* — returned, traded, leased. I can get you that. No one else alive can. Refuse, and the court takes what it takes, and the snow doesn't stop, and the lamps in that hall burn down to nothing one by one while you write beautiful claims no ghost can sign." He slid the paper across the black salt. "There is no third option, August. There is only arithmetic."

For a moment the boy said nothing. The god in the corner made a sound — low, hollow, the sound of hunger remembering itself — and Ysolde's hand found August's shoulder.

Then August looked up, and Corvane saw in the boy's face something he had seen only twice before in his long career: once in a mirror, and once across a signing table, in the eyes of a woman with amber laughter who had just sold everything she had.

"There's always a third option," August said quietly. "You taught me that. Every entry has a margin. Every margin has a space. And every space—" the boy picked up the pen, and Corvane felt, absurdly, forty years of balanced books shift beneath him like ice before the thaw, "—is somewhere to write."

"What are you doing," Corvane said. It was not a question. It was the last line of an audit that had gone wrong.

"Forging a signature," said August Winterbourne, and began to write in their mother's hand.
