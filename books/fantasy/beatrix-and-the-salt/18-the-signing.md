## 18. The Signing

The mirrored hall had gone quiet in the way a held breath is quiet.

August stood at the center of it with the reclamation laid out before them on a slab of black salt — not paper, nothing so perishable as paper down here, but a sheet of pressed stone thin as vellum, its surface crawling faintly with the court's own script, characters that rearranged themselves when unwatched like insects under a lifted rock. The terms were simple. The terms were always simple. That was the cruelty of ledgers: every monstrous thing ever done had been done in plain arithmetic.

*A soul sold under false contract may be reclaimed by the true debtor.*

The true debtor was Beatrix Winterbourne, dead twenty years, and ghosts could not sign.

But August could write in her hand.

They had discovered it without meaning to, the way one discovers most terrible inheritances. It had been Ysolde who saw it first, three nights ago on the salt road, when August was keeping their ledger of losses by firelight. *You hold your pen like someone left-handed teaching herself to be right,* the priestess had said, watching August's cramped, careful script. And then she had gone very still, and asked to see the mother's contract again, the forged one, the eleven-year sentence August had served since childhood — and there it was, side by side: the same slanting letters, the same strange little hook on the descender of *g*, the same habit of pressing so hard the line beneath showed through. Corvane had laughed at that once, early on, when August was nine and copying entries for practice. *You write like her,* he'd said, and August had thought it kindness.

It had been inventory. An asset noted. A forgery waiting twenty years for its use.

The Regent watched from across the hall, wearing August's mother's face the way a glove wears a hand. It had stopped smiling some time ago. Around them both, the lamps burned in their hundreds along the mirrored walls — stolen souls suspended in glass and salt, each one flickering at its own private rhythm, and August understood now what the rhythm was. Heartbeats. Each lamp pulsed at the tempo of the life it had been taken from, and the whole hall breathed with them, a slow tide of light, and the mountain above drew its warmth from that tide the way a leech draws blood.

"You cannot," the Regent said. Its voice was her voice, and that was the worst of it — not a mimicry, not an echo, but the exact grain and music of a woman reading aloud to a child. "You are not the debtor. You are collateral. Collateral does not sign; collateral is signed *for*."

"Then I'm doing something new." August dipped the pen.

There was no inkwell. There never had been, down here. The court wrote in what it collected, and the pen Ysolde had pressed into August's hands before they entered the hall was made of bone — the nameless god's own finger-bone, snapped off without ceremony in the dark of the last gate, offered up with the god's only remaining expression, which was hunger shaped into something almost like trust. When August touched nib to stone, the pen drank. Not ink. Warmth. August felt it leave them, a thread of heat drawn up through the arm, and understood that every word would be paid for in body-heat, in pulse, in the small coinage of being alive.

Fine. They had come down a mountain to spend themselves.

"Corvane," the Regent said, and its voice did something August had never heard a voice do: it changed registers mid-word, sliding from the mother's timbre into something older, vaster, bored the way mountains are bored. "Your asset is malfunctioning."

* * *

Corvane stepped out of the shadow between two mirrors, and August was not surprised. Of course he was here. He had been here for decades; the guild's monopoly on the miraculous had been piped straight from this hall like water from a cistern. Up close, in the lamplight of all those stolen pulses, he looked smaller than August had ever seen him — a man-shaped thing standing in a cathedral of other people's heartbeats, holding his ledger against his chest like a shield that had never once worked.

"August." His voice was the counting-house voice, level, reasonable, the voice that had explained interest rates to weeping widows for forty years. "Put the pen down."

"Tell me about the contract." August did not look away from him. "My mother's contract. Tell me it was real."

Corvane's silence lasted exactly as long as a man weighing costs. August knew that silence intimately. They had grown up inside it.

"It was real," Corvane said finally, "in every way that mattered. She came to us pregnant and desperate and she had nothing to trade but herself, and I gave her terms. Eleven years of service from her bloodline in exchange for—" He stopped.

"In exchange for what?" August's voice cracked on it. "Say it. Say what she bought."

"She bought your name out of our book." For the first time, something moved behind Corvane's eyes that was not calculation. It might have been exhaustion. It might have been shame, arriving forty years late like a debt long defaulted. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? Every soul born in this city is entered in the master ledger at birth. It's how the guild has always worked — we own you from your first breath, quietly, and most people live and die never knowing they're property. Your mother found out. She was a clerk here too, before you. She found the entry with your name in it and she couldn't bear it, so she traded the only thing of value she had left. Her soul, to the court, in exchange for your name struck from the book. You were free, August. You were the only free-born person in this city." He spread his hands. "And then I wrote you back in."

The hall's tide of light seemed to still around those words.

"I forged her signature," Corvane went on, and now the words came faster, the way confessions do once the dam breaks, ugly and relieving and unforgivable. "I created a debt that didn't exist and I bound you to it, because a free clerk is worthless to me and an indebted one is the finest instrument I ever owned. You balanced my books for eleven years. You never once questioned an entry. Do you know how rare that is? How valuable? I told myself it was mercy — that a life of useful servitude was better than a life of aimless freedom. I told myself—" He stopped again. In the mirror behind him, August could see the Regent's borrowed face, watching him with an expression of mild, patient appetite, and August understood suddenly that Corvane had rehearsed this speech for years, perhaps for the moment when he'd have to say it to a judge, or a grave, or God. He had simply always assumed he'd be saying it from a position of strength.

"You told yourself the lie was cheaper than the truth," August said. "You taught me that arithmetic myself."

"And it *was* cheaper." Corvane's voice hardened, the old Corvane surfacing like a drowned man breaking water. "It kept the guild alive. It kept the city fed while the gods slept. You think this court feeds itself? Someone has to keep the pipes open. Someone has to decide *which* souls, and how many, and I decided, year after year, and the city survived, and no one thanked me, and the alternative was winter. So yes. I lied. I built a lie with a roof on it and I put you inside it, and if you sign that page, boy, the roof comes off, and everything I kept out comes in." He pointed at the reclamation, at the lamps, at the Regent. "Sign it, and every soul in those walls goes home — fine, beautiful, let them go. But the court will not starve quietly. It will take the warmth the old way. From the world. Directly. You'll trade a hundred lamps for ten thousand hearths going cold. That is the trade you're making. I've read the terms longer than you've been alive."

"Then it's fortunate," said a voice from the doorway, rough as gravel in a tin cup, "that nobody's asking you to co-sign."

Ysolde stood at the mouth of the hall with the nameless god beside her, and even now, even here, August's chest loosened at the sight of her — the disgraced priestess with frost in her eyebrows and her god's forgiveness folded somewhere behind her ribs, carrying a lantern she no longer needed because the hall itself was light. The god shuffled forward. It looked worse than it had at the gates. Feeding the pen had cost it; feeding the road had cost it more; whatever it had spent to buy back Ysolde's memory at the toll had spent nearly all of what remained. It was translucent at the edges now. You could see the mirrors through its shoulders.

"You," the Regent said, and its face — her face — twisted with something genuine. Disgust, maybe. Or recognition. "You were ours once. Before you sold your name. You sat at this table."

"I don't remember," the god said placidly. "That's rather the point of me." It turned its blind-seeming eyes on August. "Well, clerk. Is it a fair contract?"

August looked down at the reclamation. Read it once more, top to bottom, the way they had been trained to read everything — twice, slowly, hunting the hidden clause, the buried fee, the small print where monsters lived. *A soul sold under false contract may be reclaimed by the true debtor.* One clause. No fees. The court had never needed fine print; it had never expected anyone to get this far with the pen.

"It's fair," August said. "It's the fairest thing I've ever read. That's how I know it's a trap."

"It's a trap," the god agreed cheerfully. "Everything is. Sign anyway."

"The true debtor can't sign," the Regent said, and now it had abandoned the mother's voice entirely, and what remained was vast and cold and relieved to stop pretending. "Ghosts cannot witness. Ghosts cannot sign. The law of the ledger is older than your guild, older than your gods, older than the mountain, and it does not bend for sentiment—"

"No," August said. "But it bends for handwriting."

They set the pen to the stone.

*Beatrix Winterbourne,* they wrote, in the slanting hand with the hooked descenders, pressing hard enough that the line beneath showed through — and the moment the first letter took, the hall *knew*. August felt it know. The stone warmed under their fingers; the mirrors shivered; somewhere far above, snow that had been falling for weeks hesitated mid-fall. Because the hand was not a forgery. That was the thing Corvane had never understood, the flaw in his perfect crime, the crack he himself had carved: he had taught a child to copy her letters until the copy became inheritance. August was not forging Beatrix Winterbourne's hand. August *had* her hand. It lived in their wrist. It was the last living piece of her, passed down the only way anything passes down — imperfectly, involuntarily, in the body.

The signature finished itself. August's vision grayed at the edges; the pen had drunk deep, deeper than warmth now, and they swayed, and felt Ysolde's arm catch them around the ribs and hold them upright, and heard her say, low and fierce, "Finish it. I've got you. Finish it."

*Reclaimed,* the stone wrote beneath the signature, in letters of its own. *All accounts settled.*

* * *

The first lamp went out with a sound like a sigh.

It was a small one, near the door — a soul that had perhaps been taken recently, a farmer's luck or a child's second chance — and when its light died, the light didn't vanish. It *left*. August watched it go, a bright thread pouring out of the broken glass and up through the ceiling of the hall, through miles of rock, toward the world, toward whatever small life it had been peeled away from. Then the next lamp. Then three at once. Then dozens, a cascade, the mirrored walls going dark panel by panel as the court's hoard tore itself loose and fled homeward, and with each departure the hall grew colder and brighter and emptier, and the mountain overhead groaned like a ship changing course.

"Stop them," the Regent said. It had backed against its throne of salt, and the mother's face was coming apart now — not melting, *unraveling*, threads of borrowed features lifting off the thing beneath like smoke. "Stop them, they're mine, they were *paid for*—"

"They were stolen," Ysolde said. Her voice carried down the whole hall, priestess-trained, pitched for the back pews of empty temples. "Every one. And the debt of theft comes due, Regent. You taught the guild that law. Live by it."

The Regent turned its unraveling face to Corvane.

"You," it said. "You brought them here. You fed us for forty years. Your account stands open, Guildmaster. Forty years of service rendered to this court — and service *creates* claim. You know the law. You wrote half of it."

August saw Corvane understand one full second before it happened. Saw him reach for his ledger — the master copy, the book he'd carried down the mountain clutched to his chest like an organ — and saw the ledger open itself in his arms, pages riffling backward through four decades of entries, and every soul he'd ever routed down the salt road rising off its line as a name, and every name turning to face him.

"Oh," Corvane said. Very quietly. The counting-house voice, one last time, running the numbers and finding them fatal. "Oh, that's not — the terms were *mutual*, the guild received consideration, the monopoly was the consideration, you can't call it unpaid, you *can't*—"

The creditors closed over him like water. August did not watch the end of it. Some part of them — the part that had been a nine-year-old copying letters, proud to be useful, hungry for the approval of the only tall person in their world — insisted on looking away, and August honored it. What they heard was enough: a sound like a book closing, and then no Corvane at all, just a scatter of paper-thin darkness drifting up toward the ceiling, joining the exodus of souls, though whether it was going *home* or merely going, August would never know and did not ask.

The Regent was nearly gone. It knelt in the ruins of its own face, and for a moment — one moment — the unraveling paused, and what looked up at August was not the mother's face at all but something underneath, something old and tired and strangely young, a creature that had been eating grief for centuries because it knew nothing else to eat.

"You'll freeze," it said. "Without us. All of you. We were the winter's engine and you've just put out the fire."

"Then we'll learn to make ordinary fires," August said. "We did before you. We'll manage after."

The Regent smiled then — its own smile, at last, the first honest thing it had ever worn — and came apart into salt.

* * *

The last lamp was the hardest.

It hung alone at the far end of the hall, larger than the others, older, burning a color August had no name for — gold shot through with green, the color of the light through leaves, and it pulsed slow and deep and *familiar*, and August knew whose heartbeat that was before Ysolde said it.

"Mine," she whispered. "That's — August, that's my god. That's the one I came for."

She walked to it the way pilgrims walk, and August let her go alone. But the nameless god did not. It followed her, shuffling, nearly transparent now, and when she reached up toward the lamp with shaking hands, it caught her wrist — gently, with the last of its strength.

"Not yet," it said. "Look."

And the lamp, sensing her, flared — and in its light August saw what Ysolde saw: not a soul in a jar but a presence, vast and drowsy and *hers*, the old god of her city's river-shrines, waking after decades in the dark. It had no mouth, and yet it spoke, directly into the hall, into all of them:

*Little priestess. You fled. Good. Fleeing was the holiest act of your life.* A pause, warm as sun through water. *I am free now. I will rise. But I am old, and the world has changed, and I will need a witness at my going-out — someone to carry the memory of my light into the age that follows. Not you, beloved. You have your own life to live, unledgered, at last. Him.*

The lamp's light turned, gently, toward the nameless god.

"You want me to burn," the nameless god said. It sounded pleased. It sounded, August thought, like a man being handed back a purpose he'd mislaid centuries ago. "Good. I've been cold a very long time."

"You don't have to," Ysolde said, and her voice broke clean in half. "There has to be another way, there's always — I won't let them take *you* too, I just got—"

"Hush." The god touched her cheek with a finger that was already more light than flesh. "I sold my name to keep a monopoly alive. That was the worst trade ever made in any ledger anywhere. Let me end it properly. Let me be the witness — the fire that watches the accounts close. It's the only honest work left for me." It turned to August, and grinned with a mouth that was mostly gone. "Clerk. Write it down. Everything. Somebody has to keep the true books now."

Then it stepped into the lamp.

The light that followed was not light, quite — it was *remembering*, a detonation of everything the nameless god had ever been, its whole unlabeled self given shape for one final instant: and in that instant, at the very crest of the burn, it remembered. August saw it land on the god's face like a bird coming home. One syllable. Just one, ancient and enormous, spoken once in a voice like the first morning —

and then the hall went dark, all of it, every mirror, every wall, and the mountain above them exhaled a breath it had been holding since before human memory, and somewhere impossibly far above, snow began to fall that was only snow.

In the blackness, Ysolde was crying, and laughing, and holding onto August's coat with both fists. "It knew," she kept saying. "At the end, it *knew*—"

"I know," August said. Their legs had stopped working somewhere during the signing; they were sitting on the cold floor of a dying court, holding a bone pen that had gone white and hollow as chalk. "So did I. For one second, so did I."

Above them, through the mountain's shudder, dawn was beginning — the first false dawn of a world learning to warm itself again. And in August's pocket, against their heart, the master ledger's last surviving page lay curled and blank, waiting to be written on.

They had a name to put in it. They had a road to climb.

They had, for the first time in eleven years, absolutely nothing owed to anyone — and no idea yet what a person was, once the debts were gone.
