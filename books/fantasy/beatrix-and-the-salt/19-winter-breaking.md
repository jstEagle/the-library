## 19. Winter Breaking

The mountain exhaled at the hour of dawn, though there was no dawn to speak of — only a grayness that had forgotten how to become morning. Ysolde felt it before she understood it: a shift in the air against her face, warm as breath, rising from the black mouth of the salt road behind her.

She turned. Far below, where the mountain's roots went down into the dark, something was letting go.

The snow had been falling for eleven days without stopping, and now, all at once, it stopped. Not gradually, not thinning into flurries as snow should, but *stopping* — each flake hanging in the air like a held note, then falling upward, drawn back into the sky from which the court beneath had stolen them. Ysolde watched the reversal with her mouth open and her arms full of ash.

The urn was simple clay. She had bought it in the last village still standing above the snowline, paying with a coin she'd found in the nameless god's pocket — a coin so old its king was no one anyone remembered, which seemed right somehow. The god's ashes weighed almost nothing. That was the cruelty of it. A thing that had eaten prayers for a thousand years, that had carried the hunger of every abandoned shrine in the world, that had burned itself as witness in the court beneath the mountain so that August's forgery would hold and the lamps would go out — all of it reduced to a weight Ysolde could have balanced against a loaf of bread.

It had wept at the toll gate without knowing why. It had walked beside her for three days after that, growing quieter, growing lighter, until on the third night it had sat down by a dead fire and said, in a voice like wind through an empty temple, *"I think I am finished,"* and had come apart into ash between one heartbeat and the next, gently, the way a held breath is released.

Ysolde had gathered it up with her hands. She had not used a shovel. It seemed important to use her hands.

Now the mountain breathed out below her, and the snow fell upward, and somewhere in the gray light a bird sang — one bird, tentative, testing whether the world would permit such a thing again — and Ysolde stood on the salt road with an urn of god-ash in her arms and felt grief and relief arrive together, indistinguishable, like two hands clasping hers from either side.

* * *

August woke on the mountainside to silence.

For eleven days they had slept badly, waking hourly to check the cold — the terrible professional cold of the long winter, which crept through blankets and wool and flesh alike with the patience of compound interest. Now August woke because the cold had *stopped*, and their body, trained to ledger every anomaly, registered the absence the way a counting house registers a theft.

They crawled from the shelter of stones and looked down.

The city lay far below, half-buried in white, but the white was going soft at the edges, slumping, running, becoming water. Steam rose off the rooftops in the first true warmth of midsummer-that-had-almost-not-come. In the streets, tiny figures were emerging from doorways, standing in the meltwater, some of them kneeling. From this height August could not hear them, but they could see the shapes of what people did when a winter breaks: arms raised, faces tipped back, bodies folding over other bodies.

August thought of ledgers. Of course they did; they thought of everything in ledgers, had thought in ledgers since childhood, since the counting house had become the only home they trusted. Somewhere in the world tonight, every soul-lamp that had guttered out in the mirrored hall was relighting itself — in widows' throats giving back borrowed voices, in fields warming, in prayers finally answered by nothing at all, which was the honest way for prayers to go unanswered. The court's arithmetic had unwound. The debts had come home.

And August Winterbourne had signed them home with their mother's hand.

They looked at their own fingers in the gray light, flexed them, felt the ache of the writing — hours of forging Beatrix Winterbourne's looping script while the Regent wore her face and watched, while the nameless god burned itself down to witness-seal and cinder. The hand still hurt. August suspected it always would. There was a price even for triumphant bookkeeping, and the price was knowing, forever, exactly what your own hands could do.

Below, the city bells began. First one, uncertain, then another answering, then all of them, a chaos of ringing with no liturgical order whatsoever, which was how you knew it was joy and not ritual. The sound climbed the mountain in waves.

August looked up the road, toward the summit, where Ysolde had gone before first light with the urn.

*Let me carry it,* August had said.

*No,* Ysolde had said, and her voice had left no room for appeal. *You signed him free. I'm the one who found him begging. Some things belong to some people.* She had paused at the edge of the firelight, and her face had done something complicated. *Besides. Someone should be awake when the winter breaks. Someone should see it happen and write it down. You're good at that.*

So August sat on the cold stone — less cold by the minute — and watched the world come back, and wrote it down in a small notebook that belonged to no guild, in their own hand, which was the only hand they had left and the only one they needed.

* * *

The summit took Ysolde most of the day.

The snow made the going slow even as it died; drifts collapsed underfoot into slush, and twice she went down hard, the urn clutched to her chest, swearing in the liturgical cadences of a faith that no longer existed. Her knees ached. Her lungs burned with wet air. She was fifty-one years old and had spent thirty of those years believing herself damned, and no one had told her that freedom would be this much climbing.

She talked to the ashes as she walked. She was not ashamed of it.

"You never told me anything," she said, stepping up a shelf of rock slick with meltwater. "Twenty years I served you, and you never said a word. Gods don't, do they? They just... are. And I built my whole life on your silence." She laughed, breathless. "And then I meet you begging by the docks, and you're silent too, and I think — ah. There it is again. The same quiet. And I couldn't tell if it was you or just another hole where something holy used to be."

The urn said nothing. But the sun — a real sun, breaking through the wrecked clouds for the first time in eleven days — came out at that moment and struck the clay, and the warmth of it against Ysolde's forearms felt almost like an answer, or at least like the memory of one.

"Don't do that," she muttered. "Don't be subtle. I spent my whole career being told the gods are subtle. Subtle is how they get away with everything."

She climbed. The bird followed her partway — a small brown thing, absurdly ordinary, hopping from stone to stone above the snowline, singing its four-note song over and over as if it had just invented music and could not stop practicing. Ysolde decided it was an omen. She had earned the right to decide things were omens again.

Near the top, where the air thinned and the world opened, she stopped at a shrine.

It was a small one — a niche cut into the rock, older than the guild, older than the court, older perhaps than the mountain's name. Rain and snow had worn away whatever figure once stood inside. All that remained was a shallow bowl carved into the stone floor, and the ghost of an inscription: letters eroded past reading, except one word near the bottom that time had spared for reasons known only to time.

**REMEMBER**.

Ysolde stood before it for a long moment.

"Well," she said. "That's not subtle at all."

She knelt. Her joints protested. She set the urn in the bowl, and the clay fit as if the bowl had been waiting twenty centuries for exactly this vessel, which Ysolde chose to believe it had.

"I don't know the words anymore," she said. "They took my orders when they took my ordination. I gave back the rites when I fled, all except one, and I swore I'd never use it because it's not meant for gods — it's meant for the dying." Her hands shook. She let them. "But you're both, aren't you? You've been both since the day I met you. So."

She cupped her palms around the urn, the way she had cupped them around the forehead of every dying soul in her last parish, and she spoke the rite — the old one, the forbidden one, the one that says in effect: *whatever you were, whatever you failed at, whatever you are about to stop being, you were here, and here remembers you.*

Wind moved across the summit. The four-note bird went quiet.

And in the silence, the god remembered its name.

* * *

Later — much later, when Ysolde had come down and August had climbed up to meet her, and the two of them sat on the summit stone watching the valleys turn green below — August asked what it had been like.

Ysolde considered for a long time.

"It was a word," she said at last. "Just a word. But it filled the whole sky. You know how a bell fills a church? Like that, except the church was everything." She turned her hands over, studying the ash dust that still lined the creases of her palms. "It wasn't angry. That's what I keep coming back to. A thousand years of being forgotten, of having its name sold out from under it, and when it finally remembered — it wasn't angry. It was *glad*. Glad the way you're glad when you wake from a nightmare and remember who you are and where the door is."

"What was the name?" August asked quietly. "Do you remember it?"

"No." Ysolde smiled, and there were tears in it, and she did not wipe them away. "That's the mercy of it. It passed through me like water through cloth. I heard it, and it healed something in me on the way through, and then it was gone, because names like that aren't meant to stay. They're meant to be *said*. Once. By someone. At the end." She looked out over the melting world. "I said it. Or it said itself through me. Either way. And then the ashes went out."

"Went out?"

"Like a lamp," Ysolde said. "A satisfied lamp. You know the kind — the ones in the shrine niches that burn all night, and at dawn they don't get blown out, they just... finish. All the oil becomes light, and there's nothing left to burn, and the flame doesn't die so much as *arrive*." She closed her hands over the ash stains in her palms. "There was no smoke, August. There was no smell of ending. There was a word, and then there was light with nowhere left to go, and then there was me, kneeling on a mountain, crying like a fool and grinning like one too."

August nodded slowly. Then, because they were who they were, they took out the notebook and wrote it down — the word-shaped absence, the satisfied lamp, the light arriving instead of dying — in their own hand, on their own paper, for no contract and no guild and no purpose except that some things must be recorded or they risk never having happened.

"What will you write in it next?" Ysolde asked, nodding at the notebook.

August thought of the master ledger, dissolving page by page in the counting house below. Thought of Corvane's empire of paper, burning. Thought of a grave outside the city with a headstone that read only *WINTERBOURNE*, and of a soul that had chosen to scatter rather than return, and of the question that had been sitting in their chest since the court beneath the mountain — the question of who Beatrix Winterbourne had been, besides a debtor, besides a seller, besides a name in someone else's book.

"I don't know yet," August said. "But I think—" They stopped. Started again. "When we were in the court, the Regent said something. Right at the end, as it unraveled. It was wearing her face, so maybe it was a lie, maybe it was cruelty, maybe it was the last true thing a lie ever told. But it said: *Ask the guild's oldest clerk what name she came in under.*"

Ysolde frowned. "The guild's oldest clerk?"

"The one who keeps the deep archive. The one nobody's seen leave the cellar in forty years." August closed the notebook. "Everyone calls her Mother Sable. Everyone assumes it's an honorific." They looked up, and the sun broke fully through the clouds behind them, and their shadow fell long and dark down the melting mountain, pointing toward the city, toward the counting house, toward the answer. "But honorifics are just names we give people whose real names we're afraid of. And I have a notebook now, and no guild, and no debt, and I think it's time somebody audited *her*."
