## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The wine was poisoned at the third course, between the quail and the candied pears.

Juniper Amberdale knew it the way a sailor knows weather — not by thinking but by feeling. The cup had been poured from the crystal decanter that the steward's boy carried with white-gloved hands, and the boy's gloves were wrong. New. The other servants went bare-handed through the great hall of Caer Aldric like they always had, because the steward said gloves made the guests nervous, made them think of poisoners, and the steward was very particular about what the guests thought of. But this boy wore kid leather up past his wrists, and his hands didn't sweat the way nervous boys' hands sweated. His hands were still as stones.

Juniper took the king's cup with his left hand, as he always did, and turned it twice on its stem, as he always did, and drank.

Henbane. He catalogued it without effort: the bitter-slick film across the molars, the faint smoky sweetness riding under the tannin, the way it sat in the throat like a hand laid there gently, almost kindly. Hyoscyamus niger, steeped long and low, then folded into a good red so that its harshness would hide among the oak and the dark fruit. Someone competent. Someone who knew their craft well enough to be dangerous and poorly enough to think henbane was clever.

Juniper had eaten enough poisons over nine years to have opinions about them the way other men had opinions about horses. Henbane was a coward's poison and a slow one — it wouldn't kill King Aldric tonight, only make him sick and stupid and seeing things in the corners of the hall where nothing stood. It might kill him in three days if the dose crept higher. It would certainly have killed him if Juniper hadn't been standing at his right shoulder with nine years of swallowed ruin lining his gut like armor.

He could drink this cup to the dregs and walk away with a headache and a night of sweating.

But he didn't, because beneath the henbane there was something else.

* * *

It came in late, the way a bad note comes in late to a song everyone else is singing correctly. A coldness first — actual cold, a chill on the tongue that had no business living in spiced wine — and then a sweetness that wasn't sugar and wasn't honey and wasn't any of the dozen sweeteners Juniper had learned to name for the court physicians. It was sweet the way a grave in high summer is sweet when you dig into it too fast. Sweet and old and patient, and underneath the sweetness something vast turning over in its sleep, the way a body turns over in bed, and the taste of it went down his throat and kept going, past the stomach, past everything, down somewhere Juniper did not have a word for.

He set the cup down.

His hand shook. He watched it shake the way you watch another man's hand, detached, interested. Juniper Amberdale did not shake. Nine years ago he had been a starving smuggler's runner with a quick tongue and no future, and he'd volunteered for the tasting because the pay was good and the dying was optional, and in nine years he had never once set a cup down unfinished out of fear. He had drunk scorpion venom and felt nothing. He had drunk a widow's arsenic and complimented her technique to the guards dragging her away. He had drunk things the poison manuals called impossible and woken up hungry the next morning.

This was not a poison. This was a door.

"Master Amberdale." The steward, Corvane, materialized at his elbow the way he always did, gray and dry as dust in a sunbeam. "His Majesty awaits."

"The wine is off," Juniper said. "I'll have a fresh pour."

Corvane's pale eyes moved from Juniper's face to the cup, sitting there on the cloth with an inch of dark liquid in it, and back again. Corvane missed nothing; it was his single talent and he exercised it religiously. "Off."

"Sour at the finish. You don't want his Majesty belching through the Vellish ambassador's speech." Juniper smiled the smile he used on servants and minor officials, the one that said *we are both practical men and neither of us is paid enough for drama*. "Send for the second decanter."

Something crossed Corvane's face — not suspicion exactly. Disappointment, maybe, or the flat expression of a man recalculating sums that had come out wrong. "Of course."

* * *

King Aldric ate the quail and laughed at the ambassador's jokes and did not notice that his wine arrived four minutes late from a different part of the cellar. He was sixty-one years old and looked forty, which everyone agreed was remarkable good fortune for a man who had survived two wars and a fever that killed half his court. He had thick silver hair and steady hands and an appetite like a young man's, and Juniper had spent nine years keeping him alive against all comers and had never once asked himself why the job existed. Kings get poisoned. Tasters get paid. Simple survival, simple arithmetic.

Tonight the arithmetic had a remainder that wouldn't resolve.

Juniper stood behind the king's right shoulder and did not look at the cup. He looked instead at Beatrix.

She sat three seats down at the herb master's place, which she held by right of being the only person in the castle who could keep the king's gout and the queen mother's insomnia and the whole sprawling machinery of court ailments running on willow bark and valerian. She was wearing green, because she always wore green, the exact bruised green of the sage leaves she grew on the south wall, and her dark hair was pinned up with what appeared to be a sprig of rosemary, because Beatrix had never once in her life dressed for anyone but the plants.

She was looking at the wine.

Not drinking it — she rarely drank — but watching the new pour the way a physician watches a wound, and her face was doing something complicated and terrible that Juniper had never seen on it before. He had seen Beatrix furious, Beatrix amused, Beatrix elbow-deep in a ewe's breech birth at two in the morning swearing like a drover. He had spent three years memorizing her face the way he'd once memorized the taste profiles of forty poisons, systematically, obsessively, telling himself it was professional habit.

What was on it now was grief. Old grief, worn smooth, like a stone carried in a pocket for years — and fear, fresh, rising off her like steam.

And she was looking at the wine, and her lips were moving.

Juniper couldn't hear her over the hall's roar of conversation and lute music, but he read enough of it to catch the shape: not words so much as a rhythm, a repetition, the cadence of something counted. She caught herself doing it, stopped, pressed her mouth flat, and then — deliberately, the way one turns one's back on a beggar — looked down at her plate and began very carefully not to exist.

The candles guttered, all along the high table, all at once, though there was no draft.

Nobody noticed except Juniper, and Beatrix, whose shoulders flinched.

* * *

He saved the cup. That was the part he would turn over later, in the dark, wondering when exactly he had decided, because he couldn't remember deciding. Somewhere between the candied pears and the removing of the cloth, the poisoned pour vanished from the table. A taster's first duty was evidence — every manual agreed — but Juniper had broken that rule a hundred times over nine years, flushing evidence down kitchen drains because evidence made paperwork and paperwork made questions and questions got people hanged, usually the wrong people. Tonight he cradled the crystal like a relic, tucked it inside his coat against his chest where the cold of it sank straight through linen to skin, and walked out of the banquet hall without waiting to be dismissed.

The cold did not fade against his body. If anything it deepened, settled, made itself at home. By the time he reached his rooms above the buttery, the inch of wine was colder than the cellar stone and the sweetness was still on his tongue, no stronger than before but no weaker either, as though it did not need to grow because it already considered itself fully arrived.

He lit no candle. He wrapped the cup in the wool coat he reserved for Thornfell runs and buried it in the bottom of the chest beneath false-bottomed ledgers and a coil of smuggler's rope, and he sat on the lid, and he listened to the castle.

Caer Aldric was four hundred years old and sounded like it — groaning joints, settling stone, wind worrying the gargoyles. Every servant knew the castle's voice the way a child knows a mother's breathing. Juniper had lived inside that breathing for nine years.

It had changed. Underneath the familiar sounds, faint as a pulse taken at a dying man's wrist, the stones were making a new noise. Not groaning. Humming. A deep, sweet, patient note, like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass, and when Juniper held still and let his hearing narrow to it, the note matched — exactly, perfectly — the sweetness still coating his tongue.

He got up and fetched the poison manuals. He owned eleven, three of them rare, one of them handwritten by a man the Church had burned. He went through them by lamplight, plant by plant, mineral by mineral, the venoms of beasts and the distillates of metals and the foul alchemy of spiteful monks, and every entry confirmed the same thing: henbane, yes, identified and dismissed. And beneath it, nothing. No entry, no family, no cousin. Eleven books and not one named a poison that was cold, or sweet, or — he forced himself to write it in his head, because writing it on paper felt like signing something — *alive.*

In the margin of the burned man's manual, next to the blank final page, someone long dead had written a single line in a cramped hand: *If you taste what has no name, do not swallow twice.*

Juniper closed the book and sat in the dark with his heart going hard and stupid in his chest.

Simple survival. That was the job. Taste, identify, report or don't, keep the king alive, keep yourself alive, collect your pay, mind your own gut. Nine years of clean arithmetic, and now there was a remainder: a cold sweet door standing open in a wine cup, an herbalist mouthing counts over her plate, a castle humming a note no manual named — and himself, the man whose whole life was his tongue, unable to say what he had tasted.

He should take the cup to Corvane. He should take it to the captain of the guard. He should do any of the ten sensible things a professional does with evidence.

Instead he thought of Beatrix's face, grief worn smooth as river stone, and the way she had looked at that wine like she recognized it — like she'd met it before, more than once, and knew exactly what it wanted.

There was one scholar of old rites left in this castle, and tomorrow, one way or another, Juniper Amberdale intended to have a conversation with her about herbs.
