## 2. What Beatrix Forgets

The lockbox had been under her floorboards for years, and Beatrix had no memory of putting it there.

She found it on a Tuesday, which mattered only because Tuesdays were for inventory, and inventory meant kneeling on the herb loft's cold boards counting jars of valerian and boneset while rain worked at the shutters. Her fingers found the loose plank the way her fingers always did — without instruction. Nine years in this loft had taught her hands their own geography.

But the box beneath was new to her. That was the trouble.

It was iron-banded cedar, small enough to hold bread, heavy enough to hold secrets, and it had been sitting three inches beneath her feet through every winter she could remember. She carried it to the worktable between the drying racks, set it down beside a mortar still dusted with powdered willow bark, and sat with her hands flat on the lid for a long moment, listening to the rain.

There was no keyhole. There never needed to be one; the wood had warped around its contents like it had grown there, and she pried it open with a palette knife and a patience learned from coaxing seeds out of stubborn husks.

Letters. A dozen of them, maybe more — bundled in twine gone soft with age, tied off in the neat double knot of someone who handled cord daily. Beatrix untied it because her hands were already moving before her mind agreed, and that frightened her more than anything else about the moment: the way her body knew this object. The way her thumb found the worn place on the bundle's spine as if it had rested there a hundred times before.

She unfolded the top letter.

*To the man I must not name —*

Her own handwriting. The slight forward slant of it, the way she crossed her *t*'s low and left her *r*'s open at the mouth. She had copied out labels for nine years; she knew her hand better than her own face in a mirror. It was hers beyond any doubt.

*I burned yesterday what I should not have kept,* the letter went on, *and tomorrow I will burn today's, and still I write them, because the writing is the only place he exists now. You understand. You wrote the last one too. You will write another tonight and hate yourself for it, and you will not remember hating yourself, and that will be mercy.*

*I saw him carry the king's cup again. His hands are careful. He does not know I watch him do it, and he must never know, because knowing is how the thing in the stones finds what we love, and we have both decided — haven't we? We decide it every month and then forget deciding.*

*Drink your dose. Feed it something small. Feed it me.*

Beatrix set the letter down very carefully, the way one sets down a snake one hopes is only sleeping.

She read the next one. And the next. They were all the same hand, all the same address — *the man I must not name* — and none of them named him. Some were practical, almost clinical: dosages, timings, notes on tincture strengths. *Two drops weaker than last month; the gaps are reaching past supper now.* Some were desperate in a quiet, administrative way, like ledgers of grief kept by an accountant who had forgotten what the accounts were for. One contained nothing but a pressed sprig of rosemary — *for remembrance*, said the note beneath it, in her own script — and she stared at that for longer than she could account for, feeling something move behind her ribs that had no shape and no name, like an ache in a limb amputated long ago.

She knew these things: she was the court herbalist. She had served King Aldric for eleven years, since before the taster came. She knew Juniper Amberdale the way one knows a colleague — his rounds, his silences, the careful way he held a cup to the light. She knew she liked him. She could say so plainly if asked. She liked his dry humor and the fact that he never touched the sweetmeats meant for testing until the day was done, and she liked—

And she liked—

The thought slipped sideways and would not be caught. It was like reaching into a jar for a particular leaf and finding the jar empty, finding it had perhaps always been empty, finding that she could not even swear what leaf she'd been reaching for. Only the reach remained. Only the habit of wanting, with the want itself scraped out.

*Feed it me.*

She looked at the letters and understood two things with terrible clarity, and neither of them made sense alone.

The first: she had written these. Recently. The ink on the bottom letters hadn't fully faded.

The second: she did not remember writing a single word.

* * *

She built the fire up before she burned them. That seemed right — she was a woman of method, whatever else had been taken from her, and method said dry nettle first, then the older pine knots, then the letters one page at a time so the flame caught clean and didn't gutter.

She told herself she was burning them because unread letters were dangerous. Because they referenced things she couldn't verify. Because a herbalist's loft full of paper and dried plants was no place for loose documents. Each reason was reasonable. None of them was true. She knew they weren't true while she thought them, and she thought them anyway, because apparently her mind had been trained to this — to building small false shelters over large true holes.

The first letter curled and flared. The handwriting bloomed black, then orange, then nothing.

She cried.

Not dramatically — that was the awful part. No heaving, no reason, just tears running steadily down her face while her hands went on feeding pages to the fire, calm as a cook basting meat. Her body grieved with total conviction and offered her no explanation whatsoever. It was like watching someone else weep from very far away, through glass. She tried to aim the grief — at the letters, at the man, at whoever he was — but grief without a subject doesn't aim. It pooled. It soaked everything equally: the empty mortar, the rain, the little brass scale she'd used since apprenticeship, the whole gray room.

*He does not know I watch him do it.*

Juniper. Was that his name? Was that the name she couldn't use? She turned the possibility over and it fit wrong — or fit too well? She liked him. Everyone knew she liked him. The steward teased her gently about it each solstice feast. But liking was a warm loaf of a feeling, ordinary and safe, and this grief was nine years deep and lined with dread, and those two shapes did not match. Unless liking was all that remained of something larger. Unless someone — some *her*, a her with steadier nerves and worse options — had carved the larger thing out deliberately, a surgeon removing tissue to save the body.

*You will not remember hating yourself, and that will be mercy.*

"Who was he?" she asked the fire. The fire declined to answer.

She burned them all. When the bundle was ash and the ash was cold enough to crumble, she swept it into the compost bucket with the stems and dregs, because ritual demanded completion and she had the sudden, bone-deep certainty that leaving any trace was the one forbidden act. Her hands shook only after the sweeping was done.

Then the feeling arrived.

It came slowly, the way cold comes through a wall — not a sound, not a sight, but a pressure against the inside of her skull, patient and vast and *attentive*. She had felt the castle watch her before, she realized. She'd attributed it to drafts, to the creak of old Thorn Keep settling on its foundations, to imagination. It wasn't imagination. The stones were old — older than the keep, older than the mountain road, quarried by people whose names the histories had eaten — and something lived in the quarrel of them, in the way shadows fell slightly wrong along the north wall of her loft at hours when no shadow should fall at all.

It was watching her burn the letters.

It had watched her write them.

The knowledge settled into her the way sediment settles in water, and with it came a memory that *was* hers, intact and recent, and therefore precious: last night, standing at her worktable with a dropper bottle of forget-me-not tincture — blue flowers steeped in spring water, harmless, a children's remedy for sad summers — and tipping three drops onto her own tongue, and whispering to the dark ceiling, *take this instead, take mine, leave him his loves.* And feeling the words leave her like breath, carrying something small and warm away with them. And not minding.

She stood very still in the middle of her loft, surrounded by the tools of her trade, and took inventory of herself the way she inventoried the jars. Valerian: present. Boneset: present. Memory of her mother's face: present, thank every god still listening. Memory of why she started taking a children's remedy in secret every single night: absent. Absent, and defended by absence, guarded by a hole shaped exactly like the thing that would fill it if she let it.

Because that was the trick of it, she saw now. Whatever she'd done, she'd done it *to herself*, and she'd built the forgetting as armor. If she didn't remember the man, the letters, the decision — then nothing in her could betray him. Not her face, not her voice, not the tremor in her hands when he passed her a cup. She had made herself into a locked room.

And now she'd gone and burned the key.

* * *

She slept badly, which was normal, and woke before dawn, which was also normal, and lay listening to the keep wake around her — boots on stairs, the bake ovens' first cough of smoke — feeling like a woman who has found a door in her own house she cannot account for.

Down in the kitchens, the morning's work was already loud. Beatrix descended with her basket for the usual negotiation over chamomile and mint, and stopped on the third stair from the bottom.

A scullery maid called Wren was crouched by the great hearth, half-hidden by the woodpile, lips moving fast and silent, palms pressed together the way country folk pray — thumbs crossed, fingers laced, the old grip, the one from before the New Rites, before the chapel bells. Beatrix recognized it from her grandmother. And Wren wasn't praying toward the hearth. She was angled toward the low stone channel where kitchen water ran out of the castle, toward the east, toward the river that had been rerouted underground when the keep was raised, the river nobody living had ever named aloud.

Beatrix knew the prayer too, though she shouldn't have. The cadence of it rose off the girl like heat off a kettle, and the words surfaced in Beatrix's mind unbidden, in a dialect dead four hundred years: *old one under the stones, old one under the water, we forgot you kindly, do not wake unkind—*

The hearth flames leaned east. Every candle in the kitchen guttered at once though the shutters were closed, and somewhere below the floor, below the cellar, below the roots of the mountain itself, something exhaled — a long, wet, satisfied sound, like a tide being drawn through stone — and the water in the wash basins shivered, all of them, in perfect unison.

Wren opened her eyes and smiled, radiant, certain her small god had merely listened.

Beatrix gripped the stair rail and felt, with absolute clarity, the pressure in the walls turn its attention from her — from the smoke of her burned letters — outward, downward, toward that answered prayer, and begin to stir.

The sigil was weakening. She didn't know how she knew the words *sigil* or *weakening*, the same way she hadn't known her own handwriting, but she knew, the way the body knows a wound.

And she knew, with the same borrowed certainty, exactly one person in this castle who might believe her: the man whose cup she watched, whose name she could not say, whose face her heart insisted on grieving while her mind stood empty-handed.

He was crossing the courtyard below her window right now, heading for the morning tasting, silver cup catching the early light.

Beatrix picked up her basket and went down to find him.
