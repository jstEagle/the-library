## 3. Prayers Answered

The flood came at dawn, and it smelled of henbane.

Juniper knew that smell the way other men knew their mothers' voices. Nine years of swallowing poison for King Aldric had trained his tongue into an instrument and his nose into a hunting hound, and so when he pushed open the kitchen door and the air hit him — cold water laced with something green and bitter, something that did not belong in any well in Vaelmoor Castle — he stopped on the threshold with his hand still on the latch.

The kitchens were underwater.

Not ankle-deep, not a burst pipe, not the ordinary miseries of old plumbing. The great hearth was drowned. The bread ovens gurgled like springs. Sacks of flour bobbed against the ceiling beams where they'd floated free of their ropes, fat and pale as drowned men. And the water itself — this was what made Juniper's skin crawl — the water was *moving wrong*. It circled. Slowly, patiently, in a wide spiral around the center of the room, as though the flagstones had become the bed of some patient river, as though it had all the time in the world and intended to use it.

A scullery maid sat on the long prep table, the only dry island in the room, hugging her knees. She couldn't have been more than sixteen. Her name was Wren; Juniper knew her because she was one of three people in the castle who never tried to poison anyone, which made her memorable.

"Wren," he said carefully. "What happened?"

She looked up at him with eyes gone wide and strange. "I prayed," she said.

* * *

It took him ten minutes to get the story out of her, sitting on the table beside her while the water spiraled below their feet and the cook's cat yowled from atop a shelf of copper pots.

Her brother was a fisherman on the Ald, the river that ran down from the Thornfell mountains through the valley to the sea. The river had been dying for years — silted, diverted by the king's new mills, its fish gone to nothing. And last night, scrubbing pots past midnight, Wren had done what her grandmother's grandmother had done before the mills, before the crown, before anyone remembered why you didn't speak certain names indoors.

She had knelt by the drain — the one place in the kitchen where water gathered — and she had whispered a prayer to the Drowned Mother. The river god whose name the priests had struck from every ledger two generations ago. The god who had no temple now except the memory of fishermen, and soon not even that.

"I asked her to remember us," Wren said. Her voice was flat with shock. "That's all I asked. I said, *Mother under the hill, mother under the water, we are small and we are forgotten, but we were yours once.*" She swallowed. "And then the drain started singing."

"Singing."

"Like water over stones. Like the river used to sound." Wren hugged her knees tighter. "And then it just — kept coming. It wouldn't stop. It wasn't angry. That's the worst part, Juniper. It was *glad*. Like nobody had knocked on its door in forty years and it opened the house to me."

Juniper looked down at the spiraling water. In the gray pre-dawn light, he could almost see shapes in it — the suggestion of currents, of eddies, of something vast turning over in its sleep beneath the flagstones of a king's kitchen.

He thought about the wine.

Three nights ago, at the state banquet for the Thornfell envoys, he had tasted henbane in the king's cup — expected, handled, the envoy's steward already quietly arrested. But beneath the henbane there had been something else. Something cold and sweet, like snowmelt running over honeycomb, like the smell of a cave you shouldn't enter. No poison manual named it. He had saved the cup, hidden it in his rooms behind a false brick, and told himself it was contamination, or imagination, or exhaustion.

He had been lying to himself, and he'd known it even then.

The old gods were waking. And things that slept for centuries did not wake because they chose to. They woke because something holding them down was failing.

Something like a sigil. Something like a crown.

"Wren," he said. "Who taught you that prayer?"

"My gran. She said the Drowned Mother sleeps under Thornfell, and that the kings took her crown off her bones, and that's why the river dies a little more every year." Wren shrugged helplessly. "I thought it was just a story. A thing you say to feel less alone."

Juniper stood. His legs felt distant, like they belonged to someone else. "Stay out of the kitchens until the water goes," he said. "However long that takes. And Wren — don't pray again. Not to anything. Not yet."

"Why not? It worked."

"That's exactly why not."

* * *

There was exactly one person in Vaelmoor Castle who would know whether Wren's grandmother's story was a story, and Juniper spent the walk across the inner court telling himself that this was professional necessity and nothing else.

Beatrix kept the herb loft above the stillroom, a low-beamed space that always smelled of drying yarrow and beeswax and, faintly, of the forget-me-nots she grew in boxes along the south window. He climbed the ladder and found her at her workbench, sleeves rolled, grinding something blue-gray in a mortar, her dark hair pinned up carelessly with what appeared to be a pencil.

She looked up. For half a heartbeat — he caught it, he always caught it, he was trained to catch such things — her face did something complicated. Then it settled into the familiar wry composure.

"The taster," she said. "Come to report another poisoning? Or has someone finally died of the king's cooking and you want it blamed on my herbs?"

"Flooding," he said. "The kitchens. A scullery maid prayed to the Drowned Mother and the kitchens answered."

The mortar stopped moving.

For a long moment Beatrix didn't say anything. Then she set it down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off, and turned to face him fully. "Say that again. Slower."

"A scullery maid. Prayed. To the Drowned Mother. The old river god under Thornfell. And the water came."

"And the water is still there."

"Circling. In a spiral. Around the middle of the floor."

Beatrix closed her eyes. When she opened them, something had changed in her face — the composure cracked down the middle, and underneath it Juniper saw fear, real fear, the kind he'd only ever seen on the faces of men who knew exactly how they were going to die.

"How long," she said quietly, "have you known the gods were stirring?"

"I didn't know. I suspected. Three nights ago there was something in the king's wine I couldn't name. Cold and sweet. Wrong." He stepped closer, ducking under a bundle of hanging sage. "You know what it is. Don't you. You've known longer than I have."

"You should leave," she said. "This isn't your weight to carry, Juniper. You taste cups. You keep one man alive. That's enough for any—"

"The crown." He said it flatly, watching her. "The old crown Aldric wears at every coronation anniversary. Mined out of Thornfell forty years back. Everyone says it's just gold. But gold doesn't make the stones hum. Gold doesn't put a taste in wine that no manual names. And gold doesn't scare you like this."

Beatrix laughed — a short, broken sound with no humor in it. "You impossible man. You taste everything, don't you. Including things you shouldn't."

"I'm good at my work."

"Yes." She looked away, toward the window boxes, toward the small blue flowers nodding there. "Yes, you are. And that's precisely the problem."

She crossed to the window and stood with her back to him, and Juniper watched her hands — steady hands, healer's hands — curl slowly against the sill.

"If the prayers are being answered," she said, "then the binding is thinner than I thought. Much thinner. Years thinner." Her voice dropped. "I thought I had more time."

"Binding what?"

She turned around. And here was the thing Juniper would remember afterward, in the days when remembering became the most precious and most terrible currency in the world: she looked at him then the way starving people look at bread. Longing and refusal in the same expression, love and lock both turning in the same key.

"Sit down," she said. "Pour yourself something from the blue bottle — no, not that one, the small one, left shelf. You'll need it. What I'm about to tell you is going to taste worse than anything you've ever swallowed."

He sat. He poured. The tincture burned like winter.

And Beatrix began, in a low, level voice, to tell him about the crown — about what slept coiled inside the gold, about what it ate, about what she had been doing every single day for nine years to keep it asleep — and with every sentence, Juniper understood that the simple arithmetic of his life, one man's survival measured in grains of poison, was over.

Outside the window, far below, the flooded kitchens went on circling. Patient. Glad.

Waiting to be remembered.
