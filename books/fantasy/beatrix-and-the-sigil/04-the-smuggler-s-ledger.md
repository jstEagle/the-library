## 4. The Smuggler's Ledger

The Thornfell roads had a smell Juniper never forgot: pine rot and cold iron and the faint sweetness of contraband honey. Nine years of running messages through those passes had taught him that everything in the mountains was for sale — loyalty most cheaply of all.

He'd left the castle before dawn with a forged pass and a lie about sourcing rare roots for the royal kitchens. The truth was in his saddlebag, wrapped in oilcloth: a cup of wine gone cold three days ago, still carrying that sweet cold wrongness beneath the henbane. He'd shown it to no one at court. Court was where questions got people buried.

There was one man who might answer them instead.

* * *

Maren Voss kept her counting house behind a tannery in Lowmark, because nobody with coin ever voluntarily visited a tannery. She opened the door before he finished knocking — she always did — and looked him over with the flat appraisal of a woman pricing a horse.

"You look like death's bookkeeper," she said.

"Good. That's the disguise." He stepped past her into the warmth. The room smelled of lamp oil and old paper, shelves rising to the ceiling, every ledger bound in gray leather exactly alike. Maren Voss could find any transaction in the last forty years of Thornfell smuggling inside a minute, and she charged accordingly.

"Sit," she said. "You've got the face of a man about to ask for something expensive."

"I need to know where the king's crown came from."

Her hands, which had been reaching for the kettle, went still.

"That's not a question I expected from you," she said slowly. "Poisons, routes, which captain takes bribes — fine. But the crown?"

"The crown is poisoning him. Not the kind I can taste and spit out. Something older."

Maren studied him for a long moment. Then she laughed, short and humorless, and poured two cups of tea anyway. "Forty years ago I ran cargo for a crew out of Highmoor. Before your time. Before Aldric's coronation, even. And there was a job—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening. "Juniper, some ledgers are expensive because they're heavy. This one is expensive because it's dangerous. You understand the difference?"

"I'm the king's taster. Danger is my trade."

"Your trade is dying politely if the wrong thing goes down your throat." She set the tea in front of him untouched. "This is different. This is the kind of danger that follows you home."

"Then name your price and let me decide what follows me."

She named it. It was ugly — three favors, one of them involving a shipment Juniper would have to personally escort through a checkpoint he'd spent years cultivating, burning that asset forever. He didn't blink. She noticed him not blinking, and something like respect crossed her weathered face.

"You'll regret this," she said, and turned to the shelves.

* * *

The ledger she pulled down was thinner than the others, its gray cover stained dark along one edge as though it had once been near a fire. She set it on the table between them but didn't open it.

"Crew was called the Hollow Bell. Good people, mostly. Smugglers, not tomb-robbers — there's a difference, whatever the priests say. But forty years back the money got thin, and a man named Corwin Hale came to us with a map and a commission. Someone at court wanted a mountain of gold for a coronation gift, and Hale knew where to get it." Her finger tapped the ledger's cover without opening it. "He said it was an old king's hoard. Barrow-gold. Everyone knows the barrows under Thornfell are full of it."

"And it wasn't a barrow."

"It was a cairn. There's a difference too, though it took us a while to learn it. Barrows hold dead kings. Cairns hold things that were never meant to be dug up." She finally opened the ledger, turning brittle pages until she found what she wanted — a hand-drawn map, mountain ridges rendered in faded ink, a mark deep in the high passes. "We went in with picks and lamps. The tunnels weren't natural. They were carved, and they were carved *big*, passages tall enough for a giant, and every wall covered in writing none of us could read. Except it wasn't writing. Hale figured that out first. He said the marks were more like... instructions. Like a lock."

Juniper's mouth had gone dry. "And you took the gold anyway."

"We took the gold anyway." Maren's voice had gone very quiet. "Eleven of us went in. We carried out enough treasure to buy a duchy. And within the year, nine of us were dead. Fever, accidents, one hanged himself in a chapel. Hale lasted longest. He sent me this ledger before the end — he said someone should know what the crown really was, in case anyone ever asked." She turned to the final page and rotated the book so Juniper could read it.

The handwriting was cramped, deteriorating, letters sliding downhill as though the writer's hand had grown weak. Juniper read it twice, because the first time his eyes refused to accept it.

*It is not a crown. It is a sigil. We did not steal treasure; we stole a lock off a door, and the door has been opening ever since. Whoever wears it carries the hunger. It eats what its bearer loves most — slowly, so the bearer does not notice, feeding it memory by memory, love by love, until nothing is left but the hunger itself. There is no unbinding. There is only passing it on.*

Below that, barely legible:

*If you are reading this, do not love the bearer. That is the only mercy left.*

* * *

Juniper walked out of Lowmark with the ledger against his ribs and the words repeating in his skull like a bell that wouldn't stop ringing.

*It eats what its bearer loves most.*

King Aldric had worn the crown for thirty-nine years. Juniper tried to think what the king loved. Hunting, yes. His late queen — dead twelve years now, and hadn't the grief been strangely shallow at the funeral? His daughter, distant in the south. His kingdom, in the abstract way kings loved kingdoms.

And Beatrix.

No. The thought arrived and he shoved it away, but it came back, patient as water finding cracks. Beatrix sat at the king's table three nights a week. Beatrix prepared his medicines, his tonics, the special tisane he took every evening for his nerves. Beatrix, whom the king had praised publicly just last month, saying her skill was worth more than his physicians'.

*Feeding it memory by memory.*

Juniper stopped in the middle of the road, a cart swerving around him, the driver shouting something he didn't hear. Because a memory surfaced, unwelcome: six months ago, when Beatrix had missed their shared supper — the first in years — and when he'd asked why, she'd looked at him with an expression he'd never been able to name. Not guilt. Not fear. Something closer to grief with no object attached, grief that had forgotten what it was grieving.

*I must not tell him,* her face had seemed to say. *I don't remember why, but I must not tell him.*

He'd told himself it was nothing. He'd swallowed it the way he swallowed everything — professionally, without flinching.

Now he stood in the road with a dead smuggler's warning in his bag and understood, with a clarity colder than the mountain wind, that the poison he'd been tasting in the king's wine was only the surface of it. The real poison was older. It lived in the gold on the king's head, and it fed on love, and somewhere in that castle a woman was being eaten alive and smiling through it because she didn't know what was missing.

Or worse — because she did.

* * *

He rode through the night. By the time the castle walls rose black against the graying sky, he had constructed and demolished a dozen plans. Go to the king? The king wore the thing. Tell the steward? The steward reported to the chancellor, and the chancellor's family had grown rich in the last forty years in ways no one quite explained. Burn the crown off the king's head? He'd seen what happened to men who touched the regalia without leave.

There was exactly one person in the castle who studied old rites, who read dead languages, who kept dried flowers labeled in scripts older than the kingdom. One person who burned letters she couldn't remember writing — he'd seen the ash in her grate weeks ago and filed it away as eccentricity.

One person who might already be paying the price.

The guards at the herb tower knew him and waved him through; the king's taster went where he liked, carrying baskets. He climbed the stairs two at a time, the ledger heavy under his coat, rehearsing how to say it. *Beatrix, I need to show you something. Beatrix, tell me about the king's evening tonic. Beatrix, when did you start forgetting?*

Her door was ajar. Lamplight spilled into the stairwell, and with it the smell of herbs — and beneath the rosemary and sage, faint but unmistakable, that same cold sweetness from the poisoned cup.

Juniper went very still on the landing.

Then, from inside the loft, he heard Beatrix speaking — low, urgent, to someone. Or to something. The words were in no language he knew, but the rhythm of them made the hair rise on his arms, because it sounded less like speech and more like a bargain being struck.

He pushed the door open.

Beatrix stood at her worktable, a vial raised to the lamplight, and she wasn't alone. The shadows in the corners of the loft were wrong — deeper than lamplight could explain, gathered thick as smoke, and they leaned toward her the way plants lean toward sun.

She saw him and the vial slipped from her fingers. He caught it before it hit the floor, and the liquid inside shimmered with that cold sweet light, and Beatrix looked at him with nine years of terror breaking open across her face all at once.

"Juniper," she whispered. "How much do you remember?"
