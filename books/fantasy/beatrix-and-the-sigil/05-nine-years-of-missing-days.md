## 5. Nine Years of Missing Days

He came to the herb loft at dusk, and he did not knock.

Beatrix knew his step on the stairs—she had always known it, the way she knew rain before it arrived—but this time the sound made her chest tighten in a way she couldn't name. Juniper Amberdale stood in her doorway holding a smuggler's ledger bound in oiled hide, and his face was the face of a man who had done arithmetic he wished he could unlearn.

"You've been dosing yourself," he said.

Not a question. She set down the mortar she was grinding and wiped her hands on her apron, buying three seconds, four. Outside the loft's one window, the sun bled out over Thornfell's peaks.

"Come in," she said. "Since you're already in."

He crossed the room like a man entering a temple he'd been warned against. He set the ledger on her worktable, open to the last page, where the ink had gone brown with age: *It eats what its bearer loves most.*

"How long," he said.

She could have lied. Nine years of practice said she could lie to anyone—the king himself, the priests, the captain with his too-patient eyes. But Juniper was looking at her with that taster's stillness, the same stillness he brought to a suspect cup, and she understood that lying to him now would cost more than the truth ever would.

"Nine years," she said. "Give or take a season."

* * *

She made him sit. She made tea, because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking, and because ritual is what we cling to when the story we're telling turns monstrous.

"It started the winter the crown was remounted," she said. "The goldsmith died setting the new clasp—heart failure, they said. I went to view the body because the physicians asked me to confirm no poison. His heart was fine. His *eyes* were fine. Everything about him was fine except that he was empty. Like a house swept clean of everything, down to the dust."

Juniper said nothing. He was good at silence. She'd forgotten how good—or maybe she'd never let herself memorize it.

"I went to the library. The sealed stacks. I found a rite older than Aldric's line. The sigil in the crown isn't decoration, Juniper. It's a leash. It binds the god's hunger to the king's flesh so the hunger can't roam. But leashes chafe. The thing under the mountain pulls, always, and it pulls hardest at what the bearer loves. Sooner or later it would have started eating everything Aldric loved. The queen. The princes. The kingdom, in the end."

"So you found another way to feed it."

She wrapped her hands around her cooling cup. "Memories are the cheapest coin the sigil accepts. And memories of *oneself*—" she smiled, and knew it was a bad smile, "—are cheaper still. I brew forget-me-not into a tincture. Not the flower's ordinary trick, making lovers forget each other. A deeper cutting. Each dose, I choose a memory. Small ones first. My mother's face on a market day. The name of my first pony. Then larger ones. My apprenticeship in Corvale. My father's funeral. I feed them to the sigil through my own blood—it recognizes me as kin to its keeper, some old covenant in the Nystrom line I never asked for—and it takes them and leaves the king alone."

"For nine years."

"For nine years." She looked up. "Every fortnight. A memory for a fortnight of the king's safety. It's simple accounting."

"Simple," he repeated, and his voice cracked on the word like ice on a pond. "Beatrix. Your letters. The lockbox I found burned—those were yours?"

"Gone already. Fed. Whatever they said, whoever they were for—" she watched him understand, watched it land on him like a blow he refused to flinch from, "—I paid those years ago. That's the cruelty of it. You can grieve something you can't remember grieving."

The ledger sat between them like a corpse at a wake.

"The gaps are widening," she said, before he could speak again. "That's why you're really here, isn't it? You've noticed. The way I look through you some mornings. The supper we shared last month that I set two places for but couldn't tell you why the second cup was there. It takes more now. The sigil has developed a taste. Small memories don't satisfy it anymore. Last dose I fed it the whole of my twentieth summer, and I only know I lost it because my own journal calls me a liar."

"How much is left?"

"A vial. Perhaps two doses' worth of tincture, and the flowers to make maybe one more brewing. The southern slope where forget-me-not grows wild flooded in the spring—the river god's tantrum, your scullery maid's answered prayer. What the flood didn't drown, the early frost took."

Juniper stood. He walked to the window. For a long moment he was a silhouette carved against the dying light, and Beatrix thought: *he will say we should tell the king. He will say there must be another way.* She had rehearsed arguments against every reasonable thing a reasonable man might propose.

Instead he turned and said, "How long since your last dose?"

"Eleven days. The fortnight ends tomorrow."

"And if you don't take it?"

"Then the sigil goes hungry, and it reaches for what the king loves. Or—" she hesitated, and the hesitation was itself an answer, "—or whatever else it can reach through the binding. There's bleed, Juniper. Sometimes I feel it tasting at the edges of me even between doses. Lately it doesn't feel like tasting. It feels like chewing."

* * *

He crossed the room in three steps and took her by the shoulders, and she let him, and for a moment neither of them breathed.

"You should have told me."

"When?" she asked, gently, because anger was a luxury neither of them could afford. "When we were passing the salt at supper? When you were saving the king's life with your tongue while I was spending mine in drops and droppers? There was never a good hour for it. There was only the next dose, and the next, and the arithmetic."

"You should have told me anyway."

"Yes," she admitted. "I should have. But here is the thing I learned in the sealed stacks, the thing nobody writes down: secrets shared are secrets the sigil can smell. Love is the door it uses. Every person who knows is a handhold for it to climb. I kept it alone because keeping it alone was the only way to keep it safe."

"That's not why."

She looked at him. His eyes were wet and furious and nine years late, and she realized with a strange floating clarity that she was about to do the bravest and stupidest thing left in her inventory.

"No," she said. "That's not why. I didn't tell you because you would have tried to stop me. Because you would have offered to carry it instead, and I would have let you, and then it would be *you* going hollow, cup by cup, year by year, and I would have to watch that, and I am a selfish woman, Juniper Amberdale. I have fed nine years of myself to a dead god's leash rather than watch you lose a single supper."

The words hung in the herb-smelling air. Valerian and sage and the copper tang of the tincture she kept in the blue bottle, the one she had not yet explained.

"Say it plainly," he said quietly. "Whatever it is. Say it while you still remember saying it."

And oh, that was cruel of him, and kind, both at once.

"I love you," she said. "I have loved you since the night you drank henbane for me and grinned with a numb mouth and asked if I was impressed yet. I love you in the mornings and the suppers and the arguments about whether foxglove is a poison or a medicine, which it is, obviously, both, like everything worth arguing about. I may have written it all down once, in letters I no longer remember, to a man I no longer remember naming. But I know it the way you know a taste. Not the memory of it. The thing itself. And I am telling you now, out loud, while it is still true in the only place that matters, which is my mouth and not my past."

He kissed her.

Or she kissed him—the distinction dissolved somewhere between the worktable and the wall of drying herbs, and it didn't matter, nothing mattered except that his hands were careful and her hands were not, and nine years of almosts broke open all at once like a dam that had been holding back not water but *time*, all the time they had wasted being prudent.

When they parted, foreheads touching, he was laughing or crying or both.

"There's a solution," he said. "There has to be. The cairn. The ledger says the crown was cut from a god's burial cairn in Thornfell. Counter-sigils exist, Beatrix. Old rites have old exits. We go together. We find it. We end this before your last vial runs dry."

"We?"

"I'm not leaving you alone with a bottle of forgetting and a deadline. And I have roads through Thornfell no king's soldier has ever walked. Smuggler's paths. Forty favors owed to me across three valleys." His jaw set, and she saw the other thing in his face, the thing he thought he was hiding: he had already decided that if it came down to a price, he intended to pay it himself.

She let him keep that thought. She had her own, and she would keep hers.

"The captain," she said instead. "Veyra. She watches me at court. She watches everyone, but me especially, and lately her shadow falls wrong. If we ride for Thornfell, we ride quiet, and we ride soon."

"Tomorrow," he agreed. "Before your next dose."

"Before my next dose," she echoed, and looked at the blue bottle on the shelf—her last full measure, amber liquid catching the last of the light, containing in its small glass the difference between the king's safety and her own selfhood.

Somewhere beneath the mountains, something ancient shifted in its sleep, and the stones of the castle hummed like a struck bell, and Beatrix felt it reach for her through eleven days of widening dark, patient as winter, certain as tide.

Two doses. One road. One chance.

She took Juniper's hand, and began packing.
