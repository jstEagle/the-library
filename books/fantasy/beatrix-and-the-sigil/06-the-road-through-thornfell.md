## 6. The Road Through Thornfell

The smuggler roads of Thornfell were not roads at all. They were absences — gaps in the mountain's attention, places where no tax collector looked and no king's law reached. Juniper had learned them the way he'd learned poison: one mistake at a time, each one nearly fatal.

He led Beatrix up the goat track above Miller's Drop as the sun bled out behind the western peaks.

"Smugglers call this the Widow's Stair," he said, offering his hand at a scree slope. "Because widows built it. Carrying their husbands' contraband after the mines took them."

Beatrix took his hand. Her grip was stronger than her wrists suggested, a herbalist's grip, developed from years of grinding roots against stone. "And what did the husbands smuggle?"

"Themselves, mostly. Out." He pulled her up beside him. "Ore going in unpaid, men coming out uncounted."

She was breathing hard from the climb but her eyes were bright, cataloguing everything the way she catalogued everything — the lichen on the north faces, the juniper scrub, the particular blue of altitude in the shadows. Nine years he had watched that mind work. It still astonished him.

"The ledger says the cairn is two days past the tree line," she said. "On the old shepherd paths."

"The ledger says a lot of things." He checked the sky out of habit — weather, hawks, smoke. "It also says the crown came out of that cairn, which means whatever's in it has been sitting under our feet for forty years, getting hungrier."

"And now we're walking toward it."

"Now we're walking toward it."

She laughed — short, sharp, real. "We're both idiots."

"We're both *committed* idiots. There's a distinction. It's important to smugglers."

* * *

They made the first camp in the hollow beneath a leaning shelf of granite, where a fire would read as nothing more than shepherd light to anyone watching from below. Juniper built it small and fed it dry juniper, which burned sweet and smoked less.

Beatrix sat across from him, unwrapping the bundle of supplies she'd insisted on assembling herself before they left. Dried meat. Hard cheese. A twist of paper containing something that smelled like bitter orange and iron.

"What's that?"

"Prevention." She measured a pinch into her palm. "Forget-me-not tincture, cut with ash-root. If the sigil reaches for either of us while we're up here, I want something in our blood for it to choke on."

"You've been dosing yourself with that stuff for nine years."

"I have."

"And it's been eating your memories."

She didn't flinch. That was the thing about Beatrix — she never flinched at her own wounds. "The memories were the price," she said. "Not the side effect. The sigil takes what you feed it, or it takes what the king loves. I chose which accounts to empty." She folded the paper closed with precise fingers. "Yours are mostly intact, by the way. You were never its target. It wanted the king's loves, and I stood between them wearing my own past like a shield."

"Mostly intact," Juniper repeated. "What's missing?"

Her hands went still. For a moment the firelight moved on her face and she looked, briefly, like someone standing at the edge of a very deep well.

"I don't know," she said quietly. "That's the worst part, Juniper. There might be whole years gone. Whole people. I find letters in my own handwriting addressed to strangers. I taste dishes I know I invented and can't remember inventing." She looked up. "So when you tell me about us — the suppers, the arguments, all those almosts you keep almost saying out loud — believe me when I tell you I'm not being coy. I'm trying to hold water in my hands."

Juniper fed another stick to the fire. When he trusted his voice again, he said: "Then let me be the one who remembers. Between us we make one complete person. That's an old smuggler's trick — split the cargo between riders so no single search finds it all."

"That's a terrible metaphor."

"It's how I remember everything. Bad metaphors stick."

She smiled despite herself, and for a moment the space between them across the fire felt smaller than it was, warmer than it should have been, full of every unsaid thing they'd been carrying for nine years like contraband neither dared declare.

Then Juniper heard the dogs.

* * *

They came down off the shelf with the fire drowned and the camp erased, moving along the shadow line of the ridge where the rock would break their outline. Below and behind, torchlight crawled up the switchbacks of the proper road — six points of flame, moving with military discipline, and two pale shapes ranging ahead that could only be hounds.

"Captain Vess," Juniper breathed. "I'd know that formation anywhere. She drills her outriders like clockwork. Even in the dark."

"Why is she following us?" Beatrix whispered. "If the king sent her—"

"The king doesn't know we're gone yet." Juniper's mouth had gone dry. "Which means Vess isn't following orders. She's following *it*. The thing in the crown." He remembered the audience chamber, the courtiers kneeling, the wrongness in Aldric's eyes. "It knows where we're going. Maybe it always knew."

They ran. Not the blind sprint of panic — Juniper knew better than that — but the controlled, punishing pace of the mountain trails, Beatrix matching him step for step with the stubborn endurance of someone who had hauled herb baskets up castle stairs for a decade. Behind them the torches gained, patient as tide.

By gray dawn they reached the high col below the cairn's shoulder, where the path crossed a long snowfield wedged in a bowl of cliffs. And there Juniper stopped, because the snow was wrong.

"It's too stable," he said. "Late spring. This face should be slush. Look at it — packed, glassy. Something's holding it together." He turned to Beatrix, and the words came out flat and cold. "It's bait. Or a wall. Either way, we shouldn't cross it."

Behind them, the dogs bayed — close now, close enough that he could hear the wrong note in their voices, too deep, too glad.

"There's no other way around," Beatrix said. "The ledger was clear. The cairn sits above the ice field or not at all."

Juniper looked at the snowfield. At the cliffs. At the torchlight spilling over the pass behind them like spilled oil.

"Then we cross fast and pray loudly," he said. "You said the old gods answer prayers now."

"I said one answered a scullery maid and flooded three kitchens. Pray carefully."

They were a third of the way across when the mountain screamed.

It wasn't sound exactly — it was pressure, a groan through the bones of the range, and then the crown of the cliff above them shrugged off half its face. The avalanche came down the bowl in a white flood, and it did not fall naturally; it fell *deliberately*, sheets of it peeling away from the slopes on either side, herding them, funnelling them toward the center of the field where—

Where the snow opened.

A black seam yawned in the ice directly ahead, wide as a gate, exhaling air that smelled of wet stone and old copper. Juniper grabbed Beatrix's wrist and dove for it as the white roar swallowed the world behind them.

They fell together into darkness, down a chute of smooth stone, and landed hard on ancient flagstones as the avalanche sealed the entrance behind them with a boom like a tomb door.

* * *

Silence. Then dripping water, somewhere far off, keeping time like a patient heart.

Juniper struck flint to a candle stub and light bloomed across a corridor that no human hand had built. The walls curved inward like the inside of a throat. The stones were fitted without mortar, seamless, and carved — he realized with a slow crawl of horror — carved with the same sigil that burned on the king's crown, repeating in spirals, thousands of iterations, like a prayer worn into repetition.

"Its tunnels," Beatrix whispered. She had her sleeve pressed to a gash on her forearm and hadn't mentioned it. "These aren't smuggler works. These go back further than the kingdom. Further than anything." She touched the spiral carving and snatched her fingers back. "Cold. Like touching sleep."

"Vess will dig for days and find nothing. The mountain ate our trail." Juniper helped her wrap the cut with strips of linen, working quickly, refusing to look at how pale she'd gone. "We follow these down. The ledger's last page drew a spiral — same mark. The counter-sigil should be at the center of it. The heart."

"And if the heart is occupied?"

"Then we negotiate. I'm told gods respect a good opening offer."

She laughed despite the blood, despite the dark, despite everything — and then caught his eye in the candlelight, and the laughter changed into something quieter, more dangerous.

"Juniper. Before we go down there. If it comes to a choice — if one of us has to pay whatever price this thing asks—"

"Don't."

"—then it should be me. My memories are already half-spent. I have less to lose."

"That's the worst bargain I've ever heard, and I once traded a horse for three turnips." He closed her hand around the finished bandage, holding it a moment longer than wrapping required. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "You don't get to spend yourself to save the king, Beatrix. Not alone. Not anymore. Whatever's down there, we carry it between us. Split cargo. One complete person. Remember?"

For a heartbeat she leaned into his hand. Just a heartbeat. Then duty reasserted itself in her spine like a drawn blade, and she stepped back toward the descending dark.

"Split cargo," she agreed softly. "Come on. The heart of a god won't wait, and neither will whatever Vess serves."

They walked down together into the breathing dark, and far below, something vast turned over in its sleep — and, faint as memory, began to listen.
