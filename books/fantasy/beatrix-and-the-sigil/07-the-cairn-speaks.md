## 7. The Cairn Speaks

### Juniper

The cairn was not a cairn anymore.

It had been, once — a dome of stacked stones on the shoulder of the mountain, the kind of marker shepherds add to without thinking, generation by generation. But forty years of mining had gutted it from below. What remained was a wound: a ring of shattered granite around a shaft that breathed.

That was the only word for it. The dark in the shaft moved. In and out. Slow as a sleeper's chest.

Juniper stood at the edge with Beatrix and felt the cold sweet wrongness from the banquet wine rising up out of the earth to meet him. It had been in his mouth ever since Thornfell. He'd told himself it was memory. It wasn't.

"It knows we're here," Beatrix said.

"How can you tell?"

"Because it stopped pretending to be a hole."

She was right. The breathing had changed rhythm — quickened, the way a man's does when he notices someone watching. Juniper's hand went to the ledger in his coat, the smuggler's book that had cost him three favors and a promise he didn't like thinking about. *It eats what its bearer loves most.* He'd read that line a hundred times on the road, hoping it would soften. It hadn't.

"Say the rite," he said. "Before I lose my nerve."

"You won't lose your nerve." Beatrix unslung her pack. "You're too stubborn. That's the problem."

She knelt at the rim of the shaft and began to speak in the old river-tongue — the language of prayers nobody had answered for four hundred years, until a scullery maid's wish drowned half a kitchen. Her voice was steady. Her hands were not. Juniper watched her fingers shake against her knee and understood, with the particular clarity of a man who has spent nine years tasting death in small doses, that she was more frightened than she would ever admit aloud.

He crouched beside her. "Whatever it shows us," he said quietly, "we look at it together."

"That's not how seeing works."

"Then we'll learn."

The shaft exhaled.

* * *

### Beatrix

The dead god did not speak in words. It spoke in *giving*.

Beatrix had prepared herself for horror — for teeth, for darkness, for the hunger the ledger warned of. Instead the remnant gave them its last hour alive. She felt it happen the way you feel someone else's grief settle into your own ribs: the god young and green and vast beneath the mountain, the rivers its veins, the first men laying stones over its body not in malice but in mourning, because it had chosen to die so that something worse beneath it — something hungrier — would stay asleep.

And then the miners came. Forty years ago. Iron picks through sacred stone, and the crown pried out of the burial cairn like a tooth pulled from a jaw, and the god's remnant — the small stubborn piece that refused dissolution — bound into the gold because gold remembers, and set upon a living king's head because a king must wear something.

It showed her Aldric. Young Aldric, crowned and laughing, not knowing what circled his skull. Not knowing what the crown began to drink.

Then it showed her the rest.

A vessel. The unbinding required a vessel — a living will, offered freely, to take the god's hunger out of the gold and hold it, the way a taster holds poison in his body so his king need not. And the price, written into the working like mortar between bricks:

*The vessel loses what it holds dearest. The memories themselves. Not dimmed. Gone.*

Beatrix came back to herself kneeling in the snow, her hands pressed flat to frozen ground, her face wet.

"No," she said.

Juniper was beside her, gray-faced. "You saw it too."

"The memories dearest to it." Her voice cracked on the word. "Juniper, if you do this—"

"If *I* do this." He laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. "You were going to say if *you*. Weren't you. You've been planning it since the herb loft. Don't lie to me, Bea, you've fed that thing nine years of your own head already — one more offering must feel almost tidy—"

"Stop."

"—and I am telling you, as the man whose job is knowing which cup is poisoned—" His voice broke, actually broke, and he hated it, she could see him hating it. "I'm not letting you erase yourself for him. For anyone. I'll carry the hunger. I'll forget whatever I have to."

"But you won't be *you*," she said. "Don't you understand what it showed me? It doesn't take the worst memories. It takes the best. The dearest. Whatever the vessel loves most completely — that's the toll. You'd lose—"

She couldn't finish it. Neither could he.

The wind came down off the mountain and neither of them moved. Above them the sky was the color of old pewter, and somewhere behind, faint but unmistakable, iron rang on stone. Rhythmic. Deliberate.

"He found the pass," Juniper said.

Captain Vessane. The king's own hound, who served the thing in the crown with his whole willing heart. Three days they'd led him through the smuggler roads; three days of cold camps and no fire. Now he'd stopped bothering to hide.

Juniper stood and drew her up with him. "Take the counter-sigil. Run for the lower tunnel."

"And you?"

"I'm going to buy us the time it takes to be cowardly about this later." He almost smiled. "Go."

* * *

### Beatrix

She did not go. She got exactly far enough to see what happened next, and then she turned around, because some things are worth being foolish about.

Vessane came down the slope like winter itself — armored in blackened steel, no banner, breath smoking, sword already drawn. Juniper met him with a miner's pick taken from an abandoned claim, which was a joke of a weapon against a captain of the realm, and Vessane treated it as one. He batted the first swing aside and drove Juniper into the rocks hard enough to crack a shoulder plate of the cairn's ring.

Beatrix did the only thing she knew how to do. She reached into her satchel for the henbane extract she'd carried since the court, meant for the rite, and threw the whole vial — glass and all — into Vessane's face.

It was not enough to kill. It did not need to be. The captain staggered, blind, screaming, dragging gauntlets across his eyes, and Juniper — brave, stupid, dear Juniper — did not run while the running was good. He swung the pick. It caught Vessane at the knee with a sound Beatrix would hear for years afterward, and the captain went down into the snow, and still did not stop moving, still crawled toward the shaft toward his god, dragging his ruined leg, whispering words in the old tongue that made the stones shiver.

"*Down,*" Juniper shouted. "The tunnel — now—"

The cairn answered its servant. The whole ring of standing stones groaned, tilted inward, and came down.

They ran. The mountain shrugged behind them — not an avalanche, nothing so innocent, just the god's remnant spending its last strength to bury its faithful — and the world became white noise and white powder, and Beatrix was flung forward into the dark mouth of the lower tunnel with Juniper's hand crushing hers, and then silence.

Snow dust settled. Their breathing slowed.

Something hot ran down her side. She looked down, almost curious, and saw the gash where a flying shard of granite had opened her from hip to ribs. Not deep. Deep enough. The blood was very bright against the tunnel floor.

"Oh," she said. "That's inconvenient."

Juniper was beside her instantly, tearing the lining from his coat, packing the wound with hands that shook and did not stop working. "You're fine. You're fine, this is fine—"

"Juniper."

"We have the counter-sigil, we're out, we're *alive*, you're fine—"

"*Juniper.*" She caught his wrist. Made him look at her. His eyes were wild and wet and nine years deep with everything he was about to lose, and she loved him so much it felt like the wound, like something opening. "Look at me. Remember my face. Both of us. Whatever we decide — whoever carries it — let's not waste the remembering."

For a long moment he didn't move. Then he bowed his head until his forehead touched hers, blood and snow and henbane-smell between them, and said, very quietly:

"I know which cup is poisoned, Bea. It's all of them. Every way out of this costs the same."

"Then we choose the one where the other person lives," she said. "And we cheat however we can."

Above them, muffled through forty feet of fallen mountain, Captain Vessane began, once more, to pray.
