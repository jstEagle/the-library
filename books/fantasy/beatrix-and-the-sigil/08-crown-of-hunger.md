## 8. Crown of Hunger

We rode back to the capital in a silence that had nothing left to say.

The counter-sigil rode against my chest in a leather pouch, wrapped in three layers of oilcloth and one of Beatrix's hair ribbon — her idea, not mine, and I hadn't asked why. Some things you don't ask when the asking costs more than the answer. Her wound was bound beneath her coat, a long red line across her ribs where the captain's blade had found her before the mountain did. She'd refused a litter at the last waystation. She'd refused everything except my hand getting back on the horse.

"You're favoring your left side," I said, because saying anything else was impossible.

"I'm favoring nothing. I'm riding."

"Beatrix."

"Juniper." My own name in her mouth, dry as chalk dust. "Save your worry. You'll need all of it for him."

Him. The king. Aldric, who had eaten nine years of poison from my cup and called it loyalty. Who had laughed with me over bad wine and worse poets, who trusted me the way only a man surrounded by knives can trust anyone — completely, and wrongly.

The captain's body had never been recovered from the tunnels. I told myself the mountain kept him. I told myself a lot of things on that road.

* * *

The city smelled wrong before we reached the gates.

I've spent nine years learning what wrong smells like — henbane under honey, arsenic beneath almond, the sweet cold rot of something no manual names. The capital wore its wrongness like perfume. Woodsmoke and bread and tallow, and underneath it all, faint as a memory at the edge of forgetting: that sweetness again. The taste from the banquet cup, eight months ago now, the night this began. Cold sugar. A hunger wearing flavor like a mask.

"The sigil's failing," Beatrix said quietly. She didn't look at me. She watched the walls slide past, the guards on them standing too still, faces tilted up toward the keep like men listening to music no one else could hear. "It's been failing since the cairn woke. We're out of time. We should go straight to him — tonight, before—"

"Audience," I said. "He holds audience at dawn. He'll be in the great hall with half the court between him and any door we choose."

"Then dawn."

"Dawn," I agreed, and hated how much it sounded like a promise neither of us might keep.

* * *

Dawn came up gold and rotten.

The great hall was full — full in a way it shouldn't have been at that hour. Courtiers packed shoulder to shoulder, and every one of them silent. No murmur. No rustle of silk. Two hundred people breathing in unison, slow and deep, like a tide.

Beatrix's hand found mine at the doors. Cold fingers. Steady grip.

"We do it fast," she whispered. "Palm to palm, the counter-sigil between us, speak the words backward as the god spoke them forward. That's all. Thirty heartbeats and it's done."

"And then?"

She didn't answer. In the tunnel, at the cairn, the dead thing's remnant had shown us the shape of the price with terrible clarity: *the vessel will lose what it loves most.* We'd both known what that meant before either of us said it. We'd both started lying about it immediately.

Aldric sat the throne at the far end of the hall, and my stomach dropped through nine floors of stone.

He was thin. Not gaunt — *thin*, drawn inward, his skin stretched over the architecture of his skull like wet vellum over a frame. His crown sat where it always sat, but it looked heavier now, its old gold drinking the morning light and giving none back. His eyes were open. They were fixed on nothing. And he was smiling.

"Your Majesty!" the herald cried, and his voice cracked on the second word, because even the herald could feel it — the pressure in the air, the sense of standing in a room that was filling slowly with water.

Aldric raised one hand. The court knelt. All of it. Every silk-and-velvet spine bending at once, a field of wheat under wind that wasn't blowing.

"Juniper Amberdale," the king said, and it was his voice and it wasn't. It was his voice played through something vast, something that resonated in the floor stones and behind my teeth. "My taster. Come closer. I find myself... hungry."

Every instinct I owned said run. Instead I walked forward, thirty feet of marble that felt like walking on the skin of a drum, Beatrix a step behind me with her sleeve already rolled, the counter-sigil warm against her wrist.

"Aldric," I said. Just the name. No title. Because if there was any of him left in there, I wanted him to hear it plain.

The smile widened. Too wide. The jaw unhinging by degrees, tendons rising in the neck like cables, and the courtiers kneeling below him moaned as one — not in fear. In *pleasure*. That was the worst of it. They leaned toward their king the way flowers lean toward sun, faces slack and adoring, and I understood that whatever wore his face was feeding already, sipping devotion off two hundred souls the way a man sips wine.

"He's almost gone," Beatrix breathed beside me. "Juniper — now, while the crown is distracted — give me your hand."

I gave her my hand.

Our palms pressed together around the counter-sigil, its edges biting skin, and she began the words — the dead god's liturgy reversed, syllables she'd memorized from a voice that had been dead four thousand years. The air shivered. The sweetness in the room curdled.

And the thing in the king noticed us.

The head turned. Those eyes — Aldric's brown gone milk-pale, shot through with veins of black — found me, and found her, and the smile dropped away into something older than smiles.

"*Little thief,*" it said through the king's ruined throat. "*You bring me a key? I am the door. I am the house. And I am so very, very hungry, and here you come carrying bread.*"

It moved. Not Aldric — the crown moved, and the body followed it, unfolding from the throne with joints cracking like green wood, and the whole kneeling court sighed as if witnessing a miracle. Maybe they were. Maybe it was.

"*Or,*" the god said, sweetening, the cold-sugar taste flooding my mouth until I gagged, "*or, little ones, we may bargain. One vessel. One willing throat. Give me a door of my own and the king walks free — isn't that what you crawled through my mountain to learn? Isn't that why the girl bleeds under her coat?*"

Beatrix's grip tightened. Her lips were moving through the reversal, faster now, sweat cutting lines down her temple.

"It needs consent," she said through her teeth. "That's the flaw. That's always been the flaw. It can't just take — someone has to open the door willingly. So nobody opens the door. We finish the unbinding and it goes nowhere, it starves, it—"

"*She lies,*" the god crooned. "*Ask her what else the vessel loses. Ask her what she plans to trade while you sleep, taster. Ask her whose name is written in her ledger of debts.*"

"Beatrix." I heard my own voice go flat and hard. "What did you mean to do?"

The reversal faltered. Half a heartbeat. Half a heartbeat was everything.

"I meant—" Her eyes came up to mine, grey-green and furious and terrified, and I saw it there, plain as print: she'd meant to take it herself. From the beginning. From the tunnel. From the moment the remnant showed us the price, she'd been arranging, planning, dosing me with calm the way she dosed everything, keeping me steady so that at the final moment she could step past me into the dark and call it scholarship, call it duty, call it anything but what it was.

"No," I said.

"Juniper, the words are nearly done, if you would simply hold—"

"*No.*"

Because I knew something she didn't. Nine years of tasting had taught me one lesson above all others: the dose matters less than the moment. You can survive any poison if you're the one choosing when to swallow. And the counter-sigil needed a willing vessel, and there were exactly two people in this hall who qualified, and one of them had a bleeding rib and nine years of memories that included me, and I would burn the world down before I let the god eat her name out of my mouth.

I turned my palm. Broke our grip. Took the counter-sigil alone.

"Juniper—" Her voice cracked clean through. She lunged for me.

I kissed her.

One breath. Her mouth tasted of yarrow and iron and the tea she drank too hot every morning, and I catalogued it the way I catalogue everything — bitter top note, sweet undertone, the strange cold sweetness beneath that I now understood had been the sigil all along, feeding on the edge of every hour we'd ever shared. If this was the last time I would know her taste, I wanted the whole record. I wanted evidence.

Then I stepped back, held the counter-sigil high, and spoke the reversal alone.

The hall went white.

Not light — *absence*. The god screamed through Aldric's throat and the scream came apart mid-note, unraveling like rope, and I felt the door in me open — felt it the way a lock feels a key, tumblers turning in places I didn't know I had. Cold poured in. Sweetness poured in. The hunger of a dead god, four thousand years of appetite compressed into a single swallow, and I swallowed it, because that was the job, that had always been the job, I was the king's taster and this was simply the last and largest cup.

The last thing I saw clearly was Beatrix's face. Tears cutting the dust on her cheeks. Her mouth shaping my name.

The last thing I thought was: *so that's what I'll forget.*

* * *

Silence. Then sound rushing back — courtiers gasping awake, the herald sobbing, somewhere a woman laughing hysterically into her hands. On the throne, King Aldric folded forward like a puppet with cut strings, caught himself on the armrests, and lifted his head.

His eyes were brown. Only brown. Exhausted, ancient, human.

"Amberdale?" he rasped. "Why is everyone kneeling?"

I stood in the center of the hall with a god coiled in my chest like a sleeping serpent, and I looked at Beatrix Vane across twenty feet of marble, and she was running toward me with her arms out, and I smiled at her the way you smile at a kind stranger.

Because I couldn't remember her name.

The hunger stirred, deep and patient, and somewhere far away it began, very softly, to count the things I loved.
