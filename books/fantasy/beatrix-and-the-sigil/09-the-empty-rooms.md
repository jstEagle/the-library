## 9. The Empty Rooms

The king woke at dawn, and the whole castle heard him weep.

Not the thin, frightened crying of a man who had dreamed something terrible — the deep, unguarded sobbing of a man who had been drowning for forty years and had only just broken the surface. By the time Beatrix reached the royal bedchamber, half the court had already gathered in the corridor outside, pressed together like sheep in a storm, whispering.

She pushed through them. Nobody stopped her. They were all still afraid to touch anything that had knelt yesterday.

Aldric sat up in his great bed, gray-faced and hollow-eyed, gripping his chamberlain's wrist with both hands. "How long," he was saying, over and over. "How long has it been wearing me?"

"Forty years, Your Majesty," the chamberlain said gently. "Give or take."

The king's gaze slid across the room and found Beatrix standing in the doorway with her hands still smelling of juniper and yarrow, and something in his face crumpled further. "The taster," he said. "Where is the taster? I remember — I remember him falling. I remember choosing—"

"He lives," Beatrix said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "He's below, in the infirmary. He'll want to see you when you're stronger."

The king nodded slowly, and then, because he was Aldric after all — a better man than his crown had ever let him be — he said, "Tell him the debt is acknowledged. Whatever it cost."

Beatrix bowed and left before her face could do anything she'd regret.

* * *

Juniper was sitting upright on the infirmary cot when she came down, which should have been impossible, given what she'd seen happen to him. The god's hunger had poured through him like water through a torn sail; she had watched it take him apart from the inside while he held it anyway, jaw clenched, eyes fixed on hers, mouthing words she couldn't hear over the roaring of the court. She had been certain — certain — that she was watching him die.

Instead he looked... fine. Pale, thin, a tremor in his hands he kept trying to hide by folding them. Fine in every way except one.

He looked up when she entered, and smiled politely.

It was a stranger's smile. She knew all of Juniper's smiles the way she knew her own herb beds: the crooked one he used when a lie needed selling, the flat one for courtiers he despised, the rare, unguarded one that cracked his whole face open when she said something clever about nightshade derivatives. This was none of those. This was the smile a man gives a physician who has come to change his bandages. Grateful. Distant. Correct.

"You're Beatrix," he said. "The herbalist. They told me." He said her name carefully, like a word in a language he'd studied but never spoken. "You were there? At the audience?"

"I was there."

"They say I did something. Saved the king." He flexed his fingers, studying them as though they belonged to someone else. "I don't remember any of it. I don't remember much of anything, honestly. There are — gaps." He laughed, short and embarrassed. "Nine years of gaps, they tell me. That seems careless of me."

The floor seemed very far away. Beatrix made herself cross the room and sit in the chair beside his cot, because her legs were going to give out otherwise and she preferred it not look that way.

"Do you know why you're here?" she asked. "In the castle. What your work is?"

"Taster," he said promptly. "Poison-taster to King Aldric. Nine years." He recited it like a lesson learned overnight, which, she supposed, someone had given him overnight. "I know facts. I don't know—" He gestured vaguely at his own chest. "Me. The shape of me. It's like being handed a house key with no memory of the rooms."

*The empty rooms.* That was exactly what it was. She could see it in his face — the polite blankness where nine years of suppers and arguments and almosts used to live. He had walked out of the unbinding with his body intact and his history gutted, and whatever the god's hunger hadn't eaten, the price had.

"The physicians say the memory may return," he offered, watching her face. "On its own. With time."

"Yes," Beatrix said. "They may."

They would not. She knew that the way she knew the bitterness of foxglove. The memories weren't lost — they were *held*, packed into the same vessel as the god's hunger, sealed behind the counter-sigil scarred into his sternum. And she had spent last night in her loft doing what she always did with unbearable knowledge: working. Grinding, steeping, measuring, distilling. Forget-me-not and rosemary and a thread of mandrake root, bound with a drop of the cairn-water they'd carried back through Thornfell in a stoppered vial.

An antidote. A restoration. One dose, no more — the recipe worked once, and only once, and only within a moon's turn.

One dose that would pour everything back into him. Every supper. Every argument. Every almost. His mother's kitchen. The first time he'd lied to a steward for her sake. The taste of her name in his mouth.

And threaded through all of it, inseparable, indissoluble, a fragment of the god. Sleeping, but present. A seed of hunger planted in the warm soil of a restored mind, waiting for some future weakness, some grief, some crack — and then it would begin again, and this time there would be no crown to blame, no sigil to bind it, no second unbinding possible because Juniper Amberdale had already paid the price once and the price was never charged twice.

She had known this before she brewed it. She had brewed it anyway. Her hands had needed something to do besides shake.

"How bad was I?" Juniper asked suddenly. "Before. As a person."

She blinked. "What?"

"The gaps. Everyone keeps looking at me like I'm a ghost, or worse, like I'm a *loss*. Even the king sent thanks." He turned the folded hands over. "So either I was beloved, or I owed people money. Which is it?"

And there it was — the question underneath the question, the one she couldn't answer without lying or breaking. Because he had been beloved. He had been loved specifically, particularly, by her, in the cowardly way of two people who kept finding reasons the confession could wait until spring. Nine years of shared suppers in the loft above the stillroom. Nine years of him bringing her cuttings wrapped in damp cloth, of her dosing his colds, of arguments about whether henbane could be detected under honey wine (he'd won). Nine years of almosts, and now he sat in front of her asking who he had been, and the true answer was: *you were mine, and I was yours, and neither of us ever said so, and now you never will unless I choose it for both of us.*

"You were prickly," she said. "Suspicious of everyone. You ate alone too much. You had terrible taste in poetry and knew it." Her voice caught on the last one; she covered it with a cough. "People trusted you with their lives. Literally. Daily."

"That sounds like a lot of responsibility for a man who can't remember his own favorite color."

"It's blue," she said, before she could stop herself. "Deep blue. Like bruise-wine."

He stared at her. Something flickered across his face — not recognition, nothing so kind, but a kind of unease, the way a dog lifts its head at a sound too low for human ears. "You knew me well," he said quietly.

"Well enough."

* * *

That night she sat in her herb loft with the vial in her palm.

It was small. That was the obscenity of it. Nine years, a dead god, a saved king, a man who had chosen erasure rather than let her pay — and all of it fit inside a thumb of glass, amber liquid catching the candlelight, stoppered with wax she'd sealed herself at three in the morning with tears she didn't bother wiping.

Loyalty said: give it to him. Loyalty to the truth, loyalty to the man he'd been, loyalty to the nine years that deserved better than to vanish like breath off glass. He had a right to himself. He had a right to his mother's face, his father's grave, the smuggler contacts that had saved the kingdom twice over. He had a right to her — to knowing what she'd been to him, what he'd chosen in that audience hall when he stepped between her and the captain's blade and said, *not her, take me instead.*

Truth said the same thing, in a colder voice: he would want to know. He would be furious, later, if he found out she'd had the choice and hidden it. And he would find out. Secrets in castles were candles in wind.

But the other truth — the one with teeth — sat coiled inside the vial alongside the memories. She had tasted the cairn-water during the brewing. Just a drop on her fingertip, professional habit, and for half a heartbeat she had felt something old turn over in its sleep beneath the mountains and *notice her*. The fragment in the tincture wasn't inert. It was patient. And it would wake inside a mind full of love, because love was the thing it ate, and she would have handed it a feast — handed it *him*, warm and remembering and hers again, wide open to a hunger wearing his own memories as a map.

She set the vial on the sill, next to the pot of forget-me-nots that had stopped meaning anything innocent to either of them years ago.

Below, through the floorboards, she could hear the castle settling into its new age. Bells for a healed king. Servants singing in the buttery. Somewhere down there, Juniper Amberdale was eating supper alone in the infirmary, polite and blank, thanking a nurse for bread he didn't remember liking rye over white.

Beatrix picked up the vial. Put it down. Picked it up again.

Then she took a clean strip of linen, wrote on it in her smallest, most careful hand — *For J.A. To restore what was taken. Know what rides with it before you drink* — and tied it around the glass with twine.

She would not choose for him. She had watched enough men make sacrifices without consent to know how that poison spread. But she could not hand it to him tonight, either, with her hands still shaking and the god's attention still brushing the edges of her mind like a cat deciding whether to purr or bite.

Tomorrow. Tomorrow she would bring it to him, and lay out the whole of it — the price, the risk, the fragment sleeping in the amber — and let him choose his own self, blind or seeing.

She blew out the candle.

In the dark, the forget-me-nots on the sill rustled, though the window was shut.

And far below, in the infirmary, Juniper lay awake in his cot, staring at the ceiling, turning over in his mind the strange, achingly familiar smell that had drifted up through the floorboards with the supper smoke — rosemary and mandrake and something sweet — and wondering, with an ache he had no name for, why an herb he couldn't identify smelled so much like coming home.
