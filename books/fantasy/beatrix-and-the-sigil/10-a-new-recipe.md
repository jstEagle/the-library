## 10. A New Recipe

The tincture sat in Beatrix's pocket for eleven days.

She knew the weight of it the way she knew the weight of any remedy — by habit, by the small gravitational pull of a thing meant to be used. Forget-me-not and river-moss and one petal of the cairn-flower that should not exist outside Thornfell's dark. Eleven days she carried it through her loft, up her ladder, past the drying racks where bundles of sage turned slowly in drafts that smelled of nothing anymore. Eleven days she took it out at night and held it to the lamplight and watched the liquid catch gold like something alive.

Eleven nights she put it back.

The king was well. That was the miracle the whole court toasted, the story the chroniclers were already setting down in careful ink: His Majesty Aldric, stricken before his own council by some wasting fever, restored in a single hour by the devotion of his loyal taster, who had sucked the poison from the royal cup with his own lips and very nearly died for it. It was even mostly true. The court loved it. The court needed it.

And Juniper Amberdale, hero of the hour, walked those marble corridors now with a stranger's courtesy in his face, nodding to Beatrix in passing the way one nods to a person one has been introduced to twice and cannot quite place.

*Good morrow, mistress herbalist.*

*Good morrow,* she would answer, and her voice never cracked, because nine years of practice had taught her how to hold a vial steady and a voice steadier.

But he came to the herb loft on the twelfth day, and he did not knock the way visitors knocked.

He knocked the way *he* knocked — two quick raps and then, without waiting, the sound of him already opening the door, because Juniper had never once in his life waited for permission when hunger or urgency drove him. Then he stopped, halfway over the threshold, as if the motion had been a reflex left over from someone else's body.

"Forgive me," he said. "I — I don't know why I did that."

"You always did that," Beatrix said, before she could stop herself.

Something moved behind his eyes. Not memory — she would have known memory, would have known its particular light, having spent nine years watching it dim in herself. But something. An echo finding a wall to bounce off of.

"I brought back your mortar," he said, holding up the stone pestle she'd lent him weeks ago — years ago, in the old counting — for grinding antidotes. "You said you needed it."

She hadn't said anything of the kind. She'd given it to him three winters past because he kept borrowing it and she'd decided it lived better in his rooms than hers. He couldn't have remembered that. No one could.

"Thank you," she managed.

He set it on her workbench, precisely where it had always sat, and looked around the loft with an expression she recognized too well: the polite blankness of a man reading a room written in a language he'd once spoken fluently. Drying racks. Glass vials. The crooked shelf he had built for her himself, slightly off-true, that she'd never let anyone straighten.

"It's a good workshop," he offered.

"It is."

There was a silence. In the old days silences between them had been furniture — comfortable, worn smooth, sat upon without thought. This silence was a stranger standing in the middle of the room.

"I should go," he said.

"No," Beatrix said.

Her hand had gone into her pocket without her deciding it. The vial was there, cool against her palm. One swallow and nine years came home: every supper, every argument about foxglove dosages, every night they'd stood on this exact floor arguing about whether love was a poison one built tolerance to or a medicine one took on faith. She had rehearsed the speech a hundred times. *Drink this. Remember me. Remember us.* She had imagined his face when the fog lifted. She had imagined weeping, laughter, his hand in her hair.

She had also imagined the other thing. The cairn-god's remnant, coiled quiet beneath his memories like a serpent under floorboards. The dead god's hunger sleeping inside everything he was. Wake the man, wake what slept in the man.

*It eats what its bearer loves most.*

The ledger's last line. She had burned the page but not the words.

"Stay," she said instead. "If you've no duties. I'm — I've made a dish. I make one every year at this time. Court custom." A lie so smooth it frightened her. Nine years of lying to stewards and kings, and here she was lying to the only person she'd never lied to, to spare him a truth she couldn't yet carry. "I need someone to taste it."

His brow creased. "I'm the king's taster."

"You're off duty. The king is at his prayers." She almost smiled. "Consider it private practice."

* * *

She had made honeyed lamb with burnt-barley crust and a sauce of blackberries crushed with juniper berries and one leaf of woodruff — a dish from the river valleys below Thornfell, from the villages the crown had forgotten and the old gods had not. A dish her mother had made. A dish she had made for Juniper Amberdale on a rainy autumn evening nine years ago, the first meal she ever cooked him, back when he was just the taster who'd come asking questions about henbane and stayed, somehow, for everything after.

She watched him take the first bite the way she had once watched kings take theirs: with her whole body listening.

He chewed. Swallowed. Went still.

"What—" He set down the fork. Picked it up again. Took another bite, slower, as though interrogating the food itself. "What is in this?"

"Blackberry. Juniper berry. Woodruff."

"The juniper," he said, and laughed — a short, baffled sound. "That's my name. That's absurd. Why does it taste like—"

He didn't finish. Beatrix held her breath.

Somewhere under the polite blankness, she saw it: warmth rising through cold ground. Not remembering — she knew the difference now, had learned it in her own skull, watching her own memories burn away like letters in a fire, the paper going but the shape of the ash remaining. This was the ash-shape. The groove worn deep enough that water still ran along it.

"My mother cooked something like this," he said slowly. "No. Not my mother." He frowned, pressing fingers to his temple as if he could press the thought closer to the surface. "Someone cooked this for me. Someone who argued with me about it. Who said I ate too fast and tasted nothing, and I said that was the whole *point* of being a taster, and she said—"

He stopped. Looked up at her.

"She said a man who tastes poison for a living ought to be able to taste joy too," Beatrix finished quietly. "That's what she said."

The silence stretched. Rain had started against the loft's single window, soft and steady, the same rain as that night nine years ago, or near enough that the world seemed to be repeating itself on purpose.

"I don't know who she is," Juniper said. His voice had gone rough. "I don't remember her name or her face. But I remember — not remember, that's wrong — I *feel*—" He set down the fork entirely and pressed the heel of his hand against his sternum, hard, as if something in there ached like a bruise he couldn't locate. "This room. Standing in this room eating this food. I feel like I've done it a hundred times. Like I've been happy in this exact spot and I can't find the door back to it."

Beatrix rose. Crossed the space between them. She did not touch him — not yet; her hands weren't steady enough to trust — but she stood close, close enough that he could smell the dried herbs in her hair, close as they had stood a thousand times.

"Then do it again," she said. "Eat with me. Come tomorrow, and the day after. Let me cook for you until the feeling has somewhere to live. I can't give you back what you lost." Her throat tightened. She made herself go on, made herself say the true thing at last, the thing she'd swallowed for twelve days while the vial burned in her pocket. "Juniper. There was a woman. You knew her for nine years. You loved her, and she loved you, and it cost everything, and I am telling you this because you deserve the truth even if you can't hold it." She breathed. "She isn't gone. She's standing right in front of you, asking to start again."

He looked at her for a long moment. Rain filled the silence. And then — slowly, carefully, the way a man reaches toward a flame he doesn't remember being burned by but somehow knows to respect — he reached out and tucked a strand of hair behind her ear.

"This feels," he said, "like something I used to know how to do."

"You did it very well," Beatrix said, and her voice finally broke, and she let it.

* * *

They ate the whole dish between them, passing the plate back and forth like conspirators, and he told her about his childhood in the smuggling towns — the parts he still had — and she told him safe stories, stories with the names sanded off, and when he laughed at one of them he stopped mid-laugh and stared at her with an expression of pure wonder.

"That laugh," he said. "You laugh like someone I—"

"Don't chase it," she said gently. "Let it come find you."

Outside, the rain thickened. And far below the castle, deeper than cellars, deeper than foundations, down in the dark where a dead god's hunger lay folded inside a living man's oldest memories, something stirred — not waking, not yet, not hungry — but turning over in its sleep, the way a sleeper turns toward the warmth of a fire burning in a room above.

Beatrix felt it too. She was almost sure she did. A faint warmth in the floor stones, brief as a heartbeat.

She put her hand over Juniper's on the table, and chose, freely, again, to stay.
