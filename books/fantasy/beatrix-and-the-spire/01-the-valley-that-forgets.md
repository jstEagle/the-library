## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The well at Hollowmere sang before it spoke.

Sebastian Okonkwo had learned to read its moods the way other boys learned weather. On clear mornings the rope ran smooth and the bucket rose whispering, and the water came up cold and sweet, tasting of the deep stone under the valley. But when the wind came down off the western ridge — the ridge no one climbed, the ridge where the wardline ran like a scar no eye could see — the well moaned. A low, hollow note that rose through the rope and into his palms and settled somewhere behind his breastbone, where it stayed all day like a swallowed question.

This morning it moaned.

"Stop that," he told it, the way his mother used to, before the fever took her and left him with her hands and her stubbornness and nothing else. He hauled the bucket up anyway, braced his boots against the worn lip of the stone, and let the well complain. The water came up black and mirror-still, holding a sliver of sky and one distorted boy with dark skin gone copper in the early light and hair cropped close for the forge. He tipped it into the yoke and carried it up the terraced path toward the smithy, shoulders burning, and did not look west.

He always did not look west. It was a discipline, like the breathing exercises Maren made the children do, like the way everyone in Hollowmere spoke of the outside world in the past tense, as though it had ended at the wardline the way a sentence ends at a period. *Before the war. Before the burning. Before we came here.* The valley was the place where sentences stopped.

The smithy stood at the heart of the terraces, a long low building of riverstone with a chimney that never quite drew right in damp weather. His master, Corvess, was already at the anvil, a mountain of a man gone grey at the temples, hammer falling in the patient rhythm that Sebastian had fallen asleep to for eleven years — since the night his father carried him, sleeping, through a pass he could not remember, into a valley he had never left.

"You're late," Corvess said, without looking up.

"The well was singing."

"The well sings when the wind's from the west. Wind's from the west when the world's about to change its mind." The hammer fell. *Tang.* "Water. Now."

Sebastian filled the quench trough and the slack tub and the clay jar by the door, and while he worked he let his eyes do the thing his feet were not allowed to do: wander. Out the smithy's open front, down the terraces, past the tannery and the dye-works and the low stone houses with their turf roofs steaming in the sun, to the orchard, to the millpond, to the meadow where the sheep moved like slow clouds fallen to earth. And beyond all of it, ringing everything like the rim of a cup, the ridges. Green on the south and east faces, where the pines grew thick. Bare and grey on the west, where nothing grew at all and never had, not in living memory, because magic could not cross the wardline and nothing that fed on magic — which was, the elders said, most things — cared to try.

He had stood at the edge of the meadow once, when he was nine, close enough to the western slope to see the individual stones. He remembered the feeling of it even now: like pressing his palm against the surface of a frozen pond from underneath. A smooth, total refusal. The valley did not hate the outside. It simply, absolutely, declined it.

He had turned back. He always turned back. And he had told himself, in the way of nine-year-olds and, he suspected, in the way of sixty-year-olds too, that he had turned back because he *wanted* to.

"Daydreaming," Corvess said, and this time the hammer stopped. "Give me your hand."

Sebastian gave it. Corvess turned it over, examined the blisters at the base of his fingers, the callus ridge across the palm, the old white scar where he'd caught a hot iron at twelve. The hand of a smith. The hand of a boy who would be a smith until the valley's stone wore smooth, and then be an old smith, and then be a name on a marker in the terraced cemetery with all the other names that had never left.

"You grip like your mother," Corvess said, releasing him. "Everything held too tight. Go see if Tam needs the bellows."

* * *

Tam needed the bellows, and Tam also needed someone to listen, which was the larger labor. He was eighty if he was a day, the oldest member of the guild, and he had been a hedge-witch in the low country before the burning, and he narrated his memories the way other men narrated their aches — constantly, and with great relish.

"—and the river at Marrow's Ford ran *gold*, boy, not the water, the *light* on it, because the ferryman's wife was gifted and she liked pretty things, so every morning she'd charm the sun to sit on the water like a coin, and you'd cross the river and it would be like crossing through the inside of a jewel—"

"Did you ever see the spire?" Sebastian asked, because he asked every time, and Tam answered every time, and the ritual of it had the same comfort as the bellows' rhythm.

"The Spire of the Vale." Tam's hands stilled on the bellows handles. His eyes went somewhere Sebastian could not follow. "Taller than the mountain. White stone that drank the light and gave it back warmer. And at its heart—" He stopped, as he always stopped. "At its heart, the thing that made all the rest of it possible. Gone now. Burned with everything else, or buried, or broken. Doesn't matter. It's gone, and we're here, and here is enough."

*Here is enough.* The guild's whole catechism, four words long. Sebastian worked the bellows and watched the coals brighten and thought: *enough for whom?* For Tam, who had crossed rivers of jeweled light and could measure everything after against that gold. For the elders, who remembered the world that had ended. But Sebastian had been born on the safe side of the refusal. He had been born into the *after*, into the valley that forgot, and the forgetting felt less like peace and more like a room with all the furniture pushed against one wall. Enough space to live in. No space to move.

He was twenty years old and he had never seen a river that was not the Hollow. Never seen a city, a sea, a road that went somewhere other than around. He knew the names of things — Marrow's Ford, the low country, the capital they called the Gilded Seat — the way he knew the names of stars. Real, and impossibly far, and no use to anyone hauling water.

The forge heat pressed against his face. Somewhere behind his breastbone, the well's moan was still lodged, still asking its question.

* * *

He saw Beatrix at the noon meal, as he always did.

The guild ate in the long hall by the millpond, elders at the high table and everyone else at the benches, and Beatrix sat — as she always sat — at the very end of the elders' table, alone by a choice so old it had worn smooth, like the well's stone lip. She was not the eldest. She was not, officially, anything at all; the guild's charter named her *founder, emeritus*, which everyone pronounced the way you pronounce the name of a saint you are slightly afraid of. She had built Hollowmere. She had chosen the valley, raised the first houses, set the terms of the hiding. Forty years ago. Before Sebastian's parents were his parents.

She was eating bread and winter apples and watching him.

He felt it before he saw it, the way you feel sun come out from behind a cloud. He looked up the length of the hall and there she was, grey-haired, straight-backed, her face seamed like old bark, and her eyes — pale, almost colorless, the eyes of a woman who had looked at something once that had bleached everything after — fixed on him with an expression he had spent years failing to name.

It was not fondness, or not only. It was not regret, exactly. It was the look of someone reading a letter she had written long ago and finding, in it, a mistake she could no longer correct. It was grief with the grief taken out and only the *old* left in, the way a fire leaves the shape of the wood in ash.

He looked away first. He always looked away first.

"She watches you," Tam said, leaning close, dropping his voice though everyone in the hall could have recited the conversation from memory. "Been watching you since you were a babe in arms. Your father said—"

"My father said a lot of things."

"Your father said she chose this valley for you before you were born. Said it as a joke. But your father didn't joke about her. Nobody jokes about her."

Sebastian tore his bread and said nothing. The claim was an old one, a fireside story, a thing the guild told children the way it told the story of the spire: as a wonder with no weight attached. *Beatrix chose the valley. Beatrix built the ward. Beatrix, Beatrix, Beatrix.* And somewhere in the telling, the story had grown a detail about him, and he had never known what to do with it except carry it, the way you carry a coin you suspect is foreign currency — real metal, no market here.

The noon meal ended. The benches scraped. And Beatrix rose, as she sometimes did, and crossed the hall — the whole hall went quieter as she did, the way water goes quiet over a deep place — and stopped at his bench.

"Sebastian Okonkwo."

"Founder."

That almost smiled her. "Walk with me. If your master can spare you."

"Corvess says I grip like my mother."

"Then he's seen your mother grip." She turned for the door, and he followed, and he was aware — hotly, uncomfortably — of the whole hall watching them go, the guild's two famous curiosities: the founder who had cut herself off from her own magic, and the boy who had none to cut.

* * *

She walked him up the terraces, past the cemetery with its leaning markers, past the last of the houses, to the meadow's edge. To the place he had stood at nine. She stopped there as though the ground had a name, and maybe it did; Beatrix named things the way other people prayed, constantly and in private.

"Tell me what you feel," she said.

"The wind."

"Sebastian."

He sighed and gave her the truth, because she was one of perhaps three people in the valley from whom lying had never once worked. "A wall. Smooth. Like glass under my hand. Like the well sounds when the west wind comes down."

Something moved through her face — the old expression again, the letter-with-a-mistake — and was gone. "You feel the ward," she said. "Most of them stopped feeling it years ago. They press against it so often the pressing has gone numb. But you feel it every time, like the first time." She looked at the grey slope, the bare stones, the invisible line where the world ended. "Do you know what that means?"

"That I should stop standing here?"

"It means the valley fits you like a borrowed coat." She said it plainly, without cruelty, which was somehow worse. "You've been buttoning it for twenty years and telling yourself it's yours."

The words went into him like a splinter, deep and small, and he hated her for a full three seconds, and then the hatred collapsed into the thing underneath it, which was worse: she was right, and she had been right for years, and she had waited until now to say it, and he did not know which of those facts to be angriest about.

"Why are you telling me this?" he asked. "Today?"

Beatrix turned her face west. The wind came down off the ridge and moved through the grey grass, and the well's note seemed to rise from everywhere at once, from the ground itself, from the bones of the valley.

"Because this morning," she said, "I counted the days since I last heard from the outside, and the number frightened me. Forty years is a long silence, Sebastian. Silences end." She looked at him then, and for a moment the old grief was not old at all; it was fresh as a wound, and it was aimed, he realized with a lurch, at *him* — or at whatever she saw when she looked at him, some future or some debt or some letter with a mistake in it. "And when it ends, I need to know which of us can still feel the wall."

She walked back down toward the hall without another word, straight-backed, her shadow long in the westering light, and Sebastian stood alone at the edge of the meadow with his palm out and flat against the air, against the smooth and total refusal, feeling the wall that had held his whole life — and, for the first time, feeling something else beneath it.

Far off, beyond the ridge, beyond the ward, so faint it might have been the well or might have been his own blood in his ears — a bell was ringing. Not the valley's bell. A stranger's bell. And it did not stop.
