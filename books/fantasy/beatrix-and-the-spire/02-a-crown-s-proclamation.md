## 2. A Crown's Proclamation

The bell in the meetinghouse tower rang wrong.

Sebastian knew every bell Hollowmere had to offer — the small cracked one that called the goats down from the high pasture, the deep bronze one that marked the turning of the seasons, the thin handbell old Maren used to summon the guild to supper. He knew them the way he knew the creak of the third stair and the particular groan the mill made when the river ran high. So when the tower bell began tolling at midmorning on a day with no festival, no funeral, no reason at all, he set down the basket of late apples he'd been carrying and stood very still in the orchard row, listening.

Three strikes. A pause. Three again.

He had never heard it before. That was the trouble with a valley like Hollowmere: everything strange was also unprecedented, because nothing strange ever happened here. The valley sat folded into the mountains like a coin at the bottom of a well, and the world above — the world of roads and kings and whatever else lay past the wardline — simply forgot them. That was the whole point of the place. The elders said so often enough. *Hollowmere is where the world's memory ends.*

He left the apples in the grass and ran.

* * *

By the time he reached the village green, half of Hollowmere was already there, gathered in loose, murmuring knots beneath the chestnut trees whose leaves had just begun to turn. The air smelled of woodsmoke and ripening fruit and, underneath it, something else — a tension like the moment before a summer storm breaks, when the light goes strange and the birds go quiet.

At the far edge of the green, near the eastern road where it narrowed between the standing stones, stood a figure on horseback.

Not a hunter. Not a trader. Sebastian had seen traders come up the switchback twice a year, gray-faced men with salt-crusted boots who traded iron for wool and never stayed the night. This was different. The rider wore a tabard of white and gold — crown colors, though Sebastian had only ever seen them stitched into the corner of an old map in the guildhouse — and beside him, on foot, two soldiers in matched steel stood with the patient stillness of men who had done this many times before.

"A crier," whispered Tam Vossel beside him, the miller's son, who fancied himself informed about everything. "A royal crier. My father says they haven't come past the wardline in forty years."

"Your father says a lot of things," Sebastian said, but his mouth had gone dry.

The crier unrolled a scroll. Even from across the green, Sebastian could hear the crackle of the vellum, could see the wax seal hanging from its foot like a dead red spider.

Then Beatrix was there.

She came through the crowd without pushing, without hurrying, and yet the crowd parted for her the way water parts around a keel. She was not tall. She was not imposing, not in the way the elders were imposing, with their gray beards and their measured voices. She was a small woman in a patched brown coat, sixty-some years old, with white hair cropped short and hands that looked like they had spent a lifetime doing exactly what Sebastian's did — chores, mending, the endless small labor of keeping a hidden community alive. Her eyes were pale gray, almost colorless, and when she stopped beside him she did not look at the crier at all.

She looked at the standing stones.

"Beatrix," Sebastian started. "What's—"

"Quiet," she said. It wasn't unkind. It was the voice she used when she was counting something, and Sebastian had learned long ago that when Beatrix counted, you let her count.

The crier lifted his voice. It carried across the green with the practiced ease of a man who had shouted proclamations in a hundred market squares, and it landed among the people of Hollowmere like a stone dropped into still water.

"By order of Aldric the Third, King of Vareth and its provinces, hear now the king's justice." He paused, letting the words settle. "Whereas the practice of the gift — of sorcery, hedge-craft, and all arts forbidden under the Edict of Ashes — has been found to persist within the realm; and whereas such practices endanger the peace bought by the blood of loyal subjects; the king does hereby declare a general hunt, to extend to every province, every town, every hollow and hill-country of the kingdom."

Someone behind Sebastian made a small sound. He didn't turn to see who.

"Any person found to bear the gift shall be taken into custody of the crown and judged. Any person harboring the gifted shall be judged alongside them." The crier's voice didn't waver. It was almost gentle, the way a butcher is gentle with the knife. "And to the loyal: know that the king rewards vigilance. Ten crowns of silver for each gifted soul given up. Full pardon for any crime, past or present, for those who inform. The king does not ask you to love your neighbor more than your king. He asks only that you remember which of the two can protect you."

Silence.

It was the kind of silence Sebastian had never heard in Hollowmere, where silence usually meant peace — the hush of evening, the pause between bells. This silence had teeth in it. He could feel the whole valley thinking at once, a hundred minds turning over the same arithmetic: *ten crowns. Pardon. My neighbor. My neighbor's odd daughter who never sickens. The widow on the hill whose garden grows in winter.*

Except, of course, nobody in Hollowmere bore the gift. That was the other thing about the valley — the thing the elders explained to every child by the age of six, patiently, the way you explain why you don't touch the stove. Magic could not follow you past the wardline. Whatever spark a person might carry in the wide world went dark and quiet inside Hollowmere, smothered like a candle cupped in a palm. Gifted families had fled here for generations precisely because of that. Inside the valley, everyone was equal. Inside the valley, everyone was safe.

So the proclamation was a threat aimed at no one. Wasn't it?

Sebastian looked at Beatrix.

She was still watching the standing stones, and her face — her face was doing something he had never seen it do. Beatrix's face was usually like the valley itself: settled, weathered, impossible to read anything into. But now there was a crack running through it, fine as a hairline fracture in glaze, and through the crack Sebastian saw something old and black and frightened.

"Beatrix," he said again, quieter.

"They're not here for us," she said. Almost to herself. "They can't be. They don't know we exist."

But her hand had gone to her collar, to the little iron key she wore on a cord there — the key she'd worn since before Sebastian was born, the one she touched sometimes when she thought no one was watching, the way another woman might touch a locket or a scar.

The crier rolled up his scroll. The soldiers turned their horses — one horse, rather; the soldiers stood — and the three of them rode back down the eastern road, out between the stones, and were gone as if they had never been. The bell in the tower had stopped. The chestnut leaves went on turning gold above their heads, indifferent, beautiful.

Tam Vossel exhaled loudly. "Well," he said. "Nothing to do with us, then."

Nobody answered him. Nobody moved.

* * *

The guild convened that evening in the long hall beneath the meetinghouse, the room with the low ceiling and the smoke-blackened beams where Sebastian had spent more evenings than he could count, mostly bored, listening to elders argue about grain stores and roof repairs and whether the autumn festival should feature the honey cakes or the spiced pears.

There would be no argument about pears tonight.

He sat on the bench along the wall, where the young ones always sat, close enough to hear everything and expected to say nothing. The seven elders sat in their crescent at the head of the room: Elder Coram with his beard like frost; Elder Sennet, sharp-faced, tapping one finger against the table in a rhythm that meant impatience; old Maren, who had taught Sebastian his letters and his knots and most of what he knew besides. And at the end of the crescent, apart from the others as she always was, Beatrix — founder of the guild, though she never used the word, exile, though no one said that either, keeper of whatever secret it was that made the other elders go careful-voiced around her.

"The question," Coram said, "is simple. We stay, or we scatter."

"It is not simple," said Sennet. "Nothing about this is simple. You heard the proclamation. Informers. Pardon for *any crime*. Do you understand what that means? It means the crown has just told every desperate man in Vareth that betrayal is now a virtue. It means—"

"It means they'll come looking eventually," said Maren. She was the oldest of them, older than the meetinghouse beams, and her voice was dry as seed husks. "Forty years since a crier came up the mountain. Forty years of forgetting. And now the king remembers sorcery exists. Why now? What woke him?"

"Does it matter?" Sennet snapped. "What matters is that there are roads out of this valley and every one of them leads somewhere the proclamation has already reached. If we scatter, we scatter into a kingdom that is paying silver for our heads."

"If we stay," said Coram heavily, "we stay in a bowl. And bowls are easy to find when someone goes looking."

Sebastian's stomach tightened. He had never once, in nineteen years, heard the elders speak of the valley as a trap. The valley was the refuge. The valley was the whole point. Children learned the catechism before they learned to write their names: *the world forgets us, and so we are safe.* To hear Coram call it a bowl —

"We could strengthen the ward," offered Elder Ivo, the youngest of the seven, a nervous man with ink-stained fingers. "Deepen it. If the valley forgets harder—"

"You cannot deepen what none of us can touch," Maren said. "You know that as well as I do. The ward isn't ours. It hasn't been ours since—" She stopped. Her eyes flicked, briefly, toward the end of the table.

Toward Beatrix.

The silence that followed had a shape to it, and the shape was Beatrix. Sebastian felt the whole room lean toward her without moving, the way grass leans before wind. She had not spoken since the hall filled. She sat with her hands flat on the table, the iron key resting against her collarbone, and she watched the middle distance with those colorless eyes, and she said nothing.

"Beatrix." Coram's voice gentled. "You've been quiet."

"I'm listening," she said.

"You founded this guild. Forty years ago, when the Edict fell and the burnings started, you led the first families over the mountains and found this valley. There is no one alive who knows better what a hunt looks like." Coram leaned forward. "Tell us. Do we hide, or do we run?"

For a long moment Beatrix said nothing at all. The fire popped in the hearth. Somewhere overhead, wind worried at the shingles. Sebastian realized he was holding his breath.

When she finally spoke, her voice was level and quiet and it frightened him more than shouting would have.

"Hiding worked," she said, "because the world forgot us. Proclamations are how kings make the world remember." She turned her head slowly and looked at the young ones on the wall benches — looked at *him*, Sebastian was sure of it, though her gaze seemed to pass through him to something further away. "Ten crowns is not much. But pardon — full pardon, for any crime — that is a price that will buy things ten crowns never could. Someone will talk. Not because they're wicked. Because they're frightened, and the crown has just told them fear is loyalty."

"So we run," said Sennet.

"No." Beatrix's hands curled slowly closed on the table. "Running is what I did forty years ago, and I will tell you what running looks like. It looks like burned waystations and frozen passes and children buried in shallow graves along the road. It looks like arriving somewhere new and waiting, every single day, for the knock at the door." She unfolded her hands. "I am not leading anyone onto those roads again. Not yet. Not until I know what the crown is actually hunting."

Maren frowned. "They told us what they're hunting. The gifted."

"They told us what they always tell us." Something flickered across Beatrix's face — that hairline crack again, wider now. "Kings proclaim hunts for people because people are what crowds understand. But hunts cost money, and kings do not spend money on old grudges. Something has changed. Something has been *found*, or rumored, or dreamed." She rose. She was so small standing there, and yet the whole crescent of elders seemed to shrink around her. "I need to think. No one crosses the wardline. No one speaks to travelers. And no one—" her voice caught, just barely, on the word, "—no one goes near the standing stones until I say otherwise."

She walked out. The door closed behind her with a sound no louder than a book shutting, and the elders sat looking at the space where she'd been, and nobody spoke for a long time.

* * *

Sebastian couldn't sleep.

He lay in his narrow room above the guildhouse kitchen listening to the wind come down off the peaks, and the proclamation kept replaying itself in his skull in the crier's smooth, terrible voice. *Ten crowns of silver. Full pardon for any crime.* He tried to imagine Tam Vossel informing on someone — absurd, Tam was a fool but not a monster. He tried to imagine Maren informing. Absurd. Absurd. Everyone in Hollowmere was family, or near enough.

And then he remembered the look on the faces in the green, after the crier rode away. Not horror. Not defiance. *Arithmetic.* A hundred people doing sums they hadn't known they could do until a stranger read numbers aloud to them.

He got up. He pulled on his coat and went down through the dark kitchen, out into the cold, because staying still had become unbearable.

The valley at night was a held breath. Moonlight silvered the orchard rows, the millpond, the slate roofs. Above everything, the peaks stood black against the stars, and between two of them, far to the east, he could just see the notch of the pass — the only gap in the walls of the world, the place where the road came down and the standing stones stood and the wardline ran, invisible, ancient, absolute. He had lived his entire life within sight of that notch and had never once crossed it. No one born in Hollowmere had, except the traders, and even they stopped at the stones.

He found himself walking toward them. He didn't decide to. His feet decided, and the rest of him followed, through the wet grass, past the sleeping byres, up the eastern slope with the moon throwing his shadow long and thin ahead of him.

Beatrix was already there.

She stood between the standing stones — nine of them, granite, older than any record the guild kept, taller than two men — and the moonlight made her look carved from the same material. She had her back to him. As he crunched closer over the frost-stiff grass, he saw that her hand was pressed flat against the nearest stone, and that her lips were moving, though no sound reached him.

"Beatrix?"

She didn't startle. She never startled; it was one of the thousand small mysteries of her. She lowered her hand and turned, and in the moonlight her face was stripped of everything she usually wore over it — the patience, the mildness, the settled calm. What was underneath looked young, somehow, and terribly tired.

"You should be asleep," she said.

"Everyone should be asleep," Sebastian said. "Nobody is. I could hear the houses from the guildhouse. Doors opening. People standing outside in the dark, looking east." He stopped a few feet from her, breath fogging. "Beatrix — what aren't they saying? In there tonight. What aren't *you* saying? Forty years, and Maren won't even finish a sentence about the ward when you're in the room. Coram asks your counsel like you're — like you're something more than an elder. And today, when that crier read the king's words, you looked at these stones like—"

"Like what?" Quietly.

"Like they owed you something," he said. "Or like you owed them."

The wind moved through the pass above them, a long low moan over granite. Beatrix looked up at the notch in the peaks, at the thread of starlit sky beyond it where the rest of the kingdom lay — the kingdom that had, that afternoon, declared war on people like her. Except she wasn't like anyone. That was the thing Sebastian kept circling and never landing on. The founder who wouldn't say founder. The exile no one named. The key at her throat she'd worn for forty years.

"Sebastian," she said at last, "how old do you think I am?"

He blinked. "Sixty. Sixty-five. Maren says—"

"Maren is being polite." A ghost of something crossed her face that was almost a smile and wasn't. "I was thirty-one when I led the first families over the pass. Do the sum."

Sebastian did, and the answer arrived in his chest before it arrived in his head, cold as the frost underfoot. Seventy-six. Older than Maren. Older than the guild itself, older than—

"That's not possible," he said. "You'd have — the ward doesn't stop aging, it stops *magic*, and—"

"No," Beatrix agreed softly. "It doesn't stop magic. It stops the gift. And I haven't carried the gift for a very long time, child. I carried something else instead. Something that keeps a body going long past its season, the way a wick keeps burning after the wax is gone." Her hand rose to the iron key at her throat and closed around it. "The valley is safe because of me. And the valley is hunted now because of me. And tomorrow — or the day after, or however long it takes for the crown's riders to work their way up every road in Vareth — I am going to have to tell you what I buried, and where, and why the king's men crossed a mountain range in autumn to read a proclamation to a valley that shouldn't exist on any map."

The standing stones rose around them, nine gray sentinels, and for the first time in his life Sebastian wondered — really wondered — what they were *for*. Not boundary markers. Boundaries didn't hum. He was almost certain, standing there in the dark, that he could feel a hum, faint as a bee trapped behind a wall, coming up through the soles of his boots.

"Beatrix," he whispered. "What did you bury?"

She looked at him for a long moment. Above the pass, a cloud slid across the moon, and the valley went dark as a closed eye.

"Not here," she said. "Tomorrow. Bring the lantern from my cellar — the one with the green glass. And Sebastian?" She was already walking away, back down toward the sleeping village, her shadow folding into hers. "Whatever I show you, whatever I tell you — remember that I hid it to keep people safe. Remember that, afterward. When it would be easier to remember something else."

The cloud passed. The moonlight returned. And on the eastern road, far below the standing stones, impossibly far, at the very edge of hearing — a horn sounded. Once. Long. Then silence.

Beatrix stopped walking.

Sebastian watched her stand there in the silver dark, a small straight-backed figure with seventy-six years in her shoulders, and he saw her hand tighten around the iron key until the knuckles went white.

"They're early," she said.
