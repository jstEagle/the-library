## 3. What Beatrix Buried

The valley smelled of rain that had not yet fallen.

Beatrix felt it in her teeth first — the old ache where her magic used to live, a phantom limb forty years gone and still complaining about weather. Then in the air itself: iron and wet stone, the green rot of the marsh at Hollowmere's southern edge, woodsmoke from Tam's forge lying flat under a sky the color of unpolished pewter. The valley had always been good at smelling like safety. That was its whole purpose. She had built it to smell like home so that no one would ever think to ask what it was built on top of.

She stood at the window of the longhouse, watching Sebastian cross the yard with two buckets of water he was carrying as though they had personally insulted him.

Forty years old, that boy's stubbornness, though he was barely nineteen. She recognized it because she had worn it herself once — the particular anger of someone who has been told all his life that he is safe, when what he wanted was to be needed. The valley gave its children safety the way a rich aunt gives socks. It was never what they asked for.

He set the buckets down by the well, glared at the horizon as if it owed him money, and picked them up again. Beatrix smiled despite herself, and then the smile went out of her like a candle pinched between wet fingers, because beyond him, past the wardline she could feel humming in her bones like a struck bell that would not finish ringing, there were riders on the king's road. Three of them. Grey cloaks. They did not enter. They did not need to. They were measuring.

They had been measuring for six days.

* * *

She found him that evening in the granary, which was where he went when he wanted to be alone without anyone thinking he was being dramatic about it. He was sorting barley by lamplight, separating the good grain from the mouse-nibbled with a violence entirely disproportionate to the task. Each bad kernel he flicked into the discard pail like a small execution.

"You'll wear your fingers to the bone," Beatrix said from the doorway.

"Better than wearing them soft." He didn't look up. "That's what you always say."

"It is what I always say. I say a great many things. Most of them are true, which is why they bear repeating." She came in and sat on the flour sacks across from him, lowering herself with the care of a woman whose knees had opinions. The lamp threw their shadows huge against the rafters, two shapes that might have been anything. "Sebastian. Put down the barley."

He put down the barley. That was the thing about him — beneath all that flint and grievance, he was obedient in the ways that mattered. He had been since he was seven years old and she'd caught him stealing honeycomb and instead of scolding him she'd taught him how to keep bees. He looked at her now with those dark, level eyes, waiting, and she understood that he already knew something was wrong. Children of hidden places always know. They hear the floorboards change their tune.

"The crier," she said. "You heard what he read at the ford."

"Everyone heard." His jaw tightened. "Bounty for the gifted. Pardon for informers. *By order of the Crown, all persons harboring—*" He stopped, mouth working. "Elder Maren says we hide deeper. Elder Corvin says we scatter. They've been shouting about it for two days."

"And what do you say?"

"I say the wardline has held for forty years. I say nobody's found us in forty years." He said it too firmly, the way people state things they want to be true. "Why would they find us now?"

Beatrix looked at her hands. They were an old woman's hands, spotted and knuckled, stained permanently at the nails with ink and lye. Once they had been able to hold lightning the way other hands held a sparrow. She flexed them and felt nothing, the great nothing she had lived inside for four decades, the silence where her gift had been — and underneath the nothing, buried deeper than the silence, the weight of what she had done to buy that silence.

"Because they aren't hunting people, Sebastian," she said. "Not primarily. People are the excuse. The crier's proclamation, the bounties, the burnings in the east — that's the net. But nets are thrown at fish. The crown is fishing for one thing only."

The lamp guttered. Outside, the first drops of the promised rain began ticking against the granary roof, soft as fingertips.

"What thing?" he asked, and his voice had gone quiet in the way voices do when they already dread the answer.

"A heart," Beatrix said. "The heart of the Spire."

* * *

She told him standing up, because some stories will not sit still.

She took him out into the rain, past the longhouse, past the bee-yard where his hives slept under their winter wraps, up the sheep path to the bald granite shoulder of the hill the valley folk called the Watchstone. From there all of Hollowmere lay below them — forty roofs, the forge, the terraced gardens, the schoolroom where she had taught three generations of children letters and herb-lore and how to lie beautifully to tax collectors. Rain silvered everything. The wardline ran along the valley's rim like a ring of held breath, invisible, immense, older than any of them except her.

"Do you know what this is?" she asked, gesturing at the valley entire.

"Our refuge. The guild's refuge." He pulled his collar up against the rain. "The place magic can't follow. You've said it a thousand times. Magic can't cross the line. That's why the gifted who come here lose their gifts, and why the hunters can't sense anyone inside."

"That is what I said. Now I'll tell you what is true." She turned to face him fully, rain running down her face, and saw him brace himself the way people brace before a wave breaks over them. "Magic doesn't fail to cross the wardline, boy. Magic is *eaten*. Something at the center of this valley drinks it down. Every gift that ever crossed that line, every spark of power in every fugitive who came here seeking shelter — it wasn't dampened. It was devoured. Fed to a fire that has been sleeping under our feet since before the guild existed."

Sebastian stared at her. Rain dripped off his chin. "What fire? There's nothing under the valley but rock and root cellars."

"There is a stone," Beatrix said, "no bigger than a loaf of bread. It sits in a lead-lined box behind a false wall in a cellar that officially does not exist, beneath the ruins of the first longhouse, which burned conveniently forty years ago this spring. It is black, and warm, and if you hold it too long it begins to whisper to you in your own voice. It is the core of the Great Spire. And I stole it."

The words came out of her like splinters coming out of a wound — each one a small relief and a small agony, forty years of pressure escaping through a hole she had finally allowed herself to cut.

She told him all of it. Told him about the Spire as it had been, taller than the mountains' memory of themselves, a needle of white stone around which the old world had organized itself like iron filings around a lodestone. Told him about the war — not the sanitized version in the schoolroom primers, *the Sundering*, *the Burning Years*, nice clean names for forty years of kingdoms feeding their dead into a furnace — but the true shape of it, the way the gifted lords had torn the sky open over Vaelmoor and let something through, the way the Spire's heart had drunk the lives of ten thousand soldiers in a single afternoon to seal the breach, the way the survivors had stood in the smoking aftermath and sworn, every one of them, *never again*.

Told him how the swearing hadn't lasted. How within a generation the crowns were rebuilding, hoarding, reaching for the Spire's remnants the way addicts reach for a familiar poison. How the young Queen's grandfather had ordered the Spire's core recovered and enshrined, a weapon in reserve, a god in a box. How Beatrix — then twenty-six years old, the youngest founder of the Hollowmere Guild, trusted with the evacuation of the Spire's archive — had walked into the vault with a satchel of documents and walked out with the heart of the world against her chest, wrapped in wool, beating warmth into her ribs like a second heart that hated her.

"How?" Sebastian whispered. "How could you carry it? If it whispers—"

"It whispered the whole way. Six hundred miles. It knew my name before I ever spoke it." Her laugh came out cracked. "It offered me everything, child. My gift back. My dead mother's voice. A world remade. It is very persuasive. It has had centuries of practice persuading people, and every ruin in the north is a monument to how often it succeeds."

"So you brought it here. To the safest place you could find. And you—" He stopped. She watched him arrive at it, watched his eyes widen in the rain-dark. "The wardline. There was no wardline until you came. The elders say the valley was always like this. They say—"

"The elders say what I told them to say." Beatrix sat down heavily on the wet granite, because her legs had decided the story had cost enough standing. "I buried the core at the valley's center and bound it with the last working I ever performed — the binding cost me my gift, or perhaps the core simply ate it as payment; after forty years I no longer pretend to know which. And the hunger of that thing, straining forever against its chains, became a wall. A drain. Anything magical that touches the valley's edge pours into it and vanishes. Hunters cannot sense us because their senses drown here. Fugitives lose their gifts because the heart eats them. We built a sanctuary out of a monster's appetite, Sebastian. Every safe night anyone in this valley has ever slept, they have slept on top of a caged star."

The rain fell. Somewhere below, a dog barked once and thought better of it.

Sebastian lowered himself beside her, slowly, as though the ground might also be a lie. "And the crown knows."

"The crown suspects. The old King died believing I'd sunk the core in the Sea of Glass. But the daughter is not the father. Veyra's mistress has spent fifteen years pulling the kingdom apart stone by stone, looking for anything that survived the Spire. And six days ago her riders found the road above our valley, and they stopped, and they measured." Beatrix closed her eyes. "They can't cross the line. But they don't need to. They can feel the drain — a hollow in the world this size casts a shadow, even over a wardline. They're not searching the valley, child. They're confirming it. And when they confirm it, they will bring enough men that the line won't matter, and they will dig up every cellar in Hollowmere until they find their god in its box."

"Why are you telling me this?" His voice was raw. "Why me? Why not the elders? Why not Maren, Corvin, any of them?"

"Because the elders will argue until the riders come down the hill, and then they will die arguing. Because Maren would surrender the core to the crown to save the valley, and the crown would use it, and everything we burned forty years ago would be rebuilt in a decade. Because Corvin would destroy it tonight with a hammer and a prayer, and wake it angry, and we would all learn what a star looks like when it's frightened." She reached over and took his hand — his young hand, calloused from hive-work and well-water, steady despite everything. "And because you, Sebastian Okonkwo, are the only person in this valley who has never once believed the pretty story. I have watched you for nineteen years look at our safety like it was a locked door. Well. Now you know what the door is for, and what's behind it, and I am asking you to help me open it before someone else kicks it down."

"Open it how? What are you saying we do?"

"We take it out of the valley," Beatrix said. "Tonight, while the rain covers us. We carry the heart of the Spire across the wardline — and the moment it crosses, the ward dies, and every gifted soul in Hollowmere becomes visible to every hunter in the kingdom. And we run."

He stared at her. "Run *where*? There's nowhere left. The proclamation said—"

"I know what it said." She rose, joints protesting, and looked south past the valley rim toward the drowned chapel country, the border towns, the smuggler roads — the whole vast compromised world she had not seen in forty years, waiting for her like a debt. "I hid this thing once because hiding was possible. It isn't anymore. So now we do the harder thing. We move it. And God forgive me, Sebastian, I am an old woman with no magic and a stolen god in a box, and I cannot do it alone."

For a long moment he said nothing. The rain thickened. Below them the valley lamps were going out one by one, household by household, the whole refuge settling into its nightly performance of peace.

Then Sebastian said, "If we take it, the ward falls. Everyone here loses their protection. Maren, Corvin, the children — everyone."

"Yes."

"And if we leave it, the crown comes, and digs, and takes it anyway, and burns what's left."

"Yes."

"Then we don't really have a choice." He stood, and there it was — the flint striking, the anger transmuting into something harder and more useful. "When do we go?"

Beatrix felt something loosen in her chest that had been clenched for forty years — not relief, nothing so kind as relief, but the terrible lightness of a woman who has finally set down a weight only to discover she must now run with it.

"Now," she said. "Before the elders meet again. Before the riders stop measuring and start moving. Bring nothing you love that makes noise."

They went down through the rain together, mentor and student, thief and accomplice, toward the false wall and the lead box and the warm black whispering thing that had ended one world and was patient enough, given time, to end another.

Behind them, far up the king's road, a lantern swung.

The riders had stopped measuring.

They had begun, very quietly, to count the valley's chimneys.
