## 4. The Wardline Breaks

The dogs began barking before dawn.

Not the ordinary barking — not the fox-warning chorus that rose from the pens on cold nights, nor the quarrelsome grumbling when a neighbor's hound wandered too close to the chicken runs. This was different. This was the deep, rolling alarm that every child in Hollowmere learned in the cradle: three long howls from the eastern ridge, answered by two from the west, and then silence. The silence was the worst part. The dogs had done their work; now they cowered, and waited, and so did we.

I was in the byre, mucking straw by lamplight, when the sound came down through the fog and settled into my chest like a swallowed stone. I stood with the pitchfork half-lifted and listened to the valley hold its breath.

Hollowmere had been safe for forty years. Safe the way a house is safe — not because nothing outside it wanted in, but because the walls were thick and the doors were true. The wardline was our wall. It ran along the ridgelines like a ring of pale fire no eye could see but every gifted soul could feel, a pressure against the skin, a humming behind the teeth. Inside it, magic slept. Outside it, the kingdom burned its witches and paid silver for informers. We lived in the one place where the crown's hunters could not follow, because within the ward their arts went blind and dumb, and their bloodhounds circled the boundary for a day or two before slinking home, whining.

That was what I had always believed. That was what the elders taught, what Beatrix taught, in her dry voice that made even doctrine sound like an old joke she'd grown tired of telling.

I dropped the fork and ran for the meeting hall without stopping to wash my hands.

* * *

By the time I arrived, the fog was lifting off the fields in long grey ribbons, and the whole guild had gathered in the yard between the hall and the well — maybe sixty of us, wrapped in shawls and cloaks pulled on over nightclothes, faces yellow in the light of torches nobody remembered lighting. The elders stood on the hall's low steps. Master Corvin, oldest of us, his beard white as frost. Sister Ameline, who kept the ledgers. Old Tam, who'd broken his hip twice and still insisted on standing at the front of everything, arms crossed like a gate.

And apart from them, at the edge of the steps, Beatrix.

She wasn't looking at the crowd. She was looking east, toward the ridge, toward the wardline, and her face in the torchlight was something I had never seen on her before and have never stopped seeing since: fear, worn openly, the way other people wore scars.

"Sebastian." She said my name without turning. She always knew where I was in a crowd; I'd never understood how. "Get the young ones into the root cellars."

"What's happening?"

"Get them into the cellars," she said again, softer, which from Beatrix was worse than shouting. "Now."

I grabbed the nearest children — Pip and his sister Wren, who were already crying without knowing why, the way animals cry before storms — and pushed them toward the hall's back stairs. Behind me the elders' voices rose and tangled.

"—scouts say riders at the treeline, a dozen, maybe more—"

"The ward will hold. The ward has *always* held—"

"A dozen riders is a hunting party, Tam, not a patrol. They know something. Someone talked—"

"Nobody talks. Nobody *can* talk—"

But even as he said it, Old Tam's voice cracked on the last word, and I saw it move through the crowd like wind through wheat: the thought none of us would say aloud. *Someone can. Someone did.* Because the proclamation had come three days ago, nailed to the post at the valley mouth by a royal crier with a voice like a brass bell — bounty and pardon for any soul who named the gifted among their neighbors, and a king's reward beyond counting for word of "the spire-heart, relic of the old rebellion." I had heard those words read out and felt only distant anger, the anger of a boy hearing about a war in a story.

Now the story was standing on our ridgeline with torches.

Beatrix came down the steps and cut through the arguing elders like a blade through rope. "Corvin. How did they find the valley?"

The old man's face went grey. "There's a rider coming down the road now," he said. "Under a parley flag. They want to talk to whoever leads here."

"And they said—" Ameline's voice was thin. "They called out a name. Before the parley. They called out—" She swallowed. "They called out for Dellan."

The name landed in the yard like a dropped glass.

Dellan. Dellan who had gone out over the wardline in autumn to buy salt iron and hadn't come back, and the elders had told us all he'd chosen exile, chosen the wide world, don't grieve, it happens. Dellan who had known every path over the eastern ridge. Dellan who had helped set the false trails that misled any hunter foolish enough to come close.

Dellan, whose testimony had bought him whatever the crown gave traitors these days.

"He wouldn't," someone said. "Dellan wouldn't—"

"They broke him," another voice said, flat with grief. "They break everyone. That's what they're *for*."

Beatrix closed her eyes. For one moment — one only — she looked her age, and older than her age, as though the forty years since the severing had all arrived at once to collect their debt. Then she opened her eyes and they were hard and bright as coin.

"Then they know where the paths are," she said. "And they'll be through the ward by midmorning, because Dellan knew the one thing about the ward that no one else living knows." She turned, and found me in the crowd again, and this time her gaze caught mine and held it, and I felt the bottom drop out of the morning. "It isn't a wall, children. It's a lock. And locks turn."

* * *

They came through just after sunrise, while the fog was still burning off the barley.

I was supposed to be in the cellars. Instead I was on the roof of the granary, because I am Sebastian Okonkwo, youngest of the Hollowmere guild, and being told to hide has always made my feet walk the other way. From the ridge of the roof I could see the whole eastern slope, and I saw it happen.

There was no explosion. No thunder. The wardline didn't shatter; it *opened*, the way a door opens, the way a mouth opens to speak a betrayal. Along the ridgeline where nothing had ever been visible, the air suddenly showed itself — a faint shimmer, a heat-haze standing upright in the cold morning, and then a seam of pale gold light widening from a thread to a gap to a gate. And through it came the riders.

A dozen, as the scouts had said. Black-and-silver livery, the crowned tower of the Royal Inquisition on their surcoats. They rode slowly, unhurried, the way men ride when they own the ground they're riding on. At their head a woman in a grey hood turned once to look back at the shimmering seam, and I swear — I have sworn it ever since, and I will swear it dying — that she smiled. Not cruelly. That was the horror of it. She smiled the way a locksmith smiles when the tumbler gives.

The alarm bells began. The valley erupted into motion below me — people running, doors slamming, Sister Ameline herding children like a dog with a lost flock, the elders' careful plans collapsing into pure animal flight. And I stayed on the roof, frozen, watching riders spread out across our barley fields like ink across wet paper, because my body had decided, without consulting me, that this was not real.

Then the first scream tore up from the direction of the smithy, and it became real.

I scrambled down so fast I tore my palms on the ladder and hit the ground running, and I don't remember deciding anything after that except one thing, one clear thing, cutting through all the noise: *Beatrix.* She hadn't gone to the cellars. Of course she hadn't. She'd gone north, toward the old orchard, toward the standing stones above the spring — and I understood, in some wordless way, that whatever she carried, whatever secret she'd been wearing under her skin for forty years like a second skeleton, it was buried there, and she meant to dig it up before the riders reached it.

I followed her.

* * *

The orchard was old-growth, gnarled apple trees planted by hands a century dead, and the fog still clung between the trunks in rags. Beatrix was on her knees beneath the largest stone, the flat one the children were forbidden to play on, tearing at the earth with her bare fingers. Her nails were black with soil. There was a spade lying beside her — she must have run past the toolshed without breaking stride — but her hands were faster than thought, and she dug like a woman burying or unburying her own heart.

"You should be in the cellars," she said, without looking up.

"So should you."

"Don't argue with me, boy. Not today." But there was no force in it, and she didn't send me away, and so I knelt beside her and drove my fingers into the cold earth next to hers, and together we dug.

What we unearthed was smaller than I expected. A box of dark wood, bound in iron gone green with age, no bigger than a loaf of bread, and heavy — heavy the way a grave is heavy, the way a debt is heavy. Beatrix lifted it and held it against her chest and bowed her head over it, and for a moment I thought she was praying. Later I understood she was greeting it. Forty years, and she greeted it like an old enemy.

"Beatrix," I whispered. "What is it?"

"It's the reason they're here." Her thumb traced the iron banding. "It's the reason the valley exists. It's the spire-heart, Sebastian. The core of the Spire of Aldermoor, the heart of everything the old world built and everything the old war burned. And it is going to get us all killed unless we run *now*."

Above the orchard, on the ridge path, a horse screamed.

We both turned. A single rider had detached from the others — young, ahead of his fellows, eager — and he'd seen us. He saw the box. He saw the old woman kneeling in the dirt with forty years of secrets in her lap, and he drew steel and spurred his horse down through the trees.

Everything after that happened very fast, and very slow, the way disasters do.

Beatrix rose. She put herself between me and the rider, and she fumbled with the box's clasp, and her hands — steady through every crisis of my childhood — were shaking. "Run," she said. "Sebastian. Run to the cellars."

"I'm not leaving you."

"*Run*—"

The rider burst through the last line of trees. Close now, ten lengths, eight, his sword up, his face young and frightened and certain, the face of every man who has ever believed he is the hero of the story. And Beatrix got the clasp open.

Light came out of the box.

I have tried, in the years since, to find words for that light, and I've settled on this: it was the color of the moment before lightning strikes, held forever. It poured upward out of the little wooden box like water running backward, and it touched the orchard, and the orchard *woke*. Every apple tree blazed with pale fire that did not burn it. The fog lit gold from within. The stones above the spring began to hum, a note I felt in my molars, in my sternum, in the roots of my teeth — a note I somehow recognized, though I had never heard it, the way you recognize your own name in a stranger's mouth.

The core flared.

The rider was closest. The light took him the way a wave takes a sandcastle. There was no cruelty in it — I need you to believe that, because everyone else who tells this story makes it cruel — it was simply vastness meeting smallness, the sun rising over a candle. His horse reared screaming and threw him, and he fell, and the light passed through him and left him lying in the grass with his sword fallen beside him, and he did not move again. He looked almost peaceful. He looked like a boy who'd been told a story and believed it, and the story had eaten him.

The flare died as fast as it came. The orchard went dark. The humming faded down to a whisper, then a memory.

And inside my chest, something opened its eyes.

I don't know how else to say it. All my life I had been a locked room, giftless, plain Sebastian, the boy who mucked the byres and carried the water and watched the gifted children do small impossible things with quiet envy. Now, in the space of a heartbeat, every lock in that room swung open at once. Power came up through the ground and into my knees, my spine, my skull — cold and enormous and *mine*, a river finding a channel it had been carved for and forgotten. I could feel the orchard, every tree, every root. I could feel the wardline, broken and bleeding at the eastern ridge. I could feel the box in Beatrix's arms singing like a struck bell, and my blood singing back to it, harmony answering harmony.

"Beatrix," I heard myself say, and my voice came out strange, doubled, layered with something older. "What's happening to me?"

She stared at me. In the fading gold light her face went through wonder and grief and something like horror, all in the space of a breath, and then — because she was Beatrix, because she was always Beatrix — she seized my wrist and started running.

"That," she said, "is a question for a day we survive. *Move!*"

We ran. Through the orchard, down the sheep track, past the spring where the stones still murmured their dying note, toward the western ridge where the ward had not yet been breached — the long way around, the only way left. Behind us I could hear horns, shouts, the crash of the guild's world being kicked apart room by room. Somewhere a building was burning; smoke climbed into the clean morning sky like a black tree growing in fast-forward. I did not look back. Looking back was a luxury for people whose whole lives weren't ending.

The wardline shimmered ahead of us, the pale seam of it, still intact on this side. Beatrix pulled up short at the boundary, chest heaving, the box clutched to her, and I saw her hesitate — one heartbeat, two — an old woman at a threshold she hadn't crossed in forty years.

"Can you?" I gasped. "Out there — without your magic — can you survive it?"

A ghost of her old dry smile crossed her face. "Boy, I survived it for twenty years before I ever found this valley. Magic was never what kept me alive." She looked at me then, really looked, at whatever was still burning behind my eyes. "But you're going to have to learn to hold that leash before we take another step. Can you?"

Could I? The power was still there, coiled and singing, wanting to reach for the core, wanting more, always more. I thought of the young rider lying peaceful in the grass. I clenched my fists until my nails bit through the dirt-crusted skin, and I breathed, and I pushed it down — not away, I could never push it away again, that door doesn't close — but down, folded small, leashed. The singing dimmed to a hum. My hands shook.

"Yes," I lied.

Beatrix saw the lie, and stepped across the wardline anyway, and pulled me with her.

Crossing felt like walking through a curtain of cold water, like passing out of a house you've lived in your whole life into weather you've only heard described. The world outside was louder — every sound sharper, every color rawer, my new-woken senses stripped of the ward's soft muffling. And under everything, faint and far behind us, I felt the core's song stretch thin, a thread of gold drawn out between the box and my ribs, refusing quite to snap.

Behind us, Hollowmere burned. Ahead of us lay a kingdom that had just posted a price on everything we were. Beatrix tightened her grip on my wrist, and on the box, and set her face toward the hills.

"Walk," she said. "Don't run. Runners look guilty."

And so I walked out of the only home I had ever known, carrying a stolen god under one arm of the woman who stole it, with a fire waking in my chest that no one — least of all me — knew how to put out. The exhilaration and the terror were the same feeling. I couldn't tell where one ended and the other began. I don't think they did.

Somewhere behind us, on the road we'd abandoned, hoofbeats turned onto our trail.

Veyra had our scent now. Though we didn't know her name yet — oh, we'd learn it.
