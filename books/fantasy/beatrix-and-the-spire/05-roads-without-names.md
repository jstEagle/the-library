## 5. Roads Without Names

The wardline was two days behind us and still I could feel it, the way you feel a tooth that's been pulled — an ache where something used to sit.

We walked north along a drover's road that wasn't on any map Beatrix would show me, through country I had never seen and had imagined all my life in the wrong colors. The valley had taught me hills as green and soft as moss. These were brown-gold, scalped by wind, stitched with dry stone walls that ran to nowhere like sentences no one finished. The sky was enormous and indifferent, and twice I caught myself looking over my shoulder for the shape of Hollowmere's ridgeline and finding only distance.

Beatrix walked ahead of me, and she did not walk like a woman of seventy years. She walked like weather coming. Her staff clicked against the packed earth at a rhythm she never varied, and when she stopped — which she did often, crouching to read some sign invisible to me: a hoofprint, a bent stem of gorse, a thread of smoke too thin for a chimney — the whole road seemed to stop with her.

"Eat," she said, without turning around. It was midmorning and we had been walking since the gray hour before dawn, and she had said perhaps forty words total, most of them instructions.

"I'm not hungry."

"Hunger is a luxury you'll miss. Eat."

I ate. Hard bread and a heel of cheese gone waxy with warmth, and water from her skin, which tasted of leather and of something else beneath it — a faint mineral coldness I would learn, later, to recognize. Even then, even before I understood what she had carried against her heart for four decades, some animal part of me knew I was drinking from the same vessel as the spire's core. It made my teeth ache. Or maybe that was just the gift, waking in its bed.

Because that was the other thing. Two days past the wardline, and my hands had not stopped humming.

It wasn't pain, exactly. It was more like the feeling of a word sitting on the tip of your tongue, except the word was everywhere — in the stones, in the wind, in the slow green insistence of the weeds pushing up between the wall-stones. In the valley, magic couldn't follow. That was the whole point of Hollowmere; that was the covenant. Whatever Beatrix had done with the core, whatever old bargain the founders had struck, the valley drank magic down and gave back silence. I had grown up inside that silence and called it peace.

Now the silence was gone and something in me had opened like an eye, and it would not close.

"Beatrix." My voice came out rougher than I intended. "When does it stop?"

She turned. The light came sideways across her face and filled every crease of it, and for a moment she looked less like the stern founder of the guild and more like exactly what she was: an old woman carrying a burden she had carried so long she'd forgotten how to set it down.

"It doesn't," she said. "You learn to carry it quieter."

* * *

The town was called Marrow's Edge, though no one we spoke to called it anything at all, because no one spoke to us.

Beatrix had dressed us both for it the night before, stripping away everything the valley had put on our backs. My guild-gray cloak went into a drainage culvert. The knot-work bracelet I'd worn since I was eight — woven by Maren, who taught me letters, burned now or buried, I didn't know — I hid in my boot rather than throw away, and Beatrix saw me do it and said nothing, which was its own kind of mercy. She traded her staff for a walking stick, snapped off clean, and bound her white hair under a dye-stiffened kerchief the color of walnut husks. She looked smaller. She looked, disturbingly, ordinary.

"Rules," she said, standing at the edge of the town's first street while cart-ruts and chicken droppings and the smell of woodsmoke washed over us. "We are brother and sister, come from the east because the blight took the barley. We are looking for work with the tanner, whose name is Ostrec, and whom I met once twenty years ago, which is true enough to survive questioning. You speak when spoken to. You do not look at anyone's hands. And above all—" she took my chin in fingers surprisingly hard, "—you do not reach. Do you understand me? Whatever you feel out there, whatever calls to you from the wells and the hearths and the bones of this place, you do not answer."

"I don't know how not to—"

"You will learn tonight." She released me. "For now, walk behind me and be boring. Boring is armor."

Marrow's Edge clung to a hillside above a river the color of gunmetal, a huddle of slate roofs and leaning chimneys, and it should have felt like any market day anywhere. But I had lived my whole life in a place where people greeted each other by name, and so I noticed what the valley had kept from me: the way neighbors watched one another here. Not warmly. A woman drawing water paused to watch two men talking outside the smithy, and her eyes moved from their mouths to their hands and back. A boy selling apples fell silent as we passed and did not resume until we'd turned the corner. Doors stood open — but not hospitably. Open the way a mouth opens before it speaks.

"They're afraid," I murmured.

"They're paid," Beatrix corrected quietly. "Fear is free. This has a bounty attached." She nodded, almost imperceptibly, toward a board nailed beside the well. From thirty paces I could read the top line, because it was painted larger than scripture:

BY ORDER OF THE CROWN: LET ALL WHO SHELTER THE GIFTED KNOW THAT PARDON SHALL FLOW TO THE INFORMING HAND.

Below it, in smaller letters, the numbers. So much silver for a name. So much gold for a person delivered alive. And at the bottom, freshly inked, the words that had driven us from the only home either of us had ever known: *and to him who reveals the resting place of the Spire-Heart, a barony.*

"A barony," I whispered.

"For a stone," Beatrix said. "The crown knows what it wants." Something crossed her face then, quick as a fish under ice, and was gone. "Come. Ostrec's yard is at the bottom of the hill, by the stink. Follow your nose."

* * *

Ostrec remembered her.

That frightened me more than the proclamation board had. He was a huge man gone soft, forearms scarred white with old lime burns, and when Beatrix stood in his tannery doorway among the hanging hides and the reek of urine and ash, he went still the way prey goes still.

"Beatrix of Hollowmere," he said. No greeting in it.

"Not anymore. Just Beatrix, come about work. My brother and I."

He looked at me. I looked at the floorboards and thought about bark, and mud, and the price of wool — boring thoughts, armor thoughts. But I could feel the tannery around me the way a tongue feels a broken tooth. Every hide on its hook held a ghost of the beast it had been. The lime pits breathed. Somewhere beneath the floor, the river ran, and the river was *loud* in a way water had never been loud in the valley, full of motion and appetite and a low singing I could have listened to for hours if I let myself—

"Boy."

I surfaced. Both of them were staring at me. Ostrec's small eyes had narrowed.

"He's simple," Beatrix said smoothly. "Fever as a child. Good hands, though, and strong. Give him the scraping frames and he'll ask nothing else of the world."

Something passed between the two old people then, some negotiation conducted entirely in the set of shoulders and the angle of the head. At last Ostrec grunted.

"There's a loft over the drying shed. Straw and a blanket. Six coppers a week between you and you keep your mouths shut." He wiped his hands on his apron, and then, not looking at her: "There were men here last month. Asking after travelers from the south. Crown men. They'll be back."

"I imagine they will," said Beatrix. "Thank you, Ostrec."

"Don't thank me." His voice dropped to gravel. "Forty years ago you pulled my daughter out of a burning house with your bare hands when every gifted coward in three parishes wouldn't spend a spark on her. So don't thank me. Just don't get caught. If they come asking, I'll say you robbed me and ran east."

"That," said Beatrix, and there was something almost warm in it, almost like grief, "is the kindest lie I've heard in a decade."

* * *

They came for lessons when the tannery lamps went dark.

Not that she called them lessons. Beatrix did not believe in naming things that might otherwise stay soft. She sat cross-legged on the straw of the loft with the single shuttered candle between us, and she said, "Show me what happens when you don't fight it."

So I stopped fighting it.

I had been fighting it for two days without knowing that was what I was doing — clamping down on the hum in my hands, the way you clamp down on a cough in church. Now I let go, and the world came flooding in, and it was —

It was everything. The river below us sang in a hundred braided voices. The straw held the memory of sunlight. The candle flame leaned toward me like a curious child, and the darkness beyond the shutter pressed close and attentive, and somewhere far off a fox was dying or being born, I couldn't tell, the life in things was all one bright fabric and I could see every thread and oh, I wanted to pull—

The candle went out.

Not blown out. It poured itself into my palm, a bead of light sinking into skin, and I felt it there, warm as a swallowed coal, and distantly I heard Beatrix saying my name, and I reached — I reached for the river, for its great gray strength, wanting more, wanting to be the current—

Pain. Cold, shocking, precise: her stick rapped my knuckles, hard, twice.

I gasped. The light bled back out of my hand and found the candlewick again, and the flame stood up, trembling. I was on my knees in the straw, shaking, drenched in sweat in the cold air, and Beatrix's face hovered above mine, lit from below, carved out of shadow.

"What," I managed, "was that?"

"The oldest question in the guild." She sat back down. "That is what the gift is, Sebastian. Not a tool. An appetite. Everything alive sings, and everything that sings can be heard by someone like you, and everything you hear, you will want. To touch, to shape, to spend. The war that burned the world — the one your history books call the Sundering, the one my generation survived — it was not fought over the spire-core. The core was only the match. The war was fought because ten thousand people like you reached for ten thousand beautiful voices and none of them could bear to stop."

I wrapped my arms around myself. The straw prickled. My knuckles throbbed, and I was grateful for the throbbing; it was mine, plain and human and dumb.

"How do I stop?"

"Now you ask the right question." She drew the candle closer, and the light steadied between us. "The guild taught five disciplines. We called them the Five Silences. Most initiates spent ten years learning the first. You have perhaps until the hunters reach this town, so we will do it badly and quickly, and you will hate me, and that will be correct."

The first silence, she said, was the silence of the hands. Not stillness — she made me move them constantly, slowly, tracing patterns in the air — but *disconnection*. "Your gift lives in the reaching," she said. "So teach the body that the hands are only hands. Wash pots. Feel the water as water. When the song rises, name the thing in front of you. Pot. Water. Hands. Nothing else."

The second silence was the silence of names. "Everything that sings wants to be known," she said. "If you name what you hear — *that is a river, that is a fire, that is nothing but my own fear* — you stand outside it. Unnamed, it stands inside you."

The third silence was breath. The fourth she would not describe that night, only say, "later, when you've earned the word for it." The fifth, she said, was the one she herself had never mastered, and she smiled as she said it, a smile with no floor in it, and I did not ask.

We worked until the sky behind the shutter went from black to iron. She made me wash the tannery's pots in the dark by feel, made me name every sound in the yard until the sounds were only sounds, made me breathe in counts of seven until my lungs ached and the river's song faded to a rumor and then to nothing at all. Twice I slipped. Once I nearly took the flame into my hand again, and she broke a second stick over my knuckles and did not apologize, and I hated her exactly as much as she had predicted, and she nodded at my hatred like a merchant approving payment.

Near dawn I collapsed into the straw, wrung out, hollow, and strangely — clean. For the first time since the wardline, the hum in my hands was quiet. Not gone. Quieter. Like a dog told to lie down, lying down, watching.

"Beatrix," I said, into the dark.

"Mm."

"Why me?" I hadn't meant to ask it. It had been sitting in me since the night the hunters came, since she'd shoved the core-wrapped bundle into my arms and said *you carry it, only you*, and I had obeyed because there was no time to ask. "Of everyone in the guild. You barely spoke to me before all this. I mucked the stables and copied ledgers. Why did you choose me?"

A long silence. The river sang faintly below, and this time I let it sing and did not reach, and was quietly proud.

"Sleep," she said at last. "Tomorrow we go to market and you will practice being boring among crowds. And Sebastian—" the straw shifted; she was turning away from me, "—the answer to that question is a discipline too. The fifth one. Ask me again when you've earned the word for it."

* * *

Market day came, and with it, the informers.

I saw them before I understood what I was seeing, because they did not look like anything. A woman selling eggs who asked every stranger, cheerfully, where they'd traveled from. A man loitering near the proclamation board who had read it so many times he no longer needed to. And then — this was the one that turned my stomach — the boy with the apples, the same boy from our first morning, maybe twelve years old, pressing close to a ragged traveler at the edge of the square and listening to him with his whole body, nodding, sympathetic, and then slipping away quick as a minnow to the man by the board and speaking fast into his ear.

Silver changed hands. The traveler didn't notice. He went on his way, unwatched, unnamed, and I stood among the vegetable stalls holding a cabbage I had no intention of buying and understood that this was the new kingdom: not fire and soldiers, but this. Neighbors weighing neighbors. Children priced by the ounce.

"Breathing," Beatrix murmured beside me. She had materialized at my elbow with a basket. "Count of seven."

I breathed. The cabbage was a cabbage. My hands were hands.

But it cost me something, that morning, to watch the boy take the silver. Because here was the truth I carried out of Marrow's Edge, heavier than the core itself: the crown didn't need to burn the guild's motherhouse or hunt every last witch to win. It only needed to make the price of a name cheap enough. And it had. Oh, it had.

We were loading our few purchases — salt, thread, a second blanket — into the sack when the egg-seller stopped at our stall.

"Travelers," she said brightly. Her smile was wide and her eyes were busy, moving from Beatrix's dyed kerchief to my hands. "Haven't seen you before. Come far?"

"The east," Beatrix said. "The blight took our barley. We're for Ostrec's yard, tanning work."

"Tanning!" The woman wrinkled her nose, laughing. "Strong stomachs, you two." And then, lightly, the way one remarks on weather: "Strange times, though. They say there's folk about with the old blood in them. Gifted, hiding plain as you or me." Her eyes came to rest, very briefly, on my hands. "Wouldn't know it to look, would you? That's the frightening part. Could be anyone."

"Could be anyone," Beatrix agreed pleasantly, and paid her for six eggs we did not need, and the woman counted the coins twice, smiling, memorizing our faces the way she'd memorized the coin.

We walked away unhurried. Only when we'd put three streets between ourselves and the market did Beatrix speak, low, without moving her lips.

"She marked you. Your hands, when you took the change — you lingered on the coins a half-second too long. Feeling them."

"I named them. Copper, copper—"

"Copper doesn't need naming, Sebastian. Silence isn't a trick you perform when you remember. It has to become what you *are*, down where remembering can't reach." She glanced back once at the market roofs, at the smoke rising straight and domestic into the cold air. "We leave tonight. Ostrec's kindness bought us four days and we've spent three."

"And go where? Every road has towns. Every town has that woman."

"Yes." Beatrix adjusted the sack on her shoulder and looked down the long brown fall of the road, north, toward mountains I couldn't yet see. "Every town has that woman. Which is why we don't go to towns. There's an old way — guild way, pre-Sundering, before maps were safe for the likes of us. It runs through the high fells above the river. Cold, and empty, and no one to sell."

"No one to sell," I repeated. It sounded almost like rest.

"Don't mistake emptiness for safety." She started walking, and I fell in behind her, staff-click and footfall, the old rhythm already becoming ours. "Empty roads have their own dangers. But they also have this:" she tapped the side of her nose, "no witnesses. Tomorrow I teach you the third silence properly, and the fourth, if your knuckles survive. By the time we come down out of the fells, you'll be able to stand in a crowd of hunters and feel nothing but the cold."

"And after the fells? Where does the old way lead?"

Beatrix was quiet for so long that I thought she hadn't heard. Then, at the edge of town, where the last roof gave way to gorse and the road narrowed into a sheep-track climbing toward the brown shoulder of the hills, she answered — and her voice was strange, flat, the voice of a woman reading from a ledger she'd hoped was lost.

"West," she said. "To the drowned chapel on Lake Verreth. Where I learned everything I'm about to teach you. And where, forty years ago, I swore an oath at the water's edge — an oath I broke the very same night, when I dug the spire-core out of the chapel stones and ran."

She climbed. I stared after her, the sack heavy on my shoulder, the river's song fading behind us note by note.

"You *stole* it from a chapel?"

"I saved it from a chapel," Beatrix said, not turning, "and I have been running ever since. Walk faster, Sebastian. We're losing the light — and I promise you, before this road ends, you'll wish you had every hour of it."
