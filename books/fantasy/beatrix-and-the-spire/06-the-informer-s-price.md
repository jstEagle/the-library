## 6. The Informer's Price

The border town was called Merrow's Rest, though nothing about it rested. It clung to the river like a barnacle to a hull — all tarred timber and fish-stink and the constant complaint of gulls — and its streets were narrow enough that two strangers passing could not help but learn each other's faces.

That was the danger of it. That was why we had come anyway: small town, small eyes, and a ferry that crossed twice a day and asked no questions of those who paid in coin rather than conversation.

I had not walked through a living town in forty years. Hollowmere had markets, of a kind, but they were ours, warded, inward-facing, a world folded into itself like a moth in a chrysalis. Here the world was unfolded and loud and it smelled of tallow smoke and wet rope, and I found that my hands would not stop wanting to make signs against ill luck that my fingers no longer had the strength to complete.

Sebastian walked half a step behind me, as he had since the wardline. He thought I did not notice him noticing things — the way children think their watching is invisible. He watched the crown's proclamations nailed to the customs house door, three layers deep, each one newer and crueller than the last. He watched the way shopkeepers' gazes slid to strangers and lingered just a breath too long. He was learning, quickly and painfully, what I had tried to spare him for four decades: that safety is not a place. It is a debt, and somewhere there is always someone holding the note.

*You chose this road,* I reminded myself, watching him flinch at a crier's bell. *You chose it when you dug up the heart of the world and put it in a boy's pack.*

We took a room above the Salt-White Eel, paying for two nights with silver that predated the current king's face, and I told the innkeeper we were cousins from the eastern counties come to settle our dead mother's affairs. Lies should always be dull. Dull lies are load-bearing stones; exciting ones are thrown stones.

It was on the second morning, at the fish market, that I saw Maren.

* * *

She stood at the far end of the stalls in a grey riding coat with the crown's badge worked into the collar — not the full livery of a hunter, but the half-badge of an assessor, one who catalogued and reported and let others do the dragging. Her hair had gone from black to iron at the temples. Her hands, wrapped around a paper of fried eel, were still elegant, still precise, still marked at the second knuckle of the left hand by the scar of an oath-ring burned off.

Forty years, and I would have known her anywhere. You do not forget the hands that held your own over a dying fire while you both swore things you could not keep.

*Maren Vael.* Third initiate of the guild's founding circle. The sister who had argued against hiding the core, who had wanted it destroyed outright, and who — when I outvoted her and stole away with it in the night — had screamed at me across the wardline that I had made us all liars, all cowards, all criminals waiting for the axe.

She had been right about most of that. She had simply drawn the wrong conclusion from it. Instead of hiding with the rest of us, she had walked out of the valley, walked into the nearest crown garrison, and traded everything she knew about the guild's old networks for a pardon and a salary. She had been feeding them tidbits for decades, I realized now — little names, little places, never quite enough to burn the whole forest, because some part of her had never stopped believing she was protecting us from worse. Or so I told myself, standing among the herring barrels with my heart going like a trapped bird. There are kindnesses you can only extend to people who have betrayed you if you invent reasons for them.

Then she looked up from her eel and her eyes found mine across the crowd, and I saw that the invention was unnecessary.

She knew me. And I watched the knowing move through her face like weather — shock, then fear, then something older and softer that I could not afford to name — before the assessor's mask came down over it, smooth as water closing over a stone.

She turned and walked, unhurried, toward the customs house.

I bought a pound of smoked herring because fleeing without buying anything would have been memorable, and I paid with steady hands, and I walked back to the Eel at exactly the pace of a woman thinking about her dead mother's paperwork. Inside, I woke Sebastian where he dozed over a map he could not read.

"We leave within the hour," I said. "Pack nothing you cannot lose."

"What happened?" He was awake fully at once; the boy woke like a dropped cat. I had stopped being surprised by that.

"An old friend," I said, "has grown new loyalties."

* * *

He argued with me while I counted out our remaining coin, which was right and proper. A student who does not argue is a mirror, and mirrors are useless on a road.

"You recognized her and you *let her see you recognize her.*" He said it with the particular outrage of the young, who believe that mistakes are choices. "Why not slip away? Why not—"

"Because slipping away is what guilty people do, and she'd have marked it regardless. Because if I'd turned my back too smoothly she might have doubted her own eyes, and doubt is worth more than any head start." I tied the purse. "And because I owed her a moment of honesty. Forty years of dishonesty between us. One honest look was the least I could pay."

"That's not strategy," Sebastian said quietly. "That's guilt."

I looked at him then — really looked, the way I rarely allowed myself, because looking at him too directly felt like reading someone else's letters. Nineteen years old, gift barely a week old inside him, raw as a burn, and already he had learned to see me.

"Yes," I said. "Both can be true. Strategy is just guilt wearing armor."

He didn't understand that yet. He would.

The ferry crossed at midday. We would be on it, standing among cattle drovers with our collars up, and by the time Maren finished whatever deliberations her conscience required, we would be on the far bank and moving south, and whatever she reported would be a rumor chasing a shadow. It was not a perfect plan. Perfect plans are how the gifted died, in the old war — perfection breeds confidence and confidence breeds stillness, and stillness is a target.

But as we came down the stairs to the common room, I stopped on the third step, and Sebastian nearly walked into my back.

Maren sat at our table.

Not at *a* table. At *ours*, the corner one beneath the soot-black beam, the one we had chosen precisely because it backed against the wall. She had a pot of tea poured for two, and her half-badge was turned face-down on the wood, and she was looking at the opposite wall with the studied blankness of a woman who has rehearsed not-looking.

"Beatrix," she said, without turning. "Your walk hasn't changed. Left foot heavier than the right, like a woman perpetually stepping over something."

* * *

We sent Sebastian to the bar with instructions to buy bread and to listen without appearing to listen, which he did with the gracelessness of his years, and I sat down across from Maren Vael, and for a long moment neither of us spoke. The tea steamed between us. Through the window, the river glittered with its cold indifferent light, and the ferry bell rang, once, twice — the crossing we were missing.

"You look well," she said finally. "Hiding suits you. It always did."

"Maren."

"I'm not going to report you." She said it fast, like pulling a splinter. "Before you say anything — I'm not. Not yet. Not unless—" Her jaw tightened. "There is a warrant, Bea. Not for the guild generally. For you. By name. Beatrix of the Spire, last founder living or known. They've offered a hundred crowns. A *hundred*. Fishermen talk about it over their nets like a folktale." Her eyes flicked to Sebastian's back at the bar. "Is that him? The boy from Hollowmere?"

"He's none of your ledger."

"He's carrying something," she said softly. "I've been doing this work twenty-five years, Bea. I watched you come through that market with your shoulders wrong. You carry weight high, between the shoulder blades, like a stolen thing. Like *the* stolen thing." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Tell me it isn't."

"And if it is?"

The silence stretched. In it I heard the whole architecture of her life: the garrison quarters, the carefully rationed betrayals, the decades of telling herself that working inside the machine was better than being ground beneath it. She had been me, once, making the opposite choice. We had both buried the same body, she and I. The core was our shared grave, and graves make strange kin.

"If it is," she said at last, "then you must give it up. To me. Tonight. I'll log it as recovered from an abandoned cache, destroy the provenance chain, take my pension in the spring. No hunt. No hundred crowns. No *boy* dragged to the questioning rooms in the capital, because Bea, I've seen the questioning rooms now, and they are not what we imagined when we were young and stupid and romantic about our enemies."

"They're worse," I said.

"They're worse," she agreed.

And there it was. The whole argument laid out on a tavern table beside a cooling pot of tea, and every word of it true. Give up the core, and Sebastian lived. Keep it, and we ran forever, hunted by a kingdom that had learned to smell magic like smoke, until our luck ran out on some bridge or border or bad road, and the boy who had never wanted anything but his valley went into the questioning rooms.

She was offering me the merciful arithmetic. One object, dormant, harmless in a hole in the ground — versus a life I had come to love more than my own.

"Maren," I said gently, "why did you stay with them? All these years."

Something moved behind her face — grief, or its fossil. "Because somebody has to be decent inside it," she said. "Because when they list a household for seizure, I make sure the youngest child's name gets misspelled, and misspelled children don't get taken. Because every cruelty I soften is—" She stopped. Drank her tea, which had gone cold. "Don't look at me like that. Don't you dare look at me like that, Beatrix. You hid in your valley for forty years and saved no one."

"I saved everyone I could," I said. "Which is the same thing, done slower."

We looked at each other across the table, two old women who had once been young together under a spire that sang, and I understood with terrible clarity that she believed every word she said, and that belief was her wound and her bandage at once. You cannot hate someone whose chains you recognize.

"I need until tomorrow evening," I said. "To think. To prepare the boy. If I decide you're right, I'll bring it to the customs house myself, and you'll never see me again."

It was a lie. I knew it was a lie as I said it, the way one knows a bone is broken before the pain arrives. There was no version of tomorrow in which I placed the heart of the world in the hands of the crown, however gentle the hand. But she needed the lie to let us walk out of that room, and I needed her mercy for one more day, and so the lie passed between us like currency, worn smooth by everyone who had ever spent one like it.

Maren studied me. Then she nodded, slowly, and turned her teacup over — an old guild signal, *this meeting never happened* — and rose.

"For what it's worth," she said, buttoning her coat, "I hope you run. I hope you run forever and die old and free and impossible to catch." She paused at the door. "But Bea — Veyra is coming. The chief hunter herself. She arrived at the garrison in Ashford two days ago, and she doesn't chase rumors, she chases *patterns*, and your pattern is written all over the eastern roads. Whatever you decide — decide faster than you think you can."

Then she was gone, and the door swung, and the ordinary noise of the tavern flooded back in like water into a footprint.

* * *

I did not tell Sebastian the whole of it. This will be judged, someday, by someone who loves him, and they will be right to judge it. But he was nineteen and his gift was new and his fear was already a full-time occupation, and I gave him only what he could carry: that the woman was an old friend, that we had reached an understanding, that we would cross on the evening ferry and be gone before anything could find us.

He believed me because he loved me. That is the most dangerous form of belief there is, and I spent it freely, the way I have spent everything freely except the truth.

We crossed at dusk. The river was the color of pewter and the cattle stamped and the drovers cursed in three accents, and halfway across, with the far bank still dark ahead and the near bank already letting us go, Sebastian touched my sleeve.

"The woman at the table," he said. "Her teacup. She turned it upside down before she left. You did the same thing at the market stall in Halloway, when the carter asked too many questions. What does it mean?"

For a moment I considered lying again. Then I looked at his face in the failing light — young, watchful, already so much older than the boy who had chased chickens through Hollowmere's orchards — and I understood that the lies were becoming a kind of cowardice, and that cowardice, in a mentor, curdles into betrayal.

"It means *this meeting never happened*," I said. "It is the oldest sign we have. Older than the guild, maybe. The founders used it when the spire still sang."

"And she used it because she's protecting us."

"She used it because she is trying to protect everyone at once," I said, "which is the noblest and least survivable thing a person can attempt."

He nodded slowly, accepting this, and turned back to watch the water. And I stood with my hand on the rail and let the spray sting my face, and I told myself the thing I needed to be true: that Maren Vael would sit on our secret for a day, two days, three, wrestling her conscience in the garrison latrine light, and that her slow, decent, misspelled-children soul would win, and that we would be miles south before her courage either way could touch us.

I told myself that mercy given is usually mercy returned, because I have extended mercy perhaps six times in a long life and been repaid in betrayal perhaps once, and memory is a miser — it counts the coins it wants to count.

The far bank came up dark and reedy. We stepped off onto the southern mud, and behind us Merrow's Rest lit its lamps one by one, a scatter of embers on the northern shore, and among those lamps, in a narrow office above the customs house, a grey-haired woman sat with a report form and a pen that did not move for a very long time.

I know now what she wrote. I reconstructed it later, line by line, from what followed: *Two travelers, east-county speech, elder woman and youth of nineteen. Elder matches description in circular 114. Direction of travel: south. Ferry manifest attached.*

She waited a day before sending it. One whole day, sitting with it in the drawer beside her bed, taking it out and putting it back, taking it out and putting it back. I know this because the courier who carried it told Odile, who told me, and by then it no longer mattered — except that it mattered everything, because one day was almost enough. Almost.

We made eleven miles south before dawn, sleeping in a hay barn with the core wrapped in oilcloth against Sebastian's spine, and at first light I heard it: hooves on the road, many of them, moving fast and in formation, coming from the north along the exact seam of our trail.

Not Maren's slow, conflicted riders.

Veyra's.

And I understood, standing in the barn doorway with the frost smoking off the fields, that mercy in a lawless time is not a coin that returns to your hand. It is a lamp you light for someone else in a wind you cannot feel — and sometimes it burns them, and sometimes it burns you, and the wind decides, and the wind has never once consulted the merciful.

"Sebastian," I said, turning back into the dark of the barn, "get up. Now."
