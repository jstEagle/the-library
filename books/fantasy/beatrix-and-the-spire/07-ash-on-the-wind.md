## 7. Ash on the Wind

We smelled it before we saw it.

The wind had been at our backs all morning, carrying the smell of the lowlands — wet clay, cut hay, woodsmoke from hearths that meant breakfast and warmth and ordinary lives. Then, a mile out from the waystation of Candlemere, the woodsmoke changed its character. It thickened. It went from the smell of comfort to the smell of endings, and Beatrix stopped walking so abruptly that I nearly collided with her back.

"That's not a hearth," she said.

"No," I said, though I didn't know how I knew. I had never stood downwind of a burning building before. I had never needed to. Hollowmere's fires were banked and careful, tended like children. But some knowledge lives in the blood older than experience, and my blood said: *that is a great deal of smoke, and it is the wrong color.* Not the pale gray of green kindling catching, but a deep, greasy black-brown that climbed into the sky in a column so straight it seemed drawn with a rule.

"Could be lightning," I offered, because offering hope out loud was a habit of mine and someone had to keep doing it.

"It hasn't stormed in nine days." Beatrix pulled her hood forward. Her hands, wrapped in their usual fingerless gloves, flexed once at her sides — the motion of a woman resisting an old reflex, reaching for magic that had been amputated from her before I was born. "Keep to the tree line."

We came around the shoulder of a hill called — or used to be called; who names hills now? — the Shepherd's Tooth, and Candlemere lay below us in its shallow bowl of pasture, and it was gone.

*Gone* is imprecise. The stones were still there. The waystations of the guild were built by masons who thought in centuries, and Candlemere's walls were fieldstone and lime mortar, thick as a man's arm is long, and stone does not burn. What burned was everything else: the roof of cedar shake, the shutters, the long trestle tables where travelers had eaten for three hundred years, the bunks with their straw ticks, the drying racks for herbs, the loft where the old maps were kept. All of it reduced to a black skeleton under a soft fall of ash, still drifting, still settling, as if the ruin were snowing inward on itself.

And on the standing gable-end, the only wall that still reached toward the sky, someone had painted — fresh, the paint still glossy in the morning light — the crowned sun of the Aurelian throne inside a circle, and beneath it, in letters a cubit tall:

**BY ORDER OF THE CROWN. SANCTUARIES DISSOLVED.**

Beatrix made a sound I had never heard her make. It was very small. If I had not been standing beside her I would have missed it entirely — a syllable with no consonants, the noise a door makes when it closes in a house you thought was empty.

"The maps," she said.

"What?"

"The loft. The maps are gone." She started down the hill before I could answer, moving fast and low, and I followed because following Beatrix downhill through ash was still safer than standing alone anywhere in the world.

* * *

Inside, the air still held heat. It rose from the wreckage in visible ribbons, warping my sight of the far wall, and every breath tasted of char and something sweeter underneath — resin, maybe, from the roof beams, or the ghost of the lavender they used to pack between the bed-straw against lice. Underfoot, the floor was a gray slurry of ash and melted snow, and each step printed itself behind me like evidence.

I had never been to Candlemere before. Let me say that plainly, because what happened inside me in that ruined hall makes no sense otherwise. I had never slept in those bunks, never eaten at those tables, never studied the star-charts in the loft on a winter night with my breath fogging the parchment. Candlemere existed in my life only as a name in Beatrix's lessons — *if you ever travel south, you can walk between houses for a hundred leagues and never sleep outdoors, because the waystations will have you* — a promise of a world I'd see someday.

And I stood in its ashes and wept as if I'd been born there.

It wasn't the building. I understood that even then, even through the hot pressure behind my eyes. It was this: every one of those waystations had been a sentence in a long story, the story of people like us — the gifted and the guildless-adjacent, the hedge-witches and storm-readers and herb-blessed, the ones the crown now called *anathema* — a story about how we had endured, quietly, for centuries, with tables set for strangers and beds open to anyone walking in the cold. And someone had decided the story was dangerous. Not the magic in it, necessarily. The *story*. You burn a waystation and you don't kill a witch; you kill the proof that witches were ever welcome anywhere.

"They came within the week," Beatrix said. She had found the well-head at the hall's center, its iron crane buckled by heat, and she rested her hand on it the way you'd touch a grave-marker. "Proclamation goes out, riders follow within the week. They had lists, Sebastian. Prepared lists. This wasn't panic. This was a plan waiting for permission."

"How many are there? Waystations?"

"In the old rolls? Ninety-one south of the Spur. More beyond." She looked up at the hole where the loft had been, where the sky showed through in a ragged oval. "The rolls themselves were copied to the motherhouse."

I knew what she wasn't saying. The motherhouse, which we would pass if we kept south. The motherhouse, which had stood eight hundred years and held the guild's ledgers, its genealogies, its library, the graves of every founder including — though she'd never say it — whatever remained of Beatrix's own dead.

"If they're burning these," I said slowly, working it through the way you work a splinter out of your palm, "then the motherhouse—"

"Will be first, not last. It's the loudest symbol." She turned from the well-head. "Which means we should go around it, and mourn later, and—"

"No."

The word came out of me before I had decided anything, and it surprised us both. Beatrix raised an eyebrow — that patient, terrible patience of hers, the look that had made forty years of guild elders step back from arguments they couldn't win.

"No?" she said.

I crouched instead of answering, and sifted my fingers through the ash near what had been a table leg. The gift under my sternum stirred — it did that now, constantly, a live coal I carried everywhere since the night at the wardline, and I was learning to hold it the way you hold your breath: carefully, and not forever. I let a thread of it out, thin as spider-silk, down into the gray.

Ash remembers. That's the thing nobody tells you. Fire eats wood and cloth and paper, but it leaves mineral behind, and mineral holds shape, and the gift can read shapes the way fingers read letters pressed into wax. It cost me almost nothing. It felt like dipping my hand into cold water.

Under the ash: the grain of the tabletop. Long scratches — knife-marks from two hundred years of travelers cutting bread. A child's initials carved crooked, *M.O.*, done twice because the first attempt wandered. Rings where a hundred thousand mugs had sat and sweated.

People. Centuries of people, being fed.

I stood up. My eyes were streaming and my jaw ached and I realized I had been clenching it, and I said, "They're erasing us, Beatrix. Not hunting us. *Erasing* us. There's a difference. A hunt wants you dead or caught. This—" I gestured at the crowned sun, the paint already skinning over in the heat. "This wants there to have never been an us at all. They're not just killing the gifted. They're killing the idea that we ever belonged here. That we built half the bridges and wrote half the songs and kept half the roads safe. Fifty years from now some child will stand where I'm standing and there won't even be ash. Just a story about bandits who burned it, or a plague, or nothing at all. No memory. No proof."

"And anger gives you back the proof?" Beatrix asked softly.

"No." I wiped my face with the back of my wrist, hard, the way you do when you don't want to give crying the dignity of using both hands. "But purpose does. I've been running for a week. We've been *surviving* — eating when we can, sleeping in ditches, keeping our heads down, and I told myself that was enough because staying alive *is* the job, first. But surviving is just grief moving slowly. I'm done moving slowly."

"You're nineteen years old."

"I'm the last person alive who knows the guild disciplines, besides you. I'm the one carrying—" I lowered my voice, though the nearest living soul was a crow somewhere up the valley, "—the core of the oldest power in the world in a satchel on my hip. And they are burning ninety-one buildings so no one remembers why I'm carrying it. So no. Age isn't the objection you think it is."

For a long moment she just looked at me, and the fire-heat moved through the ruin, and the crow called twice and quit.

"You sound like me," she said at last, and there was something in her voice I couldn't parse — pride and warning braided together, or perhaps grief recognizing its own handwriting. "Forty years ago I stood in a burned building too. Different fire. Same paint, more or less. And I said almost exactly what you said, and then I spent four decades hiding in a valley telling myself I'd chosen peace." She picked her way over a collapsed rafter toward me. "So hear the rest of it, since you're determined to inherit it. Anger is fuel, and fuel burns fast, and men who move on anger die in ditches with their purpose unspent. If we do this — if we stop merely surviving — we do it my way. Cold. Patient. We don't strike. We don't save buildings. Buildings are already lost." She touched my chest, one finger, right over the coal that lived there. "*You're* not lost. The core isn't lost. Whatever the guild is going to be — it has to survive this first, and it survives by being smarter than they are. Do you understand the difference between what you want and what I'm offering?"

I did, actually, better than she probably hoped. What I wanted was to hurt someone. What she was offering was to win. They only look alike from far away.

"I understand," I said.

"Then help me salvage. There may be things in the cellar. Fire goes down slower than people expect."

* * *

The cellar had been sealed by a falling beam and the fire had indeed gone slow beneath us — hungry, but the earth-floored root cellar had little to offer. We worked by the gray light through the broken hatch, hauling aside scorched timber, and found the stores half-ruined: split crocks of brine gone foul, a sack of oats fused into a single charcoal brick, wax stubs melted into one sad lake. But wedged behind the third step, in a niche only a guild member would know to check — the waystation equivalent of a hollow brick — we found Candlemere's true treasure.

A tin box, oil-clothed, holding the station's register.

The registers were the guild's real spine, though outsiders always fixated on grimoires. Every traveler logged. Every gift noted, gently, in code — a *green thumb* here, a *weather sense* there, a *sees what's buried* marked with the old sign for water-divining. Three centuries of Candlemere's entries, names and villages and small honest miracles, written in fifty different hands, some of them barely literate, all of them believing the list would matter to someone someday.

Beatrix opened it and went very still.

"This is my hand," she said. She was pointing at an entry near the front, faded brown ink: a girl of sixteen, passing through, *senses storms two days off; kind; do not tell her father.*

"You stayed here?"

"A winter. I was running the first time, too." She closed the box slowly, and I watched her do the arithmetic I couldn't: the register proved she existed, proved the guild existed, proved everything the crowned sun on the gable was sworn to unmake. It was also, carried openly, a death warrant for anyone found holding it.

"We take it," I said. Not asking.

"Yes," she said. "We take them all, if any others survive. And the ledgers at the motherhouse, if—" She stopped herself. "If. And we hide them somewhere the crown will never think to look, and someday, when this age passes the way all ages pass, someone reads them and knows we were real."

"That's the purpose?" I said. "Paper?"

"Paper outlives empires, Sebastian. Empires hate that about paper." She tucked the tin inside her coat, against her heart almost, and climbed the steps back into the daylight.

I followed, and paused at the top, and looked back once at the ruin — the black beams against the sky like ribs, the ash still sifting, the crowned sun glaring down from the one proud wall.

*They'll have a list,* Beatrix had said. *This was a plan waiting for permission.*

Lists ran both ways, I thought. They had lists of sanctuaries. Which meant somewhere, in some commander's tent or ministry drawer, there was a list of what remained — and next on it, gleaming like a beacon, the guild's motherhouse, with its eight hundred years of ledgers and graves and the pre-entered names of people not yet born. Names, I remembered suddenly, absurdly — I hadn't thought of it in weeks — though why it surfaced now, standing in ash, I couldn't say.

"Beatrix," I said, climbing out into the light. "How fast can we move if we stop being careful?"

She looked at me across the ruin of Candlemere, wind pulling at her gray hair, and smiled without one ounce of warmth.

"Faster than they'd believe," she said. "There's a smuggler's market three days south, past the ford at Harrow Bridge. Neutral ground, if such a thing exists anymore. If anyone knows which sites the crown has already taken and which are still standing — if anyone sells that kind of knowledge — they will."

"And if they also sell *us*?"

"Then we'll know exactly how the world works now," said Beatrix, and turned south with the register against her heart and the ash of the guild rising behind us like a banner no one would ever salute again.
