## 8. The Cartographer of Gifts

The market found them before they found it.

Sebastian had thought, all his life, that a market was a market — stalls and shouting and the smell of onions frying. Hollowmere had one, twice a week, when the valley traders came up the switchback road with salt and iron and gossip already three weeks stale. He had stood in that market his whole childhood, haggling over lamp oil with the same six faces, and he had believed he understood what commerce was.

Then Beatrix led him down through the marsh country at dusk, following a channel of black water that smelled of rot and river-mint, and the world opened its other mouth.

The smugglers' market sat in the drowned bowl of an old quarry, half-sunk below the waterline so that lantern light came up out of the ground like something buried and glowing. Ramps spiraled down into it, crowded with carts whose wheels had been muffled with rags. There was no shouting here. That was the first thing Sebastian noticed, and it frightened him more than any noise could have. Hundreds of people, buying and selling, and the sound of it was a murmur, a hush, a congregation at prayer. Deals were whispered against ears. Coins changed hands inside sleeves. A woman sold caged songbirds that sang in no language Sebastian knew, and the buyers leaned close to listen as though the birds were telling fortunes.

"Keep your hood up," Beatrix said quietly. "And whatever you see, don't stare. Staring is how people get remembered."

"I thought you'd never been out of the valley either," Sebastian said. "Not since—"

"Since I carried the core out of the burning spire and walked until my feet gave out." Her voice was flat as slate. "I walked through places like this. Worse than this. The world didn't stop existing because I stopped looking at it."

They descended the ramp together, two more shadows among shadows. The air changed as they went down — warmer, thicker, layered with smells: tallow smoke, wet stone, fried fish from a brazier somewhere, and beneath it all a faint mineral tang that made the fine hairs on Sebastian's arms rise. He knew that smell. It was the smell of the core's hiding place under the guildhall floor, the smell of deep stone and old lightning.

*It knows,* he thought, and hated himself for thinking it. The core rode wrapped in oilcloth in the bottom of their pack, between spare shirts and a bag of dried plums, and some part of him had become unable to stop feeling it — a low hum behind his teeth, a second pulse slightly out of rhythm with his own. Three days on the road and he had learned to sleep beside it the way one sleeps beside a sleeping animal of uncertain temper.

"You're doing it again," Beatrix murmured.

"Doing what?"

"Listening to it."

He flushed. "How do you stop?"

"You don't stop," she said. "You decide what it's for. That's the whole discipline, Sebastian. Everything else is commentary."

* * *

They came to Odile by way of a fishmonger who wasn't a fishmonger.

Beatrix had spoken to three people since they entered the quarry — a boy selling candles, a man mending nets, and finally the fishwife with forearms like ship's cable, who listened to Beatrix's question without expression, looked her up and down once, and jerked her chin toward the far side of the basin. No words. Sebastian understood then that whole conversations happened here without language, that silence itself had a grammar, and that he was illiterate in it.

The broker's stall was not a stall. It was a canal boat moored against the quarry wall, decked over with a canvas awning, hung with lamps of green glass that turned everyone beneath them the color of pond water. And hanging from the awning's frame, suspended on wires so they turned slowly in the draft, were maps.

Dozens of them. Hundreds. Maps of provinces Sebastian had only ever seen as smudges on the guild's one ancient atlas. Maps of cities, of coastlines, of mountain passes — and then maps that were not of places at all. Maps of faces. A network of inked lines radiating from a drawn portrait, connecting names, dates, sums of money. A map of a single hand, palm up, annotated in a tiny precise script along every finger.

"What is this place?" Sebastian whispered.

"A ledger," said a voice from the dark of the boat, "that walks around wearing a dress."

She emerged into the green light as if she'd been assembled from it: a broad woman of fifty-odd years, dark-skinned, silver-shot hair bound in a net of knotted cord, rings on eight fingers and none on the thumbs. Her eyes did the thing Sebastian would remember for the rest of his life — they went over him the way a butcher goes over a carcass, quick, professional, complete. Then they went over Beatrix, and stopped.

Stopped, and narrowed, and something moved behind them like weather.

"Well," Odile said. "Forty years, and you still walk like a woman carrying a piano in each hand."

"You know me," Beatrix said. Not a question.

"Everyone who matters knows you, Beatrix of Hollowmere. They just don't know they know you." Odile settled onto a stool and laced her ringed fingers over her knee. "The founder who cut out her own gift and vanished. The guild mothers tell children about you to frighten them straight. *Sew up your talent or end up like her.*" She smiled without warmth. "You're a cautionary tale with legs."

"I need passage east," Beatrix said. "Quietly. Two travelers, no questions at the tolls."

"Everyone needs passage quietly. Quiet costs double." Odile's gaze slid to Sebastian, lingered on the pack slung at his hip, and returned. "But you — you're not offering coin, are you. You're offering *that.*"

Sebastian's hand moved to the pack before he could stop it. Odile laughed, a short bark like a gull.

"Oh, don't worry, boy. If I wanted it, I wouldn't take it. I'd sell the *knowledge* of it, which is worth ten of it, and let other people do the bleeding." She leaned forward. The green glass lamps caught her rings and threw small colored ghosts across her face. "Here's my trade. You carry a reading of the core to me — what it hums, what it wants, whether it's waking or dying. I've had four separate buyers ask after spire-cores this season alone. I don't sell rumors; I sell certainty. Give me certainty, and you ride east tomorrow in a wool cart with papers that say you're my niece's servants, dull as ditchwater, both of you."

"And if we refuse?" Beatrix asked.

"Then you walk east, and I mention to no one that I saw you, because mentioning things is bad business." Odile spread her hands. "I'm neutral, founder. It's not a virtue. It's a trade. Kings and rebels and hedge-witches all pay the same rates, and I have never once picked a side, and that is why I am alive and rich and you are a rumor in a swamp."

* * *

They took a room above a chandlery that smelled of tallow and mouse, and argued by lamplight with their voices down to breath.

"She'll sell us," Sebastian said. "The moment someone pays more than a reading is worth."

"Of course she will. That's not betrayal to her; it's bookkeeping." Beatrix sat with her back to the wall, facing the door, the way she had slept for forty years apparently. "The question isn't whether Odile can be trusted. She can't. Nobody out here can. The question is what we get before she sells us, and whether we can be gone when the bill comes due."

"That's not—" He stopped, started again. "In the valley, we trusted people."

"The valley trusted people because the ward made trust cheap." Her voice softened by a degree, which for Beatrix was a great deal. "Out here trust is expensive, so people invented substitutes. Ledgers. Reputations. Neutral brokers. It's not worse, Sebastian. It's just honest about what it is."

He lay awake long after she slept, listening to the core hum its off-beat hum, and thought about the maps turning on their wires in the green light. Maps of faces. Lines running from names to names to names. Somewhere on one of those maps, he understood with a cold settling certainty, there was a line that ended at him. It might already be drawn. Some broker in some other drowned market, paid by some crown clerk, had probably inked *Hollowmere* in a neat hand and drawn the radiating lines outward, and one of those lines ran to a woman who walked like she carried a piano in each hand.

The world was bigger than the valley had taught him. That wasn't the frightening part. The frightening part was that the bigger world had *paperwork.*

* * *

In the morning, fog lay on the quarry like milk, and Odile was waiting for them at the foot of the ramp with two cups of something steaming and bitter.

"Changed your mind yet?" she asked pleasantly.

"We'll give your reading," Beatrix said. "Here. Now. Then we ride."

"Then we ride," Odile agreed, and handed them the cups.

They went down to the boat. Odile cleared her deck of everything but a single table, and onto that table, wrapped in oilcloth, Beatrix laid the core.

Even swaddled, even dormant, it changed the air. Sebastian felt it the way you feel a storm front — pressure behind the ears, taste of metal on the tongue. The green lamps flickered once, all together, like a held breath.

Odile did not reach for it. She took a stool at the table's corner, produced a slim notebook and a pencil stub, and said, "Unwrap it. Slowly. And you, boy — you do the touching. Founder's hands are burned too clean; nothing left in them to read with."

Sebastian looked at Beatrix. Beatrix looked at the core. For a moment he saw something cross her face that he had never seen there — not fear exactly, but the memory of fear, worn smooth as a river stone from handling.

"Do it," she said.

He unwrapped the oilcloth.

The core was smaller than he had imagined, the first time he'd seen it unearthed — a fist-sized knot of crystal and iron, veined with light that moved like slow blood. Up close, with his hands hovering above it, the hum became words almost, or the shape of words, the way wind through a keyhole sounds like a voice if you want it badly enough.

*What do you want,* it seemed to say, or to ask, or to offer. *What do you want. What do you want.*

"Describe it," Odile said, pencil ready. "Everything."

"It's warm," Sebastian said. His voice came out strange, distant. "Not hot. Warm like — like a body. Like something asleep that's dreaming about being awake." He swallowed. "There are layers. Underneath the crystal there's — movement. Like water under ice. And it's not empty. It's *listening.*"

"To what?"

"To whoever's closest." His hands trembled above it. "It wants to be used. That's the whole of it. It doesn't care who uses it or for what. It's a door that wants to be opened. It's been shut for forty years and it has been *dreaming about the handle.*"

Odile's pencil had gone quiet. When Sebastian glanced up, the broker was no longer looking at the core. She was looking at him, with an expression he couldn't read — something between appraisal and pity.

"Waking, then," she said softly. "Not dying. Waking." She wrote one line in her notebook, closed it, and stood. "That's worth more than I quoted you. Consider your passage paid twice over."

"Why?" Sebastian asked. "Why is that worth more?"

Odile began snuffing the green lamps one by one, and the boat dimmed around them like a day ending.

"Because there are people who want a core that's dying," she said. "Museum pieces. Curiosities. But a core that's *waking* —" she blew out the last lamp, and her voice came out of the dark, level and unhurried, "— a waking core starts wars. And there are buyers on both sides of that war already asking after it. Which brings me to the free advice I give all my clients, the advice that keeps me neutral and them alive."

She struck a match and relit one lamp, and in its small circle her face was grave.

"There's a hunter named Veyra. Crown's chief, works out of the capital, and she is not like the border trash you've been dodging. Border hunters chase rumors. Veyra chases *patterns.* She reads a fugitive like I read a ledger — habits, needs, where the feet will go next. In twenty years she has never lost a quarry. Never. Not once. People have crossed oceans, changed their faces, buried their own names, and she has stood at the end of the road waiting for them with the paperwork already signed." Odile tapped the notebook against her palm. "And right now, founder, she is reading *you.* Forty years of habits. Every safehouse you ever loved, every road you ever favored. You taught a generation of guild children to think in certain shapes, and those shapes are legible, and she is reading them like large print."

Beatrix had gone very still. "Thank you for the warning."

"Don't thank me. It wasn't kindness." Odile smiled, thin as wire. "If Veyra takes you, I lose two clients and gain one better. If you live, you owe me a favor, and favors appreciate." She turned to Sebastian last, and for a heartbeat the broker's mask slipped and he saw something underneath — tiredness, maybe, or an old scar of her own. "Boy. One more thing, gratis. That thing on the table? It told you it wants to be used. Believe it. Cores don't lie, because they don't know how. Whatever it offers you — and it will offer — remember that it has never once cared what happens to the hand that opens it."

* * *

The wool cart rolled east before dawn, through fog that smelled of the marsh and, faintly, of woodsmoke from fires too far away to see.

Sebastian sat among the bales with the pack between his knees and watched the country unroll — hedgerows, then open fields, then a village with a crown notice nailed to its post, paper white as a wound. Beatrix dozed against the cart's rail, truly asleep for the first time in days, her face in sleep older and gentler and sadder all at once.

He should sleep too. He knew that. Instead he sat with his hand resting on the pack, feeling the hum come up through the canvas like a cat's purr, patient and enormous.

*I want to be used,* it had said. Or he had heard it say. Odile's warning circled back to him: *it has never once cared what happens to the hand that opens it.*

Behind them, somewhere south, a hunter was reading patterns. Ahead, somewhere east, buyers on both sides of an unborn war were opening their ledgers. And here in the cart, wrapped in oilcloth between dried plums and spare shirts, the heart of the world purred and dreamed about the handle.

Sebastian closed his eyes and tried to decide which frightened him more: the people hunting them, or the thing they were carrying.

He could not make the answer come.
