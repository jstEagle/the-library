## 9. Veyra

The fortress at Cael Marrow had been a monastery once. Veyra liked that about it. The stones remembered discipline — the narrow windows cut to frame nothing but sky, the corridors built for processions of the silent, the cells with their iron rings still bolted where monks had once chained themselves in penance. Now the chains held other things.

She walked the lower gallery before dawn, as she did every morning, her boots making no sound on the flagstones. Below her, through the grate in the floor, she could hear water moving in the old cistern channel — a sound like whispering, like a congregation murmuring behind a closed door. The monks had called it the voice of the deep. Veyra had grown up two valleys east of here, in a village where an old woman could coax fire from wet straw and was burned for it on Veyra's seventh birthday. She remembered the crowd's face. Not cruel. Relieved. That was the word no one ever used. *Relieved* — the way a fever breaks, the way a splinter comes out. The kingdom had been sick for a long time, and everyone knew it, and no one said so until the pyre was lit.

She believed what she believed the way she believed in load-bearing walls. Not because it was comfortable. Because everything else fell down without it.

Three prisoners waited in the cells below. Guild-folk from Hollowmere, taken in the valley's breach nine days past. Two men and a woman, all of them giftless — which was itself informative. The Hollowmere guild had always kept its unmarked members closest to its heart. A guild that trusted people without power was a guild organized around something other than power. Veyra found that more interesting than any grimoire.

She descended the stair with her scribe, Pellam, trailing her with his ink-stained fingers and his ledger of questions. He was a good scribe. He wrote down what people actually said rather than what he wished they'd said, which made him nearly useless for most interrogations and invaluable for hers.

"Begin with the woman," she said. "Maren Ostwick. She was the one who talked."

* * *

Maren Ostwick sat in her cell with her hands folded in her lap like a woman waiting out a sermon. She had talked on the first day — names, routes, the wardline's rhythm — and had hated herself cleanly and completely ever since, which Veyra understood and did not exploit. Guilt was a door that locked from the inside. You didn't kick it open. You just stood outside it and let the person inside remember you were there.

"You've eaten," Veyra observed, sitting on the stool across from her. Bread, cheese, small beer. No manacles. There were no manacles anywhere in Cael Marrow; Veyra had ordered them melted down years ago. Fear made prisoners lie in interesting shapes. Comfort made them tell the truth by accident.

"I've eaten," Maren said.

"And you've slept. And no one has touched you." Veyra let that sit. "I want you to know I don't consider your talking a betrayal. I consider it arithmetic. You counted what silence would cost against what speech would cost, and you chose the smaller number. Most people can't even do the sum."

Maren's jaw tightened. "You're not what I expected."

"No one ever says what they expected. Say it."

"A monster," Maren said. "They say you burn children."

"They say that about anyone who does arithmetic in public." Veyra unfolded a sheet of paper on her knee. "I'm going to ask you about Beatrix."

Something moved behind Maren's eyes — not fear exactly. Older than fear. The look of someone hearing the name of a dead friend spoken casually.

"She founded your guild forty years ago. Then she exiled herself from it, or it exiled her — the accounts differ, and I suspect both are true. She lived among you as a giftless elder. Respected. Kept apart. Given the worst chores and the best seat at council." Veyra watched Maren's face do a small, involuntary thing at *the best seat*. "You loved her. All of you loved her, and none of you liked her, and you never asked why she stayed giftless in a valley full of the gifted when every instinct of the gifted is to gather where they're strongest."

"We don't talk about—"

"I know. That's the point." Veyra turned the paper around. On it, in Pellam's neat hand, was a list. "Here is what I know about Beatrix, assembled from thirty years of crown records and four days of your testimony. She was the Spire's chief warden before the war ended. She was present at the Razing of Vell. She survived a purge that killed every other warden of her rank. And then she spent forty years living in a valley that magic cannot enter — a valley whose ward, according to every scholar I have ever bought, requires a source of extraordinary density to sustain it. Wards like that don't run on prayer, Mistress Ostwick. They run on fuel."

Maren had gone very still.

"So here is my question, and please understand I am not asking it as a threat. I am asking it because I think you've already asked it yourself, lying awake, and been too frightened to finish the thought." Veyra leaned forward slightly. "What does a woman carry, that she must live somewhere magic cannot follow?"

* * *

Pellam caught up with her in the armory an hour later, where she was checking the tension on a crossbow she would never use. He had learned long ago that she thought better with her hands occupied.

"The woman broke," he said. "Not much. A crack. But she looked at the wall when you said *fuel*, and people look away from the thing they've seen."

"People usually do." Veyra set the bow down. "Tell me the crack."

"She said — and this is verbatim, I wrote it — 'She used to walk the wardline every morning. Rain, snow. Forty years. We thought it was devotion.'"

Veyra smiled, and Pellam, who had served her eleven years, took half a step back without meaning to.

"Devotion," Veyra repeated. "Yes. I imagine they did." She crossed to the map table in the center of the room — the whole kingdom inked on calfskin, mountains shaded, rivers silvered, and pinned to it, a hundred small flags of red and black marking every site the crown had searched since the hunt began. "A warden walks her wardline daily for forty years. Devotion, yes. But also inspection. Also maintenance. Also—" she tapped the map, thinking aloud, "—proximity. You walk the line of a fence every morning if you keep something valuable behind it. Or beneath it. Or—" 

She stopped.

Pellam waited. Pellam was very good at waiting.

"The center," Veyra said quietly. "You don't hide a heart at the edge of a body. You hide it where the body will always be between it and the world. She walks the perimeter because the perimeter is the lock, and the lock tells you where the vault has to be." Her finger moved across the map to the valley — Hollowmere, a green smudge in the eastern folds, ringed by hills like a cupped hand. "The wardline runs the valley's rim. Which means the core sits at its center. Under the village. Under the well, most likely — water masks resonance, every text agrees on that. They drew their drinking water from the thing itself for forty years and called it providence."

She straightened. For a moment she simply stood there, in the cold armory light, feeling the shape of the answer settle into place the way a keystone settles — everything above it suddenly bearing weight.

But something nagged at her. Something in the geometry of the last nine days.

"Pellam. If the core were still under Hollowmere, why did Beatrix leave?"

"She fled the breach. Anyone would—"

"Anyone would flee *outward*. She fled *with* it, if my read is right — the flare that killed Corporan Dessa at the wardline wasn't a weapon discharge. It was a source waking up. It was being carried." Veyra began to pace, three steps, turn, three steps. "So she took it out of the vault. Why? You only move a heart if the body is dying. The breach spooked her — fine. But then, where does a woman go after forty years in a cage she built herself? Where does Beatrix run?"

"Not to cities. She'd be recognized."

"No. Not to old allies — we've burned or bought most of them, and she'd assume so. Not south; the passes are snowed. She'd go..." Veyra stopped pacing. "...to ground. To stone. To places that meant something before the war. Guild sites. Waystations. Ruins the crown hasn't catalogued because the crown stopped looking at the gifted twenty years ago and started merely hunting them." She looked up at the arrow-slit window, where the sky was going from grey to the color of pewter. "She's not running to escape us, Pellam. She's running a route. An old route. A pilgrimage. She's saying goodbye to her own history, or she's looking for something in it, and either way she'll touch the places that mattered. The motherhouse. The waystations. The—"

She paused again, and this time the pause had teeth.

"—chapel," she said. "There was a chapel. Pre-war records mention a guild chapel in the lowlands east of the Sere, deconsecrated, flooded when the river changed course. Flooded ground. Water masking resonance. The same logic as the well." She was already moving toward the door. "Saddle the riders. Quietly. Six men, no banners. And send a bird to the garrison at Greyford — I want eyes on every road into the lowlands, and I want them watching for two travelers, one old, one young."

"The young one," Pellam said, keeping pace. "The valley boy who woke at the wardline. Dessa's report said his gift flared raw and huge."

"Dessa's report also failed to survive the encounter, so weigh it accordingly." Veyra pulled her cloak from its peg. "But yes. A dormant gift waking at first contact with the core — that means proximity-bonding. It means the boy isn't luggage. He's becoming part of the mechanism." She said it without drama, the way a physician says *the wound is infected*, and it was precisely this flatness that made Pellam shiver. "Beatrix chose him, or he was chosen for her, and either way he matters more than she does now. Find the boy, and the old woman will put herself between you and him. She's spent forty years learning how to stand in front of things."

At the stable door she stopped. Dawn was coming up over the courtyard, thin and colorless, and somewhere beyond the walls the cistern channel whispered its monkish whisper.

"For the record," she said, not turning around, "I hope I'm wrong about the chapel. I hope she outruns us entirely. A world with the core buried and quiet is a world I can grow old in." She settled the cloak on her shoulders. "But hope is not a method. Ride."

* * *

They rode out as the bells of Cael Marrow rang prime — six riders in undyed wool, armed like drovers, moving east along the river road while the mist still lay on the fields like a second country laid over the first. Veyra rode at the front and did not look back at the fortress. She was thinking about the old woman from her childhood village, the fire-coaxer, and about the crowd's relieved faces, and about the fact that in thirty years of hunting she had never once met a gifted person who thanked her, and never once met a village that didn't.

Both of those things were true. She had built her whole life on the space between them.

Behind her, Pellam urged his horse alongside. "If the boy's gift is bonding to the core," he said carefully, "and we take the core — what happens to him?"

Veyra watched the road unspool ahead through the mist, pale as a scar.

"Then we'll learn something," she said, "that no one has ever survived learning."

She put her heels to the horse, and the six of them went down into the lowlands like a needle going into a vein — small, precise, and aimed at the heart.
