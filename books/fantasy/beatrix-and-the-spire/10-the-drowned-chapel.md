## 10. The Drowned Chapel

The rain found them three days out from Odile's market, and by the time the light failed they were both soaked through, walking a road that had given up pretending to be one. Sebastian had learned to read Beatrix's silences the way sailors read weather. This one meant *we are lost*, and this one, longer, meant *we are lost and I will not say so first*.

The chapel appeared out of the mist like something surfacing from deep water.

At first he took it for a hill with pretensions — a rounded green mound rising from the marsh at the road's edge, crowned with a single crooked arch of grey stone that might once have been a bell tower's shoulder. Then he saw the windows: narrow lancets drowned to their sills in dark water, and beneath the surface, faint as memory, the pale suggestion of columns marching down into the flooded nave.

"Saint Verene's," Beatrix said quietly. "It was a pilgrimage church, before the war. The river changed its mind about where it wanted to be." She stood at the waterline, rain streaming off her hood, and something in her face made him think she was seeing a different building entirely.

"Is it safe?"

"Nothing is safe." She said it without drama, the way she said most true things. "But it is forgotten, which is nearly as good. Come."

They waded in through what had been the great western door, now half-submerged, the oak long rotted to lacework. Inside, the water came to Sebastian's thighs and was warmer than the air, which somehow made it worse. Their boots sent soft rings across the surface. Overhead, the roof had mostly fallen, and the evening sky showed through the ribs of the vaulting like a ribcage stripped clean. Swallows had nested in the clerestory; they muttered and resettled as the intruders passed.

A stone platform rose near the crossing of the nave — the old altar dais, high enough to keep them above the waterline. Moss had taken it over in green velvet. Someone had built a fire ring there once, years ago, the charred stones still nested together, and Beatrix looked at it too long before turning away to unpack their meager bundle.

Sebastian helped her gather driftwood and the dry heartwood of fallen beams, and soon a small fire hissed and spat against the damp. He ate hard bread and smoked fish and watched the water move in the failing light. It was strange how beautiful ruin could be. In Hollowmere he had never seen a ruin. The valley kept itself whole, or perhaps simply never let anything grow old enough to fall apart.

"Your gift is restless," Beatrix said.

He started. "How can you tell?"

"You've been clenching your jaw since the market. You clench it when you're holding it down." She fed a stick to the fire without looking at him. "Holding it down is not the same as holding it. A dam is not mastery, Sebastian. It's just a delay with good posture."

"I nearly set a man's beard on fire in the market," he said. "You told me to smother it."

"I told you to smother it *there*. There were crown informants buying eels ten feet from us." She finally turned, and the firelight moved on her face like water. "Here, there is no one. Here, I'd rather you learn than strangle. Gifts that are only ever strangled come back up wrong."

He wanted to argue. He also wanted, badly, in a way that embarrassed him, to try.

"What would I even do?" he asked. "I've done nothing but break things so far. A lantern. A lock. A man's soup, which I still feel bad about."

"Then we'll start with breaking less." She rose, water dripping from her cloak, and walked out onto the flooded floor until she stood knee-deep among the drowned pews. "Water remembers being taught. It's the most patient of the elements — it has already surrendered its shape. All you have to do is ask it to borrow yours."

"That sounds like a poem, not an instruction."

"The old guild disciplines were poems. Poems are instructions that survive translation." She held out her hand, palm up, over the black water. "Watch first."

For a moment nothing happened. Then Sebastian felt it — not saw it, *felt* it, a pressure change like a storm front passing, a hush in his teeth. The water around Beatrix's hand began to rise. Not splash, not ripple: rise, a smooth column lifting out of itself, climbing her palm, spreading into a thin sheet that hung in the air like a pane of glass. Rain fell onto its upper surface and ran off its edges. The sheet caught the firelight and threw it wobbling across the ruined ceiling, gold sliding over wet stone.

He forgot to breathe. It was the first time he had seen her do anything — truly anything — since the wardline broke, since the core had flared in her hands and killed a hunter and lit something awake in him that had no name yet. Her own magic was severed; he knew that, knew it the way you know a friend is dead. What moved the water now was borrowed, drawn thin through whatever thread still connected her to the core sleeping in their pack. He could feel that thread humming between them, faint and constant, like a wire under tension.

"You see?" she said. The water-pane trembled with her voice. "Not force. Invitation."

She let it fall. It collapsed back into the flood with a sound like applause.

"Now you."

His mouth had gone dry despite everything. "What if I—"

"You won't. And if you do, I'm standing in the water, aren't I? Let's find out what happens." But there was something in her eyes he couldn't read — not fear exactly. Anticipation, maybe. Or guilt.

He stepped down off the altar. The water closed around his waist, cold now that the fire was behind him. He put his palm out the way she had, over the surface, and reached for the thing inside him — the coiled heat behind his sternum that he had spent three days smothering, the thing that had woken when the core flared.

At first, nothing. Then the heat leaned forward, the way a dog leans toward a door, and the water *heard*.

It began with a ring around his hand, a perfect circle of stillness in the moving flood. Then the circle climbed. Water rose up his arm in a spiral, cool and obedient, and the joy of it went through him like a struck bell — *it listens, it listens to me* — and he laughed aloud, and the spiral answered the laugh by growing, widening, climbing past his elbow, past his shoulder, a column of water twisting slowly in the air beside him, catching firelight, magnificent—

"Steady," Beatrix said. "Don't feed it your excitement. Feed it your patience."

He tried. That was the problem. He tried, and trying meant reaching deeper, and deeper meant finding more — the gift was not the small coal he'd thought it was but a seam of ore running down into darkness, and every inch he descended it burned brighter, and the water rose higher, and somewhere in the delight he stopped noticing that the column was no longer a column but a shape, a swelling sphere of suspended water six feet across, groaning softly, held between his hand and the sky.

"Sebastian." Beatrix's voice had changed. "Let it down. Slowly."

"It's fine—"

"*Slowly.*"

He began to lower it. But lowering meant loosening his grip, and the grip was all he had, and the sphere shuddered — and in that shudder he felt, with terrible clarity, that he did not actually know how much of it there was. He had been measuring himself by the valley, where he was nothing. Out here, with the core humming in the pack twenty feet away, he was something else, and the something else had no bottom he could find.

The sphere slipped.

It didn't fall — it *moved*, all at once, the way a released rope moves, whipping outward through the flooded nave, smashing through a drowned pew, slamming against a wall in a wave that tore ivy loose from the stonework. And beyond the broken eastern wall, through the gap where the apse had fallen away into the marsh, Sebastian heard a scream.

A child's scream.

*

Later, he would piece it together: a village half a mile east along the causeway, a boy who had slipped out at dusk to fish the flooded fields, a boy who had seen the light of their fire and the impossible shining of the water and come closer, because boys always come closer. The wave Sebastian lost had gone through the broken wall and across the shallows and taken the boy off his feet like a hand, sweeping him out into the deeper water of the drowned nave.

Sebastian reached him first. That much he would always know — he got there first, floundering through chest-deep water, and saw the boy's head go under, saw the pale flash of a small hand, and lunged, and caught cloth, and then the boy's terror hit him like weather. Because the boy was drowning, drowning in water that Sebastian had moved, and Sebastian's gift — his stupid, eager, bottomless gift — *answered the boy's fear too*, the flood surging, churning, the whole nave beginning to turn like something waking up hungry, and Sebastian couldn't hold the boy and couldn't hold the water and couldn't tell anymore which of the things dragging at him was magic and which was panic.

Then Beatrix was there.

He never saw how she crossed the distance. One moment she was on the altar; the next she was beside him in the black water, and her hands closed over his — old hands, scarred hands, steadier than anything he had ever known — and she said one word, low, in the old guild tongue, a word he would later find written in the margins of every discipline she taught him:

*"Enough."*

The water obeyed her. Not because she commanded it — she had almost nothing left to command with — but because he yielded to her voice, and through her voice his gift remembered it had a shape. The churn settled. The boy came up gasping between them, and Beatrix hauled him against her shoulder and beat the water from his lungs while Sebastian knelt in the flood shaking so hard his teeth chattered.

They carried the boy to the altar, wrapped him in Beatrix's cloak, and fed him fire-warmed water and the last of their honey. He was maybe eight, brown-haired, gap-toothed, named Tam, and once the blue left his lips he decided the entire episode had been magnificent.

"You *threw* me," he said, with awe. "With the water. Like the miller's dog shakes off rain."

"I—" Sebastian's voice failed. "I'm sorry. I didn't know anyone was—"

"Can you do it again?"

"TAM."

The shout came from outside, along the causeway, a woman's voice cracking with fear, and Beatrix was on her feet with the speed of a woman thirty years younger. "Go home," she said to the boy, gently and absolutely. "You slipped on the stones. You fell in the shallows. You climbed out yourself. You saw nothing and no one. Do you understand me, Tam?"

The boy looked at her. Something passed over his young face — recognition, maybe, of the tone every child knows, the tone of adults who are afraid and hiding it well. He nodded.

"And if anyone asks — the crier's men, the riders, anyone — you fell. Nothing else."

"They say there's a witch on the roads," Tam offered, conversationally. "Papa says the crown's paying silver for names." He looked at Sebastian with frank, terrible curiosity. "Are you the witch?"

"No," Sebastian said. "I'm nobody."

The boy considered this, accepted it, and slipped away into the dusk toward the shouting, leaving ripples and silence behind him.

Beatrix sat back down heavily by the fire. For a long time neither of them spoke. The swallows had gone quiet. Water dripped somewhere in the dark of the nave, patient as a clock.

"I could have killed him," Sebastian said finally. His voice sounded foreign to himself. "I didn't even know he was there. I was *enjoying myself*, and he almost died, and I didn't know."

"Yes."

"That's all? *Yes?*"

"What would you like me to say? That it wasn't your fault? It was. That it won't happen again? It will, unless you become someone who can bear the weight of it. That's the lesson, Sebastian. There isn't another one." She drew her knees up, and the firelight made her look suddenly very old and very tired. "You want the gift to be a blessing. It isn't. It isn't a curse either — curses at least are honest about what they cost. It's a responsibility. It's carrying a full cup through a crowded room, forever, and knowing that everyone who bumps you wears what spills."

He stared at the water below the altar, black and calm again, keeping its secrets. "In the valley, I used to pray for this. Every night. I used to press my hand against the wardstone and wish."

"I know. I watched you do it."

"You *watched*—"

"I watched a boy press his hand against a stone that was singing to him, and I said nothing, because saying something meant the end of the life we'd built, and I was a coward for forty years." She said it flatly, without self-pity, the way she confessed everything: as fact, as debt. "So. You've paid part of your price tonight. Let me pay some of mine. You asked me once, on the road, what the core really is. I gave you half an answer. Sit down. I'll give you the rest, and then you'll understand why tonight frightened me more than any hunter does."

He sat.

* * *

"The world wasn't always divided into gifted and giftless," Beatrix began. "That division is the wound. Before the spires, magic lived in the land itself — in rivers, in stone, in the roots of forests. People bargained with it, borrowed it, sang to it. No one *carried* it. Carrying is new. Carrying is what happened after."

She fed the fire a piece of beam-end, and shadows climbed the ruined vaulting.

"There were nine spires once. Great engines of stone and song, raised by people whose names are all lost, built to draw the land's magic up and hold it — to store it the way a granary stores grain, so it could be measured, rationed, *owned*. Do you understand? The spires were not temples. They were treasuries. And whoever held the treasury held everyone who needed to withdraw from it."

"The guilds," Sebastian said slowly. "The old guilds were... banks?"

"Worse. Kingdoms." Her mouth twisted. "Each spire had its keepers, and each keeper-familia grew rich and powerful and certain, and certain is the word I want you to remember, because certainty is what burned the world. Two of the spire-houses went to war over the rights to a third's treasury. It should have been a border squabble. But they had the cores — the hearts of the spires — and a core doesn't ration. A core gives you everything you ask for, all at once, because it cannot understand the word *enough*."

She looked at him, and her eyes in the firelight were the eyes of someone reading an old scar.

"The War of the Nine Hearts lasted eleven years. Whole provinces were unmade. Not conquered — *unmade*, erased down to the bedrock, because some keeper's heir demanded power no human frame should carry and the core obliged. Rivers ran backward. Children were born already old. The land's own magic, which had belonged to everyone and no one, became a weapon aimed by a few dozen people who had never once been told *no*."

"What stopped it?"

"Not victory. Exhaustion. The surviving houses met at a place called the Grey Table and did the only wise thing anyone did in that century: they took their cores out of the spires and hid them, each house scattering its heart into the world, and they swore the spires empty and the age of treasuries closed." She paused. "Eight houses kept that oath. Cores sunk in oceans, buried under mountains, ground to sand and scattered on the wind. And one house — my house, the house of Hollowmere, the ninth — kept its oath in a different way."

Sebastian's skin prickled. "You hid yours better than anyone."

"I hid mine where no one would ever look," Beatrix said. "Inside the ward of a valley full of farmers. I stole the last core out of my own spire forty years ago, when the crown began its first purge, and I carried it here, and I fed it into the wardline, and I let it sleep." She spread her hands. "Every safe year you ever lived in Hollowmere was bought with a stolen weapon. Every quiet morning. Every harvest festival. We weren't a refuge, Sebastian. We were a vault wearing a village's face."

The words settled over him like the floodwater had — cold, total, everywhere. He thought of the wardstone he had pressed his hand to as a boy. It had been singing. Of course it had been singing. It had been a god asleep under a blanket, and he had been petting it.

"The crown hunters," he said. "Veyra. They're not hunting people."

"People are the pretext. Useful, popular, endless — there will always be gifted folk to burn, and burning them keeps the kingdom afraid and grateful. But the order Veyra carries goes back further than her, back to the Grey Table's enemies, to the houses that regretted their oath. The crown wants the ninth core. The last treasury in the world. With it, they don't need to hunt the gifted at all. They can make the gift itself a license, a tax, a leash. They can finish what the war started and win it this time, four hundred years late." She looked into the fire. "And the rebels Odile whispers about — the gifted factions who want it as a sword — they're not the opposite of the crown. They're the same sickness with different colors. Everyone who wants the core wants to be the one holding the cup. No one wants to be the room full of people getting splashed."

"Then what do we do with it?" His voice cracked on it. "If it's a weapon for them and a weapon for the rebels and a leash for the crown — what is it for *us*, Beatrix? Why carry it at all?"

She was quiet for a long time. Outside, the marsh breathed; somewhere far off, a night bird called and was not answered.

"Because it's also the last living piece of what the world was before we learned to hoard," she said at last. "Because a sleeping god is still a god, and gods dream, and lately — since the wardline broke, since it woke — I think it's dreaming toward someone. Toward *you*, maybe. And because I am an old woman who made every wrong choice slowly enough to call them prudent, and I mean to make one right one quickly, before the end." She reached over and gripped his wrist, hard, her bones sharp under his skin. "Tonight you learned what the gift costs when it's small. A boy's breath. Your own peace. Now multiply that by everything, Sebastian. By a kingdom. By the world. That's what you're carrying in that pack, and I need you to understand it not as a story but as a weight, because soon — sooner than either of us wants — someone is going to offer you a bargain for it, and you will have to know, in your bones, what *no* costs."

He looked at his hands in the firelight. They seemed ordinary. They were not.

"I pressed my hand to the wardstone," he said quietly, "and wished for this. I didn't know I was wishing on a sleeping god."

"No one ever does. That's why the disciplines exist. That's why the guild existed — not to own the magic, but to teach people to carry it without spilling." She smiled, and it was a tired, real smile, the first in days. "Congratulations, apprentice. Tonight you failed your first lesson. It's the only way anyone ever learns it."

He laughed — a broken, wet laugh, but a laugh. Above them, the swallows stirred in the clerestory, murmuring, settling.

It was Beatrix who heard the horses.

Her head came up like a deer's, and her hand closed around his arm, and the fire was dead between one heartbeat and the next — she smothered it with a sweep of her cloak and a word, and then there was only moonlight and the black water and the sound, far off along the causeway, of hoofbeats. More than two. Moving steadily. Not searching.

*Patrolling.*

"Tam," Sebastian whispered. "*They talked to Tam.*"

"Or they patrol this road every night and tonight means nothing." Beatrix was already moving, bundling the pack with the core, her face unreadable in the moonlight. "Either way, we are gone before dawn. Take the northern edge, through the water — step where I step, wake nothing, spill nothing."

They waded out into the dark of the drowned nave, toward the broken eastern wall, the cold rising around them. And as Sebastian climbed through the gap where the apse had fallen, he felt it one more time — the hum of the core against his spine through the canvas of the pack, faint and constant, like a heart beating just a little too close to his own.

It felt, he thought with a chill that had nothing to do with the water, like something listening.

Like something that had heard him laugh tonight, and heard the boy almost die, and had enjoyed both.

Like something waiting to be asked for *more*.
