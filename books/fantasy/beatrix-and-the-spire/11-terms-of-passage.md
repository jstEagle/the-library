## 11. Terms of Passage

Odile's tent smelled of ink, tallow, and something older beneath both — the mineral breath of stone that had been underground a very long time.

Beatrix recognized it before she recognized anything else in the smuggler's market: that smell. It clung to a person who had spent years near the spire-heart, and it did not wash out. She had carried it herself for forty years, buried so deep in her clothes and hair and bones that she no longer noticed it on herself. But she noticed it here, in a canvas room hung with maps and brass instruments at the edge of a market that existed on no honest ledger, and it told her that Odile Voss knew exactly what Beatrix carried, and had known since the moment they'd walked through the flaps.

The cartographer sat behind a table scarred by candle-wax and knife-marks, not looking up from a chart she was annotating with a pen cut from a raven's feather. She was smaller than Beatrix remembered — the last time they'd stood in the same room, Odile had been a girl of nineteen with ink-stained fingers and a talent for finding things that wanted to stay lost. Thirty years had bent her spine into a question mark and silvered what remained of her hair, but the eyes were unchanged: quick, gray, and utterly without warmth.

"You've aged," Odile said, without greeting.

"So have you."

"I aged honestly. You aged like a lie kept too long." The raven-quill scratched. "Sit down, Bea. Or should I say *Founder*? I hear the guild uses the old titles again when it suits them."

Sebastian shifted beside Beatrix — she heard the small restless sounds of him, the creak of his boot, the breath he pulled through his nose the way she'd taught him, slow and even, to keep the gift banked low. He was doing well. He was doing better than she had any right to expect. And he was watching her with those open valley eyes that still believed, after everything, that the world rewarded straightforward dealing.

*He is about to learn who I am,* Beatrix thought, and found, to her own quiet horror, that she felt less regret about that than she ought to.

She sat. The stool creaked under her.

"You sent word you wanted passage," Odile said. "North, past the toll-roads, past Veyra's checkpoints, to the drowned coast or wherever it is exiles go to die. My barges move tonight. My price—"

"Name it."

"—is what you're carrying in that satchel." The quill stopped moving. Odile looked up for the first time, and Beatrix saw the hunger there, banked and patient as coals. "The heart of the Hollowmere spire. Dormant core of the last true working in the kingdom. Forty years missing, forty years hunted by every crown agent and every ambitious fool between here and the capital. And now it walks into my market wearing a dead woman's face."

The silence stretched. Somewhere outside, a mule complained; a vendor called the price of dried fish in a voice worn smooth as riverstone. Beatrix let the ordinary sounds fill the pause, because pauses were weapons and she had learned, long ago, never to reach for one first.

"Word travels," she said finally.

"Word *paid*," Odile corrected. "I don't deal in rumor. Rumor is what poor people trade. I deal in certainty." She set down the quill and folded her hands, and Beatrix watched the transformation — the broker falling away, the girl from the motherhouse rising underneath, the one who had once sat across from young Beatrix in the archive and asked the question no elder could answer: *and when the kingdoms come for us, what then?* "You owe me an answer, Bea. Not as a buyer. As the girl whose name you struck from the guild rolls for asking it."

"I struck your name because you sold guild routes to a Toll-lord's factor."

"I sold three names to save two hundred. You'd have done the same. You simply lacked the stomach to be seen doing it." Odile smiled without any pleasure in it. "We are the same animal, you and I. The difference is that I stopped pretending."

Beatrix felt Sebastian's eyes on her. She did not turn to meet them.

* * *

The negotiation lasted until the candle burned down two finger-widths, and Beatrix remembered every minute of it afterward with the clarity of a wound.

Odile wanted the core. That was never in question. What unfolded, slowly, with the terrible patience of a spider repairing its web, was the shape of what Odile intended to do with it.

"Auction," she said, tapping the table with one blunt fingernail. "Not to the crown — Veyra pays in warrants and gallows, and I prefer my neck unbroken. Not to the rebels either; they'd crack it open in some cellar and take half a city with them. There are others. Old families. Foreign buyers across the strait. A prince in the south who collects impossible things." Her gray eyes glittered. "The core is worth more whole, dormant, and *mysterious* than it will ever be worth used. Scarcity, Bea. The only magic that never fails."

"And everyone who ever died keeping it secret," Beatrix said quietly, "died so you could hang a price tag on it."

"Everyone who ever died keeping anything secret died because secrets are expensive and someone always ends up holding the bill." Odile leaned back. "You held it forty years. Look how well that served you. Your wardline broke. Your guild scattered. Your people are in Veyra's cages. The secret failed, Bea. All I'm proposing is that the failure at least turn a profit."

And that was the moment. Beatrix would remember it precisely: the candle guttering, the smell of cold stone and ink, Odile's face lit from below in amber and shadow — and the realization settling over her like frost forming, silent and total, that she was going to lie to this woman. Not deflect. Not bargain up. Lie, plainly, knowingly, and walk away knowing she had made an enemy who mapped the hidden geography of the entire kingdom.

Because the alternative was handing over the heart of the world to be sold to whichever hand reached deepest into its purse, and Beatrix had not spent four decades burying a thing beneath a wardline and a false name and her own severed magic to watch it end up in a collector's cabinet.

She had made worse choices than this. She had simply never made one in front of Sebastian before.

"Here is my offer," she said, and her voice came out level, and she hated how easily it came out level. "Passage north for myself and the boy, tonight, on your quietest barge. In exchange, the core."

Sebastian turned his head sharply. She felt the motion like a draft.

Odile's smile widened by a degree. "Just like that?"

"Just like that."

"You're lying."

"Of course I'm lying," Beatrix said. "But consider the arithmetic. If I'm lying, you'll discover it somewhere over open water with my body conveniently close at hand, and you'll have lost nothing but a night's freight. If I'm telling the truth, you hold the most valuable object in the world and the gratitude of a woman who can teach you things about it that no auction catalogue will contain. Either way, you're ahead. The only version where you lose is the one where you refuse, and we both walk back out into a market full of informers, and Veyra hears within the day where the core has been sleeping." She paused. "You've sold information to the crown before, Odile. Don't pretend the thought of what they'd pay hasn't already crossed your mind. Refuse me, and you'll sell me instead — and the crown doesn't pay half what a southern prince would, and you know that too."

For a long moment Odile said nothing. The candle flame stood perfectly still, as if listening.

Then the cartographer laughed — a short, dry sound, like a branch snapping under snow.

"There she is," Odile said softly. "There's the woman who founded a guild on a stolen heart. You know what the elders used to say about you, back in the motherhouse? That you were the gentlest of them. That you wept over injured birds." She shook her head. "Gentle people make the cruelest liars, Bea. They've had more practice convincing themselves."

"Passage," Beatrix said. "Tonight."

"Passage tonight." Odile drew a sheet of paper toward her and began writing in her small precise hand. "One condition. I want to see it. Before we sail. I want to look at the thing itself, confirm it's real, confirm it's *whole*. No broker sells sight-unseen — not even a blind one."

"No."

"Then no barge."

Beatrix weighed it. A glimpse — a single unwrapping of oilcloth in a locked room, ten seconds of lamplight — against a refusal that stranded them in a town where neighbors sold neighbors for pardons, with Veyra's riders surely no more than two days behind.

"One look," Beatrix said. "In the dark. Wrapped to the wrist. And if your hand moves toward it, the boy puts you to sleep where you sit, and we leave you snoring among your maps."

Odile glanced past her at Sebastian — really looked at him, for the first time, with the assessing gaze of a woman pricing livestock — and something flickered across her face. Recognition, maybe. Pity, possibly. "The giftless boy," she murmured. "So that's what you've been raising out there in your forgetful valley. A weapon that doesn't know it's loaded."

"He's a member of my guild," Beatrix said, "which is more than you ever were."

"Mm." Odile signed the paper, blotted it, and slid it across the table along with a stub of black sealing wax. "Passage and a look, for the core. Swear it on whatever you still believe in, and we sail on the ebb tide."

Beatrix swore. She was careful to swear it in the old guild form, the binding cadence, the words that had once been unbreakable among the gifted — knowing that Odile, severed like herself, bound like herself, would feel the oath settle and believe it iron.

Knowing all the while that the oath was built around a hollow. *Passage for the core.* The words said nothing about which core.

* * *

Outside, the market had gone blue with dusk. Lanterns were being lit along the stall-rows one by one, little captive suns, and the smell of frying onions and wet rope lay thick over the mud. Sebastian walked beside her in silence for a hundred paces before he spoke, and when he did, his voice was carefully, terribly even.

"The decoy. When did you make it?"

"Two nights ago. While you slept."

"What is it?"

"A shell of river clay and ash and one thread of the wardline's residue. To any reading — any scrying, any divination, any hunter's compass — it will sing the same song as the true core. Faintly. Sleepily. Exactly as a genuine dormant heart would."

"And Odile?"

"Will inspect the decoy by lamplight and feel its weight in her hands and find it flawless, because I made it flawless, because I have had forty years and nothing else to do with my grief." Beatrix kept her eyes forward. "She'll wrap it, seal it, stow it below decks believing she holds the future of magic in a box, and by the time she learns otherwise we'll be beyond her reach."

They walked another stretch. The lanterns multiplied. Somewhere a fiddle started up, badly.

"She trusted you," Sebastian said at last. "Underneath all of it — the prices and the threats — she trusted you. I watched her face. That oath you swore, the old words — she believed them the way you believe the sun comes up. And you—" He stopped walking. "You built the lie *into* the oath. You used the holiest thing we had as a lockpick."

Beatrix halted too. Around them the market flowed, parting and reforming, paying them no attention — just another pair of travelers in the dusk.

"Yes," she said.

"That's it? *Yes?*"

"What would you have me say, Sebastian? That I agonized? I didn't. That's the truth you deserve to hear. I looked at the woman who sold our routes thirty years ago, and I looked at the hunters burning everything we ever built, and I chose, the way I have chosen a hundred times, and I will choose it again tomorrow, because the alternative is watching Veyra cage you the way she caged Wren and Tomas and the others." She turned to face him fully, and let him see all of it — no softness, no apology, the plain architecture of what she was. "You want a mentor who is good. I am a mentor who is *useful*. In the valley, wrapped in wards and routine, you could confuse the two. Out here they come apart, and you need to decide, soon, whether you can follow a woman who lies with an oath in her mouth."

Sebastian's jaw worked. His eyes were bright and hurt and furious, and beneath all of it — this was the thing that undid her, the thing she would carry — beneath all of it was the terrible dawning comprehension of a young man realizing that the person who saved him might be someone he should fear.

"You chose me before I was born," he said quietly. "That's what the ledger said. So tell me honestly, Beatrix. Was I ever anything to you? Or am I just the last tool left in the kit?"

The fiddle stumbled. Recovered. Played on.

"You were a child eating burnt porridge and pretending it was fine," Beatrix said, "and I watched you give the larger share to a younger boy who hadn't earned it, and I thought: *there*. There is the one thing I have done in forty years that wasn't calculation." Her voice roughened despite her. "Everything else I have done since the wardline broke has been arithmetic. Do not ask me to apologize for surviving. But do not ever believe that you were part of the sum."

He stared at her for a long time. The lantern light moved on his face.

Then he nodded — once, slow, unfinished, a door neither closed nor opened — and turned, and walked on toward the docks, and Beatrix followed, and the space between them was the widest it had ever been though their shoulders nearly touched.

Behind them, in a tent smelling of ink and old stone, Odile Voss blotted a second letter, addressed it in her small precise hand, and set it aside for the morning rider.

It was addressed to no smuggler, no prince, no rebel cell.

It read, in its entirety: *She lied. Come and see.*
