## 12. The Guild That Was

The motherhouse announced itself long before we saw it.

We had been walking since dawn through country that grew stranger the farther north we climbed — hills folded like sleeping animals, hedgerows gone feral, stone walls dissolving back into the earth they'd been pulled from. The road narrowed to a track, the track to a suggestion, and then even that gave out, and Beatrix led us instead by something older than roads. She walked with her chin lifted slightly, the way a hound lifts its head to a wind no one else can smell.

"You feel it," I said. Not a question. I had learned that much about her in the weeks since the wardline broke: she did not answer questions she considered beneath them.

"I feel what's left of it." Her voice was flat, but her hands were not; they kept moving at her sides, opening and closing, as if she were greeting a crowd only she could see. "The house was warded once. Deeply. Even severed, even dead, a warding leaves a texture. Like walking into a room where a fire burned out an hour ago."

I felt nothing. I said so, and immediately regretted it, because her expression did something complicated — not hurt exactly, more like a door closing on a room where hurt was already standing.

"No," she said. "You wouldn't. Not yet."

*Yet.* She had begun using that word with me lately, and each time it landed somewhere between promise and threat.

The mist came up out of the valley floor around midday, and when it thinned, the motherhouse stood before us.

I had imagined it — I would be lying if I claimed otherwise. Every guildchild grew up on the stories: the motherhouse at Greyhollow Rise, where the founders had sworn their oaths, where the nine disciplines had been written, where Beatrix herself had been young and whole and burning with a gift the world had not yet taught her to fear. In the stories it was white stone and high windows and bells that could be heard in three counties.

What remained was a ribcage.

The roof was gone entirely — taken for timber in some harder decade, or burned, or simply surrendered to rot — so the sky came down into every room. Walls still stood to their second story in places, blackened lichen-eaten granite furred with moss, and ivy had done what ivy does, threading itself through window-frames until the windows looked like eyes half-closed in sleep. A great doorway gaped at the center of the façade, its oak doors long vanished, its lintel carved with words worn nearly smooth. I ran my fingers over them. *We Keep What Cannot Be Kept.*

"That was the motto," Beatrix said behind me. "Before my time, even. The first generation thought it was humility." Something moved across her face. "The last generation knew it was a warning."

We went in.

* * *

Inside, the ruin was quieter than ruins have any right to be. Sound behaved strangely there — our footsteps fell soft, the wind moved through the broken windows without whistling, and even the drip of water somewhere in the dark interior seemed muffled, apologetic, as though the building itself were trying not to wake us.

Beatrix walked the central hall like a woman crossing a graveyard she had helped dig. She touched nothing. She looked at everything. Once she stopped before a stretch of wall where the plaster had fallen away to reveal older plaster beneath, and on that older plaster, faint as breath on glass, someone had painted a circle of figures holding hands. Children, from the size of them. Their faces had faded to smudges, but their joined hands remained, a ring of small pale hooks.

"The dormitory wing was east," she said, mostly to herself. "The kitchens south. The hall of disciplines—" She turned, and I watched her orient herself by memory alone, pointing at a collapsed archway choked with rubble. "There. You could hear the bell from anywhere in the house. When it rang, you dropped whatever you held and came. Fire, raid, birth, death. It didn't matter. You came."

"Did it ring often?"

Her mouth curved, not quite a smile. "In my first year? Eleven times. I counted. I was very young and very serious about counting things." She looked up through the missing roof at a sky going grey and slow. "By my last year, it rang so seldom we'd forgotten the rope rotted through. That should have told us something. A guild that stops being summoned is a guild that has stopped believing anyone will come."

I wanted to ask about her last year — about the severance, about the war she never named, about the core sleeping in its wrappings in the pack on my back, which I had carried now for so many days it had become less an object than an organ, something warm and patient against my spine. But the questions I wanted were the ones she answered worst, and there was a fragility in her today that made me hold them. Instead I asked the practical thing.

"Is there anything here worth taking?"

That steadied her. Purpose always steadied her. "The archive cellar may have survived. Stone below ground keeps better than pride above it. And there are things buried in this house that the crown must never inventory." She was already moving, skirt hem gathered in one fist, picking her way over fallen roof-slates with the sureness of someone who had walked this floor ten thousand times and resented every change to it. "Come. Mind the third step. It lies."

"It lies?"

"All old houses lie, Sebastian. That's how they get you to stay."

* * *

The cellar stairs descended into cold that smelled of wet iron and centuries. Beatrix lit no lamp; she didn't need one, and neither, I was discovering, did I. My gift — still new, still frightening, still mine — had begun to do small unasked things, and one of them was this: in darkness, I saw a little. Not light, exactly. More like the memory of light, a grey bloom at the edge of things, strongest near old worked stone. Beatrix noticed me noticing and said nothing, but I felt her attention settle on me the way a hand settles on a shoulder.

The cellar had flooded and dried and flooded again across the decades. Silt lay over everything in a skin like grey velvet. Racks of shelving had rotted to skeletons; jars sat in rows along one wall, their contents reduced to stains and riddles. And at the far end, half-buried, stood an iron-banded chest that Beatrix approached the way pilgrims approach relics.

She knelt. She worked the lock with two thin tools from her sleeve, murmuring something under her breath — not a spell, I think, or not only. An apology, maybe. Or a greeting.

The lid came up with a groan, and the smell that rose out of it stopped my heart for a beat: paper, ink, wax, dust — but underneath all of it, impossibly, a thread of something green and living, like crushed leaves, like the valley itself.

"Ledgers," Beatrix said. "Oaths. The rolls of membership, from the founding onward." She lifted out a book thick as a paving stone, its cover scarred leather, its corners guarded in brass gone green. "Every soul who ever swore to Hollowmere is written in these pages. Every name. Every discipline they took up. Every date."

She set it on a dry shelf and opened it, and I leaned in beside her, and for a while neither of us spoke. The handwriting changed across the years the way a river changes course — formal copperplate giving way to slanted, hurried script, ink browning, margins accumulating small annotations in different hands: *died at Marrow Ford*, *left, took the coast road*, *severed, released with honor*. Whole generations lived and died in those pages. I found myself reading names like a man reading headstones.

Then Beatrix went still.

It was such a complete stillness — breath, hands, the small perpetual motion of her jaw — that I looked up sharply, thinking she'd heard something. But she wasn't listening. She was staring at a page deep in the book, at an entry dated forty-one years past, and her face had gone the color of the silt.

"Beatrix?"

She didn't answer. So I read what she was reading.

The entry was written in her hand — younger, fiercer, the letters cut deep enough to score the page beneath. It recorded an oath-taking, a name entered into the roll of the guild, a discipline assigned. And above all of it, the name itself, written in full, in ink that had dried four decades before I drew my first breath:

**Sebastian Okonkwo.**

Below it, in smaller script, the same young hand had added a line that was not formula, not ritual, nothing any other entry bore:

*Chosen. Not yet born. Let the house keep him until he comes.*

* * *

The cellar got very loud with silence.

I don't know how long I stood there. Long enough for the cold to find its way through my coat, long enough for the drip of water somewhere behind us to count itself into the hundreds. I read the entry again. And again. As though the words might rearrange themselves into sense if I gave them enough chances.

"My mother," I said finally, and my voice came out strange, flattened, like sound underwater. "My mother wasn't born until — she was a child when you wrote this. She hadn't met my father. They hadn't—"

"I know."

"You wrote my name." I turned to look at her, and the grey bloom of my gift made her face ghostly, hollow-eyed, a figure from one of the frescoes upstairs. "Forty-one years ago. Before my parents were married. Before my mother could walk, maybe. How could you possibly—"

"Sit down, Sebastian."

"I don't want to sit down."

"Then stand," she said quietly, "but listen." She closed the ledger with exaggerated care, both hands, as if it were a wounded bird. "And lower your voice. This house has ears older than either of us, and grief makes noise carry."

I did not sit. I also did not speak again, which she correctly interpreted as consent.

"There is a discipline in the guild," she began, "older than the nine we taught openly. We called it the Reading of Lines. Most called it nonsense. It looks at a person — truly looks, with the gift turned inward and outward at once — and sees not what they are but what they are *becoming*. A trajectory. A line drawn through a life." Her fingers traced the brass corner of the chest absently. "It fails constantly. It is guesswork dressed in ceremony, and I want you to understand that I know that. I was young, and arrogant, and I believed my sight was cleaner than it was."

"But you saw me."

"I saw a line." She exhaled slowly. "The night before the severance — the true severance, the one they did to me, not the one I let the histories believe — I performed the Reading one final time. On the guild itself. All of it, every member, every child in the dormitory, every oath in those books. I wanted to know whether we would survive what was coming." Her voice caught on something and she mastered it. "Most of the lines ended. Snapped off short, like cut thread. Some bent away, scattered, dimmed. And one line — one — ran straight through the ruin of everything I could see coming, straight through forty years of dark, and arrived." She looked at me then, fully, and there was no mentor in her face at all, only a woman who had been carrying a weight so long she had forgotten it was not part of her body. "It arrived at a boy in a valley, doing chores."

"So you wrote it down. Like a prophecy."

"Like a *debt*." The word cracked out of her harder than she intended; I saw her flinch at her own voice. "Do you understand what I am telling you? I did not choose you because you were special. I chose you because you were the only future I could see, and I was so afraid of losing that future that I committed it to paper and hid it in a hole in the ground and built my entire remaining life around reaching it." She laughed, briefly, terribly. "The ward. The valley. Forty years of standing still. Do you know why I never left, Sebastian? Why I let the guild shrink and the disciplines fade and the world forget us? Because the line ended at Hollowmere. Because you were *there*, and as long as I stayed near you, the future stayed real."

The silt-smell, the cold, the drip of water — all of it receded, and there was only the ledger between us, open again somehow, though I didn't remember opening it, my own name looking up at me like a stranger wearing my face.

"You used me," I said. "Before I existed. You decided what I was before my mother decided to have me."

"Yes."

"And you never told me."

"Not until you'd chosen it yourself." She stepped closer, and her voice dropped to something almost gentle, which was worse than anger. "If I had told you at twelve, you would have spent your life trying to become the line I saw. If I had told you last month, you would have spent the road doubting it. Either way, the choice stops being yours. I stole forty years of your freedom so that you could have this one moment of it. I have regretted it every day since the wardline broke, and I would do it again tonight."

"That's not mercy," I said. "That's just another kind of cage. You just made it out of time instead of stone."

For a moment she said nothing, and I watched the truth of it move through her — watched her try to find the argument that would dissolve what I'd said, and fail, and know she'd failed. When she spoke again her voice was rough.

"No. It isn't mercy. Nothing I have ever done for you has been entirely mercy. I am an old woman who gambled a child's life against a dying cause, and the child grew up, and now he gets to be angry at me, and I get to deserve it." She picked up the ledger and held it out to me. "Take it. It's yours more than mine now. The guild, the name, the debt — all of it. I only ask one thing."

"What."

"When you decide what to do with it — and you will have to decide, sooner than either of us wants — decide as the man who read that page. Not as the boy I wrote about him. He never existed. Only you did."

I took the ledger. It was heavier than a book should be. Everything true is.

* * *

We camped that night in what had been the refectory, under a sky full of moving cloud, with a small fire in the lee of a fallen wall. Beatrix slept — or lay with her eyes closed, which for her amounted to the same thing. I sat with the ledger on my knees and read it by firelight until the flames burned low.

I read the founders' names. I read the children from the fresco — I found them, five entries in a row, same year, same discipline, and beside the last one a marginal note in faded ink: *painted on the dormitory wall, all hands joined.* I read the deaths at Marrow Ford, the ones who left, the ones severed with honor. Somewhere past midnight I found my mother's family name — distant kin, a great-aunt entered sixty years ago, *released, married out, happy,* someone had added, and the word *happy* had been underlined twice, by two different hands, as if two people had needed to insist on it.

A guild was not a building. I had known that the way you know things without knowing them. A guild was a list of names that people kept choosing, generation after generation, long after the roof fell in. The house was a ribcage. The guild was the ledger. And my name had been in it since before I was anything at all.

The question Beatrix had left me with turned over and over: *what will you do with it.*

Because I already knew, in the place where I knew things I didn't want to know, that the question was not abstract. The core pressed warmth against my spine even now, patient as a held breath. Veyra was out there in the dark with my guildmates in chains, following us with the patience of weather. Odile knew too much. The rebels Odile served knew more. Every road from this ruin bent, eventually, toward Greyford and a bargain I did not yet have the courage to name.

I closed the ledger. In the coals, the fire settled with a sound like a sigh.

And then I heard it — faint, far off, down the valley slopes — the creak of harness leather and the muffled rhythm of many hooves moving at a walk. Deliberate. Unhurried. The sound of riders who believed there was nowhere left for their quarry to run.

I put my hand over Beatrix's mouth and shook her awake, and her eyes opened already knowing.
