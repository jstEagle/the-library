## 13. Taken

The river at Callow's Ford ran the color of old pewter that morning, and Veyra stood at its edge and watched it carry the kingdom's reflections away.

She had risen before her riders, as she always did, and walked down through the alders with her boots sinking into cold mud, because she did not trust maps to tell her what ground knew. The ford was narrow here, shingled with grey stones, and on either bank the reeds grew tall enough to hide a mounted man. A good place for fugitives to cross. A good place, therefore, for hunters to wait.

She had been hunting Beatrix of Hollowmere for thirty-one days.

Veyra kept count the way other women kept prayers — not out of devotion but out of discipline, because numbers did not flatter and numbers did not lie. Thirty-one days of cold trails and burned waystations, of informers who wept and informers who lied, of her riders growing superstitious in the way men did when a quarry eluded them too long. They had begun to say Beatrix could not be caught. They said it quietly, near fires, the way one spoke of ghosts.

Veyra did not believe in ghosts. She believed in habits.

*People are rivers,* her first commander had told her, back when she was fifteen and hungry and sharp enough to be worth teaching. *They cut their channels early and then they cannot help but follow them.*

Beatrix had lived forty years inside a hidden valley, ruling a guild through discipline and silence. Forty years of the same paths walked, the same rituals kept, the same caution layered over caution like river-silt. Such a woman did not become unpredictable when driven from her refuge. She became *more* herself. She would travel by night. She would avoid garrisons. She would move toward old guild ground, because a woman who had spent decades building something could not bear to leave it unblessed even in ruin — and if Veyra read her ledger right, there was much ruin behind them now, waystations burned by Veyra's own order, motherhouses gutted years ago. Beatrix was walking backward through her own history.

And she was carrying something the crown wanted very badly.

Veyra turned from the river. Mist lay along the water in long white ropes. Somewhere upstream, a heron cried once and was silent.

"Marshal." Corporal Dunmore crunched up the shingle behind her, breath steaming. "Riders are placed. Six at the far crossing, four in the willow stand, Hesk and the twins downstream where the banks pinch."

"And the boy?"

Dunmore hesitated. "No sign. If they come, it'll be both of them."

*If.* Veyra almost smiled. She had stopped dealing in ifs around the time she'd realized that fear made better predictions than hope. They would come. Beatrix would bring the boy across this ford within two days, because the road south bent here and every other crossing was watched or drowned, and because Beatrix believed — this was in her file, in the testimony of every guildmate who had ever spoken her name — that caution was a kind of armor.

Caution was not armor. Caution was a channel, and channels could be read.

"Build no fires," Veyra said. "No lamps after full dark. If she sees so much as a spark, she'll take the high moor instead, and I won't have her dying of exposure before I've had my conversation."

"You think she'd talk? The stories say—"

"I don't pay for stories, Corporal." Veyra started back up the bank, mud pulling at her heels. "Stories are what people tell you instead of what they know. Get to your post."

* * *

They came on the second night, under a moon the color of bone.

Veyra felt it before the signal came — a change in the quality of the dark, the way the willow stand went too quiet, the horses shifting with a low unease that no amount of training ever fully cured. Gifted animals hated proximity to the core; she had learned that in the first week, when her own mare had refused to graze anywhere near the confiscated relics cart. Whatever Beatrix carried in that wrapped bundle on her saddle, it made the world's nerves sing.

Then Hesk's whistle sounded from downstream — three notes, falling — and the night broke open.

Veyra had planned this moment so many times that watching it unfold was almost boring, and she distrusted herself for thinking so. The riders in the willows swept down onto the ford in a crescent, cutting off the southern bank. Lanterns flared along the ridge — unlit until now, doused and waiting — and their light fell across the water and found the fugitives mid-crossing.

Two figures. The boy ahead, riding hard, hunched low over something bundled against his chest. And behind him, turning back — *turning back*, Veyra noted, filing the gesture away the way she filed everything — Beatrix, wheeling her horse between the boy and the closing crescent of riders.

"She's holding the ford," Dunmore shouted, somewhere to her left. "The boy's making the far bank!"

"Let the boy run," Veyra said. Her voice carried flat and clear over the water. "The woman. No arrows. *No arrows.* I want her breathing."

Because that was the arithmetic she had worked out over thirty-one days and a hundred miles: the core would not be found by chasing the boy. The boy was a courier, and couriers could be replaced, redirected, killed. But Beatrix was the map. Beatrix was the only living soul who knew what the thing truly was, how it might be woken or quieted or moved, and the crown's scholars — pale men in Greyford who wrote memoranda about *containment vectors* — needed more than a bundle in a wrapping. They needed the woman who had stolen it forty years ago and kept it sleeping.

So: no arrows. Nets, bolas, rope. Old work. Honest work.

Beatrix fought the way Veyra had imagined she would, which was to say without any flourish whatsoever. No fire leapt from her hands — the woman's magic had been severed decades ago, burned out of her by whatever ritual the guild's histories refused to describe — and she did not seem to miss it. She used her horse like a weapon, shoulder-checking a rider into the shallows, laying her staff across another's wrist with a crack that echoed off the water. Two of Veyra's riders were unhorsed before the nets even flew.

But she was old. That was the whole of it, and Veyra felt no triumph in knowing it. Age was the only hunter that never lost a quarry, and it had been tracking Beatrix longer than Veyra had been alive.

The bola took Beatrix's horse at the legs on the third pass. The animal went down screaming in the shallows and Beatrix went with it, rolled, came up standing in thigh-deep water with her staff raised and her grey hair plastered to her skull, and for a moment — one held, silver-lit moment — she looked less like a fugitive than like a monument the river had decided to flood.

"Hold," Veyra called, and rode down into the water alone.

The ford went still except for the current. Upstream, faintly, she could hear hoofbeats fading north — the boy, running, exactly as designed. Beatrix's eyes followed the sound too. Veyra watched her listen to him vanish and saw nothing in her face she could read, which unsettled her more than any resistance would have.

"Marshal Veyra," Beatrix said. Water dripped from the end of her staff. Her voice was dry as paper despite everything. "You've grown into your reputation."

"And you've shrunk out of yours." Veyra dismounted in the shallows, deliberately slow, deliberately close. This was the part of the work no one wrote ballads about: the conversation. "Forty years you sat in that valley and let the world believe you were dead. I could almost respect it. Then the crown starts asking questions and suddenly you're running roads you haven't walked since before I was born, carrying—" she nodded at the empty saddle, at the absence, "—whatever it is you stole from the spire before it fell."

Something crossed Beatrix's face. Small. Quick. Gone.

"Take her," Veyra said, and her riders closed in with ropes and practiced hands, and Beatrix did not fight anymore. That was the second thing Veyra filed away.

* * *

They made camp an hour south of the ford, in a hollow ringed with blackthorn, because Veyra would not risk a garrison town and its gossip. Beatrix sat bound to a supply cart wheel, wrists lashed behind her, and watched the riders work with the mild attention of a woman supervising chores she had done better herself.

Veyra let the camp settle. She ate. She checked the pickets. She waited until the fire had burned down to coals and the only sounds were the horses and the wind combing the blackthorn, and then she crossed the hollow and sat down on her heels in front of her prisoner, close enough to smell the river still clinging to her clothes.

"You'll want to sleep," Veyra said. "I'll keep this brief."

"I imagine you'll keep it however you like."

"The pardon is real. I carry the writ myself — sealed, in my kit. Renunciation of all claims, formal surrender of the artifact, cooperation in its study, and in exchange: freedom, protection, a stipend. The crown does not want to hang you, Beatrix. The crown wants to *close a file*. You're the last name on a list forty years old. Help me cross you off and you die old, somewhere warm."

Beatrix looked at her. In the coal-light her eyes were the flat grey of the river at dawn.

"You believe that," she said. "The pardon. You actually believe they'll honor it."

"I believe the writ is signed."

"The writ is signed by a chancellor who'll be poisoned within the year, by factions who think a severed witch is a loose thread." Beatrix shifted against the wheel; the ropes creaked. "You hunt people for a living, Marshal. Have you never once wondered who hunts the hunters' promises?"

It was a good line. Veyra had heard better ones from worse prisoners and watched them hang anyway. What troubled her was not the line but the delivery — flat, unhurried, a woman reciting facts rather than fighting for her life. Beatrix had not asked about the boy. Had not asked about the guildmates rotting in Greyford cells, whose release Veyra had fully expected to be bartered for by now. Had not asked anything at all.

A prisoner who wanted to live negotiated. This one was merely waiting.

"When we took you at the ford," Veyra said slowly, "you sent the boy north and stayed. Every instinct, every discipline your guild ever taught, says the opposite. Split the cargo from the keeper. Yet you turned your horse *toward* us."

"Sentiment," Beatrix said. "Old women are permitted some."

"You fought well enough to cost me two riders and then stopped. You haven't asked where we're taking you. Haven't asked about your people in Greyford. Haven't tried to bargain, though you must know I hold every card at this table." Veyra leaned closer. Coal-light moved across the older woman's face and found, at last, something beneath the calm — not fear, not defiance. Something that looked horribly, impossibly like relief. "Thirty-one days I chased you. I built a model of you out of habit and history, and the model says none of this should be happening. So I'm going to ask you plainly, and I want you to understand that I will know a lie."

She held the old woman's gaze.

"Do you *want* to be caught?"

The wind moved through the blackthorn. One of the horses stamped. Beatrix smiled — a small, tired, terrible smile, the smile of a woman setting down a weight she had carried so long her shoulders had grown around it.

"You give me too much credit, Marshal," she said. "I'm an old woman on a cold road. Anyone would be glad of the rest."

And it was a perfect answer, reasonable and human and exactly what an exhausted fugitive would say, and it convinced Veyra of precisely nothing, because it was also *exactly* the answer someone would give who did not want the truth known. The channel and the stone. She could no longer tell which one Beatrix was.

"Here is what I think," Veyra said, rising, brushing ash from her knees. "I think you let him go north because north is where you want the boy to go. I think you stayed because staying served you. And I think that whatever is in that bundle he's carrying, you have already decided what happens to it — and my pardon, my writ, my whole crowned and sealed authority is a piece of your board that you let me pick up." She turned to the dark. "Hesk. Watch rotation. Double on her."

As she walked back toward her tent, Beatrix's voice followed her, soft across the embers:

"Marshal. One question of mine, since you've had thirty-one of yours."

Veyra stopped. Did not turn.

"The boy," Beatrix said. "When your scholars ask you what he is — what do you intend to tell them he is?"

Veyra stood a long moment in the dark, listening to the river she could no longer see, and understood, with the cold certainty that had made her career, that she had just been handed something. Not information. An instruction. The old woman was telling her, in the only language a prisoner had left, where the true hunt was going.

North. After the boy. After the core.

And Beatrix — Veyra was sure of it now, sure the way she was sure of ledgers and rivers — Beatrix wanted her to follow.

* * *

In the grey hour before dawn, Veyra woke her riders, and gave the order that inverted thirty-one days of pursuit.

"Saddle up. We ride north."

Behind her, bound to the cart wheel, Beatrix of Hollowmere closed her eyes — and smiled.
