## 14. Alone with the Heart of the World

The river took Beatrix's scream and gave nothing back.

Sebastian ran until running stopped being a choice and became a thing his body did without him, the way a heart beats or a wound bleeds. The core rode against his chest in its sling of oiled leather, wrapped three times around with wool, and even through all of that he could feel it — not warm, exactly, but *aware*, the way you feel a stranger's eyes between your shoulder blades on an empty road.

He did not stop until the stars had wheeled a hand's width across the sky. Then his legs simply quit, folding him into the roots of an alder at the edge of a meadow gone silver with frost, and he sat there shaking, listening to the dark for hoofbeats that didn't come.

*She told me to run,* he thought, and then, because thinking it made it real: *I ran.*

* * *

Morning came gray and thin, and with it came the arithmetic he had been refusing all night.

He had the core. Veyra had Beatrix, and Tam, and little Wren from the ferry families, and however many others her riders had swept up in the weeks since Hollowmere. The trade was simple. Any merchant in any market would have made it without blinking: one stone for five lives. One old woman for everyone she'd spent forty years protecting.

Except.

Except that Beatrix had spent forty years protecting no one *from* the core by keeping it buried, and every mile Sebastian carried it toward Greyford was a mile closer to it falling into hands that would use it. He remembered what Odile had said in the smugglers' market, turning her cup of wine without drinking: *There are three kinds of people looking for that thing, boy. Those who want to own it, those who want to destroy it, and those who haven't decided yet.* He had laughed then. He wasn't laughing now.

And except that when he closed his eyes he could still see the way Beatrix had looked at the river crossing — not afraid for herself. Afraid of herself. She had shoved the sling into his arms and turned to face the riders with nothing but her severed, empty hands raised, and there had been something in her face he hadn't understood until this cold morning, sitting alone among the frost:

Relief.

She had wanted, just for one moment, to stop carrying it.

* * *

He built no fire. He ate the last of the hard cheese and half the black bread and let the cold settle into him like a tenant. Around noon, unable to bear the silence any longer, he unwrapped the sling's first fold — just one, just enough — and looked at the thing that had broken his whole world open.

It did not look like much. A heart-sized mass of spire-glass, smoky and shot through with veins of pale gold, like sunlight trapped in dirty ice. It had been beautiful once, Beatrix said, before she'd cracked it pulling it free of the spire; now one whole face of it was crazed with hairline fractures, and if you tilted it against the light the fractures bloomed like white flowers.

But it was not light that moved inside it now.

It was his own reflection — and beneath the reflection, faint as a fish under winter ice, something else. Something turning over. Looking back.

"Stop that," Sebastian said aloud. His voice sounded absurd in the empty meadow. "You're a rock."

The warmth against his chest disagreed.

* * *

He had felt the gift wake in him only days ago, there at the wardline, when the hunter's blade had been coming down and everything in Sebastian had reached out *without asking permission* and caught the man's arm in a grip of pure pressure, like the deep sea closing a fist. Since then it had been a low hum under his skin, a second pulse. Beatrix had taught him the disciplines to quiet it — the counting breaths, the naming of sensations, the old guild litany: *the gift is weather; you are the valley*. But the disciplines were built for small gifts, hearth-gifts, the kind that warmed soup and mended fences and found lost sheep. What lived in Sebastian now, so close to the core, was not weather.

It was a sea. And seas do not care where valleys are.

By late afternoon he had given up on meditation and taken to walking instead, because walking was also a discipline, and because standing still meant thinking about Beatrix in Veyra's camp. He tried not to imagine it. He imagined it anyway: the clean white tent, the map table, Veyra's unhurried voice — she had never once shouted, the captured ones said later, never once needed to — asking questions with the patience of a woman who knew the answer was already in the room.

They would treat her gently at first. She was old, and she was famous, and Veyra believed in propriety the way other people believed in gods. But gentleness has a shelf life. Sooner or later the questions would change shape. Sooner or later there would be a table, and straps, and a man whose job it was to be patient in worse ways.

*Trade it,* said the part of him that loved her. *One stone. Five lives. Walk away. Let the kings and rebels fight over the ashes of the world; you saved your people. That's enough. That's always been enough.*

And then the other voice, quieter, which was Beatrix's own, from the drowned chapel, wringing river water out of her gray braid while the rescued child sobbed in her lap: *The gift is not a blessing, Sebastian. It is a responsibility. That is why they fear it — not because it is strange, but because it is heavy, and most people would rather be light.*

If he traded the core, someone would use it. Veyra would feed it to the furnaces and end magic forever, which sounded like peace until you remembered that the crown had burned two villages last month to catch four fugitives, and that a kingdom with no magic left to check it would be very light indeed, and would press down on everyone beneath it forever.

If he kept the core, Beatrix died. Slowly, probably. Politely. Which was somehow worse.

And if he used it —

He stopped walking.

The meadow had ended somewhere behind him; he was standing at the lip of a ravine where a stream cut down through black slate, and the water made a sound like whispering, and the core against his chest had gone from warm to hot, from hot to a bright insistent pressure, like a hand pressing flat against his sternum from the inside.

*I could end it here,* he thought, and the thought did not feel entirely like his own, and that frightened him more than anything Veyra had ever done. *I could finish what she started. Pour myself into it the way I did at the wardline, crack it all the way open, scatter it so fine that no one — not crowns, not rebels, not gods — could ever gather it again.*

It was a good thought. It was a *clean* thought. It was the kind of thought that arrives already dressed in virtue so you don't notice how much you want it.

Because underneath the virtue was the other thing. The true thing. That when he touched the core, when he let the sea in him lean toward the sea in it, he felt *vast*. He felt the way the valley looked from the high pass at dawn. He felt like the oldest version of himself, the one before wards and chores and smallness, and some spoiled and starving corner of his heart had tasted that vastness and wanted it again the way the drowned want air.

*That's what it does,* he told himself. *That's how it got a war named after it. It doesn't force anyone. It just shows you the biggest room in your house and asks why you've been sleeping in the hall.*

* * *

Night came down again, clear and bitter. He made a hollow in the roots of a fallen pine, wrapped himself in the single blanket, and put the core beside his head where he could watch it, because leaving it farther away felt like leaving a door unbarred.

He dreamed, or half-dreamed, or did not dream at all.

A woman stood in the frost, made of the same smoky glass and gold-veined shadow as the core, and she was not a woman, and she was not standing, and none of the words for her were right, but *woman* was the closest his mind could come and so it gave him one. She had his mother's hands. She had the spire's proportions. When she spoke, the voice came from everywhere the gift lived — which, he realized with a lurch, now included him.

**You carry me well,** she said, or seemed to say. **Better than the old one. She carries me like grief. You carry me like a lantern.**

"You're not real," Sebastian said, in the dream, in the frost. "Beatrix said you're not alive. She said it wants nothing, it's only power, it takes the shape of whatever's nearest—"

**And what is nearest?**

He couldn't answer. In the dream his mouth was full of river water.

**She is right, in her way,** the woman said, and her voice softened, which was worse than if it had thundered. **I want nothing. I am want itself. I am the shape of every wanting poured into stone for nine hundred years — kings who wanted to live forever, priests who wanted heaven's ear, farmers who wanted one more season. All of it pooled in me. I do not tempt, Sebastian Okonkwo. I only hold up the mirror and let you see how large your hunger already was.**

"I don't want anything," he said. "I want Beatrix back. That's all. I'd give you up in a heartbeat."

**Yes,** she agreed, gently, terribly. **For her, you would give me to the crown, to the rebels, to the fires. And then you would spend the rest of your life watching the roads, waiting for the smoke. Because your wanting doesn't end where your love ends. It ends where your courage begins. And you have not yet learned the difference.**

The frost-woman leaned close, and inside her glass-dark face he saw the fractures bloom like white flowers, and beyond the fractures, far down, a single point of gold light, steady as a lamp in a window.

**They will meet at Greyford,** she said. **All of them. The huntress with her clean sad certainty. The rebels with their righteous fire. Your broker with her ledger of everyone's price. They are all coming to the same crossing, and each of them believes they are the river and everyone else is the rain. You cannot save her and keep me safe and stay your own hand. Choose which mercy you can live inside.**

"Stop," he whispered.

**I am not speaking,** said the woman made of everything anyone ever wanted. **I have never once spoken. Listen to yourself. It is finally time.**

* * *

He woke with frost on his eyelashes and dawn bleeding gray at the treeline and the taste of river water in his mouth, though there was no river anywhere near him. The core lay in its leather cradle exactly where he had left it, inert, smoky, cracked, ordinary-looking as a hearthstone. Whatever had spoken — it, or him, or the place where the two of them overlapped like transparencies — was silent now.

But he found that he was not afraid anymore. That was the strangest part. Somewhere in the night the fear had finished its work and composted down into something else, something denser and more useful, and when he sat up and pressed his palms flat against the frozen ground he could feel his gift lying quiet under his skin like a dog that has finally been given a job.

He knew what he was going to do.

Not the shattering — not yet. Not alone, not blind, not with Beatrix's knowledge locked in Veyra's camp and his own barely a week old. To break a thing nine hundred years of wanting had built, he would need more than courage. He would need her. Everything she remembered, everything she'd buried, everything she'd never told him about what the spire had cost the first time.

So he would walk into the lion's mouth and offer the lion exactly what it wanted.

Veyra was a woman of order. She kept her word — the guild archives said so, in entries decades old, in handwriting that had no reason to lie. If he offered her the core at Greyford in exchange for Beatrix and the guildmates, freed and unharmed, she would agree, because agreeing cost her nothing until the moment of exchange, and hunters like Veyra never believed the trap could close on them.

And at the moment of exchange — with Beatrix's hand in his, with the guildmates breathing free air, with every crossbow in the camp trained on his back — he would pour everything he had into the cracked stone and finish it. Break it so fine, so wide, so utterly beyond gathering that no crown and no rebellion would ever hold it again.

It might kill him. The wardline had nearly killed him, and that had been one hunter's arm and a few feet of force. This would be a war's worth of stored hunger, nine centuries deep.

It might kill Beatrix too, if she understood what he meant to do and threw herself in the way, and she would understand, and she would throw herself in the way, and so he would have to tell her — or not tell her — and either of those choices broke something he couldn't name yet.

*You cannot save her and keep me safe and stay your own hand.*

"No," Sebastian said aloud, to the core, to the frost, to the woman who was only ever his own reflection in dirty ice. "Watch me."

He packed the blanket, shouldered the sling, and turned south, toward Greyford, where three roads and three armies and everyone he loved were converging like spokes toward a hub.

Behind him, in the meadow, the frost began, very quietly, to melt — every crystal of it, all at once, though the sun had not yet cleared the hills.
