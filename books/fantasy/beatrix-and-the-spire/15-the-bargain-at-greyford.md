## 15. The Bargain at Greyford

The river at Greyford ran the color of old pewter, and it carried the smell of ash downstream from whatever the crown had burned most recently. Sebastian stood on the near bank with his hood up and his heart going like a trapped bird against his ribs, and he watched the far side of the ford where Veyra's camp had grown up overnight like a boil on the land.

There were perhaps forty riders. Picket fires in a loose ring, banners of the crown hanging limp in the wet air — a stag pierced by an arrow, stitched in thread that had once been gold. Wagons. A cookfire's smoke bending southward. And at the center, a long tent that could only be hers, its flap tied back so that anyone inside could see the crossing. She wanted to be seen. That was the message. *Come if you dare.*

Beside him, in the reeds where the water talked softly over stones, the core sat wrapped in oilcloth and Beatrix's old traveling cloak. Even through the layers he could feel it — not warmth exactly, but a kind of attention, the way a sleeping animal is aware of you without opening its eyes. It had been aware of him for three days now. Ever since the drowned chapel. Ever since it spoke.

*Use me,* it had said, or something like saying. He still did not know if the voice had been real or only his own gift, young and frightened, talking to itself in the dark of the flooded crypt. It didn't matter anymore which it was. He knew what it wanted, and knowing that was how he knew what he had to do.

He knelt in the mud and put both hands on the bundle.

"I'm not bringing you to her," he said quietly. "I'm bringing you to her *door*. There's a difference."

The core said nothing, but the attention sharpened, and for one cold second he felt it consider him — weigh him, the way Odile had weighed the coin he'd offered her, finding the edges worn and wondering what was underneath.

* * *

The night before, in the loft of a barn they'd paid a widow to sleep in, he had laid out the plan to no one, because there was no one left to lay it out to. Beatrix was across the river. The guildmates were in a cage-wagon he'd glimpsed through Odile's spyglass, six of them, hollow-eyed, Maghda's silver hair matted flat. Odile herself had vanished two days after taking her decoy — and Sebastian understood now, with the sour clarity of hindsight, that the broker had known all along the false core was false, and had let them walk away anyway because a debt owed was worth more than a prize collected. Or because she was already selling their route to someone else. With Odile you never learned which lie was the true one until it closed around your throat.

So: no one. Just himself, and the plan, spoken aloud to the dark rafters where pigeons muttered in their sleep.

Walk into the camp. Openly. No weapon, no hidden gift — she would have wards, or men who could smell a smothered gift like smoke in wool. Walk in as what he was: a boy from a valley that no longer existed, carrying the thing her whole hunt existed to find. Offer a trade. Beatrix and the guildmates, freed and unharmed and given horses and a day's head start, for the spire-heart entire.

And then, when she reached out her hand—

He made himself think it plainly, because a plan you couldn't bear to think clearly was a plan that would kill you. *Then I pour everything I am into it, and I break it.*

Beatrix had told him enough, in the drowned chapel and after, for him to know what that meant. The core was a vessel, and vessels held what was poured into them. Forty years of her severed magic had soaked into it like rain into chalk. His own gift, raw and new and stupidly large, would be another flood. And the shell — the shell was old. Older than the war. Older than the guild. It had endured because nothing had ever asked it to hold more than it could carry.

He intended to ask.

What happened then, he could not know. Beatrix thought shattering it might unmake magic everywhere, burn it out of the world like her own gift had been burned out of her — a silence instead of a song. But he had felt the core dreaming under his hands, and he believed something else. He believed it wouldn't destroy the gift. It would *loosen* it. Cut it loose from stone and law and spire and crown, scatter it the way a struck hearth scatters sparks, into everyone standing near. Into hunters. Into prisoners. Into the wind, maybe, into whoever breathed it.

Or it would kill him, and level Greyford, and start the war again with its first act.

"Yes," he told the pigeons. "That's the whole plan." They looked at him without sympathy and went back to sleep.

* * *

The ford was knee-deep and fast. The water gripped his calves like hands, trying politely to turn him back, and he let the cold climb up through his boots and steady him. Halfway across, a sentry saw him and shouted, and by the time he reached the far bank there were six riders waiting in a crescent, crossbows levelled, and a sergeant with a waxed mustache looking at him the way farmers look at a fox found in the henhouse.

"Name," the sergeant said.

"Sebastian Okonkwo. Of Hollowmere." Saying it hurt. Saying it aloud, to these people, was like handing over something dead to be weighed. "I'm expected."

"No one's expected."

"Chief Hunter Veyra has been hunting my guild since before I crossed the wardline." He kept his hands open at his sides, empty, visible. Under his sleeve, his gift coiled tight and small, wound down to a spark behind his breastbone — Beatrix's disciplines, the fourth form, *the banked fire*. "Tell her the boy from the valley is here, and he's brought what she wants. Tell her that exactly, word for word. She'll understand."

The sergeant studied him a long moment. Then he turned his horse and cantered toward the long tent, and Sebastian stood in the churned mud with crossbow bolts trained on his chest and discovered that some part of him — some deep, valley-bred part — was almost calm. This, at least, was simple. You walked into fear rather than away from it. Everything else since the wardline broke had been running, hiding, drowning, burning. This was the first thing he had chosen with his eyes open.

The sergeant returned at a trot and jerked his chin. "She'll see you. Weapons stay here, and if you've got anything buried anywhere clever, she'll know."

"She usually does," Sebastian said, and walked between the fires.

* * *

Her tent smelled of cedar and ink. Maps hung on every wall — the whole kingdom rendered in a clerk's careful hand, and over it, in red, a web of lines and circles that took him a moment to read. Patrol routes. And pinned sites. Waystations. Motherhouses. Chapels. Every mark in red was a place the guild had been, and nearly every one had been struck through with a single clean line.

His heritage, crossed off like sums.

Veyra sat at a folding table with her back to the entrance, writing. She finished her sentence before she turned — deliberately, he understood; she wanted him to watch her be unhurried. When she rose and faced him he saw what he hadn't been able to see through Odile's spyglass: a woman of maybe fifty, gray at the temples, with a broad, plain, honest face, the kind you'd trust with your purse. There was no cruelty in it. That was the worst discovery he'd made in weeks.

"The boy from the valley," she said. Her voice was low and even, a voice built for delivering verdicts. "You don't look like much."

"I get that a lot lately."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment that a joke had occurred. "Where is it?"

"On the other bank. Where I can see this tent from, and nowhere closer." He nodded at the stool across the table from her. "May I?"

"You may stand."

"Then I'll stand." He planted his feet and made himself look at the maps instead of her face, because the maps were easier. "Here are my terms. You have Beatrix. You have six of my guildmates in a wagon with bars on it. You release all seven — unharmed, no marks, no pursuit, horses and a day's lead — and I give you the spire-heart. Whole. Unopened. Yours to carry back to the capital yourself, which I imagine is what your orders actually say, beneath all this—" he gestured at the red web — "burning."

"And if I simply take you, take your friends' testimony, and follow you to the river?"

"You could try. But I can drown it in eight feet of moving water faster than your best swimmer dives, and I promise you I will." He let a little of the truth come up through the banked fire, just enough that his fingertips prickled, and saw her eyes flick to his hands and narrow. Good. Let her believe the threat. The threat was true; it just wasn't the plan. "You've hunted us for months, Chief Hunter. You know we keep our word. Beatrix kept yours, forty years ago, when she could have burned the kingdom down and didn't. Honor the trade and it ends today, clean, and you go home having done your duty."

For a while she said nothing. She came around the table slowly and stopped an arm's length from him, and studied him the way he'd seen Beatrix study weather coming over the ridge.

"Do you know why I do this?" she asked.

"Because you hate us."

"No." And she said it with such quiet certainty that he believed her. "Because I was at Kestrel's Ford when I was nineteen. Before your guild hid itself away, before your valley forgot the world. I saw what gifted men did to a village that sheltered the wrong refugee. Three hundred dead, and the ones who did it wept while they worked. They *wept*, boy, and the power didn't care. Power without law is just fire that thinks it's sorry." She looked past him, at the tent wall, at something forty years gone. "Your Beatrix understood that once. It's why she cut herself off. It's why she buried that thing in your valley and starved the world of it. She agreed with me her whole life. She just couldn't finish the work, so I am finishing it for her."

Sebastian's throat was dry. Because the terrible thing — the thing he would carry however this ended — was that he could see her case. He had nearly drowned a child with his ungoverned gift in a flooded chapel. He knew exactly what fire-that-thinks-it's-sorry looked like from the inside.

"Maybe," he said. "But you're not extinguishing power, Chief Hunter. You're gathering it. One place. One box. One throne room. That's not peace. That's the same war, waiting for a worse master."

"Then give it to me and let me prove otherwise," she said simply. "Terms accepted. Seven released at the exchange, noon tomorrow, mid-ford. You hand over the heart, my people verify it, and your people ride east and never use the word *guild* again. If any part of this is a trap—"

"If any part of this is a trap," Sebastian said, "you'll have earned it. You burned our motherhouse last month. I watched the smoke from three ridges away."

Something crossed her face then — quickly, but he saw it, and filed it away among the day's small surprises: it had looked like regret. "Noon," she said. "Bring it wrapped. Bring yourself unarmed. And boy—" as he turned to go — "if you're lying to me, I will find you afterward, wherever the world hides you. I have never lost a quarry."

"I know," Sebastian said. "Someone sold me that warning for the price of a passage. It was worth every coin."

* * *

They let him walk back across the ford without shooting him, though the crossbows tracked him the whole way, six small dark mouths breathing at his back. He did not look at the cage-wagon, parked beyond the picket line. He had seen enough of it in his mind already; he didn't need Maghda's face through the bars to make the next twelve hours heavier than they already were.

On the near bank he unwrapped the core just enough to lay his palm against it, and the attention underneath the oilcloth turned to him like a sunflower to noon.

"They said yes," he whispered. "Tomorrow, at the middle of the river. That's where it happens. Water on every side. Nobody close enough to catch what falls unless it falls on them."

The core pulsed, once, slow, and he felt — or imagined, and had stopped caring which — a vast satisfaction move through it, deep as a tide. It had waited forty years in the dark under a hill. What was one more night?

He wrapped it again, shouldered the bundle, and climbed the far ridge until Greyford's fires were embers below him. Then he sat with his back against a rowan tree, in the smell of its leaves, and did the last piece of the work, the piece no discipline had ever covered.

He thought about Beatrix's face if he failed. He thought about Maghda teaching him letters with a stick in the dirt. He thought about the child in the flooded chapel, bloated and blue, gasping back to life under Beatrix's ruined hands, and about Veyra's plain honest face saying *power without law is just fire that thinks it's sorry,* and about how both of those things were true at once, and how there was no map in any of Veyra's ledgers for the country he was about to enter.

Every choice left alive to him was terrible. So he chose the terrible one that belonged to everyone.

"I'm sorry in advance," he told the core, and the rowan, the river, the distant fires, and no one in particular. "Whatever happens. I'm sorry, and I'm doing it anyway."

Below him, the camp settled into night. A change of watch called out the hours. In the long tent, a lamp burned late — Veyra, he supposed, writing dispatches, drawing one more clean red line through one more name.

At noon tomorrow he would walk into the river holding the future of magic like a hostage at his breast, and every armed soul on both banks would believe he had come to bargain it away.

Only the river, and the core, and the boy from the valley that forgot would know he had come to break it.

He closed his eyes and began the fourth form, banking his fire for the longest night of his life — and somewhere downstream, faint on the wind, came the sound he'd been dreading and listening for all evening: hooves. Many of them. Riding hard, and not wearing crown steel.

Odile had sold the exchange to somebody after all.
