## 17. Greyford

Greyford earned its name twice over: once for the grey stone of its bridge, older than any kingdom that claimed it, and once for the fog that came off the river at dawn and swallowed everything — walls, roofs, the gallows in the market square — until the town seemed less a place than a rumor of one.

Sebastian stood on the bridge with the core wrapped in oilcloth against his chest and watched the fog eat the far bank.

It was colder than it had any right to be in high summer. The river ran dark beneath him, thick and slow, carrying leaves and a broken cartwheel and once — he told himself he'd imagined it — something pale and long that turned in the current like a sleeping body. The stones under his boots were slick with moss. Somewhere behind him, Beatrix's voice still lived in his memory from three nights ago, when they'd parted on the road: *If anything goes wrong, you run. You don't bargain, you don't explain, you run until your lungs bleed and then you keep running.*

He had promised her he would. He had lied to her while promising, which he suspected she knew, which was perhaps why she had let him go.

The core pulsed faintly through the oilcloth, slow as a second heartbeat, and Sebastian had learned over the long weeks not to pretend anymore that it wasn't alive. It dreamed against his ribs. It wanted things. And this morning, walking into Veyra's camp with it strapped to his chest like a bomb or a bride-price, he could feel its wanting sharpen to a point.

*Come closer,* it seemed to say. *You brought me all this way. Let them look at me.*

"Not yet," he whispered, and hated himself for answering.

* * *

Veyra's camp occupied the ruined mill on the north bank — a squat stone hulk with a collapsed roof and a courtyard where forty years of ivy had been cut back to bare stems, as if even the plants had been interrogated. Torches burned in iron brackets despite the daylight, because Veyra, Odile had said, liked to see everything twice: once by sun and once by fire.

Sebastian counted the hunters as he crossed the yard. Twelve visible. Probably eight more in the mill's shadowed doorways. They wore no livery — crown work had gone quiet since the proclamation, deniable as smoke — but their gear betrayed them: matched steel, matched boots, the oiled silence of people who moved together without needing to speak.

And in the center of the courtyard, kneeling on wet flagstones with her wrists bound before her, sat Beatrix.

She looked smaller than he had ever seen her. Not broken — her spine was straight as a lance, her chin lifted, her eyes tracking him across the yard with an expression he couldn't read at all — but diminished, the way a great house looks diminished when its windows are boarded. Her white hair had come loose from its braid. There was blood on her temple, dried brown, and her left hand hung at an angle that made his stomach clench.

Behind her, roped together in a line against the mill wall, were the others. Maren. Old Tobbe. The twins, Ilse and Roderick, sixteen and trying so hard to be brave that their chins trembled in unison. Four of the seven taken from Hollowmere. He didn't let himself think about the other three.

"Sebastian Okonkwo." Veyra stepped out of the mill doorway, and the yard went very quiet. She was not what the word *hunter* had prepared him for. She was fifty, maybe, broad-shouldered, with grey threading her dark braid and a face like a well-used map — every line earned, nothing decorative. She carried no drawn weapon. She didn't need one; there were twelve around her. "You came."

"You said if I came alone with the core, everyone walks."

"I said we would discuss terms." She gestured, and two hunters took up positions flanking Beatrix. "Show me."

His hands shook as he unwrapped the top fold of the oilcloth. Just enough. A sliver of the core showed between the cloth: a facet of something that was not quite crystal and not quite glass, deep amber shot through with veins of light that moved like slow lightning. Even half-hidden, it lit the fog around him from within, turning his own shadow gold.

He heard the hunters react — breath drawn in through teeth, someone's gauntlet creaking on a sword hilt. Even men trained to hate magic could not look at the heart of it and feel nothing.

Veyra looked longest. And what crossed her face was not hunger. It was grief.

"So it's true," she said softly. "Forty years we were told it was destroyed. Forty years of burning villages on a rumor." She looked up at him. "Do you know what this thing did, boy? Do you have any idea? Or did the old woman fill your head with guild stories?"

"She told me about the war."

"The war." Veyra almost smiled. "Is that what she calls it. I call it the decade my grandmother's whole valley stopped existing. One morning there were nine hundred people there. By winter there was ash and a hole in the world where the weather came from." She stepped closer, and her voice dropped, and — this was the terrible thing, the thing he would remember — she sounded reasonable. "I don't want to hurt anyone here. I have burned enough. Give it to me, and it goes into the deepest vault in the capital, and no one — rebel, crown, or child — ever touches it again. Your people walk free. That is the whole of my ambition. Peace. Boring, permanent peace."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I take it, and I stop being polite." She said it without heat, the way one remarks on weather. "But I don't think you'll say no. You walked into my camp. People who mean to refuse don't do that."

*No,* Sebastian thought, *they don't. They send a decoy.*

Because the thing wrapped against his chest was not the core.

Beatrix had built it in the drowned chapel, three nights of work with river clay and bone-ash and a sliver of true core-shaving she'd kept hidden in her boot heel for forty years, waiting. It looked right. It felt right — warm, pulsing, dreaming. But it was hollow, a lantern with no flame, and somewhere in the reeds along the south bank, wrapped in real oilcloth, the actual heart of the spire waited with Odile's smuggler cousin, who thought he was guarding a shipment of brandy.

That had been the plan. Hand over the decoy, collect the prisoners, walk away, and let Veyra discover the lie a hundred miles from anywhere.

That had been the plan before dawn, when the fog on the south bank had started moving against the wind.

* * *

It began, as bad things often do, with a bird.

A marsh bird burst shrieking out of the reeds across the river, and then another, and then the reeds themselves shivered in a long line, like something large wading through them, and one of the hunters by the waterline said, "Captain—"

The first arrow took him in the throat.

After that, the world tore open.

They came out of the fog on both banks at once — rebels, Odile's sold information made flesh, thirty or more of the gifted wearing ash-grey scarves over their faces, and they did not come quietly. Fire leapt from a man's open palm and caught the mill's dry ivy. The river itself rose in a standing wave and broke over the bridge, and Sebastian went down on his knees on the slick stones, clutching the decoy to his chest as water like a living thing tried to pry his arms apart. Someone screamed a word in the old tongue and the word became a blade of white light. A hunter answered with a crossbow, and the bolt caught the light-blade's maker through the shoulder mid-spell, and both of them fell, and neither got up quickly.

*Run.* Beatrix's voice in his memory. *You don't bargain, you don't explain.*

But Beatrix was on her knees twenty feet away with a hunter's hand fisted in her collar, hauling her upright as a shield, and the twins were screaming against the mill wall, and Sebastian discovered that his body had already decided what his mind hadn't. He was moving. Low, fast, along the bridge rail, toward the fight, his gift rising in his chest like floodwater behind a failing dam.

He had learned control in the drowned chapel. He had learned discipline from Beatrix, the old guild disciplines, breath and shape and surrender. None of it mattered now. What mattered was that a hunter had a knife at Beatrix's throat and the knife-hand belonged to a person, and persons, he was learning, were just doors, and his gift knew how to knock.

He hit the hunter with a shove of force that lifted the man off his feet and threw him ten feet into the courtyard wall. The crack of it turned heads. For one suspended moment, the battle paused around him — crown and rebel alike staring at the boy standing over the fallen hunter with golden light bleeding off his shoulders like heat-shimmer.

Then everything happened at once.

A rebel fire-caster turned her flame on him — *the core-bearer, take the core-bearer* — and Sebastian threw up a shield that had never existed a month ago and felt the fire break across it like surf. The impact drove him back a step, two, his heels skidding on wet stone. Through the shimmer he saw Veyra shouting orders, cool as a surgeon, falling her hunters into a firing line; saw the rebels' wave falter and split around the volley; saw Tobbe wrench free of his ropes with bleeding wrists and put himself bodily between the twins and an arrow.

And he saw Beatrix.

She had dropped to her knees again — no. Not dropped. She had *chosen* the ground, palms flat against the flagstones, head bowed, lips moving. And Sebastian felt it before he understood it: a frequency underneath the battle's noise, old and deep and wrong, like a bell struck in a drowned ship. Beatrix's severed magic. The scrap the guild's severing ritual had missed, buried in her for forty years like shrapnel, too small to cast a spell, too stubborn to die.

She was spending it. All of it. Every last coin of a lifetime's hoard.

Light came up out of the stones around her in a ring — not gold like the core, but white, hard, cold as starlight — and everything inside that ring simply *stopped*. Arrows hung in the air. Flame froze mid-leap. Hunters and rebels alike staggered as if the world had become honey. Only Beatrix moved within it, rising slowly to her feet, swaying, her face grey as the bridge-stone, her eyes finding Sebastian's across the frozen field.

"Take them and go," she said, and her voice carried impossibly through the stillness, the way voices carry in dreams. "The ring won't hold. Nothing holds. Go, Sebastian."

"It'll kill you—"

"It already cost me everything once." Something that was almost a smile. "I can afford to be poor twice."

The frozen hunters nearest her were beginning to twitch, fighting the stasis inch by inch, and Sebastian understood he had perhaps ten breaths. He ran. Past Veyra, locked mid-stride with her hand half-raised and her eyes wide and furious and — he would swear this later — *afraid*, not of him but of the arithmetic, of a battle suddenly containing variables no order could cover. Past the hanging arrows. To the mill wall, where he sawed through rope with a hunter's dropped knife while Tobbe swore at him to hurry and Ilse wept without sound.

Maren couldn't walk — an arrow through her calf — so Roderick took her across his back, and Sebastian took Ilse's hand, and they went, the five of them, stumbling through the gate in the mill's rear wall just as Beatrix's white ring shattered like ice on a pond.

Sound crashed back in. The battle resumed with doubled fury, because now both sides had seen the prize change hands and neither side had it, and the decoy — the decoy was still strapped to Sebastian's chest, and every eye in Greyford, crown and rebel alike, tracked him through the smoke like compass needles finding north.

* * *

They made the reed-bank in chaos. The smuggler cousin was gone — fled, sensibly, at the first scream — and the real core lay where he'd left it among the reeds, glowing through its wrappings like a coal refusing to die. Sebastian transferred it to his own chest, abandoned the decoy in the mud without ceremony, and turned to find the way east blocked.

Rebels. Six of them, ash-scarved, led by the fire-caster whose flame he'd turned aside in the yard. She pulled down her scarf, and she was young — younger than him, maybe — with burn scars climbing her neck like ivy, and her eyes were the eyes of someone who had already lost everything once and organized the loss into purpose.

"The core," she said. "You're going to give it to us, or you're going to watch what we do to the old woman."

Sebastian turned. Down in the courtyard, visible through the smoke, Beatrix stood surrounded — Veyra's hunters closing in a ring, Veyra herself dismounted from nowhere, sword drawn, saying something low and urgent that the fog ate before it reached him. Beatrix stood straight. But she was leaning on Tobbe's shoulder now, and there was blood at her mouth, and the white light was gone from her entirely, spent down to the last grain.

*The point of no return* is a phrase from stories, Sebastian thought wildly. In stories it feels like a door slamming. In life it feels like this: standing in freezing mud with a dying woman's legacy against your chest and a child with burn scars telling you the price of love is the future of the world.

"They'll kill her either way," the rebel girl said, gentler now, which was worse. "Hunters don't release witnesses. Give it to us and at least it burns in freedom instead of rotting in a vault."

And behind her, beyond her, past them all, Sebastian felt the core pulse against his sternum — once, twice, insistent — and for the first time it didn't whisper. It *spoke*, clear as Beatrix at his shoulder:

*They will all use me, little door. The crown will lock me and grow cruel in the dark. These children will light me and burn as my last masters burned. Unless.*

"Unless what?" he said aloud, and the rebels flinched, thinking he spoke to them.

*Unless someone does what has never been done. Unless someone lets me go — all the way. You have felt it, haven't you? What happens when gift pours out of a vessel and into the open air?*

He had. In the chapel. The night he'd nearly drowned the village child, his gift had slipped its leash and filled the flooded nave like light fills water, touching everything, changing everything it touched.

"You'll kill everyone," he whispered.

*I have never been given freely. Every hand that held me took. Try letting.*

Down in the courtyard, Veyra raised her sword.

Sebastian closed his eyes. He unwound the oilcloth. And the core opened to him like a flower of amber and lightning, and the whole battlefield — crown, rebel, fog, and river — drew one collective breath as its light poured upward into the grey sky like a second dawn.

The rebel girl took a step back. "What are you doing?" she breathed. "What are you *doing*—"

"What nobody's ever done," Sebastian said, and pressed both hands against the heart of the world.
