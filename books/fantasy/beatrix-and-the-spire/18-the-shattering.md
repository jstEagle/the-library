## 18. The Shattering

The world narrowed to the space between two heartbeats.

Sebastian ran through Greyford's broken marketplace with the core pressed against his chest, and it was singing. Not a sound he heard with his ears — a sound that lived in his teeth, in the wet architecture of his lungs, in the blood hammering behind his eyes. It had been singing since the river crossing, since Beatrix's hands had shoved him toward the reed-beds and her voice had cracked on a single word: *run*. Since then the core's song had grown from a whisper to something like a choir, and he understood now, with the terrible clarity of a man falling, what it wanted.

It wanted to be opened. It wanted to be *used*. And every person converging on this square wanted to be the one holding the key.

* * *

Greyford had been a market town once. He could see its bones beneath the ruin — the iron hooks where merchants had hung their scales, the grooves worn into cobbles by a century of cart wheels, the fountain at the square's center choked with ash and dead leaves. Now the crown had made it a gallows-ground: scaffold poles lashed together at the edges, cages stacked like cordwood, and banners of the sun-and-sword hanging limp in air that tasted of smoke and old rain.

He came out of the alley behind the tannery and the battle was already there.

Veyra's riders held the square's north side, thirty of them in grey-lacquered armor, crossbows leveled, moving with the drilled patience of people who had done this a hundred times. On the south, pouring through the burned gate of the old tithe-barn, came the rebels — the gifted who Odile's ledger had summoned like wolves to blood. Sebastian recognized none of them by name but all of them by kind: thin faces, hungry faces, hands already crackling with small furious magics that the valley had taught him were nothing next to what lived in his own skin. Between the two forces, kneeling in the mud with rope at their wrists, were his guildmates — Tam, gray-faced; little Sef with blood crusting one ear; old Maren, spine straight as an arrow even on her knees.

And near the scaffold, held upright by two riders because she could barely stand, was Beatrix.

Her left arm hung wrong. Her white hair was matted dark at the temple. But when her eyes found him across the square — found him, found the bundle wrapped in oilcloth against his chest — she did not look relieved. She looked afraid. He had never seen her afraid. Forty years of exile, forty years of carrying a stolen heart of the world in secret, and she had never once let fear reach her face until now.

*Don't*, her eyes said. *Whatever you're planning — don't.*

Too late. He had been planning it since the drowned chapel, since the night he'd nearly killed a child with a power he didn't understand, since the night Beatrix had told him about the war — the war the spire-core had fueled, when magic had been a crown's weapon and whole provinces had burned as bright and brief as paper. He'd been planning it since he understood that the core could not be given to Veyra, could not be given to the rebels, could not be buried again under another valley for another generation to find and fight over.

Some things you do not carry forward. Some things you end.

* * *

"STOP."

His voice came out wrong — too large, resonant, doubled, as if the core spoke through his throat as well. The square flinched. Crossbows swiveled toward him. In the sudden hush he walked into the open, boots crunching glass, and unwrapped the oilcloth.

The core sat in his palms like a captive star.

He had expected stone. What he held was not stone — or not only. It was the size of a loaf of bread and heavier than an anvil, and light moved inside it the way weather moves inside a mountain lake: slow, deep, patient, older than kingdoms. Veins of silver fire crawled beneath its surface. When he breathed, it breathed. When his heart stumbled, it steadied him, the way a hand steadies a child learning to walk — and that gentleness was the most frightening thing about it, because he knew now that the same heart had watched cities burn without blinking. It did not love. It only waited to be poured into.

"Sebastian." Veyra's voice cut across the square, level as a drawn line. She stood before her riders, helm off, gray-streaked hair pulled tight, and even now she looked less like a hunter than a magistrate — someone who believed, completely and terribly, that she was the last sane person in the room. "Put it down. Walk away from it. You have my word no harm comes to you."

"You'll destroy it," Sebastian said.

"I'll *end* it." Something moved behind her eyes — not cruelty, never cruelty; certainty. "Magic burned half the world once. I have read the accounts. I have walked the ash-fields. Everything I do, I do so that no child ever learns they carry fire in them and no village ever burns because of it. Give it to me, boy, and your friends live. Refuse—" her gaze flicked, precise as a compass needle, to the kneeling guildmates, "—and I cannot protect them from what happens next."

From the rebel line, a woman laughed — a raw, cracked sound. She was tall, burn-scarred along one jaw, and lightning crawled lazily between her fingers like a cat she'd tamed. "Listen to the queen's hound recite her catechism." She raised her voice to the square. "You hear her? Forty years they've hunted us, caged us, burned our motherhouse and our waystations — and now the thing that would make us *equal* is in a boy's arms, and she asks politely. Greyford! Are we polite?"

A roar went up from the rebels. Somewhere in it, Sebastian heard grief — real grief, decades of it, the sound of people who had been hunted so long that fury had become their native tongue. And he understood Veyra too. That was the trap of it. Both sides were right. Both sides were wrong. And the core in his hands hummed against his sternum, delighted, *fed* by all of it, drinking their rage and their righteousness alike.

"Sebastian!" Maren's voice, hoarse. "Boy, whatever you mean to do—"

Beatrix moved.

Later he would learn how — a scrap of the old severance, the last thread of magic the guild's founding rites had left knotted in her bones, spent in a single instant after forty years of saving it. She twisted out of her captors' grip like water finding a crack, and the two riders holding her simply *stopped*, frozen mid-motion as if the hour itself had snagged on a nail, and she was running — limping, dragging her ruined arm, running at Veyra with nothing in her hands at all.

"No," Sebastian breathed.

Veyra didn't hesitate. That was the thing about her; he had learned it across a hundred leagues of pursuit. She never hesitated. Her sword cleared its sheath in a single bright arc.

What happened next took less time than a heartbeat and more room than the sky.

Beatrix met the blade with her bare palm — and the air between them turned to white. A detonation of force threw riders off their horses in a ring; the scaffold splintered; Sebastian felt it through the ground, up his legs, into the core, which *sang*, oh, how it sang, swelling with the violence like a cistern filling with rain. When the glare faded, Beatrix was on her knees in the mud, and there was a long red line opening across her ribs, and Veyra stood above her with the sword still raised and blood running freely down the side of her own face from a gash Beatrix's dying burst had torn across her brow.

And Beatrix looked up at Sebastian, and mouthed one word.

Not *no*. Not *stop*.

*Now.*

* * *

Time went strange. He would remember it afterward as fragments stitched with gold thread, the way trauma sews its terrible quilts: the rebels surging forward, lightning and thrown fire tearing the air; Veyra's riders loosing a volley that dropped three gifted where they stood; Tam screaming his name from the ropes; the fountain exploding into steam; and above all the core in his hands, beating faster now, eager, whispering in a voice that was almost Beatrix's and almost his mother's and entirely its own —

*Give me to them. Give me to her. Give me away, give me away, any hand will do, I only want to be poured.*

"No," Sebastian said aloud, to it, to everyone, to the whole bent shape of the world. "No more pouring."

He drove his gift into it.

It is hard to describe what that meant, because there are no words in any language for giving yourself to a thing that has swallowed empires. His gift woke at the wardline months ago as a candleflame; in the drowned chapel it had been a flood. This was neither. This was opening a door in his chest and stepping back and letting everything he was go through it — every lesson Beatrix had taught him, every discipline, every smothered night and practiced breath, the whole quiet hoarded treasury of a life spent being careful. The core drank it and blazed like a second dawn, silver fire climbing his arms to the elbow, and still it wanted more, it reached for him, hungry as the war it remembered—

He let it take everything. And then, holding the last ember of himself like a coal cupped in two shaking hands, he did the thing Beatrix had never dared, the thing no keeper had ever dared in four hundred years of hiding it.

He broke it.

He struck his gift against the core's heart the way a smith strikes steel — not gently, not carefully, but with intent, with malice almost, with the full weight of every choice that had brought him here. The core resisted. Of course it did; it had outlived the people who made it; it clung to its own shape like a drowning man. For one endless moment Sebastian stood at the center of a silent white world, wrestling with a god's heartstone, feeling himself gutter—

—and then he felt other hands.

Maren, somewhere in the dark, her old warding-craft flowing into him unasked. Sef, terrified, stubborn, adding his small warmth. Even Tam, bound and bleeding, somehow, impossibly, reaching. The guild. The guild that was never the valley, never the ledger, never the stone. The guild was this: many hands on one burden.

Together they struck the core a final time, and the core *shattered*.

* * *

There was no explosion, precisely. There was an *unbinding*.

Light did not burst outward so much as pour, the way dawn pours over a ridge, silent and total and unstoppable. It passed through walls and bodies and banners and bones, and where it passed, it *left things behind*. Sebastian felt it leave him — his gift stripped out and scattered like seed from a bursting pod — and he felt it arrive everywhere else at once.

In the rebels, the burn-scarred woman staggered as her lightning guttered and then returned changed — smaller, stranger, hers alone, no longer borrowed from the core's vast impersonal sea but grown new in her own marrow. In Veyra's riders, men who had never felt anything but the weight of sword and saddle gasped as color bloomed behind their eyes, as sparks danced between their gauntleted fingers, as the horses beneath them shied at the sudden shining of their masters' hands. In the caged prisoners at the square's edge, in the townsfolk peering from burned windows, in Veyra herself — everywhere, everyone, the light touched and seeded and moved on.

Magic, common as rain. Ungovernable. Unhuntable. Free.

The silence afterward was the deepest Sebastian had ever heard.

Then Beatrix folded sideways into the mud, and the world snapped back into speed and sound and horror.

* * *

He reached her in six strides that felt like six years. She lay on her back in the churned filth of the square, and the wound across her ribs was bad — worse than bad, the kind of wound the body argues with and loses. Her breathing came shallow and wet. Her eyes were open, though, fixed on the sky, and when he gathered her up she turned her head with glacial slowness and looked at him.

"You broke it," she whispered. Wonder, in that ruined voice. Actual wonder. Forty years of guilt, and she sounded like a girl.

"It's in everyone now." His own voice shook apart on the words. "Small. Everywhere. They can't hunt it. No one can hold it. Beatrix — it worked, it—"

"Hush." Her hand — the good one — found his wrist with shocking strength. Around them the battle was dissolving into confusion: riders dismounting to stare at the light in their own palms, rebels kneeling, weeping, laughing; Veyra standing motionless amid her broken formation, blood sheeting down her face, staring at her bare hand where a faint silver glow rose and fell with her breath. "Listen. Listen to me, Sebastian Okonkwo. The guild—"

"Don't talk. We need a healer, we—"

"The guild," she insisted, each word a coin paid out of a shrinking purse, "was never the valley. Never the ledgers. Never the *secret*." A breath that whistled. "It was the keeping. The keeping of each other. Do you understand? Whatever it becomes now — it must be *open*. Doors wide. No more valleys. Promise me you'll build that. Promise me you'll be the door."

"I promise. I promise, just hold on—"

She smiled then — small, crooked, the smile he had first seen across a chore-yard a lifetime ago, the smile of a woman who had carried the world's most dangerous secret for forty years and finally, finally set it down.

"There was never anything wrong with you," she said. "That was the whole secret. There was never anything wrong with any of us."

Her eyes closed. Her chest rose once, twice — and stopped rising cleanly, stuttering, ragged, but *rising*, and Sef was suddenly at his shoulder shouting that the old midwife arts might hold her, that Maren was coming, that he should keep pressure *here*, press *here* — and Sebastian pressed, and prayed to no one, and held on.

Above them, the shattered spire-heart's light drifted down over Greyford like snow, settling on the living and the dead alike, on hunters and rebels and bystanders, on the freed guildmates and the frightened town — settling on everyone equally, asking nothing, belonging nowhere, belonging everywhere.

Across the square, Veyra lowered her sword.

She looked at the glow in her own hand for a long time. Then she looked at Sebastian, kneeling in the mud with his dying mentor in his arms, and her face — for the first time since the hunt began — had no order in it at all, no law, no catechism. Only a question, vast and unanswerable, forming behind her eyes.

And she did not move to arrest him.
