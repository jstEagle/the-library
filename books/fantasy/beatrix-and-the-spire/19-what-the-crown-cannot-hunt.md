## 19. What the Crown Cannot Hunt

The dust had not yet settled over Greyford when Veyra understood that her war was over.

She stood in the ruined square where the exchange had been meant to happen — a clean transaction, a core for prisoners, the ledger balanced — and watched the light still clinging to people like pollen. It hung in the air in threads of gold and green, settling on the wounded and the dead alike, sinking into skin, into stone, into the cracked bell of the chapel that had not rung in sixty years. It did not behave like magic as she had been taught to fear it. It behaved like weather. Like something that had always been here and had only now been noticed.

A hunter knelt ten paces from her, one of hers, sworn and oath-marked, holding his own hands up before his face and watching them glow. He turned them over. He turned them back. His mouth moved without sound.

Veyra knew the feeling. Her own fingertips had been tingling since the shattering — a warmth she could not dismiss, could not file, could not burn out of herself no matter how she ordered it to go. She had spent thirty years learning to be empty of it. The crown had chosen her precisely because she carried nothing: no gift, no taint, no thread of the old blood that the purges had been woven to cut. *The clean hand holds the sword*, her first commander had said. And now the sword's own hilt glowed in her grip like a lantern.

She let it fall into the ash.

* * *

They brought her reports through the afternoon because they did not know what else to do with themselves. Veyra had built her company on clarity — every man and woman in it knew their orders, their stations, the shape of the day from dawn bell to dark. Now they came to her one by one with faces like lost children, carrying fragments of a world that no longer fit together.

"The rebels are scattered," said Dain, her lieutenant, a broad grey man who had followed her since the marsh campaigns. "Half of them ran when the light hit them. Half of them are sitting in the rubble weeping. We've taken eleven prisoner."

"Names?"

"They won't give them." He hesitated, which Dain never did. "Captain — two of my own crossed the lines during the fighting. Walked right over to the rebels. Not to fight. They just... stood with them. When the light came down."

Veyra looked at him. He was waiting for her to be angry, and she found, searching herself with the thoroughness she applied to everything, that the anger was simply not there. It was like reaching into a drawer that had been emptied overnight.

"Where is the boy?" she asked.

"Okonkwo? Gone. Him and the old woman both. We tracked them as far as the mill road and then—" He made a small, helpless gesture at the air, at everything. "Then the tracks just stopped mattering. There were a hundred people on that road by dusk, Captain, and every one of them glowing like a saint's day candle. You can't follow a trail through a crowd when everyone in the crowd is what you're hunting."

*You cannot hunt a gift that lives in everyone.* The thought arrived whole, the way verdicts arrive, and Veyra stood very still inside it while the dust drifted down around her like slow snow.

* * *

In the evening she went to the prisoners.

They were held in the tithe barn at the edge of town, under guard, though Veyra suspected the guards were there more for the guards' sake than anyone else's. She walked the length of the straw-strewn floor between them and felt the strangeness of it: these were the gifted, the hunted, the ones whose names she had carried in sealed dossiers for years, whose capture she had planned across three winters of patient work. She had imagined this moment often enough. It had always ended with ledgers and rope.

Now they sat among their captors like neighbors after a flood, and half the guards glowed too.

One of the prisoners rose when she approached — an old man, rebel-marked, his forearms scarred with the brand they gave unregistered gifted in the eastern provinces. He did not flinch from her. That was new. They had always flinched before, or performed defiance; either way, fear had been the currency between them, and she had spent it freely.

"You're the hunter," he said. "Veyra. The one who never loses."

"I have lost," she said.

He smiled, and there was no cruelty in it, which unsettled her more than cruelty would have. "No. You caught everyone you were sent to catch. But you were never hunting us, Captain. We were just the trail. You were hunting the thing itself." He lifted his hands, and the light moved in them, faint and patient, like fish under ice. "And now look. It caught you instead."

She should have ordered him gagged for insolence. Instead she asked, "What does it feel like?"

The question seemed to surprise them both. Around the barn, heads turned. A young woman near the wall — sixteen perhaps, dirt-caked, one eye swollen shut from the fighting — answered before the old man could.

"Like remembering," she said simply. "Like something I forgot I knew."

Veyra left without another word. In the doorway she paused, and did not look back, and heard behind her the low murmur of voices beginning — prisoners and guards, talking. Just talking. She could not remember the last time she had heard the gifted speak to the ungifted as if the space between them were only air.

* * *

Her orders arrived three days later, and they were useless.

The courier came up from the capital under crown seal, dusty and frightened, and delivered a packet Veyra read twice by lamplight in the commandeered magistrate's house. The language of it was the language she knew best — precise, procedural, the grammar of a kingdom that believed everything could be filed. *All persons demonstrating unregistered arcane ability are to be detained for assessment. Assessment protocols to follow. Detention facilities to be designated. Further instructions pending.*

Further instructions pending. She read those three words until they dissolved. There were perhaps four hundred thousand people in the western provinces alone. By the seventh day after Greyford, travelers spoke of lights in villages from the coast to the mountains — hedge-wives mending broken bones, millers' sons kindling cold hearths with a word, children lifting pebbles for the sheer astonishment of it. The gift had gone into everyone like rain into dry ground, shallow and widespread and utterly beyond gathering.

Detain them all. Assess them all. Build facilities for all.

She spread the map of the kingdom on the magistrate's table and looked at it the way she had once looked at battlefields, and for the first time in her career the map told her nothing. There was no line to draw. There was no border between the tainted and the clean, because the distinction had died in a burst of golden light in a market square, and every order in the packet assumed it was still alive.

Dain found her there past midnight, the lamp burned to a stub.

"We have the list," he said. "The assessment lists. Four hundred names in our jurisdiction alone. Do we begin?"

"Begin what?"

"Detentions." He said it carefully, watching her.

Veyra touched the map. Her finger came to rest on Hollowmere — the valley that had started everything, the valley whose ward had failed the night Beatrix fled, the valley that even now, according to the last reports, was full of farmers doing small impossible things in their fields and being neither punished nor punished by it.

"Dain," she said, "when you were a boy, before the company — did your mother ever tell you why the gifted were driven out?"

He shifted. "That they were dangerous. That the war—"

"That the war burned half the world, yes. I was raised on it too. The spire, the great workings, the cities of glass that melted. Magic in few hands is a weapon. That was the whole of the law, and the law was good, and I enforced it because it was good." She drew a slow breath. "But the law says *few hands*. Read me the clause that covers everyone."

He was silent a long moment. "There isn't one."

"No," she said. "There isn't one."

* * *

On the fifth day she rode out along the mill road, alone, as she had not ridden alone since she was a girl in the northern hills.

She told herself it was reconnaissance. The boy — Okonkwo — had been seen, or might have been seen, or a shepherd thought perhaps; the report was thin as morning mist, but she took it, because taking it gave the riding a name, and names were how she had always kept herself upright. Veyra the relentless. Veyra who never lost. If she rode toward the boy, then she was still hunting, and if she was still hunting, the world still had a shape.

The mill road climbed through country she did not know, green and ordinary, and it was full of small miracles.

She passed a field where an old woman walked the rows and the blight lifted off the barley like smoke off a fire, curling away into the sky in grey ribbons while the stalks straightened behind her. She passed a well where children had rigged a wheel that turned with no wind and no ox, spinning lazily, singing to itself, while their mother watched from the doorstep with her arms folded and an expression Veyra could not classify — not joy exactly, not fear. Vigilance. The vigilance of people who know the world has changed and are waiting to learn whether the change will be permitted.

No one hid from her. That was the thing that undid her, riding mile after mile. She wore no colors, but hunters were known by their bearing, by the way they looked at people, and these people looked back. A carter nodded to her. A girl practicing small lights over her palm in a doorway glanced up, met her eyes, and went on practicing.

*They aren't afraid,* Veyra thought, and the thought had teeth. *They aren't afraid, and they aren't few, and there is no cell in the kingdom large enough.*

Near dusk she found the shepherd who had made the report, and the shepherd, embarrassed, admitted he had seen nothing certain — a young man and an older woman, perhaps, or perhaps only pilgrims, walking east toward the mountains, toward the valley. Toward Hollowmere. The trail, such as it was, pointed home.

Veyra sat her horse on the ridge above the road as the light went amber and long, and let herself, for the first time in thirty years, think the forbidden thought all the way through.

She had been nine years old when the purge reached her village. She remembered the bonfires of confiscated books, the family from the next farm over loaded onto a cart, the mother screaming a name — not a curse, not a plea, just a name, over and over, the way you call someone home for supper. She remembered asking her father what the woman had done, and her father saying, *she was born wrong*, and the terrible simplicity of that answer, how it had closed the question like a lid. She had built her life inside that lid. She had become the arm of the law that kept it shut, and she had been proud of it, because the alternative — that the lid was arbitrary, that the screaming woman had done nothing but exist — was unbearable.

And now the lid was open, and the world had not burned. The barley grew cleaner than it had in years. Children spun wheels with their minds and laughed about it. The great terror, the reason for everything, had come pouring out of its prison into every living soul, and the world was — what? Frightened. Changed. Alive. No more burning than it had been the week before.

*We were wrong,* she thought, and the words did not destroy her. That was the strangest part. She had expected them to be like a blade, and they were like a door: heavy, and opening.

She did not ride on toward Hollowmere. She sat until the stars came out, and then she turned the horse around.

* * *

Back at Greyford, she did what she had never once done in her career: she wrote a report that told the truth, and she filed it knowing what it would cost.

*The situation is not containable by existing statute,* she wrote, in her close, disciplined hand. *The demonstration of arcane ability is now universal or near-universal across the civilian population. There is no criterion by which the guilty may be distinguished from the innocent, because the condition formerly defined as guilt is now shared by all. I recommend the immediate suspension of detention orders and the convening of a commission to determine what law, if any, applies. Until such determination, my company will maintain peace only, and prosecute no one for the fact of their nature.*

She signed it. She sealed it. Then she opened the prisoner lists — four hundred names in her jurisdiction, eleven already in the barn — and struck through every line, one by one, with the same pen, until the pages were black with cancellation, like a field of graves plowed under.

Dain watched her do it and said nothing until the last name was crossed out. "They'll relieve you," he said. "Or worse."

"Yes."

"And you'll send it anyway."

"They can relieve me," Veyra said. "They cannot relieve the whole world. Someone has to be the first to say the hunt is over, and it seems it must be the hunter." She almost smiled. It felt strange on her face, an expression from a country she'd emigrated from long ago. "I spent my life believing the law was justice wearing armor. Today I learned they can part ways. When they do, you choose. This is me choosing."

She released the eleven that evening. The old man with the branded arms paused at the barn door and looked back at her.

"What will you do now?" he asked.

"I don't know," Veyra said, and found it was not a defeat to say it. "No one knows. That's rather the point."

He nodded slowly, as if she had passed some test she hadn't known she was taking, and walked out into the dark, glowing faintly, like everyone else.

* * *

The reply from the capital came in nine days. She had expected dismissal, arrest, perhaps a warrant. Instead the seal held a single sheet in a clerk's hand, unsigned by any minister, which somehow made it worse:

*Your resignation is accepted. Your company is disbanded by order of the Regency Council. All matters pertaining to arcane phenomena are remanded to a body yet to be constituted. You are instructed to surrender your commission and make no further report.*

Make no further report. As if silence could fold the light back into the shattered stone. As if the kingdom could simply decline to notice that its oldest law had died of irrelevance.

Veyra read it standing in the magistrate's doorway, in the morning sun, with the smell of bread from the ovens two streets over — real bread, baked by a baker who could now, if he wished, warm his ovens with a word. She read it twice, and then she laughed, once, short and disbelieving, startling a passing guard.

They had disbanded her. They had dissolved the hunt with a clerk's pen, the way you dissolve a tax office or a bridge commission, because there was nothing left to hunt and no honest way to say so. Somewhere in the capital, men in good coats had looked at the maps and the lists and the impossibility of it all, and blinked.

Let them blink. Let them constitute their bodies and draft their commissions and pretend, for a season, that procedure could catch up to what had happened. The truth was already loose in the fields and the wells and the children's cupped palms, and it would wait for them, patiently, the way spring waits.

She surrendered her sword to the garrison armorer, who took it awkwardly, not meeting her eyes. She paid her debts, thanked her company, and stood a moment in the empty square where the core had burst, where the gold light had gone up like a second sunrise and come down into everyone.

The boy had walked east. Toward the valley. Toward whatever the guild would become now that secrecy was dead and the spire was a ruin with a school growing in its shadow — she had heard the rumors, everyone had heard the rumors, of Beatrix the exile teaching openly in the ruins, of farmers' children learning disciplines older than the crown itself.

Veyra looked down the east road for a long time.

Then she bought a horse with her own coin, packed light, and rode — not as a hunter this time, for there was no such thing anymore, and no order existed anywhere in the kingdom that fit what she intended. She rode as what she supposed she had always been underneath the armor: a woman who wanted to see, with her own eyes, what the world was going to be.

The road east was long, and full of small lights, and for the first time in her life, Veyra was not following a quarry.

She was following a question.
