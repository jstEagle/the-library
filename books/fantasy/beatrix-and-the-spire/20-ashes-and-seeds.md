## 20. Ashes and Seeds

The morning after the world broke, Sebastian woke to the smell of woodsmoke and someone crying softly, two tents away.

Greyford's ruins had become a camp. That was the only word for it — the burned market square, the shattered arcade, the leaning walls of the customs house all filled now with the people who had been in the square when the core came apart. Hunters without their colors. Rebels without their banners. Bystanders who had come to sell turnips and gone home — or hadn't gone home — with something new coiled behind their ribs. They had dragged tarps over the gaps in the walls and built cookfires in the doorways, and the smoke of a dozen small fires rose into a grey sky that couldn't decide whether it was dawn.

Sebastian lay on a bedroll he didn't remember being given and watched the smoke and thought: *I did that.*

Not the fires. The smoke was ordinary. But somewhere under the skin of everything, the old machinery of the world had seized and shattered, and he had put his hands on the gears. He could still feel it in his bones, the moment of the shattering — the core's hunger, its terrible patient wanting, and his own gift pouring into it like water into a cracked bell until the bell burst. He could still hear it. Not with his ears. Somewhere deeper, a note that had not finished dying.

The crying two tents away was a woman learning, badly and in grief, that she could make a candleflame bend toward her finger. He knew this because she kept trying, and each time the flame bent she sobbed harder, and each time it straightened she sobbed harder still. No one had told her yet that it would never leave. No one had told any of them.

He got up because lying still was worse.

* * *

Beatrix was sitting up.

That was the first miracle, and he refused to call it anything smaller. He had carried her himself, out of the square, her blood soaking through his sleeves and her breath coming in wet, torn intervals, and he had spent the night — he didn't know how many hours — with his hands on her sternum doing the only thing he knew how to do, which was to push his gift into her like a man bailing a sinking boat with his cupped palms. He had not known if it was working. He had not known if a body with so little of her old magic left could even hold what he gave.

She was sitting up against a wagon wheel, wrapped in a soldier's cloak, and she was thin. Not wounded-thin. *Diminished*-thin, the way a candle is thin after it has burned all night — still a candle, still light, but visibly, permanently less than it had been. Her hands, the hands that had once carved the wardline of an entire valley, lay open and idle in her lap, and when she lifted one to push hair from her face the gesture cost her something. He saw it cost her. She saw him see it.

"Don't," she said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were about to compose a eulogy." Her voice was rough but dry, and that was Beatrix, that was always, always Beatrix. "I'm not dead. Sit down. You look like a heron that's been in a fight."

He sat. The ground was cold through the bedroll. Around them the camp was waking — a hunter from Veyra's own company hauling water with a bucket that, when he stumbled, righted itself a half-second before it should have; a rebel woman showing a customs clerk how to hold his new, fumbling little gift the way you'd teach a child to hold a knife, blade out, thumb on the spine. Small magics. Candle-bending, bucket-righting, the sense of a door before it opens. The core had not made archmages. It had made a kingdom of apprentices.

"It's in everyone," Sebastian said. It wasn't a question. He'd known it since the square, since he'd felt the shattering go out of him and *into* everything, but saying it made it real, and he needed one thing this morning to be real.

"In everyone who was near," Beatrix said. "And in their children, I'd guess, and their children's children. Magic doesn't like to be rare, Sebastian. It never has. We made it rare. All those old guilds, all those spires — we hoarded it, we built walls around it, we convinced the world that it belonged to the few, and the world believed us, and then the world decided the few were a disease." She looked out at the camp, at the hunter with the bucket, at the crying woman and her candle. "Now it belongs to everyone, and no one can hunt it, and no one can govern it, and no one — " her mouth twisted — "*no one* can put it back in a spire."

"Is that a grief or a relief?"

"Yes," she said.

* * *

They freed the guildmates on the third day.

It took that long because Veyra's chain of command had dissolved along with everything else — half her riders had gifts now, and the ones who didn't were not eager to follow orders from officers who did, and the whole apparatus of the hunt had come apart like wet rope. The prison wagons had been sitting at the edge of Greyford under a skeleton guard, and when Sebastian and Odile and a dozen of the newly gifted walked up to it, the guard sergeant looked at the crowd, looked at the faint blue shimmer around Odile's fingers, and handed over the keys with the expression of a man who had decided, sometime in the last three days, that the world had changed employers.

Maren came out first. Then Oskar, blinking, thinner, his wrists ringed with sores. Then the others, seven of them, stumbling into daylight, and Sebastian stood there and let them look at him and could not make his mouth work.

It was Maren who broke it. She looked at him — at the soot still in his hair, at the bandage on his arm, at the whole ruined fact of him — and she said, "You look terrible," and then she was holding him, and Oskar's arms came around them both, and the guild that had been a valley and then a fugitive rumor and then a name in a ledger was, for one moment, just eight people gripping each other in the mud outside a prison wagon.

"You broke it," Oskar said into his shoulder. Not accusing. Wondering. "The ward. The core. Everything."

"I broke everything," Sebastian agreed.

"Good," Oskar said, and pulled back, and his eyes were wet and furious and free. "The valley was dying anyway. You just — made it honest."

* * *

The argument started that evening, around the largest of the cookfires, and it did not stop.

It began, as these things do, with something small. Maren asked what they did now — did they go back, was there even a *back* to go to — and Oskar said there was no valley to return to, the wardline was dead, the ward was dead, anyone could walk into Hollowmere tomorrow, and Maren said then we defend it, and Oskar said with what, and Maren said with *this*, lifting her hand where a small blue light sat obedient above her palm, and Oskar said that was exactly the thinking that got every guild in the kingdom burned to its foundations.

"You want to be what they feared," he said. "A fortified place full of magic. Congratulations. You've invented the spire all over again."

"I want us to be *safe* —"

"Safe is what the crown promised the informers. Safe is what Veyra promised the kingdom. Every atrocity in the last hundred years was somebody being kept *safe*." Oskar's voice cracked, and Sebastian saw that it wasn't only anger in it. Oskar had spent weeks in a wagon. Oskar had a lifetime of reasons to never again trust a wall.

"And what's your answer?" Maren shot back. "Scatter? Be nothing? We were *something*, Oskar. We kept people alive for two hundred years —"

"We kept people *hidden*. There's a difference, and the smoke over the motherhouse is the difference."

Sebastian listened, and did not speak, and was aware of Beatrix beside him listening too, her hands folded, her face unreadable. Around the fire, the others took sides — quietly at first, then not quietly. One of the freed guildmates, a stonemason named Perrin, said the guild should become a school, openly, in the valley, teaching anyone who came. Someone else said the valley was a graveyard of everything they'd lost and they should build somewhere new. A hunter who had defected — he was there, no one had invited him and no one had sent him away, which Sebastian thought was the whole new world in miniature — said that if the guild went public, the crown would come within a year, and someone else said the crown couldn't come, the crown had no law for any of this, the crown's entire authority had been built on the idea that magic was rare and containable and that idea was now ash.

"The crown will write new laws," Beatrix said at last, and the fire went quiet for her, because she was Beatrix, because she was the founder, because she had come back from a wound that should have killed her and everyone knew what it had cost. "Laws are how power digests what it cannot kill. It will take them years. We have years — maybe. What we decide now, we decide for the ones who come after."

"Then decide," Maren said. "You founded it. Tell us what it becomes."

And Beatrix was silent for a long moment, and Sebastian watched her, and understood that she was not deciding. She was *weighing* — weighing the guild she had built against the world that had burned it, weighing secrecy against survival, weighing forty years of a dead valley against a camp full of strangers with candleflame gifts. When she spoke, her voice was quiet.

"I built the guild to keep a secret," she said. "The secret kept us alive and it also kept us small, and in the end it nearly kept us *dead*. I don't know what it becomes. I know what it can't be. It can't be a vault. It can't be a spire. It can't be mine." She looked around the fire, at each of them. "It has to be something none of us have ever seen. Which means it has to be built by people who aren't tired. Which means not me, and not tonight."

* * *

He found her later, past the edge of the fires, where the ruins gave way to a fallow field gone silver with frost. She was standing looking east, toward the valley, the way she had looked east a hundred times in his childhood without him knowing what she was looking at.

"You could go back," he said. "There's no ward, but there's the houses. The orchards. It's still —"

"A tomb," she said, without heat. "I know. I buried the core under my own sanctuary, Sebastian. I've known for forty years what the valley was." She turned her head and looked at him, and in the starlight he could see how old she was, truly old, older than the guild, older than the war. "But you could go back. And you won't. And that interests me."

"Why won't I?"

"Because you finally understand it. The valley was never the point. It was a shell. A place to put the ones we couldn't protect anywhere else." She smiled, and it was the saddest and truest smile he had ever seen on her. "You walked out of it. You crossed the line. Everything since has been the guild, the real one — the part that was never about walls."

He wanted to argue. He found he couldn't. He thought of the fire, of Maren's blue light and Oskar's fury, of the hunter hauling water, of the crying woman and her candle, of all of them camped in the bones of a burned town because there was nowhere else and no reason anymore to hide.

"What do I do?" he asked. It was the question of a boy asking a mentor, and he heard that, and heard that he didn't mind.

"Plant something," Beatrix said. "That's all any founder ever does. You don't build the tree. You put a seed in the ground and you protect it until it doesn't need you. And then — " she shrugged, and the gesture cost her, and she made it anyway — "and then you get to watch."

* * *

On the sixth morning, a rider came up the east road, riding hard, and the camp's sentries — everyone was a sentry now; everyone had a gift and everyone knew it — flagged Sebastian before the rider was even in hailing distance.

It was a boy, fourteen at most, hollow-cheeked, road-burned, his horse half dead under him. He slid off it in the mud and stood swaying, and when he saw Sebastian he fumbled at his neck and pulled free a cord, and on the cord hung a token that Sebastian knew, that every guild child knew: a spire stamped in lead, worn nearly smooth.

"From Hollowmere," the boy gasped. "From the valley. They said — they said the ward was down, they said the guild was here, they said find the one who broke the sky." He thrust the token out with a shaking hand. "There's more of us. There's dozens of us. People from the villages around — their gifts came in with the shattering, and the crown's criers are already out again, new proclamation, and this time it's not a hunt, it's — they're calling it *registration*, and people are scared, and we didn't know where to go, and someone said — " he swallowed, out of breath, out of everything — "someone said the guild was done hiding. Is it true? Is it true you're done hiding?"

Sebastian took the token. It was warm from the boy's neck. Behind him he heard the camp going quiet, and then he heard Beatrix's cane on the frost-hard ground, coming to stand beside him, and he looked east down the road, where the morning light was coming up gold behind the hills, toward the valley, toward everything.

"No," Sebastian said, and heard his own voice steady, and heard, beneath it, the dead bell-note of the core, and beneath *that*, something newer, something just beginning. "We're done hiding. Go tell them to come."
