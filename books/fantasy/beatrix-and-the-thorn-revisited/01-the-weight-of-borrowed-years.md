## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Years

The merchant slept like a man who had never once been robbed, which was the kind of sleeper Coral liked least.

It was not the difficulty that troubled him — difficulty was the trade, the whole pleasure of it, the lock and the pick of it. It was the trust. This man, Aldous Wren, wool-factor of Dunmark, third of his name, lay sprawled in a bed too large for his sleeping body with his mouth open and one arm flung across the empty half of the mattress, and he slept with the terrible confidence of someone who believed the world had already taken from him everything it wanted. He had no idea that the most valuable thing he owned was folded up behind his eyes, small as a pressed flower, and that a stranger crouched at the foot of his bed with his sleeves rolled back and his hands cold from the mountain road, ready to take it.

Coral counted the merchant's breaths. Seven to the minute, slow and deep, the rhythm of a man who drank two glasses of plum wine after supper and no more. The candle on the nightstand had burned down to a thumb's width; its light made a soft amber pond across the coverlet, and Coral worked at the edge of it, where the dark began, because that was where he had always lived — at the seam between what people kept and what they let go.

He set two fingers against the merchant's temple, light as a moth landing.

*Open*, he thought, in the old way, the way Maren-the-hedge-witch had once described as *a key turning in a door you didn't know you owned*. The mind beneath his fingers did not resist. Sleeping minds rarely did; they simply parted, like curtains in a draft, and Coral slipped through into the house of Aldous Wren the Third.

Every mind was a house. That was how Coral had been taught to see them, back when someone still bothered to teach him things, and this one was a fine house indeed: high-ceilinged, well-swept, every memory hung straight on its hook like a factor's ledgers in their cabinet. Here were the wool prices of eleven autumns. Here was the face of a dead father, severe above a beard. Here was a corridor of shame — small rooms, mostly locked — and here, near the back where the light came slanted through windows that looked out on a summer that had ended forty years ago, was the room he had come for.

The first love.

Coral paused outside it, as he always paused. Not out of reverence. Reverence was for priests and other men who could afford it. He paused the way a cutpurse pauses before lifting a purse: measuring the weight, feeling for the bell.

Then he opened the door.

Inside, it was raining. Memory-rain, the soft grey rain of a coastal town Coral had never seen, falling on a market square where a girl of perhaps nineteen stood laughing under an awning with her sleeves pushed up and flour on her hands, and a younger Aldous — twenty, gangly, hopeless with love the way only twenty-year-olds are — stood in the downpour without shelter because she had told him the rain was warm and he had believed her.

Her name was Sera. She baked morning bread for the harbor tavern and sang while she kneaded, badly, and Aldous Wren the Third had loved her for exactly one summer and forty years, though he had married another, and buried another, and built his fortune and his comfortable house and his confident sleep all on the foundation of a girl with flour on her hands who had died of a fever before the leaves turned.

She was worth four hundred marks, this memory. Coral knew because he had sold three like it in Dunmark's low quarter over the past year, each one to some gray-haired buyer who wept when it settled into them and called it *returning*, as if they had ever owned such a thing. Grief bought poorly. Joy bought well. First loves bought best of all, because there was nothing in the world so expensive as something a man believed he would die having kept.

He lifted it carefully. That much he gave the trade: he was gentle. The memory came away whole, unbruised, its rain still falling, its bread still rising somewhere off-scene in a warm stone oven, and he folded it — there was no better word; the stolen thing compressed itself into a shape like a seed, or a tear held too long in the palm — and he tucked it into the lacquered case inside his coat, into the velvet slot reserved for the night's work, next to the empty slots waiting for the other three extractions Aldous Wren had paid the guild surgeon to forget.

The merchant would wake poorer and not know it. He would feel a smoothness where Sera used to be, the way an amputee feels weather in a missing hand, and he had signed the paper saying he wanted exactly that. *Erase the ache,* the contract read, in his own tidy hand. Coral had witnessed the signature with the false name he always used — *Corvin Marsh, licensed mnemonic* — and had felt, briefly and unwelcome, the itch of curiosity about what kind of man pays good silver to have his one true thing pulled out of him root and branch.

But curiosity was another thing Coral did not keep.

He withdrew from the sleeping mind the way a diver rises, slowly enough that the surface would barely ripple, and the merchant murmured something in his sleep — a woman's name, but not Sera's — and rolled over, and went on breathing seven to the minute, and dreamed whatever dreams remained to a man being quietly emptied by appointment.

Coral let himself out through the kitchen window. He did not look back at the bed.

Looking back was keeping, and Coral kept nothing.

* * *

Thornfell at midnight was a country made entirely of the sound of wind.

Coral came down off the ridge above High Hollow with the case against his ribs and the cold working through his coat like a patient thief in reverse, robbing him by inches of warmth he couldn't spare. Below him the smuggler road unspooled along the mountainside, pale as a scar in the starlight — one of nine roads stitched through the Thornfell range, none of them on any king's map, all of them older than any king. The road-builders' names were gone. Their purpose was gone. Only the roads themselves endured, switchbacking down the black shoulders of the peaks, threading the passes that the border lords had sealed twice in Coral's lifetime and that the smugglers had reopened twice in the same span, because a sealed pass was just an unexploited market.

He knew these roads the way other men knew their own hands. Here, where the path narrowed above a drop full of pine-tops, you walked on the uphill side and trusted the stone, which had held for six hundred winters and saw no reason to fail on yours. There, at the split birch, you turned left where instinct screamed right, because right led into the hollow the locals called the Widow's Mouth and nobody who went in came out chatty about it. Far below, the white fleck of the falls at Greywater meant he was two hours from the toll-house at the pass, and the toll-house meant tollkeepers, and tollkeepers meant memories changing hands in the dark economy that ran beneath the coin economy like groundwater under a field.

Everyone paid the mountain something. That was the first law of Thornfell, and it was the only inheritance Coral had ever been certain of.

His breath ghosted. His boots found the rhythm of the grade. And because the road was long and the dark was honest, he let himself do the accounting.

Three hundred marks for the first love, once Vess Karo's people moved it down-country to a buyer in the free cities. Eighty for the second extraction — a soldier's last morning with his brothers before the ambush, purchased by the soldier himself, oddly enough, a man who wanted the weight of it carved out of him and was willing to go into debt to be lighter. Forty for the third, a child's face, a fever, a small grave under a rowan; those sold steadily, terribly, to mothers and fathers who could not carry them anymore. Coral did not judge them. Judgment was heavy, and he traveled light.

From the night's gross he subtracted the guild surgeon's tithe, the passage fee, the cost of the lacquered case and the velvet slots and the tincture of dreamless poppy that kept sleepers under while he worked. What remained was enough to winter on, if winters were all he needed it for. Which they were. Which was all they had ever been for.

That was the economy entire: memories moving through the mountains like water through limestone, surfacing in strange places, wearing new shapes. A baker in Dunmark remembered a lover she had never met. A border captain crossed his checkpoint carrying someone else's childhood in place of his own, smiling at the tollkeepers with borrowed warmth. And somewhere in the chain, always, stood Coral — source, courier, cutter — handling the raw stuff of other lives with hands so practiced they no longer trembled, selling everything, keeping nothing.

Because that was the rule. His rule. The one he had made at fifteen, bleeding on the floor of a smuggler's hold, and had never broken since, not once, not even when keeping would have cost him nothing and not keeping cost him more than marks could measure.

*Never keep anything for yourself.* A kept memory was a liability. A kept memory could be traced, could be taxed, could be taken — and worse, it could *want*. A thief who kept things started choosing jobs to be near them. Started hesitating. Started, in the end, belonging — to a place, a grief, a hope — and belonging was the first door through which the mountain took you. Coral had watched it happen to better thieves than himself. He had watched it happen to Vess Karo's whole crew, one soft attachment at a time, until they were a family instead of a business, and then he had watched what families did to the ones who didn't fit.

He had been fifteen. The road had been this same road, or one like it. There had been rain, and a quarrel whose words he had deliberately let go — sold them, actually, cheaply, to a memory-broker in Greywater, just to be rid of their weight — and afterward there had been one set of bootprints going down the mountain and none coming back.

He did not keep the memory of it. But the rule itself he kept, the way a man keeps a scar without remembering the wound: structurally. In the architecture of him.

At the bottom of the switchbacks, where the road ran level through a corridor of pines, Coral stopped.

Something was wrong with the silence. Thornfell's midnight silence had a texture — wind, and under the wind the tick and settle of frost prying at stone — and tonight the texture had a hole in it, a stillness too complete, as if the forest were holding its breath around something. Coral stood in the dark with his hand inside his coat, on the case, on the seed of a dead girl's laughter, and listened until his ears rang.

Nothing came. Nothing moved. After a long time the wind resumed its work and the ordinary night closed back over the gap like water, and Coral told himself what he always told himself: that the mountain made noises, that hunger made imaginings, that a thief who flinched at stillness would soon flinch at shadows and soon after stop eating.

He walked on. Behind him, far up the slopes he had descended, a light appeared in a window where no cabin should have been — small, steady, the color of candleflame — and if Coral had turned he would have seen it burning in a clearing that the maps had never marked, in a house whose chimney smoked for no fire he had lit.

He did not turn. Turning was looking back, and looking back was keeping.

* * *

Greywater smelled of tallow and wet wool, as it always did, and its single inn was loud with the particular desperation of men stuck in a mountain town in the last weeks before the solstice, when the passes tightened and the prices climbed and everyone calculated whether they could afford to be caught on the wrong side of winter.

Coral sat in the corner with his back to the wall and his tea untouched and conducted business the way he preferred it: quickly, quietly, in cash.

Vess Karo's factor was a woman named Odile with a lazy eye and quick hands, and she counted out three hundred and eighty marks in mixed coin without once meeting his gaze properly, which suited them both. Around them the inn roared — a card game collapsing into accusation, a drover singing something mournful about a drowned girl — and none of it touched the small clear space where the trade happened.

"The Wren job," Odile said, weighing the last stack. "Clean?"

"Clean."

"No residue? He won't wake up knowing your face?"

"He'll wake up knowing less than he went to sleep with. That's the product." Coral swept the coins into his pouch. "I need word sent ahead. I want passage through the sealed passes at midwinter, if Vess is running them."

Odile's good eye narrowed. "Nobody runs the sealed passes at midwinter. The tollhouses go strange after solstice eve. Even Vess waits."

"Then Vess can charge me for waiting too. Tell her Coral will pay."

"Coral," Odile repeated, tasting the name the way one tastes a coin for counterfeit. "That's not a name, that's a trade description. You know what the crew calls you behind your back?"

"I imagine I don't want to."

"*The Empty Man.*" She shrugged, pocketing her ledger. "No offense meant. It's said with respect. A man with nothing on him can't be robbed."

Coral almost smiled. Almost — which for him was the whole expression. "Send the word. Greywater, then up the east road. I'll be home before the storms."

*Home*. The word came out of him smooth and hollow, a coin with the center filed away, meaning only the cabin: two rooms and a slate roof halfway up the eastern shoulder of Thornfell, chosen precisely because no road passed it and no neighbor's smoke stained its sky. He had lived there nine years among his locked cases and his velvet slots and his ledger of debts owed and collected, and in nine years he had learned the sound of his own solitude so completely that he no longer heard it, the way a man living beside a waterfall stops hearing water.

He left the inn before dawn. The snow had begun — fat, deliberate flakes, the solstice-approaching kind — and the eastern road climbed white and silent into the peaks, and Coral walked it with three hundred and eighty marks against his ribs and nothing else against anything, empty as a purse turned out, light as the dead.

It should have felt like freedom. It had felt like freedom, once. Now it felt like the space in the merchant's bed where the wife used to sleep: shaped, and vacant, and very cold.

He reached his own door at dusk, stamping snow from his boots, fumbling the iron key from inside his glove — and stopped.

There was a bundle on his doorstep.

Not a bundle. He saw that at once, and the seeing dropped through him like a stone through ice. Small feet, bare despite the storm, blue-white with cold but standing — *standing*, planted on his threshold with a strange stubborn grace. A child, a girl of six or seven, hair dark as wet slate, snow gathering on her shoulders unmelted as though the cold had claimed her already and was merely being polite about it. She was not crying. She was not shivering. She was watching his door with the patience of something that had been sent, rather than lost.

And as Coral froze with his key half-turned in the lock, the child looked up at him and spoke.

The language was nothing he knew. It was nothing anyone he had ever fenced memories from had known — older than the border lords' tongue, older than the church cadences, older than the roads under his feet. It sounded the way the mountain's midnight silences felt. The candles through his window guttered, though the door was shut, though the wind came from the wrong quarter entirely.

In her small fist, held out to him like a summons, was a single page torn from a ledger — old paper, brown-edged, covered in a spidery column of figures and names.

And at the bottom, in the witness line, in handwriting Coral did not remember and yet recognized the way one recognizes one's own gait in a distant stranger:

his own. A child's version of it. Twelve years old at the oldest.

Coral stood on his threshold with the storm at his back and the impossible page in front of him, and for the first time in nineteen years, the Empty Man was afraid of what he might, after all, have kept.
