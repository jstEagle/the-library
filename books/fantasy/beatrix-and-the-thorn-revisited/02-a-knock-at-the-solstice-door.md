## 2. A Knock at the Solstice Door

The storm came down the Thornfell passes like something with intent.

Coral Zhou had watched a hundred storms gather in these mountains and knew their moods the way a moneylender knows his debtors' faces — which ones would bluster and break by morning, which ones meant to collect. This one meant to collect. It arrived at dusk on the third day of the solstice dark, riding the col between the twin peaks above his cabin, and it began tearing the frozen rime from the pines in long white sheets before the last light was gone.

He should have been asleep. He had sold a memory that afternoon — a cooper's grief over a drowned son, purchased cheap in Dunmark and resold dear to a widow in the high valley who collected other women's sorrows the way some collected spoons — and the transaction always left him thin, scraped-out, wanting things he could not name. But he sat instead at his table with a candle burning down between his fingers, taking inventory of himself the way he did on nights when the wind talked too loudly.

One satchel of wares under the floorboard. Eleven memories, wrapped in oiled cloth: a first kiss harvested from a dying miller; the smell of a harbor city no map named anymore; twenty years of a shepherd's summers compressed into something small enough to swallow. None of them his. That was the rule, and the rule had kept him alive where belonging had killed better men. Coral owned nothing that could be taken from him, and so nothing owned him in return.

The candle guttered.

Not the way candles gutter in drafts — this was different, a flattening of the flame as if a great weight had pressed down upon it, slow and deliberate, the fire bowing to something entering the room. Coral looked up. The shutter rattled once against its latch and went still, though the storm outside had not lessened. The silence inside the cabin had a texture to it now, dense and listening.

Then the knock.

Three knocks, spaced evenly, delivered at the height of his door rather than down where a child's fist would reach. Except no grown hand made that sound. It was too light, too patient. Knocks like snow falling on wood.

Coral did not move for the space of ten heartbeats. In his trade you learned that visitors after dark in the solstice weeks came in three varieties: thieves, who could be bargained with; revenants of old debts, who could be lied to; and things from below the mountain, which could only be survived. He picked up the iron poker by the hearth — not for the weight of it, but because cold iron remembered being forged, and remembering things confused what came out of the deep places — and crossed the room.

He opened the door on the storm and on the child standing in it.

She was perhaps eight years old, small-boned and pale as a birch cutting, barefoot on his threshold with snow settling on her shoulders and not melting. Her hair was dark and soaked through, plastered to her skull, and her eyes were the color of the ice on the high tarns — not blue exactly, but the memory of blue, the color water leaves behind when it gives up warmth. She looked up at him without fear, without shivering, without any of the things a child standing barefoot in a killing storm ought to be doing, and she spoke.

The language made the candles gutter again. All of them — the one on the table, the stub by the bed, the lantern in the corner he'd forgotten to snuff — every flame in the cabin bent flat at once, as though the words themselves had weight and the fire wanted no part of carrying them. It was not a language Coral knew, and yet it was not wholly strange. There were shapes in it. Under the strangeness ran something older than strangeness, the cadence of words said before there were mouths to say them, and the back of his neck prickled with a recognition he refused to have.

"I don't understand you," he said, and was annoyed to hear how carefully his voice came out.

The girl tilted her head. Snow slid from her cheek. She did not repeat herself. Instead she reached into the front of her threadbare coat — slowly, deliberately, the way you reach into your coat when you want the man with the poker to see both your hands — and drew out a folded page, soft with handling, and held it up to him.

Paper. Real paper, not bark-cloth, not the wax tablets the valley folk used. Paper was expensive this far into the Thornfells, and ledger paper more expensive still, ruled faintly in columns that had been half-erased. Someone had written over the erasures.

"Where did you get this?" Coral asked.

She pressed it into his hand. Her fingers were cold as river stones but not dead-cold, and for one disorienting moment he felt something pass between their skins — a flicker, a tug, the sensation he knew best in all the world, the feeling of a memory straining toward a thief's touch like a dog toward a whistle. The girl flinched. So did he.

He shut the door against the storm, because whatever else she was, she was a puzzle, and puzzles were safer indoors. He steered her to the hearth with a hand hovering near her shoulder, not touching, and wrapped her in the grey blanket from his cot. She accepted this arrangement with the grave courtesy of an ambassador accepting terms, sat down close to the fire, and watched him with those tarn-water eyes while he held the page to the light.

The writing on it was cramped, slanted leftward, the letters of someone who had learned to write in a hurry and in poor light. A column of figures. A name — *Beatrix* — written twice, once in the confident hand of an adult, once smaller beneath it, practicing the letters, the second *x* trailing off in a flourish like a bird's foot. Below that, lines in the smaller hand, a childish attempt at copying a formal contract, and then a phrase in the same small hand but steadier, written as if from memory:

*witnessed and bound, the debt descends to the issue.*

And then, at the bottom, a signature. Not Beatrix. Not her parents, if the two names above were her parents. The signature read:

*Coral.*

His own name, in handwriting he knew the way you know your own face in a mirror — except that he had never learned to write his name. He was sure of it. Sure the way he was sure of the rule about owning nothing, sure the way he was sure of nothing else in his life: that his childhood was a blank page, that he had come up the smuggler roads out of nowhere at eleven years old with empty pockets and an empty head, that Vess Karo had found him half-frozen at the treeline and asked him his name and he had answered *Coral* because it was the only word he had left and he did not know where he'd gotten it.

But his hand knew that signature. His hand itched to take up a pen and reproduce it stroke for stroke, the little hesitation before the *r*, the way the final letter hooked downward as if ducking away from the rest of the name. You could not counterfeit knowledge like that. You could not steal your own handwriting from yourself.

The fire popped. Outside, the storm screamed against the eaves like something cheated.

Coral became aware that the girl was watching him read her own name, and that her expression had changed. Not fear. Something worse. Recognition running both directions, the look of someone confirming a face they'd been promised long ago.

"You know me," he said. It came out as an accusation.

She shook her head slowly. Then she pointed one small finger — first at the signature, then at him, then, with terrible economy, at herself. She opened her mouth and spoke again in that guttering tongue, and this time he caught it, or thought he caught it: the shape of his own name buried in the stream of syllables like a stone in a riverbed, worn smooth, familiar to her mouth.

Then she yawned — a small, ordinary, unbearably childlike yawn — curled up on the hearthstone inside the blanket, and fell asleep between one breath and the next, as though the entire journey through a solstice storm had been merely the tedious prelude to a warm floor.

Coral stood over her for a long time.

He examined the situation the way he examined a mark's sleeping mind: methodically, looking for the trap. A mute child — he understood now that she was mute, that the sounds she made were not speech proper but something else wearing speech's clothes — arrives at the one cabin in the Thornfells belonging to a memory-thief, carrying a ledger page signed in his own lost handwriting, on the eve of the winter solstice, walking barefoot through a storm that would have killed a grown smuggler in an hour. Coincidence was possible. He had built a career on the assumption that coincidence was possible, the way other men built faith on scripture.

But the poker was still in his hand, and cold iron remembered being forged, and just now it was remembering something else too — a faint hum, the way it hummed near the sealed gates in the low passes, the ones the smugglers swore were older than the kingdoms and led *down*. The girl slept on peacefully within arm's reach of it, and the iron did not so much as whisper in her direction. Whatever she was, the iron approved of her. That frightened him more than the signature did.

He set the poker down and returned to the table and read the page again by candlelight, and the longer he read, the more the unease in his chest curdled into something with a name. Because the figures in the column — he counted them twice — resolved, when you knew how to look, into a price. He had spent fifteen years learning to read prices in every script and cipher the mountain trade used. This was not a price in coin. It was a price in what coin stands in for. Time. Years. A soul measured out in installments, debited season by season, and the balance remaining was small. The final entry was dated in a calendar he did not recognize, but the arithmetic was plain enough to a man who had spent his life counting other people's pasts:

Something belonging to this child would run out soon. Before spring. Perhaps before the solstice fires burned down.

And the witness line — *witnessed and bound* — carried his name like a brand carries a ranch's mark.

Coral looked at the sleeping child in his blanket, at the snowmelt darkening her hair, at the small hands tucked under her chin, and felt the loneliness of fifteen years shift its weight inside him like ballast in a hull. He had a rule about this. He had rules about everything precisely so that on nights like this he would not have to think. *Own nothing. Belong to no one. Take what you're paid and leave before the debts wake up.* The rules had carried him through the deaths of two crews, the betrayal of Vess Karo, the slow gray attrition of a trade that ate its practitioners alive. The rules had never once had to account for a barefoot girl whose soul was mortgaged in a language older than kingdoms, witnessed by a version of himself he could not remember being.

The candle beside him guttered a third time, and this time it did not recover. The flame bent, bowed, and went out, and in the dark the cabin smelled suddenly and impossibly of candlewax and cold stone and ink — the smell, though he had no memory to attach to it and did not understand why his body reacted as it did, of somewhere very far underground, and very full of shelves.

In her sleep, the girl murmured. One word. It was not in any living tongue, but he heard his own name inside it again, patient as a seed, waiting.

Coral sat down in the dark across from her, the impossible ledger page trembling very slightly in his hand, and understood that the storm outside was the least of what had arrived at his door tonight.

Whatever had been done to this child had been done with his consent — a consent he could not remember giving, in a life he could not remember living, by a hand that was undeniably, unmistakably his own.

He had until the solstice to find out why.
