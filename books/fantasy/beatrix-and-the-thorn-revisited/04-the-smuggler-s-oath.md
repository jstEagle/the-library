## 4. The Smuggler's Oath

The road to Vess Karo's camp was a road that pretended not to exist.

Coral had walked it twice before in his life — once as a boy of eleven, dragged by the collar through the fog-line with a smuggler's hand fisted in his coat, and once at seventeen, walking the other direction, cast out into the snow with nothing but the clothes he stood up in and a warning that if he ever came back, they'd take more than his boots.

Now the road was sealed. The passes above it had been closed since mid-autumn, when the tollkeepers of Thornfell began taking memories instead of coin, and every honest traveler had learned to go around, and every dishonest one had stopped traveling at all. But roads like this one didn't close so much as hold their breath. They waited for someone who knew how to knock.

Coral knocked.

Three raps on the hollow pine trunk that served as the camp's gate-post — two high, one low, a rhythm as old as his hands. He heard the silence change quality behind the palisade. Somewhere a dog did not bark, which meant someone had told the dog not to bark for him, which meant someone had been expecting him, or at least expecting *someone*, and had guessed wrong or guessed right and hadn't decided yet.

"Name," said a voice through the wood. Young. Nervous.

"You know my name."

"That's not what I asked."

He almost smiled. They were training them better these days. "Coral Zhou," he said. "Memory-thief. Late of Dunmark road. I'm here to see the captain."

A long pause. Then, muffled but distinct, the sound of a young man deciding very hard not to run and get someone, and losing.

* * *

They made him wait, because Vess Karo always made people wait, and because the waiting itself was a kind of reading. She'd taught him that once, sitting cross-legged on a crate of untaxed brandy, telling him: *You learn more from a man in an hour of making him stand still than you ever will from talking to him. Stillness shows you where a man keeps his fear.* He had been nine years old and had thought she meant it as wisdom. Later he understood it was also a warning about her.

So he sat still in the mud-and-snow courtyard while the camp went about its business around him with the studied indifference of people who had been ordered to look busy. He catalogued what the stillness showed him: fewer fires than there should be, this time of year. Fewer horses. A new guard on the wall who kept touching the pouch at her belt the way people touch things they've recently paid too much for. The smugglers were bleeding. Whatever the tollkeepers were doing at the pass, it was costing Karo's crew their cargo, their routes, maybe their people. Which meant Vess would be hungry for leverage, and hungry people made bad bargains, and bad bargains were the only kind worth bringing to Vess Karo, because good bargains insulted her.

The cabin door opened at dusk.

She looked older. That was the first thing, and he hated that it was the first thing — hated that some orphaned scrap of himself still measured her against the woman who had pulled him out of a slave caravan's tail-end and fed him smoked fish on a mountaintop until he stopped flinching at loud noises. Her hair had gone iron at the temples. Her left hand wore a ring he didn't recognize, carved from black horn, and he recognized *that* immediately for what it was: memory-jewelry. Someone had paid her in something precious, and she had worn the proof where her enemies could see it.

"Coral." She said it the way you'd say the name of weather coming in. "You've got nerve, I'll give you that."

"I've got coin, too, but I hear that's not legal tender anymore."

"It isn't." She stepped aside, not quite an invitation. More like moving out of the doorway before the cold decided to come in without her permission. "Come in before my people start thinking I'm soft."

* * *

The cabin smelled the way it always had: pitch-pine smoke, tallow, old leather, and underneath it all the faint metallic tang of stored memories — the smell of a strongroom full of things that used to belong to other people. Coral felt his own craft rise up in him like a hound scenting blood, cataloguing without permission. The horn ring: grief, recent, hers. The lacquered box on the mantel: something old, something she never opened. The knife on the table between them, laid casually across the bread board: not casual at all.

He sat down across from her and put Beatrix's ledger page on the table, face down, under his palm.

Vess poured two cups of plum wine without asking. That, too, was a message. In the old days she'd have poured for herself alone and let him beg.

"You've been doing well for yourself," she said. "I hear things. A thief on the Dunmark road who sells first kisses back to men who forgot them. Who charges merchants triple to return their own mothers' faces." She drank. "You found the one trade where being heartless is a professional qualification."

"You taught me everything except how to be heartless. That I managed on my own."

Something moved behind her eyes — quick, buried, gone. He'd seen that movement before, in the moment just before she'd put him out of the camp seven years ago, the flicker of a woman swallowing something large. He had spent seven years telling himself it had been regret. He had spent the last year suspecting it had been arithmetic.

"Why are you here?" she said. "Not the pretty version. Not whatever speech you rehearsed on the walk up. Why."

Coral turned the ledger page over and slid it across the table.

He watched her read it — watched the professional stillness she'd taught him fail her for exactly two seconds, no more. Her thumb moved toward the signature block and stopped short, hovering, the way a man's hand stops short of a snake.

"This is Undercourt work," she said quietly. "This is *deep* Undercourt work."

"The girl it belongs to is standing in my kitchen eating soup. She has until solstice. After that, what's left of her goes under the mountain and doesn't come back up."

"And you want passage through the sealed passes. Down to the sunken gate." Vess set the page down with exaggerated care, as if it might bruise. "Through my pass. Past my tollkeepers. Using my roads, in winter, when the mountain eats one crew in five even in a good year."

"Yes."

"No."

"Vess—"

"*No.*" It came out flat and final, a door closing. She stood, walked to the window, stood looking out at the darkening yard with her back to him, and Coral understood suddenly that she could not say it facing him. "Do you know what happened the last time someone brought Undercourt business to this camp? Do you know why your parents—" She caught herself. He saw her catch herself. "Why *some people* came through here twenty years ago, fleeing down-mountain, and why after they passed through, half my crew started dreaming in a language nobody speaks, and why I swore an oath on my own name never to ferry another soul toward that gate?"

"No," Coral said. "But I'm going to guess it involves payment."

Vess laughed. It was a terrible sound, dry as a snapped bone. "You always were too clever for your own keeping." She turned around. "Yes. It involved payment. And the payment was taken, and the debt was marked settled, and I have spent twenty years paying interest on it anyway. So no, Coral. Find another road. There isn't one, but find it anyway."

"There's the low tunnel under Graywater Crag. It floods."

"It floods."

"There's the pilgrim stair. The monks don't turn travelers away."

"The monks haven't eaten since the tolls began. They'll turn travelers away with rocks." She came back to the table and leaned on it, and now he could see what the stillness had been hiding all evening: not anger. Fear. Real fear, worn smooth by years of carrying. "You're asking me to break the only oath I've kept since I broke the ones that mattered. You know that, don't you? You walked up my mountain knowing exactly which scar to press."

"I know," Coral said softly. "It's what you trained me to do."

The silence stretched between them, taut as a wire over a drop. Outside, snow began again, ticking against the shutters. Somewhere in the camp a horse complained, and a man's voice murmured it quiet.

Then Vess sat down slowly, like an old woman, though she wasn't one yet, and looked at him across the candleflame with something that was not quite hatred and had never quite been anything else.

"One condition," she said. "One, and then we talk routes."

"Name it."

"Not coin. Coin's worthless. Not favors — I've had enough of those from the Undercourt to last me into the ground." She reached beneath the table and brought up a small lead casket, unmarked, heavy-looking, and set it beside the wine. "Payment in kind. The way the mountain takes it now. You pay me a memory, Coral. One of yours. I choose which offer I accept, and I keep it, and it stays mine, and you don't ask for it back."

His stomach dropped through the floorboards of himself. He kept his face perfectly still — the old lesson, the first lesson, *stillness shows where a man keeps his fear* — and inside the stillness he was running through his inventory with cold, sick speed. What did he have? Twenty years of stolen wares, yes, but those weren't *his*; selling those to Vess was just moving stock, and she'd know it, and she'd refuse, and worse, she'd know he thought she wouldn't notice the difference. No. She'd demand something native. Something grown in his own soil rather than lifted from another man's field.

And his own soil was nearly barren. That was the whole architecture of his life. He kept nothing. That was the rule, the law, the load-bearing wall of who he was: *the thief keeps nothing, and so nothing can be taken from him.*

Almost nothing.

"You understand what you're asking," he said. His voice came out level. He was distantly proud of that.

"I know exactly what I'm asking." Vess's eyes were steady and pitiless and, underneath, pleading. *Give me a reason not to do this,* the look said. *Make it easy to refuse you.* "I'm asking you to trust me with a piece of yourself, after everything. That's the price. That was always the price, and you've spent your whole life making sure you'd never have to pay it. Well. Here's the tollbooth, boy."

He looked at the lead casket. Memory-lead, mined from the deep seams, the only metal that held what it held without leaking. He'd sold a dozen of them over the years. He'd never once been on the paying side of one.

"What are my options," he said.

"Anything true. Anything yours. Not a purchase, not a job, not something you lifted off some drover on the Dunmark road." She ticked them off with bitter precision, showing him she'd already imagined his evasions. "Your first kill. Your worst night. The face of whoever you loved before you decided love was inventory." A pause, delicate as a blade turning. "Whatever you're keeping under that rule of yours. The thing you never sell. Every thief keeps one, Coral. Even you. Especially you."

*The sound of my mother singing.*

It rose in him unbidden, whole and warm and terrible: a kitchen in a house he could not otherwise picture, firelight he could not otherwise place, a voice singing syllables in a language older than any kingdom, and himself small, safe, held. The single possession of his entire adult life. The one thing he had never priced, never weighed, never let so much as flicker across the surface of his mind during a negotiation, because it was the keystone, and pull it and everything called Coral Zhou comes down.

He could feel it now, sitting behind his ribs like a coal. And he could feel Vess watching him feel it, because she had taught him everything except how to be heartless, and she knew precisely where the coal was buried.

"No," he said.

Her face didn't move. "No?"

"Not that one." He was surprised by his own voice — surprised that it shook and steadied at once, both things, the way a rope shakes and holds. "Ask me for anything else I own and it's yours. My first kill. The worst night. Every face I ever loved before I got careful. Take the lot. But not that one."

Vess sat back. For a long moment she said nothing at all, and the snow ticked, and the candle burned, and Coral sat in the wreckage of the only rule he'd ever kept and discovered that the rule had been holding up more than he knew. Because if he surrendered the song, then what remained? A man with no origin, no childhood, no name he trusted, no mother's voice — a hollow suit of competence walking around selling other people's pasts because he couldn't afford his own. He'd known that, abstractly, for years. He had never *felt* it until now, feeling it approach.

"You'd really give me the rest," Vess said slowly. "All of it. Everything except that."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Because it's the last proof I was ever loved, he did not say. Because a man can rebuild a self out of almost anything except the certainty that no one ever sang for him. Because you of all people should understand that some things are not currency, and if you don't, then this camp, this oath, this whole gray economy of ours is already dead and the Undercourt is just waiting for us to notice.

"Because I need something to come back for," is what he said.

Vess looked at him for a long, long time. Then she laughed — a different laugh, short and rough, startled out of her. "Gods. You finally said one true thing in this house. Took you twenty years." She pushed the lead casket aside and pulled the ledger page back toward herself, studying it with new eyes, merchant's eyes, route-planner's eyes. "Fine. Keep your song. We'll call it collateral against good behavior. You give me the worst night instead — the one you're thinking of right now, don't pretend you're not — and passage for two through the sealed passes, down to the sunken gate, no questions, no oaths broken that anyone can prove."

"The worst night," Coral repeated. He knew the one she meant. He'd known it was on the table the moment she'd named the category, because there was only one candidate and it had her fingerprints all over it: the night at seventeen, the snow, the collar, the gate, the words she'd said to him as she put him out. He would be selling her the memory of his own banishment — handing her, forever, the experience of casting him out from the inside, with all its textures and all its costs. If there was any regret buried in her, she'd have to live inside it now, nightly, permanently. It was a cruel payment. It was possibly the cruelest thing he'd ever offered anyone, and he had once sold a bishop his own funeral.

Vess met his eyes and read the shape of it, because she'd taught him that too, and something in her jaw tightened.

"Fair," she said hoarsely. "That's fair, and we both know it, and I hate you for it. Put your hands on the casket."

He did. The lead was cold, colder than the room warranted, drinking warmth the way deep stone drinks light. Vess laid her palm over his — the first time she had touched him since the gate, since the snow, seven years ago — and spoke three words in the toll-tongue, the language of the mountain's new economy, and Coral felt the memory begin to leave him.

It went gently. That was the horror of it. It didn't tear; it *poured*, like water finding a crack, and as it went he felt the edges of the night soften first — the smell of the snow going vague, the sound of the gate hinge thinning to a whisper — and then the center following, the cold, the collar, her voice saying what she'd said, all of it draining out of him into the lead box like blood from a opened vein, and he kept his face still, perfectly still, the first and best lesson, while the worst night of his life became property.

When it was done, Vess took her hand back. She was breathing carefully. She had lived the memory just now from the inside — he knew how the transfer worked — and whatever she had found in it, she folded away behind her face and did not show him.

"It's done," she said. Her voice was not entirely level. "Passage for two. We leave tomorrow, before light. There's a guide — Pike's nephew, a boy called Wren who knows the sealed stretches." She stood, and paused, and did not look at him. "And Coral. The thing you kept. The song."

He said nothing.

"When this is over," Vess said, still not looking at him, "if you come back up this mountain alive — you should know it wasn't a debt that put you out that gate. Ask me again someday, when you've got more than a song left to lose, and I might tell you whose signature was on *your* paper."

She left the room. The candle guttered in the draft of the closing door, and outside the snow went on falling, and Coral sat alone in the cabin where he had been raised and ruined, listening to the faint, fading warmth where his worst night used to live — and turning over, again and again, the one sentence she had left behind, which sounded almost exactly like the opening line of a contract.
