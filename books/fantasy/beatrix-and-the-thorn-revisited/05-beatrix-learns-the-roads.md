## 5. Beatrix Learns the Roads

Before the mountain, there was the road.

Beatrix had not known roads could be so long. In the place she came from — the before-place, the warm-place whose name sat in her chest like a stone in a streambed, worn smooth but still heavy — distance had been measured in other things. In candle-lengths. In how many verses of a lullaby it took to cross a room. Distance had been something you could hold.

The road through the Thornfell passes could not be held. It spilled out of Coral's cabin door like water finding its level, winding down between black pines and up again over shoulders of grey stone, and Beatrix walked it with her bare feet learning a new alphabet: frost-soft mud, needle-litter, stone that remembered the sun even at dusk. Vess Karo had given her boots on the second morning, cracked leather that smelled of tar and old voyages, but Beatrix took them off when the captain wasn't looking. She wanted to keep reading the ground. The ground was honest. It said *cold* and said *stone* and said *here*, and none of those words lied to her the way people's faces did when they looked at her too long with their pity folded up small behind their eyes.

She had no voice. She had known this for as long as she knew anything, which was not very long, which was everything. Her memory began the night she knocked on Coral's door, and everything before that night was a locked room with the key turned from the inside. She did not say this to herself in words. She said it in the old tongue, the one that lived under her ribs and rose out of her hands when she dreamed — the language of guttering candles and bending shadows, the language that made Coral go pale and very still whenever he caught her signing it in her sleep.

But the old tongue had no word for *road*. That was strange. A language older than any kingdom, Coral's books said — she had watched him mouth the words from his one battered volume while pretending to read something else — and yet it could not say the simplest thing: *this way, then that way, then home.*

So Beatrix began to steal.

Not with her hands. With whatever it was that lived behind her sternum, the thing that reached out without being asked, the thing that made dogs love her and horses calm and Candlewax the mule stop biting his own flank. When travelers passed them on the smuggler roads — peddlers with bells on their carts, pilgrims walking the low passes with ash smeared on their foreheads, couriers who moved like knives — Beatrix would look at them, and their stray thoughts would come loose like burrs and stick to her.

That was how she learned the word for *road*: from a pie-seller's mind, where it lay tangled up with the smell of grease and the memory of his mother saying *stay off the roads after dark, Toma, the mountain takes what walks unwatched.* The word arrived whole, with grease and mother attached, and Beatrix turned it over in her head all afternoon like a coin she'd found in the dirt. *Road.* She tried it against the old tongue and found they fit together the way two stones fit — not seamlessly, but enough to build with.

By the third day she had *pass*, *ridge*, *cleft*, *cold* (which she'd had already, in the old tongue, three different ways depending on whether the cold was kind or cruel), and *hungry*, learned from everyone at once, because the whole company was always hungry.

Coral noticed first. Of course he did. He noticed everything; it was his trade, noticing what people kept.

They were camped in the lee of a fallen fir, and Vess's crew — five of them, quiet people with rope-burned hands — were arguing over a map by firelight. The map was old and soft as cloth, and it disagreed with itself, and Pike — no, not Pike, not yet, Pike was still a week away and a city distant — the tall one called Ferren kept stabbing his finger at a junction and saying the pass had collapsed years ago.

"The map says left at the split rock," Ferren insisted.

"The map is older than my regrets," Vess said, "and I don't trust either."

Beatrix, sitting at the edge of the light with her knees drawn up, looked at the map and felt the mountain lean down and whisper into her ear.

*Left,* the whisper said, in no language at all. *Then down through the throat-stone. Then the long dark where the candles burn green. Then up.*

Her hand moved before she decided anything. Two fingers, tracing the air above the map: left at the split rock, down, along, up. And then, without meaning to, her fingers went further — past the edge of the map, into the blank vellum where nothing was drawn, and drew three more turns that no mapmaker had bothered to record, ending in a small closed spiral like a snail shell. Like an ear. Like a vault door seen from inside.

The fire popped. Everyone was looking at her.

"How does she know that?" Ferren asked, in the tone of a man asking how a fish knows water. "There's nothing there. There's never been anything there."

"She's been walking these roads three days," Vess said slowly. Her eyes were narrow and bright, the eyes of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like. "Three days, and she draws the switchback below Grey Cairn that isn't on any chart I've ever bought or stolen."

Coral crouched beside Beatrix. He did this sometimes — came down to her level as though height were a courtesy he could offer. His face was careful, the face of a man approaching a sleeping animal, or a debt.

"Beatrix," he said. He had learned to say her name gently, as if it might break. "Have you walked these roads before?"

She shook her head. It was true. It was also a lie, and both truths and the lie lived in her at once, uncomfortable bedfellows, and she didn't have the words — in any tongue — to explain the difference. She had not walked these roads. But some part of her had *been* walked *by* them, the way a river walks its bed. The knowledge wasn't memory. Memory would have edges, a where and a when. This was smoother than memory. This was the shape of knowing without the story of it, like knowing how to swallow without remembering learning.

She put her hand flat on the map instead, over the blank space where her fingers had drawn the spiral, and thought *down* at it as hard as she could, in every language she owned.

Coral went very still.

"Under," he said quietly. "You're telling me the roads go under."

* * *

After that, the company began to watch her differently. Not with fear — or not only fear. Ferren started leaving the first bite of his supper near her bedroll, the way you'd feed a luck-charm. The youngest smuggler, a wiry girl named Sefa who chewed pine pitch and said maybe forty words a day, took to walking beside Beatrix and narrating the terrain aloud, deliberately, like a tutor: *"Scree slope, mind your ankles. Stream crossing, cold enough to crack teeth."* Sefa thought she was being subtle. Beatrix drank the words like rain.

And oh, the words. That was the wonder of it, the part that made her want to weep if she let herself feel it fully, which she did not, because feeling things fully felt dangerous, felt like opening a door she couldn't close. Every traveler was a library walking. A shepherd gave her eleven kinds of weather. A funeral procession climbing toward a cliff-side cairn gave her grief, and also the beautiful useless word for the moss that grew on the north side of graves. A pair of rival trappers, arguing in whispers, gave her the entire grammar of accusation.

The old tongue stayed underneath it all, patient as groundwater. And Beatrix noticed — she could not help noticing — that the more common words she gathered, the more clearly she understood what the old tongue was *for*. It wasn't made for naming roads and pies and accusations. Those were surface words, weather words. The old tongue was made for deeper strata. For binding. For keeping. For saying a thing so precisely that the thing could not afterward be unsaid.

She knew this the way she knew the turns beneath Grey Cairn. Without knowing how.

On the fifth night, camped in a hanging valley where the wind made organ-notes of the rocks, Beatrix dreamed of stairs.

In the dream she descended them forever. They were cut from black stone that drank the lantern-light, and the walls on either side were lined — not with shelves, not with coffins, but with *people*, standing in niches, motionless, their eyes open and lit faintly from within like paper lanterns. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. And each one murmured, endlessly, in the old tongue, reciting — and Beatrix understood in the dream that they were reciting themselves, that each soul in its niche was the only copy of its own book, and that the murmuring was the sound of a kingdom remembering.

She woke with frost on her eyelashes and her hands signed halfway through a sentence in the old tongue, a sentence she could read in the moonlight on the backs of her own fingers:

*I know which niche is mine.*

She stared at her hands for a long time. Then she closed them, folded the sentence shut like a fan, and pressed her folded hands against her sternum, over the smooth shapeless knowing, and did not sleep again.

* * *

The sixth day brought them to Grey Cairn, and the split rock, and the turn.

It was exactly as she had drawn it. Left at the split rock — a boulder cloven clean as bread — and then down through a defile so narrow the sky became a ribbon, and then the long dark where — and here Beatrix stopped walking so suddenly that Sefa bumped into her back.

The candles burned green.

They weren't supposed to be candles at all. They were lichens, Sefa said, catching up, foxfire, coldlight, perfectly natural, grew on the wet stone in every deep pass in Thornfell. But Beatrix stood in the mouth of the defile and looked at the pale-green flames running along the walls into the dark, row upon row upon row, dwindling into distance like a procession, and her body knew them. Not her mind. Her body. Her skin prickled with the memory of standing at attention among them. Her lungs remembered air that tasted of wax and stone dust and the particular hush of a place where speaking aloud was forbidden.

*The long dark where the candles burn green.*

She had drawn those words in the air over a map four days before she ever saw this place. Had known the turns. Had known the *smell*.

"Beatrix?" Coral's voice, behind her. Careful. "What is it?"

She couldn't answer. She had no voice, and besides, what was there to say — *I have been here, I have never been here, my feet are afraid of a road they've never walked?* Instead she lifted her hand and pointed down the defile, at the green procession of lights, and then she pointed at herself, and then she made the sign she hated most in all the old tongue, the one that had crawled out of her dreaming fingers two nights ago, the one for a niche in a wall.

Sefa sucked air through her teeth. Behind Beatrix, someone swore softly.

Coral knelt beside her. His eyes were the color of the dark between the candles, and in them she saw something that frightened her worse than the defile did: recognition. Not of the place. Of *her*. As if he had finally matched a face to a name he'd been carrying since the night she knocked.

"You've been below," he said. Barely a breath. "Haven't you. You haven't just been sold to them. You've *served* them. You remember it somewhere your head can't reach."

Beatrix looked at him. And because she was tired, and cold, and because the green light was pulling at her like a tide, and because some small traitor part of her was *glad* — glad to be understood, even in this, even in terror — she nodded.

Then she pointed down the dark, and down the mountain beyond it, toward the solstice that waited at the bottom of the winter like a stone at the bottom of a well. And she signed, slowly, so that even Ferren could follow:

*Faster. I am fading faster here.*

Because that was true too, and she hadn't known it until the candles showed her. Every mile closer to the Undercourt, the smooth shapeless knowing grew heavier and the rest of her grew lighter, thinner, more like lantern-paper. Something down there was reeling her in. Gently. Patiently. The way a fisherman who owns the river doesn't need to hurry.

Vess appeared at the front of the column, and Beatrix saw the captain look at the green lights, and then at the child, and then make a decision the way other people swallowed bitter medicine — whole, quickly, without tasting it.

"We move now," Vess said. "Double pace through the defile. Nobody speaks below the second bend — and I mean nobody, not a prayer, not a curse, not a sneeze. Some places count voices." She paused, and her jaw tightened around something old. "And some places keep them."

They went down into the long dark, single file, silent as thieves — which most of them were — and the green flames leaned toward Beatrix as she passed, each one, all of them, like ten thousand small bowed heads greeting a returning queen.

Or a returning prisoner.

Behind them, far above at the mouth of the pass, the tollkeepers of the high checkpoint lit their lamps and began, in their hollow voices, to call out the toll.
