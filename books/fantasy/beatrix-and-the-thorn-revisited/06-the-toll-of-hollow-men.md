## 6. The Toll of Hollow Men

The pass called the Needle's Eye had swallowed better travelers than us, and it let us know it.

Three days out of Vess's camp the road narrowed to a shelf of black stone with the mountain rising sheer on one side and a drop so deep the clouds drifted below us like slow white fish. The wind came off the icefields with a sound like a blade being drawn, endlessly, and never finished. Beatrix rode in the supply cart with the canvas drawn around her, and every hour or so I checked on her the way a man checks a coin purse he suspects is lighter than it should be.

She was lighter. I could feel it. Not in her body — she ate what we gave her, slept without nightmares that I could see — but in some other weight she carried, the way a jar half-emptied still sloshes differently than a full one. The solstice was nineteen days away. Each morning I did the arithmetic without wanting to, and each morning the number was smaller, and each morning I hated the number for existing.

Vess rode point. She had not spoken to me in anything but commands since the memory-taking — *water the mules, bank the fire, wake the watch* — and I had not asked her to. The place where she'd drawn that memory out of me ached in a way I had no anatomy for, a smoothness where a roughness used to be. I kept reaching for it the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth, and kept finding nothing, and kept being surprised by the nothing, which is the cruelest part of any theft: you forget what was taken and remember only that you are poorer.

* * *

We smelled the checkpoint before we saw it. Woodsmoke and something under the woodsmoke — a smell I knew from my trade, from the back rooms where memory-brokers worked their tallow and iron. Burned rosemary and cold iron and, faintly, the ozone tang of a mind being opened. It is a smell like a struck match inside your own skull. Once you know it, you never stop knowing it.

The tollhouse sat where the shelf-road widened into a natural courtyard, walled in by the mountain on three sides. It had been a smuggler waystation once — I recognized the iron rings in the rock where caravans used to tie off, the old Vess-codes scratched into the lintel, half-chiseled away. Now a palisade of blackthorn stakes closed the gap, and lanterns of green glass hung from them, and behind the lanterns stood the tollkeepers.

There were six of them. They stood too still. That was the first thing, the thing that made the hair on my forearms lift under my gloves. People are never still; we sway, we shift, we breathe with our whole bodies. These six stood the way furniture stands. They wore grey cloaks with the hoods back, and their faces were —

I had seen faces like theirs before. In mirrors, sometimes, after a bad extraction. In the eyes of brokers who had spent too much of their own stock to pay their own debts. But never six at once, never arranged like a receipt waiting to be signed.

Their eyes were the worst. Not empty — emptiness I could have worked with, could have lied to, because emptiness sees nothing and therefore suspects nothing. These eyes were full. Full of everything they had ever taken, all at once, with nothing left to organize it into a self. A hoard with no hoarder. Looking into them was like looking into a purse turned inside out.

"Hollow men," Vess said, reining in beside the cart. She said it the way you'd say *rockslide* or *whiteout* — a weather, a fact of the mountain. "Six of them. That's a rich toll. Someone's been paying well to keep them fed."

"Fed," I said. "On what?"

"What do you think, boy?" She didn't look at me. "Memories. They take one from every traveler. Whatever they judge you can spare. You don't get to choose which. You don't get to argue. You pay, or you turn back, and no one turns back on the Needle's Eye in solstice season." She pulled her hood forward. "Rules are simple. Don't flinch when they come in. Flinching makes them curious, and curiosity makes them greedy. Don't look at their hands. And whatever they take — let it go clean. Fight it and they'll take two."

"And Beatrix?"

That made Vess turn. For a moment something moved behind her face — the same something that had moved when she took my memory, guilt or grief or the memory of having once been a person who felt both. "The child rides through," she said. "Whatever they see when they look at her, they won't want it. You'll understand when you understand, and I'd spare you that, but the mountain won't."

* * *

The hollow men did not speak. I had expected a demand, a ritual, some tollkeeper's catechology — *what do you carry, what have you loved, what will you surrender* — the theater that even the worst brokers dress their thefts in. There was none of that. The first one simply walked to the head of our line, unhurried, boots making no sound on the stone, and stopped in front of the lead mule, and waited.

Vess dismounted. She stood with her hands loose at her sides and her chin up, the posture of a woman who had done this many times and hated it in a place so deep the hate had gone quiet. The hollow man raised one hand. His fingers were long and grey-nailed and they did not touch her; they hovered an inch from her brow, and his eyes — I watched from the cart, and I could not look away, God forgive me, I could not stop watching — his eyes went still in a new way, a focusing way, the way a lens focuses.

And then Vess shuddered. One long shudder, head to heel, like a horse shedding water, and the hollow man's hand closed on nothing and drew back, and whatever he had taken went with it into those overfull eyes.

I never learned what it was. I never asked. That is a debt I owe her, one of the few I've managed to keep.

He moved down the line. The two drovers who'd hired on at Vess's camp paid next — I watched the younger one lose something that made him laugh afterward, a bright bewildered laugh, reaching for a joke that wasn't there anymore. The older drover wept without knowing why, wiped his face with a puzzled hand, and walked on. The hollow man took from each with the same terrible gentleness, the same economy. He did not savor. He did not linger. Taking was all he was; even savoring would have required a self to savor with.

Then it was my turn.

He stopped in front of me and the hovering fingers came up and I did the thing I do, the thing that has kept me alive in a hundred back rooms: I made my mind a shop with the goods arranged in the window. Here, take this — the taste of a wedding cake from a bride in Dunmark, secondhand, barely mine. Here — the color of a harbor at dawn, bought off a dying sailor, pretty and cheap. I had a whole inventory of borrowed brightness to offer, and I arranged it the way I arrange everything, and I kept my face still the way I keep my face still, and I waited to be robbed.

His eyes went into me.

I felt it as a pressure, then as a cold, then — and this is the part I will not dress up, because I am a thief and thieves know theft — as a violation. He was not browsing. He was *reading*. Turning my inventory over item by item, and then, worse, going past the window into the back rooms, past the stock, into the walls, looking for the thing under the things. Looking for what I actually was.

He found the hole where my childhood had been. I felt him find it, the way you feel a stranger's finger trace a scar. He paused there. Something moved in the ruin of his face — not recognition, nothing so kind. Appetite, maybe. Or the ghost of appetite, the way a burned house holds the ghost of a kitchen.

Then he drew back, and took the harbor at dawn, and left me the rest.

I stood there breathing. It should have been a relief — he'd taken a cheap one, a secondhand one, nothing that was mine. But my hands were shaking and I could not make them stop, because for one instant, in the moment of his reading, I had felt how it would be to be emptied. To be him. To have everything and be no one, a ledger with no name at the top.

I had spent my whole life believing that was a way to live. Standing in front of him, I learned it was only a way to stop.

* * *

Beatrix's turn came last.

She had climbed down from the cart on her own — I hadn't seen her move, that was another thing about her, she moved like a draft through a door — and she stood in front of the hollow man at her full height, which was not very much of one. She was small and pale and the wind pulled her hair sideways and she looked at him the way she looked at everything: directly, patiently, with those eyes the color of deep water under ice.

The hollow man raised his hand.

And I want to write that I did something. That I stepped between them, or shouted, or put a knife in his grey throat. I want to write that I was brave. What I actually did was stand frozen with my heart going like a drum in my ears, because Vess had said *whatever they see when they look at her, they won't want it*, and I did not yet understand what that meant, and some animal part of me understood it before my mind did and wanted no part of the understanding.

His fingers hovered at her brow. His eyes focused.

And then his eyes *unfocused*.

I saw it happen. I saw the lens of him slide off her like rain off glass. He reached for her — for whatever a child keeps in her head, the smell of her mother, the sound of her own name, the small hoard of days every child carries — and found almost nothing to grip. A few shreds. A few tatters. A soul like a coat worn to threads, with nothing left in the pockets.

He recoiled. Not in horror — horror would have required more of him than he had. He recoiled the way a hand recoils from a fire that has already burned down to ash: nothing to take, nothing to feel, nothing to want. His hand dropped. His head tilted, very slightly, the way a dog's head tilts at a sound it cannot place.

And then he stepped aside, and gestured her through with a small grey motion of his fingers, and Beatrix walked past him into the courtyard, and the other five hollow men turned their overfull eyes to watch her pass, all six of them, and not one of them reached for her.

She was waved through. Waved through like a beggar, like a ghost, like something already taken.

* * *

I found her on the far side of the palisade, sitting on a rock where the road bent down toward the long descent, her legs tucked under her, watching the clouds in the valley turn gold with the late light. I sat down next to her. I had a whole speech prepared — *it means nothing, child, it's just their way, they took from me too* — and it died in my throat, because she turned and looked at me, and she knew. Children always know. She knew exactly what had happened and exactly what it meant, and she was waiting to see if I would lie to her.

I didn't.

"You know what that was," I said quietly.

She nodded. Once. Slow.

"It means they couldn't take from you," I said, and heard even as I said it how the words bent themselves into a lie of comfort, and she heard it too, and looked at me with a patience that broke something in my chest. "It means —"

I stopped.

Because the truth arrived all at once, the way grief does, ahead of understanding, the way a wave arrives ahead of the thing that fell in the water. The truth was this: the hollow men took from everyone what they could spare. From Vess, something she'd survived. From the drovers, small bright things they'd miss without knowing. From me, a dawn I'd bought for three coppers. They took the *spare*. They took the margin, the surplus, the extra weight a self carries above what it needs to keep standing.

And from Beatrix they had taken nothing, because there was nothing to take. The Undercourt had already collected. Her parents' debt had already been paid out of her, year by year, night by night, in a currency I was only beginning to name — and what sat beside me on the rock in the gold light was the change remaining. The coins left in the coat after the robbers went through the house.

She wasn't spared. She was *already robbed*. The tollkeepers hadn't passed her out of mercy or indifference. They had passed her the way you pass an empty purse — nothing in it worth the weight of reaching.

And she had known. She had sat in that cart for three days knowing, and she had climbed down and stood in front of that grey-handed man and offered up what was left of herself for inspection, and she had not flinched, because there was so little of her left that even fear had grown thin.

I looked at her, and she looked at the valley, and the grief came into me like cold water into a boot — slow, total, finding every lowest place. Nineteen days. I had thought we were racing a deadline. We were racing a drain.

I put my arm around her shoulders. She let me. After a while, her head tipped sideways and rested against my ribs, a small weight, a light weight — and that was the worst of it, that was the thing I carried out of the pass and did not put down for a long time after: how light she was. A child should not be light. A child should be heavy with days, heavy with nonsense and sugar and songs sung wrong, heavy the way a full purse is heavy. She leaned against me and weighed almost nothing at all.

Behind us, in the tollhouse courtyard, Vess was paying the last of the passage — not to the hollow men, but to their master. I saw it only in glimpses as we walked the mules past: a figure in the tollhouse doorway, robed in the same grey, a man whose eyes were not overfull but *organized*, a man who smiled at Vess Karo with the smile of someone greeting an old debtor. And I heard him say, in a voice like a ledger being opened:

"Captain Karo. It has been a long time. Shall we discuss the oath you swore beneath this mountain — and the child you swore it over?"

Vess went very still.

And I, who had spent my life learning other people's secrets for coin, stood in the road with a fading child's weight against my side and realized I was about to learn one for free — and that this one would cost more than anything I'd ever stolen.
