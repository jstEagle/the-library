## 7. Vess's Bargain

The sea had never forgiven Vess Karo, and she had never asked it to.

She stood at the rail of the *Salt Oath* as it crawled up the coast toward Thornfell, watching the water go from green to iron to the color of old bruises. Behind her, her crew worked the rigging in silence, because they had learned years ago that silence was the price of sailing with her in the weeks before the passes opened. She counted them the way a miser counts coins: Dree at the main brace, hollow-cheeked since the fever took his brother; the twins, Osk and Olla, who spoke only to each other; young Fen, who still flinched at raised hands. Seven souls, not counting hers. She had lost more than that over the years, but never the way she'd lost the boy.

Never the way she'd lost Coral.

The thought arrived, as it always did, uninvited, and she let it pass through her like weather. That was the trick of guilt, she had learned. You did not drown in it. You learned its currents. You let it carry you where it wanted, and you pretended the steering was your own.

"Captain." Dree, at her shoulder, nodding north. "Smoke. Thin. Hearth-smoke, not signal."

She saw it. A gray thread rising off the shoulder of the mountain where no hearth had any right to be — not this close to the sealed passes, not this early in the season. The old smuggler roads through Thornfell were closed by decree of the tollkeepers, and the tollkeepers were closed by decree of whatever sat beneath the mountain wearing a crown of candlelight. Which meant anyone living in that cabin, on that shoulder, in that weather, was either a fool, a fugitive, or a dead man who hadn't been told.

She thought of a boy with quick hands and a face like a locked door, and she hated herself a little for the thought, and she turned the wheel anyway.

* * *

Twelve years earlier, on a night much like this one, a man and a woman had come down out of the Thornfell passes with a child between them and terror written on them so plainly that even the harbor dogs gave them room.

Vess had been younger then. Harder in the wrong places and softer in the wrong places, though she hadn't known which were which. She had been drinking in the back room of the Gilded Eel in Dunmark's low quarter when they found her — the man first, tall and stooped, with the gray, washed-out look of someone who had spent too long underground; the woman behind him, holding a bundle that did not cry, which was the first wrong thing Vess noticed. Babies cried. This one breathed and watched and did not cry, and its eyes were the color of candle flames, and they did not blink.

"We need passage," the man said. "South. Anywhere. Tonight."

"Everyone needs passage. It's what I sell."

"Not passage." The woman's voice was low and rough, like something dragged over stone. "Silence. Passage and silence, and never to speak of it again, not to us, not to anyone, not even to yourself if you can manage it."

Vess had smiled at that, the smile she used on customers who wanted things they couldn't afford. "Silence is the most expensive cargo on this coast, friend. What are you paying?"

The man set a purse on the table. It was heavy. Heavier than it should have been, and when Vess opened it she did not find coin. She found memories.

They were old-fashioned things, memory-tokens — wax seals pressed with sigils she half recognized from the old trade, before the tollkeepers had made the roads dangerous and the trade had gone thin and mean. She held one to the candle and it bloomed open like a flower of light: a wedding, she saw, a small one, in a village that no longer existed, two young people laughing under a borrowed arch. Another: a woman's hands teaching a child to write. Another: a man's whole childhood, summers and winters, a mother's voice, a father's proud face.

"These are yours," she said. "You're paying with your own lives."

"We're paying with the parts of them we can spare," the woman said. "The rest is owed elsewhere."

"Owed to who?"

The man and the woman looked at each other, and in that look Vess saw something she had seen only twice in her life and never wished to see again: two people who had already agreed to die and were only negotiating the order.

"You don't want that name in your mouth," the man said. "Not even as a question."

Vess was a smuggler, not a hero. Heroes died in interesting ways and got songs; smugglers got old, if they were careful, and she intended to be careful. But there was the child, still not crying, still watching her with those candle-flame eyes, and there was the purse full of a family's life, and there was the look on the woman's face when she shifted the bundle in her arms — not the look of a mother protecting her child, Vess realized later. The look of a mother saying goodbye.

"Two berths," Vess said finally. "The child rides free. And you tell me one thing, and only one thing, and then we never speak of it again."

The woman's jaw tightened. "Ask."

"Who's coming after you?"

The silence went on so long that the candle guttered. Then the man said, in a voice like a man reading his own death warrant, "Something that doesn't chase. Something that waits. It knows where we're going. It's letting us run, because running is part of the price." He swallowed. "We made a bargain to buy her time. Fifteen years. Maybe sixteen. And when the time is up—"

"Enough," the woman said, and it was not a plea. It was a door closing.

They sailed that night. Vess took them south along the coast, through fog and past the patrol lights of the tollkeeper cutters, and she did not ask again. The man spent the voyage staring at the water like a man reading a letter he already knew by heart. The woman taught the child — a girl, Vess learned, though she never heard the name spoken aloud — to write letters in the condensation on the cabin walls, over and over, letters in no alphabet Vess knew, and the candle flames leaned toward the child whenever she wrote, like flowers toward a sun.

On the last night, in the last hour, the woman came to Vess alone.

"One more payment," she said. "Not for passage. For a promise."

"I don't make promises. Promises are how smugglers die."

"This one keeps you alive. Swear that when the time comes — and you'll know when, you'll feel it in your bones, the way sailors feel a storm before the sky turns — swear that you won't abandon the girl. Whatever she is by then. Whoever she's with. Swear you'll remember what you carried, even if you never speak of it."

"And if I don't swear?"

The woman smiled, and it was the saddest thing Vess had ever seen, sadder than the purse full of lives, sadder than the child who did not cry. "Then it won't matter. Because you'll have already forgotten me, and this conversation, and the reason you feel like crying right now and don't know why." She reached into her coat and drew out one last memory-token, and this one was not wax. It was bone-white, and warm, and it beat faintly, like a small heart. "The Undercourt takes payment in what you love best. I'm paying you in what I love second best. Hold it, and you'll understand. Or hold it, and you'll forget. Either way, the bargain holds. That's the cruelty of it. That's the mercy."

Vess should have thrown it into the sea. She was a smuggler; she knew cursed cargo when she held it; she knew that some payments cost more than they weigh.

She took it.

And she swore.

* * *

The *Salt Oath* put in at the smugglers' cove below the mountain two days later, and Vess climbed the goat-track to the cabin alone, because whatever she was walking into, her crew did not need to walk into it with her.

The storm had passed, but the mountain was still wringing itself out, mist peeling off the pines in long gray banners. The cabin sat where no cabin should — too close to the old roads, too far from any village, its chimney smoking its thin defiant thread. She smelled woodsmoke and, under it, something else. Candle wax. A great deal of candle wax, for a house with one hearth.

She did not knock. She was too old for knocking. She pushed the door open and stood in the doorway with her hand on her knife and her heart doing something stupid and adolescent against her ribs.

Coral sat at the table. Older, of course. Leaner. His hair had gone from brown to the color of driftwood, and there were lines around his eyes that had not been carved by laughter. But it was him — the boy she had raised for six years and cast out in one night, the boy whose hands were quicker than his sense of self-preservation, the boy she had found, half-frozen, stealing bread from her own stores with the shameless competence of a born thief, and taken in because taking in strays was the one soft thing she had ever allowed herself, and unmade because he had done the one thing she could not forgive.

He had stolen from her. Not coin. Not cargo. He had lifted a memory out of her sleeping mind — the only memory she had ever kept of her daughter, dead at seven of a fever Vess still blamed herself for — and he had sold it before she caught him, sold it to a fence in Dunmark, and by the time she tracked it down it had been bought by a grieving father who wore it like a coat and would not give it back, and no law on this coast or any other could make him.

She had put him off her ship at knife-point in the rain, and she had told herself for twelve years that it was justice, and for twelve years the telling had not once worked.

Now he sat in a cabin that smelled of candle wax, and beside him sat a child.

Vess stopped breathing.

The girl was perhaps ten. Small, barefoot, with a stillness about her that no living child should have. She sat with her hands folded, watching Vess with eyes the color of candle flames, and she did not blink, and the candles on the table leaned toward her, very slightly, like flowers toward a sun.

*The child who did not cry.* Twelve years older. Exactly twelve years, if you counted the way Vess counted, from that fog-bound night to this one, and Vess had spent her whole life counting things, and she had never once miscounted a debt.

"Vess," Coral said. His voice was careful. He had always been careful with her, the way a man is careful walking on ice that he knows has already cracked once. "You got my message."

"I got a message. It said *trouble, passes, solstice, come alone.* It didn't say—" She stopped. Started again, lower. "It didn't say you'd found *her.*"

Something moved behind Coral's eyes — surprise, then the quick calculation of a man deciding how much he can afford to show. "You know her."

"I know what she is." Vess came into the room because standing in the doorway was a thing people did when they still believed they could leave. She pulled out a chair and sat, uninvited, the way captains do. "I carried her parents out of the Undercourt's reach twelve years ago. I took their payment. I swore an oath I've regretted every day since, because oaths are how smugglers die, and this one's come due."

The girl — Beatrix, Coral had called her that, she caught the name now, *Beatrix* — tilted her head. And then she spoke, and the language was nothing Vess had ever heard, syllables like water over old stones, like a song remembered from before songs had words, and the candle flames all stood up straight at once, every one in the room, as if called to attention.

"She says," Coral translated, and his voice had gone quiet and strange, "that she knows you. That you smell like salt and her mother's fear. She says—" He paused, and Vess watched him decide to tell the truth, and admired him for it, and hated him for it. "She says you kept the promise. That her mother's memory of you is warm. That you're the one who was supposed to come."

"I'm the one who was *paid* to come," Vess said harshly, because the alternative was to feel what she was feeling, and Vess Karo did not feel things at table. "There's a difference, and the difference has kept me alive forty years."

But even as she said it, her hand went to the inside pocket of her coat, where a bone-white token had ridden against her heart for twelve years, warm, beating faintly, patient as a debt.

She had never opened it. She had never dared. She had told herself it was because opening it might break it, the way you don't look too closely at a thing that's holding a door shut. But that was a lie, and she had known it was a lie, and the truth was simpler and worse: she was afraid of what was inside. Because the woman had said *hold it and you'll understand, or hold it and you'll forget,* and Vess had spent twelve years not knowing which of those things she was doing — whether she was keeping a promise or whether the promise was keeping her, whether the warmth she sometimes felt toward a child she'd never met was her own heart or a payment working its way through her like a drug.

* * *

They talked until the candles burned low. Coral told her everything — the ledger, the debt, the solstice, the Undercourt, the plan that was barely a plan, a smuggler's plan, which is to say a suicide with a schedule. Vess listened the way she listened to storm reports: cataloguing, discounting, weighing. The passes were sealed. The tollkeepers took memories at the checkpoints, and the tollkeepers answered to the mountain, and the mountain answered to whatever the mountain answered to. Getting a party through by solstice was impossible.

Getting a party through by solstice was exactly the kind of impossible Vess had built her life on.

"I'll take you through," she said at last, "but not my road. The old road. The one under the glacier, the one the tollkeepers think collapsed twenty years ago. It's not collapsed. I've kept it secret because it's the only way in or out of this coast that doesn't pass a single pair of hollow eyes." She leaned forward. "But I have a price."

Coral's face went still, the locked-door face. "Name it."

"Two things." She held up one finger. "First: when this is done — however it's done — you come back to my crew. Not as a passenger. As crew. I cast you out and I was wrong, and I'd rather die on the water than die owing that sentence unspoken."

She watched him take that like a blow. Good, she thought. Let it hurt. It hurt her to say it.

"And second?" he said.

Vess drew the bone-white token from her coat and set it on the table between them, and the candle flames all leaned toward it at once, Beatrix's and the room's, as if it were kin to them all.

"Second," she said, "you tell me what this is. Because I've carried it twelve years without opening it, and I've told myself a dozen stories about why, and I'm done with stories. Her mother gave it to me the night we sailed, and she said it was payment for a promise, and I have a feeling — a feeling I've had for twelve years and buried under forty years of salt — that the promise and the passage and the child at your table are all one debt, and that I've been carrying my share without knowing the weight." Her voice cracked, just once, on the last word, and she let it. "So open it, thief. You're good at opening things. Open it and tell me what I've been carrying."

Coral looked at the token. Then at Beatrix. And Beatrix — who did not cry, who had not cried, perhaps, in twelve years — reached out and laid one small hand on the bone-white thing, and looked up at Vess, and said something in that old, old language, soft as snowfall.

Coral translated, and his voice was not steady.

"She says: *It's her mother's goodbye. It was meant for the one who would come back for me. She says you were never paid, Captain. You were trusted. And the token only opens for the one it was given to.*"

Vess Karo had faced storms, tollkeepers, cutlass-men, and one thing beneath a mountain that she still did not speak of. Her hands had never shaken.

They shook now, as she picked it up.

The token was warm as a living thing, and it knew her, and as her thumb found the seam of it — as it had never done in twelve years of trying — it opened like a flower of light, and her daughter's laughter poured into her, and beneath the laughter, clear as a bell across dark water, a woman's voice said: *Thank you for coming back. Now listen, because there is something beneath this mountain that you must know before you set one foot inside it — something about the man who signed my daughter away, and the name he had before the archives took it.*

Vess went very still.

She knew that name. She had known it all her life, worn smooth with use, the name of a man she'd trusted, the name of a man who had taught a half-frozen boy thief everything he knew.

The name was the same as Coral's.
