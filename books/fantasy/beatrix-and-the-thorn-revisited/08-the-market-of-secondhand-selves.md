## 8. The Market of Secondhand Selves

Dunmark smelled of tallow, wet wool, and other people's yesterdays.

Coral had always thought that was the truest smell of the free city — not the fish off the harbor, not the spice carts on Lantern Row, but the faint, sweetish rot of memories changing hands. Every city that traded in secondhand selves developed the same odor eventually, the way a sickroom develops its own particular hush. Thornfell had pine and cold stone. Dunmark had this: candle-fat and forgetting.

He kept his hood up and his hands visible. In Dunmark, hidden hands meant hidden knives, and hidden knives meant the Watch would be on you before the knife cleared the sheath. Visible hands meant a trader. Visible hands meant honest work, or work honest enough.

Beatrix walked beside him, her small hand tucked into the crook of his elbow. She had not spoken — she never spoke, not in any tongue the waking world could hear — but her silence had changed since Thornfell. It had weight now. Direction. She looked at the market stalls the way a blind woman looks at a voice.

*She knows this place,* Coral thought, and did not finish the thought, because the rest of it was a shape he did not want to hold in daylight.

The market proper began where the lanterns changed color. In the honest quarter they burned yellow; here, past the tanner's row and the shuttered chapel of some god whose name the city had forgotten, they burned green, and then blue, and then a color Coral had no name for, a color that made the back of his teeth ache. Memory-lanterns. Each one held the dregs of a dozen sold pasts, and the light they gave was the light of other people's noons and other people's funerals.

The sellers worked the shadows between the lanterns. A woman with a tray of glass stoppers, each vial fogged from within, like breath trapped against a window. A boy no older than Beatrix selling fragments off a velvet cloth: a first snowfall, a mother's lullaby in a language the buyer wouldn't understand but would feel anyway, the specific joy of a dog greeting you at a door. A fat man with rings on every finger who claimed to sell futures and sold, Coral knew from long experience, other men's dreams mislabeled.

Coral walked past all of them. He was not shopping. He was hunting.

* * *

The dying thief kept a room above a chandler's shop on the street the locals called Whisper's End, because the walls were thin and the chandler's wax swallowed sound, and because everything on that street was half-heard, including the lives of the people who lived there. His name, insofar as names mattered in that trade, was Ossler Vane. Coral had bought from him twice before, years ago — a smuggler's map of the Thornfell passes, and once, in a moment of weakness he still regretted, the memory of a woman teaching her daughter to whistle. He had sold the whistling within a week. He still didn't know whose it had been.

He knocked the pattern: two, pause, three, pause, one. The door opened on a chain, then all the way, and the smell of the room hit him like a hand across the face — camphor, blood, and beneath it the particular ozone stink of memories going bad in their jars.

Ossler Vane lay propped on a cot among his wares, and he was dying the way only memory-thieves died, which was to say he was forgetting himself out of existence. It was the occupational hazard no one put on the guild notices. A thief who took too many memories into his own skull to move them around, who handled too many lives, began to lose the thread of his own. Ossler had been losing it for years. Now it was nearly gone. His eyes were the eyes of a man looking at a room he had lived in for forty years and could not swear he recognized.

"Coral Zhou." His voice was a dry leaf. "Or whatever you're calling yourself this season."

"Coral's fine."

"Is it?" Ossler smiled with too many teeth. "I never could tell which names you kept and which you wore. Come in, come in. Mind the jars. Mind the jars especially, the blue ones. The blue ones bite."

Coral stepped carefully between the shelves. The room was a hoard of the stolen past: stoppered vials in racks, wax-sealed lozenges in tins, a whole shelf of slate tablets scratched with extraction sigils, and in the corner, wrapped in oilcloth, the heavy brass apparatus of the trade — the crown, the needles, the little silver funnel through which a life could be poured from one skull into another.

"I heard you were dying," Coral said.

"Everyone's dying. I'm just doing it with more inventory." Ossler coughed, wet and long. "You want something. You always want something. Sit. Don't sit on the red tin, that one's a man's whole wedding, he'd haunt me."

Coral sat on a stool instead. Beatrix stood by the door, very still, watching Ossler with an expression Coral couldn't read — not fear, not pity. *Recognition*, he thought uneasily. *Like she's seen a man dying of this before.*

"I need the Undercourt," Coral said.

The room went quiet in a way rooms shouldn't be able to go quiet. The lanterns guttered. Somewhere below, the chandler's kettle went on boiling, indifferent.

Ossler's smile died by degrees. "Say that quieter, or not at all."

"You dealt below. Before the passes sealed. Everyone in the trade knew it, and everyone in the trade knew better than to say it. You sold to the Undercourt. Which means you bought from it, or at least you mapped it, because a man like you never goes anywhere he hasn't first bought the layout of."

"And if I did?"

"Then somewhere in this room is a memory of the way down. The vaults. The archive stacks. The Archivist's hall." Coral leaned forward. "I'm not asking where you kept it, Ossler. I'm asking what it costs."

Ossler looked at him for a long time. Then he laughed, and the laugh turned into the cough again, and the cough left a thread of red at the corner of his mouth that he wiped away with the back of his hand as though it were a spill of tea.

"You know what's funny?" he said. "You know what's genuinely funny? I've been sitting here for a month, forgetting my own mother's face, forgetting which hand I favor, forgetting — last Tuesday I forgot my own name for six hours, sat here calling myself *the man in the room* — and do you know what I could not forget? Do you know what stays?" He raised one trembling finger. "The way down. Every stair. Every door. Every candlelit vault with a soul in it, filed like a deed. I could burn every memory in this room and I would still know the Undercourt like my own heartbeat, because it *is* my own heartbeat now. It's the last thing in me that's nailed down."

"Then it should be easy to sell."

"Easy to sell." Ossler's eyes glittered. "Boy. Thief. Whatever you are. Do you know why the passes sealed? Do you know why no one trades below anymore?" He didn't wait for an answer. "Because the Undercourt doesn't buy memories. It *collects* them. There's a difference. A buyer takes your goods and gives you coin. A collector takes your goods and gives you a place on the shelf." He tapped his own temple. "I went down there young and hungry, and I came back up with a fortune in wares and a hole in me shaped like everything I'd sold to pay my own way. And the hole never fills, Coral. I've been pouring other people's lives into it for thirty years and it never fills."

"I know how the hole works," Coral said quietly. "I have one myself."

"Then you know better than to go down there."

"Then you know I'm going anyway."

The silence between them stretched. Beatrix moved — just slightly, just her weight shifting from one foot to the other — and Ossler's gaze slid to her, and Coral watched something move behind the dying man's eyes. Not recognition exactly. The ghost of recognition. The way a man who has forgotten a song might still hum its shape.

"That child," Ossler said slowly. "That child has been *filed*."

Coral's hand drifted, casually, toward his knife. "She's with me."

"I'm not threatening her, you fool, I'm diagnosing her. I know the look. I've seen it on a hundred souls coming up the stair with their receipts in their hands." He shook his head. "Sold. She's been sold, and the sale hasn't finished yet. That's the worst kind. The unfinished ones fade. Like a candle burning down from both ends."

"Which is why I need your map."

* * *

They haggled the way old thieves haggle — not over coin, which was the least of the currencies, but over what Coral would carry out of the room and what he would leave behind. Ossler wanted Coral's memory of the whistling woman. Coral said he'd sold it. Ossler said he was lying. Coral said, truthfully, that he'd sold it and regretted it, and that regret was not for sale. In the end they settled: the Undercourt memory, whole, for Coral's memory of the first time he'd ever stolen — the taste of it, the terror, the strange clean joy. A small memory. A foundational one. Coral kept his face still while he named it, the way he kept his face still for everything.

"Fair," Ossler rasped. "Though I'll tell you a trade secret, since I'm dying and trade secrets are the last thing a dying man can afford to keep. The foundational ones are the cheapest to give and the most expensive to lose. You'll not notice it's gone. You'll just be a slightly smoother man in a slightly emptier room."

"I've been a smooth man in an empty room my whole life. One more inch of nothing won't show."

Ossler laughed again, and this time the laugh sounded almost fond. He reached for the shelf behind him, past the blue jars that bit, and took down a vial of smoked glass with no label at all. Unlabeled, in Ossler's collection, meant one of two things: priceless, or unspeakable. Often both.

"Here's what you're buying," he said, holding the vial up to the green lantern-light so the memory inside swirled like smoke in water. "I went down the sunken gate on the north face, the one the smugglers call the Keyhole, back when it still opened for the right coin. Three days in the vaults. I mapped what I could and stole what I could carry. This is all of it, stitched: the stair, the candle-vaults, the archive stacks, the hall where the Archivist sits. And—" he paused, and his voice did something complicated, "—and the souls. You'll see the souls. They're the archives, you understand. Every sold soul down there is a *book*. They shelve them. They read them. Don't look too long at any one of them, or it looks back, and then it knows you, and then you're in the ledger too."

"I've been in ledgers before."

"Not like this one." Ossler set the vial down between them on the cot. "Last thing. The extraction will hurt, because the memory doesn't want to leave me. It's the only thing in my head with roots. But I want it out. I want it out of me before I die, because if I die holding it, I'll go down there again, and this time I won't come back up." He smiled, and it was the saddest thing in the room, and the room was full of sad things. "Consider it a discount."

* * *

The extraction took an hour and left them both shaking.

Coral sat with the crown of brass on his skull, needles kissing his temple, while Ossler poured the Undercourt out of himself through the silver funnel and into Coral's waiting mind. It came in pieces, the way all transferred memory comes — a stair of black stone worn hollow by feet; a gate that tasted of iron and old prayer; corridors of candlelight where the flames burned without heat and did not gutter, ever, because the dead do not need wind to be afraid. And the vaults. Rows and rows of vaults, and in them, standing in niches like saints in a cathedral wall, the souls.

They looked like people. That was the horror of it. They looked like people who had been told to stand very still, and had obeyed for so long that stillness had become their whole nature. Their eyes were open. Their eyes were always open. And they were *read* — Coral saw it, felt it through Ossler's borrowed eyes — clerks in grey robes coming down the rows with long needles of light, drawing memory out of the shelved souls the way a scribe dips a pen, writing with other people's pasts.

Then, near the end of the memory, in the last vault before the Archivist's hall, Ossler's eye had caught on two souls shelved side by side.

Coral came out of the crown gasping.

He sat on the stool with his hands flat on his knees and made himself breathe, because what he had seen in the last vault was not layout. It was not architecture. It was a man and a woman, young, gaunt, standing in their niches with their eyes open, and the woman had turned her head — souls were not supposed to turn their heads — and looked directly out of the memory at him, and her mouth had moved, and though the memory carried no sound, Coral had read her lips the way he read everything, easily, terribly:

*Our daughter. Is our daughter—*

He knew her face. He had never seen it before in his life, and he knew it instantly, completely, the way you know a face that has been standing in front of you for weeks wearing a child's smaller shape. Beatrix's cheekbones. Beatrix's mouth. Beatrix's eyes, except these were the eyes of a woman who had sold something unbearable and had watched it walk away.

And the man beside her — the man had not turned. The man had not moved at all. But on the shelf-plate beneath his niche, in the archive's clean grey script, Coral had read the designation, and the designation was a name, and the name was the same name that appeared, witnessed and countersigned, on the ledger page that had arrived at his door in the storm.

*They're alive,* he thought, and the thought was a trap snapping shut around his heart. *They're alive, and they're below, and they're shelved.*

He had spent his whole life believing that hope was the cruelest thing a thief could carry — heavier than any stolen good, more fragile than any vial, and always, *always* the first thing the market took as payment. He had watched hope ruin better smugglers than him. Hope made men go back for one last job. Hope made men trust a buyer's smile. Hope made men walk down stairs they should have known were one-way.

And here it was anyway, sitting in his chest like a coal: *her parents are alive.* Which meant the contract could be broken from more than one end. Which meant Beatrix might not have to fade. Which meant —

Which meant he was already planning the descent. Which meant the coal had already caught.

"You saw them," Ossler said. It wasn't a question. The old thief had slumped back against the wall, emptier now, his eyes drifting around the room as if taking inventory of what little remained. "In the last vault. I saw your face change." He laughed softly, without humor. "That's how it gets you, you know. Not the layout. Not the gold. It gets you with a *face*. Somebody down there you can't leave alone. That's the hook the Undercourt baits for every thief who ever thought about the descent." He closed his eyes. "Go on, then. Take your map and your hope and your hook. And when you're standing in the candlelight and something shelved looks at you like it knows you — remember an old fool told you the vault was left open on purpose."

Coral stood. His legs felt strange, lighter, the foundational memory gone out of him like a stone from a shoe. He didn't feel smoother or emptier. He felt like a man holding a lit lantern in a room full of oil.

At the door, Beatrix was waiting. She looked up at him, and then past him, at Ossler on his cot, and she did something she had never done in all the weeks Coral had known her.

She reached out and touched the dying man's hand — just briefly, just two small fingers on his wrist — and Ossler's whole body went still, and his eyes opened wide, and he said, in a voice that cracked down the middle:

"Oh. *Oh.* That's — I remember — that's the lullaby. That's the one I sold in Dunmark, twenty years ago, the one I could never find again—"

Beatrix took her hand back. Ossler stared at his own wrist as though it belonged to someone else, and then at her, and then he began, very quietly, to weep.

Coral did not ask. There would be time, or there wouldn't. He took Beatrix's hand and led her down the stairs of Whisper's End, out into the green-lanterned dark, where the market of secondhand selves was still open for business and every face in the crowd was wearing someone else's yesterdays behind its eyes.

*They're alive,* the coal in his chest said. *Below. Shelved. Reachable.*

And underneath the hope, quiet as a second heartbeat, the old thief's warning kept time:

*The vault was left open on purpose.*

He needed a crew. He needed a witch who could stitch what the descent would tear, and a clerk who could read what the Undercourt wrote, and a captain with a ship and a grudge and a debt she didn't know she still owed.

He knew exactly where to find all three. He only prayed, in the direction of whatever god handled fools, that they would come — and that what came with them wouldn't be exactly what the Archivist had ordered.
