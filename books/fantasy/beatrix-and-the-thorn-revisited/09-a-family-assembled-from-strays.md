## 9. A Family Assembled from Strays

Dunmark smelled of wet stone and frying onions, and I loved it the way you can only love a place that has never hurt you.

We came down off the smuggler roads at dusk, four days out of the pass, and the city rose around us like a heap of old plates — slate roofs stacked crooked on slate roofs, chimneys breathing their thin smoke into a violet sky. The low quarter sat below everything else, near the harbor, where the tide came up through the grates at night and the streets ran silver with saltwater. I had never seen so many people in one place. In the mountains there was Coral, and Vess's crew, and the occasional trapper who looked through me as if I were weather. Here there were hundreds, thousands, all of them burning with thoughts.

I did not have to try to hear them anymore. That was the strange part. On the road it had come slowly, word by word, like snow gathering on a fence rail — *bread*, *cold*, *home*, *her* — each stray thought sticking to me until the common tongue built itself inside my skull. But Dunmark was a flood. Walking down the harbor stairs I picked up a fishwife's argument about prices, a child's terror of a dog, an old man's grief for a wife dead eleven years, the whole bright chattering river of the city pouring through me. Some of it was beautiful. Some of it I wished I could put down.

Coral walked half a step ahead of me, which was how he always walked, as though the world owed him money and he meant to collect before it noticed. He wore the face he wore in towns: pleasant, forgettable, a man you would describe to a constable as *ordinary, officer, perfectly ordinary*. Only his eyes moved constantly, reading everyone we passed the way I read thoughts — quick, greedy little glances, pricing them.

"Low quarter," he said, to me or to himself. "If Pike still lives, he lives here."

"You said he fell," I said. My voice still surprised me sometimes. It had been back for weeks now, but every sentence felt like a coin found in an old coat.

"He fell from grace. Grace is high up. Falling means landing somewhere." Coral turned down an alley where the lamplight didn't bother to reach. "Clerks of the Undercourt land hard."

Vess followed with her arms crossed, big as a door in her oilskin coat, taking the alley's measure the way she took everything's measure — as if deciding whether she could fight it. Behind her came two of her crew, Bren and soft-voiced Osla, carrying lanterns they hadn't lit yet. Vess hadn't wanted to bring anyone. Vess hadn't wanted to come to Dunmark at all, if I read the set of her shoulders right, but she had made her choice back at the pass, and whatever guilt had calcified in her over the years had hardened into something useful: resolve. She walked like a woman keeping a promise she hated.

The alley opened into a courtyard where someone had once tried to grow a garden and then given up. Dead vines clung to a wall. A rain barrel held a skin of ice. And on the third floor of the leaning house across the court, one window glowed.

"He keeps a light burning," Coral said quietly. "All these years."

"How do you know him?" I asked.

"I don't, not really. I know *of* him." A pause, and I heard something rare in his voice — honesty offered without a price attached. "He was the clerk who processed my own sale."

I went cold, colder than the harbor wind. "You were sold?"

"Everyone down there is sold, little wren. The only question is whether the seller knew what they were selling." He started up the outside stair, which groaned under his boots. "Mine didn't. Or so I choose to believe, on nights when I need to sleep."

I wanted to ask more. I wanted to ask everything. But the stair ended at a door with no handle, only a knocker shaped like a coiled serpent, and Coral lifted it and let it fall three times, twice, once.

Silence. Then a voice through the wood, dry as moth wings: "The market is closed."

"We're not buying," Coral said. "We're selling."

"Everything in this city is for sale. Go away."

"We know about the seventh vault."

A long silence, long enough that I felt the whole house listening. Then bolts — many bolts, drawn one after another, a small percussion of paranoia — and the door swung inward on a room that smelled of ink, tallow, and despair.

* * *

Pike was not what I expected, though I couldn't have said what I'd expected. He was small and grayish, like something that had lived indoors too long, with ink stains up both forearms and eyes that kept moving to the corners of the room as if the walls might grow teeth. His shelves were crammed with ledgers, and I understood immediately, in the way I understood things sometimes without knowing how, that those ledgers were his hoard the way a dragon's gold is a hoard — records of debts, contracts, bindings, names. He had spent years below the mountain copying the language of ownership, and now he copied it alone, in a garret, in a city that had forgotten the Undercourt existed.

"The seventh vault," Pike repeated. He had not asked them to sit. He stood with his back against his shelves as if they might be needed to hold him up. "Nobody knows about the seventh vault. Two people knew, and one of them is me, and the other—"

"Is why we're here," Coral said.

"Then the other one sent you, and I'm already dead, and this conversation is a formality." Pike laughed, and it was a terrible sound, like a hinge that had never once been oiled. Then his eyes found me, and stopped, and changed.

I am used to being looked at. People look at me and see a girl who is too quiet, too pale, too still, a girl whose silence has edges. But Pike looked at me the way a man looks at a ledger page he thought he had burned. His lips moved. He was reading me. Not my face — the thing under my face, the shape of what remained of me, the same way the tollkeepers at the pass had seen there was nothing left worth taking.

"You're one of theirs," he whispered.

"I'm mine," I said.

It came out steadier than I felt. Coral glanced at me, and something flickered across his merchant's face — surprise, maybe, or pride — gone before I could name it.

"She's claimed," Coral said smoothly, "by a debt. Sold to the Undercourt before she could walk. The contract voids at the winter solstice if unclaimed, which gives us—" he counted on his fingers with theatrical precision, though I knew he had already done the arithmetic a hundred times, "—nineteen days. We're going down. We need a clerk who can read the old bindings and forge the release seal. I'm told there was once no better forger in the archives."

"There was no better forger in the archives," Pike agreed. "That is exactly why they cut off his fingers." He raised his hands, and I saw, with a lurch, that his smallest fingers ended at the second knuckle, neat and old, the stumps smooth as river stones. "They were very tidy about it. They're always tidy. Do you know what they did with the fingers? Neither do I. That's the part that keeps me up."

"So you'll come," Coral said.

"I said nothing of the sort—"

"Pike." Vess spoke for the first time, and the room got smaller. She did not raise her voice. She never needed to. "You've been up here eleven years waiting for them to finish the job. I know the look. I've worn it." She stepped forward, and the lamplight caught the scar along her jaw. "Here's a chance to make the jump mean something. Come down with us and burn the thing that maimed you, or stay up here and count your ledgers until your heart gives out, and either way, stop wasting our night."

The silence afterward had texture, like felt.

"Why her?" Pike said finally, and his voice had cracked open somewhere in the middle. "Why does it matter? One soul among ten thousand. You could walk away. Everyone walks away. That's the first law of the low quarter, the only law." His ruined hands were shaking. "Why are *you* not walking away?"

And I answered, because it was my debt and I would not let Coral carry even the telling of it.

"Because Coral came for me when nobody sent him," I said. "Because Vess owes my parents a bargain older than I am, and she's paying it. Because everyone in this room has been abandoned by somebody, and we're tired." I swallowed. My voice, my new voice, my stolen-and-hard-won voice, went soft. "You asked why we aren't walking away. Why are *you* keeping the light burning in the window?"

Pike stared at me.

Then he laughed again, and this time it wasn't a hinge. It was rusty and startled and almost human, and he wiped his eyes with the back of a five-fingered hand and said, "Gods help me. Because I'm a fool. Fine. FINE. I'll need paper, a wax matrix, and someone willing to commit approximately nine kinds of blasphemy, and in exchange I want—" he seemed to lose his thread, staring around his garret at eleven years of dust and dread, "—I want to never see this room again. Someone help me pack."

* * *

Maren we found the next evening, and finding her was not like finding Pike at all.

Pike had been findable because he wanted, secretly, in the locked cellar of himself, to be found. Maren did not want anything, or wanted nothing she would admit to. She kept a shop at the end of Rope Walk where she sold charms against toothache and colic and unfaithful husbands, ordinary charms, hedge-mother charms, the kind grandmothers bought with coppers and believed in the way you believe in soup. The sign above her door showed a needle and thread. It did not say what she truly sewed.

Coral had learned of her from the dying thief in the memory-market — the same transaction that had shown him Beatrix's parents alive beneath the mountain. *If you need torn things mended*, the thief had gasped, trading the last of his hoard for a few more breaths, *the stitcher on Rope Walk. Tell her Tolly sent his regards. She'll either weep or kill you.*

"She'll weep or kill you," Coral told us cheerfully, standing outside her shop in the drizzle. "Possibly both. Are we ready?"

"No," said Vess.

"Wonderful. Beatrix, you knock."

I looked at him. "Why me?"

"Because everyone in this city has learned to say no to people like me, and I suspect no one has ever managed to say no to you." He said it lightly, but his eyes weren't light at all, and I understood that he had thought about this, had calculated it the way he calculated everything, and had decided that the calculation ended in me.

So I knocked.

The woman who opened the door was tall and stooped, with gray hair pinned up in a chaos of clips and needles stuck through the bun like reeds, and hands — I noticed her hands first, everyone notices Maren's hands eventually — hands that moved even when the rest of her was still, thumb rolling over forefinger, sewing air.

"We're closed," she said.

"Tolly sent his regards," I said.

Her face did something complicated. Grief and rage fighting for the same doorway. "Tolly," she said, "is dead."

"Yes. Last week. He sold us what he knew and paid for it with what he had left." I held her eyes. It was hard. Her grief pushed at me like heat from an oven. "I'm sorry. He said you'd want to know he didn't suffer at the end. He suffered some. But less than most."

For a moment I thought she would shut the door. Then her gaze slid past me to Coral, to Vess looming behind like a ship at anchor, and settled on Pike, huddled and gray under an umbrella he refused to open, and her brows drew together.

"And what," she said slowly, "is a disgraced archive-clerk doing on my doorstep in the rain?"

Pike flinched. "How did you—"

"You have binding-ink under your nails and you flinch whenever thunder rolls. I mended a tollkeeper's memory last spring; he talked in his sleep." She stepped back from the door, and her hands kept sewing the air, faster now. "Come in before the whole street hears you. And mind the cats."

There were, indeed, cats. Seven of them, at minimum, draped over every warm surface in the little shop, and they watched us with the proprietary disdain of creatures who had chosen this woman and resented further company. The shop smelled of dried yarrow, beeswax, and something underneath — something faint and strange and familiar, like the air in a very old room.

Maren put a kettle on, which seemed absurd, and questioned us while it heated, which was less absurd. She did not ask what we wanted. She asked about Tolly — what he'd said, exactly what he'd said, twice. She asked Vess where her route ran these days, and nodded at the answer as if it confirmed something sad she'd long suspected. She asked Pike nothing at all, only looked at his hands once more, and her own hands stilled, briefly, in sympathy.

Then she looked at me, and kept looking, and the kettle began to sing, and none of us moved to take it off the fire.

"You're coming apart," she said softly. Not unkindly. The way you'd tell someone their coat was torn. "Threads hanging loose everywhere. Who stitched you last?"

"Nobody," I said. "It isn't stitching. I was sold. What's left of me is what they didn't want."

"Mm." She crossed the room, and her hands, those restless hands, hovered over me without touching, tracing shapes I couldn't follow. "Sold souls tear differently. They don't rip clean. They unravel from the middle out, like a pulled thread in knitting. Whoever holds your contract is holding the other end of the yarn, dear, and every day they wind a little more of you onto their spool." Her eyes came up to mine. "Nineteen days?"

"Nineteen," Coral said.

"And you want me to come with you. Below." She said the word the way sailors say *storm*. "Into the Undercourt, where I'd be the richest woman in the province if I delivered you, and the poorest corpse in the province if they caught me helping. You want me to leave my shop and my cats and my quiet life."

"Yes," Coral said.

"No," Vess said, at the same moment, and glared at him.

But I watched Maren, and I saw her hands, and her hands had gone still again, completely still, for the second time. And I remembered what Coral had told me about her on the road down: that the dying thief had known her name, that she mended memories for smugglers and deserters and hollow-eyed men who came in the night, and that she charged some of them nothing. A woman who charges nothing for some work is a woman doing penance. I didn't learn that anywhere. I just knew it, the way I knew turns in the mountain roads I'd never walked.

"Who did you lose?" I asked her quietly.

The kettle screamed. One of the cats yowled in protest. Maren did not move.

"A daughter," she said at last. "Fevers took her, eight years ago, and I was fool enough to think a stitcher could call back what fever takes. I unraveled her trying. There's a piece of her I keep in a locket that doesn't remember being her anymore, just remembers being loved, and I take it out at night, and I don't know which of us that hurts more." Her chin came up, fierce. "So no. I don't go below. I mend what walks in my door and I bury what I break and I do not go *below*."

"Your stitches could save her," Coral said gently, gesturing at me, and I loved him and hated him for using me so plainly, because it was true and truth was the sharpest tool he owned.

"My stitches could get her killed faster," Maren snapped. "Disguise-work in the archives, threading false memories past the Archivist's wardens — do you know what happens to a stitcher they catch below? They don't kill us. They *unpick* us. Every seam I ever sewed, every memory I ever mended, unwoven and scattered, until there's nothing left of Maren but loose thread." She turned away, busying herself with the kettle, and her hands shook as she poured.

Nobody spoke. The cats watched us. Rain ticked at the window.

I should have stayed silent. I was learning that I had a talent for silence and that most of the time it served me. But the warmth of the shop, the smell of tea, the seven sleeping cats, the woman with the locket and the shaking hands — something in me refused to let this room close its door on us the way every other door had closed.

"He keeps his mother's singing," I said.

Maren paused with the teapot in the air.

"Coral. Among everything he's stolen and sold, everything he's traded away, he kept one memory his whole life and never spent it. His mother, singing. That's what he's going to pay the Archivist. Everything he is, everything he came from — to buy me back." I looked at my hands. "I never asked him to. I never would have. But he's doing it anyway, and Pike's coming with broken fingers, and Vess is paying a debt she wishes she could forget, and I'm — I'm asking you, because you mend torn things, and we are all of us torn, and there is no one else."

The teapot came down. Very carefully, precisely, Maren set it on its trivet.

"Loose thread," she murmured, almost to herself. "All of you. Loose thread everywhere." She looked at Coral, who had gone uncharacteristically still, exposed, a man caught holding the one thing he'd sworn no one would ever learn he held. She looked at me longest of all.

Then she untied her apron.

"I'll want three days' supplies, my good needles, and someone to feed the cats," she announced briskly, and her voice only broke a little. "Bren can do the cats. He looks like a cat person. Now sit down, all of you, and tell me everything, and I mean *everything* — contracts, routes, wards, the lot. If I'm going below to be unpicked, I'll be the best-informed loose thread the Undercourt has ever hanged."

* * *

We stayed in the shop until well past midnight, maps and ledger pages spread between the teacups, and I sat in the middle of it and listened to them argue and plan and interrupt each other, and something happened inside me that I had no word for yet, because the word was *family* and I had never had the thing itself to hang the word on.

Pike sketched the sunken gate from memory, his stumped hand flying, and corrected himself twice, muttering. Vess argued about the tides beneath the mountain, which apparently had tides, which horrified her professionally. Coral lay with his chair tipped back, contributing exactly as much as necessary and no more, watching us all with his merchant's eyes — except that tonight the eyes weren't pricing anything. Tonight they were only watching, the way a man watches a fire he thought had gone out forever.

Maren fed us. Nobody had asked her to; she simply kept producing food, bread and honey and a stew with too much pepper, and I ate until I was embarrassed and then ate more. Around me the cats arranged themselves — one across Pike's ledgers, one against Vess's boot, daring her, one curled in Coral's coat pocket while he pretended not to notice.

And I sat there, in the yellow light, full of pepper stew and strangers, and realized that for the first time since the night my parents signed me away — the night I chose it, though I could not yet remember choosing — I was not afraid of the dark hours ahead. There would come a midnight when the fear arrived on schedule, the way it always did, the buried terror stirring under my ribs like something turning over in its sleep. But tonight it stayed asleep. Tonight I belonged to a room, and the room belonged to itself, six strays and seven cats stitched together in a shop on Rope Walk while the rain came off the harbor.

"It won't hold," Maren said at some point, quietly, watching us the way I watched travelers. "This feeling. Families made in a hurry tear at the seams. I've seen it."

"Then we'll mend it," I said.

She laughed — a short, surprised bark — and reached over and ruffled my hair with those restless hands, and I let her.

Later, when the others had gone to the rooms Vess had hired, I lingered at the window looking out at the harbor lights. Coral stayed too. For a while neither of us spoke, and it was a comfortable silence, which was new, and precious, and I hoarded it the way he hoarded memories.

"Nineteen days," he said finally. "Eighteen, now."

"I know."

"Beatrix." He hesitated. Coral, who never hesitated, who priced hesitation as a cost and cut it from every transaction. "What Pike said. About being one of theirs. About my sale." He was looking at the harbor lights, not at me. "When we're below. If you see the record of it, in the stacks — my contract, my origin — I'd rather you didn't read it."

"Why?"

"Because a man should get to choose what he's made of," he said. "Even if he chose wrong."

Before I could answer, the lamp guttered — all the lamps, street and shop and harbor, one long indrawn breath of darkness — and in that darkness I heard, clear as a bell rung underwater, a voice speaking my old language, the first language, the one I had spoken at Coral's door the storm-night I arrived. It came from below. From under the city, under the harbor, under everything.

And it called my name.

Not Beatrix. My other name. The one I had not spoken since the night I was sold — and which I had never told a living soul.
