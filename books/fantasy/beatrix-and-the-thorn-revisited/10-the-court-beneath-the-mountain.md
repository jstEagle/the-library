## 10. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The sunken gate did not look like a gate. It looked like a wound that had learned to be patient.

Coral had known of it for years the way smugglers know of things they hope never to need: a seam in the mountain's flank where the stone forgot itself, three days' walk beyond the last honest road, hidden behind a curtain of frozen waterfall that Pike swore was older than the kingdom above it. They reached it at dusk, when the light went thin and blue as old glass, and Beatrix stopped walking before any of them saw why.

She stood at the edge of the falls' frozen curtain with her head tilted, listening to something beneath the silence, and Coral felt the hairs on his arms lift. The child did not shiver. She had not shivered since Dunmark, and he had told himself that was Maren's stitching holding her warmth in, her borrowed years doing their work. But watching her now — barefoot still, because shoes were one more thing she refused to understand the use of — he understood that she was not enduring the cold. She was *recognizing* it.

"It knows her," Maren murmured at his shoulder. The hedge-witch's breath fogged and hung strangely, as if the air here were reluctant to let anything go. "Or she knows it. Hard to say which way the river runs with that one."

"Both," said Pike. He had his ledger out again, the salvaged contract wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, and his hands were shaking in a way Coral had never seen them shake over mere danger. "That's what frightens me. The Undercourt doesn't take sides. It takes *both*."

Vess Karo said nothing. She never said much when there was work to be done, and there was work now: she pressed her palm flat against the frozen water and pushed, and the ice gave like a door that had been waiting a long time for someone to remember the knock.

Beyond it was not a cave. It was a stairway, cut from black stone, descending into a warmth that rose up to meet them like breath from an open mouth. Candlelight moved somewhere far below — not flickering, Coral noticed. Not flickering at all. It burned steady as starlight, steady as something that had burned so long it had forgotten how to do anything else.

He looked back once. Above them the sky was going dark over Thornfell's teeth, and somewhere in that darkness was his cabin, his shelves, his careful inventory of other people's lives. Twenty-two winters he had lived above this thing without knowing its heart beat under his floorboards. Or perhaps — and the thought arrived uninvited, cold as the falls — perhaps some part of him had always known. Perhaps that was why he had chosen the mountains at all. Why he could never quite leave them.

Beatrix took the first step down. No one had told her to. No one needed to.

* * *

The stairs went down for hours.

They counted in candle-burns, because time behaved badly below the frost line — Maren's little tallow stubs melted at half their proper pace, then all at once, then not at all, and Pike's pocket-watch ticked backward twice and had to be put away like a mad relative. Coral kept his own count by his feet instead, by the wear of the steps, which told a story plainer than any clock. These stairs had been climbed for centuries. The center of each tread was worn to a shallow bowl, smooth as riverstone, and the bowls grew deeper the further down they went, until near the bottom a grown man had to walk close to the wall or slide.

"Who built this?" Vess asked finally. She had been silent for two candle-burns, which for Vess was a kind of prayer.

"No one," Pike said. "That's the answer nobody wants. There are records — I was a records clerk, I should know — and the oldest ones don't describe the Undercourt being founded. They describe it being *found*. The first archivists came down these same stairs and found the vaults already lit."

"And who lit them?"

Pike's jaw worked. "The records don't say. The records just say the candles were already burning, and that they have never gone out." He glanced down into the warm dark. "Three thousand years, give or take. Every archivist who's ever tried to count them has lost the number."

Coral said nothing, but his thief's eye was working, cataloguing the descent the way it catalogued everything: the walls tooled with script that predated every alphabet he'd ever fenced; the niches cut at intervals, each holding a stub of white wax burned down to nothing and yet somehow still alight, giving off light without diminishing, warmth without smoke; the air itself, which tasted faintly of tallow and old paper and, underneath both, of something sweeter and worse — the smell of a memory being held very carefully, the way you'd hold a bird.

He had spent his whole life handling memories. He knew their weight, their temperature, the way grief ran cold and first love ran warm and shame ran hot enough to blister if you gripped it wrong. And the air of this place was *thick* with them. Not stolen ones. Not traded ones. Held ones. Memories that had not been taken from anyone but given, signed away, sealed in contract and carried down these stairs to serve.

He thought of Beatrix walking barefoot ahead of him, and his chest did something complicated and painful.

"We're close," she said.

It was the longest sentence she'd produced in days, and her voice came out strange — not the wordless tongue that made candles gutter, but something between it and Common, as though the mountain itself were translating for her. Everyone stopped. Even Vess.

"You can feel it?" Coral asked carefully.

Beatrix looked back at him. In the candlelight from below her eyes were enormous and dark, and for a moment he saw past the child to something older looking out — something patient as the stairs, something that had walked this descent before.

"I remember the doors," she said. "I remember counting them."

Then she turned and kept walking, and no one found anything to say after that.

* * *

The last stair opened onto a landing, and the landing opened onto the Court, and Coral Zhou — who had seen the free cities burn, who had watched a memory-lord weep gold, who had stolen a dying man's entire childhood while holding his hand — forgot, for a long moment, how to breathe.

It was a cavern that had no right to exist inside a mountain. Vaulted ceilings arched overhead into darkness the candles could not reach, and the pillars supporting them were carved from the living rock in the shapes of trees — thorn trees, their branches spreading wide and bare, and hanging from every branch was a lantern. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Each lantern held a single flame behind horn-thin panes of amber glass, and each flame burned with the same impossible steadiness, and together they made a sky of fixed stars over a city that spread below the landing in terraces and bridges and stairways without end.

But it was not the scale that stole his voice. It was the people.

They filled the terraces like a congregation, like a harvest, like a tide. Hundreds upon hundreds of figures in grey robes, moving through the vaulted halls in slow processions, and as Coral watched, one of them paused beneath a thorn-tree pillar, opened a small brass door in its trunk — and stepped *inside*. The pillar closed around them. For a heartbeat the carved branches brightened, every lantern flaring gently, and then the figure simply wasn't there anymore, absorbed into the archive as smoothly as a page slid into a book.

"They're shelving themselves," Maren whispered. Her professional composure — the flat calm she wore like armor — had cracked clean through. "Saints' mercy, they're *filing themselves*."

"Not themselves." Pike's voice was thin. "What's left of themselves. Look at their faces."

Coral looked, and wished he hadn't.

The robed figures had faces, yes — but faces like rooms after the furniture has been sold. Eyes open, breathing, walking, bowing to one another with perfect courtesy. And empty. Not hollow the way the tollkeepers above were hollow, scraped out by small takings over years. These were *complete* emptinesses. Whole people who had signed away everything except the part that could keep a shelf in order, and what remained walked and served and waited with the placid devotion of a dog whose master is also its cage.

One of them turned as their party came down onto the first terrace, and smiled at them — a genuine smile, warm and welcoming, the smile of a good host.

"Visitors," it said, in a voice like dry pages turning. "How lovely. You've come to stay."

"Just passing through," Vess said, stepping forward, her captain's voice iron-flat. "Business with the Archivist."

Something moved behind the creature's emptied eyes — not recognition, exactly. Retrieval. "Ah. The smuggler Karo." Its head tilted with terrible gentleness. "You were here once before. Long ago. Two guests left through your hands and never paid their final installment." The smile widened by a degree. "We do not forget installments."

Coral felt Vess go rigid beside him, felt twenty years of guilt pass through her body like current through wire. But it was Beatrix who moved — Beatrix who walked straight up to the robed figure and stood before it, small and barefoot and utterly unafraid, and spoke one word in her older-than-kingdoms tongue.

The word hit the terrace like a dropped bell.

Every lantern in sight flared. Every robed head within earshot turned — hundreds of them, all at once, the motion smooth and synchronized as a field of wheat bending in wind — and every emptied face looked toward the small girl standing in their midst, and Coral understood, with a horror that arrived in his stomach before his mind could name it, that he was looking not at servants of the archive.

He was looking at the archive. At its living, walking, waiting body. And it had just heard a word it recognized.

The robed figure bowed — lower than courtesy required, lower than Coral had seen anything bow in his life — and when it rose, its smile had changed. The warmth was gone from it. What remained was reverence, and underneath the reverence, something that might have been fear.

"The little archivist," it breathed. "Returned at last. She will want to see you." A pause, delicate as a knife laid down on silk. "*They* will all want to see you."

"Who's *they*?" Coral asked, before he could stop himself.

The figure's eyes slid to him for the first time, and studied him — really studied him, the way an appraiser studies a piece that has come home — and Coral felt, with sick certainty, that it was reading him the way he read sleeping merchants. Turning pages. Checking a catalogue entry against the original.

"Why, your family," it said pleasantly. "All of you."

* * *

They were given rooms. That was the worst part, Coral thought later, sitting on a bed of grey linen in a guest chamber carved high into the cavern wall, watching candlelight move across stone that had been polished by centuries of devoted hands. They had expected chains, tolls, interrogation. Instead a procession of the emptied had escorted them with grave hospitality through halls lined with thorn-tree pillars — and Coral had made himself count the brass doors, made himself do the arithmetic, and the arithmetic had nearly broken him.

Four hundred pillars in the great hall alone. He'd counted eleven hundred doors before he forced himself to stop, and they had passed perhaps a tenth of the city. Somewhere below, in vaults stacked like honeycomb, were tens of thousands of souls serving out contracts written in ledgers by parents who owed debts, by debtors who despaired, by frightened people who had been promised — he would have bet his whole inventory — that the service would be gentle. That it would be *worth it*.

And Beatrix had until solstice to become one more lit window in one more thorn tree.

He found her at the chamber's window ledge — there were no windows, only openings onto the vast glowing dark — sitting with her knees drawn up, watching the processions below. Maren was asleep in the corner, exhausted from keeping her stitching anchored through the descent. Vess and Pike had gone to argue strategy in low voices by the door. And the child sat alone at the edge, silhouetted against ten thousand steady flames, and Coral sat down beside her because there was nowhere else in the world he could bear to be.

"You shouldn't have used the word," he said quietly. "Back there. You showed them what you are."

Beatrix didn't look at him. When she answered, her voice had settled into something almost fully Common, the mountain's translation loosening as she went deeper, or perhaps as she remembered more. "They already knew. They've always known. I wasn't hiding from them." A pause. "I was hiding from myself."

"Beatrix—"

"My parents didn't sell me because they were cruel." The words came out level and terrible, recited rather than spoken, like a lesson learned for an examination. "They sold me because the alternative was Tam. The debt only wanted one child. One was enough. One was *cheaper*." Her small hands tightened on her knees. "I volunteered. I raised my hand. I walked down these stairs holding my mother's sleeve, and I counted the doors so I could tell her how far we'd come, so she wouldn't be scared. Four hundred and six. Then they took my voice so I couldn't count anymore, and they sent me up and out with the ledger page in my coat, and I don't remember whether she cried because they took that too."

The candlelight didn't flicker. Nothing in this place flickered. Coral realized his eyes were burning anyway.

"That's why you came to my door," he said. "The storm night. You weren't lost."

"I was *due*." She finally turned to face him, and her expression was calm in a way no seven-year-old's should ever be, calm the way the flames were calm, calm the way the emptied were calm — and that resemblance frightened him more than anything he'd seen below the mountain. "Solstice is nine days away. My installment comes due. And I came to find the one man in Thornfell who steals things back." Something moved behind her eyes then — something young, something *hers*, surfacing through the calm like a swimmer through dark water. "Was I right? About you?"

Coral looked out over the kingdom of the sold — the terraces, the bridges, the thorn trees bearing their ten thousand patient lights, the whole vast machine of owed and owned and archived — and he thought about his rule. His one rule, the only thing he'd ever owned outright: never keep anything for yourself. Take, trade, spend, pass along. Empty hands, safe hands.

Nine days. One child. Ten thousand locked doors.

"Yeah," he said. "You were right about me."

And that was when someone knocked at the chamber door — soft, courteous, three measured taps — and a voice like dry pages turning said through the wood:

"She will see the thief now. Come alone."
