## 11. Audience with the Archivist

The summons came as a candle.

It arrived at their borrowed cell in the guest vaults — a single white taper on a black lacquered tray, carried by a servant whose face was smooth as an egg and whose eyes held nothing at all. The candle burned with a flame that leaned against the draftless air, bending always toward Coral, like a compass needle finding north in him.

Pike stared at it and went gray around the mouth. "That's a *summons*," he whispered. "I've seen them in the ledgers. Nobody's been summoned in two hundred years. The Archivist doesn't summon. The Archivist *files*."

"Then I'm being filed." Coral picked up the candle. It was warm in his hand, warmer than flame should make it, and it hummed faintly against his palm — a sound he felt more than heard, like a word spoken just below the threshold of sense. "Where do I go?"

"You don't go anywhere." Pike's hands were shaking. He was a small man made smaller by the vaults, by the endless shelves of glowing spines that lined every corridor of this place, each one a soul bound into service, each one humming its own low note until the whole Undercourt sang like a struck bell that never finished ringing. "It goes where you go. You light it, and you walk, and the flame finds the way."

Maren touched Coral's sleeve. Her fingers were ink-stained from the night's work, stitching disguises into their memories so the patrols would read them as clerks, as couriers, as no one worth stopping. "You don't have to answer it."

"Yes, I do." He managed something that might have been a smile. "When has refusing an audience ever gotten anyone anything but dragged?"

Beatrix stood apart from them, near the cell's one window — a slit of stone that looked out onto nothing, onto the deep dark of the mountain's heart. She had been quiet since the descent, quieter than even her usual silence, and when Coral looked at her now she was watching the candle with an expression he had never seen on her face before. Not fear. Something older than fear. Recognition, maybe. Or grief for a thing she couldn't name.

She signed to him, quick and small: *Don't let it take your voice.*

"I don't intend to let it take anything," he said.

But the candle flickered when he lied, and all three of them saw it do so.

* * *

The way to the Archivist was not a corridor. It was a reading.

Coral walked with the candle held before him, and the shelves parted for him — not physically, not at first, but in the way that meaning parts for a reader. A wall of bound souls would stand before him, ten thousand spines pressed shoulder to shoulder in the dark, and then the candle would gutter once, and the shelf would simply be *elsewhere*, and the path would continue. It was like walking through a book being written around him. The air tasted of vellum and tallow and, beneath both, something mineral and cold, the taste of stone that has been listening for a very long time.

He passed the archives' working floors. He could not help looking. In vast circular chambers lit by candles that needed no tending, figures moved between the shelves — the sold souls, the living archives, dressed in gray robes that matched the gray of the stone. Some shelved. Some dusted. Some simply stood, faces tilted up toward ceilings they could not see, mouths slightly open, as though listening to a song only they could hear. And here and there among them, Coral saw what the market rumors had only hinted at: souls being *read*. A clerk would draw a slim volume from a shelf — a volume with a face, with breathing, with eyes that tracked the reader's hands — and open it, and press two fingers to the page, and the soul's mouth would move, silently reciting whatever memory the clerk required. A wedding day. A betrayal. The smell of a kitchen fifty years gone. All of it filed, cross-referenced, lent out to whoever held sufficient rank.

*They don't die down here,* Coral thought. *That's the horror of it. They're kept.*

One of the standing souls turned its head as he passed. An old woman, or someone shaped like one; her face had gone smooth in places, worn blank by handling, the way a coin loses its profile. But her eyes were still hers, still wet and human, and they fixed on Coral with an expression he recognized because he wore it himself most days: the look of someone trying to remember why they are standing in a room.

She mouthed something at him. No sound came. But Coral had spent twenty years reading lips across smugglers' tables and tollkeepers' booths, and he caught it anyway:

*Run.*

Then the candle pulled him onward, and the shelves closed behind him like water over a dropped stone.

* * *

The Archivist's hall was not grand, which frightened Coral more than grandeur would have. He had expected a throne room. What he got was a study — a long, low chamber lined floor to ceiling with ledgers, real ones, paper ones, the kind written by hand, and in the center of it a plain desk, and behind the desk a figure that might have been anyone's grandfather if grandfathers were carved from candle smoke and patience.

The Archivist did not look up when Coral entered. It was writing. Its pen moved with the dry whisper of a moth against glass, and the sound filled the room the way the shelves' humming filled the corridors, total and inescapable. Coral counted to sixty before the pen stopped.

"Coral Zhou," the Archivist said. Its voice was mild. It was the mildest voice Coral had ever heard, and it made the back of his neck prickle like a blade laid flat against skin. "Memory-thief. Trader in secondhand selves. The man who bought the Vane merchant's first love last spring and sold it back to him at solstice, doubled, so the poor fool got to fall in love twice." The figure looked up.

Its face was unremarkable. That was the trick of it, Coral realized — the same trick he used, the same craft. A face you forgot the moment you looked away. Eyes the color of tallow. Hands folded neatly on the desk, ink-stained to the second knuckle.

"Do you know what I admire about your work?" the Archivist continued, in the tone of a teacher drawing out a favorite pupil. "Your *restraint*. Everyone else in your filthy trade takes greedily and keeps greedily. Hoarders, all of them. Their minds become attics. But you—" a thin smile, "—you take nothing for yourself. Twenty years of theft, and your own skull is empty as a chapel after the war. Do you know how rare that is? How *useful*?"

"I've been told I'm useful before," Coral said. "Usually right before someone tries to spend me."

"Oh, I'm not going to spend you." The Archivist rose. It was taller standing than sitting, but not much — a stooped, domestic shape, cardigan-dark robes, slippers that made no sound on the stone. It drifted along its shelves, trailing one finger across the ledger spines the way a man strokes a cat. "I'm going to *read* you. There's a difference. Spending consumes. Reading merely... clarifies."

"I didn't come here to be clarified."

"No," the Archivist agreed pleasantly. "You came here to steal a contract out of my sealed vault. Don't look surprised — the vault is sealed against thieves, Mr. Zhou, not against *me*. I know everything that happens inside my mountain. I have known since the moment you passed the sunken gate. I let you come because I wanted to see the craft up close." It turned. The tallow eyes gleamed. "And because I wanted to know what you'd do when you learned the truth."

"What truth?" Coral kept his voice level. Beneath the levelness, his heart was doing something ugly and fast. Twenty years of practice held his face still. Twenty years of practice had never once held his hands still, and he clasped them behind his back now, hard, knuckle grinding knuckle.

"The truth about your emptiness." The Archivist came around the desk. It moved close — too close, closer than any stranger should stand, and Coral held his ground because retreating felt like losing. "You believe you were robbed. It's a comfortable story. A child in the Thornfell roads, parents dead or worse, some passing thief or hedge-witch or hollow man stripping his childhood out of him for pocket money. It explains the gaps. It explains why you can't remember your mother's face, your father's name, the village that made you. It lets you hate someone. Hatred is fuel, and you've run on it beautifully for two decades."

"Get to the point."

"The point," the Archivist said softly, "is that no one robbed you."

The words went into Coral like cold water into a crack in stone — painless at first, and then everywhere.

"You were born here." The Archivist's voice dropped into something almost gentle, which was somehow worse than cruelty. "Below. In these stacks. Your mother was a bound soul — a singer, a lovely one, her spine is four rows from the west door, and she still sings when the clerks need lullabies for the new arrivals. Your father was a witness-clerk. Signatory class. You were born in the reading rooms, Coral. The first language you ever heard wasn't the surface tongue. It was hers. It was *this place's*."

"That's a lie." His voice came out strange. Distant. As if someone else were saying it. "I remember roads. Snow. Vess Karo's deck. I remember—"

"You remember what you bought your way into." The Archivist tilted its head. "At age nine. Do you truly think a nine-year-old escapes the Undercourt? Children don't escape, Mr. Zhou. Children are *inventory*. But children can be *sold*, and a clever, frightened, furious nine-year-old with a thief's instincts and nothing left to lose can negotiate. You did. You offered the only currency you had. Everything. Your name. Your origin. Your mother's face. Your father's voice. Every year of yourself down to the smell of the blanket you slept under. You sold your entire childhood to buy a door to the surface, and the door opened, and you walked through it, and you have spent twenty years calling the hole where you used to be somebody else's crime."

The room tilted. Or Coral did. He put a hand against a shelf to steady himself and felt the bound souls shiver at his touch, dozens of small warm tremors running down the spines like wind through wheat.

"No," he said.

"You kept one thing." The Archivist smiled again, thinner. "Of course you did. Even the disciplined keep one thing. We'll come to that later, perhaps. When you're ready to pay with it."

"I don't—"

"*You don't remember,*" the Archivist finished for him, mild as milk. "That's rather the entire point of the transaction. But I remember. I remember everything filed under this mountain, and you, Coral Zhou, are filed. Deeply. Would you like to hear your original designation? You had one. Every archive-child does. Yours was—"

"Don't." The word tore out of him rawer than he'd meant it to. His hands had come unclasped; they were shaking openly now, and he hated them for it. "Don't say it."

"Why not? It's yours. It's the truest thing you own." The Archivist leaned in, and its breath smelled of snuffed wicks. "Unless you'd prefer to go on being nobody. Nobody survives well, I'll grant you. Nobody belongs to no one. But nobody also has nothing to lose, and I find, in my long experience, that people with nothing to lose eventually do something *stupid* and *brave* and *expensive*. Like coming here. Like falling in love with a mute child's cause. Like planning, even now, even while we speak, some desperate little forgery with your disgraced clerk and your stitch-witch." It straightened, and its voice returned to its ordinary mildness, conversational, terrible. "So. Here is my offer, thief. Walk away. Take the girl's contract off your table. Go back to your cabin and your lonely economy and your beautiful empty head, and I will return to you the one memory you miss most — your mother singing. Filed, preserved, flawless. You could have it back whole. You could hear her again."

For one obscene moment — one heartbeat, no more — Coral wanted it so badly the wanting whited out everything else. The snow, the debt, Beatrix's small bare feet in the storm, Pike's trembling hands, Maren's ink-stained grip on his sleeve. Gone. Just the candle-hall and the offer and the image rising unbidden: a woman's voice, a lullaby in a language older than kingdoms, sung to a boy whose name he would get back with it—

He thought of Beatrix at the window slit, signing: *Don't let it take your voice.*

"No," Coral said.

The Archivist sighed, and it sounded genuinely disappointed, and somehow that was the worst thing yet. "I hoped you'd say that. The brave ones always do. Very well." It returned to its desk, sat, took up its pen. "Then run along and finish your little plan. The vault will be exactly where you expect it. Do give my regards to Karo — tell her the old bargain still stands, if she remembers what it was." The pen began to move. The whispering filled the room again. "Oh — and Coral. One more thing."

He was already at the door. He stopped. He did not turn around.

"When you find the contract — when you finally hold it in your hands and read the signatures — look closely at the witness line." The pen scratched on, unhurried. "You may recognize the hand."

* * *

The candle walked him back. He barely noticed the shelves parting. His body knew the route home the way a riverbed knows rain.

In the cell, Maren took one look at him and started forward, and Beatrix was there first — small, silent, gripping his hand in both of hers, staring up at his face with those too-old eyes, searching it. Coral knelt. His knees hit stone and he barely felt it.

"He says I was born here," he heard himself say. His voice sounded scraped, borrowed, secondhand. "He says I sold myself out. My whole childhood. My name. Everything." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Twenty years I built a life on top of a grave and I never once asked whose grave it was."

Beatrix's grip tightened. Then, slowly, she reached up and pressed her palm flat against his chest, over his heart, the way you'd steady a book that was falling open. And she signed, one-handed, deliberate, each gesture a nail driven clean:

*Whoever you were — I know who you are. That's the one they can't file.*

Coral closed his eyes. Behind them, unbidden, the Archivist's final words turned over and over like a coin that wouldn't stop spinning.

*Look closely at the witness line. You may recognize the hand.*

"Pike," he said, opening his eyes. "How soon can you get us into that vault?"
