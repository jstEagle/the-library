## 13. Coral Meets His Father

The archive stacks went down farther than the mountain had any right to be deep.

Coral had counted the stairwells as they descended — seven turns, each one colder than the last, each one lit by candles that burned without flickering, as though the air itself had been trained to stillness. Pike walked ahead of him with a clerk's lamp, its shuttered beam cutting a narrow gold seam through the dark. Neither of them spoke. In the Undercourt, Coral was learning, speech was a kind of spending, and everything here was accounted for.

The shelves rose on either side of the corridor like the ribs of some drowned cathedral. But these were not shelves for books.

They were shelves for people.

Each alcove held a soul — or what remained of one — suspended in a pane of amber light, folded into a shape like a sleeping moth. Some glowed steady and warm. Others guttered, thin as breath on winter glass. Small brass plates were riveted beneath each one, and on each plate, in a script older than any kingdom Coral had ever robbed, a name. Or rather: not a name. A *number*. Names, he understood now, were the first thing the Undercourt took. They were too heavy to shelve.

Coral kept his hands in his pockets and his thief's face on, which was easy, because his thief's face was the only face he owned. Inside, something was screaming in a register he didn't have words for. He had spent fifteen years lifting memories out of other people's heads and telling himself it was honest work — that memory left unguarded was fair game, that everyone sold their pasts eventually, in one way or another. He had built a whole philosophy on that lie, mortar by careful mortar, and here, in this candlelit vault of moth-winged souls, the lie stood exposed in its final form. This was where the trade ended. This was the warehouse at the bottom of the world.

"Third gallery," Pike whispered. "West branch. That's where the clerks work."

"The clerks," Coral said. "Not the archives."

"They're the same thing, down here." Pike's voice had gone strange, flattened, the way it did whenever he spoke about his old life. "A clerk who files souls long enough becomes one. The Archivist calls it *integration*. It's supposed to be an honor."

Coral looked at him. "Was it offered to you?"

Pike's jaw tightened. "I ran instead." He lifted the lamp higher. "Come on. If we're seen, we're inventory."

* * *

They found him at the end of the west branch, at a desk no different from ten thousand others, transcribing.

He was an old man, or shaped like one — hollowed the way driftwood is hollowed, scooped out gently over years rather than broken. His robe was grey wool gone soft with age. His hands moved across the page with a scribe's perfect patience, copying names into numbers, numbers into smaller numbers, and there was something in the economy of those movements that made Coral's stomach turn over, because he knew them. Not from memory. From the body. The way a man holds his wrist when he writes. The slight hitch of the left shoulder before the pen touches down. Coral had watched his own hands do that a thousand times in a thousand stolen rooms, and had never once wondered where the habit came from.

Because he remembered nothing. Because he had sold it all. Because the boy who might have known this man's face had traded himself away to buy a road out of the dark, and the road had cost exactly what it was worth.

"Clerk Halven," Pike said softly, using the honorific the way you'd use a warning. "We're here to verify a contract. Old one. Pre-Reformation."

The old man looked up. And Coral felt the floor of the world tilt.

His eyes were pale grey, washed nearly colorless, the eyes of someone who has looked at candlelight so long that daylight would blind him. They were gentle eyes. That was the unbearable part. Coral had rehearsed hatred the whole way down — had built it carefully in the stairwells, fed it on the image of Beatrix barefoot in the snow, her voice sold off her tongue, her name struck from her plate. He had composed speeches. He had imagined gripping those soft old wrists and demanding *why*, imagining every answer, readying himself for cruelty or cowardice or greed, the three currencies he understood.

What he had not prepared for was kindness.

"You'd be wanting the Reconciliation ledgers, then," the old man said, and his voice was mild as milk. "Second gallery. Though I should tell you, young sir, the pre-Reformation indexes were recopied after the fire. Some of the original signatures survive only in excerpt." He smiled, apologetic, folding his hands over his work. "Paper remembers better than we do. That's rather the point of us."

*Paper remembers better than we do.*

Coral stood very still. Somewhere behind his ribs, a door he hadn't known was locked strained against its hinges.

"Do you—" His voice came out wrong. He cleared his throat and tried again, keeping it flat, professional, a stranger asking after records. "Do you ever remember anything? Yourself, I mean. Before the desk."

The old man considered the question with genuine, unhurried interest, the way a scholar considers weather.

"Oh, bits," he said. "A smell, mostly. Pine smoke. And a song — no, not a song, only the *shape* of a song, the place where a voice would go if there were a voice. My mother's, I think. Or someone's mother. It's difficult, after a while, to know which memories are yours and which have merely been filed near you." He chuckled, dry and unbothered. "The Archivist says identity is a filing error. I've come to find that comforting."

*A filing error.* Coral's hands curled inside his pockets. He thought of the amber panes in the galleries behind them, hundreds of them, thousands, each one a person reduced to a record, each one perhaps comforted by the same doctrine, the same gentle arithmetic that turned theft into order and erasure into peace. He thought of Beatrix upstairs in Maren's keeping, fading by the day, her edges going soft as chalk in rain — and somewhere in the west branch of the third gallery, this hollowed, smiling old man had signed the page that put her there.

Fury rose in him like floodwater. It was clean, it was simple, it was *earned*. He let it rise. He wanted to feel it. Fifteen years of feeling nothing but competence, and here at last was a rage with a face on it.

"You signed a contract," Coral said. "Twenty-odd years ago. A child. Sold to cover her parents' debt." He stepped closer, and let the flatness drop from his voice just enough. "Say you remember."

The old man's pen stopped.

For a long moment he looked at Coral with those washed-grey eyes, and something moved behind them — not recognition, not guilt, but a faint disturbance, the way still water is disturbed by a stone dropped far upstream. He set down his pen with great care, aligning it parallel to the page's edge, and pressed his palms flat against the desk as if to hold the world in place.

"A child," he repeated. "There were... there were children, in those years. The famine contracts. Whole families, sometimes." His brow creased, and the crease looked unfamiliar on his face, like a fold in paper that had been stored flat too long. "I witnessed them. That was my function. A witness must be neutral. A witness must not—"

He stopped. His gaze had drifted, without his quite seeming to command it, to Coral's face. To his hands. To the hitch of his shoulder as he stood.

"Oh," the old man said, very quietly.

It was not a word of recognition. It was smaller than that. It was the sound a man makes when a memory brushes past him in the dark — close enough to feel the wind of it, too fast to catch.

"I had a son once," the old man said, to the desk, to the candles, to no one. "I believe I did. There's a gap in me, shaped like a boy. The archivists call such gaps *savings*. What you withdraw leaves a space, and the space keeps the shape of what filled it." He looked up, and smiled, and the smile was the loneliest thing Coral had ever seen. "Isn't that remarkable? You can spend a whole life and still know, somehow, what it was. The emptiness remembers."

Coral could not move.

This was the moment he had climbed seven stairwells for. This was the confrontation, the accounting, the reckoning he had carried down through the sealed passes and the toll of hollow men and the market of secondhand selves. And now that it stood in front of him, mild and grey-eyed and offering him tea it did not have, it would not hold still long enough to be hated.

Because the man was guilty — Coral was sure of it, sure down to the bone — and the man was also *gone*. The signature was his; the hand that wrote it was his; the wrist-hitch and the shoulder-hitch were his, living on in Coral's own body like ghosts in the joints. But whatever part of him had chosen to sign had been withdrawn decades ago, spent down, filed away, until what remained was a function wearing a father's shape, gentle as furniture, remembering nothing but the shape of its own emptiness.

How do you hate a man who has already been robbed?

How do you punish a theft by punishing what the theft left behind?

"You don't remember his name," Coral said. It wasn't a question. His voice sounded like someone else's.

"No." The old man said it simply, without self-pity, and that absence of self-pity was somehow worse than weeping would have been. "Names are the first expenditure. But I know he was small for his age, and quick with his hands, and that he laughed at things that weren't funny, because I know the gap laughs." He paused. "You laugh at things that aren't funny, young sir. I noticed it when you came in. You almost did, just now, at *filing error*."

Coral had. He had felt it twitch at the corner of his mouth and killed it before it lived. He had not known anyone could see that far into him. No one had ever looked at him that closely, except to price him.

"The contract," Coral said, dragging himself back to the surface, back to the job, back to the girl fading above them whose life depended on his hands staying steady. "The one with the child. Where is the original?"

"Sealed vault," the old man said promptly, glad of a task, glad of a function. "Archivist's keeping. Third door past the reconciliation gallery, black iron, no handle on the near side." Then he tilted his head, and the disturbance crossed his face again, deeper this time, a boat rocking at its mooring. "But you haven't told me why a stranger asks after a famine contract with his father's hands."

The words fell into the corridor and lay there.

Neither of them picked them up.

Coral stared at him. The old man stared back, and Coral watched — actually watched, in real time — the sentence arrive in him a second time, late, the way thunder arrives after lightning. Watched the grey eyes widen. Watched his mouth open around a word that never came, because there was no word left in him for *son*, only the gap shaped like one, and the gap had just been filled for three full seconds by a living boy with his own hands, and then emptied again, and the old man's face as it emptied was the most terrible thing in all the deep places of the world.

"I'm sorry," the old man whispered. "Forgive me. I don't know why I said that. Clerks don't speculate. Clerks file." He bent back over his page, pen trembling, and began to copy a name into a number, and the trembling got worse, and he copied anyway, because that was all he was, that was all they had left him: a hand that writes, writing.

Coral stood there while the floodwater of his fury did the thing floodwater does when it meets something it cannot drown.

It receded. And what it left behind was worse. Pity, vast and salt and cold, pooling in all the low places fury had scoured out. He had come down the mountain to confront a monster and found instead a victim of the same trade he had practiced for fifteen years — the trade he had told himself was honest, the trade whose bottom floor this was. Every memory Coral had ever lifted, every past he had ever sold in Dunmark's markets, ended here, in a grey robe, at a desk, smiling politely at the gap where its life used to be.

And the man was his father. And the man would never know it. And some ruined, practical corner of Coral's mind noted that this was, in its way, a mercy — because if the old man *did* know it, he would have to choose, and everything in the Undercourt conspired to make certain that no one down here ever chose anything again.

Save him, then. That was the other road. Break him out the way Coral had been broken out, carry him up seven stairwells into a sky he probably couldn't bear, and give him back a life shaped exactly like the gap — pine smoke and the place where a voice would go. A life made of absences. Would that be salvation, or just a crueler room?

Coral didn't know. He stood in the candle-still air between hate and rescue, and for the first time since the storm night when Beatrix knocked on his door, he had no plan at all.

"Thank you for your help, Clerk Halven," Pike said quietly, touching Coral's elbow. "We should go."

The old man didn't look up. His pen scratched. Name into number, number into smaller number. But as they turned to leave, he spoke once more, softly, to the page and not to them:

"He'd be grown now. My son. If he ever came down here, I think I'd know him by his hands."

Coral walked out of the west branch with his fists closed tight around those hands, and did not look back, because if he looked back he would stay, and if he stayed, Beatrix would fade, and he could save one soul in this mountain tonight and it was not going to be his father's.

* * *

They were halfway to the reconciliation gallery when Pike stopped walking.

"Lamp's guttering," he murmured, kneeling to shield the flame with his body. But his eyes weren't on the flame. They were fixed on the shelf beside him, on a brass plate worn nearly illegible with age, and his face had gone the color of tallow.

"What," Coral said.

"The signature on Beatrix's contract." Pike swallowed. "In the excerpt copy, the witness line — I told you I almost recognized it. I've been trying to place it since Dunmark." He looked up at Coral, and his whisper barely stirred the still air. "It's not just his handwriting, Coral. I checked it against the clerk registers while you talked to him. Halven witnessed the sale. Your father co-signed Beatrix's soul."

"And?" Coral's voice was ice. "I worked that out in the gallery. Why do you think I asked him about the contract?"

"That's not why I stopped." Pike's hand closed on Coral's sleeve, and his grip was shaking. "There's a clause. Famine contracts have a reclamation clause — the original signatory can void the debt by reclaiming it, taking it back onto his own account. It's never used, because no one down here can reclaim anything. They have nothing left to reclaim *with*." His eyes came up, wide and terrible. "But your father isn't empty, Coral. He kept something. They all keep one thing — the last thing, the thing too dear to spend. And if the original signatory reclaimed Beatrix's debt—"

The lamp flame steadied. In its small gold circle, Coral saw the shape of it entire: the contract, the clause, the hollowed gentle man at his desk who had just told him, without knowing, the one thing that could save her.

"—the debt transfers," Coral finished. His heart was slamming against his ribs like something trying to get out. "To him."

"To him," Pike said. "Or to whoever reclaims it in his name. Which means there may be more than one way to pay it." He paused, and lowered his voice to almost nothing. "Which means the original contract in the Archivist's vault isn't just evidence anymore. It's the door. And we have to steal it tonight."
