## 14. Theft of a Living Page

The descent had taken three days. Three days of rope and candle-smoke, of ledges slick with a condensation that tasted of iron and old grief, of passages so narrow that Coral had to shrug off his pack and drag it behind him by its strap, listening to it scrape the stone like something reluctant to follow.

Now they stood in the antechamber of the Archivist's vault, and the air itself felt different — thicker, sweeter, like the inside of a sealed jar. The Undercourt breathed around them in slow pulses. Somewhere far above, water dripped through the mountain's bones at the rate of one drop per heartbeat, and the whole kingdom kept time to it. Coral could feel the rhythm in his teeth.

Maren sat cross-legged on the cold floor, her stitch-bag open beside her, needles laid out in a fan like a surgeon's instruments. Each needle was bone, each threaded with hair — some hers, some bought from Dunmark's low quarter, some, she'd admitted once with a shiver, taken from the dead. She was chewing a sprig of dried yarrow, working up the nerve for what came next.

"Say it again," she said. "Slowly. What you want me to make them see."

Coral crouched beside her and spoke the lie aloud, carefully, the way he'd practiced it against the wall of their borrowed room in Dunmark until his throat ached.

"A clerk named Odell Vane entered this chamber at the second bell," he said. "He carried a ledger of tallow-tithes. He consulted the sealed shelf, took nothing, misfiled one folio, and left at the fourth bell complaining of a headache."

"And nothing else happened."

"Nothing else happened."

Maren's eyes narrowed. "You understand what I'm about to do. I'm going to unpick the seam of the night itself — the way it remembers itself — and sew in three hours that never were. The vault's wardens won't be watching the door. They'll be watching the *record*. If the record says nothing happened, then nothing happened. That's how places like this work. They don't remember events. They remember what they believe about events."

"That's why we need you," Coral said. "Pike can forge ink. You forge *time*."

Maren spat out the yarrow. "Flattery. From a thief." But there was warmth under the dryness; there always was now, fragile as frost on a windowpane, and Coral had learned not to lean on it too hard lest it crack. Found families were load-bearing structures built out of people who had all been dropped before.

She began.

It was slow work, and terrible to watch if you let yourself truly see it. She pressed two fingers to the stone floor and hummed — a low, wordless note that made the candle flames bend toward her like grass toward wind. Then she pulled a needle through the air itself, through whatever invisible fabric lay over this place, and Coral watched the light stutter. A thread of gold came away between her fingers, fine as spider silk, glimmering with everything these walls had witnessed: every clerk who had ever crossed this threshold, every bell, every breath.

"You'll feel it," she warned him, not looking up. "When I sew the false hours in, the room will try to convince *you* too. Don't argue with it. Don't correct it. Just hold your own name in your mouth and don't swallow."

*I don't have my own name,* Coral thought, and said nothing, because saying it aloud would only remind the room — and himself — of the hole where it used to be. He held instead the memory he still carried: his mother's voice, singing. He wrapped it around himself like a coat. *That's mine. That's mine. Whatever else the night claims to remember, that song is mine.*

The stitching took the better part of an hour. When Maren finally knotted the last thread and bit it clean, the antechamber looked exactly as it had — and yet it didn't. There was a softness to the shadows now, a kind of settled quality, as though the room had been told a story about itself and believed it completely. Coral tested it, walking the perimeter, touching nothing. The stones under his palm felt warm where they should have been cold, drowsy, half-asleep.

"It'll hold until dawn," Maren said. "Maybe longer, if no one looks too closely. After that the seam will itch, and this place scratches its itches with knives." She repacked her needles. "Go. And Coral — come back. All of you. I've stitched enough torn things to know that some tears don't close twice."

* * *

Pike went first.

This was Pike's country, or had been, before his disgrace — twelve years as an Undercourt clerk, twelve years of cataloguing souls the way a tax assessor catalogues livestock, before the day he'd refused to enter a child into the rolls and had been stripped of his seal and cast up through the sunken gate with nothing but the clothes he wore and the knowledge in his head. The knowledge had proved heavier than any pack. He knew the vault's geography the way a drowning man knows water.

"The corridor bends left after the third arch," he murmured, hood up, voice flattened to almost nothing. "There's a pressure plate under the second flagstone — don't step on it, step *over* it, stride long. The hollow men patrol in pairs but they don't look up. They've forgotten ceilings exist. Everything down here forgets eventually."

They moved in single file: Pike, then Coral, then Vess, with Beatrix tucked between them like a coal banked in ash. The girl walked without sound. She always did. Her bare feet found the silent path through any room as if the rooms whispered their quiet routes to her directly — and perhaps they did. Coral had stopped asking. Some of what Beatrix knew could not be asked after without breaking it.

Vess moved like a ship under bare poles, heavy and deliberate, each footfall placed rather than dropped. She had argued against coming inside at all — wanted to hold the surface gate, keep their exit alive — but Coral had overruled her, and she had swallowed it the way she swallowed everything now, with a grimace and a silence and a flask passed hand to hand afterward. Old wounds, reopened in Dunmark, had scabbed into something that looked almost like trust. Almost. Coral knew better than to test the *almost*. He'd spent his whole life testing other people's *almosts*, and it had taught him exactly how much weight they bore: less than you hoped, more than nothing.

The corridor bent left. The third arch passed overhead, carved with names worn to smooth ghosts. The second flagstone — Coral strided over it, long, feeling the faint tremor of the plate beneath, a held breath in the stone. Ahead, the corridor opened into the vault proper.

It was smaller than Coral had imagined, and vaster. A single round chamber, ceiling lost in darkness above, its walls lined from floor to unreachable height with niches — thousands of them, honeycombed, each holding a sealed vessel of dark glass. Souls. Living archives. He had seen the scale of it once already, on the descent through the public stacks, and awe had curdled in him then into horror, and the horror had since hardened into purpose. But this room was different. This room was the *private* collection. The vessels here were older, their glass smoked nearly black, their seals stamped with the Archivist's personal mark: a circle bisected by a vertical line, like an eye closed by a blade.

And at the center of the chamber, on a pedestal of black basalt, under a cage of iron filigree thin as lace: the contract.

Even from ten paces, Coral could feel it. Not see it — *feel* it. The page radiated something, a pressure, a warmth, a wrongness, like standing near a hearth that burned cold. It was a living document. Of course it was. In the Undercourt, paper was never just paper. The contract that bound Beatrix's soul was itself partly made of her — written in the ink of her parents' consent, sealed with wax pressed by a thumb that had belonged to someone Coral had almost recognized, and animated, kept alive, fed, for eleven years, on the interest of a child's fading self.

Eleven years of interest. Coral did the arithmetic and hated every number.

"The cage isn't locked," Pike whispered, and Coral flinched. "Look. The filigree hinges are free. It hasn't been locked in — I don't know how long. The Archivist doesn't lock the private vault. Nobody's ever gotten this deep with intent. Locks are for people who expect thieves."

"We're people who expect thieves," Vess muttered. "We're counting on it."

"No," Pike said, and his voice went strange. "We're counting on being *impossible*."

That was the plan, and it was Maren's plan, and it was beautiful in the way that only desperate plans are beautiful. They would not steal the contract. Stealing left a hole, and holes in the Undercourt were screams waiting to happen. Instead, Coral would leave something *in its place* — a forgery so complete that the vault would not merely fail to notice the theft, it would continue to *enforce* the forgery, believing it to be the original. A living page replaced by a sleeping one. The debt would appear intact. The Archivist would appear unrobbed. And somewhere above, in the world of weather and bread and ordinary mornings, Beatrix would simply — stop fading. The true contract, removed from the presence of the soul it bound, would lose its feeding line. Debts, Pike had explained, were not erased by running from them; they were erased by *starving* them. Take the ledger away from the debtor and the interest stops accruing. The principal remains — but principal alone, Maren had added, is something a hedge-witch can work with. Something a signature can be undone from.

All of it depended on the forgery.

Coral had made it himself, in Dunmark, over nine nights, working by tallow-candle with Pike dictating and Maren stitching fragments of authentic memory into the fibers — slivers of the real thing, harvested from the dying thief back in chapter eight of his life, if his life had chapters, which he no longer believed it did. The forged page contained actual pieces of the actual contract: a sentence here, a seal-shadow there, woven into new vellum aged with smoke and grave-dirt. It was not a copy. It was a *graft*. Half true. Which, in the economy of the Undercourt, was worth more than wholly false and cheaper than wholly true, and Coral had built his entire trade on that margin.

He approached the pedestal.

The iron lace swung open without a sound, without resistance, and Coral's skin prickled with a warning he couldn't name — the same prickling he'd felt once, years ago, opening a merchant's strongbox that had been *too easily* unlocked, and finding inside not coin but a note: *We know who you are.* He had burned the note and told himself it was a bluff. He had been younger then, and better at lying to himself.

But there was no time for caution dressed as wisdom. Dawn was a fixed point, and Maren's seam would itch when it arrived, and this room scratched its itches with knives. So Coral lifted the contract.

It was warm. Blood-warm. It weighed almost nothing and infinitely much, and when he tilted it in the candlelight he could see movement within the vellum itself — grain shifting, fibers flexing, the page breathing at the mountain's drip-pace, one contraction per heartbeat. Eleven years old and still alive. Still *hungry*. He could feel it reaching for him, faintly, the way a root reaches through soil — tasting the thief, sampling him, deciding whether he was food.

He was food. Everyone was food, down here. The difference was that he knew it, and knowing was a kind of armor, thin but real.

He slid the forgery into the cradle of the pedestal, aligned it to the shadow-marks the original had left in the dust of the basalt — Pike had insisted on that detail, the dust, the *dust*, because the Undercourt noticed displacement the way spiders notice vibration — and closed the iron lace around it with both hands, gently, the way you'd close a coffin.

Then he waited, kneeling, and watched.

The forged page lay still. Too still. The original had breathed; the forgery held its breath like a child hiding. Coral counted forty heartbeats, fifty, sixty. Nothing reached for it. Nothing tasted it. The vault's attention — that diffuse, ambient noticing, the room's slow conviction about itself — slid over the forgery and accepted it, the way a tongue accepts a false tooth.

Maren's seam was holding. The record said a clerk named Odell Vane had come and gone and done nothing, and the room believed the record, and the room was telling the wardens, and the wardens were believing the room.

Coral stood. His knees ached from the cold stone. His hands, he noticed, were trembling — not from fear exactly, or not only. From the nearness of it. He had stolen memories his entire life: love affairs, childhood summers, the taste of a dead man's wedding cake, a soldier's last sunrise. He had never stolen anything *alive*. He had never stolen anything that was *someone*. The contract in its oilcloth wrapping against his chest was Beatrix — the part of Beatrix that had been signed away, the part that had been leaking out of her for eleven years, drop by drop, the way the mountain leaked water through its bones. Carrying it, he could feel her. Faintly. A pulse not his own, a small stubborn rhythm underneath his heartbeat, like a second clock in the same case.

*Hold on,* he thought at it, wordlessly, the way you think at a fire you're trying to carry through wind. *Hold on. We're getting you out.*

* * *

The climb back through the antechamber was quieter than the descent, as exits always are, success having drained the noise out of everything. Pike checked the pressure plate's flagstone from above with a probe of wire before letting anyone cross. Vess counted heads twice, then a third time, compulsively, and Coral understood the compulsion — Vess had lost crews before, had abandoned him before, and the arithmetic of her guilt was conducted in headcounts. Four. Four. Four. Beatrix, Pike, Vess, Coral. Four.

Maren met them at the seam-line with her needles already half-packed, eyes rimmed red from the hour of humming. "Dawn's in two hours," she said. "Move."

They moved.

Through the sunken corridors, past the public stacks where the sold souls stood in their glass vessels like candles in a cathedral of candles, each one dim, each one flickering at the drip-pace, each one a person reduced to a *record of a person*, filed and shelved and consulted. Coral walked faster past them than he needed to. Looking at them too long did something to him he couldn't afford — it reminded him that he had been born among them, that the Archivist had said so, that his missing childhood wasn't stolen at all but *sold*, by his own hands, to buy the road out. He had not yet decided what to do with that knowledge. He carried it the way he now carried the contract: wrapped, warm, against the sternum, ticking.

The final gallery before the ascent was a long hall of niches, and halfway down it, Beatrix stopped.

She didn't say anything. She couldn't; her voice was still a rumor, still weeks away from returning, if it returned at all. She simply stopped walking, and turned her head, and looked at a niche at eye-height on the left-hand wall, and looked at it the way you look at a house you once lived in.

Two vessels. Side by side. Their glass less black than the others — clouded, yes, dimmed, but with a faint warmth at the core, two small lights suspended in smoke, like embers under ash. A man and a woman. Young. Coral could read almost nothing from the outside; the vessels were designed to be read only from within the Archivist's apparatus. But Beatrix didn't need apparatus. Beatrix knew them the way you know your own reflection in dark water.

Her parents. Alive. Below. Not dead — *archived*. The memory Coral had bought from the dying thief in Dunmark had shown him a glimpse of exactly this, and he had called it hope, and hope had felt like a trap, and now the trap had a shape and a location and a shelf number.

Beatrix pressed her hand flat against the glass. Both vessels brightened — minutely, measurably, the embers under the ash breathing toward her touch like flowers toward sun. Coral watched, and his chest tightened around the wrapped contract until breathing hurt.

*They can feel her.* Even archived, even sold, even eleven years drained — they could feel their daughter, and they leaned toward her, and she leaned toward them, and between them stretched the thinnest filament of contact, and the whole machinery of the Undercourt had never managed to sever it entirely. That was the flaw in the design. That was the flaw in every design the Archivist had ever built: you cannot archive love. You can shelve it, label it, tax it, starve it. You cannot make it stop reaching.

Vess put a hand on Beatrix's shoulder — rough, brief, the gesture of a woman who had buried tenderness so deep she had to dig for it every time. "Not now," she said quietly. "Not yet. When we're out, and the contract's dead, we come back for them. With lawyers. With witnesses. With the whole free city if we have to. But not now. Now we move, or dawn finds us here, and dawn down here is knives."

Beatrix obeyed. She was eleven, and obedient, and wise beyond her years in ways that made Coral's throat ache — she had chosen sacrifice once already, he would learn later, had chosen it to spare her brother, and children who have chosen sacrifice carry a patience most adults never grow. She tore her hand from the glass the way you tear a root from soil it has grown into, and the vessels dimmed again, and she wept without sound, and they climbed.

* * *

They broke the surface an hour before dawn, through the smuggler's hatch Vess had held for three days — she had indeed held it, alone, trading threats with a tollkeeper patrol and spending, Coral suspected, more of her hoarded memories than she'd ever admit — and the cold hit them like absolution. Real cold. Weather-cold. The smell of pine and snowmelt and distance. Above them, the sky was doing its pre-dawn work, bruise-purple shading to iron, the stars burning off one by one like lamps snuffed by a careful servant.

They made camp in the lee of a granite shoulder, and Maren checked the contract's wrapping, and Pike checked the forgery's alignment one last time by memory, and Beatrix slept with her head against Coral's pack, and for one hour, one hour only, everything had worked.

Triumph. Coral knew the flavor of it well enough to recognize it, though he rarely permitted himself the taste. It tasted like the moment after a lock yields. Like the second between the pick turning and the door swinging — that suspended instant when the plan has survived contact with reality and reality hasn't yet answered back.

He sat apart from the others, as he always did, keeping his small ritual of solitude, and unwrapped the contract just enough to feel its pulse against his palm. Stronger now. Already stronger. Eleven years of leakage, and one hour of separation, and the page was plumping like a dried fruit in water. Beatrix's stolen self, flowing home through the vellum. By spring, Maren estimated, the girl might have half of herself back. By summer, more. The debt was starving. The principal remained, but principal was negotiable, principal was *paper*, and paper could be burned, and they had fire.

Coral allowed himself the taste of triumph for exactly as long as it took the eastern sky to turn from iron to slate.

Then he thought about the cage.

He had meant to think about anything else — the route north, the tollkeepers, the question of Beatrix's parents, the question of his father whose signature bound her, the question of the man he himself might be. But his mind returned to the cage the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. *The filigree hinges are free. It hasn't been locked in — I don't know how long.*

*Locks are for people who expect thieves.*

He turned it over. He turned it again. And the triumph curdled, slowly, the way milk curdles in a storm — imperceptibly at first, then all at once, irreversibly.

Because here was the thing Coral knew better than any man alive: nobody leaves a treasure unlocked. Not out of arrogance. Not out of oversight. The Archivist did not *have* oversight; the Archivist was a creature composed entirely of oversight, a mind that remembered everything, that had *built* the practice of remembering everything, that had turned remembering into a kingdom and a currency and a cage. Such a mind does not forget to lock a vault. Such a mind does not neglect a hinge.

Such a mind *leaves a door open*.

Coral had seen it before. Once. Years ago, in his first year of trade, he had robbed a collector in Dunmark whose strongbox had opened at a touch, and he had taken the prize inside and fled, triumphant, and it had taken him two years to learn what the unlocked box had meant: the collector had *wanted* the theft known. Had wanted the thief identified, tracked, catalogued. The unlocked box had been a lure, and the theft had been a *registration*. Every crime committed against the Undercourt, Pike had told him once, grimly, laughing without humor, becomes an entry in its books. The court does not punish thieves. It *files* them.

And now Coral sat on cold granite in the hour before dawn, holding a living page against his chest, feeling its pulse grow stronger, feeling Beatrix sleep beside his pack, feeling his found family breathe around their small fire — and he understood, with a vertigo that made the mountainside seem to tilt beneath him, that the vault had been left unlocked *on purpose*.

That the theft had been permitted.

That somewhere below, in a chamber lined with smoked glass, a regent who remembered everything was watching a thief walk away with exactly what he'd come for — watching with the patient attention of a fisherman who has already closed his hand around the line.

The question was not whether the Archivist knew. The Archivist knew; the Archivist had always known; the Archivist had probably known before Coral himself had decided to come. The question was *why*. What did a kingdom of archives want with a thief who had already surrendered his name? What use was a man with a hole where his childhood should be — unless the hole itself was the thing they wanted? Unless the empty space was a *socket*, shaped precisely for something the Archivist intended to give back — or to fill — or to hold over him?

Or unless — and this thought arrived last and worst, the way such thoughts do — the contract itself was the lure. Not the vault. The *contract*. Let the thief steal the binding. Let the binding travel. Let the debt-starved page grow fat again in the open air, in the company of the very soul it bound — and then, at the moment of greatest hope, at the moment when Beatrix's voice might return, when her parents' vessels might be reached, when four strays might dare to believe they had beaten a kingdom —

— call the debt due.

Coral wrapped the page tighter against his chest. Its pulse beat on, steady, strengthening, innocent. Or seeming innocent. Down here, beneath Thornfell, nothing that seemed innocent was, and nothing that seemed guilty was either, and the only honest thing in the whole mountain was the drip of water keeping time, one drop per heartbeat, indifferent as snowfall.

He looked east. Slate turning to pearl. Dawn coming, and with it the itching of Maren's seam, and with the itching, sooner or later, the knives.

"Vess," he said quietly, waking her. "We need to move. Now. Tonight if we can."

Vess blinked awake the way she did everything — instantly, completely, a woman who had slept for thirty years with one boot on. She read his face in a single glance, the way she'd always been able to, the way she'd read it the day she cast him out and the day she took him back, and she didn't ask what he'd realized. She could see it. It was written across him like a watermark.

"How much time do we have?" was all she asked.

"I don't know," Coral said. "Maybe until the solstice. Maybe until the next bell."

Below them, through a mile of stone, the mountain went on dripping. One drop per heartbeat. And somewhere in the dark, in a vault that had never once been locked, a page lay in an iron cradle, holding its breath, waiting to be missed.
