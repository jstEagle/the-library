## 15. Beatrix Speaks

The dark below the mountain did not feel like night. It felt like a throat.

Beatrix had known that before she could say it — had known it in her body, in the way her skin prickled when they passed the sunken gate and the candlelight bent toward them as if drinking. The Undercourt breathed around them now, slow and wet, its corridors ribbed like the inside of some enormous sleeping animal. Coral walked ahead of her with a stolen lantern-memory cupped in his hands, a little sphere of someone else's summer noon, and it lit their way the way a held breath lights nothing at all.

She could not have said any of this aloud. That was the shape of her life: everything arriving whole inside her and going nowhere, pressing against the backs of her teeth like water behind a dam.

But here, in these halls, the pressure changed.

It began at the first vault door.

They were walking through a gallery of arches when she felt it — not heard it, *felt* it, the way you feel a name called from another room. A hum in the stone, low and patient, and beneath the hum something that was almost singing. Her chest went tight. Her hands, which had been cold since the tollgate, went warm for the first time in weeks.

Coral stopped. Pike stopped behind him, clerk's satchel clutched to his chest like a shield, and Maren's hand found Beatrix's shoulder.

"Do you hear that?" Coral whispered.

Beatrix nodded. And then — because the hum was rising through the soles of her bare feet, because it knew her, because it had been waiting down here in the dark for six years saying her name over and over into the stone until the stone learned it too —

She opened her mouth.

"*Haela.*"

One word. Her voice came out rusted, cracked down the middle, a door opening on hinges that had never once been oiled. But it came out. Sound left her body and entered the world and the world *answered* — every candle in the gallery flared upright at once, every flame leaning toward her like grass toward sun, and the hum in the walls shivered into something that was unmistakably, terribly, glad.

Maren made a small sound. Pike dropped his satchel.

Beatrix stood in the middle of all that light with her hand pressed over her own mouth, feeling the word still vibrating in her teeth. She had forgotten what it was like — to be a thing that spoke. To have the inside of you come out.

"That word," Coral said slowly. He had turned to face her fully, and in the lantern-light his face was doing something complicated, hope and fear braided together. "That's the language. The old one. The one from your ledger."

She nodded again. Her eyes were burning. She tried a second word — "*sennu*" — and the candles guttered, and somewhere far off down the corridor a bell tolled once, softly, as if the mountain itself had cleared its throat.

"Careful," Pike whispered. "The archives hear their own language. Everything spoken in it gets... written."

Beatrix looked at him. And then, deliberately, in the traders' tongue they had all learned to share on the road, she said:

"Then I will be careful what I say."

Her voice was rough as gravel and thin as winter ice, but it was hers, and hearing it made three people stare at her as if she had walked out of her own grave. Perhaps she had. She smiled at them — a crooked, unpracticed smile, a muscle long unused — and picked up Pike's fallen satchel, and handed it back to him, and kept walking.

The dam had cracked. What came through it next, no one could have stopped.

* * *

The vault where her parents were kept was deeper than grief.

They descended by stairways cut without tool-marks, spiraling down through stone that grew warmer instead of colder, until the air smelled of tallow and old paper and something faintly sweet, like flowers pressed too long in a book. Pike called it the Stacks. He walked with his head bowed the whole way, muttering shelf-numbers under his breath like prayers, and would not look at the alcoves they passed.

Beatrix looked at everything.

The souls of the sold were shelved like wine. Each alcove held a glass vessel the size of a heart, and each vessel held a soft gold light that moved — that *breathed* — and when she came close to one, the light leaned toward her the way the candles had, straining against the glass. Some of the vessels were very old. Their light had gone the color of honey held up to a dying sun. Some were newer. One, in an alcove near the stair, flickered in a rhythm she recognized before she understood why: two beats, pause, three beats, pause. A lullaby's rhythm. Her mother's lullaby, tapped out on her shoulder blade, every night of her smallest years.

She stopped walking.

The others went on a few paces before they noticed. Coral came back first. He followed her gaze to the vessel, and Beatrix watched him understand, watched his face do the thing it always did — close, like a shop pulling down its shutters — but not fast enough. Not before she saw it.

"I know them," she said. Her new voice shook. "These are my mother and father."

The vessels sat side by side in their alcove, labeled in the old language on strips of vellum yellowed with age. She could read the labels. That was the horror of it — she had never learned to read this language, and yet she could read it, the way you can read your own name written in fire. *Zhou-line debt, third portion. Zhou-line debt, fourth portion.* Third and fourth portions. Which meant there had been a first and second.

Which meant there had been someone else sold before them.

"No," she said, and reached for the glass, and Maren caught her wrist gently.

"Child — don't. Touching them unsheltered, you'd lose—" Maren stopped herself. Recalibrated. Hedge-witch's honesty won out over kindness, as it always did. "You'd lose more of yourself. You haven't got much more to lose."

"I know," Beatrix said. "I know exactly how much I have left. I've been counting since the tollgate."

And then the memory came.

It did not arrive gradually. It arrived the way the mountain's bells rang — all at once, from everywhere, filling every hollow of her. Later she would understand that standing beside her parents' archived souls had opened something in her, that proximity to what had been taken from her family was pulling the rest of the taking back into her, memory flooding home to the house it had been evicted from. In the moment she only knew that the floor tilted, and the candlelight smeared, and she was six years old again in a kitchen that smelled of woodsmoke and burnt sugar—

* * *

— the night of the sale, and the snow coming down past the window like the sky emptying itself, and her parents kneeling on the floor with the ledger open between them, and her mother crying without any sound at all, which was somehow worse than screaming.

She had been sent to bed. She had not gone. She had stood in the doorway in her nightdress, watching, the way children watch things they are too young to survive and survive anyway.

"The solstice is in nine days," her father was saying. "If we don't pay, they take the whole line. All of us. Both children."

"And if we do pay?" Her mother's voice was flat, scraped hollow. "We pay with what? We have nothing left to sell but—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Everyone in the room knew what was left.

"They'll take a child," her father said. "A child's soul, fresh, unmixed. It's worth more than anything we owe. It clears the ledger entire." His hands were shaking on the ledger page. "One child buys the other child free. Buys us free. It's — it's arithmetic, Elenna. It's only arithmetic."

"Then we choose," her mother whispered. "Gods forgive us, we have to choose which one."

And Beatrix, six years old, standing in the doorway in her nightdress, felt the strangest thing: not fear. Clarity. Cold and complete, like stepping out into the snow itself.

Because she already knew what her parents could not say. She had heard them talk for months about her brother — how Tam was quick, how Tam remembered everything, how Tam had their grandfather's head for numbers and would need it, would need a whole mind, a whole life, to become whatever he was going to become. Tam was eleven and already correcting his teachers. Tam was the family's future wearing a boy's skin.

And she was the younger one. The quiet one. The one who spoke a language nobody taught her and nobody else could understand, who stared at fires and made them lean.

She did not know yet what that language meant. She did not know she had been born with the archives' first tongue sitting in her mouth like a seed. She only knew that she was the cheaper loss.

So she walked into the kitchen. Bare feet on cold flagstones. And she said, in the traders' tongue, very clearly, so there could be no mistaking her:

"Take me. I choose it. Take me and leave Tam."

Her mother made a sound Beatrix had never heard a human being make before or since. Her father said her name like a prayer being broken in half. And Beatrix stood between them with her chin up and her fists at her sides and repeated it, because she had learned already that the world did not believe children the first time.

"I choose it. Write it down. I'll sign."

They argued until the candles burned to pools. They offered themselves instead, both of them, twice over; the ledger would not take it — a soul already partially owed could not cover a fresh debt, the ink simply refused, sliding off the page like rain off wax. They offered half of each. The ledger refused that too. In the end it was arithmetic, her father had been right about that much, and arithmetic does not care who volunteers.

But the contract needed a signature from the one who was sold. That was the old law, older than kingdoms: a soul given must be given knowingly, or the gift rots in the keeping. So they brought the page to her, and dipped the pen, and Beatrix — six years old, barefoot, snow ticking against the window like fingernails — signed her own name in a language she had never been taught, in letters that glowed briefly and sank into the paper like stones into water.

And the last thing she saw, before the men from below took her hand and the kitchen and the snow and the whole warm world folded away like a letter being closed, was her brother's face in the stairwell doorway. Awake. Watching. Eleven years old, and understanding every word.

She had done it for him. She had spent herself like a coin so that he could stay whole.

And he had watched her go, and said nothing, and let her.

* * *

Beatrix came back to herself on the floor of the Stacks, with Maren's hands on her temples and a stitch of warmth sewing through her skull, and Coral crouched in front of her saying her name — her road-name, the plain one, the one Vess's crew used — over and over like a man hailing a boat through fog.

When her eyes focused, he exhaled.

"You were gone four minutes," he said. "Four minutes and your eyes were open and there was nobody in them. Don't ever do that again."

"I remember," Beatrix said.

His face went carefully blank. "Remember what?"

"Everything." She sat up. The gold light of her parents' vessels pulsed beside her, two beats, pause, three beats, and she found she could bear to look at it now, because now she knew why it hurt. "The night I was sold. I remember all of it. I remember that I chose it."

Coral went very still. Of everyone in the world, she thought, he would understand this best — a person built out of other people's memories, learning that the cruelest ones are always the ones you gave away yourself.

"Why?" he asked quietly. "Why would a child choose that?"

"Because of my brother." The words came easier now, her voice strengthening with every use, thawing like a stream in spring. "Tam. They were going to take one of us. I was the smaller debt. I signed so he wouldn't have to." She looked at the vessels, at her parents breathing their slow gold light in their glass hearts. "They're not dead. They're *here*. They've been here the whole time, shelved, and I spent six years thinking I was an orphan."

"Beatrix." Coral's voice had gone strange. Careful. The carefulness of a man approaching a trap he has already sprung. "Your brother. Where is he now?"

She turned to look at him.

And the answer rose in her the way the language had risen, whole and certain, because some knowledge had never been taken from her at all — it had only been waiting for her voice to carry it.

"He's here," she said. "He was never sold. He *works* here."

The candles nearest them dimmed, as if the mountain were listening harder.

"He serves the Archivist. He's been serving him for six years. He signs the ledgers." Her hand drifted, without her willing it, toward the strip of vellum on her parents' alcove — toward the small neat countersignature stamped beneath the Archivist's seal, the clerk's mark she had read without reading, the handwriting she had known in her body before her mind caught up, because she had watched it move across schoolroom pages across a kitchen table across a hundred letters to their grandmother.

Two beats, pause, three beats, sang her mother's soul in its glass.

And beneath it, in her brother's tidy hand: *received and archived.*

"He knows we're here," Beatrix said. Her voice did not shake. She had spent all her shaking six years ago, in a kitchen, signing. "He's known since we came through the gate. And Tam always did protect the things he loved by putting them somewhere safe."

She stood. The gold light washed over her face.

"Somewhere," she said, "like a vault."
