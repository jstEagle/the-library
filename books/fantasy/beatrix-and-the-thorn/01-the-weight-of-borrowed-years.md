## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Years

The merchant's memory tasted of rain on hot stone.

Edmund Almeida sat across from her in the back room of the Salt Gate tea house, hands folded on the table like a man waiting for his order, and drew the thread out of her with the patience of a fisherman who has learned that the catch comes to him or not at all. She was called Mistress Halloway, and she dealt in saffron and indigo along the river road, and she had come to the Gilded Thorn because her husband had died in the spring and she could no longer bear the weight of a love that had nowhere left to go.

She did not know she was giving it away. None of them ever did. They believed they had drunk too much plum wine, that the afternoon had gone soft at its edges, that they were simply tired. Edmund kept a cup of untouched tea before himself as theater, and he let his gaze rest just past her shoulder, on the water stain shaped like a running hare, and he listened for the memory the way other men listen for a knock at the door.

There. A courtyard in some southern city where the winters were kind. A boy of sixteen holding an umbrella neither of them needed, because the girl had said she liked the rain, and the boy—Halloway's husband, forty years dead now—had held it anyway, over her, letting the water run down his own sleeve. Rain on hot stone. The smell of it. The particular courage of being seventeen and certain.

Edmund lifted it whole. It came free the way a ripe plum comes free—no tearing, no bleeding, only a small silent giving-way, and then the branch stands bare and does not remember what grew there.

Mistress Halloway blinked. Her face did something complicated and brief, like weather crossing a field, and settled into mild confusion.

"I'm sorry," she said. "What were we discussing?"

"The terms of your account," Edmund said gently. "The Thorn thanks you for your custom."

She would go home lighter. She would sleep, perhaps, without dreaming of courtyards. She might even be grateful in some animal way she could not name, the way a body is grateful when a splinter finally leaves it. And in exchange for this mercy, the Gilded Thorn would hold the first love of a dead saffron-merchant's wife until someone else wanted it—the widow of some other man, perhaps, hungry for the shape of a love she'd never had; or a young actor preparing to play a lover on some gilded stage; or one of the crown's collectors, who bought memories the way they bought relics of saints, to prove that ordinary people had once been capable of extraordinary feeling.

Everything could be sold. That was the first law of the Thorn, older than Beatrix, older than the guildhall with its thorn-carved lintel. Grief, joy, the taste of a mother's bread, the exact blue of a sky over a battlefield no map recorded—if a mind could hold it, the ledger could price it. Edmund had once catalogued a memory of snowfall sold by a man who had never seen snow, purchased secondhand from a traveler, worn soft as an old coin from passing through so many minds. It still fetched four crowns. People wanted borrowed lives the way the poor want candlelight: not because it is theirs, but because the dark is so very large.

* * *

The guildhall stood behind a chandler's shop on Candlewick Lane, which was itself behind nothing at all—a street that appeared on no tax roll, a door that most eyes slid past the way water slides past oiled stone. Edmund had helped choose the location, years ago, though no one remembered that either. He had a talent for places that did not insist upon themselves. He had a talent, if he was honest, for not insisting upon himself, and he had spent eleven years refining it into something close to art.

Inside, the ledgers waited in their alcove: forty-one volumes bound in green calfskin, each page ruled in Beatrix's own hand, each entry written in Edmund's small, precise script. He lit two lamps, took down volume thirty-eight, *Acquisitions, Autumn*, and dipped his pen.

*Mistress E. Halloway, saffron-merchant, River Road.* He wrote the description the way he always did—not the facts but the feel of it, because facts could be forged and feelings could not. *Rain on hot stone. An umbrella held badly. Courage, aged forty years in the cask. Provenance clean; seller unaware. Condition: unblemished. Reserve: twelve crowns.*

His pen hovered. Then, in the margin, smaller than the rest, he added what he always added and hoped no one ever read: *She will miss it less than she fears.*

It was not sentiment. He told himself it was not sentiment. It was provenance—a note on the condition of the seller, which was part of any honest valuation. But he wrote it every time, in every ledger, hundreds of entries now, and he knew what it was. It was the closest thing he allowed himself to a prayer.

"Still writing your little footnotes?"

Odile leaned in the alcove doorway, arms crossed, a bolt of stolen velvet over one shoulder like a general's sash. She was the Thorn's fence, which meant she moved its goods through the city's honest markets wearing a smile that made dishonesty look like neighborliness. She was the only person in the guildhall who looked at Edmund directly, and she did it now with the flat, appraising stare she used on everything she meant to keep.

"Provenance is part of the record," Edmund said.

"Provenance." She spat the word out like a pip. "You write elegies in the margins and call it bookkeeping." She dropped the velvet onto the table. "Beatrix wants you. Upper room. And Edmund—" she paused at the stair, and something in her voice shifted, some grain of seriousness in all her lacquer, "—she doesn't look well today. Worse than usual."

He capped his ink. Around him the guildhall went about its evening: Corvin arguing price with a client who wanted to forget his brother entirely, root and branch, and couldn't afford it; the twins, Sable and Sennet, sorting a crate of childhood summers into lots. Eleven years Edmund had worked among them, and they trusted him the way furniture is trusted—present, useful, beneath notice. Corvin thought him dull. The twins thought him duller. Only Odile, and Beatrix, seemed to suspect there was a room inside him with the lamps lit.

That was the trick of survival in the Thorn, and Edmund had mastered it before he turned twenty: be the ledger, not the entry. Be the hand that writes, never the story written down. A man who takes memories for a living learns quickly what happens to thieves who are interesting. Interesting men get counted. Counted men get spent.

So Edmund Almeida kept his own mind like a locked shopfront: nothing displayed, nothing wasted, the shutters always half-drawn. He had sold nothing of himself in eleven years. He had taken almost nothing from others that wasn't paid for. He walked home by different streets each night so that no one could chart the geography of his habits, and he slept alone in rooms chosen for their two exits, and he told himself this was prudence, this was professionalism, this was the quiet pride of a craftsman who owns his tools.

He told himself it was not loneliness.

Most nights he almost believed it.

* * *

The upper room smelled of dried feverfew and lamp oil, and Beatrix sat by the window with her hands folded on the arms of her chair like a woman posing for a portrait no painter would ever finish. She was very old. Not old the way the twins' clients were old, but old the way buildings are old, the way rivers are old—as though age were simply the material she was made of. The gifted aged strangely, those who survived long enough; their years pooled in them rather than passing through.

"You logged the Halloway woman," Beatrix said. It was not a question. Nothing with Beatrix ever was; questions implied doubt, and doubt implied that the world might surprise her, and she had long since stopped allowing it that courtesy.

"Twelve crowns reserve. Clean provenance."

"Mm." She turned from the window, and her eyes found him with that terrible directness, that look that made Edmund feel like a ledger opened against his will, every margin exposed. "Do you know why I keep you writing the entries, Edmund? Corvin has a fairer hand. Sable counts faster than you."

"Because I don't steal from the stock." It was true, but it was not the answer, and he saw her know it.

"Because you understand that a memory is not a thing," Beatrix said. "It is a life, folded small. Every entry you write is a life you are carrying. Most of our brothers have forgotten that, and so they are careless, and careless men make noise." She coughed then, briefly, wetly, into a cloth she produced from her sleeve, and folded the cloth away again with the same unhurried precision, and pretended it had not happened, and let him pretend it too.

Outside the window, the lamplighter was making his rounds along Candlewick Lane, and the first cold of the season had come into the air, the faint iron smell of frost promised before morning. Winter was coming early this year; Edmund could feel it in the way the city held itself, shoulders up, breath short.

"There is an entry coming," Beatrix said quietly, "that I will ask you to write soon. One entry only. And afterward—" she paused, and for the first time in eleven years Edmund watched her look away from him, out at the darkening lane, at the lamplight failing to reach the end of the street, "—afterward, you must promise me you will never sell it. Whatever they offer you. Even if they offer you your life."

The lamplighter finished his round. The last light on Candlewick Lane guttered, steadied, burned.

Edmund heard himself say, "I promise," and understood, in the way one understands distant thunder, that he had just agreed to carry something heavier than any entry in forty-one volumes—and that whatever Beatrix was about to place in his keeping had already begun, somewhere deep below the floor of his own mind, to wait.
