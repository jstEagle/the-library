## 2. Proclamation at the Salt Gate

The heralds came at dawn, when the fog off the harbor was thickest and the city's eyes were still bleary with sleep. Edmund heard them before he saw them—three trumpets, then the flat, practiced voice of a man who had said these words in a hundred towns and would say them in a hundred more.

He was crossing Salt Gate Square with a loaf of black bread under his arm and his collar turned up against the cold, one gray man among forty others hurrying to their trades, and for a moment he considered simply walking on. Invisibility, he had learned, was not merely a talent but a discipline. You did not look. You did not slow your step. You became, in the eyes of any watcher, furniture—a thing so unremarkable that memory itself declined to keep you.

But the crowd had already begun to clot around the notice board beneath the gate's stone arch, and the herald's voice carried the way voices do when they are paid to carry.

"By decree of His Majesty Aldric the Third, sovereign of the realm by grace of the Thornless Throne—" Edmund's jaw tightened at that old name; no one had called it that in two hundred years, and yet here it was, resurrected like something exhumed—"all persons possessing the unsanctioned art of memory-taking shall present themselves for registration within thirty days. Those registered shall be permitted to live out their natural lives in service to the crown. Those who conceal their gift—" the herald paused, wet his lips, and delivered the rest with the smooth indifference of a man reading a grain tariff, "—shall be put to death by fire."

* * *

Edmund did not run. Running was a kind of announcement.

He walked home through streets that had changed texture overnight, the way a room changes after someone dies in it. The fishwives still shouted their prices. The gulls still screamed over the gutting tables. But he caught, now and again, the small telltale hesitations: a woman glancing sideways at her neighbor as if seeing him for the first time; two men by the chandlery falling silent mid-sentence as he passed; a child pointing at an old beggar with milky eyes and being hushed, hard, by her mother.

Everyone was suddenly wondering what everyone else remembered.

That was the genius of it, Edmund thought, and the obscenity. The crown had not posted soldiers on every corner. It had posted suspicion. Every mind in the city was now a locked door behind which every other mind wanted to search—and the gifted, who had spent lifetimes learning to slip through such doors, found themselves suddenly unable to lock their own.

The Gilded Thorn's front was a countinghouse on Candlemakers' Row, all ledgers and lamp oil and respectability. Edmund let himself in with the key he kept on a cord around his neck, next to his heart, where a braver man might have kept a locket. The fire in the grate had burned down to a red eye. He fed it and stood warming his hands, listening to the building settle, and told himself the dread sitting in his chest was only the season arriving early.

It was not the season. It was frost forming, slow and precise, over everything he had spent eleven years building: the careful dullness, the ledger-hand neat enough to be forgettable, the face that made clerks ask him twice whether they'd met.

Thirty days.

* * *

By evening the countinghouse filled with guild brothers, and Edmund learned what fear looks like when it wears the faces of powerful men.

Corvus arrived first, as always, taking the master's chair without ceremony, his rings clicking against the armrests. He had taken the memory of a duel once—the whole of it, both blades, both men—and he wore the winner's confidence like a borrowed coat that fit him perfectly. Behind him came the twins, Sef and Maro, who dealt in childhoods; then Halloway, whose specialty was grief, bought cheap from the bereaved and sold dear to those who wished to feel nothing; then a dozen more, filtering in with the guilty quietness of men who had all read the same notice.

"The edict is a bluff," Corvus said. "Or nearly one. Registration is the trap. No one registers and walks away. They take your name, your gift, your habits—and then one night there's a fire, and the record shows you came to them willingly." He rapped the armrest. "We scatter the stock. Deep vaults, split locations. We go dark until the crown loses its appetite."

"And how long does a crown lose its appetite?" Halloway asked. His voice was soft; grief-dealers learned to speak gently. "They've been hungry for two hundred years. This isn't appetite. It's digestion."

"We deny," said Sef—or Maro; even Edmund could rarely tell them apart, which was rather the point of twins in their trade. "The gift is subtle. Who can prove what we are? I've never taken a memory in my life." His smile was thin. "I'll swear it before any priest they like."

"You'd swear away your own hands," Halloway murmured, "if the price were right."

"Gentlemen." Corvus raised a hand, and the room stilled. "No one sells anyone. No one registers. We hold, we hide, we wait. Beatrix will be told when she's strong enough to be told."

At the mention of her name, something moved through the room like a draft under a door. The matriarch had not come down from her rooms in three weeks. Edmund had carried her broth up himself, had watched her hands tremble over the cup, had seen in her clouding eyes a look he recognized professionally: a woman editing herself, deciding what to keep and what to let go.

She knows, he thought. She has known since before the edict. Perhaps she has known for years.

The meeting broke into smaller, uglier conversations—vaults, routes, bribes for harbor officials—and Edmund slipped toward the door with his bread and his silence. At the threshold, Corvus caught his sleeve.

"Almeida. The ledgers."

"What of them?"

"Burn the client pages. Tonight. Names, dates, transactions—all of it. If Vane's witch-hunters kick down that door, I want them finding a countinghouse that counts nothing." Corvus's grip tightened, and for just a moment Edmund felt the brush of the man's gift against his own, probing, casual, the way one man might touch another's shoulder in passing. Edmund gave him back nothing but the memory of a bored clerk locking up shop. Corvus released him, satisfied. "You're good, Almeida. Best forgetter in the house. That's why you're still alive."

* * *

He burned the pages alone, by lamplight, and it took until the third hour past midnight.

Eleven years of entries. He had written every one in his own hand, and there was a terrible intimacy in feeding them to the flames—like burning letters from a lover, or the diary of a stranger who happened to be yourself. *Item: one first kiss, harbor-side, seller aged seventy-one, buyer wishes to feel seventeen again.* *Item: one mother's lullaby, taken from a dying woman, sold thrice.* *Item: one war, fragmentary, provenance disputed.*

The paper curled and went orange, then black, then to flakes that floated up the chimney like gray moths. Each page had been a person's most precious possession, weighed and priced and logged in his neat clerk's hand, and now each was smoke, and the smoke smelled faintly sweet, like sugar left too long in a pan.

When the last page was ash, Edmund sat back on his heels and understood that he had accomplished nothing.

The ledgers were copies. The original stock lived in heads—his head, chiefly, the guild's deep archive, the vault no thief could crack because its door was flesh. Every transaction he had ever recorded, he had also memorized; that was the archivist's burden and the archivist's craft. The crown could burn paper until the rivers ran black. It could not burn what lived in him.

And neither, he realized, with a cold that had nothing to do with the season, could he.

There were things in his mind he had never chosen to carry. Eleven years of inventory, yes—but deeper than that, older than that, something sealed. Something Beatrix had put there, long ago, in a season he could not quite remember, though he had tried. A locked room in the house of himself. He had stopped knocking on its door years ago, the way one stops asking a silent relative about the family scandal.

Now, sitting in the ash-smelling dark, he found himself looking at that door again. And the door, he thought—or imagined, or dreamed, or remembered imagining—seemed very slightly ajar.

From beyond it, faintly, came a smell of orchards burning.

* * *

He slept badly and woke to frost on the inside of the window glass, feathered white across the pane like something trying to get in. The edict had been nailed to every gate and churchyard post in the city overnight, and by noon the first denunciations had begun. A hedge-witch turned in by her own son. A ferryman dragged from his boat, screaming that he had never taken anything from anyone, that his gift was only a knack for knowing where lost things were—as if the crown would care about the distinction.

On the fourth day, the first burning was held in Salt Gate Square, in the same spot where the herald had spoken, and the city came to watch because cities always come.

Edmund went too. He hated himself for it and went anyway. A man in his trade needed to know the shape of the thing hunting him.

They built the pyre low and efficient, no spectacle wasted. The hedge-witch was old and small and did not struggle; she looked out over the crowd with the mild curiosity of someone reading a room she was about to leave. Edmund stood at the edge of the press of bodies, gray and collared and invisible, and made himself watch, because forgetting was his gift and he refused to spend it here.

But as the smoke rose, something in him rose with it.

Not horror—he knew horror, catalogued it, filed it. This was older. The smell of pitch and burning fruitwood reached him and unlocked, for half a heartbeat, that sealed door again, and this time it did not creak—it *swung*, and he was standing in an orchard under a white sky, and the trees were heavy with ripe apples and on fire, and banners he did not recognize hung scorched from the branches, and somewhere ahead a man was being crowned with a circlet of living thorns while ten thousand voices roared a name Edmund almost—

The vision snapped shut. He was in the square, the crowd roaring around him, the witch already ash. His hands were shaking. Frost had formed on his eyelashes, though the day was not so cold as that.

Around him the crowd dispersed, satisfied, chattering about supper and taxes and the price of coal, and Edmund stood alone in the trampled snow thinking: *thirty days, they said, but the seal is failing faster than that, and whatever Beatrix buried in me is waking up—and somewhere in this city, a man named Vane is already counting the days with me.*

That night, a letter arrived at the countinghouse bearing the seal of the Crown Inquisition. It was addressed, in a fine and patient hand, not to the guild.

To *Edmund Almeida*, keeper of ledgers.

Someone had finally seen him.
