## 3. What Beatrix Kept

The pain came at dusk now, punctual as a debt collector. Beatrix Voss had spent seventy years teaching the gifted of the Gilded Thorn that memory was currency, and currency obeyed laws: it could be earned, hoarded, stolen, squandered. Her own body had become a ledger in arrears. Every evening the ache arrived to collect what the years had borrowed, and every morning she woke owing more.

She sat by the window of her rooms above the guildhall and watched the lamplighters work their way down Tallow Row, small flames blooming one after another like a thought being remembered slowly. Below, the city of Corvane went about its business of forgetting—drunkards forgetting their sorrows, merchants forgetting their debts, lovers forgetting old wounds so they could make new ones. The Thorn fed on that forgetting. She had built it to.

Her hands would not stay warm anymore. She wrapped them around a cup of mulled wine gone cold an hour ago and felt nothing but the cup's weight, which was itself becoming a rumor.

*Soon*, she thought, and was surprised to find not fear in the word but something almost like relief, and beneath the relief, guilt, sharp as a fishhook.

Because there was Edmund.

She heard his step on the stair before she heard his knock—the same careful, apologetic tread he had used since the day she'd bought him off a slaver's barge at fourteen, all elbows and watchfulness, a boy who had already learned that being noticed was the first step toward being taken from. He'd had nothing when she found him. No name anyone honored, no gift anyone wanted. That last part had been a lie, though even he didn't know it yet, and she had made sure no one else ever learned otherwise.

"Enter," she said, before he could knock.

He came in with the day's ledger under his arm and a wet look about him, the particular dampness of a man who has stood too long in the rain pretending not to mind it. The proclamation had been nailed to the Salt Gate that morning. She had sent him to see it because someone from the Thorn needed to see it, and because she wanted to know how he would carry bad news. He carried it like a man carrying water across ice: very carefully, very slowly, spilling nothing.

"They've set the burning for the first frost," he said. "Registration first. Names taken at the customs houses." He set the ledger on her desk without being asked, squared its corners. "Maren's already fled south. The twins are saying they'll swear they never had the gift at all."

"The twins have never told the truth in their lives. It's almost a kind of honesty." She gestured him closer. "Sit, Edmund."

"I can stand."

"You can sit. You've been standing at attention outside my door for eleven years. Sit down where I can see your face."

He sat. Lamplight caught the ink stains on his fingers, permanent as bruises. A ledger-keeper's hands. She had chosen those hands herself, in a way—had steered him away from the taking-trade and into the keeping of it, had taught him that the safest place for a dangerous man is behind a desk where no one looks twice at him. He had made himself unremarkable with the devotion other men gave to glory. It had been her design, and watching it succeed year after year had been like watching a wall she'd built hold against weather.

Walls held until they didn't.

"Do you remember the winter you turned nineteen?" she said.

His eyes flickered—caution first, then effort. "The fever year. Half the undercity coughed itself inside out. I kept the ledgers through it. You were ill yourself."

"I was dying, actually. For six days. Odile will tell you she never left my side, which is true, and also that I never woke, which is not." Beatrix smiled, and knew the smile was terrible, the smile of a woman handling her own bones. "I woke on the seventh night with a clear head and a decision made. And I did something to you while you slept, Edmund, three floors below, dreaming your honest dreams. Something I have never told another living soul."

The lamp guttered. Neither of them moved to trim the wick.

"You put something *in* me," he said quietly. Not a question. He was a ledger-keeper; he understood that accounts eventually come due.

"I planted a seed. Or a splinter. The metaphors fail me—I'm a merchant, not a poet." She rose, slowly, joints announcing themselves like town criers, and crossed to the iron strongbox behind the tapestry of the drowning bride. Inside lay a key, and beside the key, a single dried rose with thorns still on its stem, black as a nail. She brought both to the table between them.

"When the Thorn was young, before your father was born, we were not merchants. We were soldiers. Or we were the reason for soldiers—it depends which history you read, and I have read them all, including the ones written in ash." She laid the rose down. "There was a king once who wore a crown of living thorn. The histories call him the Thorn-King, and they call what followed the War of Roots, and they agree on almost nothing except that it ended badly and everyone agreed to stop remembering why. Do you know why everyone agreed?"

Edmund shook his head. His face had gone the color of tallow candle.

"Because remembering was the weapon. Whoever held the whole of that memory held the whole of the war—every grievance, every name, every reason either side had to keep killing. So the survivors broke it apart. Scattered it. Buried the heart of it somewhere no one would think to dig." She tapped her own temple with one crooked finger. "And then, over long years, dug it out again. Because I am a hoarder, Edmund. It is my one great sin, and I committed it thoroughly."

"What is it?" His voice was steady. She gave him credit for that. "What did you put in me?"

"The last hour of the Thorn-King. His crowning. The burning of the orchards at Vale Amaranth. The moment the first side drew first blood and convinced itself it was second." She watched him absorb this the way dry ground absorbs rain—too fast, too deep, already flooding. "It is sealed. You cannot open it. You have perhaps brushed against it in dreams—a smell of smoke, a taste of iron, a banner in the corner of your eye. That is the seal working. When it stops working, you will know, and knowing will be the least of your problems."

"Why me?" The question came out raw. "You had brothers. Daughters of the guild. Odile. Why bury your treasure in a boy off a slave barge?"

"Because a vault is only as safe as its inconspicuousness." She reached across the table and took his ink-stained hand, and her fingers were cold enough that he flinched, and she did not let go. "Every gifted mind in this city can be entered by another gifted mind—that is our blessing and our curse, and it is why the witch-hunters' lists will be accurate. But a sealed memory inside an unremarkable man whom no one thinks to search? Edmund, you have spent ten years perfecting the art of being overlooked. I merely gave you something worth overlooking."

"That's not an answer."

"It is the only answer I trust myself to give soberly." She released his hand. "The truer answer is that I liked you. That you were fourteen and furious and utterly alone, and I thought: here is a boy who knows what it costs to carry things that aren't his. Here is a boy who won't drop it."

* * *

They talked until the candles burned to stubs. She told him what she could—fragments, warnings, the grammar of the thing if not its text. She told him the seal was woven from her own oldest memories, decades of them, paid out like rope, and that it would hold as long as she lived, and weaken when she began to die in earnest, and fail—

"And when it fails," Edmund said, "the memory wakes up. In me."

"In you. All of it. Every grief, every justification, every screaming name." She poured the last of the cold wine and did not drink it. "You will dream another man's war until you cannot tell whose hands are whose. I will not lie to you: it may unmake you. I have watched lesser memories eat stronger minds than yours."

"Then take it back. Take it out of me and put it in a box, or a stone, or the river—"

"No."

"*Beatrix.*"

"No." The word cracked like a whip, and then, because she was dying and had no time left for dignity, she let her voice break instead. "Edmund, listen to me. If it sits in a box, Vane's people—or whoever comes after Vane, and there is always someone coming after—will find it. If it sinks in the river, some fisherman with a scrap of the gift will dredge it up and sell it to the highest bidder, and the highest bidder will always be a man who wants a war. There is exactly one place in this world where that memory is safe, and it is behind a seal that dies with me, inside a man nobody believes matters." She leaned forward. "That is the whole architecture of my life's last work. An unremarkable man, holding an unbearable thing."

He was silent a long moment. Rain had started against the window, soft as static. "You're asking me to carry a war I didn't start, for people who'll burn me for the gift I barely have, and never tell me why."

"I'm not asking." She met his eyes. "I'm telling you what I did, and begging forgiveness after. That is what matriarchs do. It is why I never had children; I would have been monstrous at it."

A ghost of a laugh moved through him and died. "If I refuse? If I go to some mind-surgeon and have you cut out—"

"You'll die on the table, or worse, you'll succeed, and the memory will be loose in the world with no seal and no keeper." She folded her hands. "But you won't refuse. I've watched you for eleven years, Edmund Almeida. You keep accounts. You honor debts. And I am about to hand you the largest one in the history of the guild."

"This isn't a debt. This is a curse."

"All debts are. That's what makes them binding." She pushed the dried rose across the table toward him. "Take it. When the seal thins—and it will thin, sooner than I'd hoped—you'll need an anchor. Something real, something yours, to remind you which hands are yours. Keep it where you keep things you don't sell."

He picked up the rose as if it might bite. Thorns pricked his thumb; a bead of blood welled, absurdly bright. He didn't wipe it away.

"Promise me," Beatrix said. "Not that you'll protect it. Promises of protection are for men with armies. Promise me you will never sell it—not for gold, not for passage, not even to save your own life. Especially not to save your own life. Swear it on whatever you still believe in."

The rain thickened. Somewhere below, the guildhall clock struck the hour, each note falling into the next like coins into a purse.

"On the ledgers," he said at last. "I swear it on the ledgers. I don't sell what's given to me to keep."

Something in her chest loosened that had nothing to do with the dying. "Then we're done, and I can get about the business of it properly."

"What business?"

"The business of being found," she said, and smiled her terrible smile again. "Vane will come for me within the month—he's been circling the Thorn like a gull round a fishing boat, and he wants one thing, though he doesn't yet know its shape. When he takes me, I will tell him nothing, because I am old and stubborn and nearly past caring what fire does to flesh. But hear me, Edmund: everything depends on what happens after. On whether you run, or hide, or do the one thing I cannot predict." She closed her eyes, suddenly exhausted past all pretense. "Go home. Sleep. And if you dream of banners tonight—of orchards burning, of a king crowned in thorns—do not fight the dream. Just count the banners. Count them for me, and tell me the number in the morning."

He stood, tucked the rose inside his coat over his heart like a stolen letter, and paused at the door.

"Beatrix. Why give it to *me*? Truly. Last time. No architecture. No strategy."

She looked at him—at the ink-stained keeper of impossible things, the boy who had learned to be invisible so well he'd forgotten he was the most valuable object in the room—and told the truth, because the dying are owed one truth apiece.

"Because everyone else I ever trusted wanted to *use* it," she said. "Only you, I think, will want to understand it. And understanding, Edmund, is the only thing that war has never had enough of."

He left. She listened to his careful steps descend the stairs, fade into the rain, become indistinguishable from any other footfall in Corvane—which was, after all, the point of him.

Then Beatrix Voss took out a fresh page, dipped her pen, and began writing the letter that would bring Inquisitor Vane to her door.
