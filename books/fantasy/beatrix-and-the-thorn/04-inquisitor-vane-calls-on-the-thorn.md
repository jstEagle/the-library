## 4. Inquisitor Vane Calls on the Thorn

The snow came to Vell on a Tuesday, and the Inquisitor came the day after.

Edmund knew both facts with the same helpless certainty, because he had watched the first from his window above the counting room and heard the second in the way the guildhouse changed its breathing. Buildings have breath. The Gilded Thorn had been exhaling fear for weeks—doors opening and closing too quickly, voices dropping to whispers in the stairwells, the smell of tallow candles burned at hours when no honest work was done. But that morning the whole house held its breath at once, and Edmund, dressing in the grey dark, understood that something had arrived.

He went down anyway. That was the discipline of it. Nine years of making himself unremarkable had taught him that the moment you flinch is the moment you are seen. So he took his ledger under his arm—the new one, the one with nothing damning in it, pages of innocuous transactions about candle wax and river tolls—and descended into the common hall where the fire was lit and the brothers were gathered like crows around a dead thing.

Inquisitor Vane stood with his back to the hearth.

He was not what Edmund expected, though Edmund could not have said what he had expected. Perhaps someone louder. Vane was quiet the way deep water is quiet. A tall man gone slightly stooped, as if he had spent centuries apologizing for his height, dressed in the plain black wool of the crown's service with no braid, no sigil, nothing but the thin silver chain at his collar that marked an officer of the Inquisition. His face was long and tired and almost kind. His eyes were the color of a winter sky just before snow.

"Master Almeida," he said, before anyone had introduced them.

The hall went very still. Odile, at her table of fenced goods near the far wall, set down the brass scale she had been weighing and did not pick it up again.

"You have me at a disadvantage," Edmund said. His voice came out level. He was proud of that, later, in the way a drowning man might be proud of the neatness of his shoes.

"I read ledgers," Vane said. "It is how I find people. Everyone keeps accounts. Even those who believe themselves invisible." He smiled faintly, and there was something in the smile that made the hair rise along Edmund's forearms—not menace exactly, but recognition. "Yours are very good. The best in the house, I'm told. You record what others only remember."

*What others only remember.* The words settled into Edmund like a splinter working toward bone.

Brother Cassian stepped forward, all silk and desperation, hands spread in the gesture of a merchant greeting a difficult customer. "Lord Inquisitor, the Gilded Thorn is a trading company. Curiosities, antiquities, certain—private acquisitions. Whatever rumors—"

"I am not here for rumors." Vane turned from the fire. Heat moved across his face and did not warm it. "I am here to make an offer, and I will make it once. The crown's edict stands. Registration, then the pyre. But the crown is also practical, and so am I. Bring me the guild's remaining stock—every memory in your vaults, every vessel, every sealed thought—and the gifted among you will be granted letters of immunity. Signed by my hand. You may live out your small lives trading in candle wax and river tolls until you die of old age in your beds."

Silence. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked, the house's held breath finally breaking.

"And if we refuse?" Cassian asked, though his voice already knew the answer.

Vane looked at him with something like pity, which was worse than anger. "Then I take the stock anyway, and I take the keepers with it, and the fires at Salt Gate burn a little longer into spring. I would prefer not to. I have been burning things for a long time, and I find I have grown—" he searched for the word, and found it with visible distaste, "—tired."

*Been burning things for a long time.* An odd phrase. A careless phrase. Edmund filed it away the way he filed everything, automatically, helplessly, the archivist's curse: *he speaks of decades as other men speak of seasons.*

"The stock," Edmund said, and hated himself for speaking, for being seen, for the way Vane's head turned to him with the slow attention of a hawk that has finally located the mouse it saw three fields ago. "You want it catalogued. Inventoried."

"I want it *read*," Vane said softly. "Every memory examined. Most of them are worthless—a baker's grandmother, a soldier's shame, a girl's first snow. Trinkets. But somewhere in this city, in some vault or some skull, there is one memory that matters. One." His eyes did not leave Edmund's. "And I will go through everything in the world, Master Almeida, if I must, to find it."

*One memory.*

The splinter reached the bone.

Edmund felt it then, for the first time in nine years: the weight in his own head shifting. The thing Beatrix had buried in him, the sealed chamber behind the door he had promised never to open, stirred the way a sleeper stirs when someone says their name in another room. It did not wake. But it *heard*.

He kept his face still. Nine years of practice. He thought about candle wax. He thought about river tolls. He thought about the merchant's first love he had lifted two months ago, the taste of rain on a boy's hand, logged and priced and sold to a grieving widow who wanted to remember loving something.

"You'll have your inventory within the week," Cassian was saying, bowing, sweating, selling them all with the smoothness of long habit. "The vaults will be opened to you, Lord Inquisitor. The Gilded Thorn has always been loyal to the crown."

"The Gilded Thorn," Vane agreed, "has always been loyal to whoever is winning." He drew on his gloves, finger by finger, unhurried. At the door he paused, and without turning, without looking, said:

"Master Almeida. When you catalogue the stock, do be thorough. Sealed vessels especially. Some of the oldest work in this city was done by Beatrix herself, and she was always—ambitious about where she hid things." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "The mind, for instance. There is no vault so secure as a mind that does not know what it carries."

Then he was gone, and the cold came in behind him like a tide, and the fire seemed to shrink.

* * *

They argued for six hours. Edmund sat through all of it with his ledger open and his pen moving, recording nothing, hearing everything.

Cassian wanted to give Vane the vaults and be done with it; the stock was stock, and gold could be rebuilt. Brother Maren wanted to flee south with what they could carry. Old Tobbe, drunk since noon, wanted to fight, which made everyone else feel brave by comparison and changed nothing. Only Odile said nothing, weighing her brass weights over and over, watching Edmund across the room with her sharp fence's eyes, as if she were appraising him and finding the price uncertain.

When the hall emptied, she came and stood over his table.

"He named you," she said. "First through the door, last out of it. He came here for you, Edmund. The rest of us are scenery."

"He read the ledgers."

"He read *you*. There's a difference." She sat down across from him, and for once there was no mockery in her face, only the flat honesty she reserved for bad bargains. "Beatrix put something in you. Don't insult us both by denying it—I've seen you touch the back of your skull when you think no one's looking, these past weeks, like a man checking a wound. And now a witch-hunter walks into our hall and asks after sealed vessels hidden in minds, and looks at you while he says it."

Edmund closed the ledger. His hands were steady. He noticed that they were steady and understood it was the same terrible steadiness as a man standing very still in high grass.

"If I have something," he said carefully, "I don't know what it is. That was the terms. That was the promise."

"Promises," Odile said, "are also stock. Everything is stock. That's what Beatrix taught us, and it's what will hang us." She leaned close. Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Vane isn't hunting the gift. If he wanted the gift dead, he'd burn the whole city and sleep well. He wants *one memory*. Which means somewhere there's a memory worth more than all our lives, and either it's in the vaults—" she let it hang, "—or it's in you."

Outside, the snow was falling again, thick and silent, erasing the streets, erasing the tracks of the Inquisitor's carriage, erasing everything except what could not be erased: the weight behind Edmund's right ear, warm now, almost feverish, like a coal wrapped in wool.

That night he did not sleep. He sat with his back against the wall of his narrow room and pressed his palm to the place beneath his hair where the seal lived, and for the first time in nine years he pressed *back*, gently, the way one tests a locked door in a house one does not own.

Deep inside, behind the door Beatrix had built, something turned over in its sleep.

And far across the sleeping city, in a tower above Salt Gate, a lamp burned in a window, and Edmund knew—with the sick, absolute certainty of a man who realizes the shadow on the wall has been shaped like him all along—that Inquisitor Vane was sitting awake beneath it, reading ledgers, waiting, patient as deep water, for the one memory in the world to come looking for *him*.
