## 5. Brothers of the Ledger

The vote was taken in the counting room, where the ledgers lived behind iron grating and the air always smelled of tallow and old paper. Edmund stood at the center of it, which was itself an indictment—he had spent eleven years learning to stand at edges, in doorways, in the blur of other men's attention, and now every eye in the Gilded Thorn was on him like a lamp turned too bright.

Twelve brothers. Twelve faces he could have described from memory a season ago, back when describing people from memory was a thing he did without fear.

Master Corvane sat at the head of the long table with Beatrix's empty chair beside him, draped in black cloth because no one had wanted to look at it vacant. The old woman had not been seen in six days. Her rooms above the counting room stayed shut. Her cough had stopped being heard through the floorboards, and somehow that silence was worse than any sound.

"The question is simple," Corvane said. His voice had the flatness of a man reading figures aloud. "The Inquisitor came to us offering terms. One name. One vessel. In exchange, the Thorn continues. Our clients keep their secrets. Our people keep their heads."

"And you would give him me." Edmund kept his own voice level, ledger-flat. It was the voice he used when logging a memory of particular cruelty—when a husband sold his wife's grief, or a mother her child's face. *Log it clean. Log it cold. Feeling is for after.*

"You carry something," said Halvard, the extractor with hands so fine he could lift a lullaby from a sleeping infant without waking it. He would not meet Edmund's eyes. "Beatrix put it in you. Everyone knows it. Vane knows it. You've been walking around this city for years with a price on your skull and calling yourself unremarkable."

"I called myself nothing. That was rather the point."

"Then the point failed." This from Tam, youngest of them, who still had the soft cheeks of a boy who'd been lifted out of the gutter by Beatrix's charity. Tam's gift was small—fading, they called it, the ability to soften memories until they lost their teeth—and he clutched his hands together as though holding himself in place. "Edmund, I'm sorry. Truly. But there are thirty of us against one of you. There's arithmetic even in mercy."

*Arithmetic.* Edmund almost laughed. Of course. They were ledger-keepers all; even betrayal would be entered in columns.

He looked around the table slowly, cataloguing them the way he catalogued everything—not because he wanted to remember, but because forgetting had become a luxury the crown taxed. Corvane, gray-stubbled, sweating despite the cold. Halvard staring at a knot in the wood. Tam, miserable. Old Sef with his milky eye. The twins, Ferren and Ferro, identical in their shame. And at the far end, arms crossed, one boot propped on the bench—

Odile. Who was not a brother at all, whatever the guild's charter pretended. Odile the fence, who moved stolen things between worlds: memories out of minds, minds out of danger. She met his gaze and gave him nothing, which from Odile was a whole conversation.

"The vote is already taken," Edmund said quietly. "Isn't it. You only wanted me here to see it done properly. Witnessed. Logged."

Corvane's jaw tightened. "Vane's men come at dawn."

"Dawn." Edmund nodded. Eleven years, and the sum total of his worth to these men was one night's grace. "For what it's worth, brothers—I never carried anything I wouldn't have given up to save you. I just never believed you'd ask."

He turned for the door.

"Edmund—" Tam began.

"Don't apologize to me. Apologize to Beatrix, if they leave enough of her."

The door boomed shut behind him, and the sound followed him down the stair like a verdict.

* * *

Odile caught him in the stable lane, materializing out of the fog the way she always did, wrapped in a gray traveling cloak that made her look like weather deciding to happen.

"You knew," he said.

"I suspected. Corvane's been hoarding candle wax for two weeks. A man about to make a hard choice buys candles to burn past midnight." She pressed a bundle into his arms: dry clothes, a knife, a twist of bread still warm. "You'll want to argue. Don't. We have maybe four hours before someone decides to sell Vane the dawn instead of waiting for it."

"They voted, Odile. Twelve men who ate at my table."

"Eleven men and a frightened boy." She was already moving, and he fell into step because his legs understood before his pride did that she was right. "Grief makes cowards of careful people, Edmund. They watched witch-hunters burn a memory-taker alive in Saltmarket square. They watched him beg for his mother's face while the fire took it. You think their fear is about you? Their fear is about being next."

"That doesn't make it less a knife."

"No," she agreed. "It just makes it a knife you can understand."

They went down, because down was where Odile lived—in the flooded undercity beneath the merchant quarter, where the old canals had burst their banks decades ago and the streets became rivers of black water knee-deep and rising. The city above had forgotten this place existed, which made it the only honest country left.

They waded through corridors that had once been wine cellars and counting houses, water sliding cold into his boots, the walls furred with white mold that glowed faintly when their lantern touched it. Somewhere ahead, water dripped in a rhythm like a slow clock. Odile moved through it all with the certainty of a woman reading her own handwriting.

"There's a gatekeeper at the Weir," she said. "Old friend. He'll pass you through the drainage tunnels to the tannery district, and from there—"

"From there I run alone while you go back to them."

"From there you survive." She didn't break stride. "I'm not going back to them. I'm going to find out what happened to Beatrix."

That stopped him, waist-deep in cold water, his breath fogging the lantern light. "She's dead, Odile. Six days of silence."

"She's silent." Odile turned, and in the glow of the mold-light her face was stripped of everything clever, everything fenced and bargained, and what remained was simply afraid. "There's a difference, and I know it the way you know a forged coin from true. Beatrix has been dying for three years. Dying people are quiet. Dead ones are *absent*, and hers isn't absence—it's silence, held, deliberate, like a held breath. Someone taught her to hold it."

"Who could—"

And then he understood, and the understanding dropped through him like a stone through dark water.

Vane.

*Vane, who offered immunity. Vane, who asked questions about stock and inventory but whose eyes had gone, twice, to Edmund's temples—to the place behind the eyes where a sealed thing slept. Vane wasn't buying memories. Vane was hunting one.*

"She told me never to sell it," Edmund whispered. "Even to save myself. She made me swear. I thought it was sentiment. An old woman's last piece of pride."

"It was a lock," Odile said grimly. "And you're the door. Which means somewhere in this city, there are men with pliers." She gripped his sleeve. "Keep moving. Grieve later. It's the only luxury we've ever deferred well, you and I."

* * *

The Weir was a rusted grate across a canal-mouth, and behind it crouched the gatekeeper—a huge shape of a man called Brannoc who had once been a strongman in a traveling show until the floods took the show and left him the undercity. He took one look at Odile and one longer look at Edmund, and his face did something complicated.

"Word came down an hour ago," he rumbled. "Guild's posted a finder's fee. Your description's on every wall from here to the Salt Gate. 'Unremarkable man, brown hair, forgettable face.'" A wheezing laugh. "Whoever wrote it has never actually tried to find anyone."

"Brannoc." Odile stepped close. "The tunnels. Please."

"For you? For him?" Brannoc looked at Edmund for a long moment, and Edmund felt the great weight of that attention, the way he always felt attention now—like a hand pressing on a bruise he hadn't known he had. "I pulled drowned folk out of these waters for twenty years. You know what I learned? The river doesn't choose. It takes whoever's nearest. Men like Vane—" he spat into the black water, "—they choose. That's the whole difference. Go on, then. Through, and God forget you kindly."

The tunnel beyond was low and dripping, barely shoulder-width, and Odile walked him into it herself, wading, until the lantern light from the Weir was a distant coin behind them.

"This is where I leave you," she said. "Straight on until you smell the tanneries. Then up the ladder, third landing, and don't stop for anything that calls your name—the undercity echoes, and echoes lie."

"Odile." He turned in the narrow wet dark. "Come with me. Past the pass. There are camps—"

"And leave my city? My contacts? My whole life's architecture?" She smiled, and it was her trading smile, but underneath it ran something older and more tender. "Someone has to stay inside the ledger, Edmund. Someone has to learn what they did with her. If she's alive—if they've got her hooked to some machine of Vane's making, pulling at the seal inside your skull from her end—then you need to know it, and the only way you'll know is if I'm here to hear it."

"If they catch you—"

"They won't. I'm very good at moving stolen goods." Her hand found his shoulder in the dark, brief and fierce. "Listen to me. Whatever's inside you—whatever she locked away—you don't open it. Not out of fear, not out of need, not even out of love. Promise me as you promised her."

"I promised her never to sell it."

"Promising never to sell and promising never to open are different vows, and I want both."

The water dripped its slow clock around them. Somewhere far above, muffled through stone and flood, a bell began to ring—not the hour bell, but the shorter, sharper peal of the watch changing, or perhaps of something found.

"Go," Odile said softly. "Third landing. Don't stop."

He went. The dark swallowed the tunnel behind him in stages—first her lantern, then her outline, then the sound of her wading back toward the city that had sold him, carrying her loyalty like contraband.

At the second bend he smelled the tanneries, sharp and animal, and found the ladder by touch, iron rungs slick as eels. He climbed toward the thin gray line of night sky above, toward a district where his face was chalked on every wall, toward winter and the passes and whatever waited in the sealed dark behind his eyes.

And it was there, on the ladder, halfway between the drowned city and the open air, that the seal shifted.

Not opened. Not yet. But it turned over in his mind the way a sleeper turns in bed, and for one terrible half-second Edmund was not Edmund at all—he was standing in a burning orchard, wearing a crown of thorns, watching banners come down the hillside like a tide of blood, and hearing his own voice call out orders in a language that had been dead for three hundred years.

Then it was gone, and he was clinging to cold iron, shaking, tasting smoke that wasn't there.

Below him, faint through the stones, he thought he heard the watch bells change their rhythm—from search to alarm.

They had Beatrix. They must have Beatrix, and Vane was pulling threads through her.

Edmund closed his eyes against the gray sky and began, silently, to climb.
