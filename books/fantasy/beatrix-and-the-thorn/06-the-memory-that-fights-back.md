## 6. The Memory That Fights Back

The first hour I lost was a Tuesday.

I know this because I had marked it in the margin of my ledger — *Tues., collect the ferryman's grief, payment deferred* — and when I came back to myself the ink was dry and the light had moved from one side of the garret window to the other, and there was mud on my boots that no road in the undercity could account for. My hands smelled of woodsmoke and crushed apples. I did not remember leaving the room.

I sat very still for a long time, the way one does after waking from a fall, taking inventory of the body. Ten fingers. Two eyes, stinging as if from wind. A taste in my mouth like copper coins left too long in a child's pocket. And beneath all of it, behind the wall Beatrix had built in me twenty years ago, something turning over in its sleep.

"Stay where you are," I said aloud, to no one, to it. My voice sounded thin in the empty room. "You are not mine. You are inventory."

It did not answer. It never answers. But somewhere behind my eyes an orchard was burning, and I could smell it — green wood, sweet fruit splitting in the heat — and I knew with absolute certainty that I had never stood in any orchard in my life.

* * *

I had been careful, all those years, to keep nothing of my own worth stealing. That was the discipline of invisibility: no loves logged, no grudges banked, no first snows or last farewells hoarded against loneliness. The other brothers of the Thorn kept private vaults of their finest takings, replaying them at night like misers counting coin. I kept only the ledgers, and the ledgers were honest, and honesty is a kind of emptiness that fits through any door.

But Beatrix had filled that emptiness before I ever understood it was there. She had poured something into the vessel when the vessel was young and unformed, and sealed it with craft so fine that even I, who could feel the seams of any mind I touched, had never found its edges. I knew it existed only because she told me it did, on the last night I saw her free, her hands like paper around a cup of willow-bark tea.

*It will knock,* she had said. *When winter comes, it will knock. Don't open, Edmund. Whatever it shows you, whatever it promises — don't open.*

Winter had come to the city in earnest now. Frost furred the inside of the shutters each morning. The canals of the flooded quarter wore lids of grey ice, and the crown's criers walked the frozen embankments reading names from lists that grew longer by the day. In such cold, everything knocks.

* * *

The second hour I lost was not an hour but a night.

I woke standing in the middle of Salt Market Square with snow in my collar and my arms raised above my head, fingers spread, as though I had been holding up a banner. There was no banner. There was only the proclamation board, and the edict nailed to it, and the long shadow of the gibbet beyond, where three of the gifted had turned in the wind since the month's beginning. My throat was raw. Had I been shouting? The square was empty except for a dog worrying at something frozen, and it looked at me the way dogs look at things that are wrong, ears flat, and slunk away.

I ran home through streets white and silent, and in the privacy of my room I did what I should have done years ago: I opened the ledger to a fresh page and began to write down what I could find of myself, so that I would know later what had gone missing. *My name is Edmund Almeida.* The pen scratched. *My mother sold eels on Cordwainer Stair. I have taken four hundred and eleven memories and kept none. I am afraid of deep water. I am afraid of Vane's grey eyes. I am—*

The pen stopped.

Because there it was again, rising like a bubble through dark water: banners. Not one banner — hundreds, a river of them, black and gold, streaming over a hillside where ten thousand people stood in the rain and wept. And at the crest of the hill, on a throne grown rather than built — roots and briar woven into a seat that flowered as you watched — a man in a crown of living thorns, the points of it sunk into his brow, blood running bright down his face while he smiled as though it were oil of roses.

I knew his smile. That was the horror of it. I had never seen him, and I knew his smile the way I knew my own hands.

I wrote: *There is a king. He is crowned in thorns. He is glad.*

Then I burned the page, because some entries do not belong in any ledger.

* * *

The losses came faster as the cold deepened, the way ice claims a pond — first the edges, then all at once.

An afternoon gone, returned to me as a smear of sensation: horsehair blankets, a woman's voice singing in a language I did not speak, the ache of a wound in my side that was not mine and faded by morning. An evening gone entire; I found myself at dawn three miles upriver with river-mud to the knee and a fish, inexplicably, in my coat pocket, still cold from the water, as though some part of me had paused mid-flight to feed itself.

I began to leave notes for myself, pinned inside my coat the way a drunkard hides warnings from his evening self. *You are Edmund. You live above the chandler's on Rope Walk. Do not follow the banners.* Each morning I found the notes crumpled, sometimes torn, once — this frightened me worst — answered, in my own handwriting, in letters older than my own hand had ever learned: a looping, archaic script that spelled out a single word.

*Soon.*

"You are not me," I told the thing behind the wall, pacing my narrow room, breath smoking. "You are a memory. You are stock. You are four hundred and twelfth in a ledger of stolen things, and I have never once opened the vault for anything, and I will not open it for you."

But memories are not like other stock. That was the first lesson of the guild, the lesson they beat into every apprentice: a taken memory does not sit quiet in its jar. It grows toward whoever holds it. Keep a dead man's love of the sea long enough and you will wake craving salt. Keep a murderer's patience and your own hands will learn to wait. We were taught to rotate the stock, to never hold one mind's years too close to our own.

Beatrix had made me hold hers — his — for twenty years. Sealed, yes. But seals are walls, and walls are thin places, and in the deep of winter I could press my ear to them and hear the war on the other side.

* * *

It came for me fully on the night of the hard frost, the night the canal froze solid enough to walk on, though I would not learn until later how far I had walked.

I was logging the week's takings — Odile had brought in a midwife's memory of two hundred first cries, a beautiful piece, warm as bread — when the candle guttered without wind, and the orchard came back, and this time it did not recede.

I was in it. Not watching it: standing in it. Rows of apple trees heavy with fruit gone soft and black, fire moving down the rows with the unhurried confidence of something that has done this many times before. Heat on my face. Ash settling on my shoulders like grey snow. And soldiers — crown soldiers, in the livery I saw now on every patrol in the city, though their coats were older cut, their pikes longer — moving through the burning rows with hooks, dragging people out by their hair. A woman pressed an infant into my arms and ran back into the smoke for another child and did not come out.

I looked down at the baby. I looked at my hands holding it, and the hands were not my hands — broader, scarred across both palms, a signet ring of black iron worn smooth — and yet they were my hands, they were the only hands I had, and the baby was screaming, and somewhere very far away a voice that might have been mine was saying *this is not yours, put it down, none of this is yours* —

And then the hillside. The rain. The ten thousand weeping. The throne of roots. The king turning to the crowd with blood in his eyes and his thorn-crown flowering, and lifting his voice in that language I did not speak and somehow understood word for word:

*They burned the orchards. They will burn the gifted next. Let every soul who bears the gift take up the thorn, and let the crown learn what it has planted.*

And the crowd roared, and the roar went through me like a spear, and I was roaring too, I was one of the ten thousand, I had always been one of the ten thousand—

I came back to myself on the frozen canal, four miles north of the city, walking north, walking steadily, purposefully, toward the pass. Toward the refugee camps. Snow falling into my open eyes without my blinking. My lips cracked and bleeding, and only when I stopped and worked my jaw did I understand why: I had been shouting the king's words into the empty white miles, over and over, in a dead language, for I did not know how long.

The moon was high. My feet had not frozen, which meant hours, not days. But when I took out my notebook by moonlight to mark the loss, my hand was shaking too badly to write, and what I finally managed was not an entry at all, only a question, the question that had been growing in me since the square, since the orchard, since the beginning of winter:

*If it walks me north, what does it want me to find there?*

I knew the answer before I finished writing it. The seal was failing the way all Beatrix's work was failing now that she was gone — no, worse: failing because it was *meant* to fail, timed like a lock with a season for its key. She had not hidden the memory in me to bury it. She had hidden it in me to carry it somewhere. North. To the camps. To the gathered remnant of the gifted.

She had made me the courier of a war.

Behind my eyes, patient as frost, the thing that was not mine settled deeper into its borrowed flesh, and smiled with the king's smile, and waited for the next hour it would take.

I turned south, back toward the city, back toward Odile, back toward the only friend I had left — and found, printed crisp in the fresh snow ahead of me, a line of bootprints coming north. Someone had been walking toward me through the night.

They stopped ten paces away. Grey cloak. Grey eyes.

"Hello, Almeida," said Inquisitor Vane. "You've been a difficult man to find. But lately—" he tilted his head, studying me the way one studies a jar with something moving inside it, "—you've become much easier to follow."
