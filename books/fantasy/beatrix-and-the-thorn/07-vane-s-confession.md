## 7. Vane's Confession

The cell smelled of wet stone and old iron, and Inquisitor Corvus Vane had known it for three days before he finally went down to the prisoner.

Three days. He had told himself it was procedure—the long, patient work of letting a man sit with his fear until fear did the questioning for him. But that was a lie, and Vane had grown too old, in the uncountable way, to tolerate his own lies comfortably. The truth was simpler and worse: he had not wanted to look at Edmund Almeida yet. Looking at the man meant beginning, and beginning meant admitting that after four hundred years of searching, the thing he wanted was sitting in a cell beneath the Salt Gate, wearing a ledger-keeper's ink-stained cuffs, trembling like any other mortal man who had discovered that invisibility was a debt that always came due.

Vane carried the lamp down the last of the stairs himself. He did not trust the turnkeys with what he was about to do, and he did not trust his own men, and least of all he did not trust the quiet, hungry part of himself that had waited so long for this that it had begun to eat him from the inside, patiently, the way water eats a mountain.

Edmund sat on the plank bed with his back to the wall. They had taken his coat, his boots, the little bone stylus he used for fine entries. His hands were bare, and Vane found himself staring at them—long fingers, ink in the creases of the knuckles that no scrubbing would lift. Hands made for writing down other people's lives.

"You're not eating," Vane said.

"Neither are you." Edmund's voice was hoarse but steady. "I've been watching the trays come down. Yours go back untouched too."

So the little archivist had been watching even here. Good. That was the gift in him, or near enough to it. Vane set the lamp on the floor between them, and its light climbed the walls and turned the dripping ceiling into a slow, dark constellation.

"Do you know what I am?" Vane asked.

"A witch-hunter."

"Yes. And before that?"

Edmund said nothing. Wise man. Silence cost nothing and could be sold later.

Vane sat down on the opposite stool, unhurried, arranging the fall of his grey coat with the care of a man who had worn the same gesture for centuries. Then he pulled off his right glove.

The hand beneath was not a hand. It had been one once—it still held the shape of one—but the flesh had gone the color of bark gone silver with age, ridged and split, and from the heel of the palm grew a single thorn, curved like a hook, black as a nail driven out of wood. It had been there since the orchard. It had been there since the king fell.

Edmund's breath caught. Whatever he had expected—denial, theater, the ordinary cruelties of an Inquisitor establishing dominance—it was not this.

"You feel it already, don't you," Vane said quietly. "You've felt it since autumn. Something under your ribs that isn't yours, turning over in its sleep when the frost comes." He watched the man's face do what faces do: deny, then fail to deny. "That is because you are carrying the last living memory of Aurelian Thornwrought, King of the Gifted, and I am carrying the last living piece of his body."

* * *

He told it plainly, because he had told it to no one in four hundred years and plainness was all he had left of it. The lamp guttered once and steadied, as if it too were listening.

"There was an orchard," he said, "in the valley called Merrow, where the gifted kept their trees that remembered. Apple trees that held the taste of every summer they'd ever stood through. You could bite one of those apples and taste your grandmother's wedding, or a battle you never fought, or rain on a country you'd never see. The crown called them weapons. They were never weapons. They were *libraries*." His thorned hand curled slowly shut. "I was a soldier of the third company. Unremarkable. Like you, keeper—I made myself unremarkable, though my reasons were cowardice and yours were craft.

"The war began the way all our wars begin. Not with fire. With a *ledger*. The crown demanded a registry of every gifted soul and every memory they held. The gifted refused—not because they had something to hide, but because a list is the first page of a pyre. Both sides were right. That is the horror of it, keeper. Both sides were *right*, and it burned anyway.

"I saw Aurelian crowned at winter's edge, in a camp much like the ones you're running toward now. They pressed a crown of blackthorn onto his head because gold would have been a lie—he was not chosen by any power above; he was chosen by the desperate below. And I knelt with three thousand others and swore, and I believed it, the way you believe things when the snow is coming and someone has finally given you a banner to stand behind.

"He died at Merrow. In the orchard. The crown's witch-finders fired the trees first—they understood before anyone else did that memories were the true territory—and Aurelian went into the burning rows to pull the oldest tree out whole, roots and all, and a pike took him through the throat, and he fell among his own library, and the burning apples rained down around him like falling stars full of other people's summers."

Vane stopped. The thorn on his hand had begun, very faintly, to weep a clear resin that smelled, impossibly, of apple blossom in a valley that had been ash for four centuries.

"When he died," he went on, softer, "his closest—Beatrix, though she was young then, Beatrix before she was ever the Thorn's matriarch—took his final memory. Everything. The crowning, the war, the orchard, the moment the pike came. She sealed it, because a memory like that in the open world would be either a torch or a treasure, and both meant more dead gifted. And she hid it. She moved it from vessel to vessel down the years, always choosing the safest place she knew." He looked up, and the lamplight caught his eyes, which were old in a way no living eyes should be. "The safest place she ever found was a boy with no family and no gift worth mentioning and a genius for being overlooked. She buried a king in the mind of a clerk, and she let you live your whole small life without telling you, because telling you would have painted a target on you the day you learned to read."

Edmund's voice, when it came, cracked in the middle. "She made me promise. Never sell it. Even to save myself. I didn't know what *it* was."

"She couldn't tell you. Telling is opening." Vane rose, walked to the barred window slit, where snow was coming down in the slow, indifferent way of deep winter. "And I have been alive since the orchard, keeper, because when Aurelian fell I crawled to him through the fire, and he—dying, furious, generous past all sense—pressed his hand to my wound and gave me what was left of his strength. A soldier's mercy. He thought he was sending me home to my wife." A sound that might once have been a laugh. "Home was three hundred years dust by the time I understood what he'd done. I cannot die. I have tried, in the early centuries, in ways I won't describe to a starving man. The thorn grows. Every year, a fraction. I am being slowly crowned from within, and I have decided to interpret it as instruction."

"So you hunt us," Edmund said. "Your own kind. For four hundred years."

"I hunt the *memory*." Vane turned, and for a moment the mask of the Inquisitor slipped entirely, and what stood in the cell was only a very old soldier standing at a grave. "Every registry, every pyre, every name I've burned into the crown's books—that was the price of access. The church gave me eternity's patience and the crown's warrant, and I spent both looking for Beatrix's seal. Do you understand? I don't want your guild's stock. I don't want your confession. I want what's behind your eyes, because the war is *unfinished*, keeper. Aurelian died mid-sentence. There was no treaty, only exhaustion. No justice, only silence. His last memory holds his final order—what he meant to do, what he would have done with the gift if he'd lived. Finish that, and I can finish this. One way or the other, the war ends properly, and I am permitted—" his voice failed, just slightly, and repaired itself— "I am permitted to stop."

* * *

For a long time there was only the drip of water and the wind worrying at the window slit.

Edmund had drawn his knees up, and he looked very young, and very tired, and Vane hated him a little for the youth and pitied him more for the tiredness, and understood with terrible clarity that these two feelings had long ago braided themselves together in him beyond separating.

"You mourn him," Edmund said at last. "All of it—the hunting, the registries, the burnings—you did it as mourning."

"Don't flatter me."

"It wasn't flattery." The clerk's eyes lifted, and there was something in them Vane had not seen before—not fear, not calculation, but a kind of horrified recognition, one archivist reading another's books. "It frightens me more than hatred would. Hatred I know how to survive. But a man who has burned hundreds of innocents out of love—" He shook his head slowly. "What happens to that love when he learns the truth of how the war began? Because that's what's in here, Inquisitor. I've started to see it, in the hours it steals from me. Banners. Burning orchards. And something else—something about the registry, about *who refused to share what*. You may not want what he actually remembers."

Vane crossed the cell in two strides and took the man's face in his thorned hand, gently, which was somehow worse than roughly, the black point of the thorn resting against Edmund's temple like a pen finding its margin.

"Then I will read it myself," he said, "and I will grieve whatever it says, and I will go on. That is what soldiers do, keeper. We are not permitted the luxury of being wrong about why we fight." He released him, straightened, became the Inquisitor again in the space of a breath. "Rest. Eat. Tomorrow we ride north, you and I, to the camps at winter's edge—because Beatrix escaped her escort tonight, and I think I know where she'll run, and I think you know too."

At the stairs he paused, his back to the prisoner, the lamp throwing his shadow long and thorn-crowned against the wall.

"And Almeida. If the seal breaks before we reach the pass—if the king wakes in your head on the road—" His voice was almost kind. "Run toward me. Not away. Of everyone in this world, I am the only one who will kneel."

Above, far off, muffled by stone and snow, a bell began to ring the alarm—and Vane closed his eyes, because he knew the count of it, and it was the bell that meant the Thorn's remaining brothers had found the cells empty of one fence named Odile, and that somewhere in the flooded undercity, a woman who owed him nothing had already bought a passage north.
