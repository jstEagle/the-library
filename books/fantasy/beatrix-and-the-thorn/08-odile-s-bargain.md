## 8. Odile's Bargain

The safehouse Odile chose was a former counting-house sunk half into the flooded quarter, its ground floor given over to black water and its stairs to rot. We reached it by punt, poling through streets that had become canals, past doorways where lamplight no longer burned because the lamplighters had fled or been taken. The city above us had gone quiet in the way of things holding their breath. Somewhere to the north, bells that should have rung vespers did not.

Odile poled the last stretch herself, refusing my offer of the oar with a flick of two fingers. She was sixty if she was a day, small as a wren and twice as quick, and she moved through the drowned streets like a woman visiting old friends. Perhaps she was. She had been fencing stolen memories longer than I had been alive; she knew every smuggler's cut and every watchman's blind spot in this city the way I knew the ledgers—by heart, by weight, by the particular sound each one made when it lied.

"Down," she said, tying off at a window that had once been a second-story door. "And mind the third stair. It lies."

Inside, the air smelled of mildew and tallow and, beneath it, faintly, of her: bergamot and old paper. She lit a single candle, cupped it against drafts that came through walls like whispers, and set it on a table whose legs stood in an inch of standing water. Her reflection looked up at her from below, wavering, and for a moment there were two Odiles—one above, one beneath—and I could not have said which looked older.

"You're bleeding," she said.

I looked down at my side, surprised, as though the wound belonged to someone else. The brothers had not been gentle when they voted. Corvin's fist had done most of the work; the rest had been done by the vote itself, eleven hands raised against me in a room where I had logged ten thousand stolen years. Eleven hands. I had counted them the way I counted everything. It is what I am for.

"It's nothing," I said.

"Everything is nothing until it kills you." She was already moving, pulling a battered tin from a shelf, unwinding linen gone yellow with age. She cleaned the wound without tenderness and bound it without ceremony, and all the while she talked, which was how she told you she was frightened. "Vane's men took Beatrix at moonrise. Four of them, hooded, no livery. They carried her out like a rolled carpet."

"I know."

"They'll have her at the Salt Bastion by morning. And she'll give them your name, Edmund, because everyone gives names eventually. Even matriarchs. Especially matriarchs." She tied off the bandage, bit the thread, and sat back on her heels to look at me. Her eyes were pale gray, the color of water over slate, and they missed nothing. They never had. That was why the guild brought her everything worth selling. "You felt it, didn't you. What she put in you. It's waking."

I said nothing. Outside, water lapped at the walls with the patience of something that intended to outlast us all.

* * *

She fed me salt fish and hard bread and watered wine, and while I ate she laid out on the table the things she had gathered: a purse of coin, a knife with a blade the color of dusk, a folded map drawn on skin so fine it might have been silk. The map showed the pass beyond the northern hills, and beyond the pass, marked in her own cramped hand, a cluster of symbols at the edge of the winter forests.

"The camps," I said.

"What's left of the gifted are gathering there. Shepherds, midwives, a boy who can find anything that's lost, an old man who remembers other people's dreams so well he's begun to believe they're his own." She tapped the map. "They have no leader. They have no plan. They have, at last count, forty-one souls and a great deal of fear. But they're beyond Vane's writ, for now, because the crown's men don't ride well in snow."

"Why are you helping me?"

The question came out harsher than I meant it. She didn't flinch. She poured herself wine and drank, and when she answered, her voice was very even.

"Because Beatrix asked me to. Before they took her. She sent a girl with a message—no name, just words. *Keep the archivist breathing. He carries the end of it.*" She turned the cup slowly on the table. "And because thirty years ago, a young ledger-keeper sold me back my mother's voice for less than it was worth, and never told anyone what discount he'd given, and never held it over me. Not once. Not in thirty years." She looked up. "You thought I didn't know. I fence memories, boy. I know every kindness that passes through my hands."

I had forgotten that trade. Or I had buried it, the way I buried everything that made me visible. That was the whole of my art, I realized—not the taking, but the burying. And here was Odile, who had kept the receipt.

"There's a price," I said. There was always a price. It was the first law of the Thorn, older than any of us.

"Yes," she said simply. "There is."

* * *

What Vane wanted was me. What the guild wanted was to be rid of me. What stood between those two hungers was a single checkpoint at the Weeping Gate, where Vane's men had begun searching every face that left the city, and where my face—my unremarkable, carefully forgettable face—had been nailed to a board beside the proclamation, rendered in charcoal by someone who had somehow seen me clearly after all these years of my making sure no one did.

But Vane's men were not all Vane's men. Some of them were bought. And what buys a witch-hunter is not always coin.

"He has a lieutenant," Odile said. "Marek. A big sad ox of a man who lost his daughter to fever four winters ago and has spent every night since drinking himself toward forgetting her. He'd sell his post for a bottle. He'd sell his soul for—" She stopped. Her hand, resting on the table, had gone still.

"For what?" I asked, though I already knew. My stomach knew before my mind did. It dropped the way a stone drops.

"For a memory of her," Odile said. "A good one. A true one. And I have one."

"No." The word came out of me fast and flat. "No, Odile. Take the coin. Take the knife. Take the map and come with me—"

"And leave the Thorn? Leave the city?" She smiled, and it was the smile she wore over her stall, the professional one, the one that meant the price had been set and the haggling was over. "Edmund. Look around you. This is what's left of my life: a room with water in it and a candle that won't stay lit. I'm sixty-three years old and everything I ever fenced is either burned or buried or in the Bastion's vaults. There is nothing here for me but the waiting." She leaned forward. "But there is one thing in my head worth more than all of it. One thing I've refused to sell for thirty years, through famine and plague and two purges, because I told myself I'd need it on the day I died, to prove I had been someone."

"Don't."

"My daughter," she said, and her voice did not break, which was somehow worse than breaking. "Liesl. Six years old, standing in her father's orchard with rain in her hair, laughing at something I can no longer remember her saying—but I remember the laugh. Gods, I remember the laugh. I have carried it through every bad year like a coal in my pocket." She closed her eyes briefly. "It is the only thing I have never priced. And tomorrow I will hand it to a drunkard in exchange for your life, and he will hold it carelessly, the way people hold things that aren't theirs, and it will fade in him within the year because borrowed memories fade, everyone knows that, and then Liesl will be gone twice. Once from the world and once from memory entirely."

"Then don't do it," I whispered. "Please. I'm not worth—"

"You are worth exactly what I say you're worth," she snapped, and for a moment the wren was a hawk. "Beatrix trusted you with the end of a war. Do you understand what that means? That woman hoarded centuries. She sold kings their own childhoods. And the one thing she would not sell, the one thing she sealed inside a clerk who wanted only to be invisible—that is worth a memory. That is worth a hundred." She reached across the table and gripped my wrist, her fingers dry and strong as roots. "You will carry it for both of us, do you hear me? You will go north, and you will open whatever she put in you, and you will make it mean something. Because if it means nothing—if it's just another thing hoarded, another treasure locked in a vault of one skull—then I have sold my daughter's laugh for nothing, and I will haunt you until the sun burns out."

I could not speak. I nodded instead, because it was all I had.

* * *

We went to the Weeping Gate at dawn, when the fog off the canals was thick enough to drink. Odile walked ahead of me with the map hidden in her coat and her chin up, a small gray woman going to market. Marek stood under the gate's arch like a monument to some forgotten grief, and when she spoke to him—low, quick, the language of the trade—I saw his eyes change. Saw the hunger come into them like a lamp being lit in a dark room.

They stepped into the guardhouse together. Through the shuttered window I watched her take his face in her small hands, watched her lips move in something that was not quite a kiss and not quite a prayer, watched the big man shudder and weep without knowing why, already homesick for a moment that had just stopped belonging to him.

When she came out, she was smiling.

That was the worst of it. She was smiling the way Liesl must have smiled in the orchard—a bright, uncomplicated, emptied-out joy, the smile of a woman whose deepest wound had been quietly removed along with everything that made it a wound. She pressed the map into my hands. Her fingers were warm. Her eyes, when they met mine, were kind and vacant as a summer sky.

"Safe travels," she said pleasantly, to me, whom she had known for thirty years. "Mind the pass. It snows early."

I walked through the gate. I did not look back, because looking back would have been for me, and I had taken enough from her already.

Behind me, Marek was humming. Some little tuneless thing, a child's song maybe, held wrong in a grown man's mouth. By spring it would be gone from him too, worn smooth by handling, and somewhere in the world a girl named Liesl would die a second death, unwitnessed, unmourned, unremembered.

The road climbed north through bare fields toward hills the color of ash. In my coat pocket, the map crackled like a living thing. And deep beneath my breastbone, behind the seal that Beatrix had woven and winter was unraveling thread by thread, something ancient stirred and turned over in its sleep—and for the first time, I heard it clearly.

Not a weapon. Not a treasure.

A voice, calling roll. Counting names. Beginning, as all ledgers begin, with the dead.
