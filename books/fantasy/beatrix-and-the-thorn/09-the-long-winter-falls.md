## 9. The Long Winter Falls

The camp at Greywater Pass smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and the particular sourness of too many bodies sharing too little warmth. Edmund came down out of the high snowfields on the ninth day of his flight, when his boots had given up pretending to be boots and his hands had stopped registering cold as anything other than a fact of existence. Below him, in the bowl of the valley, the refugee camp clung to the mountainside like lichen—tents of oiled canvas, lean-tos of stripped pine, cookfires smoking thin and desperate into a sky the color of old pewter.

He counted the fires because counting was what he did, because numbers were the last clean thing left in him. Forty-one fires. Perhaps three hundred souls. The gifted and those who sheltered them: hedge-witches who could coax milk from a dry cow, memory-takers like himself, a boy he'd passed on the switchback road who could speak to crows and had been doing so, apparently, about Edmund's approach.

"They say you're carrying something," the boy had said, crows wheeling around his shoulders like a black thought made visible. "They say it's burning you from inside."

Edmund had said nothing. What was there to say? That the thing Beatrix had sewn into his mind eleven years ago had begun, as winter deepened, to wake? That he was losing hours now—not minutes, hours—waking at dawn with frost on his eyelashes and no memory of the night, only residue: the taste of smoke that wasn't from any cookfire, the phantom weight of a crown he'd never worn, grief so large and old it didn't fit inside a single skull and leaked out of him like light under a door?

Below, a sentry saw him and raised a horn. He let them take his name and his fear both, and walked down into the smell of humanity huddling against the end of the world.

* * *

They gave him a place by the largest fire, in the shadow of the old waystation wall, and they gave him soup he couldn't taste, and they gave him questions.

"You're Quintana's man," said a woman with silver threading one braid—a memory-taker herself, he guessed, or had been before the proclamations went up and the profession became a pyre-worthy offense. "Odile's network. They said someone was coming out of the city with something important."

"Odile can't come," Edmund said. The words cost him. He still saw her sometimes, standing in her shop among her jars of traded years, smiling at him with an emptiness so complete it looked, from a certain angle, like peace. *Take the east road,* she'd told him, her voice bright and vacant and kind. *And be happy for me. I got a good price.* "She paid your passage, though. All of you. She paid more than you'll ever know."

The woman studied him with eyes that had taken memories and knew what taking cost. "And the thing you're carrying?"

"It's not mine to describe."

"No." She nodded slowly, and he understood she'd already tasted the edges of it, the way you can taste a storm coming without seeing the cloud. "But it's leaking, friend. Whatever it is. We've all been dreaming the same dream this week—orchards burning, banners the color of dried blood, a man on a throne of thorns asking us something we can't hear. Three hundred people, same dream. That's not weather. That's you."

That night he slept badly and woke at moonrise standing outside the camp perimeter with snow to his knees, holding a stick he'd sharpened into something between a staff and a spear, having no memory of the hour since midnight. In his mind, unbidden, unsealed, a fragment played: *the coronation*. Not his own—he had never been crowned—but he felt the thorns go in, felt them drink, heard ten thousand voices shouting a name that was almost his name, that wanted to be his name, that had been trying on his mouth like a glove these past weeks.

*Thorn-king,* the crowd roared in the memory. *Thorn-king, thorn-king.*

"I'm not him," Edmund said aloud, to the snow, to the stars, to whatever piece of a dead king lived behind his eyes. "I keep ledgers. That's all I've ever been."

The memory did not answer. It never answered. It simply waited, patient as winter, for him to stop resisting.

* * *

On the third morning in camp, the crows went silent.

Edmund was mending a tent seam when he noticed—the boy who spoke to them came running through the rows of shelters, white-faced, and every crow in the pass rose at once, a black rag torn out of the grey sky. From the south, down the valley, came the sound Edmund had been dreading since the Salt Gate: hoofbeats. Many. Disciplined. The particular rhythm of soldiers who had done this before.

"Vane's riders," the boy gasped. "Three days out. Maybe two. The crows say they're burning farmsteads as they come. Anyone harbored. Anyone gifted." His voice cracked. "Anyone who dreamed the dream."

The camp turned on him within the hour. Edmund stood in the open ground between the waystation wall and the fire pits while frightened people made their arguments, and he listened, because listening was also what he did. Some wanted him gone—carried out into the snow, set loose, given to Vane like an offering. Some wanted him to run. And some—the woman with the silver braid among them—wanted to know what he carried, because if three hundred people were going to die for it, three hundred people deserved to know what *it* was.

"I don't know," he told them, and it was the truest thing he'd said in weeks. "Beatrix sealed it. She made me promise never to sell it, never to give it, never even to look. She said some things, once seen, can't be unseen, and once known can't be unknown, and she was dying and she trusted me and I have kept that trust for eleven years through everything—" His voice failed. He started again. "But she's dead now, or worse than dead, and Vane is coming, and I think—I think the seal was always meant to break here. At winter's edge. With all of you. She planned this, and I'm only now seeing the shape of it."

"So open it," said the boy, fierce, crows landing on the broken wall behind him like a jury. "Open it or throw it away. But choose."

*Choose.* Edmund closed his eyes.

There were, he saw clearly now, only two doors. He could destroy the memory—he had spent long nights convincing himself it was possible, prying at the seal's edges, imagining the thorn-king's history unraveling into nothing—and perhaps it was possible, and perhaps it would buy the camp nothing at all except a Vane who arrived angry instead of curious. Or he could open it, and become whatever the thing demanded, and learn at last why Beatrix had chosen *him*, a bookkeeper, an invisible man, to hold a king's war behind his eyes.

He chose the second door. He suspected, in his bones, that Beatrix had built the lock so there was only ever the one.

* * *

He opened the seal that night, alone, sitting cross-legged in the snow beyond the camp with the waystation wall at his back and the southern sky glowing faintly with the watchfires of an army that hadn't yet arrived.

It was not like opening a door. It was like being opened.

The seal came away the way a scab comes away—from underneath, all at once, with a sting and then a strange relief—and the memory poured up through him like water finding its old channel after a century of drought. He did not watch it. He *was* it. He was—

—standing in an orchard the summer the war began, apples heavy on the boughs, watching the crown's tax-collectors nail a list to the orchard gate: names of the gifted, and beside each name, the tithe owed. One year of memory per head. The best year. The year they'd choose, and the gifted would surrender it, smiling, because the alternative was written below in smaller letters—

—kneeling in a cellar while a young woman wept over the hole in her mind where her wedding day used to be, sold to pay the tithe, and feeling beside him the fury of the man who would be thorn-king, who held her hand and said, quietly, in a voice Edmund recognized as the voice from his dreams: *They don't just take our memories. They make us grateful. That's the theft under the theft. That's what we end.*

—standing in the great hall at the first gathering of the gifted, hearing the argument that split them forever: *hide* or *share*. The hoarders wanted vaults, secrecy, power kept close; the sharers wanted to give the gift to everyone, flood the world with it until it could no longer be taxed or owned or burned out. And the man who would be king—no, who already was king, crowned in thorns by acclamation in a burning season—had chosen neither. Had chosen war. Had chosen to make the gift a weapon rather than a commons, and Edmund watched, helpless inside the memory, as the whole catastrophe assembled itself out of pride and grief and the very human refusal to imagine a third option.

—dying. He was the king, and he was dying, and the war was lost or won depending on who told it, and the orchards were ash and the tithe lists had become execution lists and the gifted were scattered into the hills where their descendants hid to this day. And the king, in his last hour, took his own memory of how it began—all of it, the whole terrible ledger of it—and spoke to someone kneeling beside him, someone whose face Edmund could not see, whose voice said:

*"Seal it. Hide it where no one will think to look—in the plainest vessel you can find. A man who keeps accounts. A man no one sees. And tell him—"*

And the memory ended. Not faded. Ended, like a ledger line drawn shut.

Edmund knelt in the snow, weeping without sound, understanding at last.

It was not a weapon. It had never been a weapon. Vane wanted it because Vane had fought in that war, because Vane believed the story ended unfinished, needed the king's memory the way a mourner needs a letter never sent. But the memory itself was only a record—a warning, pressed flat and hidden in the plainest man available. *This is how wars begin,* it said. *Not with monsters. With a tithe, and a tear, and a meeting hall full of people who each believed the answer was keeping or taking, and no one brave enough—or foolish enough—to suggest giving.*

One side hoarded. The other refused to share. And everyone burned.

Clarity, arriving too late to be comfortable. Because the army was two days out, and the camp had three hundred souls and forty-one fires, and Edmund now knew exactly how the last war began—which meant he knew exactly how this one would, unless someone found the third option the thorn-king never could.

He looked south, toward the glow on the horizon. He looked back at the camp, at the fires, at the faces of people who had already dreamed his dream.

He put his hand flat against his own chest, against the place where the memory now sat unsealed and enormous, and began—for the first time in his life—to consider giving something away instead of keeping it.

Behind him, a crow screamed once, low, like a struck bell. The boy came running through the snow.

"They've changed course," the boy panted. "The crows see them—they're not following the valley road anymore. They're coming straight up the mountain. Straight at us." He swallowed. "Inquisitor Vane is riding at the front. And he's not alone. He's brought wagons. Wagons with cages."

Edmund stood, and brushed the snow from his knees, and felt the whole of the thorn-king's history settle into him like a stone finding the bottom of a well. Two days. Perhaps less.

"Wake everyone," he said. "Every soul in this camp. Wake them all."

The boy stared. "Why? What are we going to do?"

Edmund looked at the fires, at the faces, at the ledger in his mind waiting to be rewritten.

"What Beatrix should have asked me to do from the start," he said. "We're going to make everyone remember."
