## 10. Ledger of the Guilty

The memory had stopped fighting me by the time the horns sounded at the pass.

That was the strangest part. For weeks it had thrashed inside my skull like a netted fish—banners, burning orchards, a man kneeling in a field of thorns while a crown of them was pressed down into his scalp until the blood ran black in the torchlight. It had eaten hours of my life whole. I had woken on my knees in the snow with no idea how long I'd been there, my mouth full of someone else's prayers. But now, with Vane's riders cresting the white ridge and the refugee camp below me holding its breath like a held wound, the thorn-king's last afternoon lay against my mind quiet as a sleeping animal. As if it knew what I intended to do. As if it approved.

I stood on the frozen millrace above the camp, and I looked down at four hundred people who were about to die for what only I carried.

They were not fighters. That was the first thing you understood about the camp, the thing the crown had never grasped in all its centuries of hunting us: the gifted were mostly small people with small gifts. A woman who could warm milk with her hands. Twin brothers who could find anything that had been lost, so long as the loser truly wept for it. A boy who could remember other people's dreams better than his own. They had fled here because their gifts were too minor to hide and too real to deny under torture, and now Inquisitor Vane's lances glinted along the ridgeline like frost coming early, and the camp's fires looked very small and very human beneath them.

Odile should have been standing beside me. She was not. When I closed my eyes I could still see her in Vane's tent, smiling her empty smile, her most precious memory—a summer street, a fiddler, a girl laughing with her head thrown back—sold out of her forever to buy me one night's lead on the road. The ledger-keeper in me had already entered it. *Given, not taken: one fiddler's tune, Odile's, freely surrendered.* The ink of it burned worse than any theft I'd ever recorded.

Below me, someone began to sing. An old song, a thorn-song, the kind Beatrix used to hum while balancing her books—the kind that had been punishable by drowning for three hundred years. The woman who warmed milk started it, and others joined, badly, out of tune, because none of them had ever been taught properly. Four hundred voices failing together at something beautiful.

I took out the seal.

Not a thing—you couldn't hold it, couldn't burn it, though God knows I had tried in the first weeks, pressing a knife to my own palm and begging the thorn-king to bleed out of me onto the snow. The seal was a shape in the mind, a door Beatrix had built with her dying strength, and all winter it had been swelling and splitting as the memory behind it grew restless. Now I put my hands against it the way she had taught me to put hands against a locked strongbox, and I felt the lock answer, and I understood, finally, fully, what she had never told me:

The memory was not a weapon.

It was a record.

I had seen it a hundred times by then, from the inside. The last thorn-king had not gone to war over pride or plunder. He had gone to war because the crown of his day had discovered the gift of memory-taking and decided—quietly, in council, with ledgers of its own—that such power must belong to the throne alone. One side hoarding. One side refusing to share. And the king of thorns, who could have kept his gift secret, had chosen instead to make it common—to give it away, every scrap, to every farmer and fishwife who would take it—so that no edict could ever again name the gifted *them*, separate, burnable. He had lost the war. He had won the argument, briefly, before the burning started.

Beatrix hadn't hidden the spark of the war in my head. She had hidden the proof that sharing was the only survival. And she had trusted a man who made himself unremarkable to carry it until unremarkability failed.

"Forgive me, Beatrix," I said, to the snow, to the wind, to whatever was left of her in the deep rooms of my mind. "You told me never to sell it. I'm not selling."

I opened the door, and I let go.

* * *

You cannot imagine what it is to give a memory away. Taking is easy; taking is what I was trained for, the soft slide of another life into your own empty shelves. Giving is the opposite. Giving is being poured out.

The thorn-king's last day left me like light leaving a lamp—and I felt it go out of me and outward, not as a shout but as a tide, moving through the camp the way cold moves through a floor, finding every mind, every door, slipping under every sill. I felt it enter the milk-warming woman, who dropped to her knees in the trampled snow. I felt it enter the twins, the dream-boy, the old fence-runner with one lung, the newborn in its mother's arms—who would grow up thinking he had always known how wars begin. Four hundred minds, and into each one the banners, the burning orchards, the thorned crown, the terrible ordinary truth of it: *this happened, and this is how, and you were not there but now you have no choice.*

Some screamed. Some sang louder. Most simply went still, the way a congregation goes still.

And then I walked down the hill toward Inquisitor Vane.

He had ridden ahead of his lances—I should have guessed he would; a man does not send soldiers to fetch his own heart. He sat his grey horse at the camp's edge with the firelight sliding over his face, and up close, for the first time, I saw what three centuries had done to him: nothing. Nothing at all. His skin was smooth as paper that has never been written on, and his eyes held the particular exhaustion of someone who has watched every person he ever mourned turn to dust and kept riding anyway. The last soldier of the thorn-king. Sustained past all sense by a war he could not finish and could not abandon.

"Almeida," he said. "Where is it?"

"You know where it is," I said. "You've always known. You've been reading me since the Salt Gate. You could have taken it from me at any time." I stopped an arm's length from his stirrup. "But you can't, can you? You can't take memories. You can only keep yours. That's your curse—that's what the king did to you when he crowned himself. He gave his men forever, but he never gave them the gift. You've had three hundred years to remember and no way to make anyone else remember with you."

Something moved in Vane's face. Paper, written on at last.

"They burned the orchards," he said, and his voice cracked like a boy's. "They burned everything, and the histories call him a tyrant. No one living remembers what he actually—" He stopped. Looked down at me. Understood.

"You've seen it," he whispered.

"I'm finished seeing it," I said. "Now everyone has."

I reached up, and I did the hardest theft of my life.

His guilt came into me like drowning—centuries of it, the weight of every order followed, every pyre lit, every friend's descendant hunted down in Vane's long, patient, monstrous grief. He had told himself he was keeping faith. Holding all that guilt, I understood for one staggering moment why he had never died: guilt is a kind of anchor, and anchors hold things upright. I took it all. Every year. Every flame.

And then, because I am what I am—an archivist to the bone, incapable of holding anything without also giving it—I opened myself the way I had opened the seal, and I gave it to the camp.

Four hundred minds received the Inquisitor's three hundred years of sorrow. They staggered under it. And they held it. Because that is what you do, when grief is shared. You do not get to set it down alone.

Vane slid from his horse. On the ground he looked smaller than a man should, older suddenly, as if the years I had taken from him had only been waiting for permission to arrive. Around us, his lances were dismounting too, uncertainly—and I saw a sergeant put a hand to his temple and sway, because he had just remembered the thorn-king's orchards as if he'd stood in them, and he had just remembered Vane's guilt as if he'd earned it, and there was no edict in the world, no register, no list, that could pull one guilty man out of a crowd where everyone was guilty of the same remembering.

"No one can be singled out," Vane said. Not bitterly. Wonderingly. "That was his plan. All along. That was always—"

"Yes," I said.

He looked at the campfires, at the singers gone silent, at the milk-woman helping a soldier to his feet. "What happens to me now?"

"Now," I said, "you learn to carry it with company. Like the rest of us."

* * *

The hunt ended that night without a treaty, without a battle, without a single word written by the crown—for what edict survives when every subject of the realm shares one memory, and that memory indicts the throne? Word traveled, mind to mind, city to city, faster than any rider: the gift was loose, it was everywhere, it belonged to everyone. Within a month the registers were being burned in the same squares where people had burned for them. Within a season, the witch-hunters' commissions were dissolved by magistrates who woke weeping from dreams of burning orchards they had never seen.

I stayed at the camp through the thaw. Someone had to keep the records.

I bought a new ledger in the spring market—plain paper, good binding, the kind of book that means to be trusted—and on the first page, in my careful, unremarkable hand, I wrote a new heading: *Given, not taken.* Beneath it I entered Odile's fiddler's tune. Beatrix's sealed trust, returned ten thousandfold. The thorn-king's last day, given away four hundred times over and counting. Vane's centuries of guilt, now borne in common. Each entry signed not by me but by the giver, for that was the rule of the new book: I recorded only what was surrendered freely, and I recorded it so that no one—not the crown, not the guild, not history itself—could ever again pretend these things had belonged to only one of us.

On the evening the ice went out of the river, the milk-warming woman found me at my table by the fireside, chewing my pen the way I do when an entry resists phrasing.

"There's a girl down by the east fires," she said. "Sixteen. Says she has something she wants to give. Says she was told to find the archivist."

I looked up. "Told by whom?"

"She wouldn't say. Only that whoever it was promised her you'd know the shape of it." The woman hesitated. "She says it's a memory of what happened to the thorn-king's body. After the war. Where they buried him."

I set down my pen.

Some entries, I thought, even a ledger of gifts is not ready for.

"Show me the girl," I said, and reached for a fresh page.
