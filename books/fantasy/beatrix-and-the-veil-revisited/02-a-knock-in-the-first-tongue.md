## 2. A Knock in the First Tongue

The rain came down like a verdict.

Jude heard it before they saw it—heard it through the walls of the Gilded Veil, through the shelves of impossible things that should have been silent and were not, quite, tonight. A faint restlessness had entered the inventory around dusk, the way a dog grows restless before an earthquake no one else can feel. The bottled laughter on the third shelf had gone from amber to something nearer gold. The jar of unspent goodbyes was sweating. And the feather—the last feather of the bird that once carried prayers between realms—had begun to tremble against its velvet cradle like a heart afraid of what it remembered.

Jude noticed these things because noticing was their whole profession now. Nine years of ledgers. Nine years of prices paid and prices owed, of ink-stained fingers and a name they did not speak aloud even to themselves. *Inventory is a kind of prayer,* the old guildmaster liked to say, though Jude had long suspected Corvane's prayers were addressed to different gods than most.

They moved down the aisles with a lamp turned low, checking seals, noting disturbances in a hand so small and cramped it could only be read by someone who had spent years trying to make themselves illegible. That was the trick of exile, Jude had learned. You did not hide by disappearing. You hid by becoming so ordinary, so meticulous, so *boring* that no magistrate's eye would ever linger. The storm-caller who had drowned a harvest festival became, in nine years' time, a clerk with neat columns and a cough and no story worth telling.

Outside, the rain thickened. Inside, the wares hummed.

Jude paused at the shelf of borrowed shadows—little stoppered vials of darkness people had sold when their own became too heavy—and pressed one ear close. The hum was not mechanical. It was not the settling of glass or the creak of old wood. It was *answering*. Something out in the wet dark was speaking, and the things in the guild's keeping were leaning toward the sound like flowers toward a sun that had been dead for centuries.

Jude knew that sound. They had hoped never to hear it again.

It had been the last thing they heard at their trial, before the sentence: a voice reading the charges in a language with no country, no alphabet any mortal school taught, words that had made the courtroom's iron fittings shiver and the magistrates' seals darken as if bruised. The First Tongue, the eldest of them had called it, with something like reverence and something like fear. The language that had bound the mortal world and the Veil together, back when binding was still considered a kindness. The language of the covenant. No one spoke it anymore. That was rather the point of the exile laws—that the bridge be burned on both ends, and the burners forget fire.

And yet something outside the Gilded Veil was speaking it now, into the rain, into the night, and every stolen thing on every shelf was straining toward the voice like a debt remembering its debtor.

* * *

The knock, when it came, was small.

Three taps, low on the door—the height of a child, not a courier, not a magistrate. Jude stood very still in the aisle of borrowed shadows and listened to the pattern of it, because everything in this guild had a pattern, and patterns were how you told a customer from a threat. Customers knocked in threes, evenly spaced, impatient. Magistrates knocked in twos, hard, official. This knock was uneven. Hesitant. The rhythm of someone who had never been taught that doors opened for people who asked properly—or someone who had been taught, long ago and far away, in a language that was not this one.

Jude set down the lamp. Crossed to the door. Put one eye to the seam.

A child stood in the rain. Small—seven years old, perhaps eight, though hunger made a liar of ages. Mud to the knees, mud to the elbows, mud dried in pale crescents under eyes that were too wide and too calm at once, the calm of someone who had run until running stopped being frightening and started being simply true. Hair plastered flat. Clothes that had once belonged to someone bigger. And clutched against their chest, wrapped in oilcloth with the desperate tenderness of the drowning, a bundle about the size of a loaf of bread.

The child looked up—not at the door, but at the seam where the light leaked through. Straight at Jude's eye, as if mud and dark and nine years of careful invisibility were nothing at all.

Then the child spoke.

Jude did not understand the words. That was the terrible part. They understood them *anyway*—the way you understand a bell, the way you understand grief. The syllables were old and round and heavy, each one landing in the wet street like a stone dropped in a well, and inside the guild, the humming rose. The bottled laughter sang. The jar of unspent goodbyes rattled its lid. The feather beat once, twice, against its velvet, a trapped thing hearing the sky named aloud.

And beneath all of it, deeper, in the place where Jude kept the thing they had sworn never to touch again—the place where the storm lived, leashed and starved—something turned over in its sleep.

Jude's hands went cold. Not fear, exactly. Recognition. The body remembers what the mind is permitted to forget, and Jude's body remembered standing in a courtroom while those same syllables fell like sentencing stones, remembered the taste of ozone on their tongue when they tried to answer in kind and were forbidden, remembered the exact weight of the word *exile* spoken in a language older than mercy.

The child said it again. Whatever *it* was. Patient. Unhurried. As if the child had all night, and perhaps believed the world owed them the courtesy of listening.

Jude unlocked the door.

Later—they would have years to examine it later, if there were later—they would ask themselves why. Why open the door to a stranger in the rain, when the first law of survival at the Gilded Veil was *open nothing after midnight*? But in the moment there was no decision, only motion, only the latch giving way, only the cold wet smell of a child made of riverbank and road dust stepping across a threshold that had not admitted anything impossible in nine careful years.

"You shouldn't be here," Jude said, in the common tongue, uselessly.

The child tilted their head. Answered in the First Tongue. Three more syllables, soft, almost gentle, and the lamp flame bent toward them like a listener leaning in.

"I don't—" Jude began, and then stopped, because the child's bundle had shifted, and the oilcloth had slipped, and underneath it Jude saw what the child had carried through miles of mud and rain without once setting it down.

A stone. Ordinary-looking, river-smooth, grey. Except that it was humming too, humming in time with the shelves, humming in time with the child's voice, and when the light touched it, letters moved across its surface like fish under ice. Old letters. Letters Jude had seen exactly once before, branded into the iron collar they had worn for a year after the trial, before even the collars were deemed too merciful and the exile was made absolute.

"Where did you get that?" Jude whispered.

The child looked at them with those too-wide, too-calm eyes, and said something in the First Tongue that Jude could not parse and yet felt land in their chest like a hand: *I was sent. I am the sending.*

Or something near enough. Translation was a dangerous business with a language like that; it translated the listener as much as the words.

* * *

The night porter's boots rang on the stair at half past two.

Jude heard him coming the way animals hear weather—a wrongness in the building's breathing, a pause in the drip of the eaves. Tammas Vole, night porter of the Gilded Veil, honest as a hammer and nearly as clever, making his rounds with his ring of keys and his lantern and his unshakable conviction that nothing strange ever happened in this building because he had worked here eleven years and seen nothing strange, which was itself the strangest fact about the place.

There was no protocol for this. There was no ledger line for *child speaking the dead language found on doorstep*. Jude had spent nine years learning every rule of the guild precisely so that moments like this would find them prepared, and now the moment had arrived and every rule stood silent, useless, staring.

So Jude improvised, which was the one skill exile had actually taught them well.

The child went into the counting room—behind the great ledger desk, in the gap between the wall and the cabinet of confiscated names, a space barely deep enough for a cat and smelling of dust and old ink. Jude gave them the blanket from the clerk's cot, the oilcloth bundle tucked against their chest, a finger pressed to lips in a gesture that needed no translation in any tongue. The child's eyes stayed calm. Too calm. The calm of someone who had already been hidden from worse things than a porter.

Then Jude sat down at the desk, pulled the current ledger close, and became the most boring person in the world.

Tammas's lantern swung into the doorway. "Still at it, Winterbourne?" He always used the surname, never the given name—guild custom, or perhaps his own small kindness, since the given name had been struck from the rolls along with everything else. "Corvane'll have your eyes if you ruin them."

"Columns don't balance themselves," Jude said, in the flattest voice they owned.

Tammas grunted, shuffled, lingered. He was a man who lingered when something itched at him without ever finding the itch. His lantern light swept the counting room, caught the cabinet, slid past the gap behind it where a child's muddy footprints ended in a smear of shadow. Jude turned a page. Turned another. Let the silence do its work.

"Rain's bad," Tammas said at last. "Door held fine?"

"Held fine."

"No one come?"

"No one comes." Jude looked up then, just enough, and met his eyes with the particular blankness that nine years of practice had ground smooth as river stone. "No one ever comes."

Tammas laughed—the laugh of a man reassured by his own dullness—and his lantern swung away, down the stair, out toward the front door, where he would check the bolts twice and find them sound and sleep the sleep of the thoroughly deceived.

Jude sat in the dark a long moment after his footsteps faded, heart beating somewhere up around the throat, listening to the child breathe behind the cabinet. Small breaths. Steady breaths. The breath of someone sleeping sitting up, wrapped around a humming stone, guarded by a stranger who owed them nothing and was beginning, horribly, to suspect they might owe them everything.

Because here was the thought Jude had been refusing all night, the thought that kept returning like water finding its level:

The trial. The syllables. The humming shelves. The stone with moving letters. The child sent, the child *sending*. None of it was random. Jude had spent nine years believing their exile was a full stop at the end of a sentence, and now a mud-soaked eight-year-old had walked out of the rain speaking the language of the sentence itself, and every impossible thing the guild owned was singing toward her like iron filings toward a lodestone.

Something was coming undone. Something old. And it had knocked on *this* door, of all doors, in the whole sprawl of both realms.

Jude rose quietly, crossed to the cabinet, and knelt in the dust beside the gap. The child was awake. Of course the child was awake. Those wide calm eyes found Jude's in the dark, and the bundle shifted, and the stone's letters swam, and the child spoke again—softly, gently, the way you speak to someone you have traveled a very long way to find.

Jude didn't know the words.

But for the first time in nine years, Jude realized, with a fear that curdled slowly, reluctantly, into something almost like hope:

They were going to have to learn.
