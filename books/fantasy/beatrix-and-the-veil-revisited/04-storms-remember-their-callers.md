## 4. Storms Remember Their Callers

The magistrates came at the hour when even the Gilded Veil pretended to sleep.

Jude heard them before they knocked—the particular silence of boots that had been told to be quiet, which is louder than any shout. Nine years of keeping ledgers had taught them the guild's every sound: the settling of the roofbeams at midnight, the sigh of the cellar doors breathing cold air, the way the wares murmured to themselves in the dark like children talking in their sleep. Tonight the murmuring had stopped. Every impossible thing in the building was holding its breath.

Jude set down the inventory pen. Ink pooled at the nib and fell, a small black star on the page.

*They've come for the child.*

Sable was asleep in the linen room, curled among bolts of cloth woven from a widow's grief—sold to the guild by a man who could no longer bear to look at it. The child slept the way Sable did everything: completely, without apology, one hand closed around a brass key that hummed faintly against their palm. Jude had learned not to touch it. When they had, three nights ago, they'd tasted rain on the back of their tongue, rain from a sky they hadn't seen in nine years.

Another silence. Then voices, low and clipped, at the front of the house. Corvane's answering voice, smooth as poured honey over something with teeth.

Jude rose. Their body made the decision before their mind agreed to it—an old instinct, older than exile, older than the ledgers: *get between the danger and the small thing.* They moved through the dark corridors without a lamp. They knew this house blind. That was the joke of it, wasn't it? Nine years invisible, and now invisibility was the only mercy left to spend.

At the top of the cellar stair, they stopped and listened.

"—the warrant is sealed, Guildmaster. We are within our rights."

"Of course you are." Corvane's voice, warm and reasonable, a hearth-fire voice. "Search whatever you like. But I'll remind the honored magistrates that the Gilded Veil's neutrality is written into the Accords of Both Realms. You trample my threshold tonight, and tomorrow every guildhouse from here to the Salt Gate closes its books to your courts."

A pause. Then the second magistrate, drier, colder: "The child speaks the First Tongue. That supersedes your accords, Corvane. The covenant is failing. Everything your guild ever sold is being *called home.* You know what that means. Your fortune is standing in a linen room upstairs, wearing mud."

And there it was. Not a search. A foreclosure.

Jude's hands went cold. Not with fear—or not only fear. Something under the fear had begun to stir, something they had buried so deep and for so long that they had almost convinced themselves it was dead. It woke the way an old scar wakes before weather comes: a pressure, a pull, the sense of a vast attention turning toward them through nine years of stone.

*No,* Jude thought, in the discipline of nine years. *Not here. Not now. Not again.*

But storms remember their callers.

It began in their fingertips—a static itch, a taste of ozone blooming at the root of the tongue. The lamp-flames in the corridor leaned toward Jude like flowers toward a sun that should not exist indoors. Downstairs, someone said, "Do you feel that? Pressure's dropping."

Jude turned and climbed.

* * *

Sable woke the moment Jude touched their shoulder—not with a start but with a terrible readiness, eyes open and already focused, as if the child had been waiting all night for exactly this hand.

"They're here," Sable said. Not in the common tongue. In the First Tongue, the language that had once stitched the mortal world to the Veil, the language Jude had last heard spoken across a trial chamber while their own name was struck from every register in both realms. The words went through Jude like a bell struck against the breastbone.

"Yes," Jude answered, in the same tongue, and hated how rusty it sounded, how much like a wound reopening. "We have to go."

"Through the water?"

Jude stared. "How do you—"

"The rain told me." Sable sat up, clutching the brass key. "It's been telling me since I got here. It says—" the child frowned, searching, "—it says it remembers you. It says you owe it a conversation."

Outside, thunder answered. Not distant thunder. Close. A rolling, deliberate sound, like something enormous shifting its weight, listening.

Jude felt the storm-magic rise another inch inside them, warm and terrible, like standing too near a lit forge. For nine years they had kept it down with ledgers and routine and the small daily violence of being no one. They had told themselves the exile had taken it—that the magistrates' sentence had burned the calling out of them the way a brand burns out infection. They had almost believed it.

Almost.

Because here it was anyway, rising through the ash of them, recognizing its name in a child's mouth.

"The undercity," Jude said aloud, because speaking ordinary words helped, gave the rising thing somewhere to go besides outward. "There are flood-channels beneath the guild. Old ones, from before the Veil was built on top of them. They run to the river past the cisterns. If we can reach the second lock—"

Sable was already pulling on their ruined shoes.

Downstairs, a door burst open hard enough to crack wood. Voices sharpened. Boots on stairs.

Jude took the child's hand—small, mud-grained, gripping back with surprising strength—and ran.

* * *

The cellar was cold and smelled of brine and old iron, of things kept too long in the dark. Jude pulled aside a rack of bottled echoes—little glass spheres in which voices lived, sold to people who wanted to hear the dead say one more kind word—and revealed the seam in the wall behind them. The lock was guild-made; it knew Jude's hands. It opened with a groan like something waking reluctantly, and beyond it lay the flood-channel: black water moving slow and patient in a tunnel of ancient brick, the ceiling lost in darkness above the reach of any lamp.

"In," Jude said. "Don't let go."

The water was waist-deep and shockingly cold. Sable didn't whimper, didn't hesitate—walked into it like walking into a held note. Behind them, torchlight spilled down into the channel, and a voice shouted something about the warrant, about both realms, about what happened to covenant-thieves and storm-callers.

That word. *Storm-caller.*

The magic in Jude surged toward the surface like a drowned thing breaking for air.

"No," Jude whispered—and then the first bolt came, unbidden, leaping from their fingertips into the black water ahead of them, illuminating the whole flooded tunnel in one terrible white instant: brick arches receding into forever, fish hanging suspended in the flash like a held breath, Sable's face turned up toward Jude with an expression that wasn't fear at all.

It was wonder.

"Do it again," the child breathed.

"I can't control it," Jude said, and the honesty of it cracked something open. "That's why they exiled me. I never meant—I was fifteen, I didn't call it on purpose, I just *grieved*, and the sky grieved with me, and three villages drowned in what my sadness shook loose. They wrote my name out of the world for it. And now—" their voice shook, "—now it's coming back and I don't know if it will drown us too."

Sable considered this with the gravity of someone far older than eight or nine or however old the child was; age seemed beside the point with Sable.

"You're holding it wrong," the child said simply. "You're holding it like it's yours to hold alone."

Behind them, splashing. Lantern-light. The magistrates had found the channel.

Jude ran.

* * *

What followed, Jude would remember afterward in fragments, the way one remembers a fever.

Running through waist-deep water that fought them like living hands. The tunnel branching, branching again, Jude navigating by some animal sense while the pursuit-lamps swung wild reflections across the wet brick. Sable's hand in theirs, small and fierce. And underneath everything, rising with every step, the storm—

—it wasn't coming *back*, Jude realized. It had never left. It had gone underground with them, into the ledger-dark, into the nine years of careful nothing, and all that time it had been growing roots. Now those roots were tearing loose, and power flooded up through Jude's body like spring flood through a dry riverbed, terrifying, ecstatic, *familiar*—

Coming home to a burning house. That was the truth of it. Every step was terror and every step was joy and the two feelings were the same feeling, indistinguishable, the way grief and love are sometimes the same feeling wearing different coats. The magic didn't ask permission. It had never asked permission. It only answered—grief with rain, rage with lightning, loneliness with wind that howled at windows wanting in.

And Jude had been lonely for nine years.

Ahead, the channel opened into the great cistern, a cathedral of water and echo where the undercity's floods gathered before bleeding into the river. Moonlight fell through grates far above in pale broken columns. And the water in the cistern was *rising*, Jude saw—responding, answering, climbing the walls toward the storm that lived in their bones.

"Jude." Sable tugged their hand. Pointed up, at one of the high grates, where rain was beginning to fall *through* the grate—downward, inward, impossibly, rain falling into the earth to find them. "It wants to help."

"It's not supposed to be able to find me," Jude said, half laughing, half sobbing. "I paid to make sure it couldn't. Nine years ago, I paid the guild everything I had to make sure the sky forgot my face."

"But it didn't forget." Sable looked up at them, rain gathering on their lashes. "Storms remember their callers. You said—you said it yourself, in the cellar. Maybe that's not the curse, Jude. Maybe that's the covenant. Maybe that's what it *means*."

Lamps flared at the tunnel mouth behind them. Shouted orders. Crossbow-strings drawn taut with a sound like teeth setting.

The storm broke loose.

Jude didn't call it. That was the thing they would carry afterward, the thing that changed everything: they didn't call it, and it came anyway, and instead of drowning anyone it *lifted* them. Wind roared down through the grates in silver columns; the cistern's waters heaved upward in a spiraling slope; and Jude and Sable rode the rising water like a hill being built under their feet, up through moonlight and rain, up through the grate and out—out into the open night, onto the wet rooftops of the lower city, the storm raging around them like a loyal hound finally allowed off its chain.

Below, the magistrates' lamps dwindled. Above, the rain fell and fell, and every drop of it felt like a word in a language Jude had been punished for knowing.

Sable was laughing. Wild, soaked, triumphant laughter, the laughter of a child who has just seen the world admit it was bigger than the adults claimed.

Jude laughed too—helplessly, terrified, alive for the first time in nine years.

Then, across the rooftops, they saw it: a single lamp burning in a garret window at the edge of the Mapmakers' Quarter, in a tower that appeared on no map at all. And nailed to the garret door, fluttering in the storm-wind, a paper chart whose ink glowed faintly blue—glowed the way First Tongue words glow when they are true.

Sable stopped laughing. "Someone's expecting us," the child said quietly.

Jude's stomach dropped through nine floors of night. "How can you possibly know that?"

"Because," Sable said, "the rain just told me who lives there. And Jude—it used her name. It called her *Wren*."
