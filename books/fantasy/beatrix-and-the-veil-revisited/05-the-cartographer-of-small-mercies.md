## 5. The Cartographer of Small Mercies

Wren Ashgrave had been drawing the same impossible city for eleven years when the door of her shop opened without being knocked.

She knew better than to look up too quickly. In her trade—the *real* trade, the one that had cost her her name and her seat at the Guild of Cartographers—you learned to let strangers commit to their entrances before you committed to your exit. So she kept her pen moving, laying a thin silver line along a street that had never existed and never would, and she listened.

Two sets of feet. One heavy with hesitation. One light as a dropped coin. And beneath both, a sound she hadn't heard since she was a girl kneeling in her grandmother's map-room: a low harmonic hum, like a tuning fork struck against bone, coming from the rolled charts in her cabinet.

The impossible things in her maps were waking up.

"You're Wren Ashgrave," said the taller figure—a woman, rain-slicked, with the gray-pale look of someone who had spent nine years standing very still so no one would notice her. "They say you chart places that don't exist."

"They say I forge maps," Wren corrected, not looking up. "There's a difference. Forgers copy what's real. I invent. It's more honest work than it sounds."

"I need a road," the woman said. "One that isn't on any honest map."

Wren set down her pen at last and looked at her properly. Behind the woman stood a child—mud to the knees, hair like storm-wrack, eyes the color of deep water where something vast moved underneath. The child was staring at the charts on the wall with an expression Wren recognized from mirrors: homesickness for a place you'd never been.

"That depends," Wren said slowly, "on whether you mean a road that doesn't exist yet, or a road that stopped existing because someone unmade it."

The woman's jaw tightened. "Stopped."

"Then you'd better sit down," Wren said, "and tell me what in the name of every unmapped place has happened to the world. Because three of my charts went blank this morning. Blank. Not torn, not burned—*blank*. The ink simply gave up. A river I drew twenty years ago, one that has never existed anywhere but on vellum, dried up in the night." She laughed, and it came out wrong, thin as old paper. "Do you understand? My inventions are dying. Which means they were never inventions at all."

* * *

Her grandmother had taught her the first rule of cartography before she taught her to hold a pen: *a map is a promise the land makes to itself.* Wren had believed it the way children believe in the honesty of adults—completely, and until proven otherwise.

At nineteen she had presented the Cartographers' Guild with her masterwork: a chart of the Hollowmere, a marsh-country that appeared on no survey, answered to no census, and which she swore on her guild-ring she had walked herself. They had sent surveyors. The surveyors found sheep pasture. She was stripped of her ring for forgery, and worse—for insisting, afterward, that the marsh was real, that it had *moved*, that the land rearranged itself like a sleeper turning over.

Eleven years of selling invented coastlines to collectors who wanted beautiful lies. Eleven years of noticing, and refusing to notice, that her fictional places were always *almost* somewhere real—that the Hollowmere sat exactly where an old survey recorded a drowned village called Mercy's Rest; that her invented city of Vell occupied the footprint of a town struck from every record in a single season two hundred years ago.

She hadn't known she wasn't inventing. She'd been *remembering*, in the only language left to her.

Now the woman—Jude, she said her name was, just Jude, no family, no title, a name worn smooth by use—sat across Wren's worktable while the child Sable drifted along the walls, trailing fingers over framed charts. Wherever the child touched, the ink stirred faintly, like pond water disturbed by a fish.

"The covenant," Jude was saying. "It bound the mortal world and the Veil together, in the First Tongue. Everything impossible was a *loan*, Wren—not a gift, not a theft exactly, though theft is close enough. The guild sold pieces of it. Rivers that forget their courses. Luck you could bottle. Roads through country that officially never existed." Her eyes flicked to the blank charts. "Your Hollowmere."

"Mercy's Rest," Wren whispered. "It drowned. Or—it was *made* to drown, and then made to be forgotten, and somehow the forgetting leaked into me and came out as maps." She pressed her palm flat against the table to stop her hands shaking. "I've been drawing stolen property for eleven years and calling it art."

"Now the debt's being called home," Jude said. "Everything the guild ever sold is going back to the world's bones. And there are people—" she paused, choosing the word carefully, "—*bearers*, scattered across both realms, who each hold one word of the original covenant. Living words. If we don't gather them and reforge the pact at the Well of First Words, everything impossible comes home permanently. Including, I suspect, a great many people who shouldn't be possible anymore."

"Such as?"

Jude's smile was a small, dry, terrible thing. "Exiled storm-callers. Forged mapmakers. Children who speak dead languages." She laid a folded paper on the table—a guild chit, stamped with the Gilded Veil's sigil, worth more than Wren's shop. "Payment. And a request. We need the first bearer. An old ferrywoman on the Lachrymose, upriver past the Salt Gate. There's no road there. Not on any map."

"There's no road there on *anyone's* map," Wren agreed. Then, before she could stop herself, before eleven years of caution could catch her words and drag them back: "But I've drawn it. A hundred times. A towpath along the western bank that doesn't exist, through a forest that was clear-cut sixty years ago, except that it wasn't, except that it *unwasn't*—" She laughed again, giddy and frightened. "Gods. I know the way. I've always known the way. I just thought I was making it up."

Across the room, Sable turned from the wall of charts and looked at her. The child spoke then—a short phrase in a language like stones settling in a riverbed—and though Wren understood nothing of it, the blank charts on the shelf behind her hummed in answer, and for one moment she saw, superimposed over her shop's water-stained ceiling, the pale underside of willow branches, and heard water moving where water had never been.

"What did she say?" she asked hoarsely.

"She says," Jude translated, and her voice had gone soft and strange, "*welcome home, cousin.*"

* * *

They left before dawn, three figures with a hand-cart full of maps, moving south through a city still dripping from the week's floods. Wren led them down alleys she had charted but never walked, and the strange thing—the thing that made her chest ache in a way she refused to examine—was that the alleys were *there*. Every one. A passage behind the tanneries that no surveyor had ever found: real. A stair descending between two buildings toward a canal that had been bricked over in her grandmother's time: real, and wet, and singing faintly under Sable's small hand on the rail.

"It's her," Jude murmured, watching the child walk. "Wherever Sable goes, the forgotten things lean toward the light. Like plants."

"Like debts," Wren said. "Debtors lean toward the creditor."

They crossed the flooded undercity by ferry—a silent, gray woman with rope-scarred palms who took one look at Sable and waved away their fare. At the far bank, as they climbed toward the southern gate, the child stumbled, and Jude caught her, and the look that passed between them—quick, awkward, fiercely tender—made Wren feel like an intruder and a guest at once.

Three broken things, she thought. A storm that couldn't storm. A mapmaker whose maps told the truth by accident. A child carrying a dying language like a lantern through a collapsing house.

Not a family. Families had names and tables and histories. This was something rougher—an unfinished coastline, a border drawn in pencil, subject to revision. But walking behind them through the waking streets, listening to Sable narrate the undercity in the First Tongue while Jude translated in a low voice and the forgotten canal sang beneath their feet, Wren found herself, absurdly, humming. An old tune. A lullaby about a ferrywoman who carried souls across the Lachrymose and charged only true names for fare.

She stopped mid-note.

"Wren?" Jude glanced back. "What is it?"

"I didn't learn that song anywhere," Wren said slowly. "I made it up when I was eight. Grandmother said it was clever of me." Her mouth had gone dry. "Jude—the first bearer. The ferrywoman. What's her name?"

"No one knows it. That's rather the point."

"Well." Wren laughed, high and unsteady, and unrolled a fresh chart across the top of the cart, pinning its corners against the wind as the southern gate rose before them, dark against a sky bruising toward storm. "Either I'm remembering things that haven't happened to me, or I've been carrying around other people's memories for eleven years and one of them belongs to a woman on the Lachrymose who may or may not be expecting us."

Sable tugged her sleeve and pointed at the chart. On it, in Wren's own silver-fine line, the towpath wound along the western bank—but the ink was shifting, redrawing itself, and where the path met the river it now ended at a small square mark that had not been there a moment ago.

A landing stage. And beside it, written in an old-fashioned hand that was not Wren's:

*Come by water. Come before the magistrates. Bring the child — and bring my song.*

Behind them, far off across the rooftops, bells began to ring in the pattern Wren had dreaded since dawn: the magistrates' alarm. Three strokes, pause, three strokes.

A hunt had begun.
