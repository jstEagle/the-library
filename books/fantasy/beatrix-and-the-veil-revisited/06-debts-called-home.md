## 6. Debts Called Home

Wren had charted the river Serle three times in her life, and each time it had been a little different, because the Serle was one of those waterways that could never quite decide where it wanted to go. She had drawn its oxbows with the affection of an old friend documenting a wanderer. She knew where it deepened into green silence beneath the willows at Marrow Bend, and where it went shallow and chattering over stones bright as dropped coins. She had mapped the ferry crossing at Dunlow, and the drowned mill two miles south of it that the locals swore still turned on moonless nights, grinding flour no one ever collected.

So when she crested the ridge above Dunlow on the third morning out from the flooded undercity, and looked down at where the Serle should have been, she stopped walking so abruptly that Sable walked into her back.

"That's wrong," Wren said.

The riverbed was there. That was the terrible part of it. The bed remained—every stone, every groove the current had worn over centuries, every pale scar of silt—laid open to the sky like the vein of the world exposed. But the water was gone. Not dammed, not diverted, not dried up. Gone the way a word is gone once it has been unsaid. The stones were wet. The reeds still leaned the way reeds lean when a current combs through them. And yet nothing moved down there but light, pooling in the empty channel like something spilled.

Sable stood on her toes against Wren's pack strap and made a sound low in her throat—not a word in any tongue Wren knew, though by now she recognized the shape of them. First Tongue. The child said something soft and grieving, the way you might speak over a deathbed, and then translated herself, in her flat, careful trade-speech: "It is being called home."

"Called home," Wren repeated. Her mouth had gone dry. "Rivers don't have homes."

"They do," Sable said. "You just couldn't hear where they lived."

---

They came down into Dunlow because there was no way around it—the bridge was the only crossing for twenty miles—and because Wren told herself that a town with a missing river would at least have a well. What it had instead was a crowd gathered silent at the foot of the bridge, all of them facing the empty channel as if attending a funeral, which, Wren supposed, they were.

She heard fragments as she led Jude and Sable along the crowd's edge. A fisherman saying, over and over, to no one in particular, "Thirty years. Thirty years I've thrown my nets in her." An old woman holding a clay jar filled with nothing, weeping without noise. Two boys crouched at the bank's edge, touching the wet stones with their fingertips and pulling their hands back as if scalded.

"It isn't only here," Jude murmured. They had gone very still in the particular way Wren had learned meant their ledger-mind was running. "I processed requisitions. Three years ago, the guild sold a *winter* to the salt merchants of Corrane—a genuine winter, cold enough to freeze the shallows so they could haul stone across. Where did we source it? I never asked. I inventoried it. Crate forty-one, winter, one, provenance withheld." Their voice thinned. "Provenance was always withheld."

"You didn't know," Wren said.

"I balanced the books." Jude said it like a confession, which, Wren was beginning to understand, it was. "Nine years I kept accounts of impossible things and told myself the arithmetic was clean. Every entry has two sides. If something arrived in our vaults, something left the world."

The words settled over Wren like cold fog. Because she had spent fifteen years drawing maps of places that did not exist, and being stripped of her credentials for insisting that they *did* exist—that there were islands off the western charts that appeared only to the desperate, roads that led out of the world entirely, wells whose water remembered being rain. The cartographers' guild had called her a fraud. They had taken her seal and broken it in front of her.

But what if the truth was worse than fraud? What if the places she had charted were not imaginary at all, not quite—but *withdrawn*. Sold. Taken out of the world's reach and locked behind the Veil, visible only to people standing outside ordinary hope. She had been mapping, all these years, the inventory of a theft.

Her hands, she noticed, were shaking. She knelt and pretended to retie her boot until they stopped.

---

They crossed at midday, and that night, in the shelter of a hollowed-out barrow hill that Wren found by memory—one of her impossible places, a barrow that appeared on no honest map—they built a small fire, and Wren unrolled her charts, and began, methodically, dreadfully, to check her work against the world.

She had a system. She always had a system; it was the one comfort her disgrace had left her. Each chart was dated. Each place marked. So she took the western coast sheet, and her memory of the fishing village of Aldersey where she'd once spent a week charting a harbor that appeared only at slack tide, and she asked Jude, who had traveled widest: *"Is Aldersey still there?"*

Jude went quiet. "I don't know," they said slowly. "I passed through four years ago. There was a village." A pause. "There was a village, and there was luck. You could feel it. The boats came home heavy. Nobody quarreled. Children were born easy. I remember thinking it was the most fortunate place I'd ever seen, and wondering vaguely why, and then—" they stopped. Their jaw worked. "Then I got back to the Veil and filed an invoice. Lot sixty-six. *Fortune, municipal, one township's worth, minor.* Buyer confidential. I remember the entry because the sum was obscene and the description was so small. One line. One township's worth."

"Aldersey," Wren said.

"It can't be."

"Check the date."

Jude closed their eyes. Wren watched them do the thing she had seen them do in the undercity—turn inward to the great archive of their nine years, the ledgers written behind their eyes. When they opened them again, they looked old.

"The dates match," Jude said. "The sale cleared six weeks before I passed through. Six weeks is exactly how long a township's luck might take to... to arrive somewhere else." They put their head in their hands. "Gods. It doesn't even stay stolen. It just moves. We moved it. Someone's fortune was another someone's whole life, and I initialed the transfer."

Across the fire, Sable was feeding twigs to the flames with the solemn economy of a child who has done adult work. She spoke without looking up. "The covenant is calling everything back. All the pieces. To pay for being broken." She placed a twig with care. "The world wants its bones back."

"*Its bones,*" Wren repeated.

Sable nodded as if this were obvious. "Rivers are blood. Luck is marrow. Winters are breath. The guild took pieces of the world and sold them to other parts of the world, and now everything that was taken is going back to where it belongs, and everyone who bought it is learning that they never owned it at all." She looked up then, and her eyes in the firelight were ancient and seven years old at once. "That's what a covenant is. It's the world agreeing about who owns what. And yours broke it."

*Yours.* Wren felt the word land like a stone in her chest. She wanted to say *not me, I was never inside whatever this is, I've been outside the walls my whole life*—but even as she thought it, she understood that was precisely the point. She had been outside the walls. She had seen through them. That was why her maps were true and her career was dead. The disgraced mapmaker and the exiled storm-caller and the mud-soaked child: the ragged edge of a family, assembled from everyone the covenant's breaking had cut.

---

In the morning they found the first proof that it was not going to stop.

A tinker came up the road toward the barrow hill, driving a handcart, and he was talking to himself in the high, cracked voice of a man who had been talking to himself for days. Wren hailed him for news, and news is what she got, more than she wanted.

"—and in Corrane, see, the salt merchants, they had their frozen causeway, hauled stone across it every winter, rich as bishops—and now—" he laughed, a horrible sound, "—now the thaw won't come. Or rather the thaw came and *took the cold with it*, all of it, every scrap. Corrane's warm as summer and the salt pans are ruined and half the merchants have hanged themselves, and the other half are saying a guild did it, saying somebody *sold* their winter away years ago and the bill's come due—"

"Where did you hear that last part?" Jude asked, very quietly.

"Tavern talk. Everyone's saying it now." The tinker wiped his mouth. "And it's not just Corrane. Up north the whispering marshes have gone silent—all those years of voices out of the fog, gone, like a choir dismissed. And the city of Vell—you've heard about Vell?"

"No," said Wren, though she had begun to fear she already knew.

"Vell's lamps burn blue." He said it the way you'd say *the sea is rising*. "Every flame in the city, blue as a bruise. They bought something once, generations back, some bargain at a masked fair, and nobody remembered the price, and now the price is coming out of the wick. My grandmother used to say nothing in this world is free, only borrowed." He gathered his cart reins. "Well. The lender's come to collect."

He rolled on down the road. None of them spoke for a long time.

Then Jude sat down on the barrow's turf edge and pulled out the small traveling ledger they had carried since the flight from the Gilded Veil—the habit of nine years, the ink-stained fingers—and began, in a voice like a man reading his own sentence, to recite.

"Winter of Corrane, sourced unknown. Fortune of a township, provenance withheld. The whispering voices of the northern marshes—I catalogued those, crate eleven, labeled *voices, assorted, melancholy.*" A bitter breath. "*Assorted.* I thought it was odd phrasing at the time. I thought it meant the seller hadn't sorted them properly. It meant they were different people's voices. Different dead marsh-folk, or living ones, I'll never know, singing in the dark of our vault for thirty years while I dusted the shelves around them."

"Stop," Wren said.

"Why? It's the truth."

"Because you're reciting it like a ledger. Like it's still your job." She crouched in front of them and took the little book out of their hands—gently, but she took it—and closed it. "Listen to yourself. *Lot sixty-six. Crate forty-one.* You're doing their work for them even out here. Even fleeing. The numbers are how you keep from feeling it."

"And what would you have me do instead?" Jude's voice cracked open, and underneath the clerk was someone much younger and much more frightened. "Feel it? All of it? A township's luck, a river, a hundred winters, voices—Wren, I can't. If I feel the size of what that guild stole—if I feel what *I* carried for them—I will lie down in the road and never get up."

Sable had come to stand beside them. She put one small hand on Jude's knee and one on Wren's shoulder, and she said something in the First Tongue, three syllables, soft as riverbed stones. Then she translated: "It means: *what is taken can be carried back.*"

Wren looked at the child. At the fire's dead ring of stones. At the road running west, toward the coast, toward the first covenant-bearer, toward whatever came next.

"Then we carry," she said, and was surprised at the steadiness of her own voice. "But we carry it *feeling*, Jude. Both hands full. That's the price of helping to fix this—that you don't get to be numb anymore. None of us do."

Jude held out their hand. Wren gave the ledger back. And Wren watched them take a fresh page and, instead of lot numbers, write in small careful letters: *What we owe. What was taken. Whose it was.*

Below it, after a moment, they added: *Aldersey. The Serle. Corrane. The marshes. Vell.*

Not entries. Names. The beginnings, perhaps, of a very different kind of accounting.

They struck camp within the hour. As they came down onto the west road, Sable walking between them with her hand in Wren's, the wind changed, and from far off—from the direction of everything, from everywhere at once—came a sound Wren had no chart for and never wanted to make one: a vast, slow exhale, like the world turning over in a sleep that was ending.

Somewhere ahead, the next debt was coming due.

And somewhere ahead too—she held to this, walking, one step and another—was a rendezvous, a bearer of the second word, and maybe, at the end of all the reckoning, a way to give everything back without the world forgetting how to be generous. She had mapped impossible places her whole life. She could map this: the route from theft to restoration, from ledger to covenant, three broken travelers carrying the world's bones home.

She only hoped the world would let them arrive before it finished foreclosing on everything—including them.
