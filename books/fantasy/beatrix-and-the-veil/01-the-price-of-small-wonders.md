## 1. The Price of Small Wonders

The morning began, as all Edmund Underhill's mornings did, with paperwork.

He sat in the narrow back room of the Scribes' Guild annex on Candlewick Lane, copying out certifications in his best temple hand. *Certified Non-Miraculous Object*, each document read, followed by a description, a serial number, and the embossed seal of the Temple of the Ledger — a fat, dignified book stamped in grey wax. The seal was the whole point. The seal said: *this charm does nothing. This charm is permitted to do nothing. The bearer of this paper has paid four coppers for the privilege of owning nothing at all.*

Edmund had written eleven hundred such documents in six years, and he took a quiet, complicated pride in them. They were lies, technically. But they were lies filed correctly, and in Corvane there was no safer place for a small magic than inside a lie that was properly stamped.

He finished the last one — a *Non-Miraculous Warming Stone, River-rounded, Grey* — blotted it, and went downstairs to open his stall.

The stall lived three turns off Cordwainer's Row, tucked between a coffin-maker who never seemed to run out of customers and a pie shop whose pies were widely believed to contain no meat whatsoever. Edmund's table was four feet of weathered oak. His stock sat in a single shallow tray: river stones, knotted red thread, copper pennies polished bright, little folded papers of dried lavender. Nothing glowed. Nothing hummed. Nothing, if you asked it politely to do so in front of witnesses, would do anything at all.

That was the arrangement. That was the covenant between Edmund and the city of Corvane, unwritten but strictly observed: he sold hope shaped like junk, the customers bought hope shaped like junk, and everyone agreed not to notice when the junk occasionally worked.

"Morning, Underhill," said the coffin-maker, whose name was Herrick and whose hands smelled permanently of pine shavings. "Damp one today. My knee says rain by noon."

"Your knee isn't certified," Edmund said, laying out his tray. "I'll have to report it."

"My knee's been reported since the war." Herrick laughed at his own joke, as he did every morning, because every morning was the only time he told it.

Edmund smiled the way you smile at a joke you've heard nine hundred times — which is to say, warmly, and without any smile reaching further than the corners of his mouth. It was a skill. Most of his life was skills like that: smiling at jokes, keeping his handwriting bland, letting the gift sit coiled and quiet behind his breastbone like a swallow that had decided to winter over indoors.

Because the truth was this: the warming stones worked. Not much. A degree or two, held through an evening. The knot-threads eased headaches if you held them against your brow and thought, for a moment, of someone who loved you. The lavender papers kept night terrors off children — not by magic anyone could name, just by making the dark feel watched over rather than watchful. Small wonders. Harmless ones. The kind of thing the old law had called *domestic graces* and permitted freely, back before the dragons' world had cracked open and everything with a spark in it became suspect.

Now the new statutes hung on every corner post, printed in heavy black type:

**BY ORDER OF THE CROWN: ALL WORKING CHARM, SPELL, OR ENCHANTMENT IS OUTLAWED. INFORMANTS WILL BE REWARDED. IGNORANCE OF THE LAW IS NOT A DEFENSE.**

Edmund read it every morning, the way you'd touch a scar to check it still didn't hurt. Then he put up his little sign — *Certified Curiosities, Temple-Licensed* — and waited for customers who wanted nothing more than a stone that might, in defiance of all certification, be warm.

* * *

The morning's trade was gentle. A laundress bought two knot-threads, one for herself and one for her sister who was poorly. A carter's wife examined the warming stones at length, chose the ugliest one on the theory — correct, in Edmund's experience — that ugly stones worked harder, and haggled him down from four coppers to three with the serene confidence of a woman who had haggled better men than him into the grave.

And then, near midday, the child came.

She was perhaps seven, in a coat two sizes too large, trailing her mother by half a step the way children do when they've spotted something interesting and are pretending they haven't. She stopped at the stall. She looked at the tray. She looked up at Edmund with the terrible frankness of the very young.

"Which one works?" she asked.

"Hush, Tansy," said her mother, arriving flustered. "You mustn't say such things."

"But I want the one that *works*," Tansy said, with the iron insistence of a child who had not yet learned that wanting things out loud was dangerous.

Edmund crouched slightly, bringing himself nearer her level, and performed the speech he had given a thousand times. "None of them work, miss. See here—" He held up a certification slip. "This is official paper from the Temple of the Ledger. It promises these objects do nothing whatsoever. You can trust me, because I'm a scribe, and a scribe's whole business is writing down true things."

Tansy looked at him the way only seven-year-olds and cats can look at people — directly, without mercy, straight through the performance.

"You're lying," she said. "But it's a nice lie. Like when Gran says the soup is delicious."

"Tansy!" her mother hissed, going pale around the mouth.

Edmund felt something loosen in his chest — that dangerous warmth, the swallow stirring in its sleep. He should have sold the mother a knot-thread and sent them both on their way. Instead he heard himself say, quietly, "For your gran, then? Is she cold at night?"

Tansy nodded, suddenly solemn. "Her feet. All the time. Even under three blankets she says her toes are like ice cubes."

There was a pause, brief as a held breath, in which Edmund Underhill made the mistake of his orderly life.

He picked up a warming stone — a smooth grey one, river-worn, unremarkable — and cupped it in both hands. And he let the gift move, just a thread of it, just enough to sink warmth into the stone's slow heart. To anyone watching it was a man picking up a rock. But the rock remembered him now. It would hold heat until dawn, gently, faithfully, like a cat curled at the foot of a bed.

"For your gran," he said, pressing it into Tansy's palm. "Temple-certified non-miraculous. Four coppers."

"I'll give you three," said Tansy, "because you're old."

"Tansy!"

"Two," said Edmund, "and we'll tell nobody about your knees."

They shook on it gravely, hand swallowed in hand, and the mother paid with the frightened gratitude of someone accepting charity from a stranger, and they went off down Cordwainer's Row, and Edmund stood at his stall feeling warm and foolish and pleased with himself.

He would think about that moment often, in the days to come. How good it had felt. How completely he had forgotten, in the space of one child's honest eyes, thirty years of careful invisibility.

* * *

It happened within the hour.

Edmund was packing up early — the sky had gone the color of wet wool, and Herrick's knee had been proven right about the rain — when he saw the boy running toward him up Cordwainer's Row. A temple novice, twelve or thirteen, white surcoat flapping, face white beneath it.

"Master Underhill." The boy caught the edge of the stall, breathing hard. "Master Underhill, you have to close. Now. They're coming."

"Who's coming?"

"The provost's men." The novice glanced over his shoulder, and lowered his voice to a whisper that trembled. "A woman came to the chapter-house. She'd seen something. On Cordwainer's Row. She said a charm *worked*, master — visibly, in front of witnesses, a child holding it up and it glowing—"

"It wasn't glowing," Edmund said, too quickly, too calmly. "Stones don't glow."

"I know." The novice's eyes were enormous. "I know that. You know that. But Master Aldery wrote it down anyway, because the new statute says *reported manifestations must be entered in the register regardless of credibility*, and once it's in the register, master, it goes to the provost, and the provost sends men. There's a reward now. For informants. Did you know that? Ten crowns." He said the figure with the horror of a boy who had recently learned what ten crowns could buy. "Ten crowns, and half the street would sell their own mothers for two."

The rain started then, soft and indifferent, pattering on the canvas awning. Somewhere up the row, a shutter banged. Ordinary sounds. Corvane going about its afternoon. And yet Edmund found he could no longer tell which of the passersby were simply walking somewhere and which were walking *toward* something — which pair of eyes at the window belonged to a neighbor and which to a neighbor-turned-informant with ten crowns in her imagination already.

Six years, he thought. Six years of bland handwriting and properly stamped lies, of selling hope shaped like junk and asking nothing of the gift but that it stay asleep. And undone in an hour by a stone no bigger than an egg, warmed for a grandmother's cold feet by a man who should have known better.

"Where did the woman see it?" he asked. "Exactly."

"Cordwainer's Row, near the pie shop. About noon." The novice hesitated. "She described the seller. She said — master, she said he *smiled at the child*. Like he knew her. Like it mattered."

*Like it mattered.* Edmund closed his eyes briefly. Of course. It wasn't the stone that had betrayed him. Stones were silent; stones left no testimony. What had betrayed him was the look on his own face — the one flicker, in six years of practiced blankness, of the thing he actually was.

"Thank you, lad." He pressed a copper into the boy's hand, then thought better of it and pressed a silver. "Forget my face. Forget this lane. Go home by the long way."

The novice ran. Edmund stood in the rain and packed his tray with hands that were almost steady. Stones, threads, lavender papers, the little sign reading *Certified Curiosities* — all of it into the satchel, all of it ordinary, none of it evidence. The evidence was under his skin and always had been.

He looked up the row once more. At the corner, outside the pie shop, a man in a grey oilcloth coat stood beneath the eaves, sheltering from the rain, doing nothing at all except looking at the street with the patient, professional attention of a man being paid to look. As Edmund watched, the man turned his head — slowly, deliberately — and met his eyes across forty yards of wet cobblestones.

Then the man smiled, touched two fingers to the brim of an imaginary hat, and strolled away up Cordwainer's Row. Toward the chapter-house. Toward the register.

Edmund pulled his hood up and walked the other way, unhurried, a tired scribe heading home through the drizzle. Behind his ribs the swallow stirred again, restless, and for the first time in six years Edmund Underhill understood that the quiet life was over — and that somewhere ahead of him, though he could not yet have said where, a woman in grey was already waiting.
