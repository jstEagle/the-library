## 2. A Visitor in Grey

Edmund Underhill had always believed that if the crown's men ever came for him, they would come loudly — boots on stairs, a battering ram at his shutters, a herald reading his crimes to the whole of Corvane Street. He had rehearsed the scene a dozen times while lying awake, and in every version he behaved admirably: calm, dignified, perhaps even faintly witty.

So when he opened his door that evening and found only one woman standing there — grey-cloaked, rain-beaded, unremarkable as a Tuesday — his rehearsed dignity evaporated entirely.

"Master Underhill," she said. "I'd like a word about your inventory."

"My inventory," Edmund repeated. "I'm a scribe. My inventory is ink."

"That's going to be a problem for you," she said, "because I've been standing across the street since dawn, and I watched you sell a luck-charm to a boy who then won three games of dice in a row, kissed a girl, and didn't get soaked when it rained on everything else in the square."

Edmund considered several responses. *The boy is naturally lucky* was dishonest. *Charm-craft is not technically magic* was legally interesting but morally thin. What came out instead was: "He tipped me well, though."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. The ghost of one, perhaps, come back to check whether anyone still lived there.

She stepped past him without being invited, which told him two things at once: she was an officer of some kind, and she had been raised in houses where doors were things other people closed behind you. She pulled back her hood. Her hair was cropped short and iron-grey though she could not have been older than five-and-thirty, and her hands — he noticed them immediately, the way one notices a scarred face or a missing finger — were wrapped in fingerless gloves, old ones, darned at the knuckles.

"Beatrix Vane," she said. "Formerly Inquisitor Second Class, Office of Anomalies." She let the word *formerly* sit in the room like a dropped stone.

"The disgraced one," Edmund said, before his brain could stop his mouth. "The one from the pamphlets."

"You've read my pamphlets."

"I read everything. It's a professional affliction." He gestured vaguely at the shelves lining his narrow rooms: ledgers, folios, commentaries, three copies of the same legal codex because no one ever bought it. "For what it's worth, I thought the charges were absurd."

"They were accurate," she said. "That's what made them useful."

Edmund's stomach performed a slow, deliberate turn. He busied himself with the kettle, because a man with a kettle cannot be arrested mid-sentence; this was folk wisdom, possibly invented by him just now.

"Inquisitor Vane—"

"Beatrix."

"—Beatrix. If you're here to arrest me, I'd ask that you wait until after tea. If you're here for something else, I'd ask the same, but with more hope."

She sat down on the one chair that wasn't buried under paper, and looked around his rooms with an expression he couldn't parse — something between pity and reconnaissance. "You sell charms out of a stall behind the chandler's. Warding knots. Sleep-salves. A little bottle of 'brave water' that I suspect is wine."

"It's mostly wine," Edmund admitted. "Courage is ninety percent hydration."

"And none of it's outlawed magic, strictly speaking, because none of it works without the buyer's own belief doing half the labor. Except today." She leaned forward. "Today something worked. In public. With witnesses. One of whom reports directly to Captain Renn's new office."

The name landed like cold water down his collar. Ilsa Renn. He hadn't heard it spoken aloud in years, but he felt it in his teeth anyway — the girl who used to steal plums off the temple cart with him, now commander of the crown's hunt for exactly the sort of man he'd become.

"So," Beatrix said. "Here is what happens next. Tomorrow morning, an anonymous informant's report crosses Renn's desk. By noon you're in irons. By winter you're a name on the pyre lists, because the crown needs examples and scribes make excellent examples — everyone knows your face, no one will miss your trade." She paused. "Or."

"Or," Edmund said carefully.

"Or you help me reach the Veil, and the report somehow never gets written."

* * *

The kettle screamed. Edmund took it off the heat mostly so he'd have something to do with his hands, and made tea with the exaggerated care of a man buying himself time one spoonful at a time.

"The Veil," he said finally. "Nobody says that word anymore except priests, and they say it like it's a stain."

"Because they're embarrassed by it," Beatrix said. "It used to be the proudest thing the temple owned. The boundary between our world and the dragons'. Kept bright by covenant-fire for nine hundred years. Then, forty years ago, it went dark, and the temple told us the dragons broke faith, and the crown told us magic was treason, and everyone agreed very quickly because agreeing was safer." She unwound one glove.

Edmund had seen burned hands before. Corvane was full of tallow-vats and bad stoves. But he had never seen anything like hers: the scarring wasn't ragged, it was *patterned* — fine lines tracing over her palms and up her fingers like the veins of a leaf, like script in a language he almost recognized.

"What happened to your hands?" he asked, and immediately wished he'd asked anything else.

She turned them over in the lamplight, considering. "I was a fire-keeper," she said. "Before the Office of Anomalies recruited me. Before I helped them hunt the last few people who remembered what fire actually was." A muscle moved in her jaw. "This is what's left of the order's mark. They tried to burn it out of me when I was disgraced. They failed, because you can't burn out what lives in the burn."

"That's a horrible sentence."

"Yes. I've been working on it for three years."

"No, I mean—" He set down the teapot. "What do you mean, what *lives* in the burn?"

Beatrix Vane looked at him for a long moment, the way a person looks before stepping off a ledge. Then she drew one bare hand slowly through the air above the table.

Fire followed it.

Not flame — not the greedy orange leap of a hearth or a torch. This was something older and quieter: a ribbon of light the color of dawn on copper, hanging in the air where her fingers had been, curling gently, casting shadows that moved *wrong*, shadows that seemed to bow. The room grew warmer. The tea in Edmund's cup steamed without being asked. And somewhere in the walls, in the floor, in the old bones of Corvane itself, Edmund felt something stir and attend — the way a sleeping house attends when someone whispers its name.

His mouth had gone completely dry. "That's—"

"Covenant-fire," she said. "Dragon-fire. The last living spark of it in the world, as far as I know, and I have looked." She closed her fist and the light sank into her palm and was gone, leaving only the patterned scars glowing faintly, fading like embers. "They harvested it, Edmund. For a century, quietly, while the Veil starved. And when the harvest was nearly done, they burned the keepers and called it cleansing, and I ran, and I was good enough at running and bad enough at caring that they made me an inquisitor instead of ash." Her voice stayed level the entire time, which was somehow worse than weeping. "Then I found out what my hands were still carrying, and I stopped being useful to them, and here we are."

"Here we are," Edmund echoed faintly. He sat down across from her. His legs insisted on it.

"You're wondering why you."

"The thought had crossed my mind, along with several others, most of them screams."

"You spent six years in the temple archives. You transcribed the old covenants — the pre-harvest charters, the ones they locked away after the Veil went dark. You have the finest memory for dead languages of any scribe south of the Spine, and more importantly—" she pointed at him with a scarred finger, "—you are nobody. No one watches a charm-seller. That is a rare and precious thing right now, and I intend to exploit it shamelessly."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then tomorrow I write my report, because I am many things but I am not a liar about paperwork, and you burn, and I go to the Veil alone and probably fail." She said it without cruelty, which he appreciated and resented in equal measure. "I'm not blackmailing you, Master Underhill. I'm offering you the truth early, which is more than anyone offered me."

"That's *absolutely* blackmail."

"It's blackmail with excellent manners."

He stared into his tea. The trouble — the genuine, infuriating trouble — was that she was right about all of it, including the part he least wanted examined: that some traitorous corner of him had been waiting years for a reason to run. Six years of transcribing beautiful, forbidden things and sealing them away. Six years of selling brave water to frightened people because real courage was illegal. He had built a small, careful life inside a cage and called the bars *prudence*.

"If I come with you," he said slowly, "what exactly do you need from me?"

"The charters. From memory, if the originals are sealed — and they'll be sealed. The old covenant has terms, keeper-rights, obligations on both sides. If the fire can be returned to the Veil, someone must speak those terms properly, in the old tongue, witnessed. Otherwise it's just arson with better lighting." She flexed her burned fingers. "And I need someone who can tell me, honestly, whether the temple's story holds together. Because if it doesn't—if the dragons never broke faith at all—"

"Then everything the crown has burned for forty years was theater," Edmund finished quietly.

"Now you see why they call it treason to say so."

* * *

He walked her to the door an hour later, their bargain sketched in whispers and sealed with nothing but mutual, wary need — each of them holding a knife to the other's secret, and both pretending this was friendship. At the threshold she paused.

"One more thing," she said. "Captain Renn. When the report doesn't arrive, she'll come looking herself. She's thorough, and she knows this quarter, and—" a beat of something almost gentle crossed her face, "—she knew you, didn't she? Before."

Edmund looked out at the rain-slicked street, at the chandler's shuttered window, at the ordinary dark of a city that would happily burn him by spring. "We grew up together," he said. "On the temple hill. She used to do my arithmetic."

"Then I'm sorry," Beatrix said, pulling up her hood, "for what tomorrow makes her to you."

She vanished into the grey like smoke remembering it had once been a fire, and Edmund stood in his doorway for a long time.

Then he went inside, took down his satchel, and began — with the steady, terrified calm of a man rehearsing admirable behavior at last — to pack.
