## 3. What the Temple Keeps

The Scriptorium of Saint Aldric kept its hours the way a miser keeps coins: counted twice, hoarded jealously, and never spent on anything so frivolous as warmth. Edmund had copied ledgers in its cold for eleven years, and he knew every draft in the building the way a sailor knows the moods of a sea.

He came back at dusk, when the brothers were at evening prayer and the corridors belonged to the dust motes and him.

His plan was simple, which was fortunate, because simple plans were the only kind he'd ever executed successfully. Go to the records office. Find his file—the one with his name on it, *Edmund Underhill, licensed scribe, third class*, and beneath that, in Brother Cassian's careful hand, the incident report from the market square. The child's charm glowing like a firefly in a jar. The informant's statement. The word *investigation* underlined twice.

Burn it. Go home. Sleep badly, but sleep.

He had even brought the right tools: a stub of candle, a flint, and—because he was an honest man down to his cowardly bones—a small clay pot labeled, in his own tidy script, *Ashes of Devotional Incense (Genuine)*, into which the evidence could be decanted should anyone ask questions later. He was aware this made him both a criminal and a liar about incense. He had made peace with it somewhere around the second stair.

* * *

The records office smelled of vellum and mouse. Edmund let himself in with the key he was technically authorized to carry—he had decided, on the walk over, that this made everything legally ambiguous rather than illegal, which was the traditional theological position of scribes everywhere—and went straight to the U drawer.

His file was there. Thinner than he'd feared. A license, two commendations for neat penmanship (he allowed himself a small, doomed flicker of pride), and the report. He read it once more by feel, mostly, because his hands were shaking too much to hold the candle steady.

*Subject displayed unlicensed thaumaturgic output in public thoroughfare. Informant reliable. Recommend escalation to the Office of the Hunt.*

There it was. His whole life reduced to a sentence someone else had written about him.

He fed the pages to the candle flame over the clay pot, watching the ink curl and go brown, then black, then nothing. It felt less like destroying evidence than like erasing himself, which, he supposed, was the point. The temple had written him; now he was unwriting himself. There was probably a sermon in that. There was probably a heresy in that too, knowing the temple.

He was tamping down the last embers with the end of a quill when he noticed the humming.

It started low, in the floor, under the soles of his shoes. A vibration, steady as a hive of very large, very patient bees. He stood still. The mice did not hum. The heating pipes, such as they were, clanked and groaned but did not *hum*. This was something else—something with a rhythm to it, almost like breathing, if breath could be stored in barrels.

Edmund told himself firmly that curious scribes died curious deaths, and then, being Edmund, followed the sound anyway.

Down the corridor, past the binding room, past the closet where Brother Petch kept his scandalous collection of illustrated herbals, there was a door he had walked past a thousand times without seeing. Not because it was hidden. Because it was *boring*—plain oak, plain iron hinges, a plain plaque reading simply VAULTS, SUB-LEVEL. Nobody looked twice at boring doors. Boring doors were how you hid things in a temple; everyone knew the fancy locked doors were just where they kept the good wine.

The door was unlocked.

That, more than anything, unsettled him. Every supply closet in Saint Aldric's had a lock and a key and a brother whose entire personality was guarding it. But the vaults—the vaults that hummed—stood open, as if whatever was inside no longer needed protecting. As if it were not treasure but *infrastructure* now, as ordinary to whoever tended it as the well or the bellows.

He lit his candle, said a short prayer to a saint who was almost certainly embarrassed to be invoked under these circumstances, and went down the stairs.

* * *

The sub-level was not what he expected, which was disappointing, because he had expected something dramatic: chains, skeletons, forbidden books chained to forbidden lecterns. Instead there were rows of iron casks, each taller than a man, each banded with copper and etched with sigils he recognized from his own coursework—containment script, the kind used for holding volatile essences. And set into each cask, a small round window of thick glass, like the porthole of a ship.

Through the windows glowed a light he knew.

Not candlelight. Not lamplight. Dragon-fire—or what remained of it after it had been drawn off, filtered, tamed, and stuffed into a barrel like pickled herring. It burned low and gold and *hungry*, pressing against the glass the way a moth presses against a window, and Edmund felt it press against something inside him too, some answering thing he had spent eleven years pretending wasn't there.

He put his palm flat against the nearest cask. Warm. Warm as a sleeping animal.

"This is where it goes," he said aloud, to nobody, in the voice people use when they've found the thing at the bottom of the pond and wish they hadn't.

Because he understood it now, all at once, the way you understand a joke a beat too late. The outlawry. The hunt. Eleven years of men like him hiding charms in their boot-heels while the temple preached that magic was corruption, that dragon-fire was a plague the covenant had mercifully ended. And underneath the temple, in the dark, in barrels: the fire itself. Harvested. Hoarded. Fed downward through copper conduits in the floor—down, he realized, tracing the pipes with his eyes, toward the river, toward the crown's new engines, the great pumping works and forge-lines that had made Corvane rich and smoky and obedient.

The hunt wasn't hunting magic. The hunt was *harvesting suppliers*. Every charm confiscated, every hedge-witch dragged in, every "unlicensed thaumaturgic output" reported by a neighbor—that was yield. That was stock going into the cellar.

And he had been selling small wonders out of a back-alley stall like a man scooping water from a lake with a teaspoon, never once wondering why the crown wanted the lake empty.

He sat down on the cold stone, right there among the humming barrels, because his legs had opinions of their own.

* * *

"You always did sit down at the worst possible moments."

The voice came from the dark at the far end of the vault, dry as old paper, familiar as his own heartbeat. Archivist Loewen stepped into the light with a lantern raised, and Edmund's stomach performed a maneuver usually reserved for falling off roofs.

Loewen had been his teacher for eleven years. He was a narrow man, all elbows and eyebrows, with the particular patience of someone who has read ten thousand bad student essays and survived. He had taught Edmund containment script, archival law, and the fine art of looking busy when the Abbot walked past. He was, in every way that mattered, the closest thing Edmund had to family who wasn't dead or fictional.

"Master Loewen," Edmund managed, standing up too fast and knocking his elbow on a barrel of stolen divinity. "I was—I was just—"

"Burning your file." Loewen's voice didn't rise. It never rose. "In my vaults. Which you entered through a door I left unlocked specifically because I wanted to see who would walk through it."

"That seems like a trap," Edmund said weakly.

"It is a trap. You're in it." Loewen set the lantern down on a workbench between two casks, and the light caught his face, and Edmund saw something he had never seen on his teacher before: tiredness. Real tiredness, the kind that lives behind the eyes and doesn't leave. "Sit down again, Underhill. Properly this time. On the stool, not the floor. You'll catch your death, and then I'll have to explain to the infirmarian why you froze to death in a heated room, and he asks *questions*."

Edmund sat on the stool. It was, he noted, positioned facing the barrels, which meant Loewen had been sitting here recently, watching them, thinking about them. Possibly for years.

"How long?" Edmund asked. "How long have they been—down here?"

"Before you were born. Before I was young, which is a longer time than either of us enjoys contemplating." Loewen folded his hands. "The covenant ended, officially, ninety-one years ago. The dragons withdrew beyond the Veil. The temple declared the age of fire closed, praised the saints, and began quietly collecting what was left. Confiscations. Donations. 'Purifications.' Every hedge-charm melted down, every fire-bearer registered and then *relieved of their burden*, always for their own health, always with paperwork." He tapped the barrel with one knuckle, and it rang like a bell. "This is a century of small kindnesses, Underhill. Bottled."

"And the crown's engines—"

"Run on it. Every loom, every pump, every forge in the industrial quarter. The city's prosperity is a lie with a copper pipe through the middle of it. And the hunt—" Loewen sighed, a long, defeated sound, "—the hunt is my department's finest administrative achievement. We built the forms. We trained the informants. We wrote the sermons about the dangers of unlicensed wonder, and we wrote them *well*, because we are very good writers, you and I." He looked at Edmund with something terrible and gentle in his face. "You wanted to know what the temple keeps. Now you know. Now you can never unknow it, and I need you to understand what that means before you decide what kind of man you're going to be about it."

"I'm going to tell people," Edmund said. His voice surprised him by coming out steady. "Someone has to—"

"Tell whom?" Loewen asked mildly. "The magistrates? The magistrates drink from the same aquifer. The guilds? Their fortunes are literally piped in from this room. The people? Half of them inform on their neighbors for bread rations, and the other half would burn you as a witch for saying it, because we taught them to." He leaned forward. "Here is what I offer instead, and I offer it once, so listen. Tomorrow morning, the investigation into your little market incident lands on my desk. I will lose it. Misfile it. Send it to the sub-cellar with the flood-damaged records, where documents go to be forgotten. By winter you'll have a posting in the coastal scriptorium—quiet, salt air, excellent fish. No hunt. No questions. A life."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange, you never come down here again. You never speak of this room. You take the posting, you copy your tide-tables, you grow old, and you let the men whose job it is to carry this weight carry it." Loewen's voice softened, which was worse than anger. "I am trying to save you, boy. I have watched better men than you try to fight this and end up in these barrels. That is not a figure of speech. Check the manifests sometime. Names, dates, causes of death: 'fever.'"

Edmund looked at the barrels. All those confiscated charms, all those frightened hedge-witches, all that light pressed against glass, wanting out. Somewhere in one of them, maybe, was the glow from a child's paper bird—the one that had started all this, the one that had flown in circles over the market square while its owner shrieked with delight and Edmund's whole world tilted sideways.

"You left the door unlocked," Edmund said quietly. "You said you wanted to see who'd walk through."

"I did."

"Why? If you only ever intended to offer me silence?"

"Because I'm old," Loewen said, "and tired, and I have been carrying this alone for thirty years, and some small, shameful part of me hoped—" He stopped. Recomposed himself, the way he recomposed everything, into neat archival order. "Doesn't matter. Hope is not an archiving strategy."

But Edmund had heard it. The hope. Thirty years of silence, and underneath it, faint as the hum in the floor, a man who wanted, just once, to be contradicted.

Edmund thought about the coastal posting. Salt air. Excellent fish. Safety, bought with silence, sold by the barrel.

Then he thought about Beatrix Vane—the disgraced inquisitor who had arrested him three days ago and then, inexplicably, let him go, with burns like old candle wax across her hands and a request that made no sense yet: *help me reach the Veil.* He hadn't understood her then. Standing in the humming dark, he was beginning to.

"I can't take the posting," Edmund said.

Loewen exhaled slowly, and for a moment Edmund thought the old man might actually smile. "No," he said. "I suppose you can't. You never could resist a locked room." He picked up his lantern. "Then hear the rest of it, Underhill, because there is a rest of it, and it concerns a woman with burned hands who has been asking after the Veil road charts in my archive. The hunt knows she's in the city. Captain Renn's riders reach the south gate by dawn. Whatever you two intend—you should intend it faster."

The barrels hummed around them, patient as centuries, as Edmund got to his feet and realized, with a lurch, that the night had stopped being about burning a file at all.
