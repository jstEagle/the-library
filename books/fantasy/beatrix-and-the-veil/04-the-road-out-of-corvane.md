## 4. The Road Out of Corvane

The salt marshes began where Corvane's cobbles gave up pretending to be civilization, and Beatrix Vane had never loved a landscape more.

Mud to the ankle, reeds to the shoulder, and a sky the color of dirty pewter pressing down on all of it. No roads. No lanterns. No witnesses. She moved through the grey water with the ease of long habit—twenty years of inquisitorial work taught you how to walk without being heard, even when your boots were two sizes too large and borrowed from a scribe who owned nothing else worth stealing.

Behind her, Edmund Underhill was making enough noise for three men and a cart horse.

"You're walking on the *reeds*," she hissed, not turning around. "Stop walking on the reeds."

"They crunch."

"Everything crunches out here. That's the point. You want to sound like everything else." She paused, listening to him flounder closer, and felt the old familiar irritation rise—the one she'd carried for every temple man she'd ever worked beside. They learned Latin and lock-craft and the proper way to fold a warrant, and none of them learned how to be quiet in a world that wanted them dead. "Step where I step. That's all. Can you manage that?"

"I managed a locked archive last night."

"You picked a lock in a warm room. This is different." She started forward again. "This is everything trying to hear you."

He didn't answer, but the next half mile went easier. She could hear him concentrating—the small, careful sounds of a man placing each foot like a signature on a document he wasn't sure was legal. It would do. For now.

Dawn came up behind them like a slow accusation, gilding the tops of the reeds while the water stayed dark. Somewhere east, muffled by distance and marsh fog, a bell began to ring. Not the hour bell. The hunt bell—three strokes, pause, three strokes, the rhythm every child in Corvane learned before they learned their letters.

*They're moving,* she thought. *Renn's riders are at the gates.*

She glanced back at Edmund. He stood hip-deep in a channel, grey light catching the ink stains on his fingers, watching the city smoke on the horizon with an expression she couldn't read. Grief, maybe. Or relief. With scribes it was always hard to tell which loss they were mourning—the truth or the paperwork.

"Keep moving," she said.

"I'm moving."

"You're standing in water having feelings. There's a difference."

Something that might have been a laugh escaped him, quickly strangled. Good. A man who could still find things funny with the hunt bell ringing behind him was a man who might survive the week.

* *

They made the old drover's causeway by midmorning—a raised spine of packed earth running through the marsh like a scar, built centuries ago for cattle that no one had driven since the covenant broke. Beatrix took them along it because it was faster, and hated herself for it, because fast meant visible, and visible was the one thing she could not afford.

Because the fire was waking up.

It did this sometimes. In cold, in fear, in the space between sleeping and waking—it stirred against the backs of her hands like a cat demanding attention, and her scars would flush from white to pink to a faint, terrible gold, as if her skin were parchment held too near a candle. Twenty years she'd kept it fed on discipline and distance. But exhaustion loosened things. And she had not slept since the night she'd let a charm-seller walk free and ruined her own life in the process.

"Rest here," she said, when the causeway crossed a wide pool fringed with rushes. "Five minutes. Then we move again."

Edmund sat down gratefully, pulled off his oversized boots, and began pouring brown water out of them with the resigned air of a man who had accepted his circumstances. Beatrix stood watch, arms folded, hands tucked under her elbows where no one—not even herself—had to look at them.

"So," Edmund said, after a while. "Are we going to talk about it, or are we going to keep doing this thing where you give orders and I pretend I don't notice you don't trust me?"

"We're going to rest."

"We've been resting for ten seconds and you've already insulted my boots twice."

"Your boots deserve it." But she turned, because there was no putting it off any longer, and a part of her—the inquisitor part, the part that had survived purges and tribunals and the slow rot of her own order—wanted to see his face when she said it. "You want to know what I think, Underhill? I think the temple doesn't lose scribes. It lends them. I think Loewen caught you in his vault and let you walk out, and now you're walking exactly where he wants you—straight to me, straight to the Veil road, with those clever ink-stained hands taking notes the whole way."

His boot stopped mid-pour.

"And when we reach the Veil," she went on, quietly, "there will be someone waiting. Or a message. Or a knife. Because that is what the temple does. It does not forgive, and it does not forget, and it has never once in four hundred years let a debt go unpaid." She lifted her chin. "So tell me why I'm wrong. And be careful, because I have spent twenty years listening to men lie about the temple, and I can hear the seams."

For a long moment Edmund just looked at her. Then, instead of answering, he did something she hadn't expected: he set down his boot, reached into his coat, and drew out a charred scrap of paper—the file he'd burned in Loewen's vault, or what remained of it—and held it out flat on his palm.

"This is what's left of my career," he said. "I burned it myself. Loewen offered me everything—protection, silence, my old life back—if I walked away and forgot what was in those barrels. I said no." His hand didn't waver. "I'm not saying you should trust me. Trust takes years and I've known you three days. But I'm telling you the temple already owns everything I had, and I chose the locked room anyway. That's the only credential I've got left."

Beatrix studied the scrap of ash. Studied his face above it. An honest face, mostly. Tired. Frightened, though he was doing his best to hide it behind that dry little humor of his.

Every spy she'd ever caught had been frightened too.

"The fire," she said finally, and unwound her hands from her sleeves.

She let him see them. The scars ran from wrist to fingertip, old burns mapped over older burns, and beneath them, deep down, something glowed—a coal-light, banked but alive, shifting when she flexed her fingers like a fish under ice. Edmund went very still. She watched him fight the reflexive awe every temple-raised child was trained into, and win, mostly.

"This is what they want," she said. "Not me. Me, they'd just hang. *This*, they'll take apart slowly, in a cellar, for a hundred more years." She closed her fists. "I've carried it since I was nineteen. Every keeper of my order died passing it forward, and now I'm the last, and I am tired, Underhill, in a way sleep doesn't fix. So understand what you're asking to be trusted with. If you betray me, you're not betraying a fugitive. You're betraying the last fire in the world."

The reeds whispered. Far off, the hunt bell had gone silent, which was worse than its ringing.

"I believe you," Edmund said simply. "And for what it's worth—I'm sorry. About your hands. About all of it."

It was such a small thing. Such an ordinary, human thing. No one had said it to her in twenty years. Beatrix looked away first, out over the grey water, and found to her annoyance that her eyes were stinging, and told herself firmly it was the wind off the marsh.

"Eat something," she said gruffly. "We move in five minutes."

* *

They moved for the rest of the day, and the fire betrayed her at dusk.

It happened without warning. One moment she was wading a channel ahead of him; the next, pain lanced up both arms—white-hot, familiar, *hungry*—and the glow broke through her skin like sunrise through shutters. Gold light spilled across the black water, lit the reeds, painted her own terrified face, and somewhere behind them a dog began to bark.

"*Down*," she gasped, and plunged her hands into the cold water.

Steam screamed up around her wrists. She knelt in the channel and held them under, teeth clenched, while the fire fought her—flaring, guttering, flaring again—and Edmund dropped into the mud beside her without a word and did the only useful thing available: he pulled handfuls of wet reed-mat and draped them over her shoulders, over her arms, breaking up the light, hiding the glow the way a screen hides a lamp.

"Breathe," he said. "Just breathe. I've got the light. You've got the rest."

The dog barked again, closer now. Voices—two, maybe three, out on the causeway they'd abandoned an hour ago. Lantern-light swung through the fog in long yellow blades.

Beatrix knelt in the freezing water with her hands buried in the mud and thought, with perfect clarity: *this is how it ends, in a ditch, because my own blood won't behave.* And then she thought: *but he stayed.* The scribe. The temple man. He could have run—should have run, the sensible move, the self-preserving move—but he was kneeling in the muck beside her with reeds in his hair, holding a screen of woven grass between her and the world.

The flare died. Slowly, grudgingly, the glow sank back beneath her scars, and the darkness came down around them like a blanket.

The voices passed. The lanterns swung away north, following the causeway, following nothing.

For a long time neither of them moved.

"Well," Edmund whispered at last. "That was nearly catastrophic."

"Yes."

"Good news is, I think the reeds helped. We should get more reeds. Possibly a hat."

Despite everything—despite the ache in her arms and the barking still echoing in her memory—Beatrix felt her mouth twitch. "A hat."

"A big one. Very fashionable. Completely fireproof."

"You're a fool, Underhill."

"Frequently. It's served me well so far." He offered her a hand up out of the channel, and after a moment's hesitation she took it—his grip warm and steady around her scarred fingers, and he didn't flinch, didn't stare, just hauled her upright as if her hands were anyone's hands at all.

They walked on through the darkening marsh, side by side now rather than front and back, and Beatrix found the silence between them had changed shape. Still wary. Still unfinished. But no longer empty.

Near midnight, the land began to rise. The salt flats gave way to gorse and stone, and ahead of them, black against the stars, the foothills climbed toward the pass—the only road west, the only road to the Veil. Beatrix stopped at the edge of the flats and looked up at that dark line of mountains, and the fire stirred gently under her scars, almost eager, and she did not trust it.

"There's a checkpoint at the pass," Edmund said quietly. He had gone very still beside her. "I know it. I grew up in these hills. It's manned by the garrison from Harrowgate, and the garrison from Harrowgate reports to—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "Captain Renn."

Beatrix turned to look at him. In the starlight his face had gone pale and carefully blank, the way faces go when a name costs something.

"Renn," she repeated. "The hunt commander."

"My oldest friend," Edmund said. "Since we were six years old." He looked up at the pass, at the tiny, distant gleam of lanterns along its spine, and exhaled slowly. "Beatrix. There's something you should know about Ilsa Renn before we go up there."

Far above them, on the mountain road, a lantern flared—once, twice, three times. A signal. And the wind carried down the smell of woodsmoke, and something else beneath it, faint and wrong: the sharp, sweet reek of harvested dragon-fire, burning in a soldier's brazier on the high pass.

Beatrix's scars answered it before she could stop them, glowing softly in the dark like two struck matches.

"Oh no," Edmund breathed. "She's already there."
