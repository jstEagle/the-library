## 5. Ashes of the First Oath

The covenant-station at Hollowmere had been a wayhouse once, if the guidebooks Edmund had catalogued in his temple days could be believed. Now it was a wound in the hillside: a stone archway holding up nothing, walls like broken teeth, and a roof that had surrendered to the weather sometime before either of them was born.

"We'll stop here," Beatrix said, in the tone she used for things that were not suggestions.

Edmund looked at the ruins. "It's not even a ruin. It's a rumor of a ruin."

"It's out of the wind." She was already moving toward the archway, her scarred hands tucked into her coat, and Edmund had learned by now that when Beatrix Vane decided something, arguing with her was a form of self-harm. He followed.

Inside what remained of the main hall, the floor was littered with slate and old mortar and, oddly, a great many small ceramic tiles, each no bigger than a coin. Edmund picked one up. It was glazed blue, and stamped into its face was a sigil he knew intimately: the open hand beneath a rising sun. The mark of the covenant-stations. The mark that had been on the paperwork he'd spent six years forging.

"They were everywhere once," Beatrix said quietly. She'd taken up a post against the one wall still standing to shoulder height, where the wind couldn't reach her. "Stations like this. A day's walk apart, all along the borderlands. Fire-keepers staffed them."

"What did they do here? Exactly?" Edmund asked. It was a question he'd been circling for two weeks now, ever since a disgraced inquisitor with dragon-fire in her hands had let him walk free of his own arrest, and he'd been careful — very careful — never to ask it directly. "I mean, I know the official history. The stations were garrisons against draconic incursion. The fire-keepers held the line while the Veil weakened."

Beatrix made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter were capable of disappointment. "Held the line," she repeated. "Yes. That's what we said."

She closed her eyes then, which she did sometimes when the memories got heavy, and Edmund took the opportunity to do what he always did when a conversation threatened to become emotional: he tidied something. He gathered the little blue tiles into a neat stack, arranging them by shade, darkest to lightest.

"You're doing the tiles again," Beatrix said without opening her eyes.

"I'm organizing the tiles."

"You're hiding behind the tiles."

"There's a difference. Hiding implies shame. This is curation."

One eye opened to regard him. It was remarkable, Edmund thought, how much judgment a single grey eye could contain. Then she closed it again, and he returned to his curation, and for a while there was only the wind moving through the broken archway with a low sound, almost like breathing.

* * *

He found the vault by accident, which is how Edmund Underhill found most things worth finding.

He'd gone looking for dry wood among the fallen rafters and instead put his boot through a section of floor that sounded wrong — too hollow, too deliberate. Beneath the rotted plank was a stone slab with an iron ring set flush into it, and around the ring's base, worn nearly smooth but unmistakable, ran a groove in the shape of a hand.

An oath-lock. Temple work, old temple work, from before the seals had been standardized. Edmund had read about them in Loewen's archive and had assumed, like everyone else, that they were ceremonial. You pressed your palm into the groove and swore your purpose aloud, and if the oath was true, the lock opened. If it was false—

Well. No one wrote down what happened if it was false. That was rather the point of oaths.

"Find something?"

He jumped hard enough to crack his elbow on the rafter above him. Beatrix stood at the top of the rubble slope, arms crossed, windblown and unimpressed.

"A cellar," he said. "Possibly. Or a very ambitious hole."

"Show me."

He showed her. She knelt beside the slab, and when she saw the groove her breath went strange — caught somewhere between recognition and grief. She laid one scarred hand over it, not pressing. Just resting. Her fingers trembled.

"Fire-keeper's lock," she said. "We could all open these. Every keeper, every station. It's keyed to the covenant itself, not to any person. Anyone sworn to the old order—" She stopped. Looked at him. "You're not sworn to anything except paperwork fraud."

"Alleged paperwork fraud."

"Edmund."

"Look," he said, "the lock wants an oath. Fine. I've spent my whole life saying true things in ways that make them harmless. Let me try."

And because Beatrix was tired, and cold, and because some part of her — he was beginning to understand — wanted the door opened more than she feared what was behind it, she moved aside.

Edmund knelt and fit his palm into the groove. The stone was warmer than it should have been. Not warm like sun-warmed rock; warm like skin. Like something waiting under the surface with a pulse.

"I swear," he said, feeling slightly ridiculous and trying not to show it, "that I come seeking truth, and mean no harm to what sleeps below."

For three long seconds, nothing happened, and Edmund prepared himself for the humiliation of explaining to Beatrix that the ancient magic had rejected him on grounds of insincerity, or possibly poor posture.

Then the slab sighed open.

Not slid. *Sighed* — a long exhalation of air that smelled of dust and cedar oil and something underneath both, faint and mineral and alive: the smell of the temple's sealed vaults, the humming ones, the ones full of harvested fire. Edmund's stomach dropped through the floor he'd already broken.

Below was a small chamber, dry as a tomb, lined with lead shelves. On the shelves sat books. Dozens of them. Hundreds. Ledgers bound in temple-blue leather, spines cracked with age, each stamped with dates going back further than Edmund had ever seen temple records go back. And on the shelf nearest the opening, alone, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, lay a single document in a case of glass.

"The charter," Beatrix whispered.

* * *

They carried everything up into the last light of the afternoon, because Beatrix refused to read it underground, and Edmund didn't argue. She sat with the charter case in her lap for a long time before she let him break the seal. Her hands hovered over it and withdrew twice.

"You should read it," she finally said. "You're the scribe. Words are yours."

"Words are mine," Edmund agreed. "That's usually how I get into trouble."

The charter was written in Old Liturgical, which Edmund read the way other men breathed. He began aloud, translating as he went, his voice settling into the cadence of formal prose:

*"Let it be known to all generations: that the dragons of the far world and the people of the near world are bound by this covenant, freely given. The dragons grant their fire to kindle the Veil, that both worlds may live. The people grant safe passage, honest keeping, and the truth entire. Neither side shall take what is not given."*

"Yes," Beatrix said softly. "That's it. That's what I was taught. What comes next?"

Edmund kept reading. And next came the amendments.

There were eleven of them, each initialed by temple signatories, each dated across a span of ninety years, and each one, in careful liturgical language, a small theft dressed as bookkeeping. An amendment permitting the *temporary borrowing* of covenant-fire for the crown's winter engines. An amendment establishing *reserve stores*, quantities unspecified. An amendment authorizing collection from *unclaimed sources* — and Edmund felt cold as he realized that *unclaimed* meant the wild fires, the hearth-flames of villages that hadn't signed anything, the small warm magics of ordinary people that the temple had been quietly skimming for a century.

The final amendment was the worst. Dated sixty-one years ago. It dissolved the fire-keepers' authority entirely, transferring custody of all covenant matters to the Office of Records.

To the archivists. To Loewen's office. To Edmund's own desk.

"The dragons didn't withdraw," he heard himself say. His voice sounded far away. "They didn't turn on us. They *stopped giving* — because we'd been taking. For ninety years. And when they noticed—"

"When they noticed," Beatrix said, very quietly, "we called it betrayal. We told the fire-keepers the Veil was failing because the dragons had broken faith." She was staring at the middle distance, at nothing. "My whole order. Every keeper who died holding a line that wasn't a line. My mother trained me for this. Her mother trained her. Three generations of women taught to guard against a crime committed by the men who paid our bread."

Edmund looked at her hands. The scars ran from wrist to fingertip, old burn-mapping, the marks of a keeper who had carried living flame. She'd told him once, on the marsh road, that the fire had found her young, that carrying it hurt every hour of every day, and that she had never once considered putting it down, because someone had to keep faith even if no one else did.

Someone had to keep faith. While her own temple emptied the account.

He could tell her. It was all right there in the ledgers, names and dates and tonnages, a century of theft itemized in beautiful clerical script — his script, essentially, his craft, his inheritance. He could tell her right now, and it would be true, and true things were supposed to be his whole religion.

But her face. He watched her face, and saw a woman standing at the edge of a country she'd defended her whole life, about to learn she'd been defending the wrong border, and Edmund thought of every kindness he'd ever performed by withholding — the forged charms sold as trinkets so no one would be arrested for real magic, the letters he'd softened before delivering, the whole quiet architecture of mercies he'd built his life inside of.

*Don't tell her,* said the part of him that had survived Corvane. *Tell her tomorrow. Tell her when she's stronger. There will be a better moment.*

And then Beatrix turned and looked at him — really looked, the way she did, as though his skull were a locked cabinet and she already knew the combination — and said:

"You've gone pale. What else does it say?"

"Nothing," Edmund said. "It says what I read."

The lie came out smooth. Six years of practice will do that.

Beatrix nodded slowly. She rose, walked to the broken archway, and stood looking out at the darkening hills with her back to him, and Edmund understood, with the sick clarity of a man watching his own trap spring shut, that she knew. That she had perhaps known since he'd gone silent mid-translation. That she was giving him the night to decide whether he would be the man who told her the truth or the man who managed her.

He sat alone among the sorted blue tiles, the ledgers stacked beside him like a verdict, and listened to the wind breathe through the stones.

Somewhere down the valley road, faint but unmistakable in the evening hush, came the sound of hoofbeats. Many horses, riding hard, and at their head a horn call that Edmund knew from childhood — the hunt's signal, the same one Ilsa Renn used to blow to call him home to supper.

They had until dawn, perhaps. Maybe less.

And Beatrix still hadn't turned around.
