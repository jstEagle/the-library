## 6. The Hunter's Kindness

The pass had a name, once. Beatrix could see it carved into the waystone at the trailhead, worn to a ghost of itself: *The Shepherd's Rest*, or maybe *The Shepherd's Wrist*—the stone had crumbled right where the interesting word should be. She decided she liked the ambiguity. A pass that couldn't decide what it was seemed like a fitting place to make a stand.

"Snow by nightfall," Edmund said, squinting up at a sky the color of old pewter. He'd developed the habit of narrating weather as though someone were paying him for it. "We should be through before then."

"You said that about the marshes."

"The marshes were an anomaly."

"The marshes were a *character flaw of geography*, Edmund." She shifted her pack and looked back down the trail they'd climbed all morning—a thin grey ribbon stitched between black pines, empty in both directions. Empty was good. Empty meant Ilsa Renn hadn't found them yet.

Empty also meant they had perhaps half a day left of not being found, and Beatrix had learned to count on Renn the way you count on winter: late, maybe, but inevitable.

They climbed. The pines gave way to bare granite and stubborn tufts of grass that grew sideways out of spite. Somewhere below, a hawk complained about something. Beatrix kept her hands wrapped—she always kept them wrapped now, two lengths of linen wound tight over the scars, over what slept beneath—and when the wind gusted off the high snowfields, she felt the thing under her skin stir like a cat hearing a can opener three rooms away.

*Not now,* she told it. *Please.*

It never listened. That was the trouble with carrying a miracle; miracles had no manners.

* * *

They made camp in the lee of a boulder the size of a chapel, and Edmund built a fire with the anxious competence of a man who had spent his life making things look harmless. Small flame, dry wood, no smoke worth naming. He was good at small wonders. It was the large ones that terrified him.

Beatrix sat with her back to the stone and watched him across the flames. He'd changed since Corvane. Not dramatically—he still fussed, still apologized to inanimate objects, still had the posture of a man perpetually braced for a temple auditor—but there was a new spine in him. The charter at the ruined station had done it. He'd read the truth of his own house and hadn't flinched, or rather he'd flinched enormously and then kept reading anyway, which was braver.

He hadn't yet told her everything he'd read. She'd seen him decide, night after night, not to tell her. She recognized the look. It was the look of a man carrying something heavy and hoping the road would be long enough to set it down gently somewhere.

She let him keep it. For now. Everyone deserved one secret on a road like this.

"Renn," Edmund said suddenly, into the quiet. "You've been avoiding saying her name for two days."

"I have not."

"You've been calling her 'the hunt.' 'The pursuit.' Once you called her 'that woman,' which I felt was unnecessarily sinister."

"She commands soldiers who burn people, Edmund. Sinister is earned."

He poked the fire. "Ilsa isn't—" He stopped, started again, stopped again, like a man testing ice. "Ilsa used to steal apples from Canon Merrick's garden. Badly. She once fell through the trellis and blamed a raccoon. There were no raccoons in Corvane. Everyone knew this. She maintained the raccoon theory for years."

Beatrix felt her mouth do something dangerously close to a smile. "A formidable liar, then."

"The worst kind—the sincere kind." He was quiet a moment. "She'll have orders. Bring us back alive, probably. Loewen wants me alive. You he wants..." He didn't finish it.

"In a jar," Beatrix supplied, cheerfully grim. "I'm aware."

"That doesn't frighten you?"

"Everything frightens me. I've simply gotten better at scheduling around it." She pulled her cloak tighter. "Your friend will come up this pass tomorrow, or the day after, with thirty men and a warrant. And you'll have to choose, Edmund, in front of her, whether you're the scribe who sells charms or whatever it is you're becoming. So sleep. Tomorrow you'll want your wits."

Edmund did not sleep well. Neither did she. Under her bandages, the fire dreamed, and its dreams leaked warmth into her bones until she lay sweating in the freezing dark, listening to the mountain hold its breath.

* * *

They heard the column before they saw it—not drums, nothing so courteous, just the sound of many boots finding the same rhythm on frozen ground, a heartbeat made of men.

Beatrix stood at the mouth of the pass with the morning fog rolling past her knees and counted helmets in the murk. Thirty-one. Thirty-two. A banner too furled to read, but she didn't need it read. Grey cloaks. Temple grey, crown grey, the color of paperwork given swords.

"There," Edmund breathed beside her, and his voice cracked down the middle.

The woman at the head of the column stopped forty paces out. Tall, broad-shouldered, sword worn at her hip like an apology she'd stopped meaning to make. Even at distance, even through fog, Beatrix saw the moment Captain Ilsa Renn recognized Edmund Underhill: her whole face forgot its discipline at once, like a dropped mask.

"Edmund?" she called. "Edmund Underhill?"

"Hello, Ilsa," Edmund said, wretchedly. "About the raccoon—"

"Don't." Her voice went rough. "Don't you dare start with the raccoon." But she was almost smiling, and her soldiers behind her were very confused, because their commander had gone soft as butter in front of two fugitives.

Then her eyes moved to Beatrix, and the butter hardened again. "Beatrix Vane. Inquisitor Third Rank, stripped of rank, wanted for—" she consulted memory, not paper, "—a great many things, most of them invented."

"All the best charges are," Beatrix said.

"Step away from her, Edmund."

"No."

The word landed small and enormous. Beatrix glanced at him. He stood straighter than she'd ever seen him, hands trembling at his sides, chin up, looking like a man reciting a charm he wasn't sure worked.

Isla Renn walked forward alone. Her soldiers stayed put—she'd ordered it with one raised hand without breaking stride, and Beatrix noted the habit of command, the economy of it. Up close, Renn was younger than her reputation and more tired than her years. There were shadows under her eyes that no campaign caused. There was a smell about her, faint, chemical-sweet, that Beatrix knew intimately: harvested fire. Temple-kept, engine-fed, soldier-carried. They were burning it in lanterns now, feeding it to war-engines, letting boys carry vials of it against their hearts.

And it was eating them.

Beatrix saw it in Renn's hands first—the same scarring Beatrix bore, but wrong, uncontrolled, weeping at the edges where the fire had nowhere holy to go. Saw it in the way Renn held her sword arm slightly away from her body, favoring it. Saw it in the flicker behind her eyes when the wind shifted and Beatrix's own wrapped hands pulsed warm in answer, two fires recognizing each other across the fog—one caged and sick, one free and dying.

"You've been carrying it," Beatrix said quietly. "In the vials. Against regulation, I'd guess, since regulation would say hand it to an officer."

Renn's jaw tightened. "It keeps the men warm on the heights."

"It keeps the men *burning*. Slowly. From the inside. You know that. You've watched it happen."

Silence. Fog drifted between them like a slow animal.

"Private Halloway," Renn said at last, flat. "Two months ago. Carried a flask of the stuff for a week. Woke up one morning and his shadow was on fire. Just the shadow. He screamed for six hours and then he was ash, and the report I wrote said *equipment malfunction*, because the alternative report gets me hanged." She looked at her own scarred knuckles. "Equipment malfunction. Eleven times now."

Edmund made a small wounded sound. "Ilsa—"

"Don't." She turned on him, and her eyes were wet and furious. "Don't *Ilsa* me yet. Do you know what my orders are? Bring Vane in alive. Bring *you* in alive, and Loewen promised—Loewen *promised*—a full pardon, restoration, your old desk, your name cleaned. All you have to do is walk away from her." She laughed, short and bitter. "Your name cleaned. As if the temple ever once cared what your name was before it needed it dirty."

"They offered you the same bargain they offered me," Edmund said.

"They offer everyone the same bargain. That's how you know it's theirs and not yours." She stared at him a long moment. "You were at the ruined station. Don't deny it—we found the charter case open, pages missing. What did it say, Edmund? What does the temple keep down in those vaults?"

Edmund looked at Beatrix. Beatrix looked steadily back, giving him nothing, giving him the choice. *This is yours,* her silence said. *Yours to tell, yours to carry.*

He opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it again.

"I can't tell you here," he said finally. "Not with your soldiers watching. But Ilsa—if you come with us, if you see the Veil—I'll show you everything. The vaults. The ledgers. Whose fire it really is, and who broke the covenant, and it wasn't—" His voice failed and rebuilt itself. "It wasn't the dragons."

Something moved across Ilsa Renn's face that Beatrix would spend a long time afterward trying to name. Not belief. Belief would come later, or wouldn't. It was the look of a woman standing on a bridge already cracking beneath her, deciding which bank to jump for.

"I have thirty-two men," Renn said. "Half of them are carrying fire-vials against their hearts because I let them, because it kept them alive on the heights, and I think—I *think* it's killing them slower than the cold would. If I turn my back on my orders, Loewen breaks me. If I report what I suspect, Loewen breaks me quieter." She looked down the pass, back toward Corvane, toward everything that had made her. "You understand that either way, I'm choosing to fall."

"Yes," Beatrix said. "That's what mercy costs. Nobody advertises the price."

Renn studied her. "You love him?"

The question came out of nowhere and hit Beatrix like a thrown apple. "I—what? No. We're colleagues. Colleagues in crime. It's professional."

"She talks in her sleep," Edmund offered helpfully. "Mostly about dragons. Sometimes about me, but in a disapproving register."

"*Traitor,*" Beatrix hissed, and the fire under her bandages flared hot enough that Renn, standing close, stepped back with her scarred hand raised—as if in greeting, or surrender, or recognition.

But Renn only looked at that answering heat in her own ruined knuckles, and something in her settled. Decision, arriving not as thunder but as a slow internal collapse, the last pillar of her loyalty giving way with a sound only she could hear.

"I'm going to report that I found you," she said slowly. "That I engaged, that conditions deteriorated, that the pass closed with snow. I'm going to write *equipment malfunction* one more time, and this time it's going to mean mercy instead of murder, and I don't know which of those is worse." She stepped back from them, boots grinding on frost. "Go. Through the pass, north fork, don't take the south—it avalanches on Tuesdays, don't ask how I know. And Edmund." Her voice caught, steadied. "When you get where you're going. When you learn the whole truth. Send word somehow. Because if my men are dying for a lie, I need to hear it from you, and I need it to be true, or I'll have burned everything I am for nothing."

"Ilsa," Edmund said softly, "it's true. I swear it on the raccoon."

"The raccoon never existed."

"Then I swear it on the trellis I fell through."

She almost smiled. Then she turned, whistled her column to heel, and led them away down the mountain, thirty-two grey ghosts marching back into the fog, leaving the pass open behind them like an unguarded door.

Beatrix watched until the fog took them. Then she looked at Edmund, who was staring at the empty trail with the expression of a man who has just discovered his heart had more rooms than he'd furnished.

"She lied to her own report," he said wonderingly. "For us."

"For the truth," Beatrix corrected. "You're just the courier." She hoisted her pack. "Come on. North fork. And Edmund—whatever you read at that station that you haven't told me yet?"

His face went pale as the snowfields above.

"You'd better tell me before the Veil," she said quietly. "Because I've carried enough secrets for one lifetime, and yours are starting to weigh more than mine."

Above them, far up the pass, the clouds parted for a single breath, and Beatrix saw it—far off, impossibly high, a smudge of gold moving against the grey. Wings. One beat, unhurried, ancient.

Vessarath was waiting.
