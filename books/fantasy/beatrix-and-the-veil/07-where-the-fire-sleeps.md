## 7. Where the Fire Sleeps

Edmund told her on the second day of the high pass, because she was right about the weighing.

They had stopped to eat cold rice and colder sausage beneath an overhang of blue-grey stone, and Beatrix sat with her scarred hands wrapped around a tin cup of snowmelt tea, watching him with the patience of a woman who had once interrogated smugglers for a living and could wait out a mountain.

"The charter," he said finally. "At the station. I read all of it."

"I know."

"You know?"

"Edmund." She blew on her tea. "You've been walking like a man carrying a trunk full of ledgers for three days. Your shoulders have opinions."

So he told her. The siphons in the charter's margins. The tithe of fire, drawn through the old covenant-stations, year over year, century over century, initialed by archivists whose names he'd grown up revering. Loewen's own master. Loewen's master's master. He told her how the dragons hadn't withdrawn from the world—they'd been *drained* from it, blamed for the dimming they were dying of, hunted as punishment for wounds the temple itself had made.

He told her, and then he said the hardest part: "Your order didn't fail, Beatrix. It was *spent*. Like fuel."

She was quiet for a long time. Above them the wind moved through the scree with a sound like turning pages.

"I suspected," she said at last. "The last ten years, I suspected. Knowing is different." She looked into her cup. "My whole order died believing we were guarding a door. We weren't. We were guarding a *tap*, and none of us knew which side of it we stood on." Then she glanced up, and there was something almost like a smile in the ruin of her tiredness. "You told me anyway. Good. I'd have hated to kill you at the top of a mountain. Terrible place to hide a body."

"That's your idea of encouragement?"

"It's mine of affection. Take it or leave it."

He took it. He was beginning to understand that everything Beatrix Vane gave him would come wrapped in threats, and that underneath the wrapping it was warm.

* * *

On the fourth day the trees gave out entirely, and on the fifth, the world did.

Edmund had imagined the Veil his whole life—the way temple children imagine it, as a wall of flame on the horizon, dramatic and distant. What he found instead was a valley that ended in *absence*. The peaks simply stopped, sheared off clean as if a god had run a thumb along them, and beyond the line where stone should have continued there was nothing—not darkness exactly, but a kind of held breath, a shimmer like heat above a forge, except cold. Through it, faintly, impossibly, he could see mountains on the *other* side, and a sky the color of embers, and shapes moving in that sky with the slow grandeur of clouds given opinions.

And the Veil itself—the boundary, the great work, the covenant made visible—it was nearly dark. A threadwork of ember-light crawled across it in slow veins, guttering, the way a fire gutters when no one has fed it. Here a stretch went black entirely, and where it went black, the far side vanished too, as though a page had been torn out of the world.

"It's starving," Beatrix whispered.

Her hands were glowing.

Not brightly—a dull gold seeped up between the old burn scars like light through shutters—but enough that Edmund saw it clearly against the grey afternoon, and understood, with a lurch, why she had kept them gloved and hidden for the entire journey north.

"Beatrix—"

"I know." She flexed her fingers, wincing. "It knows I'm here. It always knows. Like standing next to a starving dog when you're made of meat." She started down toward the valley floor. "Come on. Before it gets ideas."

"What kind of ideas does a hole in the world get?"

"You don't want to know. That's not rhetorical. You genuinely don't want to know."

* * *

The last dragon was smaller than Edmund expected, and this somehow made it worse.

Vessarath lay coiled at the base of the anchor stone—a pillar of black rock the height of a cathedral, shot through with the same dying veins as the Veil behind it—and the dragon was the size of a plow horse, maybe less, gaunt as famine, scales gone the dull grey-brown of old ash. When it lifted its head, the motion had the terrible economy of something that could not afford to waste anything anymore, not even movement.

Its eyes were the only living gold left in the valley.

"*Scribes,*" it said, and its voice was dry wind through a burnt forest, and it tasted the word like something rotten. "Two of you. One stolen, one fallen. Come to count what remains? Come to collect the final tithe?"

"We've come to restore it," Beatrix said.

The dragon laughed. There was very little left in the laugh either.

"Restore." It uncoiled, joint by joint, and rose up until its head was level with theirs, close enough that Edmund could see the hollows beneath its cheekbones, could smell ash and something older, like lightning remembered rather than seen. "Little fire-keeper. Do you know what I eat?"

Beatrix said nothing.

"Memory," Vessarath said. "I am nine hundred years old and I have eaten nothing but memory since the last honest flame crossed this stone. I remember my mother's voice. I remember the taste of the first sky. I have chewed those memories so long there is nothing left of them but the swallowing." Its gaze moved to Edmund, and he felt it pass over him like a searchlight. "And now you bring me a spark. One spark, sewn into a woman's hands by desperate fools who thought hiding it was mercy." The nostrils flared. "One spark is a meal. One spark is *nothing*. The Veil needs a hearth, not a crumb."

"Then we find more fire," Edmund said. "There are vaults in Corvane full of it—"

"There are *corpses* in Corvane full of it." The dragon's tail moved, once, a slow irritated sweep across the stone. "Fire pulled from a living vein and bottled dies. You cannot reforge a covenant with pickled scraps. The Veil must be lit from within—from a keeper bound to it in flesh and oath. As it has always been. As your temple well knew, when it decided keepers were expensive and tithes were cheap."

Edmund's mouth had gone dry. "Bound how?"

Vessarath turned its ancient head and looked at him fully then, and there was something in the look that was almost—almost—pity.

"Flesh," the dragon repeated gently. "*Oath.* Not a signature, scribe. Not paperwork. The Veil takes a keeper the way a hearth takes wood. You would stand at the stone and be burned into it—not dead, not quite alive, but *held*, your years feeding the flame, your name becoming the door. Every keeper before your temple grew clever did this. Why do you think the order was sworn, celibate, small? Why do you think it was called an *order*?" A slow blink. "It was a queue."

The silence after that was very long. Somewhere above, wind worried at the shattered peaks. In Edmund's chest, his heart performed several maneuvers it had never trained for.

Beatrix spoke first, voice low. "How long?"

"For a strong keeper? A lifetime." The dragon settled back onto its haunches, folding its ruined wings. "For a man who eats like our friend here? Less. But long enough to matter. Long enough for the Veil to mend and the far folk to return. Long enough," and here the gold eyes glittered with something dry and ancient that was, unmistakably, humor, "for someone to eventually write it all down properly. I am told you are good at that."

"I'm a *forger*," Edmund said weakly. "I sell charms that make bread rise. I once charged a widow four coppers for a luck-knot I tied while drunk."

"Yes," Vessarath said. "I know. I have eaten the memories this mountain holds of your little journey. You complain in your sleep. At length. About the sausage."

"He does," Beatrix agreed solemnly.

"This is not funny," Edmund said, and his voice cracked in the middle of it, which undermined the point considerably. He looked at the Veil—at the torn places where the world simply stopped, at the ember-veins guttering like a candle in a draft—and then at Beatrix's hands, still faintly shining, still carrying the last living fire of a murdered age.

One spark was a meal. One spark was nothing. Unless—

"If her fire goes into me," he said slowly, "and I go into the stone—"

"Then the hearth is lit," Vessarath said, "and stays lit. And she lives. Diminished, mortal, ordinary—" the dragon pronounced the word like a blessing "—and free. Or she pours herself directly into the Veil alone, and buys perhaps a season, and dies, and the dark comes back, and the crown sends more scribes with more questions." It lowered its great grey head until one gold eye filled Edmund's whole vision, a coin of fire in a face of ash. "That is the arithmetic, Keeper-that-might-be. There is no version where it costs nothing. There never was. Your temple simply stopped telling people the price, and called the silence civilization."

Far below them, faint but unmistakable, carried up the pass on the thin cold air: the sound of many boots. Of harness. Of horns.

Beatrix went very still. Edmund knew that stillness; he'd learned it in five hundred miles beside her. It meant she was counting.

"How long has the Veil got," he asked quietly, "if we do nothing tonight?"

Vessarath closed its eye.

"A day," it said. "Perhaps two."

Down in the dark of the pass, the horns sounded again—closer now—and Edmund Underhill, counterfeiter, coward, keeper of nothing, felt the weight of every choice he had ever avoided come down the mountain after him like weather.
