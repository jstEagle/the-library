## 8. Loewen's Bargain

Beatrix had learned, over a long and mostly ruinous career, that the temple never sent its worst first. It sent its kindest. The soft voice, the reasonable terms, the offer shaped exactly like mercy—that was how you knew the knife was already out of its sheath.

So when the horns sounded at dawn from the eastern ridge of the pass below the Veil, and she saw not soldiers but a single palanquin carried up the switchback trail by eight grey-robed men, she felt something worse than fear. She felt recognition.

Archivist Loewen had come himself.

She watched him from the ledge where Vessarath's anchor stone stood—a cracked pillar of black basalt, warm to the touch even now, even with the Veil guttering above it like a lamp running dry. Edmund stood beside her, arms crossed against the mountain wind, his face doing that thing it did when he was trying very hard to look calm and mostly achieving *resigned*. He'd been practicing the look for three days, ever since Ilsa Renn let them through the pass with a warning that Loewen would follow before week's end.

Week's end had turned out to be generous. It was Tuesday.

"He brought chairs," Edmund said, squinting down the trail. "He actually brought chairs."

"I told you he would."

"You said he'd bring soldiers."

"Soldiers are for people you intend to kill." Beatrix pressed her scarred palms flat against the cold stone, feeling the faint pulse beneath—the last ember of everything the temple had stolen, everything she had carried across half a kingdom in the hollows of her hands. "Chairs are for people you intend to *buy*."

The palanquin crested the final rise an hour past midmorning, and there he was: Archivist Loewen, First Keeper of the Corvane Temple, wrapped in dove-grey wool despite the season, stepping down onto the scree with the careful dignity of a man who had not walked uphill without assistance since the reign of the old king. Behind him, at a respectful distance, came forty soldiers in temple livery—because Beatrix had been right about that too—and two scribes carrying a folding table, a ledger bound in vellum, and an inkwell that looked suspiciously like it was made of dragonbone.

Edmund recognized the inkwell. He'd catalogued three like it in the sealed vaults, back when he still believed the vaults held records instead of engines.

Loewen arranged himself before the anchor stone as though it were an altar, which, Beatrix supposed bitterly, it had once been. He surveyed the darkening Veil overhead—the tear in the sky where the dragons' world bled through, its edges flickering like torn gauze—and he did not flinch, which told her everything. A man who understood what he was looking at would have wept. A man who had spent thirty years harvesting it simply checked its color against the ledger in his memory and found it acceptable.

"Edmund Underhill," Loewen said, and his voice carried the way voices do when they have been trained in lecture halls for four decades—warm, resonant, engineered to make obedience feel like affection. "My boy. My finest student. Do come closer; the wind up here is no friend to old bones, and I would speak plainly."

Edmund did not come closer. He stayed where he was, and Beatrix watched his jaw set in that particular way that meant he had already decided something and was now merely enduring the interval before everyone else found out.

*Good*, she thought. *He's faster than I was.*

"I confess," Loewen continued, undeterred by the geography of the conversation, "that I grieved when word reached me of your flight. Grieved genuinely. You were the best of us—the neatest hand, the sharpest mind, the most faithful heart. And then this business with the charms, the arrest, the... unfortunate alliance." His gaze slid to Beatrix with the smooth courtesy of a man acknowledging a stain on the carpet. "I understand how it happened. Confusion. Manipulation. A woman with a tragic story and a talent for extracting sympathy—I remember her file, Edmund, I wrote half of it myself. The fire-keeper who burned too bright and blamed her order for the ashes."

"That is roughly what happened," Beatrix said pleasantly, "except for every part after 'fire-keeper.'"

Loewen inclined his head toward her as though her interruption were beneath his dignity, and moved on. This, too, she recognized. Inquisitors were trained to endure heckling. Archivists were trained to pretend it hadn't occurred.

"But I am not here for recriminations," he said. "I am here because the temple forgives, Edmund. That is what the temple *does*. Better than any institution in the history of the realm, we forgive. And I have brought the terms of your forgiveness with me, written fair, witnessed twice, sealed with the Chancellor's own ring."

He gestured to the table. The scribes unfolded it with practiced efficiency. The ledger opened. The vellum caught the light.

Beatrix read the terms from twenty feet away, because she had spent eleven years reading documents she wasn't meant to see, and the habit had not left her. Full pardon for the sale of outlawed charms. Restoration of his archive rank, seniority intact, back-pay calculated to the day of his flight. A stipend. A residence in the temple quarter. And, in the clause written smallest, in a hand so fine it was nearly invisible: *the bearer further undertakes to deliver into temple custody the fugitive Beatrix Vane, together with all fire currently contained upon her person or within her keeping.*

There it was. The mercy, priced.

"They want my hands," she said quietly, to Edmund, not to Loewen. "They've wanted them since the night they took the rest of me."

Edmund didn't answer her directly. He looked at the document, and then at the man behind it, and Beatrix watched him do the arithmetic that she had done years ago in a different office, under different lights, with a different offer on the table. Hers had been smaller. Forgiveness for silence. She had taken it, and it had cost her everything she'd sworn to protect.

"The fire," Edmund said slowly, testing the words like a man walking on ice. "In your accounting, Archivist. Whose fire is it?"

Loewen smiled. It was a good smile. Well-maintained. "The temple's, naturally. All fire is the temple's. The covenant named us stewards, and stewardship implies ownership—we have merely been more *efficient* stewards than our predecessors. The dragons hoarded theirs. We put ours to work. Is that not what stewardship means, in the end? Use?"

"The charter says otherwise."

"The charter," Loewen said, with the gentle patience of a man correcting a beloved student's error, "is a historical document. It reflects the needs of a historical moment. Institutions grow, Edmund. Obligations evolve. You of all people should understand that a text must be read in the spirit of its age, not chained to its letter." He spread his hands. "But I did not climb a mountain to debate hermeneutics. I climbed it to bring you home."

And there it was—the whole architecture of the thing, laid bare in a single sentence. Not justice. Not truth. *Home.* The word that had been used on Edmund Underhill his entire life, gently, relentlessly, until the word and the walls around it had become indistinguishable. Beatrix had heard it aimed at a hundred witnesses, a hundred suspects, a hundred frightened clerks. It was the temple's truest instrument. Not the engines. Not the hunt. The word *home*.

"Tell him," she murmured. "Or don't. But know that whatever you choose, I'll stand where I'm standing now."

Edmund stepped forward. One step. Toward the table, toward the pardon, toward the man who had taught him his letters and his silences in equal measure. Beatrix felt her breath go shallow—but she kept her word, and did not move.

Then Edmund did something she had not predicted, and she had prided herself, these three weeks, on predicting everything about him.

He picked up the ledger.

Not the pardon. The *ledger*—the great vellum-bound account book the scribes had carried up the mountain, the one Loewen had brought, Beatrix realized with a cold drop in her stomach, to record the transfer of custody. Edmund lifted it off the table, weighed it in both hands like a man judging a melon, and opened it to the first page.

It was blank. Of course it was blank. It was waiting to be filled with the terms of her surrender.

"This," Edmund said, holding it up so the soldiers could see, so the scribes could see, so the wind could get at it, "is what you brought up a sacred mountain to write in. An empty book, waiting for a name. Mine, if I say yes. Hers, if I say yes. And underneath hers—" he turned to the fine-print page, holding it high, reading aloud in the flat clear voice he used for reading aloud, the voice that had made him the temple's best copyist and its worst liar— "*the bearer undertakes to deliver into temple custody the fugitive Beatrix Vane, together with all fire currently contained upon her person.*"

He read it twice. Slowly. Forty soldiers heard it. Two scribes heard it. Loewen heard it, and his face performed serenity while his hands, Beatrix noticed, gripped the arms of his chair.

"You wrote this yourself," Edmund said. "Didn't you. Your hand. Your phrasing. The same careful small print you taught me to hide inside my charm receipts, back when hiding things was the only skill this temple valued in me."

"Edmund—"

"And the fire in those vaults under Corvane. The engines. The hundred years of it, drained out of the Veil while the dragons starved on the far side and the temple told the realm it was *their* fault." He closed the ledger. "You taught me to read, Archivist. That was the mistake. You should have left me illiterate."

Loewen rose from his chair. For the first time, the warmth left his voice entirely, and what remained underneath was the thing Beatrix had always known lived there—the flat administrative certainty of a man who believed that everything, everywhere, was already his. "You are throwing away your life, boy. Your name. Your future. For *her*? For a disgraced woman and a dying flame and a boundary nobody in Corvane remembers exists?"

"For the truth," Edmund said. "Which you seem to think is the same thing as nothing."

And then he asked her.

That was the part Beatrix would keep, afterward, whatever the fire took and whatever it left. He turned to her—not to the soldiers, not to the mountain, not to the audience whose opinion might yet save him—and he asked, quietly, formally, the way one asks permission to touch another person's most wounded thing:

"May I?"

She held out her hands. The scars caught the light off the Veil—silver, flickering, alive. She drew a thread of flame up between her palms, thin as a hair, trembling, the last living fire in the human realm offered freely for the first time in a century rather than taken.

Edmund touched it to the corner of the ledger.

Vellum burns beautifully. Everyone knows this. It is why temples burn books and never admit to it. The flame ran along the edge of the account book with a sound like a whispered *yes*, and Edmund held it until the fire had eaten the pardon and the fine print and the blank pages waiting for their names, and then he dropped the blackening thing on the scree between them and ground it under his heel, and the ash blew east toward Corvane, toward the temple, toward home.

"There," he said. "Now I'm nobody. No rank, no stipend, no residence in the temple quarter. Just a man on a mountain with a woman whose hands are full of the only honest fire left in the world." He looked at Loewen, and Beatrix saw—not triumph, nothing so cheap—just a kind of clean, exhausted clarity, like a man setting down a weight he'd been told was his to carry. "You can't pardon someone who doesn't recognize your authority to pardon. I quit, Archivist. Formally. Publicly. With witnesses."

Loewen stared at the ash. Then at Edmund. Then, briefly and with visible distaste, at Beatrix.

"Well," the Archivist said finally, settling back into his chair with the composure of a man reordering reality in his own favor, "then the matter proceeds on the original terms." He raised one finger, and the forty soldiers raised their crossbows. "Custody by force. The fire will be harvested regardless—it is, after all, temple property—and you will be charged with obstruction, theft of temple assets, and conspiracy with a known heretic. I did try kindness, Edmund. Remember that I tried."

Beatrix felt the pulse in her hands quicken. Felt the ember lean toward her palms, eager, terrible, ready. Beside her, Edmund went very still—the stillness of a man who has just realized that words have run out and the world has moved on to the other argument.

Above them, the Veil flickered. Below them, the crossbows leveled. And from the shadow of the anchor stone, where it had been listening the whole time in the long patient way of very old things, Vessarath lifted its head.

* * *

The dragon's eyes opened like furnace doors.

Every soldier on the mountain saw it. Every soldier on the mountain stopped breathing. Beatrix had lived beside the creature for three days and had grown almost accustomed to the amber gleam watching from the dark—but there was no accustoming forty men from Corvane to the sight of a dragon uncoiling itself from behind a holy stone, scales the color of cooled iron, wings unfolding joint by joint with a sound like ship sails in a storm.

"*Temple property*," Vessarath said, and its voice rolled down the slope and rattled the scree and made the folding table walk several inches on its own. "A century your houses drank from the Veil. A century my kin died dimming in the far dark while your archivists called it stewardship. And now you climb my mountain with an empty book and call the fire yours."

Loewen's chair scraped backward. The Archivist's composure, Beatrix noted with a satisfaction she would deny later, required both hands to maintain.

"You have until the count of one hundred," the dragon went on, mild as a schoolmaster announcing an examination, "to take your soldiers, your scribes, your chairs, and your inkwell of dragonbone—which I trust you did not purchase honestly—off my mountain. After that, the negotiation concludes, and I begin collecting what is owed. I have," it added, with the terrible gentleness of the very old, "a great deal of time to make up for, and an excellent memory."

The crossbows wavered. Beatrix watched the front rank do the arithmetic that Edmund had done at the table, except theirs ended differently, because theirs included the option of running.

Loewen did not run. Loewen raised his voice into the wind, pitched to reassure, pitched to command: "Hold. HOLD. It is one beast, starving, alone—"

"One," Vessarath agreed pleasantly. "Presently."

And that was when Beatrix saw it—far to the south, beyond the pass Ilsa Renn had opened for them, beyond the smoke of the burning ledger drifting east—a light on the horizon that was not the sun. A glow, low and orange, spreading along the ridgeline like dawn arriving from the wrong direction.

The Veil, above them, shivered. Brightened. And somewhere on the far side of the broken boundary, in the dying dark where the dragons had been starving on memory for a hundred years—

Something answered.

Edmund grabbed her arm. "Beatrix. Tell me that's not what I think it is."

She was watching the horizon with her heart going like a hammer, and her scarred hands—her ruined, faithful, burdened hands—had begun, for the first time in eleven years, to warm on their own.

"It's not what you think it is," she said. "It's worse. It's *family*."
