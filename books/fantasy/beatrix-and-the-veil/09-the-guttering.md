## 9. The Guttering

The thing on the far side of the Veil was not a dragon, not exactly. It was more like the idea of a dragon that someone had tried to sketch from a description given secondhand by a frightened man. Vessarath had warned them: *the young ones come wrong now. They come as memory tries to hold them.*

Edmund did not have time to be terrified properly, which was probably for the best, because the horns started.

They came out of the eastern pass at dawn—or what passed for dawn under a Veil that was beginning, very faintly, to glow. Loewen's column moved with the tidy precision of everything the temple touched: pikes in ranks, wagons of harvested fire caged in glass, and at the head of it all, riding a mule because he had never learned to ride anything that might argue back, Archivist Loewen himself, looking like a schoolmaster arriving late to an examination he intended to fail everyone in.

"He brought soldiers," Edmund said. "I thought—I don't know what I thought. That he'd bring clerks."

"Soldiers *are* his clerks now," Beatrix said. She stood at the edge of the anchor terrace, her scarred hands loose at her sides, and the light of the Veil ran along her fingers like water finding old channels. "Look at their faces, Edmund."

He looked. He wished he hadn't. Half the soldiers were grey around the mouth, the way men get when they've been standing too close to the fire-wagons for too long. The harvested flame ate from the inside; he'd seen it in the vaults, in the temple servants who tended the cages and never spoke above a whisper. Loewen was feeding his own army to his own engines just to move them fast enough to catch them.

"There's Ilsa," Beatrix said.

Captain Renn rode at the column's left flank, and she alone sat her horse like a woman who wanted very much to be somewhere else. Her eyes found Edmund across half a mile of broken stone, and even at that distance, he could read the message in them plainly enough: *Run, you idiot.*

"Ilsa," Edmund said again, and something in his chest did a complicated thing that had no name in any of the four languages he could read.

"She won't run," Beatrix said. "She'll hesitate. There's a difference, and today it matters."

Vessarath's voice came from everywhere at once, the way it always did—not sound so much as pressure, an opinion forming in the air. *THE TEMPLE COMES WITH CAGES. THEY MEAN TO TAKE WHAT IS LEFT OF HER AND POUR IT INTO THEIR ENGINES, AND THE VEIL WILL DIE WITH HER IN A GLASS BOX.*

"Yes, thank you," Edmund said. "Very clarifying."

*YOU HAVE A PLAN?*

"I have a document," Edmund said. It sounded absurd even as he said it, holding up his satchel—the original covenant charter, copied in his own careful hand, with Loewen's signature countersigning a century of theft. "It's very well organized. There are footnotes."

For a moment the air itself seemed amused. Then Vessarath said, grimly: *THEN HOLD IT UP AND HOPE.*

* * *

Loewen did not charge. That was the worst part. He walked his mule up the switchback road to the anchor terrace with his hands folded on his saddlebow, while two hundred soldiers fanned out below in a crescent, and he smiled at Edmund the way he used to smile when Edmund got an answer right in lessons.

"My boy," Loewen said. "You look terrible. Marsh fever?"

"You knew," Edmund said. His voice came out steadier than he deserved. "You've known about the siphoning since before I was born. You countersigned the first harvest warrant. Your name is in your own hand, Master, on a page that says the dragons never broke anything."

"The dragons broke nothing," Loewen agreed mildly, dismounting. "The dragons *are* nothing. Fire without a temple is just a forest burning down, Edmund. We gave it purpose. Heat for the capital. Light for the archives. Order." He spread his hands. "You think I'm ashamed? I am the most honest man in Corvane. Everyone else lies about where the warmth comes from."

Beatrix stepped forward, and the temperature of the whole terrace changed. Every soldier below took an involuntary step backward, all at once, like a field of wheat bending.

"Inquisitor Vane," Loewen said, and his smile thinned. "Or should I say Keeper Vane. The last one. Do you know, we searched for you for eleven years? Not to harm you." He said it gently, sincerely, which made it obscene. "To *house* you. All that fire, living rough in those poor ruined hands, wasting itself keeping your heart beating. It belongs in a cage, child. Where it can do some good."

"Master Loewen," Edmund said, "shut *up*."

And then—because there was nothing left to negotiate about, because the truth was already in his satchel and would soon be in every archive that mattered or nowhere—Edmund pulled out the charter copy and read the harvest warrants aloud at the top of his voice, the way he'd been trained to read legal findings since he was nine years old. Clear. Carrying. In the flat, merciless tone of a man who has checked his citations twice.

The crescent of soldiers stopped being a formation and started being two hundred individuals listening.

That was when Ilsa Renn swung down off her horse, drew her sword, and planted it point-down in the stone between her men and the anchor terrace.

"Hold," she said. "All ranks, hold."

Loewen turned. For the first time in thirty years of knowing him, Edmund saw genuine surprise on the Archivist's face.

"Captain," Loewen said. "Your orders—"

"I've seen what's in the wagons," Ilsa said. Her voice didn't shake, but it was close. "I've buried forty men this year who stood watch over the glass cages. Grey-lung, they're calling it. My brother among them." She looked up at the terrace, at Edmund, and her jaw worked. "You were right, Underhill. Back in Corvane, all those years, every charm you sold me that 'didn't work'—you were right about all of it."

"They mostly didn't work," Edmund said weakly. "The luck ones were just buttons."

"*Hold,*" Ilsa said, to everyone and no one.

But Loewen had not ridden a mule up a mountain on hope alone. He snapped his fingers, and the fire-wagons opened—and the harvested flame came out wrong, hungry, a hundred ribbons of stolen gold pouring uphill against the wind toward Beatrix, toward the spark inside her, called home like iron filings to a lodestone.

What happened next, Edmund would spend the rest of his life failing to describe honestly.

Beatrix raised her hands, and the fire stopped. Mid-air. A hundred ribbons hanging in the morning cold like a held breath. And then they turned—slowly, like a flock changing its mind—and came to her instead of from her, and she drank her stolen family back into her scarred hands, and the Veil above them blazed so bright that the snow on the far peaks flashed to water.

Below, two hundred soldiers knelt. Not by command. By instinct.

Only the wagons' glass, cracked by the reversal, began to sing—a high thin whine of stressed material—and one of the ribbons, wild with freedom, lashed sideways and struck the anchor stone.

* * *

The sound the anchor stone made when it shattered was small. That was the horror of it. A century of covenant, the boundary between worlds, the hinge of everything—and it went with a noise like a dropped dinner plate.

The breach opened beneath the terrace like an eye waking up.

Through it Edmund could see the other side: the dragons' dying country, vast and dark and starved, its skies empty, its mountains hollow. And through the wound, the dark began to pour *out*—not evil, nothing so simple, just absence, just the hunger of a world with almost nothing left in it, spilling toward theirs.

"IT MUST BE HELD OPEN," Vessarath roared, suddenly, urgently, present in a way he hadn't been before. "NOT CLOSED—HELD. THE YOUNG ONES ARE COMING THROUGH. IF THE BREACH COLLAPSES NOW THEY DIE IN THE CROSSING, AND THE VEIL DIES WITH THEM, AND THEN EVERYTHING—" The ancient voice frayed into something Edmund had never heard in it before. Fear.

"How do we hold it?" Edmund shouted.

"WITH FIRE," Vessarath said. "LIVING FIRE, GIVEN, NOT TAKEN. ALL OF IT."

Edmund understood then. He understood it completely, in one terrible clean instant, the way you understand arithmetic. And he turned to Beatrix, and Beatrix was already looking at the breach with an expression Edmund had seen on her face only once before—in the marshes, when she'd talked about the order that died protecting the temple's lie. It was the expression of a woman who had spent eleven years carrying something precious and exhausting, and had finally, finally been shown what it was for.

"No," Edmund said.

"It was always going to end somewhere," she said. "This is a better place than most."

"Beatrix—"

"Edmund." She took his face in her scarred hands, and they were warm, warmer than skin should be, warm as a hearth, warm as the first morning after a lifetime of winter. "You are going to be keeper here someday, so learn this now, because I'll only say it once. The fire was never mine. It was only ever borrowed. Everything I am that matters—" her voice caught, and she laughed at herself, gentle and rueful, "—everything I am that matters, I got the same way you did. By choosing whose truth to serve."

"That's a terrible speech," Edmund said, crying. "You're a terrible speechwriter. I could fix this in draft—"

"I know you could." She kissed his forehead, and it did not burn, because her fire had never once burned anything it loved. "Keep my pages, scribe. Get the words right."

Then she turned to the breach, spread her arms wide, and gave it all back.

The light was indescribable. Edmund tried anyway, later, in the margins of the truest book he ever wrote: *like sunrise happening in a room*. The dark pouring from the dying world met her flame and stopped, and held, and became a doorway instead of a wound, and through it, distantly, impossibly, Edmund heard wingbeats—young ones, clumsy and eager, crossing at last.

And Beatrix, at the center of it, grew brighter and brighter and thinner and thinner, like paper held to a candle, smiling—

The breach flared white. Edmund threw his arm across his eyes. Through his fingers he watched her silhouette gutter, flicker, shrink—

—and go out.

The Veil stood whole above the terrace, burning steady and gold from horizon to horizon. The breach sealed itself around the passage of the last young dragon with a sound like a door closing gently in another room.

Edmund stood alone on the anchor stone, in the ringing quiet, holding a satchel full of documents nobody needed anymore.

Then, from behind a shelf of fallen rock where she'd dragged the two of them when the fighting started, Captain Ilsa Renn stood up slowly, staring at the place where Beatrix had been, and said in a voice like a broken hinge:

"Underhill. Tell me that wasn't her."

And from the far side of the Veil—faint, furious, and unmistakably alive—came a voice like banked coals:

*"She's not dead, you idiot. But she will be if someone doesn't fetch her."*
