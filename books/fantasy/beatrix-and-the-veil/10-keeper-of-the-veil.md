## 10. Keeper of the Veil

Edmund had always assumed that if he ever witnessed a miracle, it would feel like thunder. What he got instead was the sound of a hearth being lit on a cold morning—a soft rush, a settling warmth, and then light where there had been none.

The Veil burned.

Not the frantic, guttering blaze of Beatrix's sacrifice—that had lasted only moments before the anchor stone drank it down—but a steady, patient flame that ran along the shattered boundary like dawn along a mountain ridge. Where the stone had cracked, gold now seamed the fracture the way a smith seams iron, holding what was broken together without pretending it had never broken. The Veil would always carry its scar. But it would carry it alight.

Edmund sat in the ash of the battlefield with Beatrix's head in his lap and watched dragons come home.

They came out of the far distance first as smudges, then as shadows, then as shapes too vast for his eyes to make sense of all at once. They crossed through the burning boundary one by one, wings catching the firelight so that each looked briefly made of stained glass. Some were enormous. One was small—not young, exactly, but compact, weathered, the color of river slate—and it landed near the anchor stone with the deliberate care of someone entering a sickroom.

*Vessarath*, Edmund realized. The last dragon who had stayed behind. Except the dragon did not look like a guardian anymore. It looked like a host welcoming guests to a house it had swept alone for a hundred years.

"You held it," Beatrix murmured against his knee. Her voice was thin as old paper. "You absolute, stubborn clerk. You held it."

"I filed it," Edmund corrected. "There's a difference. A keeper files things. Preferably in triplicate."

She laughed, and then coughed, and Edmund's heart lurched sideways in his chest—because he had watched her pour everything into the breach, every last spark of the fire she had carried in her scarred hands since she was a girl, and he had been certain, *certain*, that he was holding her while she died.

But her hands, when he took them, were just hands. Scarred, cold, ordinary. The fire was gone from them entirely. He could feel its absence like feeling silence after years of living beside a bell.

"Vessarath," he called, and his voice cracked on the name. "She gave everything. Take it back. Give her some of it—the covenant doesn't need *all* of her, does it? There must be margin. There is always margin in a well-kept ledger."

The great slate dragon turned its ancient head. Its eyes were the color of deep coals, banked but not dead.

"The covenant does not need all of anyone," Vessarath said, in a voice like a landslide deciding to be gentle. "It never did. That was the temple's first error—they believed the fire was a treasure to be hoarded and extracted. It is not a treasure, little scribe. It is a *conversation*. And conversations can be brief."

Something vast and warm moved over them both—breath, Edmund realized, dragon-breath, smelling faintly of woodsmoke and rain. Beatrix shivered under it.

Then, from somewhere inside her, something kindled.

It was nothing like the inferno she had carried before. It was small. It was the size of a candle flame, the size of an ember raked from a dying fire, the size of exactly enough warmth to get a person through one more night. It rose in her palms—a flicker, a tremble, a stubborn little light—and stayed.

Beatrix stared at her own hands and began, very quietly, to weep.

"It's so small," she said.

"It's yours," said Vessarath. "Only yours. No order gave it to you. No oath binds it to a purpose. You are, as of this morning, the freest creature on either side of that wall."

* * *

The temple's soldiers did not know what to do with a miracle.

Edmund watched them from the slope above the Veil—what remained of them, that is, after Ilsa Renn had turned her company against Loewen's guard in the chaos of battle. They stood now in ragged lines, weapons grounded, faces smudged with ash, staring at a boundary of fire that their sermons had taught them was a wound in the world, a leak of monstrous power, the very thing the hunt existed to seal forever.

It did not look like a wound. It looked like a lamp in a window.

Ilsa climbed the slope to him at midmorning, if mornings still meant anything here, where the light came from the Veil itself. She had a bandage around her forearm and Loewen's signet ring hanging from a cord around her neck—taken from his hand, Edmund suspected, in a manner Loewen had not survived. She looked ten years older than the girl who used to steal honeycakes with him behind the archive stacks.

"So," she said. "Keeper of the Veil."

"That's what they're calling me." He nodded toward the dragons, several of whom had settled around the anchor stone like cats around a hearth, watching him with what he chose to interpret as approval. Vessarath had informed him, in the tone of someone closing a contract, that the oath was taken and the office was his, and that the paperwork—dragon paperwork, apparently, which involved oaths spoken in a language that made his teeth hum—was already complete. "I didn't exactly interview for the position."

"No," Ilsa said. "You just told the truth in front of two hundred witnesses, burned your credentials, and refused a pardon from the most powerful man in Corvane." She almost smiled. "Terrible career management, Ed. Always was."

"What happens now? Down there, I mean. In the city."

Ilsa was quiet for a while. Below them, the marsh fog was lifting off the lowlands, and beyond it, impossibly far, Corvane lay somewhere under its smoke.

"Now there's a mess," she said finally. "Loewen's dead. The council will pretend they never knew what was in those vaults, which is a lie, and I'll let them tell it, because the alternative is a second war inside our walls. The hunts are done—I've countermanded them myself. Every charm-seller and hedge-witch in the city can sleep tonight." She looked at him sidelong. "Every one except you. You understand that. You can't come home, Ed. Too many people need you to be either a traitor or a saint, and you won't hold still long enough to be either."

"I know," Edmund said. And found, to his surprise, that it didn't hurt the way he'd expected. It hurt like a healed thing hurts—in the bone, on certain days, but no longer in the flesh.

"There's a farmstead up the Salt Road," Ilsa went on, gruff now, because she had never in her life been able to say a kind thing straight. "Abandoned since the purges started. Stone barn, good well, view of the pass. If a keeper happened to live there, close enough to tend the Veil and far enough that the council could pretend he didn't exist—" she shrugged "—well. Stranger arrangements have been made. I'd know. I've made most of them."

"Ilsa."

"Don't. Don't make a speech, I'll only remember it wrong." She turned to go, then stopped. "The child. The one whose charm started all this, back in your alley. Milly Cobb, her mother's a dyer off Tanner's Row. When word reaches the city that the hunts are over—and it'll reach them today—Milly's going to ask her mother whether the charm-man with the nice handwriting is safe now." Ilsa pulled her cloak tight. "I'd like to be the one who tells her yes. Make it true for me."

Edmund's throat was too full for words. He managed a nod. Ilsa nodded back, once, the way soldiers do, and went down the hill toward her ruined, redeemed company.

* * *

They built the house slowly, which was to say Beatrix directed and Edmund did nearly all of the work, an arrangement she declared was simply good delegation.

He learned the shape of his new days. Mornings, he walked the boundary line with his ledger—the one habit of his old life he had kept, because someone had to record what crossed the Veil and when, and because writing things down was how he prayed. Dragons came and went through the fire like travelers through a gate; some paused to speak with him, mostly about weather, occasionally about oaths, once at surprising length about the proper way to store winter apples. Afternoons he studied the charter fragments Vessarath had saved from the ruined station, restoring the true covenant clause by clause, so that no future temple could claim ignorance. Evenings he cooked badly, and Beatrix ate it anyway, and complained, and lit the stove with her small stubborn flame rather than suffer him to use flint like a common person.

Her hands healed. Not into what they had been—she was slower now, tired sooner, mortal in every particular—but into something she seemed, cautiously, to like better. One evening in early autumn he found her teaching herself to knit, muttering at the needles, her little ember of a flame hovering over her shoulder like a curious bird.

"I used to warm whole garrisons," she said darkly, dropping a stitch. "I held back the Guttering itself. And these cursed needles have defeated me utterly."

"Everything worth doing is defeated by you at first," Edmund said. "Ask any of us."

She threw a ball of yarn at his head. He caught it. Under the table, her foot found his and stayed there, and neither of them mentioned it, because some things filed themselves without paperwork.

On the first anniversary of the reforging, they walked up to the anchor stone together in the grey hour before dawn. The Veil burned steady and gold along its seam. Beyond it, in the dragons' country, winged shapes rose against the fading stars—not fleeing something now, but greeting the day.

"Do you regret it?" Beatrix asked. "Any of it. The stall, the arrest, all of it. You had a quiet life, Edmund Underhill."

"I had a hidden one," Edmund said. "There's a difference too." He turned the thought over, testing it the way he tested a ledger line, and found it balanced. "No. I don't regret it. I spent thirty years keeping other people's records—temple records, crown records, lies with good penmanship. This is the first thing I've kept that keeps me back."

Beatrix leaned her head against his shoulder. Far below, on the Salt Road, a cart creaked—early traders, probably, salt-dyers heading north, ordinary people starting ordinary days in a world that had quietly refused, against all institutional effort, to end.

And then Edmund saw them: three riders coming up the road through the mist, moving fast, flying no colors he recognized. Not temple grey. Not Ilsa's company. Something else—something new, with the ink barely dry on whatever banner they carried.

Beatrix lifted her head. Her little flame flared, bright and wary, casting their shadows long across the anchor stone.

"Well, keeper," she said softly. "Someone's come to see you. Shall we go find out what the world wants this time?"

Edmund sighed, tucked his ledger under his arm, and started down the hill.

"Triplicate," he muttered. "I hope it's triplicate."
