## 1. The Valley of Stillness

The bell at the Temple of the Valley of Stillness rang six times before dawn, and Orsen Almeida was awake for all of them, because Orsen Almeida was awake for all of them every morning, and had been for eleven years.

They lay in their narrow cot in the scribes' dormitory and counted the bells the way other people counted sheep. Six meant rise. Seven meant wash. Eight meant the first ledger of the day was open on your desk and the ink was mixed and Brother Cassian was already walking the rows with his little brass lantern held high, looking for anyone whose shoulders suggested an insufficient devotion to the work.

Orsen's shoulders were very good at suggesting devotion. They had been trained for it.

*Peace*, said the temple, in its carved lintels and its painted walls and the slow, honeyed voice of the abbot at festival. *This is peace.* And most days Orsen believed it, or believed it enough, which in the valley amounted to the same thing. The valley was quiet. That was its whole virtue and its entire law: no magic crossed the border. Not a spark, not a whisper, not a hedge-witch's cantrip to sweeten milk or find a lost button. Magic stopped at the ridgeline the way weather stopped at a window, and inside, everything stayed where you put it. Milk soured at its own pace. Buttons were lost and stayed lost. Nothing hummed. Nothing watched.

It should have felt like safety. Most mornings it did.

Orsen dressed in the dark — grey robe, grey sash, the little wooden stylus-case that was the only object they owned outright — and joined the shuffling river of scribes flowing down the dormitory stairs into the courtyard, where the cold sat like a polite guest who refused to leave.

"You look terrible," whispered Dennet, falling into step beside them. Dennet was round as a teapot and cheerful in a way the temple had never managed to grind down, though heaven knew it tried. "Did you sleep?"

"I slept."

"Liar. You had your thinking face on when the bell went."

"This is my face."

"That's your *thinking* face wearing your regular face as a disguise." Dennet ducked under the low arch into the scriptorium's antechamber, breath fogging. "You were doing sums in your head again. I could hear them squeaking around in there from across the room."

Orsen didn't dignify this. They were doing sums in their head. They were always doing sums, because Orsen owed the temple four hundred and sixty-two crowns, eleven years of interest compounding gently against them like frost creeping up glass, and the only way to keep from drowning in that number was to know exactly how deep it was at all times.

Four hundred sixty-two crowns. A scribe earned two marks a day after room and board. One hundred marks made a crown. Orsen had done the arithmetic so many times the numbers had worn smooth, like river stones: at current rate, with current interest, with the temple's generous policy of deducting candle-fees and ink-fees and the annual Festival of Stillness donation — voluntary in name only — Orsen would be free of debt in fifty-one years, give or take.

Orsen was twenty-six.

"Ledger forty-one today," announced Brother Cassian from the front of the great hall, and three hundred quills dipped as one, a sound like wind through dry grass. "Harvest tithes, western parishes. Neatness is devotion. Devotion is stillness. Begin."

And the valley's greatest industry got underway: the copying of accounts.

Here is what nobody outside the valley ever understood about the Temple of Stillness. It was not a place of contemplation, whatever the pilgrims' pamphlets said. It was a counting-house with incense. Every farm, every mill, every widow's loom and cooper's yard in the valley owed the temple something — a founding debt, they called it, incurred generations ago when the founders paid the price of the valley's protection. Nobody remembered what that price had been. Nobody asked. The debts were simply there, like the mountains, and the temple kept the books of them with a tenderness it extended to nothing else in creation.

Orsen copied. Column after column, name after name. *Alma Grell, miller's widow, 3 crowns 4 marks. Tovan Rhee, farrier, 11 crowns.* The names ran through their fingers like water through a sieve. That was the skill, really — not the letters, anyone could make letters — but the art of letting none of it stick. Three hundred scribes in this hall, and each one a wall with a small window, and through each window the same gray light.

Orsen had gotten so good at not sticking that some mornings they weren't entirely sure anything of them remained to stick to.

* * *

At midday the bell rang for bread and broth, and Orsen walked to the refectory beside Dennet and Sister Maren, who was seventy and sharp as a paper cut and the closest thing Orsen had to family, if family could be someone who mostly insulted your handwriting.

"You press too hard," Maren said, taking her bowl. "Your letters. You dig into the page like you're trying to get at something underneath it."

"Maybe I am."

"There's nothing underneath it. That's the whole point of the valley." She said it comfortably, the way one says *the sky is up*, and slurped her broth. "Eat. You're thin. Debt doesn't collect well off skeletons; I've asked."

"You've asked," Orsen repeated.

"Cassian told me my curiosity was unbecoming. I told him his hairpiece was crooked. We're even." She squinted at Orsen over her bowl. "You dreamt badly last night. Don't argue. You've got the look. What was it this time?"

Orsen hesitated. It was silly. It was a nothing-dream, the kind that dissolved as you reached for it, and yet it had left something behind, a residue, like ash on the fingers.

"A sound," they said finally. "Like humming. Far away. Under the floor."

Maren's spoon paused — just for half a heartbeat, just long enough for Orsen to notice — then resumed. "Wind in the pipes," she said briskly. "The temple's full of old pipes. Eat."

But she hadn't finished her bread, and Orsen noticed that too, because noticing things was the one rebellion available to a person whose life consisted of columns, and Orsen hoarded their small rebellions like coins.

* * *

The afternoon ledger was worse than the morning's. Ledger forty-four: the debt rolls of the outer parishes, the poor ones, where the numbers grew fat with penalties and the handwriting of previous scribes grew careless, as though carelessness were a mercy. Orsen copied a name — *Yeva Sorn, herbwoman, 2 crowns 9 marks, penalty accruing* — and thought, briefly, of the woman behind the number. Herbwoman. In the valley, an herbwoman who knew too much found her debts reviewed with special attention. The temple did not burn witches. The temple simply priced them out of their homes, year by year, until they left or starved, and the ledgers recorded it all without a single cruel word.

Neatness is devotion.

Orsen pressed hard on the page. Then caught themselves, and smoothed the letter flat, and made it perfect, because perfection was survival and survival came first. This was the bargain they'd struck with the world at age fifteen, when their mother died owing a debt Orsen inherited like eye color: *I will be invisible, and invisible people don't get hurt.*

Eleven years of practice. They were excellent at it.

Which is why it was strange, near dusk, that Orsen looked up at all.

The light in the scriptorium was failing; Cassian's lantern-bearers were making their rounds along the aisles, and shadows pooled between the desks like standing water. Orsen's hand ached. Their eyes ached. And through the tall window at the hall's east end, above the shelf-rows and the sleeping moths, they saw the ridgeline of the valley's border, black against a sky going bruise-colored, and for no reason they could name their chest went tight.

There was a story every child in the valley learned before they learned letters. Long ago, the founders — wise and good, the story always said, wise and good and frightened for their children — built a wall. Not of stone. Of *stillness*. They drew a line around the valley and told the magic of the world: *no further.* And the magic, being reasonable, agreed. And inside the line, nothing unnatural could live or enter or even think about entering, and the people slept safe, and in gratitude they gave the temple everything it asked forever, amen.

Every child learned it. Every child repeated it at festival with candles in their hands. Orsen had repeated it hundreds of times, and had never once, until this moment, wondered the obvious question — the question that was probably why Maren's spoon had paused, the question that lived under the floor of the world like a hum:

*Paid for with what?*

A wall that strong wants building materials. The founders weren't masons.

"Almeida!" Cassian's voice cracked across the hall like a whip. "Is column twelve amusing you? Finish it."

"Finishing it," Orsen called back, head down, quill moving, heart doing something odd and arrhythmic against their ribs.

They finished column twelve. They finished column thirteen. The bell rang for evening prayer, and three hundred scribes rose and filed out in order, and Orsen went with them, grey among greys, a wall among walls, telling themselves the tight feeling in their chest was only the cold.

* * *

Later — much later, past the hour when the dormitory lamps were supposed to be out — Orsen lay staring at the beams overhead and listening.

Dennet snored. Rain had started somewhere around compline, a soft businesslike rain, ticking at the shutters. Somewhere below, the night brothers turned the hour-glass. Ordinary sounds. Valley sounds. Sounds with no magic in them anywhere, because there was no magic anywhere, not within a week's walk in any direction, and this was the safest place in the world, and Orsen had spent eleven years learning to mistake that safety for peace.

They almost slept.

Then they heard it again. Faint, far, beneath the rain and the snores and the sand whispering in the glass: a tone, sustained, patient, rising from under the floor of the world. Not wind in the pipes. Pipes don't hold a note. Pipes don't *wait*.

Orsen sat up in the dark, heart hammering, and listened until the sound faded — or until they convinced themselves it had faded; by then they couldn't have sworn which — and lay back down, and pulled the blanket to their chin, and performed, with tremendous discipline, the act of being asleep.

Invisible people don't get hurt. Invisible people stay.

Outside, the rain went on falling on the valley of Stillness, on the temple and its ledgers and its four hundred and sixty-two crowns of gentle frost, and Orsen Almeida, heir to nothing, debtor to everything, drifted at last toward dreams of a hum beneath the floor —

— and did not see, in the courtyard below their shuttered window, where no lamp burned and no brother walked, a figure staggering out of the dark toward the gate, leaving a trail across the wet stones that glowed.
