## 2. A Stranger at the Gate

There were exactly forty-one things Orsen Almeida was permitted to do before the evening bell, and copying the tithe ledgers was the first, the ninth, and — on days when Elder Maren was feeling punitive — also the thirty-seventh.

Orsen had long ago stopped minding. This was, they told themselves, the great secret of a contented life: that if you made your cage small enough, and kept it tidy enough, you could convince yourself the bars were walls of your own choosing. The scriptorium smelled of oak gall ink and beeswax and the faint vinegar tang of too many bodies bent over parchment in one room. Forty scribes sat in their stalls like well-ordered teeth in a jaw, quills scratching in unison, a sound like gentle rain that never fell anywhere. Orsen's stall was third from the window, which meant third-best light and second-best view of the courtyard below, and they had spent six years perfecting the art of looking at neither for longer than three seconds at a time.

The rule about windows was unwritten, which in the Valley of Stillness made it ironclad. Unwritten rules were the strongest kind here, because no one had to admit to writing them, and so no one could be blamed for enforcing them. *Do not look out the window during working hours.* *Do not hum while the elders are counting.* *Do not ask what lies past the northern ridge.* Orsen followed all of these with the diligence of someone who had learned early that attention was a debt, and debts in this valley were collected with interest.

Orsen's own debt was eleven years old and would be paid off in — they did the arithmetic every morning, because the arithmetic was a kind of prayer — nineteen more years, four months, and some days that varied depending on whether Elder Maren found fault with their lettering. Their mother had borrowed against Orsen's labor to buy medicine she hadn't lived long enough to finish taking. The temple had absorbed the remainder of her debt into Orsen's account, as was customary, as was generous, as was written in the ledger in beautiful copperplate: *Balance transferred. Gratitude is its own collateral.*

Gratitude. Orsen copied the word forty times a day in other people's accounts and had never once managed to feel it. But they were very good at pretending, and pretending, done consistently enough for long enough, becomes nearly indistinguishable from peace. That was the valley's finest product, after all. Not grain, not cloth, not the famous stillness itself — but peace, the manufactured kind, sold by the ledger-line.

"Almeida." Elder Maren's voice carried the way a wet cloth carries cold. "Your margins are wandering."

"Yes, Elder."

"Your margins," Maren said, descending upon Orsen's stall like weather, "are a moral document. A wandering margin is a wandering mind."

"I'll correct them, Elder."

"See that you do." She lingered a moment, sniffing at Orsen's ink pot as though it might contain rebellion, then moved on down the row, dispensing small humiliations like a farmer scattering seed.

Orsen straightened their margins. Outside the window, the courtyard lay in its usual afternoon order: swept flagstones, the bell-cord hanging slack, two guards at the gate doing their patient imitation of statues. The guards were brothers, Hask and Dannel, and between them they possessed roughly one personality, which they shared amicably.

It was a Tuesday, or close enough to a Tuesday that nothing should have happened. Nothing ever happened on Tuesdays. The Valley of Stillness had, over its long history, become remarkably skilled at preventing Tuesdays from producing surprises, in the same way a riverbed prevents water from going anywhere interesting.

So when the stranger came through the gate, Orsen's first thought — absurdly, shamefully — was *she's going to ruin my afternoon.*

* * *

She didn't come through the gate so much as arrive at it, the way a stone arrives at the bottom of a hill. One moment the guards stood at their post; the next there was a woman crumpled against the gatepost as though she'd been thrown there by something with poor aim and great strength.

From the third stall by the window, Orsen saw everything, and — this was the terrible part — so did everyone else. Forty scribes, two guards, an elder passing through the cloister walk. The courtyard was not large. It was impossible *not* to see her.

She was perhaps fifty, gray-streaked hair escaping its braid, wearing traveling clothes that had once been fine and had since been through a war, or several. And she was bleeding. Orsen could see the dark wet bloom along her side even from above.

Except it wasn't dark. That was the thing. That was the thing that stopped Orsen's quill mid-word and left a blot the size of a beetle on the ledger page.

She was bleeding light.

Not glowing, not shining — *bleeding* it, the way a person bleeds blood, in sluggish pulses that matched some private rhythm inside her. Where it touched the flagstones it didn't spread; it sank, and the stones drank it, and for half a heartbeat the courtyard looked the way Orsen imagined the world must look to someone who had never seen it: new, wet-bright, humming.

Then it faded, and the woman tried to stand, failed, and said in a voice pitched to carry:

"Please. I need to speak with whoever keeps the records."

Nobody moved.

That was the second remarkable thing, and later — much later, on bad nights, on roads far from home — Orsen would turn it over and over like a stone with something underneath it. Nobody moved. Not Hask, not Dannel, whose entire purpose was to move toward trouble. They looked at the woman, and then they performed the most practiced maneuver Orsen had ever witnessed: they looked *away*. In unison. It had the smoothness of liturgy. Hask turned his gaze to the bell-cord; Dannel studied the middle distance with the intensity of a man being paid by the hour to see nothing.

In the scriptorium, forty quills resumed scratching within four seconds. Four seconds of silence, then rain-sound again, as though the world had hiccupped and been politely ignored. Even Elder Maren, visible through the archway of the cloister, paused only long enough to arrange her face into an expression of mild inconvenience before continuing on her way.

Orsen sat with a blot spreading under their pen and understood, with the clarity that sometimes visits people in the moment they least want it, that they were watching a ritual. An old one. Everyone knew their part. The part was *this does not happen here.*

Magic cannot cross the valley's borders. Every child learned it before they learned letters. There was no magic in the Valley of Stillness, none, and therefore whatever bled light in the courtyard was not magic, and therefore there was nothing to see, and therefore the sensible thing was to return to one's ledgers, because the ledgers were real, the ledgers always balanced, the ledgers never bled light onto flagstones and asked to speak with whoever kept the records.

Orsen's quill hovered. Somewhere behind them, a scribe whispered, "Should someone fetch the elder?" and another whispered back, "Fetch which elder? For what?" and the first said, "Nothing. Never mind," and both returned to work.

*This does not happen here,* said the valley, in forty small voices.

The woman's head drooped. Her hand, pressed to her side, came away shining.

And Orsen — who had spent thirty-one years cultivating the fine art of invisibility, who knew all forty-one permissible activities by heart, whose margins never wandered twice — heard themselves say, aloud, in the silent scriptorium, in a voice that sounded like someone else's:

"I keep the records."

* * *

Later, Orsen would try to reconstruct how they'd gotten down the stairs. There was no memory of deciding. There was only the memory of the stairwell's cool dimness rushing upward past them, and then the courtyard's flagstones under their feet, shockingly bright after the scriptorium's brown gloom, and the woman looking up at them with eyes the color of deep water.

"You keep the records," she repeated. Her voice had gone thin, worn through like fabric rubbed too long. "Good. Good. I've crossed—" she coughed, and the cough produced light, actual light, flecks of it drifting from her lips like seeds from a dandelion, "—I've crossed a long way to find someone who writes things down properly."

"You're wounded." Orsen knelt, hands hovering uselessly, trained by a lifetime of rules against touching anything not on one's own desk. "We should fetch a physician."

"No physicians." The words came out sharp, and then she softened, and there was — Orsen would remember this — humor in her face, even now, even bleeding light. A crooked smile, tired as an old dog. "No physicians. No elders either. You'll want to note that in your record, scribe. The patient declines all treatment offered by the management." She glanced around the courtyard, at the guards studiously guarding the horizon, at the blank windows above. "They can't see me, you know. Not properly. It's not cruelty. It's design."

"That isn't possible. People don't just—not see—"

"In the valley?" She laughed, which cost her visibly. "Child, in this valley, people don't just not-see. It's the local specialty. Now help me sit up properly. My mother raised me to die sitting upright, facing my audience, and I intend to honor her."

"You're not dying." Orsen heard their own voice crack on it, absurdly, as if the woman's death were a breach of etiquette they could protest. "You're bleeding, but people don't die of—of *light*—"

"People don't bleed light," the woman agreed pleasantly. "And yet."

Orsen got an arm under her — warm, alarmingly light, as though she were mostly conviction and very little body — and eased her upright against the gatepost. Up close, her wound was worse and stranger: a long tear along her ribs, edges clean as a paper cut, and from it the light welled slow and golden-white, pooling in the hollows of the flagstones and sinking away.

"What's your name?" Orsen asked, because names went first in every ledger, and the question was the only professional instinct they had left standing.

"Beatrix." She said it like she was handing over something heavy. "Beatrix Vael. And yours?"

"Orsen Almeida. Scribe, third stall by the window. Eleven years' service, nineteen remaining." The recitation came automatically, and Beatrix's eyebrows climbed.

"Nineteen years. What did you do, kill someone?"

"My mother was ill."

"Ah," Beatrix said, and the *ah* contained an entire library of understanding. "Debt."

"The temple was generous."

"The temple," Beatrix said, "was arithmetic." She caught her breath, steadied. "Listen to me, Orsen Almeida, because I have perhaps ten minutes of eloquence left and I mean to spend every one of them, and I promise — I swear on my mother's grave, which is in a much more interesting location than yours — that everything I say will sound insane, and every word of it is true."

Somewhere above, the evening bell prepared itself. Orsen felt it coming the way one feels a sneeze. Nineteen years, four months, and a blot the size of a beetle on the tithe ledger. Elder Maren's voice in memory: *a wandering mind.*

"I'm listening," Orsen said, and was surprised to find it true.

"There was a covenant," Beatrix said. "Between this valley and the wide world. Between the quiet folk and the quick ones, the ones like me. Long ago it held both realms together like stitching holds a seam. And it broke. Not frayed — broke. Snapped by hands, on purpose, by the people your grandparents built statues of." Her eyes, deep-water eyes, fixed on Orsen's with terrifying steadiness. "I've spent my life trying to mend what was torn, and I have failed, and I am now, medically speaking, a fountain. Which means the mending passes to the next name on the list. And Orsen—" her hand shot out, surprisingly strong, and gripped Orsen's wrist, and where her fingers touched, Orsen felt warmth sink into skin like ink into parchment, "—the next name on the list is yours."

"That's not—" Orsen's voice came out very small. "I'm a scribe. Third stall. I copy tithes. I'm nobody, I'm *specifically* nobody, that's the whole point of me—"

"Oh, I know," Beatrix said warmly. "I chose you *because* you're nobody. Nobodies are the only ones the valley doesn't watch." She smiled her crooked, dying smile. "Congratulations. You're heir to a broken covenant. Do try to look pleased; the previous candidates wept."

Above them, the evening bell began to ring.

And beneath Orsen's skin, something began, very gently, to write.
