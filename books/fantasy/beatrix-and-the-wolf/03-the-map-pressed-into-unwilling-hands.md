## 3. The Map Pressed Into Unwilling Hands

Dying, Beatrix had expected, would be quieter than this.

She had imagined something dignified. A hillside, perhaps, at dusk. Her own name spoken gently by the wind. Instead she was sprawled in a temple courtyard that smelled of lye and wet stone, being dragged by the collar by a scribe who looked like they'd rather be doing literally anything else in the world.

"Stop—helping," she managed.

"You're bleeding," the scribe said. Their voice was flat with panic, the way a pond is flat right before something enormous breaks the surface.

"I'm aware."

"You're bleeding *light*."

Ah. So it had gotten to that stage already. Beatrix looked down at herself and saw what the scribe saw: her left side seeping gold, not red, the wound from the ford glowing like a crack in a lantern. The light pooled on the flagstones and did not run off the edges. It simply stayed, gathering, patient, as if waiting for instructions.

*Well,* she thought. *That's inconvenient.*

She had planned for this moment for eleven years. She had rehearsed her words in taverns and graveyards and once, memorably, while hanging upside down from a cliff face waiting for a patrol to pass. She had imagined finding the heir calm, prepared, perhaps even grateful. She had not imagined the heir would be this — this person with ink to their elbows and an expression like a man discovering the floor has been removed from beneath his life.

But the covenant does not consult our imaginations. It never had. That was rather the whole problem.

"The courtyard," she said. "Is there somewhere less — observed?"

The scribe glanced around. The guards at the gate had turned their backs with the practiced elegance of men who had been paid very well to see nothing, which meant someone in the Order was closer than she'd hoped. The other scribes had evaporated into doorways. Only this one remained, kneeling in the spreading gold light, holding her collar as though she were a drowned thing washed up on their shore.

"There's the scriptorium," the scribe said reluctantly. "It's empty at this hour. But you can't—"

"I can," Beatrix said, "and I'm about to be extremely rude about how."

* * *

Getting her there was a comedy she would have laughed at, in another life, if she'd had the breath. The scribe — Orsen, she'd heard a guard mutter the name, Orsen Almeida, third ledger desk, no family listed, which was itself interesting — half-carried, half-dragged her through a corridor of whitewashed stone while she tried to keep her leaking light cupped against her side like spilled soup.

"You're getting it on the floor," Orsen hissed.

"I'm getting it on everything. It's a whole thing."

The scriptorium was long and low and smelled of oak gall and tallow, rows of desks each with its little lamp, its little pot of ink, its little carefully walled-in life. Beatrix felt the stillness of the place press against her skin like a hand. This close to the valley's heart, the old suppression lay thick as felt over everything. Her light dimmed just being here. Which was, she supposed, the point. The founders had built their prison well.

They laid her down between two desks, and Orsen knelt beside her, and for a moment neither of them spoke. Somewhere above, a bell marked the hour with its soft, toneless clank — even the bells in this valley refused to ring properly.

"I need to tell you things quickly," Beatrix said, "because I am running out of quickly."

Orsen's jaw worked. "I should fetch a physician."

"A physician will look at me, see light coming out of my ribs, and call the Order. And then we'll both die, only one of us more slowly." She caught their wrist. Her fingers left faint gold prints on their skin, like the memory of sunlight. "Listen to me. What is your name?"

"...You know my name. You said it in the courtyard."

"I want to hear you say it. Names matter where I come from. They matter more than you know." She held their gaze. "Say it."

A long pause. Then, quietly, the way a person says a word they've been taught is dangerous: "Orsen Almeida."

"Good," Beatrix breathed. "Oh, that's good. Do you know why that's good? Because your name fits. I've carried a list of names for eleven years, Orsen Almeida, names of everyone born in this valley with the old blood still in them, and yours is the last one left that the stillness hasn't chewed soft. Everyone else — faded. Assimilated. Happy, even." She smiled, and knew it must have been a terrible smile. "You're the last unspoiled thread. Isn't that funny? All those hundreds of scribes copying ledgers, and the covenant picks the one who can't even afford shoes with soles."

Orsen looked down at their boots. One sole flapped slightly when they walked. Something in their face flickered — offense, absurdly, cutting through the fear — and Beatrix thought: *yes. There you are.*

"What covenant?" Orsen said. "What are you talking about? Magic doesn't exist here. It can't. That's the whole—"

"That's the whole *lie*, yes. Say it with me: magic doesn't exist in the Valley of Stillness. Very soothing. Very tidy. Now ask yourself why a valley needs a rule against a thing that doesn't exist. You don't post guards against the weather that isn't coming. You don't build an Order—" she watched them flinch; so they'd heard of the Order, good, good, "—to keep out nothing. The valley isn't empty of magic, child. It's *full* of it. Stuffed full, packed down, pressed under the stones like flowers in a book. Your founders didn't escape magic. They stole it. Every drop. And they've been sitting on it for three hundred years, calling the silence peace."

The lamp flames along the desks bent toward her as she spoke, all of them, all at once, like a congregation leaning in. Orsen stared at them. Then at her. Then at the flames again, with the expression of a person watching their entire understanding of the world attempt to stand up and walk away.

"That's not possible," Orsen whispered.

"Mm. I said that too, once. To a woman named Maren, who was dying in exactly this position, saying exactly these things." Beatrix coughed, and the cough came up gold-flecked, and she saw Orsen's eyes go wide. "She was the heir before me. I was her scribe. Does that sound familiar? It should. The covenant has a sense of humor. It's a cruel one, but it's consistent."

* * *

There wasn't time for all of it. There was never time for all of it. Eleven years of searching, of reading burned archives by candlelight in towns that had sold their memories, of learning the shape of the theft — the three shrines, the hollowed altars, the names taken from living mouths to feed the prison spell — and she had perhaps a quarter hour of breath left to spend.

So she spent it the way Maren had spent hers: badly, too fast, pressing everything into hands that hadn't asked for any of it.

"Give me your arm," she said.

"Why?"

"Because I'm about to do something to you that you are absolutely going to object to, and I would prefer you object to my face rather than to my ghost."

To her mild surprise, Orsen gave her their arm. Perhaps shock made them obedient. Perhaps — and she filed this away as the small warm coal she would carry into whatever came after dying — perhaps some part of them had already decided to believe her. People always knew, underneath. People always knew when the floor of their world was false. They built their lives on it anyway, because a false floor holds weight better than truth does, but they *knew*.

Her blood-light rose to her fingertips. The map woke under her skin — she felt it stir, the great slow living thing she'd carried for eleven years, older than her, older than Maren, older than the theft itself. It had been drawn in the first age of the covenant by hands that wrote in light, and it remembered every shrine, every border-stone, every name that had ever been given freely to hold two worlds together. When the covenant broke, the map had broken with it, and it had been passing itself from bearer to bearer ever since, healing a little and hurting a lot, looking for the one it could finish itself inside.

"This will hurt," she told Orsen honestly. "I won't lie to you. Lying to the heir is the one tradition I refuse to keep."

"Wait," Orsen said. "Do what? What will hur—"

Beatrix pressed her palm flat against their forearm and let go.

The transfer took four seconds and lasted, she suspected, considerably longer in Orsen's experience of it. Light poured from her hand into their skin the way water finds the cracks in stone, and the map went with it — she felt it leave her the way you feel a tooth leave your jaw, that shocking absence, that sudden lightness that is also loss. Orsen made a sound that was not quite a scream, more the sound a scream makes when it's strangled by discipline, and their whole body locked, and the gold spread up their arm in branching lines like frost blooming across a window, like rivers filling in on a chart, like — yes, exactly like that. Like a map drawing itself in the only ink that lasts, which is a person.

Then it was done, and she fell back against the desk leg, nearly empty, nearly cold, and watched the heir of the broken covenant sit on the floor of a stolen valley and hyperventilate.

"It's under my skin," Orsen said. Their voice came out very high and very controlled, the voice of a person holding a door shut against a flood. "There's a *map* under my *skin*. I can feel it. It's — it moved. Why did it move. Maps don't move."

"They do if they're alive," Beatrix said. "Yours is. It's the oldest living document in either world. Treat it kindly. It remembers more grief than either of us has years."

"I didn't ask for this." Orsen's composure cracked clean through, and underneath it was just a young person, terrified, ink-stained, alone. "I didn't *ask*. I copy ledgers. I owe this temple six hundred crowns. I have a *routine*. I get up, I grind ink, I copy, I eat, I sleep, nobody looks at me, and that's — that was the *point*, that was the whole point of everything, and now there's a map in my arm and you're telling me about stolen magic and I don't — I never wanted to be *chosen* for anything. Being chosen is just being *blamed* with better vocabulary."

Beatrix laughed. It cost her most of what remained, and it was worth every coin.

"Oh, heir," she said softly. "Yes. Exactly that. You understand it already, then. Better than I did at first. Maren told me I was chosen and I thanked her. *Thanked* her, like a fool, like it was an honor instead of a debt with a nicer name." She reached up, and Orsen — after a visible war with themselves — leaned down enough to let her touch their cheek. Her hand left a faint golden print that faded slowly, like warmth leaving a window. "Hear me now, and hold onto it when everything gets loud: you can refuse. Did you know that? Everyone thinks the covenant binds. It doesn't. It only *asks*. It asked Maren. She said yes and it broke her. It asked me. I said yes and it brought me here, to die in a room that smells of ink, which at least is a room I like. It will ask you, Orsen Almeida, and you can say no, and the map will sleep in your skin and bother no one, and the valley can keep its quiet a hundred years more, maybe two. No one will blame you. Well. I will, a little, from wherever I end up, but I'll be dead, so my opinion will hardly carry weight."

"That's not comforting."

"I'm not a comforting person. I'm a dying one. We're allowed to be honest; it's in the rules."

Orsen sat back on their heels. The gold lines under their skin had settled to a faint shimmer, visible only when the lamplight caught them right — a secret written on the inside of their wrist, their elbow, the crook of their shoulder. They were staring at their own arm as though it belonged to a stranger. In a sense, Beatrix thought, it did now. It belonged to everyone the valley had ever silenced.

"The covenant broke," Orsen said at last, slowly, tasting each word. "You said that. In the courtyard. What does — what happens if it stays broken?"

"Nothing," Beatrix said. "For a while. That's the horror of it, really. Nothing happens. The world goes on. The valley keeps its stillness, the realms drift apart, the shrines stay hollow. And then, one day — not soon, but not far — the last thread frays through. And the stolen magic doesn't stay pressed anymore. Three hundred years of it, all at once, with nowhere to go and no covenant to shape it." She let the image sit between them. "Your valley won't burn, heir. Burning is quick. It'll drown. Stillness, all at once, everywhere, forever. Every mind in the valley going quiet as the ledgers you copy. Your founders' theft doesn't fail loudly. It fails *politely*."

The lamps guttered. Outside, far off, a horn sounded — one note, then another, the Order's hunting-call, and both of them went still. Closer than she'd estimated. The Order had better noses for light than she'd credited.

"How long do I have?" Orsen asked. Not *how long do you have.* Beatrix noticed that. Noticed, and loved them a little for it, the way you love a stranger who turns out to be kind without meaning to be.

"Time is short," she said. "That's the truest sentence I own. Days, perhaps, before the Order traces the transfer. Weeks before the map finishes waking and starts pulling you — it pulls, heir, it has opinions, it will want to show you the shrines. Follow it or don't. But learn to read it first. It speaks in memory. My memory, mostly, and Maren's, and others back and back. You'll grieve people you never met. Let yourself. It's the only inheritance anyone ever leaves that's worth the carrying."

"And you?" Orsen asked, and their voice gentled. "What happens to you?"

Beatrix looked around the little scriptorium — the neat desks, the patient lamps, the young person kneeling in another person's blood-light because no one else in this whole walled garden had dared. Dying here, she thought, was not the worst ending. Maren had died on a mountainside alone. She got a scribe with flapping boot-soles and a spine that had surprised them both.

"I go where the map was," she said. "Wherever that is. Probably somewhere with better lighting." She fumbled at her neck and drew out the cord she'd worn for eleven years — a plain iron key on a leather thong, ugly as sin, warm as a heartbeat. She pressed it into Orsen's palm and closed their fingers over it. "Third shrine. Under the mountain. Don't lose it. Don't trust anyone who smiles easily. And when a wolf finds you — and he will find you, he's probably found you already, he's insufferable that way — do not run. Running tells him you think you're guilty. Stand still and be rude. He respects rudeness."

"A wolf."

"A wolf-spirit. Judge of the old covenant. Sentimental under all the teeth, but if you repeat that I said so, I will haunt you specifically."

The horn again. Nearer. Orsen's head turned toward the door, and Beatrix watched them weigh it — the door, the corridor, the safe gray life humming along on the other side of the wall — and she watched them turn back. Not choosing yet. But weighing. The scales had been moved; that was all the covenant had ever needed from anyone. Just the moving.

"One last thing," she whispered. The cold was in her feet now, climbing, patient as the tide. "When they ask you — and they will ask, the elders, the inquisitors, everyone with a stake in the quiet — when they offer to make it all go away, to give you back your ledgers and your debts and your invisible little life — remember what it cost to build that life. Remember whose stillness you were sleeping in." Her vision was going soft at the edges, the lamplight blooming. "Peace that's stolen isn't peace, heir. It's just a theft with good manners."

"Beatrix." They said her name — she hadn't told them, had she? No matter; the map told them things now; they'd be telling each other things for years, in a manner of speaking — and their hand closed hard around hers. "Beatrix, I don't know how to do any of this."

"Nobody does," she said. "That's what makes it a covenant and not a recipe."

And because she was dying, and dying people are permitted one indulgence, she smiled at the heir of the broken covenant — the last unspoiled thread, the reluctant one, the one with ink to the elbows — and said:

"Ask the wolf about the ford. He pretends he lost that fight on purpose. He did not."

The light in her went out the way a lamp goes out when a hand cups it: gently, completely, with the warmth lingering after.

* * *

Beatrix had expected dying to be quieter than this.

Instead there was a courtyard, and shouting, and a young scribe standing over her body with their arms spread wide and their voice cracking, yelling at armed men that she was a beggar, a fever case, nothing, *nobody* — lying, badly, beautifully, with their new map-hand hidden behind their back and gold light already seeping between their fingers.

*Oh no,* Beatrix thought, from wherever she now was, watching. *Oh, heir. You terrible liar. You magnificent, doomed, unspoiled thing.*

*Run.*

She had a feeling they couldn't hear her.

She had a feeling they were going to anyway.
