## 4. Debt Collectors

Orsen had always believed that the worst sound in the Valley of Stillness was the bell that marked the hour of rising. It was a cruel, tin-voiced thing, and it hated sleepers the way some people hate rain.

They were wrong about that. The worst sound was a knock at their cell door at an hour when no honest knock was ever made.

Three raps. Evenly spaced. The kind of knock that had already decided what it wanted before it arrived.

Orsen sat up in the dark, heart going like a moth against glass. The map burned under their skin — there was no other word for it. For two days they had felt it crawling along their forearm, up past the elbow, a warmth like sunlight through a window that shouldn't exist. They had wrapped it in linen bandages and told everyone who asked that they'd scalded themselves at the ink-fire. People believed this. Orsen was very good at being believed; it was the whole architecture of their life.

The knock came again.

"One moment," Orsen said, in the voice of a person who had merely been asleep, and not lying awake cataloguing every way this could end.

They dressed. They looked at the little room that had been theirs for eleven years: the cot, the shelf of borrowed books, the cup with the chipped rim they'd never been allowed to replace because replacement required requisition and requisition required paperwork and paperwork was Orsen's own job, so really they had only themselves to blame.

They opened the door.

Brother Cassian stood there, which was bad. Behind him stood two men from the Office of Accounts, which was worse. And behind *them*, at the end of the corridor where the lamplight gave out, stood Elder Maren herself, small and grey and still as a stone in a stream, which meant that everything Orsen had ever carefully built was already on fire and nobody had bothered to tell them.

"Brother Orsen," said Cassian. He was a soft man, fond of cheese and gossip in equal measure, and he could not quite meet Orsen's eyes. "The elders would like a word."

"At this hour?"

"The elders would like a word," he repeated, with the gentle stubbornness of a man reciting something he'd been made to memorize.

* * *

The counting room at night was a different animal than by day. By day it was all shuffling paper and the dry cough of Brother Pell and someone always burning the midday porridge. At night it was vast and cold, lit by a single lamp, and the ledgers lay open on the great table like sleeping birds.

Elder Maren sat behind them. She did not ask Orsen to sit. That was the first sign.

"Do you know," she said, "how long I have served this temple?"

"Thirty-one years," Orsen said. "It's in the annals. I copied them."

Something flickered across her face — not quite a smile. "You did. Your hand is very fine. One of the finest we've produced." She folded her hands. "Which is why it grieves me that you thought we wouldn't recognize it."

"I don't—"

"Two nights ago," Maren said, "a woman died in the scriptorium courtyard. A stranger. The guards, following standing orders, saw nothing, heard nothing, and reported nothing, because that is what our orders are *for*." Her voice stayed level, unhurried, the voice of a woman reading a shopping list aloud. "But bodies must be dealt with, even here. Especially here. And when the body-tenders prepared her for the ash-pit, they found this."

She lifted a scrap of parchment from the ledger table. On it, in Beatrix's failing hand, were three words:

*He carries it.*

"They also found," Maren continued, "that the woman's blood had glowed. Which is impossible. There is no magic in the valley. There has never been magic in the valley. So the tenders assumed a trick of the light, and I would have assumed the same, except that yesterday morning Brother Cassian noticed you wince when you reached for an inkpot. And last night, while you slept, the wardens of the gate report that the boundary stones hummed. All nine of them. In unison." She paused. "Show me your arm, Brother Orsen."

Every sensible instinct Orsen possessed — and they possessed a great many, honed over years of being small and quiet and owed-to — screamed at them to lie. To laugh. To say *wardens hear humming the way sailors hear mermaids, Elder, too much salt pork before bed.* Lying had kept them alive for thirty years.

But the map chose that moment to pulse. Warmth rolled up Orsen's arm and into their chest, and the lamp flame leaned toward them like a curious child, and Elder Maren's eyes went to the flame, and then to Orsen, and Orsen understood that the lie had just become a physical impossibility.

Slowly, they unwound the bandage.

The map moved under their skin. Lines of silver-gold script crawled from wrist to shoulder, branching, blooming, and where it passed the lamplight caught it and threw faint letters onto the wall — letters in no alphabet the valley used, glowing softly, patient as embers.

Nobody spoke for a long time. The two men from the Office of Accounts had gone pale. One of them quietly put down his inkhorn, as though ink might be contagious.

"So," said Elder Maren at last. "It's true, then. The covenant heir." She said the word *heir* the way one says *plague* or *flood*.

"I didn't ask for it," Orsen said. Their voice came out smaller than they wanted. "She was dying. She pressed it into my hands and — I don't even know how. I didn't consent. I didn't—"

"No," Maren agreed. "You didn't. That is, in its way, the tragedy of it." She rose, and came around the table, and looked at Orsen's arm without touching it, the way one looks at a snake one hopes is dead. "Do you know what your debt was, Orsen Almeida? The full sum?"

"Four hundred and twelve marks, six coppers," Orsen said automatically. "Accrued from my mother's healing-fees, interest compounded annually at—"

"Four hundred and twelve marks, six coppers," Maren confirmed. "Eleven years of copying to work it down. You had — let me see — nineteen years remaining. You would have been fifty-three when the ledger closed. Did you ever resent it?"

The question was so unexpected that Orsen answered honestly. "Every day."

"Good," said Maren. "Honest resentment is the only honest thing most of us have." She returned to her chair and sat, folding her hands again, and now her voice changed — softened, almost, which frightened Orsen more than anything else so far. "Here is what happens now. The law of the valley is older than me and older than this temple, and it says: *what carries magic cannot remain.* Not because we are cruel. Because we are *safe*. We bought our safety a long time ago at a price recorded nowhere, and the interest on *that* debt, Orsen, makes yours look like pocket change."

"You're saying I'm expelled."

"I'm saying you have a choice, which is more than most get." She slid a sheet of parchment across the table. On it, two lines, written in the elder's own careful hand.

"*The bearer of the vessel may submit to severance,*" Orsen read aloud, and stopped. "Severance."

"The arm," said one of the Account men, helpfully, and then, at Orsen's look, added, "and possibly somewhat more than the arm. The ritual is old. The margins are... unclear."

"Or exile," Maren said. "Tonight, before dawn, before the gate-wardens change shift. You will be walked to the border stone and turned out, and the valley will forget you, and the ledgers will record that the debt of Orsen Almeida was voided by death of the debtor." She met Orsen's eyes. "I am told that is called mercy. I have never entirely believed it, but it is what we have."

Orsen stared at the parchment. Eleven years. Eleven years of cold mornings and ink-stained fingers and the chipped cup and Brother Pell's terrible porridge and the particular silence of the scriptorium at dusk, which Orsen had spent a decade convincing themselves was peace. Gone. Not taken — *voided*. As if they had never owed anything, which meant as if they had never been owed anything either, which meant as if none of it had ever counted.

"And if I refuse both?" they asked. "If I just... stay? Hide it?"

Maren's face did something complicated — grief and duty wrestling briefly behind her eyes, and duty winning, as it always does in institutions. "Then the Order of the Quiet comes, and they do not offer choices. I have seen what they leave. I would spare you that, child, if you'll let me."

*Child.* She had never once called them that in eleven years. It nearly undid them more than the rest of it combined.

* * *

They gave Orsen one hour.

That was, in its grim way, generous. Orsen packed in a daze: the good pen (theirs by three years of faithful service), a spare shirt, the book of valley folk-tales they'd copied out as an apprentice and kept ever since, though technically it belonged to the library and taking it was theft, and Orsen found they did not care even slightly, which was itself a strange new feeling, like discovering a room in a house you'd lived in all your life.

The chipped cup they left on the shelf. Someone else would inherit it. Someone else would hate it and then, without noticing, love it.

At the door of the cell, they stopped. Looked back. The room was so small it took half a second to see all of it, and somehow that half-second was the longest of Orsen's life.

"Well," they said aloud, to nobody, because humor was the only weapon they'd ever owned. "That's that, then."

Their voice cracked on *that*, and they pretended it hadn't, and went out.

Cassian walked them to the gate. Of course it was Cassian; the elders knew exactly how much cruelty the messenger could be spared. He carried a small bundle he pressed into Orsen's hands at the threshold: bread, hard cheese, and — Orsen huffed despite everything — a wedge of the good smoked cheese from the elder's own table.

"Cassian, you'll be flogged."

"I'll be *questioned*," he corrected. "Flogging requires evidence, and I intend to eat the evidence." His eyes were wet and he was smiling and he gripped Orsen's shoulder once, hard. "Whatever she wrote in you — whatever it is —" He faltered. "Be careful. And write to us. Somehow. If the world outside allows letters."

"It doesn't," said the gate-warden flatly.

"Then invent a way," Cassian said. "You're a scribe. Inventing ways is the whole profession."

And then the gate was opening, and the night air of the outside came in — smelling of pine and rain and something else, something green and wild and utterly unledgered — and Orsen stepped through, and the gate closed behind them with a sound like a full stop.

* * *

The road away from the valley was nothing but moonlight and gravel for a long while. Orsen walked because walking was a thing the body knew how to do when the mind had resigned.

Behind them, the valley lay in its bowl of hills, dark and silent and safe, the lamps of the temple just visible, tiny and warm, like a hearth seen from a drowning ship. Orsen watched it until the road bent, and then watched the place where it had been.

They had expected to cry. What actually happened was stranger: the map woke fully.

Heat poured up their arm, and the silver lines blazed through their sleeve, and suddenly Orsen could *see* — not with their eyes but with something older — the shape of the land around them. The road was a vein. The hills were ribs. Far off to the north, something vast and slow turned over in its sleep, and far to the south something bright and broken throbbed like an unhealed wound, and threaded through everything, everywhere, was a hum — the same hum the boundary stones had made — as if the world were a struck bell that had been muffled for a thousand years and was only now remembering its note.

"Oh," Orsen said, to the empty road, to the burning arm, to the woman who had done this to them with her last breath. "Oh, you could have *asked*."

The map pulsed, and for one absurd instant Orsen would have sworn it felt like laughter.

They walked on. Behind them, the valley slept on in its stolen stillness, and ahead the dark world hummed, and somewhere between the two — ink-stained, homeless, carrying four hundred and twelve marks of debt that no longer existed and a debt that very much did — Orsen Almeida began, at last, to run.

They did not see the eyes watching from the treeline. Yellow, patient, and old beyond the valley's oldest lie.

But the eyes saw them.
