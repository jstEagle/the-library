## 5. The Border of No Magic

Orsen had never been outside the valley before.

This was not an unusual thing to say in the Valley of Stillness. Most people hadn't. The valley was shaped like a cupped hand, ringed by hills that were themselves ringed by mountains that were themselves, according to the temple maps (which Orsen had copied so many times they could draw them blindfolded and once, memorably, had), ringed by a world nobody much talked about. The valley was the world. Everything else was margin.

For twenty-six years, Orsen Almeida had lived comfortably in the center of the page.

Now they stood at the edge of it, at the place where the pilgrim road narrowed between two leaning stones — the *Wardstones*, the ledgers called them, though no ledger ever explained what they warded against or why — and discovered that their legs had opinions about the matter. Specifically, that they would prefer not to.

"Come on," Orsen told them quietly. "You've walked me to the scriptorium every morning for eleven years. This is just a longer commute."

Their legs did not find this persuasive.

The sun was going down behind them, which meant the light fell forward into the world beyond the stones, laying long gold fingers across a road that unspooled down through scrubland toward a horizon with actual *weather* on it. Clouds. Real ones, stacked up like unwashed dishes. In the valley, weather arrived politely, pre-approved, the same four seasons every year like a liturgy. Out there, apparently, the sky just did whatever it wanted.

Orsen's satchel held everything they owned now, which was a humbling inventory: one spare robe, half a loaf of bread gone hard as scripture, a stub of ink-pencil, three quills (a person has priorities), and the small clay pot of lamp-black they'd stolen from the scriptorium on the way out, because if you were going to be exiled for carrying magic, you might as well also be exiled for theft. It gave the exile a certain symmetry.

And under their skin, coiled along the left forearm from wrist to elbow, the map burned.

It had been quiet since the temple. Quiet the way a held breath is quiet. Orsen had tried to look at it in the reflection of a cattle trough outside Tarnwick — the last village, all shuttered windows and people who suddenly remembered urgent business indoors when a fugitive shuffled past — but skin is a poor mirror, and all they'd seen was a faint silver shimmer, like moonlight caught in water, arranged in lines too orderly to be a scar.

*Heir,* Beatrix had called them. Dying in the courtyard, bleeding light, pressing something into their arm that wasn't ink and wasn't wound and was somehow both.

*The covenant broke. Time is short.*

Orsen had spent four days walking and not thinking about that sentence, which was a full-time occupation requiring tremendous discipline. They thought instead about smaller things. About how Elder Cassian's voice had gone very soft when he'd said *your debt is void*, the way a knife goes soft right before it isn't. About how the guards who'd looked away from Beatrix in the courtyard had not looked away from Orsen. About how the temple bell had rung vespers as they fled through the orchards, and how they had hated it, and how they had, horribly, missed it already.

About how home could be a thing that tried to kill you and still be home. That was the one they kept circling back to, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

"Right," Orsen said aloud, because the silence between the Wardstones was getting ideas. "I'm going to walk through these stones now. And nothing is going to happen. Because magic can't cross into the valley, and I am standing in the valley, and I have been tested by three different priests with three different instruments and all of them said I was as magical as a turnip."

This was true. Every child in the valley was tested at age seven — a formality, like the debt-ledger signing at twelve, like the marriage registry at twenty, like all the other small papers that bound a life flat and neat. Orsen's test had produced a reading so flat the priest had laughed and written *exemplary* in the margin. Exemplary stillness. It was, Orsen reflected bitterly, the only commendation they had ever received from the temple, and it had turned out to be a lie.

They put one foot between the Wardstones.

Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened. They were still inside; the border ran somewhere ahead, a line only visible —

There.

It was visible. That was the first wrong thing. Between the stones, where the road dipped and rose again, the air itself changed color — not dramatically, nothing a painter would bother with, but the way a page changes when you hold it up to the light and see the watermark. A seam. A faint vertical shimmer, knee-high to hip-high, like heat off summer stone except it didn't move with the air. It simply *was*, patient as a rule, and Orsen realized with a lurch that they had spent their whole life a few miles from this line and had never once seen it, because you cannot see the wall of a room you have never left.

"Oh," Orsen said. Their voice came out very small in the wide evening. "Oh, that's real."

The map woke.

Not gradually. Not politely. One moment it was a held breath under their skin; the next it was a struck bell. Silver light flared along their forearm, blazing up out of their sleeve, and Orsen staggered back from the border clutching their arm like it had been stung — which it had, or something adjacent to stinging, a sensation like cold water and hot needles and someone writing on you from the inside.

"*Ow* — fine — *fine,*" they hissed at their own arm, which continued to glow with what Orsen could only describe as enthusiasm. "Yes! I see it! You don't have to — we're going, aren't we. You want to go through."

The light pulsed. If light could smugly confirm a suspicion, this light did.

"You're a map," Orsen informed it, with the dignity available to a person arguing with their own skin on an empty road at dusk. "Maps do not get opinions."

But Beatrix had bled light into them and called them heir, and heir to what, if not to whatever this was? And the map — Beatrix's map, Beatrix's memory, Beatrix's last desperate act pressed into a stranger because there had been no one else, because time was short — the map wanted through the border the way Orsen wanted, despite everything, despite the bell and the orchards and the smell of the scriptorium's drying ink, to know why.

Orsen took a breath. Then another. Then, because the first two hadn't helped, a third.

"I'm Orsen Almeida," they told the seam in the air, formally, the way you'd address a superior officer or a particularly intimidating door. "Debt-number 4471-C, scribe, third class. Formerly. I don't actually know what I'm doing. But I'm doing it anyway."

Which was, they suspected, the most honest prayer they had ever offered.

They stepped through.

* * *

Later, Orsen would try to describe it to Vharen, and fail, and try again, and eventually settle on: *it was like hearing your own name after years of being called 'you there.'*

In the moment, there were no words. There was only this:

Crossing the line was like surfacing. Like spending a lifetime underwater without knowing you were drowning, and then breaking through into air so rich with sound and signal and *meaning* that the first breath of it hurt. The world beyond the Wardstones was — loud. Not with noise. With *hum*. A deep, layered, chorded hum that came up through the ground and down from the sky and sideways out of every living thing, grass and gorse and distant pines and the fat contented beetles going about their beetle business in the darkening soil, each one singing its small note into a vast ongoing music that had apparently been playing forever, everywhere, on the far side of a line drawn by frightened men.

And underneath the hum, threaded through it, the map sang back.

Orsen dropped to their knees in the dirt on the far side of the border, because their knees had decided the situation required kneeling, and clutched their glowing arm, and laughed — actually laughed, a cracked and startled sound — because it was beautiful. It was unbearable. It was both at once, the way grief and joy are often the same sensation wearing different clothes.

Lines of silver script rose off their skin and hung in the twilight air, hovering, rearranging themselves. Not words. Not yet. Shapes — rivers, roads, ranges — drawn in light, and around them annotations blooming and fading like breath on glass, and beneath everything a slow pulse, patient as a heartbeat, that Orsen understood without knowing how they understood was the map taking its bearings. Finding itself. Finding *them*. Checking its new bearer against the world like a key against a lock.

"It knows where we are," Orsen whispered. "It always knew where we are. It was just — asleep. We were asleep." They swallowed. "*I* was asleep."

Because here was the vertigo of it, arriving on schedule and knocking their sense of self sideways: the valley's stillness wasn't empty. Stillness implied an absence, a quiet room. But standing here, hearing the world's true volume for the first time, Orsen understood that the valley's silence was not an absence at all. It was a *muffling*. Something enormous had been wrapped around their whole life like cloth around a bell, and everyone inside had agreed, generation after generation, to call the muffling peace.

Every hymn. Every ledger. Every flat little test that pronounced a seven-year-old exemplary in her emptiness. All of it conducted inside the cloth.

"How many of us," Orsen asked the hanging light, and the light had no answer, only pulsed gently, which was somehow worse, "how many of us never got to hear this?"

The script drifted closer, settled over their arm like frost forming in reverse, and went dim and quiet — not gone, just folded away, a book closed but marked. And in the folding, Orsen felt something else arrive: a direction. Not a compulsion. An inclination, the way water inclines downhill. South and west, the map suggested, without saying so. Toward the hills. Toward the first of whatever sites Beatrix had died trying to reach.

Toward answers, or toward more questions wearing answers' clothing. With Orsen's luck lately, the latter.

They sat in the road for a while as true dark came down and the stars came out — *thicker* out here, crowded, humming faintly in keys too high to hear properly — and ate half the rock-hard loaf, and let themselves feel it, all of it: the terror, the wonder, the bone-deep homesickness for a place that had sentenced them to death, the stranger and more painful homesickness for a place they'd never been, some self that had existed before the cloth came down, some Orsen-shaped space in the music waiting to be filled.

"Exemplary," they muttered, and laughed again, wetter this time. "Turnip."

Then they slept badly, as befitted a fugitive, waking at every owl-hoot convinced it was debt-collectors, and set off south and west at gray dawn with their hood up and their heart doing something new and unsupervised in their chest.

* * *

Two days of walking taught Orsen several things.

First: the world outside was bigger than the maps said, and Orsen had *copied the maps*, so this was professionally insulting. Second: kindness was more common out here than the temple claimed, though it came with squinting — a farmwife gave them bread and watched them the whole time they ate it; a carter let them ride in the straw and asked no fewer than nine questions, six of them variations on *where are you from, exactly.* Third: whenever Orsen passed near anything old — a boundary oak, a leaning cairn, a bridge with moss-eaten carvings — the map stirred under their skin and hummed a low approving note, like a dog scenting home, and Orsen was beginning to compile a list.

Fourth: they were being followed.

They noticed it on the second evening, in the way you notice weather changing — not a sight or a sound but a pressure, a prickle along the neck. The road bent along a ridge above a river valley, all bracken and shadow, and Orsen stopped, ostensibly to retie a bootlace that needed no retying, and listened with ears that were, thanks to two days outside the cloth, embarrassingly better than they used to be.

Wind in the bracken. River below. Beetles, singing.

And underneath it, at the edge of hearing, a rhythm that was not a beetle and not the wind: footsteps. Large ones. Unhurried. Keeping pace with theirs, thirty yards off the road, patient as dusk.

Orsen's mouth went dry. Debt-collectors didn't range this far — surely? The temple's writ ended at the Wardstones; even Cassian's soft voice couldn't follow past the border. Could it?

The map flared.

Not alarm — Orsen was learning its dialects, and this was not the sting of warning. This was the low thrum of recognition, the same note it gave for the old oaks and cairns, but deeper, richer, threaded with something that felt absurdly like *anticipation*. The silver lines surfaced along their forearm and pointed — actually pointed, a thin bright tendril extending off their wrist like a compass needle with convictions — straight into the dark bracken where the footsteps were.

"No," Orsen said. "No, absolutely not. We do not point at the large hidden thing. That is the opposite of what maps are for."

The tendril did not waver.

The bracken parted.

It was a wolf. That was the first fact, delivered with great clarity to the older, wiser parts of Orsen's brain, the parts that predated ledgers: *wolf, large, teeth, run.* It stood at the edge of the bracken in the last red light, and it was the size of a yearling calf, gray as slate rain, with eyes that caught the dying sun and held it — amber, ancient, and unbearably *aware* in a way no animal's eyes should be. It looked at Orsen the way Elder Cassian read a ledger: thoroughly, finding everything, missing nothing.

The map sang. Joyfully. Helpfully. *Him,* it seemed to say. *Him him him.*

"Him?" Orsen breathed. "Who's *him*?"

The wolf tilted its head. And then, in a voice like gravel poured slowly over velvet — a voice that came from everywhere and nowhere and, impossibly, from the wolf's open jaw — it said:

"So. The heir finally leaves the nursery."

Orsen's entire body performed a maneuver best described as *attempting to flee in all directions simultaneously* and achieving none of them. They tripped over their own satchel strap, sat down hard in the bracken, and looked up at the enormous spirit from the undignified vantage of the ground, which at least had the advantage of making it seem larger.

"You talk," Orsen said.

"You glow," the wolf replied. "We all have our embarrassments."

The map pulsed warmly against Orsen's arm, insufferably pleased with itself, and far to the north — too far to matter yet, but coming, coming — the bells of the Valley of Stillness rang vespers into a silence that, for the first time in Orsen's life, they could hear from the outside: small, muffled, and terribly far away.

The wolf stepped closer, and lowered its great head, and studied them with eyes that had been watching the valley's theft for centuries.

"We need to talk," said Vharen, "about what you're carrying. And about what it's going to cost you."
