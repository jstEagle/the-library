## 8. The Town That Forgot Its Name

The map had opinions about where we should sleep.

It had been expressing them all afternoon, in the way it expressed most things: a slow warmth spreading across my left forearm like a cat settling into a sunbeam, then a tug, faint but insistent, pulling my attention northwest toward a fold in the hills. I had learned to read the map's moods the way one learns a housemate's — not because anyone explained it, but because living in close quarters leaves you no choice.

"Not yet," I told it. "We're being followed. We stop when there's walls."

Vharen, loping beside me in his wolf-shape, gave me a look I had come to recognize as *you are negotiating with your own skin again*. His eyes were the color of deep water and about as easy to argue with.

"I know how it sounds," I said. "But the map has never once steered me wrong. Well. It steered me into a bog last week, but that was clearly a punishment for complaining about its handwriting."

The map's warmth pulsed once, which I chose to interpret as dignity rather than guilt.

* * *

I should explain what I was, by then. Three weeks out from the Valley of Stillness, three weeks since the temple elders had looked at the glowing lines crawling up my arm and pronounced me dead to them in the polite voice they used for funerals and tax assessments. Three weeks since Vharen had stepped out of the treeline like a shadow deciding to have teeth.

I was a fugitive with a dead woman's life written into my flesh, accompanied by a spirit who had been sent to judge me and had apparently failed to reach a verdict. He stayed. When I asked him why — once, at a fire, in the small hours — he'd said only, "The judgment isn't finished," and gone back to watching the dark. I'd decided not to press. Some questions are like poking sleeping animals; you can do it, but you'll deserve what happens next.

So we walked. And on the evening of the twenty-third day out of the valley, the map stopped tugging northwest and began instead to burn — a bright, urgent heat, the first time it had ever raised its voice.

"Or here," I said, blinking at the hills ahead. "Here is also acceptable."

There was a town in the fold of the hills. I want to be precise about this, because for the rest of my life I would be certain of what I saw and unable to prove any of it: there was a town, with slate roofs going blue in the dusk, chimney smoke standing straight up in the still air, lamplight beginning in windows, maybe forty houses around a well and a squat stone church with no symbol on it at all. A town that existed as surely as my own two hands.

And no road leading to it.

There were paths — footpaths, worn smooth — but no cart-road, no milestone, no signpost. The land around it lay fallow in neat strips, tended, but nothing grew that needed carrying anywhere. A town built to be invisible, or nearly so, or perhaps just built to need no one.

"They're not on the map," I said, unrolling Beatrix's old paper copy across my knees as we crouched on the ridge. The living map under my skin hummed; the paper one, inked in her hand years ago, showed hills, a river, a shrine marked with an X that made my stomach tighten — but between the river and the hills, blankness. "She mapped everything else. Every farmstead, every ruined watchtower. But not this."

Vharen's ears swiveled forward. "A town that paid to be forgotten," he said. "They exist."

"You know of them?"

"I know of the practice." His voice, even through the wolf's throat, carried that careful flatness he used when a subject displeased him. "In the centuries after the covenant broke, some border folk found ways to hide. Bargains. Small magics, bought at cost."

"What cost?"

"Come and see," he said, which was not an answer, and we went down anyway, because the map was burning and night was falling and a man can only refuse so many things at once.

* * *

The first person we met was a woman drawing water at the well, and she looked at us the way people in the valley looked at strangers: politely, briefly, and with a firm decision already made behind her eyes.

"You'll be wanting the inn," she said.

"There's an inn?"

"There's an inn." She set down her buckets. Up close I could see she was perhaps fifty, with a broad, capable face and eyes that kept sliding away from mine as though my face were a page she'd already finished reading. "You came over the ridge, did you. From the east."

"Yes."

"Mm." She studied my sleeve, where the map's glow had been damped under wrappings. "You'll want to keep that covered here. Not a rule. Just kindness." And she picked up her buckets and walked away, unhurried, without another word, leaving me holding the strange sensation of having been warned and welcomed and dismissed all in a single breath.

The inn stood where inns stand, near the well, two stories of timber and stone with a sign hanging outside. I squinted at the sign in the lamplight. It was a picture of a fish, painted and repainted many times, the colors gone soft as old fruit.

"It doesn't say the inn's name," I said.

"No name to say," said Vharen.

"That's not — towns have names. This one has to have a name. Someone named it something. Even if it's terrible. Especially if it's terrible. Terrible names survive better than good ones; look at the village of Bogbottom in the valley, four hundred years and nobody has managed to rename it—"

"Orsen." He nodded toward the door. "Inside."

Inside was warm and smelled of bread and woodsmoke, and inside was also where I learned the shape of the bargain.

The innkeeper was a round man with flour on his forearms who greeted us like weather — acknowledged, accommodated, not especially interesting. He gave us a room without asking names, took coin without counting it, and when I asked, casually, what the town was called, he smiled at me with genuine warmth and said:

"This town?"

"Yes."

He thought about it. I watched him think about it. It was like watching a man reach into his pocket for a key and slowly, pleasantly, discover he had no pockets.

"Well," he said at last, cheerfully. "It's called home, isn't it? Sit down, sit down, there's stew."

* * *

We sat. There was stew. It was very good stew, and I ate it while conducting the quietest investigation of my life, which mostly consisted of listening.

At the corner table, four farmers played cards. I listened for ten minutes. They knew each other's hands — literally; one man glanced at another's cards without shame and got no objection. They knew each other's children, each other's fields. What they did not know, what surfaced nowhere in forty minutes of talk, was anything before. No "my grandfather used to say." No "back when the mill was my father's." No stories that began *once*. Their past seemed to start every morning, fresh, at the moment of waking, and their future extended exactly as far as next harvest.

A young woman sat mending by the fire, and a child — six, maybe seven — climbed into her lap and asked her something I caught only because the fire had gone quiet.

"Mama, what was my name again? Before?"

"Before what, love?"

"The song says I had a name before. In the song."

The woman went still. Then she kissed the top of the child's head and said, gently, "The song is just a song," and resumed her mending, and the child, satisfied or trained, slid down to play with buttons.

I looked at Vharen. Vharen was looking into his cup with an expression I had never seen on him before, and it took me a moment to name it, because it was so ordinary, so human: grief.

"The song," I whispered. "What song?"

"Ask the mother tomorrow," he said. "Not tonight."

* * *

But it was the innkeeper's wife who told me, later, unprompted, in the way of people who have been waiting a long time for someone to tell.

She brought us blankets for the room — good wool, beautifully woven — and lingered in the doorway while Vharen went down to check the street, which he did every night with a thoroughness I pretended not to find endearing. She looked at my wrapped forearm. Then at my face.

"You're from Stillness," she said. Not a question.

"How could you tell?"

"You walk like them. Small steps. Like you're afraid of taking up space." She said it without malice, almost fondly, the way you'd note someone's accent. "We had a visitor from the valley once, years ago. A scholar. He wrote everything down. Names, dates, the price of eggs." Her mouth twisted. "We sent him away. Gently. But we sent him away."

"Why?" I asked, though I was beginning to guess, and the guessing was a cold thing in my chest.

"Because writing is remembering, and remembering is the one thing we sold." She came into the room then, set the blankets down, smoothed them with both hands as if smoothing something else entirely. "My grandmother's grandmother, they say — though 'they say' is all we have — the wars were coming up the border roads. Soldiers, and worse than soldiers. Things that hunted by scent and by story. You could bar a door against soldiers. Against the other kind, you needed to be no one. Unfindable. So the town made a bargain." She laughed, short and bitter. "I don't even know what word we used for it. There isn't one anymore. There was probably a lovely old word for exactly this and we spent it too."

"Sold memories," I said. "To whom?"

"To whatever would take them. The same powers that broke the great covenant, I'd guess — the little thieves learned from the big ones." She shrugged. "It worked. Nothing has ever found us. Armies marched past on the far side of the ridge and never turned their heads. Maps show nothing here. Scholars write nothing here. My own mother couldn't have told you her childhood if you'd asked, and wouldn't have minded, because you don't miss what you don't remember missing."

"And you?" I asked carefully. "Do you mind?"

She was quiet a long time. Downstairs, a chair scraped; the card game went on, nameless men playing nameless games in a nameless town, safe as stones.

"I mind the edges of it," she said finally. "That's the cruelty of the thing. You keep the shape of the hole. I know I had a brother — there's a place in me shaped like a brother — but the brother himself is gone, and worse, I agreed to let him go. I wasn't even born. My ancestors sold him before he existed, the way your valley—" she looked at me sharply, "—the way your valley sold something too. Don't look surprised. Everyone from Stillness walks like a person carrying stolen goods. You just don't know whose stall you lifted it from."

"I'm starting to," I said.

She patted my hand, briskly, the gesture of a woman who had comforted a hundred travelers and remembered none of them. "Sleep. Keep that arm covered. And if you hear singing after midnight — the old ones sometimes sing, fragments, things that survived in the body when they couldn't stay in the head — listen if you like. Just don't write it down."

"Why not?"

"Because," she said, pausing at the door, "the last person who wrote our song down was Beatrix, and we've been afraid ever since."

* * *

I didn't sleep.

I sat by the window with the shutters cracked and the wrapped map warm against my arm, and past midnight, it happened: singing. Thin, wandering, from somewhere up the hill — an old woman's voice, cracked and sweet, looping through a melody with words that surfaced and sank like fish. I caught only pieces.

*...and the wolf lay down at the door...*
*...and the scribe opened her hand...*
*...Beatrix, Beatrix, the bridge will come...*

I sat bolt upright.

The map burned. Not the insistent tug of navigation, not the warm contentment of a heading confirmed — this was different, urgent, grief-stricken, the whole length of my forearm alight like a brand. And beneath the heat, for the first time, I felt something new pushing up through the skin: a line of script surfacing like a word rising through dark water, in Beatrix's hand, the hand I now knew better than my own.

Two words.

*I heard.*

Down the hall, Vharen's door was already open. He stood in the corridor with moonlight behind him, and his eyes weren't wolf-calm at all.

"She sang here once," I breathed. "Before she found me. She came here, she heard the song, and she wrote it down — the town's memory, the piece of themselves they lost. It's in the map. It's been in me this whole time, and I didn't—"

"Orsen." His voice was low. "If the town knows what you carry — if they understand that the song came back—"

The singing on the hill had stopped.

And in the silence after it, from the direction of the well, from several directions at once, came the sound I had grown up hearing described in temple sermons about the dangers beyond the borders, the sound every valley child was taught to dread: lantern-light moving in formation, boots in rhythm, and a voice calling through the dark streets, clear and cold as a blade laid flat —

"Inquisitor Sable thanks the town for its hospitality. We have come only for the thief."

Vharen's hand closed on my shoulder.

"The Order found us," he said. "Get your coat."
