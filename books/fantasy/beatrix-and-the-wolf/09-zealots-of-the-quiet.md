## 9. Zealots of the Quiet

Vharen had been following the heir for six days, and in that time he had learned three things.

First: the heir could not walk quietly to save their life. Literally. He had watched them step on the one dry branch in a mile of damp forest, twice, and once apologize to it.

Second: the heir talked to themselves while walking, narrating their own fears in a mutter, which should have made them easy to abandon and instead made him keep listening.

Third — and this was the dangerous one — Vharen was beginning to like them.

He resented this enormously.

He lay along the branch of an old pine, gray as weathered bark, gray as regret, and watched Orsen Almeida pick their way down a slope of loose stone with the map burning faintly under their sleeve like a coal wrapped in linen. The heir had stopped flinching at the glow around day four. That was its own kind of progress, he supposed. A person learning not to fear the thing growing inside them. Vharen had seen it before, centuries ago, in others. He had outlived all of them.

*Don't get attached,* he told himself, the way his mother had told him, the way every spirit of the long roads told every young wolf at their first watching. *You are a judge, not a friend. You weigh the heir. You do not carry them.*

His mother had also said he had a problem with carrying things. She'd meant it as a criticism.

Down the slope, Orsen sat abruptly on a rock, pulled off one boot, and tipped out a pebble with the grim ceremony of someone performing surgery. "I know," they muttered — to the boot, or the map, or possibly the pebble. "I know we're being followed. I can hear you breathing, you know."

Vharen went very still. Wolf-still. Stone-still.

"Big heavy dramatic sighing breaths," Orsen continued. "Like a bellows with opinions."

*I am a spirit of the old roads,* Vharen thought with dignity. *I have crossed mountains without disturbing dew.*

The pebble, dislodged from the boot, rolled downhill and struck him squarely on the nose.

He did not yelp. He would maintain until his last century that he did not yelp. But his paw slipped, the branch shed a fall of gray needles, and Orsen looked up directly into two silver eyes floating in a pine tree.

"Oh," said the heir. "There you are."

* * *

They had spoken perhaps forty words to each other in six days. Most of them had been "go away," from him, and "no," from them, which he privately acknowledged was a fair summary of their entire arrangement so far.

Now Orsen sat across a small fire — a small fire, in open country, because the heir apparently had never heard of the Order of the Quiet, or zealous men with crossbows, or the basic physics of light — and studied him with the frank, unafraid stare that Vharen found more unsettling than any weapon.

"You're hurt," Orsen said.

"I am a spirit," Vharen said. "Spirits are not hurt."

"There's blood on your shoulder."

"That is ceremonial."

"It's dripping."

"Ceremonially."

Orsen put down the root they had been scrubbing for supper, stood up, and crossed the fire with the total disregard for protocol that seemed to be the heir's defining feature. Before Vharen could decide whether to allow this — before he could decide whether he *wanted* to allow it, which was worse — they had pushed back the fur at his shoulder and found the gash beneath.

Three days old. He had thought himself careful. He had forgotten what it was to be touched.

"What did this?" Orsen asked quietly.

"Nothing that concerns you."

"The Order?"

Vharen said nothing, which among his kind is a whole speech, though clearly it was wasted here.

"They came through the town after us," Orsen said. Their hands were doing something to the wound — cleaning it with water from the skin, pressing moss into it, brisk and gentle at once, the way you treat something you're angry at for being breakable. "Two days behind us, then one. I saw their gray cloaks at the ford. I thought—" They stopped. Started again. "I thought you were just watching me to see if I was worth anything. To whoever sent you."

"I am."

"Then why are you bleeding?"

*Because,* Vharen did not say, *on the second night, when three Quiet brothers found your trail and followed it toward the hollow where you slept with your mouth open like a landed fish, I led them eleven miles in the wrong direction and let one of them put a quarrel in me rather than kill him, which my order forbids and my pride regrets.* 

What he said was: "The wind turned."

"The wind," Orsen repeated.

"Spirits are close with the wind."

"Mm." They tied off the dressing with a strip torn from their own sleeve — their only spare sleeve, Vharen noted; the heir owned three shirts and one of those was technically a sack — and sat back on their heels. "Well. The wind should eat something."

They put the cleaned root, half of it, on a flat stone and pushed it across the fire.

Vharen stared at it. It had been a hundred and forty years since anyone had fed him. Spirits ate moonlight and old promises and the smell of rain on stone; they did not need roots cut in half by nervous scribes. He ate the root. All of it. Too fast. Like a dog.

"Don't look at me like that," he said.

"I'm not looking at you any way."

"You're looking at me like I'm somebody's pet."

"I'm looking at you like you're a wounded soldier who won't say who wounded him, which is the oldest story in the world, and my grandmother had a version of it where the soldier was a cat." Orsen poked the fire. Sparks climbed. "In the story, the cat eventually tells."

"I am not a cat."

"In the story the cat says that too."

Vharen bared his teeth, which with wolves is laughing, and Orsen — to his lasting astonishment — laughed back.

*Oh no,* he thought. *No, no. This is how it starts.*

* * *

He remembered how it started the first time, too. That was the trouble with spirits: memory does not soften. It keeps.

Her name had been Maren, and she had not been an heir, only a herbwoman's daughter with a crooked smile and a habit of feeding strays. The covenant still breathed then, thin but breathing, and his order had charged him with watching the border-folk for signs of the stolen magic moving. He had watched Maren for a year. She had named him within a month — Vharen, she'd decided, though spirits are not supposed to be nameable by mortal tongues, and the name had stuck to him like burrs. When the Quiet came for her village's little hedge-magics — the charms against fever, the songs that made cows calm — he had stood between her and the gray cloaks, and he had broken the oldest law of his kind: he had been seen defending a human.

She died anyway, sixty years later, of age, holding his ear the way you hold a dog's, saying *good wolf, good wolf*, and he had carried the name and the law-breaking both ever since.

So he knew the shape of this. Feed a stray once and it stays forever. He had been the stray once. Now he was being made the stray again, from the other side, and there was nothing in the world he could do about it except be very careful.

Which was, of course, exactly when the horns began.

* * *

He heard them a full minute before Orsen did — the flat bronze note of Quiet signal-horns, two voices, answering each other across the dark hills. Not the hunting call. The *found* call.

"Up," Vharen said, and was on his feet before the word finished, and the world tilted, and the wound in his shoulder tore open like a mouth.

Orsen was already scrambling, kicking dirt over the fire, grabbing the map-arm as if Vharen might have misplaced it. "How many? Which direction?"

"Twelve. Maybe fifteen. North and northeast — they've split to pinch the valley of the stream." He tasted the air. Under the horn-smoke: lamp oil, cold iron, and the particular scorched-wax smell of consecrated null-powder, the stuff the Quiet packed into their quarrel-tips. Enough to make even a spirit's blood run sluggish. "They know the ground better than we do. In an hour this hillside is a trap."

"Then we run south—"

"*You* run south." He said it harder than he meant to. Orsen stopped, and in the last red coals' light their face did something complicated. "Listen to me, scribe. Fifteen Quiet brothers do not chase a fugitive scribe through the night. They chase an heir. Every moment they spend following your scent is a moment they are not spending on the map. And I—" the lie assembled itself in his mouth with terrible ease, "—am faster than you, and worth more to them dead than you are alive. I will draw them north. You go south, along the water, where scent dies. Two days. Then east, over the shepherd's pass. Do not wait for me."

"Do not wait for—" Orsen's voice cracked upward. "You don't even like me. You've told me to go away eleven times. I counted."

"Twelve," Vharen admitted. "Go away. That makes twelve."

"Not until you tell me why."

"Because you are the heir of a broken covenant, and I am the spirit sworn to judge whether you are worth following, and I have judged." The truth of it arrived in his own ears a half-second after the words, the way truth always did, rude and unannounced. "You are. Worth following. Now *run*, before I revise my finding."

For one heartbeat Orsen looked at him the way nobody had looked at him since Maren — as if he were large and foolish and worth worrying about — and then they ran, south, along the water, boots squelching, branches whipping, making enough noise to wake the drowned. Some things, Vharen reflected, could not be helped by any amount of inheritance.

Then he turned north, lifted his head, and sang.

* * *

A spirit's challenge-call is not a sound so much as a change in the meaning of the night. Birds go quiet. Rivers seem to listen. And fifteen men of the Order of the Quiet, trained all their lives to dread exactly one sound, stop as one body, as if hooked through.

*Here,* the song said. *The wolf. The old law-breaker. The thing your grandmothers warned you walks the border. Come and be afraid.*

They came. Of course they came. Zealots always do; it is their one virtue, and Vharen had learned to hunt with it.

He led them a merry road. Down scree that thundered behind him. Through a peat bog that swallowed two brothers to the chest and cost them an hour of cursing extraction. Along a ridgeline where he deliberately, ceremonially, left tracks in every soft patch — big tracks, exaggerated, the tracks of a wolf the size of a haywagon, which flattered no one's courage.

But the null-powder slowed him. He felt it working in the old shoulder-wound like frost in a joint, dulling the world's edges, making the night feel far away instead of near. By the third hour he was breathing hard, and spirits do not breathe hard, and somewhere in his hindbrain a small cold voice observed that he had done this before — drawn hunters, spent himself, bled in the dark for someone running south along a river — and that last time it had ended with *good wolf, good wolf* and sixty years of silence.

*Different this time,* he told the voice. *This time I'm not doing it for love. This time it's professional assessment.*

The voice declined to comment, smugly.

They cornered him at last in a box canyon where the hills folded shut like a hand — a good place for it, he had chosen it himself, which shows you the state of his thinking. Twelve gray shapes fanned out below him in the moonlight, quarrels leveled, and at their center a brother-captain with a voice like a drawer of knives called up:

"Spirit of the border! By the law of Stillness, stand and be unmade!"

"By the law of Stillness," Vharen called back, "come up and try. The climb is bad. Take your time."

A quarrel answered instead. He dodged — slower than he should have been — and it raked his flank instead of his heart, and the null-powder bit in like winter teeth, and the world went white at the edges. He heard the brothers begin to climb.

*Well,* he thought, with the strange bookkeeping clarity of the badly wounded. *Assessment complete. Verdict: the heir runs like a duck and laughs at bad jokes and fed me half a root. Worth following. Pity about the ending.*

He set his feet and prepared to make the canyon expensive.

And then, from the canyon's southern wall — from a scramble of rocks no reasonable person would have descended in the dark — came a clatter, a curse, and a shower of loose stone, and Orsen Almeida, heir of the broken covenant, terror of absolutely no one, slid down into the moonlight with a burning brand in one hand and the most furious expression Vharen had ever seen on a human face.

"You *lied*," the heir announced to the entire canyon. "Twelve times you told me to go away and then you go and bleed on a hillside alone, and I am *not* — " here they jabbed the brand at the nearest gray cloak, who took an involuntary step back — "*not* losing the only creature out here who thinks I'm worth anything, even if he says it in wolf!"

Silence. Fifteen zealots regarded one scribe. One scribe, breathing hard, hair full of twigs, stood beside one enormous bleeding wolf.

"Well," said Vharen. "This changes the tactical picture."

"Does it?" said Orsen.

"No. It's exactly the same picture. It's just somehow more embarrassing."

The brother-captain recovered first, and raised his hand, and twelve quarrels rose with it — and Vharen, whose options had narrowed to arithmetic, was already calculating how many brothers he could take on the way to dying, when Orsen did the last thing anyone expected, including the map.

They raised their marked arm, and the living ink woke.

Light poured from under their sleeve — not the coal-glow of the last six days but a flood, Beatrix's whole written life blazing up the heir's skin and out into the air in rivers and whorls, and the null-powder in the quarrels guttered like candles in a gale. The canyon filled with the smell of ink and summer. Somewhere in that light Vharen glimpsed, unmistakably, a woman's hand writing a word in a language older than the valley, and the word was *run*.

They ran. Spirit and scribe, side by side, out through the gap while fifteen brothers of the Quiet stood blinking in afterimage, and the night opened ahead of them like a page.

It was a mile before either of them spoke.

"You waited," Vharen gasped. "I told you not to wait."

"I didn't wait," Orsen panted. "I got lost. Entirely different."

"You followed me into a canyon against fifteen armed zealots."

"I followed the blood. You bleed *loudly*." They stumbled, and he shouldered them upright without deciding to, his body simply declining to consider any other option. "Besides," Orsen added, quieter, "you judged me worth following. Someone has to return the finding."

Vharen ran on, flank burning, heart doing something unsanctioned, and understood with perfect clarity that he had lost. Not to the Order. Not to the hunters behind or the hard road ahead. He had lost, several days ago, probably at a campfire, possibly at half a root, to a scribe who apologized to pebbles.

Loyalty, he thought bitterly, is the one thing that never asks permission.

Behind them, far off, the horns changed their note — the found-call fading into something new, something summoned. And ahead, at the edge of hearing, the map under Orsen's skin began, very softly, to hum a tune Vharen hadn't heard since the covenant broke: the sound of the next shrine, calling its heir home.
