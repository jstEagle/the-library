## 10. What Beatrix Promised

The shrine at Aldermoor had not yet been gutted when Beatrix first knelt before it. She was twenty-three years old, and she had come with a satchel of chalk, a stolen psalter, and the unshakable conviction—common to the young and the doomed—that she alone had read the old texts correctly.

She remembered it now from the inside of her own dying, the way one remembers a childhood home after the house has burned: every detail preserved, every detail useless.

* * *

Aldermoor in spring smelled of wet stone and new grass, and the covenant-shrine stood in its grove like an old woman waiting to be recognized. It was smaller than the texts promised—a waist-high altar of grey stone, ringed by nine standing markers, each carved with a name so worn that only the first letters remained legible. Beatrix had spent four years and two forged travel passes finding this place. She had expected to weep. Instead she found herself laughing, breathless and absurd, because it was *small*, because the hinge of all the world's quiet turned on something you could cover with a cloak.

"Hello," she said to the altar, because no one was there to hear her be foolish. "I'm going to fix everything."

The altar said nothing. Altars rarely do. But the air above it shivered, faint as heat-haze, and Beatrix chose—generously, she would think later—to take that for encouragement.

She had grown up in the borderlands, in one of those thin villages where the valley's stillness leaked out at the edges like cold from a badly hung door. Her mother had been a hedge-witch of the small, honest kind: charms against milk-souring, salves that worked mostly because she believed in them, a lullaby that could genuinely, reliably, put a feverish child to sleep. Nothing grand. Nothing that would have gotten anyone burned.

But Beatrix had seen what happened when the stillness thickened. The year she turned eleven, the quiet rolled up the valley mouth like a tide, and her mother's lullaby simply—stopped working. Not grew weaker. *Stopped*, mid-verse, as though the song had been plucked out of the air by an unseen hand. Her mother had sat on the edge of the bed with her mouth open, humming into silence, and little Beatrix had watched something go out of her face that never fully came back.

"They took it," her mother said, when she could speak again. "They take and they take and they call it peace."

Her mother died believing that. Believing it angrily, which is a hard way to die. And Beatrix had inherited the anger the way other children inherited quilts and copper pots, and she had spent twelve years turning it into scholarship, and scholarship into a plan, and the plan into a satchel of chalk and a stolen psalter and a long walk to a small grey altar in a spring-smelling grove.

The plan, in fairness to her younger self, was elegant. The covenant between the realms had been written in three parts: one held in the magic realm's deep places, one held here, at shrines like Aldermoor, and one—the keystone, the part that made the whole thing answerable to mortal voices—held in blood. A bloodline of keepers, sworn at the founding, who could renew the terms each generation. That was what the texts said. That was what the worn names on the standing stones were: keepers, generations of them, their names rubbed smooth by centuries of hands laid on in renewal.

And the keepers were gone. Extinct, scattered, or quietly absorbed into the valley until no one remembered they had ever been anything else. Which meant the covenant had gone unrenewed for—Beatrix had counted carefully, cross-referenced obsessively—ninety-one years. Ninety-one years during which the valley's founders had been free to siphon, to wall, to steal, and no living voice existed with the standing to say *no*.

But the texts also said—and here Beatrix had underlined it twice, in ink she'd mixed herself from oak gall and spite—that a covenant unclaimed did not die. It waited. And any soul who came to a shrine in good faith, who offered themselves without coercion, might stand proxy for the lost line. Might renew the terms. Might, in the oldest formula, *take up the thread*.

She had checked the passage eleven times. She had made Vess check it too.

Vess. She thought of him now, in the last minutes, and the memory hurt worse than the wound in her side, which was saying something, because the wound in her side hurt quite a lot indeed.

Vess had been her teacher, or as close to a teacher as a smuggler-archivist with a limp and a fondness for gambling could be. He had found her at sixteen, copying forbidden passages in a market-town scriptorium with the frantic precision of someone who believed knowledge was a weapon she'd been issued late. He'd bought her dinner, corrected her transliteration of a Third Age glyph, and said, "You know there are people who've died for less than what you're copying, girl."

"Then they died carelessly," she'd said, seventeen years old and insufferable, and instead of slapping her he'd laughed until his tea went cold.

He was the one who taught her the difference between reading a text and understanding it. He was the one who warned her, over and over, in his dry way, about the passage she loved best.

"'Any soul in good faith,'" he'd quoted back at her, the night before she left for Aldermoor, tapping the page with his bad leg stretched out by the fire. "You notice it doesn't say any soul *sufficiently prepared*. Or any soul *with allies*. Or any soul who has told a single living person exactly where she's going."

"I'm telling you."

"I don't count. I'm an accomplice, not a witness." He looked at her across the fire, and for once the gambler's twinkle wasn't there. "Beatrix. The texts are three hundred years old. The people who wrote them assumed the keepers' line would stand. Proxy-renewal was a footnote. A legal curiosity. Nobody ever tested it because nobody ever needed to. You're not taking up a thread. You're betting your life on a footnote."

"Somebody has to bet eventually, or the house wins forever."

"And there she goes," Vess said to the ceiling, "quoting gambling philosophy at me in my own house." But he'd packed her provisions himself, and pressed a knife into her hand—"for cutting chalk, obviously"—and walked her to the crossroads at dawn, and neither of them had said anything true or useful, because sometimes the truest thing two friends can do is fail to say goodbye properly.

* * *

At the shrine, she worked through the morning. Chalk circles first, the geometry of invitation, each line checked against the psalter. Then the offerings—bread, salt, a lock of her own hair, because the old formula asked for *something of the body freely given*. The sun climbed. Birds carried on being birds. Twice she caught herself talking to the altar again, narrating her work like a cook explaining a recipe, and twice she decided this was a charming habit rather than a warning sign.

At noon she knelt, laid both hands flat on the stone, and spoke the renewal.

She had rehearsed it a hundred times. The words fit her mouth like a key fits a lock it was cut for. And for one shining moment—one moment she would spend the rest of her short life trying to get back to—it *worked*.

The grove lit up. Not with fire; with recognition. The nine standing stones hummed, each one sounding a name she couldn't hear but could feel, syllables pressing against her skin like moths. The air above the altar opened—not a door, nothing so crude—a *window*, a view sideways into somewhere green and vast and awake, and something on the other side looked back at her. Something curious. Something glad. She felt it the way you feel sunlight through glass: warmth with a barrier still in place.

*Who comes?* it asked, in the wordless way such things ask.

"Beatrix," she said. "Of Thornhollow. Hedge-born. I come in good faith, freely, to renew the covenant and stand keeper until the line can be restored."

The warmth gathered itself. Considered her. And then, gently, with what she would later recognize as genuine sorrow, it declined.

*The terms require a keeper's blood. Good faith opens the door. Blood turns the key.*

"I am offering myself—"

*Freely given, yes. Freely given is the hand on the door.* The presence rippled, and she felt it searching, casting back through the years, and what came back through the window was grief. Old grief, patient grief, the grief of something that had been watching ninety-one years of theft through a window it could not open. *We have waited for a hand. We are still waiting for a key.*

"But the footnote—"

*Was written in hope,* the presence said, *by people who could not bear to write 'never.'*

The window began to close. And here is the thing Beatrix could never explain afterward, not to Vess, not to the few others who learned fragments of the story, not even to herself: she should have stepped back. The ritual had failed cleanly. The door was shutting. Any sensible person withdraws, licks wounds, plans again.

Instead she lunged.

She shoved her hand through the closing gap—through the threshold, into the space between—and seized the thread of the covenant with her bare will, the way you grab a rope off a moving cart because stopping seems like a problem for future-you.

For three seconds, she held it.

Three seconds in which the grove blazed, in which the stones sang full-voiced, in which she felt the whole architecture of the covenant open beneath her like a cathedral—every shrine, every term, every stolen name recorded in its ledgers, the thefts of the founders laid out in columns of light. Three seconds in which she understood, completely and instantly, things it would take her fifteen more years to learn how to say.

And three seconds in which the covenant, unkeyed, unbalanced, holding far more weight than one hedge-witch's daughter could brace, came down on her like a roof.

* * *

She woke at dusk with blood in her mouth and the grove dark and silent around her. The window was shut. The stones were just stones. Nine of the chalk lines had burned themselves black.

She tried to stand and found her left arm wouldn't answer. She tried to gather her things and found the psalter's pages blank—all of them, every passage of covenant-law wiped clean, as if the book had paid her fine alongside her. She tried to walk and managed, on the second attempt, a lurching shuffle toward the tree line, leaving a trail she was too hurt to hide.

It cost her the arm—withered from that day forward, a dead branch she carried strapped to her side. It cost her the psalter, and with it the texts, the formulas, the accumulated proof of everything she knew. It cost her Vess, though she didn't learn that for another month: the Order of the Quiet had traced her journey backward, patiently, the way water finds cracks, and they had come to a limping archivist's fire and asked him questions, and Vess—who could have bought his life with her name, who instead told them, reportedly, several extremely creative things about their parentage—had not survived the asking.

She learned it in a border tavern, from a sympathetic carter, and she sat very still with her ale untouched for a long time, and then she went upstairs and did not cry, because crying was a luxury and she had work.

That was the arithmetic of her failure, tallied honestly: one arm, one friend, one book, fifteen years.

Fifteen years, because that was how long it took her to understand what those three seconds of sight had shown her. The covenant could not be renewed by force of will. It could not be renewed by scholarship, or sacrifice, or righteous anger, though all three helped. It needed a key—keeper's blood, keeper's inheritance, freely accepted, freely borne. And if the old line was truly gone, then a new line must be *made*: not seized, not grabbed off the moving cart, but handed over, willingly taken, the transfer witnessed and complete.

Blood and choice together. That was the formula the footnote had fumbled. You cannot take up a thread. You can only be *given* one—and you must accept it knowing what it costs, because the cost is the point. The covenant answered to mortals precisely because mortals pay for what they promise.

So Beatrix spent fifteen years learning to give instead of take. She found the remaining shrines—two intact, one gutted, one hidden under a chapel floor. She mapped them, not on paper but in the only medium the Quiet couldn't burn: living memory, inked into her own skin by arts she traded favors to learn, so that the map of the covenant would survive even if she didn't. She cultivated patience the way her mother had cultivated herbs—daily, unspectacularly, in dirt.

And she searched for a key. Not a descendant—she'd given up on descendants; ninety-one years dissolves bloodlines like rain dissolves footprints—but a person. Someone of the valley, shaped by the stillness, who could stand in both worlds without either rejecting them. Someone whose good faith was real. Someone who, shown the truth, would choose it *freely*—because a coerced heir was just another thief with better intentions.

She had candidates, over the years. A ferryman's daughter with second sight. A defrocked monk who talked to rivers. Each one, examined closely, wanted the magic more than they wanted the truth, and Beatrix had learned what wanting does to a doorway.

Then, in a temple scriptorium in the Valley of Stillness itself—in the belly of the beast, among the ledgers, invisible among hundreds—she had found a scribe with ink-stained hands who flinched, just slightly, at a hymn sung wrong. Who noticed. In a valley engineered so that no one noticed anything.

She had been watching that scribe for six days when the Quiet finally caught up with her, and she had run, and she had been struck down at the gates, and she had crawled the last hundred yards on one arm because the map in her skin had shown her no other path and because—Vess, forgive her—she had never learned to say goodbye properly.

* * *

Now, dying in a courtyard while a frightened scribe knelt beside her, Beatrix felt the old footnote rise in her memory one last time, and she almost laughed. *Any soul in good faith.* Vess had been right about everything except the ending. The footnote hadn't failed. It had just needed fifteen years, one arm, and a stranger with careful eyes to become true.

*Forgive me,* she thought, toward the green vastness behind the world, toward the wolf she could feel circling somewhere beyond the walls, toward the scribe whose name she didn't even know. *Forgive me for the shortcut. Forgive me for handing you a burning thing. I grabbed the rope off the cart, and it broke me, and I am putting it in your hands instead because your hands are yours, and chosen, and that is the whole of the law.*

The scribe was saying something—protesting, probably, they had a protesting face—and Beatrix smiled, and pressed her palm to theirs, and gave away the last thing she owned.

The thread, at least, went to willing hands.

Somewhere beyond the valley walls, a wolf lifted his head.
