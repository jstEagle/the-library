## 14. The Second Site and the Second Lie

The mountain did not want us.

I say that with the confidence of someone who spent three days climbing it and listening. The first shrine, the hollow one by the river, had felt like a robbed grave—sad, violated, but passive about it. The mountain shrine was different. It resented our approach the way an old housekeeper resents mud on clean floors. Every switchback offered a fresh insult: scree that slid precisely when Vharen stepped on it, wind that came out of a clear sky to shove me toward the edge, thorn-brush that arranged itself across the trail like it had been expecting us and had dressed for the occasion.

"It knows we're coming," I said, somewhere past the second day, flat on my back with my ankles in the air because the trail had decided I'd walked enough.

"Good," said Vharen. He stood above me against the pale sky with his arms crossed, entirely unbothered, being mostly wolf and therefore immune to gravity's opinions. "Let it prepare tea."

"Spirits drink tea?"

"Spirits drink everything. It is one of our defining qualities." He leaned down and hauled me up by the collar with one hand, which should have been insulting and instead felt like being picked up by a very large, very disapproving dog who loved you anyway. "The shrine tests. That is its function. Yours is to be tested. Mine," he added, "is to stand here looking magnificent while you do it."

"You've been practicing that line."

"Since the ford." His ears flicked—the closest thing to a flinch he allowed since we'd carried him, torn and bleeding shadow-stuff, away from the water where Tam had sold us. Three days of rest in a shepherd's barn had closed his wounds and left something else open underneath, something he didn't talk about, which meant of course I thought about nothing else. "Walk, scribe. The mountain grows bored."

The map burned under my skin as we climbed. It had been doing that since the second site had come into focus on my forearm—a new branch of silver lines threading down from my elbow like frost forming on a window, drawing itself toward the mountain's heart with the slow insistence of ink finding its way across wet paper. Beatrix's memories came with it, sometimes. Not words. More like weather. A smell of pine smoke that wasn't there. An ache in a knee I had never injured. Once, falling asleep against Vharen's flank, I dreamed in a language I couldn't read and woke with tears frozen on my cheeks, grieving someone whose name I didn't know yet.

That was the thing nobody warned you about inheriting a dead woman's map: she kept dying inside you, a little at a time, and you kept learning to carry it.

* * *

We found the shrine at dusk on the fourth day, in a cup of stone below the peak, and my first thought—my shameful, honest first thought—was *that's it?*

The first shrine had been grand even gutted: carved pillars, a sunken altar, the bones of ceremony. This one was small. A ring of standing stones no taller than my shoulder, lichened gray-green, huddled together like old men at a funeral. In the center, a flat table of rock. No gold. No ornament. Just stone and silence and a cold that came from underneath, the way cold comes up through a cellar door.

"Well," I said. "It's very modest."

"Don't apologize to it," Vharen said. "Or compliment it. Shrines take everything personally."

"I wasn't going to—"

"You were composing a sentence. Your face does a thing when you compose sentences. Like a cat deciding whether to jump."

I shut my mouth, which proved his point, which was annoying.

The map knew what to do before I did. The silver lines under my skin warmed, then pulled, dragging me forward a half-step like a fish on a line. When I laid my palm on the altar stone, the world went quiet—and not valley-quiet, which is a held breath, a silence with hands pressed over its own mouth. This was the other kind. The listening kind.

Then the stones began to speak.

Not in sound at first. In names.

They rose off the altar like heat-shimmer, one after another after another, hundreds of them, thousands, a slow ascending flock of written light—and each name, as it passed me, *knew itself*. I felt them the way a scribe feels a well-made page: the rightness of letters in their proper order. Maren, daughter of Illen. Ossik of the terraced fields. Yeva, called Three-Lanterns. Names in the old border script, the one the temple called *debased* and forbade us to study, though I'd copied fragments secretly for years the way some boys collect beetles.

"What are they?" I whispered.

"The stillness," Vharen said, and his voice had changed—all the humor gone out of it, ground down to something flat and old. "Look closely, heir. You asked what holds up your valley's peace. Here is the answer. Read it."

And because I am, at root, a scribe—because reading is the one prayer I have ever known—I read.

The names weren't drifting past. They were *turning*, slowly, like pages. And beneath each name, in smaller script, ran the same line, repeated ten thousand times down the layers of light like a watermark:

*Given freely, holding fast. Given freely, holding fast.*

"That's not true," I said.

"No," said Vharen.

I put both hands on the stone and pushed my gift deeper, the way Beatrix's memories had taught me, and the shrine—perhaps because I was its first honest visitor in centuries, perhaps because it simply hated the lie too—opened all the way.

The watermark changed.

*Taken,* it read. *Taken. Taken. Taken.*

The names stopped rising gently like birds and began to fall upward instead, dragged, streaming like smoke sucked into a chimney. And with them came the rest: ledgers, actual ledgers, ghost-pages unfolding in the air around the ring of stones, and I knew ledger-hand when I saw it because it was my hand, or near enough—the careful temple script drilled into every novice's wrist—and the entries were beautiful and monstrous in the way only truly organized cruelty can be beautiful.

*Received into keeping, for the security of the Valley: one name, freely surrendered (see waiver).*

*Received: three names, household of Ossik (debt settlement).*

*Received: one name, infant, estate of the mother (arrears).*

An infant. They took a baby's *name* for arrears. I thought of the debt-ledgers in the scriptorium, the ones I copied every day of my life—the columns of sums, the interest accruing in neat little rows—and I understood, all at once and forever, what the debts had been *for*. We weren't paying the temple. We were paying the prison. Every coin of it, every hour of copying, every family broken against interest rates—that whole grinding economy of obligation existed to harvest names, because names were fuel, and the fuel fed the spell that made the valley Still.

Magic cannot cross our borders, they taught us. Magic cannot exist among us. As if it were weather. As if it were a law like gravity, ancient and neutral and no one's fault.

It was a cage. Our peace was the bars, and the bars were made of stolen people.

* * *

I don't know how long I knelt there. Vharen let me kneel, which I appreciate now; he's learned when to leave a wound uncovered.

But eventually the shimmering ledgers folded themselves away—or I looked away, I genuinely couldn't tell you which—and I found myself staring at the last page, the one that hung stubbornly in the air before me, larger than the rest, older, written in a hand I recognized with a sick lurch of the stomach.

Because it was mine.

Not similar. Not in the style of. It was *my* handwriting, the specific crook I make in capital J's, the way I never quite close my g's—which was impossible, unless the shrine dealt in impossibilities, unless it reached forward as well as back, and I have stopped pretending to know what shrines can't do.

The entry read:

*Of the line of Joubert: seven names received. Six expended. One reserved.*

"Reserved," I said. My voice came out wrong, thin, like paper held over flame. "Reserved for what?"

"For the end of the accounting," Vharen said quietly. He had gone still in the way wolves go still, all his attention aimed at the stones like an arrow at a target. "A covenant requires two signatories, heir. The founders wrote theirs in stolen blood so it could never be unmade by consent. But a bridge—" he looked at me, and his yellow eyes were very old and very sorry— "a bridge must be built of both banks."

"And my family's name has been sitting in a ledger for generations. Waiting. Like a deposit."

"Like a seed."

I laughed. It was a horrible sound, and I'm not ashamed of it; some sounds have to come out sideways or they break something on the way. All those years I thought I'd chosen the temple. Chosen anonymity, chosen the quiet, chosen to be one faceless scribe among hundreds because I liked the routine, liked the order, liked being small. Sufficient, obedient, invisible Orsen, who mistook suffocation for peace because peace was all the valley had ever shown them.

Even my smallness had been *arranged*. Some ancestor's name sat in reserve, and the spell had needed a vessel shaped just so, and so I had been shaped—gently, patiently, with ledgers and rules and soft sermons about the blessed quiet—into exactly the kind of person who would never, ever ask what the quiet cost.

"They farmed me," I said. "Like a field."

"Yes."

"And Beatrix knew."

"She chose you," Vharen said, "if that helps."

"It doesn't." I pressed my palms into my eyes until the dark bloomed red. "It would help more if anything in my life had ever been mine. My name isn't even—it's collateral, Vharen. I'm walking collateral. I'm a *promissory note*."

He was silent a moment. Then he crossed the ring of stones and sat beside the altar, close enough that his shoulder pressed warm against mine, fur and heat and the faint smell of winter forests, which is apparently what a spirit smells like when he is trying to comfort you without admitting to it.

"When I was young," he said, "by the measure of my kind, which is to say some centuries ago, I believed my shape was given to me. Wolf, shadow, judge—I thought the First Ones made me for a purpose, and that I was therefore their creature, bound to their law, no more free than a hammer." His ear twitched. "It took me a long time to learn that a purpose imposed is only a starting place. What you are named is not what you are. Even a stolen name may be *worn differently* than the thief intended."

"That's very comforting for a hammer."

"I was not finished. The hammer part was the beginning. There is also a part where I say something wise about seeds growing into trees that choose their own fruit, and you tell me I'm terrible at speeches, and then you feel marginally less wretched."

Despite everything—because of everything—I laughed again, and this time it was a real laugh, cracked and wet and human. "You are terrible at speeches."

"I told you." He nudged me with his great head, nearly knocking me over, which is a wolf's way of an embrace. "Now. The shrine shows truth, and truth has a shape. Ask it the last question. You have been circling it since we climbed."

I knew what he meant. I had been circling it since the ledgers unfolded; I'd been circling it, if I'm honest, since Beatrix died in my arms in the courtyard while guards studied the sky.

The map on my arm pulsed once, patient, encouraging, like a teacher's hand on a shoulder.

"All right," I said to the stones. To the mountain. To whatever remained of the person I'd been four days ago. "Show me the rest. Show me the third site. Show me the way under the valley."

The stones went dark. For one long heartbeat the ring held nothing but starlight and cold, and I thought the mountain had finally gotten its wish and rejected us—

Then every stolen name rose at once. Thousands of them, all the fallen and taken and expended, wheeling overhead in a slow silver storm, and they gathered themselves—not into ledgers this time, not into lies—but into a single line of script hanging in the air, written in my own impossible hand:

*The heart lies beneath the temple floor. You have been walking on it all your life.*

Below it, smaller, almost gentle, one more line:

*Come home, little ledger. Bring the wolf.*

Vharen read it upside down, which spirits apparently can do, and his hackles rose slowly along his spine like a field of dark grass bending before wind.

"Heir," he said, "I do not think that message was written by a shrine."

The storm of names scattered. The stones went gray and dumb and ordinary. And in the sudden silence, from far down the mountainside—from the direction of the valley, the direction of home—came the sound of bells.

Temple bells. Ringing the pattern I'd heard only twice in my life, and never for anything good.

The valley knew we were coming. And something down there, beneath the floor where I'd copied ledgers every day of my life, was glad.
