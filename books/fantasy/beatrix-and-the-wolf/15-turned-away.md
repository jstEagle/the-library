## 15. Turned Away

The village was called Hessel's Ford, according to the sign at its edge, though someone had scratched out *Ford* and carved *Crossing* beneath it in a hurried hand, and beneath that someone else had scratched out *Crossing* again, so that the sign now read, with great authority, **HESSEL'S ~~FORD~~ ~~CROSSING~~**, which seemed to Orsen like a fair summary of every place they had ever tried to belong.

"You're staring at a sign," Vharen said.

"I'm gathering my courage."

"From a sign."

"It's how scribes do things," Orsen said. "We gather courage the way we gather everything else. Slowly, and from written sources."

Vharen did not laugh, because Vharen almost never laughed in his wolf-shape, but his ears did something that Orsen had learned to read as amusement — a small tilt, like a flag changing its mind. In the week since the fortress, since the door he had broken and the fire he had carried Orsen through, the wolf-spirit had become something Orsen had no proper word for. Not a guard. Not quite a friend. A presence, large and warm and faintly offended by doors.

The map under Orsen's skin itched that morning. It had been itching since dawn, a slow crawl of warmth along their forearm where Beatrix's living ink had settled, and when Orsen pushed back their sleeve, the lines had shifted. Roads redrawn themselves. A river wandered north. And there, glowing faint gold like a coal wrapped in cloth: the second covenant-site, high in the mountains behind them now, already visited, already understood, already a wound. The third site lay ahead — and the roads to it ran through country where people lived.

"We need food," Orsen said. "Rope. Another blanket, because yours turns into smoke when you shake yourself and I refuse to sleep in smoke. And information."

"Information," Vharen repeated, in the low gravel voice that came out of the wolf's mouth as if it embarrassed him.

"People. Someone who knows the old paths to the heart-shrine. Someone whose grandmother remembered the valley before the Stillness." Orsen tucked the sleeve down over the map, over the faint shimmer that never quite hid anymore, no matter how many layers they wore. "Someone who might help us."

"And if they don't?"

Orsen had been rehearsing an answer to that for two days and had not found one, so they said, "Then I'll be very polite about being thrown out," and started down toward the village before the fear could catch up.

* * *

Hessel's Crossing-or-Ford was a market town of perhaps three hundred souls, built in the crook of a river the color of old pewter. It smelled of woodsmoke, wet wool, frying onions, and — underneath all of it, so faint Orsen might have imagined it — of absence. That was the thing about the borderlands, Orsen was learning. The magic here wasn't gone the way it was gone in the Valley of Stillness, flat and absolute. It was gone the way heat leaves a room someone just left. You could feel the shape of where it had been.

They left Vharen in the birch woods above the town, which required fifteen minutes of negotiation and one insult about stubbornness that Orsen chose to take as a compliment. A wolf the size of a pony tended to end conversations before they began, and Orsen needed conversations to begin. Needed them badly enough that they had washed their face in a stream, straightened their collar, and practiced saying *good morning* in the local accent until even Vharen had looked away, politely.

The first person Orsen spoke to was a woman selling apples, red-cheeked and brisk, and she answered questions about rope and blankets readily enough, taking Orsen's coin with hands quick and businesslike. Encouraged, Orsen bought three apples and asked, casually, gently, the question they'd rehearsed:

"Would you know anyone here who remembers the old ways? The old paths, east of the mountains? Covenant-paths?"

The woman's hands stopped moving.

It was a small thing. A pause of perhaps two seconds. But Orsen had spent eleven years copying ledgers in a temple where every lie announced itself as a neat column of numbers, and they had learned to hear the silence between sounds. The woman's smile stayed exactly where it was while everything behind it packed up and left.

"We keep to ourselves here," she said. "You'll want the rope-seller, end of the row. Good day."

She did not watch Orsen go, which was how Orsen knew she was watching them go.

* * *

By noon, Orsen understood that the apple-seller had not been an anomaly. They had spoken with the rope-seller (rope purchased, quickly, without eye contact), the miller (no), a carter watering his mule (*absolutely* not, delivered while walking away mid-sentence), and an old man on a bench who had looked at Orsen's face for a long moment and then said, quietly, "You've valley blood. I can always tell. Something about the eyes. Like folk waiting for permission."

Orsen had stood there holding an apple, feeling that sentence go in like a splinter.

Because he was right. Of course he was right. Orsen had been born three days' walk inside the Stillness, raised in its shadow, shaped by its silences — and apparently the shape showed. They wore the valley the way other people wore family names. They could scrub their face in a stream until their cheeks were raw, but they could not scrub out the particular quality of a person raised in a place where raising your voice was a sin and wanting anything too much was worse.

And now, apparently, everyone east of the border could see it.

Orsen sat on the well at the market's center, eating the apple they didn't taste, and let the loneliness settle over them like the weather. It wasn't the sharp loneliness of the scriptorium years, which had at least been private. This was public. This was loneliness performed in front of strangers, loneliness with witnesses. Every door in this town would close politely, and the closing would be *their fault*, inherited through no act of their own, a debt owed on a loan taken out four generations before they were born by people who — as the hollow shrine had shown — had been thieves.

*You have valley blood,* the old man said in memory. *Like folk waiting for permission.*

"Well," Orsen said aloud, to the apple, "at least you're consistent."

"You're talking to fruit," said a voice above them. "That's a bad sign in a town like this. They'll talk about it for a season."

A boy of perhaps twelve stood beside the well, barefoot despite the chill, holding a basket of candles he was very clearly supposed to be selling elsewhere. He had the specific expression of a child who has identified the most interesting thing to happen in his town this year and intends to be present for all of it.

"I was talking to myself," Orsen said. "The apple was eavesdropping."

The boy grinned, sat down on the well's edge without invitation, and studied Orsen with frank, unembarrassed curiosity. "You're the one asking about the old paths."

Orsen's stomach performed a small, controlled collapse. "Am I?"

"Old Tammi told Mother, and Mother told the neighbors, and now everyone knows there's a stranger asking after covenant-roads." He said the last two words in a whisper that was somehow louder than his normal voice. "Nobody says why. That's the interesting part. When people won't say why, it's usually because the why is frightening."

"What do people say," Orsen asked carefully, "when they speculate?"

The boy shrugged with the whole of his skinny body. "Some say you're a treasure-hunter. Some say you're from the Order, testing loyalty." He leaned closer. "But Marta the candle-wife says you've got the look. She says her grandmother told her the heir would come someday wearing ink on their hands, and she says—" he grabbed Orsen's wrist, turned it over, and peered at the stains between their fingers with enormous satisfaction, "—there you are, then."

For one reckless, soaring moment, Orsen felt hope rise like bread.

"Marta," they said. "Your mother's friend. Would she speak with me? I don't want anything from these people except—"

"She's dead," the boy said cheerfully. "Two winters past. Grandmother's stories die hard around here, mostly because we kill 'em." He hopped off the well. "You shouldn't stay till dark, whoever you are. The ford floods at night and so does the talk. Somebody's already gone up to the Hollows to fetch somebody, and when folks fetch somebodies about strangers, it's never to bring them soup."

"Wait — who's gone to the Hollows? Fetching whom?"

But the boy was already gone too, candles swinging, absorbed into the market crowd like water into sand, leaving Orsen alone on the well with half an apple and the growing certainty that this day was arranging itself into the shape of every bad chapter in every cautionary tale they had ever copied.

* * *

Vharen was waiting at the treeline, and he knew before Orsen spoke. He always knew; Orsen suspected the map under their skin broadcast feelings the way a struck bell broadcasts sound, and that the wolf simply stood downwind of them and listened.

"No one will help," Vharen said. It wasn't a question.

"No one will help." Orsen dropped the pack of purchases at his feet. Rope, blankets, dried fish, a pan they didn't need but had bought anyway because the rope-seller's pity was making their skin crawl and buying something useless had felt like an act of dignity. "Vharen, they weren't cruel. That's the worst part. The apple-woman was frightened. The old man was almost kind. They looked at me like—" They stopped, started again. "Like I was the Stillness itself, walking around in a coat."

Vharen lowered his great head to be nearer their level. His breath was warm and smelled of pine and lightning. "Four hundred years," he said, "the valley has eaten what it wanted from the borderlands. Rain that fell wrong. Children born quiet. Rivers that forgot fish. My people watched your founders' spell drink this country to feed the valley's peace, and the border folk watched too, and buried their dead, and remembered." His yellow eyes held Orsen's without cruelty and without mercy. "They are not wrong to fear the valley, little scribe. They are only wrong about you."

"Yes, well." Orsen's throat was tight. "I appear to be the only one making that particular error."

They should have left then. Later, lying awake under a sky the map insisted was full of watching constellations, Orsen would replay the moment and understand exactly where the mistake was made. But instead they heard themselves say:

"One more house. There was a light burning in the last farm before the river — the one set apart, with the overgrown orchard. If someone lives at the edge of a town, at the edge of everything, it's usually because the town decided they belonged at edges. Which means—" they gestured vaguely at themselves, at the wolf, at the entire catastrophic situation, "—kinship, possibly."

"That is the worst reasoning I have heard from you," Vharen said, "and you once argued that a locked archive door was merely a suggestion."

"Thank you. I'll be following it anyway."

* * *

The farmhouse at the edge stood with its back to the village and its face to the mountains, as if it had picked a side long ago. The orchard around it had gone properly wild — branches knotted, fruit frozen black on the bough — but someone had recently chopped wood; the pile was high and fresh-chipped. Smoke rose from the chimney in a thin, determined line.

Orsen knocked.

The woman who opened the door was ancient in the way of trees — bent, weathered, clearly built to outlast everything. Her eyes went to Orsen's face, then to their ink-stained hands, then past them to the treeline where a shadow the size of a pony stood failing entirely to look inconspicuous among the birches.

"Huh," the woman said.

"Good evening," Orsen began. "I know how this looks. I'm not from the Order. I'm not here to harm anyone. I was hoping—" and the whole speech they'd prepared, the gentle one, the disarming one, crumbled all at once, and what came out instead was the truth, hoarse and unplanned: "I was hoping someone in this country might once, just once, look at me and see a person instead of a border."

The old woman considered this for a long moment. Wind moved through the ruined orchard.

"My grandmother came from inside the Stillness," she said at last. "Ran out as a girl, the year the walls of quiet went up. She used to say the saddest thing about the valley wasn't what it took. It was what it made the rest of us do." She stepped back from the doorway, and Orsen's heart leapt—

—and she said, "I'm sorry. Come spring, maybe. Ask me again in spring, when I know the Hollows aren't watching my house. But tonight there are riders coming down from the north road, boy, and whatever you are, you standing on my step makes my grandchildren less safe, and I have buried enough."

Through the open door, behind her, Orsen saw them: two children at a table, frozen mid-supper, spoons halfway to mouths, watching the stranger with the wide dark eyes of children who already understood that safety was a thing measured in doors closed.

"I understand," Orsen heard themselves say, and hated how steadily they said it. "I'm sorry to have troubled you."

"The map-bearer," the old woman said, just as Orsen turned away. Orsen froze. "My grandmother had words for your kind too. She said: *they will turn you away from every table, and it will not be malice, only arithmetic.*" Her old face worked, somewhere between apology and grief. "Go around the ford, not through it. The water lies about its depth. And—" she moved suddenly, thrust a wrapped parcel into Orsen's hands: bread, hard cheese, something still warm — "this is not kindness, it's indigestion prevention. If you starve on my land it's bad luck for the orchard."

Then the door shut, gently, and Orsen stood alone in the darkening orchard holding dinner they hadn't been allowed to eat indoors, and discovered that this — precisely this, the bread given and the shelter refused, the arithmetic — hurt worse than any shouted curse would have.

* * *

They ate in the birch woods by a fire the size of two cupped hands, because Vharen refused larger fires now ("Smoke is a letter addressed to everyone"), and Vharen lay with his chin on his paws and watched Orsen not enjoy the bread.

"They gave you food," he observed.

"They gave me food and closed the door."

"In my experience," Vharen said, "that is nearly the best outcome available to your species. Food plus refusal is practically a love letter."

Orsen laughed despite everything — a short, cracked sound — and threw a crust at him, which he caught out of the air with insulting elegance and swallowed without chewing, as a point of principle.

"They're not villains," Orsen said, quieter. "That's what I keep having to learn out here. In the temple we painted everyone beyond the border as either monsters or marks. But they're just people doing sums. Four hundred years of dead, weighed against one stranger at the door." They pulled their knees up, rested their chin on them. "I'd close the door too. That's the part I can't say out loud in daylight. I'd close the door, and I'd hate myself a little, and I'd sleep fine, because my grandchildren were safe at the table. The Stillness didn't invent the arithmetic, Vharen. It just industrialized it."

The wolf was silent a long moment. Firelight moved in his eyes like weather.

"When my people first saw the theft," he said at last, "there were those who wanted to fall upon the valley and unmake it in a night. And the elders said no — not out of mercy. Out of recognition. They said: *what the valley does, any frightened people would do, given the same fear and the same power. We wait for one who understands this from inside, who can stand at the door and neither close it nor break it.*" His gaze slid to Orsen's forearm, where the map glowed faintly through the sleeve. "The elders waited four hundred years for a door-keeper. Instead they got a debt-ridden copyist who talks to apples."

"I contain multitudes," Orsen said, "most of them overdue for library return."

But the joke was armor over an ache, and Vharen knew it, and let it be.

Orsen lay back against the wolf's warm flank — a liberty taken sometime during the fortress escape and never since revoked — and watched the stars through the bare branches, and thought about tables. Every table in the world, it seemed, had a fixed number of places, and Orsen's name was not written at any of them, and the terrible dawning truth of this whole wretched inheritance was that restoring the covenant might not earn them a seat anywhere at all. Bridge-builders, after all, live on the bridge.

They must have drifted, because it was Vharen's growl that brought them back — a sound felt more than heard, rising through the wolf's ribs into Orsen's spine like a struck bell.

Firelight. Then no firelight; Vharen smothered it with one paw. In the sudden dark, Orsen heard what the wolf had heard first: hoofbeats. Many. Coming down the north road, exactly as the old woman had said — but not passing toward the village.

Turning onto the orchard track.

"Order?" Orsen breathed.

"Worse." Vharen rose, a mountain deciding to relocate, every line of him gone taut. "Horses shod in the valley pattern. Temple outriders — the elders' own. And riding with them—" his hackles lifted, and the map on Orsen's arm flared hot enough to make them gasp, "—something the elders should not possess. Something that smells of the Quiet's fortress and of ash."

Down in the dark fields, a lantern swung, and in its light Orsen glimpsed the riders: temple livery, yes — but at their head, robed in grey, rode a figure whose stillness was wrong, whose silhouette made the map under Orsen's skin recoil like a living thing.

And the figure turned its head, across half a mile of darkness, and looked directly at Orsen — as though it could feel the map calling to it — and raised one hand in a gesture that was horribly, gently, unmistakably a wave of welcome.
