## 16. The Inquisitor's Bargain

High Inquisitor Sable had a theory about tea.

Most people, she had observed over thirty years of service to the Order of the Quiet, believed that tea was a comfort. They wrapped their hands around the cup, they inhaled the steam, they said *ah* in small satisfied voices, and for the length of a cup they believed the world was a kind and orderly place. This, Sable had concluded, was precisely what tea was *for*. Not the drinking of it—the believing. The tea was a small, warm lie you could hold in your hands.

She poured two cups now, in her tower room above the fortress of Ashenhold, and set one on the far side of the desk, and waited.

The prisoner arrived between two brothers of the Order, walking on her own feet because Sable had instructed that she be permitted to walk. It was important, Sable had explained to the brothers—who were young and eager and had wanted to drag the woman, because dragging made them feel like the instruments of righteousness they imagined themselves to be—that the prisoner arrive with her dignity intact. A frightened prisoner told you only what fear wanted you to hear. A composed one might, given time and warmth, tell you the truth.

The prisoner was not composed. She was furious, which Sable considered far more useful.

"Sit, please," Sable said. "The tea is chamomile. I'm told it settles the nerves. Personally I find it tastes like hay that has been apologized to, but I keep it for guests."

The woman did not sit. She stood in the middle of the room with her wrists still bound in quiet-iron—gray metal, dull as old spoons, the Order's one indulgence in the metallurgical arts—and she looked at Sable the way people look at a snake they have found in a boot.

"You're the High Inquisitor," the woman said.

"I'm the High Inquisitor."

"They said you'd question me. About the heir."

"I will. Eventually. Drink your tea first." Sable settled into her own chair, arranged her gray robes with the care of a woman smoothing a bedsheet over a corpse, and smiled. It was a good smile. She had practiced it in a mirror as a girl, back when she was plain Sister Maren Sable of a border village no larger than the one the heir had passed through, and she had decided—correctly, she still believed—that a smile was a tool like any other and ought to be kept sharp. "You've had a long road. You were taken at the mill ford, I understand? The one with the crooked willow?"

The woman's jaw tightened. "You know where we were taken."

"I know where *you* were taken. I know considerably less about where the heir was taken, which is the entire reason you're drinking my tea." Sable lifted her own cup, blew across it, sipped, and made a small face. "Hay. Apologized to. I told you."

"They'll never tell you where she is."

"*She*," Sable repeated, and let the word sit in the room like a coin placed on a table. "Interesting. The Order's reports have been saying *they*—the heir and the wolf. But you said *she*. You know her. Not just of her."

The woman's face did something complicated and unhappy, and Sable noted it, and filed it, and moved gently on, the way one moves a piece on a board one has already won.

"May I tell you a story?" Sable said. "It's not a long one. I know prisoners expect stories to be long. Ours is a profession with a reputation for tedium."

* * *

The story, as Sable told it, began with her grandmother.

"My grandmother lived in a village called Ferrow's End, on the near side of the border. You won't have heard of it. That's rather the point of the story." Sable turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "When my grandmother was a girl, magic still leaked across the border sometimes. Not the great workings—the covenant was already broken by then, whatever the spirits like to claim—but the runoff. The seepage. A well that whispered. A child born with eyes like a cat's. A hedge that bloomed out of season, in the middle of winter, in colors that hurt to look at."

"And?" the prisoner said. "Sounds lovely."

"It was, for a while. The child with the cat's eyes was my grandmother's sister. Her name was Ilse." Sable said the name the way one sets down a heavy stone—carefully, at a chosen spot. "When Ilse was nine, the whispering well decided she was interesting. It isn't the spirits' fault, you understand. Magic doesn't love. It *attends*. It noticed her the way you or I might notice a candle in a dark room, and it began to lean toward her, and by the time anyone understood what was happening, my great-aunt Ilse was not entirely my great-aunt Ilse anymore. She would stand at the well at night, in the snow, barefoot, listening. She forgot how to sleep. She forgot my grandmother's name. In the end she stepped into the well, and the well was only a hand's width of water, and she went through it the way a stone goes through the surface of a pond, and she did not come back."

The prisoner had gone still. Good, Sable thought. Stillness meant listening. Listening meant the story was doing its work.

"The Order was founded three years later," Sable continued. "Not out of hatred. I want that understood, because everyone assumes we hate. We don't. Hatred is a passion, and the Order has no use for passions. We were founded out of *arithmetic*. My grandmother did the arithmetic herself, later, when she was old: how many children lost to the seepage, how many wells, how many hedges, how many small lovely terrible things. She counted for a whole winter. Then she gave her count to the first Inquisitors, and she said: *the stillness is not a cage. The stillness is a wall around a cradle.*" Sable looked up. "The valley is full of cradles. Farmers. Scribes. Children learning their letters. People who did not ask to be attended by forces older than the moon. Someone has to keep the wall mended. That is all the Order is. Masons."

"You kill people," the prisoner said.

"We mend the wall," Sable said. "Sometimes the wall is made of people. I won't pretend otherwise; pretending is for the temple, and the temple does it beautifully." She refilled the prisoner's cup without asking, an intimacy that was itself a kind of question. "But here is what I actually want to talk about, and then you may sleep, and in the morning you may decide how much of what I've told you was true."

* * *

Sable rose and went to the window. Below, the fortress yard was quiet in the evening gray, brothers walking their rounds with lanterns that cast no shadows, because the lanterns were quiet-iron too, and quiet-iron gave nothing back. Beyond the walls, the hills went down toward the valley, toward the temple spires, toward the soft smug stillness that most of the world believed was simply the way the world was.

"Three hundred years ago," she said, "the founders of the valley did a terrible thing. I know this because we have their letters. The Order keeps the letters that the temple burns." She said it lightly, but she watched the prisoner's reflection in the glass for the flinch, and got it. "They were afraid—genuinely afraid, the way my grandmother was afraid—and so they did not merely close their borders to magic. They *stole* it. They took the covenant—the old treaty between the valley and the other realm, the one that let the two worlds lean on each other without swallowing each other—and they unmade it, and they kept the pieces. Every hearth in the valley that never goes cold. Every ledger that never loses a page. Every debt that can never quite be paid off, because the interest is written in a hand that doesn't exist anymore. All of it is powered by what they stole. The stillness isn't an absence, you understand. It's a *reservoir*."

"You're an Inquisitor," the prisoner said slowly. "You're telling me the temple is corrupt."

"I'm telling you the temple is a *bank*," Sable said. "Corruption implies they're doing something the depositors didn't agree to. The valley agreed. The valley signs its name every day, in every ledger, in every quiet prayer. They traded wonder for safety, and they'd sign again tomorrow, and so would you, and so would I. I don't despise them for it. I *am* them." She turned from the window. "But the theft had a cost that the founders did not read carefully before signing. The magic they stole didn't vanish. It *waited*. And it has been waiting, all this time, for an heir. Someone with the right—call it blood, call it name, call it debt—to take the stolen pieces back and reforge the covenant."

"And if she does—"

"If she does, the wall comes down. The seepage returns. The wells begin to whisper. And some little girl in some border village stands barefoot in the snow, listening to something that knows her name better than her mother does." Sable let that stand for a moment. "I knew such a girl. I told you. I have never gotten over it, and I have never wanted to."

The prisoner's tea was going cold. Sable noted that she hadn't drunk it. Pity. The believing worked better when they drank.

"So here is the bargain," Sable said, "and I give you my word it is the only one I will offer, and I have never broken my word, which my enemies find very inconvenient. The heir is going to the heart-shrine. She doesn't know that yet—she thinks there are more shrines to visit, more truths to gather, more time than there is. She will arrive, and she will attempt the reforging, and she will fail, because the reforging cannot be done from outside. It must be done from *within* the covenant's own blood, and hers has been valley-blood for six generations. The founders made sure of that. It was their cruelest trick and their cleverest: they stole the magic and they *diluted the heirs*, so that no child of the valley could ever be whole enough to take it back." She smiled again, and this time she did not bother to make it warm. "Except the map has been writing itself into her. Every mile she walks, every shrine she touches, the map is *un*-diluting her. By the time she reaches the heart-shrine, she will be whole enough. Which is why she must not reach it."

"And you want me to tell you where she is."

"I want to tell you how it ends," Sable said, "and then you can decide what you know."

She went to her desk and opened a drawer, and took out a small object wrapped in gray cloth, and set it on the wood between them. She did not unwrap it. Unwrapping was theater, and theater was for the temple.

"This is a working," she said. "Three hundred years in the making, assembled from the founders' own stolen pieces, refined by every Inquisitor who came before me—my contribution was a correction to the seventh seal that took me eleven years and cost me two fingers, and I'd show you the fingers but that would be theater. It is a *null*. Not a ward. Not a suppression. An ending. When it is completed—properly completed, with the right component—it will not merely close the valley's borders. It will unmake magic *everywhere*. Every realm. Every spirit. Every whispering well. Permanently. Irreversibly. No more heirs, because there will be nothing left to inherit. No more Ilse at the well. No more wall to mend, because there will be nothing on the other side of it at all." She sat down again, and folded her hands, and her voice went gentle, which was the most frightening thing in the room. "Do you understand what I'm offering? Not the valley's safety. *Everyone's* safety. The wolf-spirit's realm included. The spirits will hate it, of course. Spirits always hate an honest grave. But the world would be *quiet*, truly quiet, for the first time since the beginning. Every child safe. Every well just a well. No more maps written into skin. No more people like Beatrix dying in courtyards, bleeding light, because some covenant needed mending."

The prisoner was staring at the wrapped working. Her breath had changed. Sable had seen that breath before, many times, in many rooms. It was the breath of a person doing arithmetic of their own.

"And the component," the prisoner said. "The right component. You said—"

"Her blood." Sable said it plainly, because plainness was mercy. "The heir's blood, freely given, at the moment of completion. That is the seal I could not correct alone. The null must be *invited* by the thing it ends. It's elegant, really. The magic's last law: it cannot be murdered, only persuaded." She leaned forward. "So I do not need to hunt her, in the end. I do not need to kill her. I need only to *ask her*—at the right moment, with the right truth in her ears—whether she would rather be the bridge between two worlds, forever, carrying every debt and every danger across her own back until she breaks, or whether she would rather let the whole weary business end, gently, and let everyone go home."

"You think she'd say yes."

"I think," Sable said, "that she is a scribe. A debtor. Someone who has spent her whole life being *used* by other people's covenants. And I think that somewhere on that road, in the small hours, she has already wondered whether everyone might be better off if the magic simply stopped. I think she has looked at the wolf who follows her and wondered whether he follows her because he loves her or because he was *sent*. I think she has read Beatrix's memories in her own skin and felt how much of that woman's life was spent being spent." Sable picked up her tea, found it cold, and drank it anyway. "I am not asking her to lie. I am asking her to say the quiet thing out loud. That is what makes me dangerous, if you want the truth of it. Not the working. Not the brothers. The fact that I am *right*, and everyone who meets me slowly discovers it."

* * *

The prisoner slept that night in a clean cell with a blanket that smelled of lavender, because Sable believed in lavender, and in the morning she would be asked what she knew, and Sable was fairly confident of the answer. Not certain. Sable was never certain; certainty was a temple vice. But the woman had a brother in the border towns, and a brother's family, and families were the oldest arithmetic there was.

Sable stood again at the window as the fortress settled into night. Somewhere out in the dark hills, a scribe with a burning map was walking toward a shrine, and a wolf was walking beside her, and both of them believed—she was quite sure of it—that they were running *from* something.

They were running *toward*. That was the tragedy of heirs. They always mistook the road for an escape.

Sable touched the window glass, once, the way one touches a headstone.

"Hold on, Ilse," she said to the dark. "I'm nearly done."

Below in the yard, a lantern went out. And in the morning, the prisoner would talk, and the Order would move, and at a mill ford with a crooked willow, someone would make a choice that could not be unmade.
