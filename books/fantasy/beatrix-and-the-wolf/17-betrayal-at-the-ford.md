## 17. Betrayal at the Ford

Doss whistled while he worked.

That was the thing about him Orsen had never quite learned to trust. A man who whistled while mucking out a waystation stable, while patching a leaky roof, while burning the morning porridge for the third day running—a man that cheerful had either found some secret peace the rest of the world was denied, or he was carrying something heavy enough that the whistling was the only thing keeping it from crushing him flat.

Orsen had met him six weeks ago, at a crossroads inn where the ale was thin and the fire was generous. Doss had a way of appearing useful. He'd fixed the innkeeper's cart axle. He'd found Orsen's dropped glove. He'd laughed at exactly the right moments and asked exactly no questions, which in Orsen's experience was the most suspicious quality a person could have. Everyone asked questions. The whole world was made of questions wearing friendly faces.

But Vharen had sniffed him—literally, nose to the man's sleeve when Doss wasn't looking—and pronounced him, in that gravel-and-rain voice of his, *clean*.

"Clean," Orsen had echoed. "Meaning what, exactly? He doesn't lie? He doesn't steal?"

"Meaning he doesn't smell of the Quiet. No incense. No oath-oil." Vharen had turned his pale eyes on Orsen. "It is more than most people manage."

So Doss had traveled with them. Not with them, exactly—he'd insisted he was going the same way, which was a thing people said when they were going with you but wanted the dignity of pretending otherwise. He carried the pots. He told terrible jokes about a goat named Marguerite who had, over six weeks, become the protagonist of an increasingly elaborate saga involving a duke, a cheese wheel, and a curse that could only be broken by sincere flattery. He taught Orsen a card game called Beggar's Fortune and then lost at it with such theatrical despair that Orsen suspected he was letting them win, and then lost so badly the next night that Orsen suspected he wasn't.

Orsen had, without quite meaning to, started to like him.

Which was, they would think later, in the cold and the blood and the noise of the ford, exactly the point.

* * *

The river called the Senn came down out of the hills in a hurry, all white noise and gray muscle, and the ford at Bracken's Crossing was the only place for twenty miles where anything heavier than a heron could get across. The map on Orsen's skin had gone quiet and hot that morning, the lines along their forearm glowing faintly through the skin like coals under ash, and it had tugged them east with the gentle insistence of a hand on the elbow. The second shrine waited on the far side of the Senn. So, Orsen suspected, did other things.

"Water's high," Doss said, crouching at the bank and trailing his fingers in the current. He pulled them back fast. "Cold, too. Snowmelt. We cross now or we camp and cross at dawn, and I'll be honest, I don't love the idea of the Quiet finding us camped."

"The Quiet doesn't know where we are," Orsen said, and heard how it sounded even as they said it. A hope dressed up as a fact.

Vharen stood a little apart, upwind, his head tipped as if listening to something in the trees. He'd been doing that all morning. Wolves heard what people didn't; Orsen had learned that much. But Vharen had been hearing *too much* lately—stopping mid-sentence, staring at empty hillsides, his hackles rising under his human skin in ways he couldn't always smooth down.

"Vharen?" Orsen said.

"Wind's wrong," he said. "It's been wrong since the ridge." He turned, and his eyes were the color of old ice, and he was looking at Doss when he said it. "It carries the smell of a fire that burned two days ago and a man who stood near it."

Doss laughed. He had a good laugh, open and easy, and he used it now like a man uses a lantern in a dark room. "You're saying I smell? Thanks very much. Six weeks of sharing a fire and *now* he mentions it."

"I'm saying," Vharen said, very quietly, "that someone has been tracking us. And that the tracker has stood where you have stood."

The laugh died in Doss's throat. Orsen watched it happen—watched the good humor drain out of his face like water out of a cracked cup, and what was underneath wasn't guilt, exactly. It was worse. It was *grief*, arriving early, the grief of a man looking at the last hour of something he'd loved.

"Doss," Orsen said slowly. "What did you do?"

He didn't answer at first. He looked at the river, and the river roared, and somewhere behind them, far back along the road they'd traveled, Orsen finally heard what Vharen had been hearing for an hour: the faint, disciplined creak of many boots moving in step.

"I have a wife," Doss said. "And a daughter. They live in Millbrook, under the Quiet's eye, like everyone does. Nine months ago the Order came through and took inventory of every soul in that town, and when they got to my house, the inquisitor looked at me a long time and said, *You have a brother in the borderlands, Doss Ferrow. Brothers write letters.*" His hands were shaking. Orsen had never seen them shake before. "I said I hadn't heard from him. She smiled like she was doing me a kindness. She said, *Families are safest when they're honest. And when their friends are honest too.*"

"You told them nothing," Orsen said. "Six weeks. You had six weeks of chances."

"I told them nothing for six weeks!" Doss's voice cracked, went high, went young. "Do you know what they do, Orsen? They don't torture you. That's the horror of it. They *wait*. They put your daughter in their school and they teach her to sing the quiet-songs and they're *kind to her*, kinder than you could afford to be, and every week they ask her what her father says in his sleep. And then a rider came to the waystation two days back—not for you, for me, just a note, just a line in a ledger—and it said my girl had been moved to the fortress at Harrow's Reach *for her education*, and that fathers who cooperate get visiting days." He was crying now, ugly and open, not bothering to hide it. "I sat by your fire last night and I watched you laugh at the goat story and I thought, *this person would die for me and I am going to sell them*, and I did it anyway, because she's *seven*, Orsen. She's seven years old and she has my nose and she thinks I'm a good man."

The boots were closer. Orsen could hear them properly now, and under the boots, the thin bright note of Order-horns, the ones that didn't signal attack but *arrival*—the sound the temple had played every Founding Day, the sound Orsen had grown up thinking meant safety.

Vharen had gone very still. In all the weeks Orsen had known him, they had learned his stillnesses: the stillness of sleep, the stillness of hunting, the stillness of a spirit holding a shape together by will. This was the third kind, and it was the most frightening, because it was the stillness of a creature deciding.

"Run," Vharen said.

"Vharen—"

"The ford is narrow. Water slows them. Water confuses their hounds." He was already pulling off his coat, already rolling his shoulders, and Orsen saw the air around him shimmer the way it did when the wolf came up close beneath the man. "You cannot fight thirty of them. I can *delay* thirty of them. Those are different things and you know it."

"I'm not leaving you to hold a river against the entire Order of the Quiet!"

"You are." His voice was gentle, which was how Orsen knew he'd already decided. "Because if you die here, everything Beatrix bled for dies here, and everything your ancestors stole stays stolen, and I will have spent three hundred years watching a valley sleep for nothing." He stepped close, and for a moment his forehead nearly touched Orsen's, and his voice dropped to almost nothing. "You once asked me why I follow you. I told you it was my charge. That was a lie, or mostly one. I follow you because when you look at the world you look at it like it could still be *worth* something. I have not looked at it that way in a very long time. Go be worth something. I will catch up."

"Spirit—" Doss started, and Vharen turned on him with such a flash of the old wildness that Doss staggered back a full step.

"You I do not forgive," Vharen said. "But I understand, and understanding is worse. Go. Take the north bank. If you're quick, you reach Millbrook before they think to look there. And Doss Ferrow—" something in his face softened, horribly, "—when you see your daughter, tell her a story about a wolf. Make it a good one. She'll need it, in the world that's coming."

Then he was gone—coat, man, all of it—and the wolf stood on the bank of the Senn, huge and gray and grinning, and Orsen had one heartbeat to see that grin and understand it was not joy. It was what a wolf does instead of weeping.

* * *

They crossed in the roar and the cold.

The water hit Orsen like a wall of knives, and the map flared along their arms and ribs, hot against the freeze, and Doss grabbed the back of their collar and hauled them upright when the current tried to fold them under. Behind them, on the far bank, the wolf turned to face the tree line.

Orsen saw the first of the Quiet come out of the trees as they reached the shallows—white-robed, unhurried, terrible in their patience, the way the temple elders had been terrible, the way everything in Orsen's old life had been terrible *politely*. There were more than thirty. There were so many more than thirty.

"Vharen!" Orsen screamed it into the noise of the river, and the wolf's ears turned, and for one absurd moment across the gray water their eyes met—Orsen's full of everything they didn't have time to say, Vharen's full of everything he'd already made peace with—and then the wolf *laughed*, a great huffing sound, turned, and met the first rank of the Order like a landslide meeting a procession.

Orsen didn't see the rest. That was mercy. What they heard was bad enough.

They heard the horns change their note. They heard men shouting, and the wet heavy sounds of bodies hitting bodies, and once—a sound Orsen would carry for the rest of their life, whatever length that turned out to be—a howl. Not the hunting howl, not the calling howl. The other kind. The kind wolves are not supposed to make, the kind that means *I am hurt and I am alone and I am still here*.

Doss dragged them up the north bank. Orsen let them, because their legs had stopped taking instructions. They stood dripping and shaking in the mud with the battle roaring behind them, and Doss said, "Orsen. Orsen, we have to *move*," and Orsen turned and looked at him, and Doss flinched.

"You should go north," Orsen said. Their voice came out flat and strange, someone else's voice. "Millbrook. Your daughter."

"Orsen—"

"You sold us to save her. I understand that. I even—" and here their voice did break, finally, uselessly, "—I even believe you'd have rather died. But you don't get to walk with me now. Not today. Maybe not ever. Go be her father. That's the only penance anyone's going to give you and it's the only one that matters."

Doss stood there a moment, soaked and sobbing and forty years old and seven years old all at once. Then he nodded, once, and turned north, and Orsen watched him go and hated how much they hoped he made it.

* * *

Orsen did not go east, toward the shrine. Orsen went back down to the bank.

They told themselves it was strategy. The map needed them alive; Vharen was the only sword between them and the Order; a wounded wolf couldn't hold a ford alone forever. All true. None of it was why. They went back because six weeks of goat jokes and shared fire had built something in their chest, and because Beatrix had pressed a covenant into their skin and the first law of every covenant Orsen had ever copied out in the temple scriptorium was the same: *what is bound is not abandoned*.

The ford was a wreck. The Quiet had lost their formation and their patience both; the wolf had seen to that. Bodies lay in the shallows, groaning or still. Three white-robed figures had backed a fourth shape against a boulder at the river's edge, staves raised, and the shape at the rock was gray and dark and *wrong*—one foreleg bent where no leg should bend, red thread bright on gray, breath coming in great tearing pulls.

Orsen had never done magic. Had been raised in the one place in the world where it couldn't be done, where the very word tasted of heresy. But the map woke along their arms like a struck bell, and the river *answered*—rose, all along the ford at once, a standing wall of gray water that lifted the three robed figures off their feet and set them down hard twenty feet inland, coughing and stunned.

Orsen stood in the shallows with their arms out and their heart slamming and thought, distantly, *so that's what I am now.*

"Vharen." They got an arm under his jaw, under the good shoulder. He was heavier than he looked, heavier than anything had a right to be, and he growled low—not at Orsen, at the pain, at the world. "Up. Come on. Up, you great stupid loyal—"

The wolf's laugh, when it came, was mostly blood. *Stupid,* he agreed, and then, as Orsen hauled him into the trees on the north bank, as the horns sounded again behind them and the water wall came down and the Order regrouped with the terrible patience of people who had all the time in the world: *Orsen. Listen. They were not hunting you today.*

"What?"

*This was a herd-drive. They wanted you running north, tired, alone.* His breathing hitched. *The shrine road east is open. It is open because they have already taken it. Sable is at the second site. She has been there for days.*

Orsen stopped in the dark under the pines, the wolf's weight burning across their shoulders, the map glowing like a brand, and far to the east, faint through the trees, they saw it: a light on the mountain where no light should be. Not fire.

The shrine, waking up. For someone else.
