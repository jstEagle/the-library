## 22. Beatrix and the Wolf

The children arrived the way spring did—noisily than anyone wanted, all at once, and impossible to turn away.

Orsen heard them before they reached the schoolhouse door: six pairs of boots squelching through the mud of what had once been the temple courtyard, back when the courtyard had been swept by novices who feared their elders more than they loved the morning. Now it was a garden. The rosemary had opinions. So, apparently, did the children.

"—told you she's real," a girl's voice insisted, breathless with the certainty of someone who has been arguing since breakfast. "My gran saw her. She *bled light*, Tam. Light."

"Everyone bleeds light now," said Tam, with the weary authority of a boy of nine who had decided the world was mostly disappointing. "My mum bleeds light when she pricks her finger sewing. It's not special."

"It is if it *glows*."

"Everything glows. That's the whole problem with everything."

Orsen smiled at the ledger in front of them—not a ledger anymore, really; old habits died hard, and Orsen still ruled lines across every page out of pure muscle memory—and waited. The door banged open on cue.

Six children stood in it, arranged roughly by height, which was also roughly by how much trouble they intended to cause. At the front, chin up, mud to her knees, stood Wren Almeida-Joubert, aged ten, who had been named for no one in particular and would tell anyone who asked that she had named *herself* at age four and everyone else could cope.

"You're late," Orsen said mildly.

"We're not. The bell's broken."

"The bell isn't broken. The bell is resting."

"Vharen says the bell is haunted."

"Vharen says a great many things he should keep to himself." Orsen set down the pen. "Sit. Today we're reading the map."

The room changed. It always changed at those words. Six small bodies found their benches without further argument, because whatever else school was, it was not this. This was the thing the children whispered about to each other after dark, the thing their parents pretended to disapprove of while asking endless questions at supper.

On the wall behind Orsen's desk, where a temple elder had once hung a framed list of debt regulations, the map lived.

It was not paper. It had never been paper. It covered the wall the way ivy covers stone, in slow silver-and-ink whorls that moved when you weren't looking directly at them, and it was warm to the touch, like a sleeping animal, which in every way that mattered it was. Beatrix's life was written there still—not as words, exactly, but as weather. Rivers remembered her swimming them. Mountains held the shape of her climbing. And in the far corner, where the Valley of Stillness sat drawn in careful, loving detail, there was a small inked figure of a scribe at a desk, added long ago by the map itself, without anyone's permission, in handwriting suspiciously like Orsen's own.

"Who can tell me where we are?" Orsen asked.

Six hands shot up, including Tam's, who did not know and never let that stop him.

"Wren?"

Wren pointed without standing, because standing was for people with less confidence. "There. By the river bend. Except the river moved last month so we're actually—" she squinted, "—there now. The map just hasn't caught up."

"Very good. And why hasn't it caught up?"

"Because it's not a picture," the children recited, in the bored chorus of the well-drilled. "It's a memory. Memories take their time."

"Memories take their time," Orsen agreed. "Now. Who remembers what Beatrix wrote into the mountains?"

This was the part Orsen loved, though they would have bitten off their own pen-hand before admitting it. Hands again—all of them, even Tam—and the answers tumbled out over each other. *She climbed it in winter.* *She was scared of the ice bridge but she crossed anyway.* *She left bread for something in a cave.* *A fox! She left bread for a fox!* *It wasn't a fox, it was a spirit in a fox shape, my gran says—*

"My gran," Tam announced over all of them, "says your gran makes things up."

"Your gran," said Wren witheringly, "thinks the miller's cat is spying for the Order of the Quiet, and the Order doesn't exist anymore, so."

"The Order could come back."

"The Order couldn't come back if it *tried*." Wren jabbed a finger toward the map, toward the valley's borders, which were no longer borders at all but simply places where one kind of weather became another. "Miss Orsen, tell him. Tell him about Sable."

And so Orsen told him—or told them all, again, the story they demanded weekly and never tired of, though Orsen told it differently each time, leaving things out, putting things in, the way all stories about true events eventually get told. They told about a woman who bled light in a courtyard full of people too afraid to look at her. About a map pressed into unwilling hands. About an altar gutted by the founders of a valley that had bought its peace with stolen names, and a prison spell disguised as holiness, and a High Inquisitor who had been, in her own cold way, the most honest person in the whole sorry history—because Sable alone had never pretended the stillness was anything but what it was.

"And then what happened?" breathed the smallest child, a boy called Pip who mostly came to school to sit near Wren and say nothing.

"Then," Orsen said, "I made a very foolish choice."

"*Brave*," Wren corrected. "Gran says it was brave."

"Gran wasn't there." Orsen tapped the desk. "It was foolish. I wrote myself into a covenant older than the valley, with ink I didn't understand, standing on an altar that was shaking apart, while a wolf fought an inquisitor's entire guard behind me. There was no plan. There was barely a thought. There was just—" they paused, hunting for it, the way they hunted for it every time, "—a feeling that the world had been holding its breath for four hundred years, and someone finally had to let it out."

Silence, the good kind, the kind teachers live for.

"What did it feel like?" Pip whispered. "When the magic came through you?"

Orsen considered lying, or softening it, and then didn't, because these children deserved better than softened things.

"It felt like being read," they said. "Like something enormous opened me the way I used to open ledgers, and went through every page, and didn't find a single line it wanted to strike out. Not even the bad ones. Especially not the bad ones." They smiled. "And then it felt like breathing. It still does. That's the secret nobody tells you about magic, children. Everyone expects fire and thunder. Mostly it feels like breathing, after a lifetime of holding your breath."

Tam raised his hand. "Did it hurt?"

"Yes."

"Did Vharen cry?"

"I have been sworn on three covenants never to answer that question."

"So yes," said Wren.

"*So yes*," Orsen conceded, and the room dissolved into giggles, and outside the window the afternoon light came slanting through the garden gold and unhurried, and somewhere below the floorboards of the world the old heart-shrine hummed its low contented note, the note it had hummed ever since the day the borders dissolved and the valley woke up gasping and free.

* * *

School ended at the bell—which was, in fact, haunted, or at least enchanted, and rang itself whenever it judged the lesson complete, which Vharen claimed proved bells had souls and Orsen claimed proved Vharen would say anything to avoid fixing it.

Orsen walked home along the river road, past the market where half the stalls sold ordinary bread and turnips and the other half sold charms of wildly varying quality, because that was the valley now: a place where the miraculous and the mundane shared a table and haggled. Old Mistress Corvel, who had spent forty years as the temple's strictest bookkeeper, now ran the most successful stall of all, selling luck-knots she tied herself, and she waved Orsen down with the imperiousness of a woman who had once collected their debt and had never quite gotten over the embarrassment of it.

"Scribe." She pressed a knot into Orsen's hand before any protest could form. "For the house. It's fraying at the edges, I can feel it from here."

"Houses don't fray."

"Yours does. Yours is held together with covenant-thread and stubbornness. Take the knot."

Orsen took the knot. Resistance was pointless; it always had been. "How are the ledgers coming?"

Corvel's face did something complicated. Since the Reforging, she had taken on the work of striking out the old debts—one name a night, by candlelight, reading each account aloud before crossing it through, so that no one's history would simply vanish unremarked. It was slow. It was holy in a way the temple had never managed. She was, everyone agreed, unbearable about it.

"Three hundred remain," she said. "And before you ask—yes, yours was first. Yes, I read it aloud. Yes, I cried, and if you repeat that I'll deny it under oath before the shrine itself." She flapped a hand. "Go home. Your wolf will be sulking."

"He doesn't sulk."

"He absolutely sulks. I've seen him. He lies in your doorway looking tragic until you feed him the good cuts. The whole street talks about it."

The whole street did talk about it, and the whole street was right, and both of them knew it, and neither of them cared.

The house sat where the old scriptorium annex had stood, rebuilt from its own stones by hands that included nearly everyone in the valley, because that was another thing about the valley now: when someone needed building, people built. It was small. It was crooked in two directions. The rosemary had staged a hostile takeover of the eastern wall and showed no signs of negotiating. The door was painted blue, because Beatrix's memories had contained one blue door, in one remembered childhood, and Orsen had wanted it to exist somewhere again.

And in the doorway, filling most of it, gray as weathered slate and large as a pony and pretending magnificently to be asleep, lay the wolf.

"Don't," Orsen said, walking up the path.

One amber eye opened.

"Don't give me the look. Corvel already told me you've been performing tragedy for the entire street."

The eye closed with dignity. The great tail, however, thumped once against the doorstep—the single involuntary betrayal of a creature who had spent centuries perfecting his composure—and Orsen laughed, and scratched the broad head, and stepped over him into the house, and he followed at a decorous distance as though none of it had happened.

Inside, the evening assembled itself the way evenings did now: bread, cheese, a pot of stew that had learned to keep itself warm (a gift from the hearth-spirit who lived in the chimney and charged only gossip as rent), lamplight, quiet. Vharen curled up by the fire in a space worn smooth by years of curling. Orsen sat at the desk by the window—the good desk, the one with the view of the garden—and took out the day's work.

Teaching the children to read the map was one thing. But there was other writing too, slower writing. Every night, Orsen added to a plain paper book, in plain ink, the story of everything: the courtyard, the flight, the shrines, the ford where a friend's fear had cost so much and been forgiven anyway, the altar, the choice. Not because the living map couldn't hold it—it held everything—but because some things ought to exist in a form that could be held in ordinary human hands, by people who would never touch magic, and still be true.

Tonight's page was nearly done. Orsen dipped the pen and finished it:

*Beatrix's memory rests now. It happened in the spring, quietly, the way she seemed to do everything important. The map's rivers stopped remembering her swimming and began simply being rivers. Her mountains settled into themselves. And in the place where her voice had lived in the ink for all these years—a voice I heard perhaps a hundred times, teaching me, scolding me, laughing at me in that dry way of hers—there is now only warmth. Not gone. Finished. There is a difference, and I know it the way I know my own heartbeat, because for seven years her heartbeat and mine shared a wall.*

*I lit a candle for her at the hollow shrine, which is hollow no longer. I told her about the children. I think she would have liked Wren enormously and found Tam exhausting, which is to say she would have liked him too.*

*The covenant holds. Not the old one—that one was built on theft and needed breaking. The new one. Mine. Half-valley, half-magic, all stubborn. Some nights I feel both realms turning in me like two hands clasped, and I am tired in the way that means the work is good.*

By the fire, Vharen's ears twitched. "You're doing the face," he said. His voice was a low rumble, like a landslide that had learned manners.

"What face?"

"The one where you write about feelings and pretend it's scholarship."

"It *is* scholarship. Feelings are primary sources."

"Mm." He stretched, joints popping like green wood. "Sable's memorial is tomorrow. The Order's remnant is holding it. Small turnout expected."

"They invited us?"

"They invite us every year. You decline every year."

Orsen set down the pen. This was the one wound that had never fully closed, and both of them knew it, and neither of them forced it. Sable had fallen at the last altar fighting to end magic forever, and she had been wrong, and she had also—for twenty years before that—been the only person in the valley honest enough to say aloud what everyone else conspired to forget. You could hate a thing and honor it. Orsen had learned that the hard way, in a fortress cell, listening to a reasonable voice offer a comfortable lie.

"Maybe this year," Orsen said.

Vharen's tail moved, just slightly. "Maybe this year," he agreed, in the tone of a wolf who had heard *maybe* annually for two decades and would happily hear it for twenty more.

Outside, the first stars came up over the mountains that remembered being climbed. The hearth-spirit rattled its grate companionably. Somewhere down the lane, children were being called in to supper, and calling back, and calling in again, the oldest negotiation in the world.

Orsen blew out the lamp, and the wolf rose from his place by the fire, and they went together to the blue door, and stood a moment in the cool dark, looking at the garden, the stars, the valley breathing easy beneath them at last.

"Well," Orsen said. "Home."

The wolf leaned his warm weight against their leg—the whole weight, no pretense, no dignity, the way he only did here, only at this door, in this light.

"Yeah," Orsen said softly, answering him. "Mine too."

And behind them, on the wall inside, the living map turned gently in its sleep, and in its far corner the little inked scribe at the little inked desk put down its pen, and rested.
