## 1. The Valley That Refuses Magic

The city of Vellamare did not exist yet, and Cyrus Vane was finishing its harbor.

She worked by lamplight in the back room of her cottage, the one with the north-facing window and the floorboard that lifted, and she worked the way she always worked: fast, sure, and slightly ashamed. The pen moved as if it knew where to go before she did. It usually did. That was the whole trouble with her gift, if you could call it a gift—she never drew what she saw. She drew what would be.

Vellamare's harbor curved like a cupped hand, three breakwaters fanning out from a headland that was currently, in the real world, a sheep pasture forty miles east of the valley. The quays were drawn in fine brown ink, each mooring post individually rendered because Cyrus had learned early that the details mattered—that the more precisely she charted a place that didn't exist, the more surely it *would* exist someday, somewhere, exactly as she'd made it. Nobody knew why. She certainly didn't. Her mother might have known, but her mother had been dead for nineteen years, since Cyrus was four, and had left behind nothing but a cedar box of half-finished maps and a warning delivered so many times it wore smooth as river stone:

*Never let them see what your hands can do.*

Cyrus dipped her pen. In the corner of the parchment, below the compass rose she'd drawn without thinking—a rose with nine points instead of eight, which no honest mapmaker would use—she wrote the date. Not today's date. Today plus fifty years, which was when Vellamare would be founded, according to the ink, which had never once been wrong.

There were thirty-one finished maps beneath her floorboards. Cities, roads, bridges, one entire river delta that had rearranged itself eleven years ago to match her drawing down to the sandbar. She told herself every time she hid another one that this would be the last, that she'd settle for drawing the valley as it was, honest and flat and safe, the kind of cartography that earned a person bread money and no attention at all.

Then the pen would start moving on its own, and she'd let it.

She blew across the parchment to dry the ink. Outside, the evening was doing what valley evenings did: settling blue over the orchards, pulling woodsmoke up from chimneys, calling the sheep down from the high pasture in their slow gray flood. The Valley of Aldenmere sat folded between two ridgelines like a coin in a palm—sheltered, self-contained, unremarkable. That was its genius. That was why her mother had brought them here, or been brought here, or fled here; Cyrus had never learned which, because the story changed depending on who told it and how much they'd drunk.

But everyone agreed on the important part: magic could not follow you into Aldenmere. Whatever old covenant or accident of geography kept the world's wonders out also kept the crown's witch-hunters out, because a hunter's tools were all charms, and charms went dead at the warding stones like lamps in wind. For sixty years the valley had been a pocket of plainness in a kingdom gone strange, and the people who settled it had mostly come here to forget something.

Cyrus had come here to hide something. Same difference.

Her gift was the only magic she'd ever heard of that worked inside the stones, and she'd spent twenty-two years making sure nobody learned that. To the village she was the mapmaker's daughter, competent, quiet, useful—the woman who surveyed boundary disputes and drew fair copies of the mill race and charged fair prices and never, ever talked about her mother beyond a respectful shake of the head. She sold honest maps. She burned the dishonest ones. She had a system.

The system required that she finish hiding Vellamare.

She slid the parchment into the oilcloth sleeve with the practiced economy of long habit, knelt, hooked her fingernails under the loose board beside the hearth, and lifted. The hollow below was lined with waxcloth and smelled of cedar—her mother's box had given up its scent to the whole cavity—and she laid the map flat atop the others, thirty-one under thirty-two, and was reaching to replace the board when she heard the hooves.

Not the rhythm of farm horses. Too many animals, too even a pace, coming down the western road at a trot that meant business rather than homecoming. Cyrus froze with the board half-lifted, the lamplight falling into the hollow, illuminating the top map in the stack: the delta, its channels branching like veins.

The hoofbeats came on. She lowered the board. She stood. She crossed to the window and put her eye to the gap in the shutter.

Four riders came down the western road into the last of the light, and even at this distance Cyrus could see they were wrong for the valley. Their horses were tall, dark, and groomed, not the shaggy hill ponies everyone else rode. They wore travel cloaks over what looked like matched leather, dull black, and they rode in a formation—two ahead, two behind—with the unconscious discipline of soldiers. At their head rode a broad man with a grey streak in his beard, and on his saddle he carried a coiled length of brass and wire that caught the light and threw it back wrong, glinting gold and then green and then a color Cyrus had no name for.

She knew what that was. Every child raised on the valley's stories knew what that was, the way children in other places knew wolves by their tracks.

A compass. A hunter's compass, the kind that didn't point north but pointed at talent—at the low hum of the uncanny in a person, the way a divining rod pointed at water. Except those stories belonged outside the stones, where magic pooled and eddied and could be smelled. Inside Aldenmere there was nothing for such an instrument to find.

Nothing except her.

The thought arrived cold and complete, and Cyrus made herself breathe through it the way she'd taught herself to breathe through panic since childhood: four counts in, seven counts held, eight out. The riders reached the fork where the western road met the market square. They did not turn toward the inn, which any traveler would do at this hour. They paused, all four, as one body, and the man with the grey beard uncoiled the brass thing from his saddle and held it flat on his palm.

Even from the window, even across the square, Cyrus felt the moment it woke. There was no sound, no flash. But the evening changed texture, the way air changes before lightning, and every hair on her arms stood up, and somewhere behind her sternum her own gift stirred like a sleeper rolling over—*there*, it seemed to say, *something like me*—and she clamped down on it with the mental equivalent of both hands.

The needle swung. She couldn't see the needle from here. She watched the man's face instead, watched him read the instrument, watched him say something to the rider beside him, watched all four heads lift and look—not at her cottage, thank every merciful power, but around the square generally, the slow surveying sweep of men taking the measure of a place.

Then the man coiled the compass away, and they rode on toward the inn after all, unhurried, and the blue dusk closed behind them, and the valley went on being the valley.

Cyrus stayed at the window a long time.

*They can't find anything,* she told herself. *The stones kill their charms. That's the whole point of the valley. Mother chose it for that reason.*

*Then why does the compass work at all?* asked the other voice, the one that lived in the same skull and had been keeping accounts longer than she had. *Why did it move?*

Because it was broken. Because the man was a fraud performing theater for provincial rubes. Because any of a dozen reasonable explanations. She listed them the way she listed things when her hands wanted to shake, and by the end of the list her hands were still, and she went and lifted the floorboard anyway, and took out the stack, and looked at what she owned.

Thirty-two maps of places that did not exist, drawn by a woman who should not be able to draw them, in a valley that magic could not enter. Either the third part of that sentence was false—and everything her mother had built her life around was a lie—or the second part was false, and her gift wasn't magic at all, but something older that the stones didn't recognize as foreign because it had never been outside the family that carried it.

Her mother's warning came back to her, worn smooth as ever: *never let them see.* Not *never use it*. Never let them *see*. As though her mother had known that seeing was the beginning of every bad thing—as though her mother had died knowing someone, somewhere, was looking.

The hunters would take rooms at the inn. Hunters on the crown's business always announced themselves eventually; secrecy was for smugglers, and these men rode matched leather with royal tack on their horses. By morning the magistrate would have their papers read aloud in the square, and the valley would learn what they'd come for, and Cyrus would stand in the crowd with her honest face on and listen.

Until then there was work to do. She took the top map from the stack—the delta, oldest of them all, the first one she'd ever drawn at age eleven, when she hadn't yet understood that the ink was prophecy—and she fed it to the hearth fire page by page. It curled and brightened and went to ash. Eleven more followed, each one a year of her life going up in orange light, until the stack was thin enough to fit in the cedar box, and the box fit in the hollow, and the board went back down, and she scattered the ash in the garden where it would pass for stove leavings.

It hurt worse than she'd expected. She'd told herself they were just paper. But watching the delta burn she understood that she'd been burning a version of herself, the version that believed the valley was a sanctuary rather than a shelf—somewhere things were stored, not saved.

By full dark the cottage was clean. Honest maps on the table, honest tools in their rolls, a week of honest commissions queued by the door. She banked the fire, and ate bread and hard cheese standing up, and did not sleep.

Near midnight the hoofbeats woke again—no. Not hoofbeats. She went to the window and saw lanterns moving in the square, a knot of villagers gathered despite the hour, and above them the magistrate's voice carrying in the still air, reading something aloud in the formal cadence of proclamations. Fragments reached her across the dark.

"—by order of the Crown—" 

"—persons possessing the gift, however small—"

"—required to present themselves for assessment—"

Assessment. The word had a soft, administrative sound, the sound of ledgers and waiting rooms, and it was somehow worse for that.

Cyrus pressed her forehead against the cool shutter slats. Below in the square, torchlight moved over upturned faces, and she picked out Bram's grandmother among the crowd, small and straight-backed, and the boy beside her—Bram, the choirboy, thirteen maybe, mouth moving with the hymn someone had started singing against the fear, moving with it the way the whole congregation moved, except that no sound came out of him at all. She'd noticed that about him months ago at market. She'd noticed the hollow look too, and told herself it was none of her affair.

Now the proclamation rolled on overhead, and the hymn wavered, and the compass lay coiled somewhere at the inn with its brass needle pointing—she was suddenly certain, with the sick certainty of the mapmaker who is never wrong about distances—at her floorboards.

Her sanctuary had never been safe. It had only, until tonight, been unnoticed.

Cyrus Vane stepped back from the window, into the dark of her clean, honest cottage, and began, silently, to plan.
