## 2. A Boy Who Cannot Sing

Market day in the valley smelled of wet wool and woodsmoke and the particular sourness of turnips that had frozen once and thawed badly. Cyrus moved through it the way she always did: hood up, eyes down, hands in her pockets where they could stop trembling against her thighs.

She hated market day. Not because of crowds—she could navigate a crowd the way she navigated anything, by mapping it, and she had mapped this one so many times she could walk it blind. She hated it because markets were full of things that didn't exist yet. A farmer selling lambs that would be mutton by spring. A woman trading in apples from an orchard that wouldn't fruit for another month. Promises stacked on promises, all of them invisible roads leading somewhere, and every one of them tugging at her like a fishhook behind her ribs.

Her gift did not care that magic couldn't follow into the valley. Her gift was not magic, exactly. Or it was something older than the distinction, something that lived in her blood the way a river lives in its bed, and the warding stones at the valley's mouth had never managed to dam it entirely. They had only made it quieter. Made *her* quieter. Which was, she supposed, the point.

She bought what she always bought: salt, lamp oil, a heel of hard cheese, thread. She paid in coin counted out slowly, deliberately, so no one would look too closely at her face. And she was nearly done—nearly at the edge of the square, nearly free—when she heard the choir.

The valley chapel had no proper choir. It had six farmers' children and a rector who sang through his nose, and on feast days they performed the old valley hymns with the enthusiasm of people being paid in bread. But today someone had brought a traveling priest, or perhaps just a man with a loud voice, and he stood near the well leading a hymn, and a knot of children had gathered to sing along.

One boy wasn't singing.

He was standing at the edge of the group, maybe fourteen, maybe fifteen, dressed in the grey cassock of a choirboy—the real thing, court-made, the kind with silver stitching at the collar that cost more than the rector earned in a year—and his mouth was moving. Perfectly. Every word, every shape of every vowel, precise as a lockpick working a tumblers. But no sound came out.

Cyrus stopped.

She told herself later that she stopped because the crowd shifted, because a cart blocked the lane, because any of a dozen ordinary reasons. But the truth was simpler and worse: she recognized him. Not his face—she'd never seen him before—but the *shape* of him. The hollow way he held himself, like a house with one room walled off. Like a jug with the bottom knocked out and waxed over so no one would hear it ring wrong.

She knew that shape. She drew that shape. In fifty years of charting places that did not exist, she had learned that every place had a missing piece somewhere—a room no architect could find, a street that ended in blank parchment—and the places were never whole until you found what had been taken from them. People were the same. She had simply never seen a person wear their absence so openly.

The boy's hands rose slightly as the hymn climbed, fingers shaping a chord in the air, and Cyrus saw that they were moving in time not with the melody but with something underneath it—a harmony, or a countermelody, or something only he could hear. His eyes were half-closed. For a moment he looked less like a boy at a market and more like a man listening to a voice calling from very far away, straining toward it, unable to answer.

Then the hymn ended, the little crowd clapped, and the boy opened his eyes and became ordinary again. Almost. He folded his hands. He bowed his head. He stepped back from the singers as though he had merely been watching, a stranger with no part in it.

But his mouth kept moving for three seconds after everything went quiet. Silent words, still singing, still following the song down past its ending into whatever came after. Then he pressed his lips together, hard, the way you press a hand over a wound, and looked at the ground.

Cyrus's chest did something complicated and unwelcome.

She should have walked away. That was the rule. That had been the rule her whole life: notice nothing, know nothing, owe nothing. The valley was safe because she was small in it, because her maps stayed under her floorboards, because she had spent thirty years making herself a woman whom nothing remarkable could happen to. Compassion was a door, and doors let in weather.

She walked away. She got as far as the cheesemonger's stall.

And then the grandmother found her.

* * *

Cyrus saw her coming the way you see weather coming—no single detail, just a pressure change, a wrongness in the crowd's flow. An old woman pushing through the market with the terrible straight-backed determination of someone who has been rehearsing this moment for days. Small, stooped, hands like tree roots wrapped in fingerless mittens. She was heading directly for Cyrus, and Cyrus had time to think, absurdly, *I should map an exit*, before the woman planted herself in front of her like a fencepost.

"You're the Vane girl," the woman said.

"Cyrus Vane, yes." Keep it flat. Keep it boring. "Can I help you, mistress—?"

"Tallow. Marta Tallow." The name meant nothing. Half the valley was named after candles or sheep or both. "They say you read."

"I read."

"Not books." Marta Tallow's eyes were pale as skimmed milk and missed nothing. "I mean *read*. My neighbor's boy broke his leg last winter and you looked at it across the table and said which bone and how bad, and you'd never touched him. Old Hedwig says you told her where her lost goat was before she'd finished asking. Don't bother denying it, girl, I've had a week to collect stories and I've got a sackful."

Cyrus felt the familiar cold slide down her spine, the feeling of a sanctuary becoming a spotlight. "People say a lot of things."

"They do." The old woman reached into her coat. "So I'll say one thing back. I'm not here about goats."

What came out of her coat was folded cloth, and inside the cloth was something that made Cyrus's gift sit up inside her like a dog hearing its name.

It was a piece of hide. Not parchment—hide, cured thin but unmistakably skin, and written on it in ink that had sunk deep and browned with age were lines of script in no alphabet Cyrus knew, looping and interlocking like briars. Except that she *did* know it, some animal part of her knew it, the way you know the shape of your own hand in the dark. It was the script her impossible maps used when they wrote themselves. It was the handwriting of places that hadn't happened yet.

"Where," Cyrus said, and had to start again. Her voice came out wrong. "Where did you get this?"

"It's Bram's contract." The old woman's chin trembled once and then steadied, the tremor of someone who had cried already and budgeted for no more. "My grandson. The choirboy you were watching. Don't look surprised, girl, I've watched you watch things my whole life and I know what noticing looks like." She held the hide out between them. "The men from beneath the mountain wrote it when they took him. Two years ago now. Nobody in this valley can read it. The priests say it's devil-writing. The magistrate says it's a lawful instrument of the crown's debt and none of his business beyond that." Her voice dropped. "But you're not the priests or the magistrate. You're the one who sees things. So *see it*."

Around them the market roared on, oblivious. Somewhere the hymn-leader had started another song. Cyrus's hands, traitorous things, were already rising.

"I charge for readings," she said, because refusing outright would raise more questions than accepting, and because a price was armor, a transaction was a wall, and walls were how she had survived.

"Name it."

"Nothing. This once." The words left her before she could weigh them. *Owe nothing.* Too late. "Come to my cottage at dusk. Not before—I've errands. And tell no one."

Marta Tallow's face did something Cyrus hadn't expected: it crumpled with relief so total it was almost frightening, the relief of a drowning person whose rescuer turns out to be real. "Bless you," she whispered. "Bless you, whatever you are."

"Don't bless me." Cyrus pulled her hood lower and turned away. "Just come at dusk."

* * *

She spent the afternoon pretending to run errands and actually walking the long way around the valley, up past the orchards where the frost had silvered every branch, trying to think.

The problem was that she had already read the contract. Not the words—those she needed light and quiet and probably several hours for. But contracts written on skin weren't really writing, not the way books were. They were *maps*. Maps of a person, drawn in the medium of the person, and her gift had taken one look at that scrap of hide and begun, helpfully, unbidden, to chart it: a boy's outline, and inside the boy's outline a great hollow space shaped exactly like a voice, and leading out of that hollow space a road. A road going down. Down and east and down again, beneath roots and stone and water, to a vast dark place that her gift rendered not as lines but as *music*—as structure, as architecture built of sound, tiered and vaulted and endless.

The court beneath the mountain. Everyone in the valley knew the stories. A kingdom under the earth, older than the crown above it, richer than sense, which bought things ordinary kingdoms didn't put prices on. Years of your life. Your luck. Your shadow, if yours was particularly fine. And sometimes—rarely, secretly, in whispers that stopped the instant anyone listened—your voice.

Two years ago, a debt had come due in the Tallow household. Two years ago, men had come up out of the ground, courteous and terrible, with pen and knife and a strip of prepared hide. And a boy had sung one last time while they wrote.

Cyrus stood among the frosted apple trees and made herself look at the whole shape of it, because looking away was how mistakes happened. The boy, Bram. The hollow where his voice had been. The road down to the Choir. And threading through all of it, faint as watermark, something else—something that made her stomach drop:

A second road. Older. Fainter. Running parallel to Bram's, down the same dark, and ending at the same tiered vaults of sound.

Her gift refused to label it. But it started, she noticed, near a grave in the valley churchyard with a worn stone, and it was the same age as her grief, and she turned away from it so fast she nearly fell on the ice.

*Dusk,* she told herself. *Read the contract first. One disaster at a time.*

* * *

Marta Tallow arrived at dusk with the boy.

Cyrus had expected this and dreaded it; sending the grandmother alone had clearly never been on the table. Bram stood in her doorway like a heron that had wandered indoors—all elbows and uncertainty, grey cassock brushed clean, silver collar-stitching catching the lamplight. Up close his hollow was even louder than at market. Her gift mapped him without permission: *absence, absence, absence, and below it, far below, a filament of connection running south and down, taut as a plumb line.* He was tethered. He was still attached to whatever held his voice.

"Sit," she said. "Both of you. Don't touch anything on the desk."

They sat. The boy sat like someone accustomed to pews, back straight, hands folded, eyes obediently lowered. Only when Cyrus spread the hide contract flat on her desk and set the lamp beside it did he look up—and then he looked at *her*, sharply, searchingly, as if reassessing everything, and Cyrus understood that he was not a passive child in this story. He had begged his grandmother to find her. He had known what she was, or guessed.

She bent over the contract and began to read.

It took hours. The script fought her—not because it was hostile, but because it was *alive*, the way her own maps were alive, and reading living script was like transcribing a conversation happening in another room. But gradually, line by line, the shape of it emerged, and the shape was worse than she had feared.

"It's a sale," she said finally. Her voice sounded scraped. "Your voice, sold in perpetuity to the Court Beneath. In exchange—" she traced the clauses with a fingertip, hating each one, "—the debts of this household are discharged. All of them. Forever. Your family will never owe another coin, another favor, another hour of labor, to anyone."

"That's what they promised," Marta said.

"That's what it says. But listen." Cyrus made herself look at the old woman, then the boy. "Listen carefully, because whoever wrote this wanted it misunderstood. The discharge isn't unconditional. There's a clause here—clause nine, the briar-knot at the bottom left—that says the debt is considered *paid in full* only so long as the purchased property remains in the buyer's possession and use." She paused. "If the voice is ever returned—if the sale is ever undone—the original debts revive. Doubled. Compounded at the court's own rates, which are not rates, they're teeth. And if payment can't be made—" she heard her own voice go flat and careful, the way it did when she was carrying something fragile, "—then the contract extends. To the bloodline. The whole family follows. Grandmother, mother, sisters, everyone. Down beneath the mountain. As voices."

The silence in the cottage was absolute. Outside, wind worried at the eaves.

"So we don't ask for it back," Marta whispered. "We never ask. Even if it's cruel. Even if he—" She looked at her grandson and couldn't finish.

Bram wasn't looking at either of them. He was looking at Cyrus, and his expression was not despair. That was the thing that unsettled her most, later, remembering it. His expression was *calculation*. He tapped one finger against his knee—three beats, rest, two beats, rest—some rhythm from the hymns, unconscious, habitual—and watched her the way she imagined the mountain watched the valley.

Then he raised his hands and signed, quickly, fluently, a language Cyrus didn't know, and his grandmother translated in a cracked whisper: "He says—there's a third option. He says the contract binds the voice, not the singer. He says if the *Choir itself* were to end—if the court that holds it were to fall—every clause falls with it. He says he's thought about this every night for two years."

"He's a child," Cyrus said.

"He's a boy who's had two years to become something else," Marta said quietly. "Girl, why do you think I came to *you*? Not the priests, not the magistrate, not the cunning-men. Because whatever you are, you see roads nobody else can see. And my grandson needs somebody who can see a road down into that mountain and—God forgive us both—maybe back out again."

Cyrus looked down at the contract. Under the lamplight, the briar-script had begun to move—slowly, almost shyly, the way her own maps stirred when she was near—and new lines were surfacing in the margins that she was quite sure had not been there an hour ago. Lines pointing down. Lines converging on the tiered dark. And at the center of that dark, where the Choir sang, one small notation was forming, patient as a spider, in handwriting she recognized because it was hers.

Her gift had already started mapping the way in.

She thought: *No.* She thought: *You owe these people nothing. You owe the world nothing. You have spent your whole life buying safety with smallness and it has worked, it has worked, and tomorrow is market day and next month is winter and there is a life here, small and safe and yours.*

Outside, far off down the valley road, hooves struck the frozen mud in a rhythm too disciplined for any farmer, and lantern-light swung through her window, and a herald's horn blew once—the long note and the short, the crown's call, the sound that meant a proclamation was coming at dawn.

Bram's head came up. Marta's hands closed around her mittens. And Cyrus Vane, cartographer of unmapped realms, looked at the moving ink on the skin-contract and understood that her sanctuary had never been safe at all—only unnoticed, and the noticing had begun.
