## 3. The Proclamation

Bram heard the proclamation the way he heard everything now: through his feet.

The square had been packed tight since the third bell, and the crowd's shuffling traveled up through his boot soles in a language of its own—impatience, dread, the small restless movements of people who wanted to be somewhere else but knew better than to leave before the magistrate dismissed them. He stood near the well because it was the one place in Ashford Market where a boy could loiter without purpose and draw no eye. Well-splashers, bucket-haulers, idlers. The well forgave everyone.

He could not have said aloud, even if he'd had the voice for it, why he'd come. The decree had been nailed to the tollhouse door two days past; everyone in the valley had read it by now, or had it read to them over supper by someone who had. *By order of the Crown: all persons possessed of unlicensed gifts shall present themselves for assessment.* Everyone knew what assessment meant. Everyone knew what happened to the assessed. They went up the mountain road with their families waving, and they did not come back down, and after a decent interval their houses were sold to cousins from over the pass who repainted the shutters and never spoke of them again.

So there was no need to stand in the cold and hear it twice. And yet here he was, hands jammed in his pockets, watching his breath ghost upward while the magistrate's clerk arranged papers on the platform with the fussy precision of a man who believed ceremony could make cruelty respectable.

Bram watched things. It was what remained to him. When you lose your voice at eleven years old—not lose it, he corrected himself, not the way a man loses a knife or a season—you learn to live through your eyes. You become a creature of observation. You learn that most people say nothing worth saying anyway, that faces speak more honestly than mouths, and that silence, imposed long enough, turns into a kind of hearing. He could read a room the way the priest read scripture: fluently, and always finding doom in it.

The room today was saying: *someone is about to be taken.*

* * *

The magistrate was a gray man named Corvus, which Bram had always thought was either a joke or an omen, depending on how much faith you put in names. Corvus climbed the platform steps, accepted the scroll without reading it—he'd written it, or at least signed it—and turned to face the square.

Bram found Cyrus Vane three rows deep on the far side, near the chandler's stall.

He'd noticed her first at market last week, or rather he'd noticed her noticing him, which almost no one bothered to do anymore. She had looked at him the way she might look at a map with a river running through the wrong country—with recognition, with a cartographer's horror at an error she understood perfectly. Most people looked at him and saw only a choirboy who'd gone quiet. She had seen the hollow. She had known exactly what shape of thing had been carved out of him, as if she kept a chart of such absences.

His grandmother had made him bring her the contract after that. Wrapped in oilcloth, pressed flat between boards, carried through the streets like something both precious and cursed. The map-woman had unrolled it in her dim rooms above the chandlery and gone very still, and then she'd read it twice, and then she'd looked at Bram with an expression he'd spent the following days trying to decode. Pity, partly. But also something hotter underneath the pity. Something that looked, uncomfortably, like guilt.

Now she stood in the crowd with her arms crossed against the cold, and Bram watched her because watching her was easier than thinking about midwinter, when the contract came due, and his mother's hands, and his little sister's voice—his sister who still had hers, who sang rounds in the kitchen until grandmother told her gently to hush, and every time she hushed Bram felt the debt settle another inch closer to all of them.

Corvus cleared his throat. The square went quiet the way water goes still before it freezes.

"Good people of Ashford Valley." His voice carried easily; magistrates' voices are chosen for carrying. "You will have read the decree. Some of you will have hoped it did not concern you. Let me relieve you of that hope."

A ripple through the crowd. Beside Bram, a farmer shifted his weight; behind him, a woman gathered her child closer.

"The crown has learned"—Corvus consulted the scroll anyway, though Bram would have bet his sister's voice he wasn't reading—"that this valley harbors persons of unsanctioned talent. Gifted persons. Workers of what the law calls *unmapped arts*." A pause, letting the word land. "Such persons are hereby commanded to present themselves at the assize hall within seven days, for assessment of their gift."

Assessment. The word had a clean bureaucratic sound, like *tithe*, like *levy*. Bram had learned young that the worst things wore the tamest words. His family had signed a contract once with words just as tame. *Consideration rendered. Services rendered in perpetuity.* Nobody had told an eleven-year-old that *services* meant standing in a stone gallery under the mountain while men in gray robes took his voice out of him like a tooth, carefully, root and all, and left him hollow-mouthed in the pew while the congregation sang around his silence.

"You may ask," Corvus continued, "what manner of gifts concern the crown." He smiled. It was a professional smile, distributed evenly across the front rows. "I will tell you. The crown concerns itself with any art that alters roads, ways, distances, or passages. Any hand that can chart what does not exist. Any person whose presence makes maps lie."

Bram felt the crowd's attention drift the way it always drifted, toward suspicion of neighbors. He saw old Marta Tull glance sideways at the tanner, who was rumored to find lost livestock too reliably. He saw the ferryman's son go pale, because everyone knew his father's crossings were uncannily safe.

But Bram was not watching the crowd.

He was watching Cyrus Vane.

* * *

She had gone white.

Not dramatically—the map-woman was not a dramatic woman—but with a completeness that frightened him to see, the way a hillside goes still before it slides. Her arms uncrossed slowly. Her hands hung at her sides, and he saw her fingers move, faintly, tracing shapes against her skirt. Drawing. He realized, with a small cold shock, that she was sketching escape routes on her own body. Left shoulder to the chandler's alley. Right hip to the drainage cut behind the tannery. He had grown up in this square; he knew those routes; he could follow her fingers like text.

*Any hand that can chart what does not exist.*

Her eyes lifted from Corvus and swept the square—not searching for exits anymore but for something else, and Bram followed her gaze and understood: she was looking at faces. Reading them. Seeing who had noticed her going white. Seeing who remembered that the Vane woman drew maps nobody could verify, maps of places no one had been.

Her eyes passed over him.

Stopped.

Came back.

For one moment they regarded each other across three rows of neighbors and forty feet of frozen air, and Bram thought, absurdly, of the contract. Of ink on skin. Because that was what her look contained—not fear exactly, though fear was in it, fear like groundwater rising—but a message, transmitted without a single sound, in a language he knew better than anyone alive:

*I am seen. What do I do now?*

And because he could not answer in words, he answered in the only way he could. He gave her a single nod. Small. Almost nothing. The nod a choirboy gives when the soloist loses their place and looks to the section for help: *keep going, we're with you, don't stop.*

Something moved through her face then. He watched it happen the way he watched weather come over the valley rim—a change in pressure, a darkening, then light breaking in the wrong place. The fear didn't leave her. That was the remarkable thing. He could see it staying, taking up residence, settling into her shoulders like a tenant who intended to outlive the landlord. But under it, through it, something else rose to meet it. Resolve, he would have called it later, though resolve seemed too tidy a word for what was actually happening, which was less a decision than a kindling. Fear catching fire and becoming fuel.

Corvus was still talking. "...seven days. Those who surrender freely will be assessed with leniency. Those who must be sought"—he let the sentence hang, then folded the scroll—"will be found. The crown employs compasses that do not fail."

Compasses. Bram filed the word away with the instinct of a boy who had survived four years in a house of creditors by knowing which threats were procedural and which were promises. That one was a promise.

The dismissal bell rang. The crowd exhaled and began to break apart, slowly, reluctantly, everyone pretending they weren't looking at everyone else. Corvus descended the platform with his clerk scurrying behind, and the square dissolved into its ordinary noise, vendors calling prices that suddenly sounded obscene, a dog barking at nothing, children being pulled home by hands slightly too tight.

Cyrus Vane crossed the square toward him.

She walked unhurried. That was the first thing he noticed—that she had chosen, visibly and deliberately, not to run. Running would be a confession. She walked like a woman buying thread, and when she reached him she stood close enough that her next words would reach no ears but his, and she didn't whisper, because whispering draws eyes, but spoke at the low ordinary pitch of neighborly talk.

"Well," she said. "You heard the man."

Bram nodded.

"They're not after hedge-talents. Not really. Livestock-finding, safe ferries—that's noise. Cover." Her eyes flicked toward the tollhouse, where a rider in crown colors sat his horse like a man waiting for a name to be spoken to him. "They're after cartomancy. Mapping. Someone told them a map-maker lives in this valley, and someone described her work very precisely." She paused. "You know what's strange? I've hidden my whole life. Fifty years ahead, cities that won't exist—I draw them and burn half and bury the rest, and I've been so careful. And it took them one proclamation to make me stand in a square full of my neighbors and realize the hiding never worked. I was never safe. I was unnoticed. There's a difference, and the difference just rode into town wearing crown colors."

Bram held her gaze. Then, deliberately, he raised one hand and tapped two fingers against his sternum. Once. Twice.

*Me too,* the gesture said. *I know about being hunted by paper.*

She looked at his hand. Understanding moved across her face—she'd read the contract, she knew precisely what paper had done to him—and something in her expression hardened from fear into anger, and Bram thought: *there it is.* Anger was sturdier than courage. Anger you could build with.

"They'll come for you too, eventually," she said quietly. "Your family's contract is crown paper, did you know that? I read the seal marks. The court beneath the mountain isn't some independent creditor—it's an arm of the throne. Which means when the hunt widens, and it will widen, your family's debt becomes leverage. They'll offer to forgive it in exchange for—" She stopped herself. Looked at his mouth, the way people did and immediately regretted. "In exchange for whatever's left of you."

The bells tolled the hour. Somewhere behind them, the crown rider turned his horse and began, casually, to ride the perimeter of the square.

Cyrus straightened. Bram watched her make the choice—he actually watched it happen, saw it arrive in her eyes like weather, the same kindling he'd witnessed during the proclamation, only now banked and steady and aimed. When she spoke again her voice was low and even, and it was the voice of someone who had stopped asking whether she dared and started planning how.

"Not seven days," she said. "Tonight. There's a gap in the warding stones below the mill—the old shepherd's path. My maps show what's beyond this valley, Bram. Places the crown hasn't reached. Roads that don't exist yet." She glanced at the rider, then back at him, and her mouth did something that was almost a smile. "I've drawn them all my life. I suppose it's time to walk one."

She turned to go, then paused, and looked at him with the directness that had unsettled him the first day and steadied him ever since.

"You know the mountain better than anyone in this valley knows anything. You lived under its shadow your whole childhood, and you paid it the worst price anyone's paid." Her jaw set. "If I'm leaving tonight, I'm not leaving alone. I'm going to take back what they took from you. Every stolen voice in that court—including, if my suspicions are right, ones I have my own reasons for wanting free."

Bram stared at her.

No one had ever offered him that. Not rescue—rescue was charity, and charity he'd learned to refuse with a smile. This was different. This was a woman drawing a line on a map from her own danger to his, and saying: *these connect.*

His chest did something complicated and silent. Then he lifted his chin toward the mountain road, and tapped the rhythm against his thigh—one beat, rest, two beats, rest—the old hymn rhythm his grandmother used to hum, the one that meant *ready when you are.*

Cyrus Vane smiled properly then, fierce and brief as a struck match.

"Sunset," she said. "Pack nothing that matters. Anything that matters, they'll use against you."

She walked away across the emptying square, unhurried still, a woman buying thread, while behind her the crown rider watched her go and, Bram was almost certain, reached down to adjust something hanging from his saddle.

It glinted like glass.

Like the glass face of a compass.
