## 4. What the Contract Cost

The contract was written on skin.

Not parchment made from skin—Cyrus had seen vellum before, had handled calfskin smooth as river stones, and this was not that. This was skin that had once been someone's, cured thin as onion paper and stretched across a frame of blackthorn wood, and when she held it up to the lamplight she could see the ghost of pores, the faint whorl of what might have been a thumbprint near the lower left corner. Someone had signed this. Or someone had been signed.

She set it down on her worktable and pressed her palms flat against the wood until the trembling stopped.

Bram's grandmother sat across from her in the good chair, the one Cyrus's mother had reupholstered with geese-down before Cyrus was born. The old woman's hands were folded in her lap like sleeping birds. She had walked three miles through the market crowd to find Cyrus, had pressed a coin into Bram's hand and whispered something to him that made the boy go pale and still, and then had followed Cyrus home without asking whether she would come. She had simply assumed. As if Cyrus were the kind of person who read contracts written on skin every day before supper.

"His father signed it," the grandmother said. "Before Bram was born. Before any of them knew what it was they were signing."

Cyrus looked at the skin again. The writing on it was not ink. It was scar tissue, raised in fine lines like the marks left by a branding iron worked by a very patient hand, and the script was one she did not recognize—which meant nothing, because Cyrus recognized very little and invented the rest. That was her gift, or her curse, or whatever you called the thing she kept folded up beneath her floorboards along with forty years of cities that had never been built: she could read what did not exist yet. Maps of places still unborn. Roads not yet cut. And apparently, though she had never tried it, contracts not yet fulfilled.

"Read it," the grandmother said. Not begging. The woman had the voice of someone who had done all her begging years ago and found the well dry. "You're the only one in this valley who can."

*How does she know that?* Cyrus almost asked. But she already knew the answer, or suspected it, and suspicion was a door she did not want to open tonight. Her mother had known too. Her mother had said things, near the end, about blood and paper and the price of seeing too far ahead, and Cyrus had been eleven and had not listened, because eleven-year-olds do not listen, and then her mother had walked out one autumn morning and never come back, and the valley had closed over the absence like water over a dropped stone.

"I'll try," Cyrus said.

She unstoppered the lamp and turned the wick high. The light caught the scar-script and made it gleam, and for a moment—the length of one held breath—the contract seemed to breathe with it. Then she bent over it and began to read.

* * *

It took her three hours.

The language shifted as she went. It began in formal legalese, the kind stamped into land deeds and tax rolls, and then slid sideways into something older, something that tasted of cold stone and deep water, and by the middle pages Cyrus understood that she was not reading one document but two, layered over each other like double-entry bookkeeping. The surface text was a debt agreement. The text beneath it was a harvest schedule.

The surface text first. A man named Aldous Merrin—Bram's father, though the surname was different, which told its own story about how many times this family had run—had borrowed from an institution identified only as *the Court Beneath*. The sum was modest. Cruelly modest. Eleven crowns, which was what a farmhand earned in a season, which was the kind of money a desperate man borrowed to bury a wife or buy seed after a blight, and the interest was written in a way that made Cyrus's gift itch behind her eyes, because the numbers did not behave like numbers. They grew when unobserved. She checked them four times and each time the total owed had increased while she wasn't looking, patiently, politely, the way moss grows.

The collateral was listed on the third page. Not land. Not livestock. Not labor.

*A voice, being the whole and entire singing voice of the issue of the borrower, male issue preferred, to be delivered to the Court upon the child's seventh year, there to be entered into service of the Choir in perpetuity.*

Male issue preferred. Cyrus thought of Bram at the market that afternoon, mouthing the hymn while the other boys sang around him, his jaw moving through shapes his throat would never make. Seven years old. He would have been seven when they came for him. She tried to picture it—a collector arriving at the family's door, polite as a tax assessor, holding out a hand for a child's voice the way you'd hold out a hand for a rent payment—and her stomach turned over and refused the thought entirely.

There was a clause about maintenance. The Court, it seemed, maintained its assets. The boy would be fed, clothed, housed beneath the mountain. His voice would be *kept in good repair*. In exchange, the family's remaining debts would be frozen—not forgiven, never forgiven, but frozen, like a pond in winter, solid enough to walk on so long as no one disturbed the ice.

And then the final clause. Cyrus read it five times because she kept hoping she had misread it, and her gift, treacherous thing, kept confirming that she had not.

*Should payment be interrupted—should the asset be reclaimed, damaged, silenced beyond use, or otherwise rendered unserviceable—the outstanding balance shall fall due in full upon the whole of the borrower's line, principal and accrued interest both, such balance to be collected in kind.*

Collected in kind. From the whole of the line. From the grandmother sitting in the good chair, from whatever brothers and sisters Bram had scattered through the valley, from children not yet born whose names were already, somewhere in that growing arithmetic, accruing interest.

"When is midwinter?" Cyrus asked, though she knew.

"Six weeks past harvest," the grandmother said. "Eleven weeks from tomorrow."

* * *

She put the contract down and walked to the window because if she stayed at the table she was going to be sick onto a document written on human skin, and that felt like a desecration even if the document deserved it.

Outside, the valley lay under the first true dark of autumn. Woodsmoke stood up straight from the chimneys. Somewhere a dog was barking at nothing, and somewhere else the crown riders who had come that morning were bedding down their horses, and their presence sat on the valley like a hand on the back of the neck. Two kinds of hunters in one week, Cyrus thought. The crown's, hunting the gifted. And the mountain's, hunting its debts. The valley had never been safe. It had only ever been unnoticed, and the noticing had begun.

"You knew," she said to the grandmother. "When you asked me to read it. You knew what it said."

"I knew most of it." The old woman did not flinch. "I've heard it read aloud twice, in the old tongue, by men who came to count us. But hearing isn't understanding. The words slide off. I needed someone to look at it with eyes that see what isn't there yet." She paused. "I needed to know if there was a way out of it. Written small, in a corner. There always is, in crown law. Some mercy clause. Some escape."

"Is there?"

"No," the grandmother said simply. "You read it. Is there?"

Cyrus turned back to the table. The contract lay there, breathing its slow scar-tissue breath. And here was the thing she could not say aloud, the thing that had been rising in her since the second hour of reading, since the moment the layered texts had separated and she had seen what lay underneath:

There was no escape clause. But there was something else. Something she doubted even the collectors fully understood, because it was buried in the deep text, in the cold-stone language, in phrases that predated the Court itself. The contract did not merely describe the sale of a voice. It described a *fitting*. The words used were architectural. *Provision.* *Placement.* *Completion.* The Choir, the deep text said, lacked a note. Had always lacked it. The contract for Bram's voice was not a contract at all but a search warrant, a net cast wide through generations of borrowers, seeking one specific timbre, one missing frequency, and Bram—

Bram, who mouthed hymns he could not sing, whose silence had a shape to it that Cyrus had noticed across a crowded market square the way you notice a wrong note—

Bram was either the answer or the last failed candidate. The deep text did not say which. It only said that the Court waited, and waited, and that midwinter was not a deadline so much as an *audition*.

She could not say any of this. The grandmother wanted a mercy clause, and there wasn't one, and the truth Cyrus had found instead was worse than no mercy—it was a purpose, and purposes are heavier than debts.

"There's no way out written in it," Cyrus said. "But contracts are read by people, and people can be lied to, delayed, misdirected. Nothing is due until midwinter. That's eleven weeks. Eleven weeks is a country. You can cross a lot of country in eleven weeks."

The grandmother looked at her for a long time. Her eyes were the same grey as Bram's, Cyrus realized. The same flat, waiting grey, the color of a sky deciding whether to snow.

"You're going to help him," she said. Not a question.

"I'm going to think about helping him," Cyrus said, which was a lie, and they both knew it, and the old woman had the grace not to smile.

* * *

After she left—refusing payment, refusing supper, taking only the loan of Cyrus's lantern for the walk home—Cyrus sat alone with the contract and the lamp and the truth she had been circling all evening like a moth around a flame it cannot bring itself to touch.

Her maps had guided the debt-collectors here.

She had not meant them to. Of course she had not. Ten years ago, when she was nineteen and reckless with a gift she barely understood, she had drawn a road. Just a road—one of hundreds, one of the dozens she'd burned or buried or hidden under floorboards—but this one had survived, tucked inside an atlas she'd sold to a traveling book merchant for bread money, because nineteen-year-olds need to eat and do not think about consequences. The road had connected the lowland trade towns to this valley through the western pass, a pass that did not exist when she drew it, a pass that her gift had insisted *would* exist, carved by floodwater within a decade, and it had. The flood had come. The pass had opened. And the merchant's atlas had passed from hand to hand until it reached the desks of men whose business was finding places that were hard to reach, and the Court Beneath the Mountain had sent its collectors west through a gap in the hills that Cyrus Vane had invented at her kitchen table with a goose-quill and a pot of walnut ink.

She had never proven it. She had no proof now. But she remembered the map—she remembered every map she had ever made, that was part of the curse—and she remembered the little flourish she'd added, young and vain, a decorative border of musical notes around the cartouche. Notes. On a map that led collectors to a valley where they would find a family to strip a child's voice from.

Coincidence, probably. Almost certainly. Her gift did not choose what it revealed; it simply saw, and she simply drew.

Almost certainly.

She got down on her knees and pried up the floorboard. Beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the archive of her life's secret sin: forty maps of places that did not exist, drawn in her own hand across twenty years, each one accurate, each one prophetic, each one a door she had opened without knowing what walked through. She unwrapped them and spread them across the floor by lamplight, hunting for one map in particular, and found it in less than a minute because she always found them, because they were stitched into her memory like scars.

The western pass. The musical border. The road running clean and certain from the lowlands to the valley, drawn ten years before the flood carved it real.

She knelt over it for a long time. The lamp guttered. Outside, the dog had stopped barking, and the silence of the valley pressed against the windows, and somewhere beneath all that silence, beneath the stone and the roots and the old dark, a court full of stolen voices was singing the world into shape, and one of the notes it needed was walking around in a mute choirboy's chest, and the road that had brought the collectors to him ran straight through her own guilty hand.

*You drew the road,* she thought. *Now draw another one.*

Because that was the other thing, the thing she had confirmed in the third hour of reading, the thing that had made her hands go cold and steady at the same time. The deep text of the contract was not just a harvest schedule. It was directions. Buried in the cold-stone language, woven into the clauses like a watermark, was a description of the way down—the approaches to the Court, the doors, the turns, the long stair. The collectors' route, written into every contract so that no courier could ever claim to be lost. The Court Beneath the Mountain had told her, in the act of binding a child's voice, exactly how to come and take it back.

Cyrus reached for fresh paper. Her quill hovered. And then, without deciding to, without permitting herself to decide, she began to draw—not the valley, not the pass, not any place that existed, but the road down, the impossible road, charting as she went a route no living cartographer had ever mapped, her hand moving with the terrible fluency of the gift she had spent her whole life hiding, while upstairs in the loft above her studio, in the room she rented to the quiet boy who mouthed his hymns, Bram slept without dreaming, and did not yet know that his landlady was drawing him a way into hell.

* * *

Dawn found the map finished and Cyrus unchanged in every visible way and altered in every way that mattered. She rolled it, tied it, and set it beside the door with her boots and her mother's old traveling cloak, and then she went to wake Bram for breakfast, because whatever came next, the boy would need feeding first.

Some things, her mother had always said, you do in order. Even the end of the world.
