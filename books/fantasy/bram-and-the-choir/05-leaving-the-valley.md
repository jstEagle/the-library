## 5. Leaving the Valley

The warding stones stood at the valley's mouth like a row of bad teeth, and Cyrus had spent her whole life pretending not to notice them.

Everyone in the valley noticed them, of course. Children were taught to touch them for luck on their way to market. Farmers left sheaves of wheat at their bases at harvest, a tithe nobody remembered starting. The stones were gray granite, taller than a man, carved with spirals so worn they looked less like symbols than like fingerprints pressed into the rock by some enormous patient hand. And every soul who passed between them felt the same small thing: a faint pressure behind the eyes, a sensation like walking through a curtain of cold water, and then nothing. Just the valley again, safe and ordinary and utterly without magic.

That was the point. That had always been the point.

Cyrus had learned the truth of the stones when she was nine years old, from her mother, in the last conversation they ever had that mattered.

"Feel that?" her mother had asked, taking her hand as they walked home from market. Nine-year-old Cyrus nodded—the cold-water shiver, the pressure. "Good. That means it's working. The valley refuses magic, Cyrus. It doesn't just lack it. It *refuses* it. Anything with power in its blood gets wrung out crossing those stones, like laundry. Whatever you are inside—" her mother had squeezed her hand, hard, almost painfully— "stays outside. You come home empty, and you fill back up again only when you leave."

Cyrus hadn't understood, then, why her mother's voice shook. She'd understood only that her mother was frightened, and that the fear had a shape: the stones, the valley, the life they lived here, small and gray and safe.

*Safe*, Cyrus thought now, standing in the dark at the tree line above the stones with a packed satchel and a boy who couldn't speak beside her. *It was never safe. It was only unnoticed.*

She'd told Bram to meet her here at moonrise, and he had come—of course he had come, because his grandmother had pressed both her hands to Cyrus's cheeks that afternoon and said, in the voice of a woman spending her last coin, *"Take him. Whatever it costs you, take him where they can't follow."* He stood now at the edge of the trees with his one bag over his shoulder, thin as a birch sapling, watching the stones with an expression she was beginning to learn how to read despite the silence where his voice should have been. His mouth was set. His hands were still. But his eyes moved constantly, cataloguing, always cataloguing—a boy who had learned to live entirely through what he could see, because it was all he had left.

He looked at her now and raised his eyebrows. *Are we doing this?*

"We're doing this," Cyrus said, and was surprised at how steady her voice came out. Her hands were not steady. Her hands had not been steady since the magistrate read the proclamation in the square, since she'd watched the words *all persons harboring unnatural gifts shall surrender themselves for assessment* land on the crowd like a net thrown over fish. Since she'd understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the hunt was for *her*.

Below them, the crown riders' campfires glowed along the valley road. Three fires. Maybe eight men. They'd arrived two days ago and shown no sign of leaving, going door to door with their papers and their questions and their compasses—Bram's grandmother had warned her about the compasses, little brass instruments that didn't point north but pointed at talent, at the thing Cyrus had spent twenty-six years hiding beneath her floorboards and behind her careful, unremarkable face.

By morning, she estimated, the compasses would find her. The riders were methodical. They worked outward from the center of the valley, house by house, and hers was three doors from the well.

So: tonight, or never.

She touched Bram's arm and pointed down the slope, tracing the route she'd memorized: down through the pines, along the drainage ditch that ran behind the tanner's fence, coming up on the stones from the east where the ground dipped and the road bent away. There was a gap between the fourth stone and the fifth—not a gap in the magic, she suspected, but a gap in the sightlines, a place where a person could cross without being seen from the road.

Bram nodded once and started down without hesitation, moving quick and light over the pine needles, and Cyrus followed, and the last thing she saw of her sanctuary was the glow of the hunters' fires against the underside of the clouds.

* * *

The ditch smelled of tannin and rot, and Cyrus kept low in it, mud soaking through the knees of her trousers, thinking absurdly of the maps she'd left rolled under her floorboards—the city that wouldn't exist for fifty years, the coastline that hadn't eroded yet, the archipelago rising from a sea that was currently open water. She'd buried them deeper this time, under the loose stone of the hearth foundation, wrapped in oilcloth. If the hunters found them—

But they wouldn't recognize them. That was the mercy of her gift, if it was a gift: her maps looked like artistry, like fantasy, like the work of a very skilled and slightly mad cartographer. Only she knew they were records. Only she knew that when she closed her eyes and let the gift move through her, she wasn't imagining places. She was *reading* them. Places that didn't exist yet, pressing themselves against the membrane of the world like fruit against cloth, and her mind simply—touched them. Charted them. Drew what would be.

Her mother had called it a wound. "You weren't given anything," she'd said once, drunk on festival wine, staring into the fire. "You were *left* something. There's a difference."

Cyrus crept forward in the ditch and tried not to think about that.

The stones rose ahead of them, pale in the moonlight, and the gap between the fourth and fifth was exactly where she remembered it—she'd mapped this valley a hundred times in her head, ordinary maps, real maps, the kind any surveyor might draw, because knowing your own ground was the first rule of hiding. Bram crouched beside her in the shadow of the fourth stone, close enough that she could see the pulse beating in his throat, and looked at her with a question in his face.

*What happens when we cross?*

And Cyrus realized, with a lurch, that she didn't know. Twenty-six years of living wrung out and refilled, wrung out and refilled, and she had never once been on the outside of the stones with her gift intact. She knew what she was when she was empty. She had no idea what she was when she was full.

She held up one finger—*wait*—and listened. Wind in the pines. Water in the ditch. Far off, a dog barking twice and stopping, which was nothing, probably nothing. No hoofbeats. No voices.

She took Bram's hand. It was cold, and it gripped hers hard enough to hurt, and she understood suddenly that this boy, who had already lost the single most intimate thing a person could lose, was about to walk out of the only world he knew on the strength of her word alone.

"It'll be all right," she whispered, and hated how much it sounded like a lie, and stepped between the stones.

The cold-water feeling came, the pressure behind the eyes, the curtain—and then, instead of ending, it *reversed*.

Cyrus had braced herself for emptiness. What flooded into her instead was everything.

It came like cold water, yes—her mother had chosen the right image—but cold water after years of thirst, cold water in a flood, cold water that did not ask permission. It poured through the place inside her that had always been hollow, the place she'd kept carefully unfurnished for twenty-six years, and it filled it in a single heartbeat, and then it kept pouring, and Cyrus understood in one white-hot instant that the valley hadn't been protecting her from the world.

It had been protecting the world from *her*.

She heard herself make a sound. She was distantly aware of Bram's hand crushing hers, of him pulling her the last few steps past the fifth stone, of the ground tilting. Because the gift wasn't waking—it had never been asleep, it had been *starving*, and now it fed, and what it fed on was the world itself.

She could see it. That was the horror and the glory of it. She closed her eyes and the darkness filled with lines—roads that didn't exist yet threading through the hills like veins under skin, a bridge arcing over the river two miles downstream where no bridge stood, a town growing out of a hillside forty years from now, its chimneys smoking, its bells ringing, its streets laid out in her mind's eye with terrible loving precision. And beyond that, further, deeper: the mountains to the south folding themselves into shapes they hadn't yet chosen, a pass that would exist, a road that would be cut, a *door*—

A door in a cliff face, far to the south, under stone older than kingdoms.

She gasped and opened her eyes and the vision broke apart, and she was on her knees in wet grass with Bram kneeling over her, his mouth open in a soundless shout, his hands hovering over her shoulders afraid to touch her.

"I'm fine." Her voice came out wrong, resonant, as if the air itself were carrying it further than air should. "I'm fine, I just—"

She looked down at her own hands.

They were glowing. Faintly, guttering, like embers under ash—but glowing, and as she watched, the light gathered along her fingertips and began to *move*, tracing lines in the air, sketching the ghost of a map against the night. A road. A fork. A river crossing. The gift was drawing without her, charting the dark around them the way a struck bell rings whether or not anyone asked for music, and every line it drew was true.

Bram grabbed her wrists. He pulled her hands down, pinned them against the grass, and looked at her—really looked at her—with an expression she'd seen once before, on the face of a woman at market whose son had been taken by the court beneath the mountain. Not fear of her. Fear *for* her, which was worse, because it meant he understood, somehow, what she was only beginning to understand herself.

This is what the compasses will find, she thought. Not the woman. This. This light, these lines, this ringing.

They had maybe hours before the hunters realized their quarry had flown. Less, if the gift kept shining like a beacon into the night sky.

"Bram." His eyes snapped to hers. "I need you to do something for me, and I need you to do it fast." She pulled one hand free and pointed south, toward the black shoulder of the mountains. "There's a road. Not a real road—not yet, not for anyone else. But I can see it, and I think—I think I can *make* us follow it. I don't know how long I can hold it. I don't know what it costs. But the hunters have compasses, and right now I am the loudest thing in this valley, and we cannot be here when they come looking."

He stared at her. Then he did something she hadn't expected: he squeezed her hand, once, deliberately, the way you'd squeeze someone's hand across a table to say *I'm with you*. Then he pointed at her glowing fingers, and at the dark, and made a gesture like a pen writing.

*Draw,* the gesture said. *We'll follow.*

Something in Cyrus's chest cracked open—gratitude, terror, and underneath both, a wild exhilaration she hadn't felt since childhood, since the first time she'd finished a map of a city that didn't exist and known, known absolutely, that she was seeing truth. She had spent her whole life being small on purpose. And here, on the cold grass outside the valley that refused her, with a mute boy holding her hand, she was enormous.

She closed her eyes and reached for the road she'd seen.

It came to her eagerly. Too eagerly. The line unspooled from her chest like thread from a spindle, silver-bright against the dark hillside, running south through the pines, over the ridge, down into the next valley—and as it went, the world along it *shifted*. Grass leaned aside. Stones rolled from their sockets. A path pressed itself flat where no path had been, and Cyrus felt each change like a finger drawn across her own skin, because the road wasn't separate from her. The road was *her*, poured out onto the earth, and every mile of it cost her something she couldn't name.

Stop, she told herself. Smaller. Just enough for two people to walk.

The road dimmed to a faint ribbon, barely visible, winding south. Bram hauled her upright, shouldered both their bags, and took the first step onto it, and the grass closed behind his heels like water behind a swimmer.

They walked all night. Cyrus counted her heartbeats to keep the gift leashed, and the road held, and the mountains grew, and somewhere behind them—she never saw it, but she felt it, the way you feel a storm approaching through a shut window—the compasses in the hunters' camp swung as one, found nothing where she had been, and began to search.

Near dawn, on a ridge overlooking a country she had never seen and yet knew intimately, Cyrus stopped and looked back. The valley was a gray smudge between the hills, invisible now, already becoming memory. Somewhere in it was her mother's grave, and her floorboards, and her whole careful life.

Bram stood beside her. He followed her gaze, then touched her elbow and pointed at the horizon—at the mountain far to the south, the one her blood-map had shown her, the one with the door.

Then he looked at her, and tilted his head, and raised his eyebrows in a question she couldn't answer:

*What did it reveal?*

Because that was the thing she hadn't told him. In the flood, in the first white instant when the gift had poured in and drawn without her permission, it had drawn one thing more clearly than all the others. A door in a cliff face, far south, under old stone. And around that door, in letters that were not letters, in a script her gift seemed to *recognize*—

Her own name.

Not Cyrus Vane. Something older. Something the gift had written there long before she was born, waiting for her to come and read it.

Cyrus turned south and started walking, and did not look back again.
