## 6. The Road of Borrowed Names

We walked out of the valley wearing names that had never belonged to anyone.

I chose them myself, standing at the ford where the road split toward the lowlands, testing them in my mouth like coins I suspected were clipped. *Marren*, for me. *Tally*, for Bram. Small names. Dull names. The kind of names a tax clerk writes down without looking up.

"Say it back to me," I told him.

Bram mouthed it — *Tally* — and tapped his chest twice, then pointed at me and made a shape with his lips that was unmistakably *Marren*, though without sound it looked almost like a curse. He'd developed a whole language of mouthings on the road, half lip-reading and half theater, and he deployed it now with the flat expression of a boy who found the entire business of false identities beneath him.

"It's not funny. If someone asks you your name and you hesitate, we're finished."

He rolled his eyes and tapped his throat twice — his sign for *voice*, which he also used for *promise*. Then he pointed at me again: *Marren*. Pointed at himself: *Tally*. Done. Bored now. He picked up our bundle and started down the lowland road as if borrowed names were the most natural thing in the world, which, I supposed, for a boy whose voice had been sold out from under him, they might be.

I followed, and did not tell him what I could not stop feeling: that the valley's silence was still draining out of my bones, and something else was pouring in to fill the space. That every mile from the warding stones, the gift under my skin grew louder, like water rising in a cellar. That when I looked at the road ahead, I didn't just see the road. I saw the roads it wanted to become.

Three days south of the valley, the land changed. The hills went soft and green, stitched with sheep tracks and drystone walls, and the villages came closer together, close enough that smoke from one chimney mingled with the next. We learned the rhythm of borrowed life quickly. We slept in hay barns when the weather held and paid for loft space with my mapwork when it didn't — honest maps, drawn from sight, the kind any journeyman cartographer might sell. It felt strange, drawing the world as it actually was. Like speaking in a whisper after years of shouting.

Bram earned his keep differently. He was good with animals in a way I couldn't explain — horses that bit their owners let him handle their hooves, and a farmer's dog that hated everyone slept against his back all night. And he had a way of listening. Not hearing — listening. When two farmers argued about a boundary line, Bram would follow the whole dispute with his eyes, head tilted, and at the end tap out the solution on the fence rail: three taps here, two there, until one of them laughed and said the boy was smarter than the magistrate.

They liked him everywhere we went. They fed him first and asked questions second, which suited me fine, because questions aimed at me were questions I couldn't afford.

It was raining when we reached Thistlehollow — or that's the name on the signpost; the woman who lived there called it something older — and by then we'd been traveling under our false names for nine days, and I'd begun to believe, the way you believe in fair weather, that we might simply keep going forever.

The hedge-witch's cottage sat apart from the village, at the end of a lane choked with comfrey and old nettle beds, its roof furred with moss so thick it looked upholstered. There was a sign nailed above the door, but instead of a name it bore a painted eye, open, and beneath it someone had carved, long ago: *SHE SEES WHAT YOU BROUGHT WITH YOU.*

"I don't love the sign," I said.

Bram shrugged and knocked.

She opened the door before his knuckles landed the third time. She was small and round and ancient in the way of trees that have survived lightning — not delicate, but scarred into permanence. Her hair was white and cut bluntly at her jaw, and she wore an apron stained with things I chose not to identify. Her eyes went to Bram first, softened, then came to me, and stopped.

Stopped the way a compass needle stops.

"Well," she said. "Come in before the rain does."

Her name was Odile. She said so once and never again, and I gathered that names, in her house, were currency spent carefully. She fed us nettle soup and dark bread and dried apples stewed to leather, and while we ate she talked about nothing — the rain, the price of wool, a feud between two neighbors over a dead ewe — a stream of harmless talk that I gradually realized was a test. She was watching how we answered. Watching whether we lied easily or badly.

Bram lied beautifully. Not with words — with his face. He gave her the open, guileless look of a traveling boy glad of soup and a roof, and it was nearly true, which is what made it work.

I lied worse. I said I was a mapmaker heading to the coast for commission work, and Odile nodded and said the coast was lovely that time of year, and her eyes stayed on mine a moment too long, the way you hold a weight in your hand to guess what's inside.

After supper she put us to work. Bram split wood under the lean-to until his shoulders shone with rain, happy as a boy doing anything at all. She set me to sorting dried herbs, and it was while my hands were buried in chamomile that she said, conversationally, to the window:

"You hide it well. For a beginner."

My hands went still in the chamomile.

"The way you look at things," she said. "You don't look *at* the lane outside. You look along it. Where it goes. What it isn't yet." She turned and looked at me directly, and her eyes were pale grey and absolutely without cruelty. "My mother had a cousin who did that. They hanged him from the mill bridge when I was six. So I know the look."

"Madam," I began, "I don't—"

"Don't," she said, not unkindly. "Don't waste the lie on me. I've been brewing it out of people for fifty years. Save it for the ones who'll hurt you." She took the chamomile from my hands and set it aside, and then she did something I hadn't expected: she sat down across from me, heavily, like a woman settling in for weather. "Now. Tell me what's chasing you, girl, because something is. Nobody walks the lowlands in borrowed names with a silent boy and a hungry look unless something's behind them."

And I don't know why I told her. I want to say it was wisdom, but it wasn't. It was nine days of fear and the specific loneliness of carrying a secret so large it changes your posture. Odile listened without interrupting — to the valley, the riders, the contract, the mountain, all of it except the parts I couldn't speak aloud yet, the living map and the blood and the door — and when I finished she was quiet for a long time, looking into the fire.

"A soul-contract," she said finally. "Under the mountain. The Choir." She spat into the hearth, a small precise gesture of contempt. "Older than the crown that serves it. Older than the kingdom it sings into place. You understand that, don't you? The court beneath doesn't serve the throne. The throne sits where the Choir decides it sits. Every border, every charter, every king's peace — sung. Held in place by voices they stole."

"I'd guessed some of it."

"You've guessed the size of a coin by holding one edge." She leaned forward. "Then listen to the rest, because it matters to you specifically. The hunters the crown sends out — the witch-hunters — they carry compasses. Not iron needles. Bone. Cut from singers who died unclaimed, and they don't point north. They point at *talent*. At the gifted. A gifted person can stand in a crowd of ten thousand and the needle will find her through stone and rain and prayer."

The fire popped. In the yard, Bram's axe kept its steady rhythm, oblivious.

"How far can they feel?" I asked.

"Depends on the talent." Odile's voice dropped. "A hedge-cunning like mine, a few miles, maybe, if the wind's wrong. But what you have—" she shook her head slowly, "—what I saw flickering off you when you walked up my lane, girl, that's not hedge-magic. That's the deep kind. Charting-kind. The kind the Choir itself runs on." She looked at me the way you look at a house fire in a dry summer. "With that, they could feel you across a country. Maybe further. Maybe right now."

I thought of the phantom roads at the ford — the valley flooding with routes that had never existed, hunters' compasses spinning like frightened birds — and understood for the first time that I hadn't escaped that night. I'd *announced* myself. A shout of raw talent across half the countryside, and somewhere, surely, needles swinging toward it.

"They'll have felt that," I said. My voice sounded far away.

"Oh, they'll have felt it." Odile got up and began moving through her cottage with sudden purpose, pulling a clay pot from a shelf, a bundle of ash keys, a knife. "Which means you leave tomorrow at first light, and you leave carrying this." She pressed the pot into my hands. Inside was a grey-green salve that smelled of river mud and crushed yarrow. "Ash-key and grave-mold. Rub it into your skin — wrists, throat, behind the ears. It won't blind a bone compass, but it'll blur you. Make you read as sick instead of strong. Sick talent they pass over; nobody wants to harvest a dying field."

"And Bram?"

"Bram's clean. Whatever they took from that boy, they took it *root and branch*." Something moved across her face — pity, or anger, hard to say. "He reads as empty. Which is its own kind of hiding." She glanced out the window at him splitting wood in the rain, tireless, humming silently with the rhythm of it. "Though I'll say this for free: empty vessels ring loud to certain listeners. The Choir knows exactly where every sold voice is. Always has. The question you should be asking yourself, Marren-or-whatever-your-name-is, is why they haven't come to collect him."

The question settled into my chest like a swallowed stone, because I had been asking it, in the wordless place where I kept the worst thoughts. Why had the debt-collectors waited? Why did the contract's deadline sit at midwinter like a patient trap? *Payment due in full.* I'd assumed it meant money.

It had never meant money.

Odile fed us again that evening, and after, she did one more thing. She took down a jar of plum brandy and poured three cups, and set the third in front of Bram, who looked at it, then at her, then drank it like a man being knighted. And she talked to him — really talked, telling stories of the village, the dead ewe feud, a bear she'd outargued forty years ago — and she watched his hands while he answered in taps and gestures, and by the end she was laughing at his answers before he'd finished making them, and I realized I was watching something I hadn't seen since the valley: someone meeting Bram and finding him whole.

When she caught me watching, her laughter gentled into something more careful.

"Fragile, this," she said quietly, nodding between herself and us. "Friendship on the run. Don't mistake it for safe. Everyone you love becomes a door they can knock on." She refilled my cup. "But drink anyway. Doors swing both ways."

We left at first light in fog thick as wool. Odile walked us to the lane's end, wrapped in a shawl, and pressed a last bundle into my hands — bread, the salve, and a folded scrap of paper.

"What's this?"

"The name of a ferryman at the Sorrel, three days east. Tell him the gray hen counts at midnight. He owes me, and he owes the crown less." She gripped my wrist, hard, her small hand surprisingly strong. "Stay off high ground. Stay off old roads. And girl—" her grip tightened, "—if the compasses find you anyway, remember: they point at what you are, not where you are. Sometimes the only way to lose a needle is to become something it can't read."

I thanked her. Bram kissed her cheek, and she cuffed him gently and told him to grow, and then we were walking east into the fog with her cottage dissolving behind us like something dreamed.

We'd gone perhaps a mile when Bram stopped.

He was staring back the way we'd come, and his face had gone wrong — not afraid exactly, but alert, the way animals go alert before the ground tells them why. I turned.

Far behind us, at the crest of the southern hills, thin with distance but unmistakable, rose a thread of smoke. Too straight. Too deliberate. A signal fire, burning where no farm should be.

And even as I watched, the salve on my wrists seemed to prickle, and beneath my feet — faint as a pulse through floorboards — I felt the old roads wake, the hidden ways threading under the hills, and one of them, unbidden, drew itself across my mind in lines of cold light, running east toward the Sorrel:

and running, I saw with a lurch, straight toward the signal smoke.

Someone had already found the crossing.
