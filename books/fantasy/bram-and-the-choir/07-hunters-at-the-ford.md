## 7. Hunters at the Ford

I learned to read people before I learned to read words. When your voice is taken from you, you learn to watch mouths instead of using yours, to study hands, to notice how a person's shoulders sit when they are lying and how their eyes move when they are afraid. It was the only language left to me, so I became fluent in it.

Cyrus Vane spoke that language too, though she didn't know it.

For three days on the road of borrowed names she had been teaching me without meaning to. The way her thumb pressed flat against her forefinger when she was counting our remaining coins. The way she touched the strap of her satchel—once, twice, always twice—when a stranger looked at us too long on the road. The way she said *we're fine* in a voice pitched just slightly too high, which meant we were not fine at all.

So when she stopped at the crest of the hill above the river valley and went very still, I knew before I saw them.

* * *

The ford lay below us like a silver seam stitched through green cloth. The river ran shallow there, wide and chattering over stones, and on the far bank stood a crossing-warden's hut with its little smoke thread rising straight into the windless morning. A good place to cross. A fast place to cross. Which meant it was exactly where hunters would wait, if hunters were looking for two travelers who moved too quickly and asked too few questions.

I counted four horses tethered by the hut. Grey cloaks. The long staffs they carried were wrapped in canvas against the rain, but I had seen enough drawings scratched into tavern dirt to know what waited under the wrappings: iron-shod compass staves, the kind that didn't point north.

They pointed at people like Cyrus.

She crouched beside me in the bracken, and I watched her do the arithmetic every hunted person does—the distance to the ford, the distance around it, the hours we'd lose going around, the days we might not have. Her lips moved through numbers. Then her hand went to her satchel strap. Once. Twice.

*I know,* I wanted to say. *I see them too.*

Instead I touched her sleeve and pointed downstream, where the river bent through alder trees half a mile on. There would be stones there, maybe. Maybe a crossing no warden kept. Maybe nothing but cold water and a long swim.

Cyrus shook her head. She pulled out the map she'd made at the hedge-witch's table two nights past—a proper map this time, inked and dried, showing the river country in her tight, precise hand—and traced the bend with one finger. Then she tapped the paper twice and shook her head again.

No crossing there. I understood. The alders grew out of a gorge; we'd seen it from the ridge yesterday, black water sliding between walls of stone. We could not go around. We could not go downstream. And behind us, somewhere back along the road of borrowed names, the hunters' compasses were already swinging toward us like slow needles finding true.

Which left the ford, and the grey cloaks, and the wrapped staves.

Cyrus sat back on her heels. For a long moment she did nothing at all, and I watched something happen in her face that frightened me more than the hunters did. It wasn't fear. Fear I knew how to read; fear looks like everyone. This was the look of a woman standing at the edge of a room she has spent her whole life avoiding, hearing music through the door.

Her gift. Since the night we'd slipped past the warding stones and magic had come flooding back into her like cold water into a dry well, she had been holding it down. I'd seen her do it—saw her press her palms flat against tables, against the earth, saw her breathe in that careful measured way, keeping a lid on something that wanted to boil. At the hedge-witch's cottage she hadn't slept. She'd sat up drawing lines on scrap parchment and burning them one after another in the candle flame, because the lines kept moving when she wasn't looking.

*It wants out,* she'd told me, tapping her own temple, then the parchment, then her chest, as if to show me the thing lived in all three places at once.

Now she looked at the ford, and the hunters, and the road behind us, and I watched her decide to open the door.

I caught her wrist. She turned. I shook my head hard and pointed at her, then at my own eyes, then made the gesture we'd invented on the second day: fingers spread wide, blown apart like something bursting.

*I'm not going to burst,* her expression said, cool and stubborn as slate.

Then she took out her knife and pricked the pad of her thumb, and pressed the bead of blood to the corner of the map, and I understood that maybe she was.

* * *

We came down to the river through the willows, walking apart the way travelers do who aren't traveling together—her twenty paces ahead, me trailing with the slouch of a boy hired to carry bundles. My bundle was her satchel. Inside it, folded small, was the map with her blood drying brown at its edge.

The warden's hut smelled of peat smoke and fish. The four grey-cloaked men sat outside it eating bread, and their leader rose when he saw us, a broad man with a face like a closed door and a compass stave already unwrapped across his knees.

"Travelers." He said it pleasantly. His eyes did not travel pleasantly at all. They went from Cyrus's face to mine to the bundle on my shoulder and back. "Crossing today?"

Cyrus smiled the way market women smile at tax collectors—warm, empty, practiced. "If the water allows."

"The water allows everything." He stepped forward, and his men set down their bread. "Names?"

"Bree Aldous," Cyrus said, smooth as poured cream. "This is Tam. We're going to my sister's, past Harrowmere."

"Harrowmere." He nodded slowly, and I watched his hand drift toward the stave. "Funny thing about Harrowmere. Nobody's lived in Harrowmere since the fever year."

Cyrus didn't blink. "My sister's stubborn."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile. Behind him, one of the other hunters had risen and was unrolling a flat brass instrument on the warden's table, a thing like an astrolabe crossed with a sundial, its needle trembling under glass.

And the needle was turning.

Not toward north. Toward us.

I felt it before I understood it—that same pressure I'd felt in the hedge-witch's kitchen, a sensation like a finger drawn down the back of my neck, like a hymn sung slightly wrong. The needle swung, steadied, pointed. The hunter leaned over it. Looked up. Looked at Cyrus.

"Well," the leader said softly. "Bree Aldous."

Time did something strange then. Later I would try to put it into order and fail, because what happened next happened all at once and also in pieces, the way a window breaks.

Cyrus dropped the bundle from my shoulder—I heard the satchel hit the mud—and her hand went into it and came out with the map, and she tore it open, and the world tore open with it.

I have tried a hundred times to describe what I saw, and I have never found the words, because I am a boy without a voice describing a sound. But I will try, because someone should try.

The valley filled with roads.

They came out of the map like threads pulled from a hem—pale, shining, impossible roads, dozens of them, hundreds, spilling across the meadow and the river and the sky itself. Roads that climbed the air in gentle switchbacks. Roads that dove beneath the water and kept going. Roads that branched and braided and forked into roads finer than hair, until the whole valley was a woven thing, a basket of light, and every road led everywhere and nowhere and each one hummed faintly with the promise of arrival.

The hunters' horses screamed. One of them bolted straight into a phantom road and vanished—I mean *vanished*, horse and rider both, swallowed by a shining line that rippled like disturbed water—and the other three reared and plunged and threw their riders into the shallows.

The brass instrument on the warden's table began to spin. Not swing—spin, faster and faster, its needle a blur, and then it cracked down the middle with a sound like a knuckle popping and lay still, dead, smoking faintly.

"No," the leader said. He was on his feet now, stave in both hands, and for the first time his voice wasn't pleasant. "No, no—you stop that. You *stop*—"

But Cyrus wasn't listening. Cyrus was somewhere else entirely.

She stood in the center of her own creation with her arms flung wide and her head tipped back, and her mouth was open, and though no sound came out of her, I swear the roads sang. Every one of them. A chord of a thousand arrivals, a thousand departures, the sound a door makes opening onto a place you've dreamed of all your life. And her face—

Her face was the worst part. Or the best part. I still don't know.

She was *laughing*. Silently, hugely laughing, tears running down her cheeks, lit up from inside like a lantern, and I understood in that moment that some part of her had been dying in the valley all those years, the part of her that was made for this, and now it was alive and it did not want to stop.

"Cyrus!" I shouted it with my whole body, my hands, my face, my useless throat. *Cyrus.*

She didn't hear me. The hunters were scrambling, shouting, one of them firing a crossbow bolt that flew into a phantom road and simply ceased to exist. The leader was crawling toward the broken instrument, toward a horn at his belt, a signal horn, and if he blew it there would be more of them, many more, within the hour—

I ran at him.

I had never fought anyone in my life. I was a choirboy; my body was built for standing still and breathing. But grief makes fighters of quiet people, and I hit him at the waist and we went down together into the shallows, and the horn flew from his hand and spun away downstream, and he swore and shoved me off and raised the iron-shod stave—

And the stave shattered.

Not broke. *Shattered*, like ice, like glass, exploding into powder and slivers, because Cyrus had seen, had finally seen me, and whatever she was doing to the valley she bent a thread of it down into the river and through the iron like a needle through cloth.

The hunter stared at his empty hands. Then he did the wisest thing any of them did that day. He ran.

They all ran. Three men and a memory of a fourth, splashing up the far bank, crashing into the willows, gone. The horses were long gone. The warden's hut stood empty, its peat fire still smoking, its fish still hanging, its breakfast bread abandoned on the table.

And the roads remained.

They hung over the valley, fading slowly, edges softening like breath on glass, and Cyrus stood among them, swaying, and I watched the light go out of her the way a tide goes out—fast at first, then slower, then not at all, just wet sand and wreckage.

"Cyrus—" I caught her as she fell.

* * *

She weighed almost nothing. That was the first thing I thought, kneeling in the shallows with her head against my chest, and the second thing was terror, pure and simple, the animal kind, because her skin had gone grey as the hunters' cloaks and her lips were the color of the river stones and her breathing came shallow and far apart, like someone climbing a very high stair.

*Nosebleed,* I thought uselessly. *People get nosebleeds. People faint. People wake up.*

Her eyes opened halfway. Found my face. And she smiled—that same terrible, blazing smile, smaller now, guttering.

"Did you see," she whispered. Her voice was sand and paper. "Tam. Did you *see*—"

I had seen. I nodded, and I held her tighter, and I hated her a little bit right then, honestly, cleanly, the way you hate someone who frightens you by not fearing for themselves. Because I had watched her laugh while she burned, and I knew—I knew the way you know things about people you love without knowing how you know—that given the choice she would burn again. Would burn brighter. Would keep opening that door until there was nothing left of her to come through it.

"You have to stop," I said, with my hands, with my whole shaking body, pressing her palm flat against my chest over my heart. *Feel that. Feel what it costs the rest of us, watching.*

Her fingers curled weakly against my shirt. "Couldn't," she breathed. "They'd have taken us. Bram. They'd have—" A cough racked her, wet and bad. "I couldn't let them take you. Not you. You've had enough taken."

I went still.

That was the first time she'd said my name. My real name. Not Tam, not *boy*, not the borrowed syllables we wore like stolen coats. She'd known it since the contract, since the night I'd shown her the skin-written debt and watched her go white as milk, but she had never spoken it aloud, as if saying it might summon something. As if my name belonged to the court beneath the mountain and speaking it here, in the open air, might make the debt collectors turn their heads.

She'd said it while half-conscious, bleeding her gift out into a valley full of ghost roads, and she'd said it to tell me she'd done all this—for me.

The river chattered on over its stones, indifferent. Somewhere upstream, the warden's hut smoked its thin thread into the empty morning. On the table inside, I knew, lay the broken halves of a brass instrument, and broken instruments get reported, and reports travel faster than boys carrying bundles.

I got her up the bank. I got her under the willows, out of sight of the road. I found the satchel in the mud and fished out the bread we'd bought at the last village and the flask of hedge-witch tea that tasted of pine and iron, and I propped her against a trunk and made her drink, and she drank, obedient as a child, her eyes drifting shut between swallows.

And that was when I saw the map.

It lay where she'd dropped it, at the water's edge, half-soaked, the blood-stain at its corner spread now into a great dark bloom. But the bloom wasn't spreading randomly. As I watched, the blood moved—crawled, deliberate as an insect—along the inked lines of the river, past the ford, south and east, into the blank margin of the parchment where no cartographer had ever drawn anything.

And in the blank margin, in letters of rust-red, fine as stitching, the blood was writing a road.

A real one. I knew it was real the way you know a face in a crowd. It wound down and down, off the edge of the map, deeper than any map goes, and at its end the blood pooled and darkened into a shape I recognized from the contract written on my grandmother's arm: the sigil of the court beneath the mountain.

The map was drawing the way down. By itself. Using her blood. Using *her*.

I looked at Cyrus, asleep against the willow, grey-faced, breathing in that far-apart climb of hers. I looked at the map, patient and hungry in the mud. And I understood that we had not escaped the hunters' compasses at all.

We were the needle now. Something vast and old beneath the earth had felt her gift flare like a struck bell, and it was pointing at her, and it had just shown us the way home.

I folded the map carefully, the way you fold a thing that might be listening, and tucked it inside my shirt, against my own skin, where the contracts live.

Then I sat down beside her to keep watch, and to think about doors, and about what waits behind them when they open too wide.
