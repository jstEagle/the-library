## 9. The Village That Forgot Its Name

**CYRUS**

The road stopped being a road two miles before the village.

Not ruined—*unmade*. The ruts simply faded into packed earth, as if no cart had ever passed this way, as if the ground itself had forgotten what wheels were for. Cyrus noticed it first because her feet noticed it: the small, familiar resistance of a well-traveled path was gone, and walking felt like stepping on fresh snow that had never learned to hold a print.

She said nothing. Bram walked beside her, and he had developed a way of looking at her when something was wrong—a tilt of the head, a slight narrowing of his eyes, patient as a dog waiting at a door. She'd learned to read him in the weeks since the ford, since her blood had drawn them this map, and she knew he was reading her now.

"The road's wrong," she admitted.

He nodded slowly, then pointed ahead, past a stand of bare alders, to where rooftops showed gray against the gray sky. He tapped his chest twice, then held up one finger. *One more village before the mountain.* They both needed shelter; the nights had turned bitter, and Bram's cough had come back with the cold. And Cyrus needed—though she hadn't said it aloud—to see people again. Real people, living ordinary lives, in a world where names stayed where they were put.

The village had a name once. She could tell from the shape of the place: the way lanes curved around a vanished market square, the way a lane widened for a well that must have been there, the worn hollows in doorsteps where generations of feet had polished stone. Villages like this grew around their names the way pearls grow around grit. But when they reached the first house, there was no sign above the door. No carved lintel. Just raw wood, pale where someone had pried something off—or planed something away.

"Maybe it fell," Cyrus said, and heard how thin her own voice sounded.

Bram didn't answer. He was staring at the doorstep hollows, and his hand had gone to his throat—the gesture he made when he wanted to speak but couldn't, the gesture that still broke her heart a little every time, though he'd have hated to know it.

They walked deeper in. Smoke rose from a few chimneys. Somewhere a dog barked, listlessly, twice, and stopped. A woman hung washing between two houses, and Cyrus opened her mouth to ask the way—

—and the woman looked up, and Cyrus saw her face, and the question died.

It wasn't fear in the woman's expression. It wasn't suspicion, or the flat hostility villagers gave strangers. It was *blankness*. The woman looked at Cyrus the way you look at weather. Her mouth moved—she said something, words with the shapes of greeting, *good day* or *who's there*, but her eyes never changed, and halfway through the sentence she seemed to lose interest in it herself, trailing off, turning back to her washing.

Cyrus stood in the lane with her question unasked and her skin crawling.

"Bram." She caught his sleeve. "We shouldn't—"

But he was already moving toward the churchyard at the village's heart, drawn the way he was always drawn to churches, maybe because he'd been a choirboy, maybe because the silence in him ached loudest in places built for song. She followed because following Bram had become, without her deciding it, the organizing principle of her days.

The churchyard gate hung open. Inside, the graves lay in neat rows under frost-killed grass, and Cyrus understood, all at once and completely, why the road had forgotten itself.

Every headstone had been scraped blank.

Not broken. Not weathered. *Scraped*—the names gouged away with something patient and precise, leaving shallow scars in the granite where letters had been. Dates remained, some of them. 1712. 1698. 1740. But the names were gone, every one, fifty stones and not a single name among them, and the scraping hadn't been done carelessly or long ago. Pale dust still sat in the grooves. Someone had done this within the season.

Bram knelt beside the nearest stone. He laid his palm flat against the scarred face of it, and Cyrus watched his shoulders change—not shaking, exactly, but settling, the way a building settles when something beneath it gives way.

"No," she said, uselessly, to the stones, to the village, to whatever had done this. "No, that's not—that isn't how grief works. You don't scrape the names. Even if you hate the dead, you don't—"

An old woman stood at the churchyard wall.

Cyrus hadn't seen her approach. She was small and bent and wrapped in a shawl the color of ash, and she was watching Bram with an expression that was not blank at all—it was sharp, and wet-eyed, and terribly awake. Of everyone they'd seen in the village, she alone seemed to be home behind her eyes.

"You're not from here," the old woman said. Not a question. "You walk like the road still means something to you."

"We're passing through," Cyrus said carefully. "We need shelter for the night. We can pay."

"Pay." The woman smiled, and it was the saddest thing Cyrus had seen since her mother's funeral, and her mother's funeral had been very sad indeed. "There's nothing here worth paying for. There hasn't been since the singers came."

Cyrus went cold from the scalp down. "The singers."

"They came in autumn. Before the frost." The old woman's hands worked at each other inside the shawl, twisting. "Beautiful voices. They came down from the mountain and they sang in the square, and everyone came to hear, because who doesn't come to hear singing? And while they sang—" She stopped. Started again. "While they sang, my daughter forgot my name. Standing right beside me. I watched it go out of her face like a candle. And by morning nobody remembered what the village was called, and the priest—he tried, he wrote everything down, he wrote our names in the parish book so we wouldn't lose them—but the book was empty too. Ink still wet on the page and the words just... weren't there anymore. Words about us. Names of us. Gone."

Bram had risen. He stood very still, and Cyrus could feel the tension coming off him like heat off a stove.

"What happened to the singers?" Cyrus asked. "Did anyone stop them?"

"Stop them?" The old woman laughed, a dry rustle. "Child, we thanked them. That's the worst part. When they finished singing, we clapped. We clapped for the thing that was eating us, because it was so *lovely*." Her voice cracked and steadied. "Some folk left after that. Walked out and never came back—I don't know if they're alive somewhere or just... unwritten. The rest of us stayed. Where would we even say we were from?"

Cyrus thought of the map seeping out of her own blood. She thought of the court beneath the mountain, the Choir that sang a kingdom into being, and she thought: *this is what it costs. This is the price of permanence. Somewhere, right now, a kingdom is holding its borders because this village cannot hold its name.*

And Bram's voice had gone into that. One voice among hundreds. And hundreds of villages like this one, or worse, scattered along the roads the debt-collectors rode, roads her maps had helped draw.

"I'm sorry," she said, and meant it so hard it hurt her teeth.

"It's not your doing." The old woman studied her, then Bram, with those sharp awake eyes. "Though you've got a look about you. Both of you. Like parcels half-opened." She turned and beckoned. "Come. You can sleep in my barn. It's the least I can do for people who still have names."

* * *

**BRAM**

The barn smelled of hay and old apples, and Bram lay in the dark listening to the village fail to make sounds.

That was the thing he kept circling back to, lying sleepless on the scratchy hay. Villages were never quiet. Even asleep, they breathed—dogs shifting, beams ticking, someone's cough through a wall, the small industry of mice. This village made almost none of those sounds. As if sound itself had grown thin here, worn through like the names on the stones.

He missed his voice most at night. Everyone did, he supposed, whoever they were missing. In the dark the silence in his chest got loud.

He heard Cyrus turn over on her pallet across the barn. Then again. Then her whisper, barely a breath: "Are you awake?"

He knocked twice on the floorboard. Their code. *Yes.*

"Do you think they'll come here?" she asked. "The singers. Do you think they come back?"

He considered it honestly, which was the only way he ever answered her. Then he knocked three times, slow. *I don't know.*

A pause. "My map led us here. Did you know that? Not the blood-map—the other one. The one I drew before we left the valley. I charted a route through these hills, and I put a village here, and I named it." Her laugh had no air in it. "I named it *Harrowmere*. I don't know why that name. It just arrived, the way names do when I'm drawing. And now there's a real village here with no name at all, and I keep thinking—"

She stopped. Bram sat up in the dark.

"—what if it didn't arrive? What if I didn't invent Harrowmere? What if I *remembered* it?" Her voice dropped lower. "What if my gift doesn't make things up, Bram. What if it takes them. What if somewhere there's a ledger, and every city I've ever drawn has been subtracted from somewhere else, and I'm just another kind of singer."

He crossed the barn without letting himself think about it, because thinking would make it awkward, and sat down beside her pallet. She was a pale shape in the dark, hugging her knees. He reached out and found her shoulder and squeezed it once, firmly, the way you steady someone on a ladder.

Then, because a squeeze wasn't enough, he did the thing he'd only begun doing since the court's shadow had fallen over them: he tapped a rhythm against her arm. Slow, deliberate. The opening bars of the valley hymn—the one every child in the valley learned before they learned their own name. *Steady the plow and steady the heart / what the mountain takes, the valley keeps.*

Her breath hitched. "That's cheating," she whispered. "You can't sing at me. You don't have—"

He tapped it again. Stubborn. Four beats, rest, four beats. The hymn lived in rhythm even without pitch; rhythm was the skeleton the song wore, and skeletons couldn't be sold.

After a moment her hand came up and covered his fingers, and she tapped the answering phrase back at him, clumsy but true. They sat like that in the dark, trading the hymn back and forth through skin and bone, two people keeping a song alive between them the way you carry a coal in cupped hands through wind.

When she finally slept, he stayed up a while longer, listening to the village not-sound, and made himself a promise in the wordless way he made all his promises now: *whatever took this place, I'm going to watch it die.*

* * *

**CYRUS**

Morning brought the old woman to the barn with bread and a warning.

"Eat fast," she said, setting down the loaf. "And go today. The hunters passed through three days ago—crown men, with their brass needles and their questions. They scraped nothing here themselves, mind. They just wrote down what was already missing and rode on toward the mountain. But they'll circle back. They always circle back." She squinted at Cyrus. "You especially want to be gone before they do. You've got the look they needle for. Don't ask me how I know. Old women know things; it's the last memory we're allowed."

Cyrus froze with the bread halfway to her mouth. "The hunters. Which way did they—"

"Toward the ford, last I heard. Back the way you came." The old woman's eyes glittered. "Which means the road ahead is clean. For now."

Bram appeared at the barn door, dressed, alert, reading the tension in the room the way he read everything. Cyrus stood, brushing straw from her skirt, and made herself ask the question she'd been carrying since the churchyard.

"Grandmother. The songs. You said the singers sang in the square. Do you remember any of it?"

Something moved across the old woman's face—fear, longing, shame, all tangled. "No," she said. "That's the point, child. Nobody remembers it. That's why it's safe to hum."

"Hum it anyway," Cyrus said quietly. "Please."

The old woman looked at her for a long moment. Then she closed her eyes, and her cracked lips parted, and out of her came a thread of melody—wordless, fragmentary, wandering up through intervals no valley hymn had ever used. Strange, ancient, gorgeous. It sounded the way the blood-map had looked: impossible, alive, hungry.

Bram went rigid. His hand flew to his throat. His eyes were wide and fixed on the old woman's mouth, and Cyrus saw recognition detonate in his face, note by note, like lamps being lit down a dark corridor.

Because he knew the tune. Cyrus could see that he knew it. It was in the way his lips moved, shaping silent harmony against the melody, finding the line a beat before the old woman sang it—as if the song lived in him too, sold and stolen, and had leaked back out again into a stranger's humming three hundred miles from the mountain.

The melody trailed off. The old woman opened her eyes, bewildered. "Why did I do that? I don't know any songs. I've never—" She pressed her hand to her mouth. "Where did that come from?"

"Nowhere," Cyrus said gently. "It came from nowhere. Thank you."

Outside, the frost was burning off the fields, and the road north waited, climbing toward the mountain that ate voices. Cyrus shouldered her pack. Bram stood beside her, and when she glanced at him he was still trembling faintly, and his hand was pressed flat over his sternum, over the hollow where his voice used to live.

He looked at her and mouthed, with terrible clarity, three words:

*I know it.*

Cyrus looked back at the village—at the blank stones, the nameless lanes, the old woman already forgetting why her hand was raised in farewell—and felt the horror settle into her bones and harden into something colder and more useful than fear.

The Choir's leavings were everywhere. The theft was vast beyond anything she'd imagined, and it was *leaking*—fragments of stolen song escaping into old women and scraped gravestones and nameless roads, the whole countryside haunted by the runoff of that beautiful machine.

And Bram knew its music. However it had gotten back into him, some shard of the Choir lived in his body still, and if a shard could leak out, then perhaps—her mind raced ahead of her caution, drawing conclusions the way her gift drew roads—perhaps the Choir could be reached through him. Perhaps it could be *unmade* from the inside, using the one voice it had failed to fully digest.

She took his hand. Together they turned north, toward the mountain, toward the court that had waited its whole existence for a cartographer.

Behind them, faint and fading, the old woman was humming again—and this time, Cyrus could have sworn, the melody had changed.
