## 10. The First Door Beneath

The map had been pulling for two days.

Cyrus felt it the way you feel a fish on a line—not a tug so much as a living weight, a direction that existed somewhere behind her sternum. She'd stopped fighting it after the first morning. The parchment lay rolled in her pack, but even rolled, even wrapped in Bram's spare shirt to keep it from touching her skin, it knew where it was going. She knew because she did too.

They climbed out of the valley of scraped gravestones as the sun came up behind them, which meant they were walking into their own shadows, two long dark shapes stretched thin across the scree. Bram walked ahead. He had taken to that in the last day or two—walking point, scanning the ridgelines, moving with a quiet competence that made Cyrus feel both grateful and ashamed. Grateful because she was exhausted and her hands still trembled when she thought about the ford. Ashamed because he was fifteen and voiceless and doing it anyway.

He glanced back at her now, catching her watching him, and raised an eyebrow. *You staring at me, cartographer?*

"Checking your feet haven't fallen off," she said. "Would slow us down."

His mouth twitched. He turned back to the climb.

By midmorning the terrain changed. The soft gray shale gave way to something older, darker, stone with a grain to it like wood, like it had grown rather than been laid down. And the cliff rose out of the hillside without warning—a wall of black rock perhaps forty feet high, sheer as a cut gemstone, running east to west across their path as far as she could see in either direction.

It should not have been there.

Cyrus stopped and stared. She was a cartographer. She had spent her whole life reading land the way priests read scripture, and this cliff was wrong in every way a thing could be wrong. There were no mountains near enough to have shed it. No river had carved it. No earthquake leaves walls this clean. It simply stood, abrupt as a fence, as if someone had drawn a line on the world and the world had obeyed.

She unslung her pack with hands that were not quite steady and pulled out the map.

Unrolled, it looked worse than before. The ink had continued to move overnight—the road they'd traveled rendered in fine brown lines that matched, stroke for stroke, the actual path of their journey, and ahead of them, in a heavier hand, a single line running straight into the heart of the cliff and stopping there. Not fading. Stopping. As if the map itself didn't know what came next, and resented it.

"It ends here," she said aloud, mostly to hear a human voice.

Bram crouched beside her. He studied the parchment, then the cliff, then her face. Then he held up one finger—*wait*—and jogged along the base of the wall, northward, his boots crunching. Cyrus watched him go, her stomach tightening. The wall was maybe three hundred yards long before it bent out of sight around a shoulder of hillside. Bram followed its whole length and came back shaking his head.

Then he knelt, brushed the scree away from the cliff's foot with both palms, and looked up at her with an expression she was beginning to dread: the look of a boy who has found something and wishes he hadn't.

She joined him. Where his hands had cleared the rubble, the rock at the base of the cliff was not rock at all. It was worked stone—dressed, fitted, ancient—and set into it, low, almost buried, was a door.

Not a grand door. That was the first shock of it. After the cathedral scale of the cliff itself, the door was almost domestic: a plain rectangle of dark timber bound in green-black metal gone soft with age, no taller than Cyrus herself. No handle. No hinges visible. No keyhole. Only, carved into the lintel above it, a line of script in no alphabet she knew—characters like bird tracks, like musical notation fallen ill, like something written by a hand that had wanted to say *sing here* and had forgotten all words except one.

"Well," Cyrus said. "That's welcoming."

Bram touched the door's surface lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. His fingers came away clean, but he wiped them on his trousers anyway. Then he looked at her and pointed at the script, then at his own throat, and shook his head slowly.

"I know," Cyrus said. "I can read it. Or—I can almost read it. It's not letters, exactly." She crouched closer, tracing the air above the carvings without touching them. Her gift stirred as she did, that cold-water feeling rising in her chest, and the meaning surfaced the way meanings surfaced on her maps: not understood so much as *remembered*. "It says the door opens to a note. A specific one. Sung true and held." She paused. "It says the door will know if it's false."

Bram closed his eyes.

She watched him do it—watched him stand there in the wind with his head bowed and his hands loose at his sides, and she understood what she was asking him to face. This was the wound itself. This was the exact shape of the theft: a door that required a voice, and a boy standing in front of it with nothing. Of all the cruelties the court beneath the mountain had designed, this might be the most elegant—to make the road home pass through the very absence they had carved into him.

He opened his eyes. He pointed at himself, then shook his head once, firmly. *Not me.*

"I know," Cyrus said quickly. "I know. I wasn't going to ask you to—no. I'm going to do it."

He frowned. Pointed at her throat. Raised both eyebrows.

"My gift isn't a voice," she said. "But it makes things. Roads. Doors that don't exist yet." She sat back on her heels, thinking, and the thinking frightened her more than the cliff did. "A note is just a shape, Bram. Air shaped a certain way, held steady. My gift shapes things. I've never tried to shape sound, but—" She shrugged, hating how flippant she sounded when her pulse was hammering. "There's a first time for everything. Usually a bad time."

Bram caught her sleeve. His grip was gentle but insistent, and his eyes were fierce. He tapped his chest twice—*you*—then pressed his palm flat against the ground. *If this goes wrong, you fall too.*

"Yes," she said. "I'd noticed."

He held her gaze a moment longer, then let go. And then, deliberately, he sat down against the cliff's base, folded his arms, and nodded at the door. *Go on then.*

Cyrus laughed despite herself—a short, cracked sound—and it helped. It always helped, the way he could deflate terror with a shrug. She took a breath, unrolled the map fully, and weighted its corners with stones. The living ink pulsed faintly, the dead-end line at the cliff throbbing like a held breath.

"All right," she murmured, half to the map, half to whatever was listening inside the mountain. "Let's see what you want."

She closed her eyes and reached for the gift.

It came eagerly—that was the part that always frightened her, how eager it was, how it surged up like water finding a breach. She'd learned on the road to hold it loosely, to let it fill her without directing it, because the times she'd grabbed at it, it had grabbed back. At the ford it had flooded an entire valley with phantom roads. So now she held it the way you hold a wild bird: firm enough that it couldn't escape, loose enough that it wouldn't panic.

And she listened.

Under the wind, under the blood in her ears, there was a tone. Faint, subterranean, rising out of the rock itself—the way a tuning fork hums after it's struck, except this fork had been struck centuries ago and never allowed to stop. The door's note. The pitch the lintel demanded, sunk into the stone like a nail sunk into wood.

*There,* said the gift, and showed her the shape of it.

She built the sound the only way she knew how: as a map. In her mind she drew the note the way she'd draw a coastline—its edges precise, its interior filled, every curve of it charted and fixed. Then she pushed. Not from her throat—from everywhere, from the air between her mouth and the door, shaping the silence the way she'd once shaped phantom roads across a valley floor.

The air in front of the door began to sing.

It was the strangest thing she had ever done, and she had recently flooded a valley with imaginary highways. A pure tone hung in the air, sourceless, steady, coming from nowhere and everywhere—the space itself vibrating, the note standing in the air like a figure stands in a doorway. It was not beautiful, exactly. It was *correct*, which was somehow worse. Nothing natural sounds that correct.

The door shivered.

Green light seeped out from under it, thin as spilled water. The old metal bindings brightened. And the carved script on the lintel began to move, the bird-track characters rearranging themselves, flowing like ants toward a new arrangement, and Cyrus felt the note in the air begin to bend—felt the door *pulling* at her shape, testing it, tasting it—

And then the door remembered her.

She didn't have another word for it. The tone shifted, subtly, and the shift was familiar, and it took her a horrible second to understand why: the door had taken her forged note and was folding something else into it, something underneath, a second voice braided into the first. Low. Warm. A woman's voice, singing the same pitch a hair's breadth off, creating a beat frequency, a slow wobble—

Her mother's voice.

The sound of it went through Cyrus like a blade through wet paper. She nearly lost the note entirely; the air-shape wavered, the green light guttered, and Bram was on his feet instantly, gripping her arm, steadying her, his eyes sharp with the question. *Hold. Hold.*

She held. Teeth clenched, eyes streaming in the wind, she held the shape while the door sang her mother's ghost back at her, harmonizing with a forgery, welcoming her with a voice harvested decades ago, and some cold, distant part of her mind noted the implication with terrible clarity: *the door knows her voice because the Choir has it. The door and the Choir are one instrument. Everything down there is one instrument, and we are walking inside it.*

The lock turned. It sounded like a bone setting.

The door swung inward—slowly, grudgingly, exhaling a breath of air that smelled of deep stone and candle smoke and something older underneath, something mineral and vast—and the note died in the air as though swallowed.

Silence rushed in.

Bram's hand was still on her arm. She became aware of her own breathing, ragged, and of tears on her face she had no memory of crying. She wiped them with the heel of her hand, roughly, furious at them.

Beyond the threshold: stairs. Wide, shallow steps descending into warm darkness, cut from the same black grain-stone as the cliff, each step worn smooth in the center by centuries of feet. Lantern brackets lined the walls at intervals, empty, waiting. And from far below—so far it might have been imagination—came a sound.

Faint. Layered. Many-voiced.

Singing.

Bram heard it too. She saw it hit him physically, a flinch that started in his shoulders and dropped through his whole body, and she saw what it did to his face: hunger and horror in equal measure, the look of a starving man shown food he knows is poisoned. His own voice was down there. Some piece of him, sold and filed away, was singing in the dark, and the walls between him and it were only stairs.

He stepped forward. Cyrus caught his shoulder.

"Not yet." Her voice came out rougher than she intended. She nodded at the empty lantern brackets, at the worn steps, at the vast patient dark. "We don't know what's waiting. We go carefully. We go together." She hesitated, then said the other thing, the thing sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone. "Bram. The door sang with my mother's voice."

He went still.

"It knew her. It's been carrying her voice for twenty years, down there, and it used it to welcome me." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "That door remembered her better than I do. I was six. I barely remember her face anymore, and that door—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. Started again, quieter. "Whatever's down there took her the same way it took your voice. Maybe earlier. Maybe differently. But it has her, Bram. Or had her. And I need to know which."

Bram studied her for a long moment. Then he did something he had never done before: he reached out and took her hand. Deliberately, formally, the way you take someone's hand to seal a bargain. He squeezed once, hard, and let go, and the gesture needed no translation.

*We go together. We finish this.*

Cyrus nodded, unable to speak. She rolled up the map—the dead-end line had changed while they weren't looking, she noticed distantly; it no longer stopped at the cliff but plunged onward, down, down, into uncharted dark, drawn in ink that gleamed wetly—and slung her pack. From the doorway, cold air flowed upward over them both, carrying the scent of candles and stone and, beneath everything, that layered distant singing, patient as water wearing away rock.

Bram went first. Of course he did.

Cyrus paused at the threshold, one hand on the ancient frame, and looked back once at the daylight—the valley behind them, the long road, the sky going white and high overhead. Then she stepped through, and the door swung shut behind them with a sound like a held breath finally released, and the last thing she heard before the dark took them was the singing below, growing louder with every step.

And threaded through it, unmistakable now, a single voice rising clear above the rest—one that harmonized, perfectly, terribly, with the note the door had stolen from her mother's throat.
