## 11. Stairs Made of Silence

The stairs went down the way a throat does.

That was the thought Bram kept circling as they descended, one worn step after another, into the dark beneath the door. Not like a road, which went somewhere. Like a swallow. The walls closed in smooth and pale and slightly warm, and if he put his palm against the stone—which he did, once, and regretted—it gave back heat like something living that had been asleep a long time.

Cyrus walked ahead with her map held out before her like a lantern. It needed no light. The parchment glowed faintly along its inked lines, and every so often a new line drew itself while he watched, quick as a spider dropping on silk, charting the turn before they reached it.

*It knows where we're going,* he thought. *Or it knows what wants us there.* He couldn't decide which was worse, and neither, judging by the set of her shoulders, could she.

He counted steps because counting was the only voice he had left. Four hundred and six. Four hundred and seven. His boots made almost no sound; the stone ate them. Cyrus's made almost no sound either. That was the strangest thing about the stairs, at first—the silence wasn't empty. It was full. It pressed on his ears the way water presses when you dive too deep, and it had texture, layers, like a chord held so long everyone had forgotten it started.

Four hundred and twelve.

Then, from somewhere below—or above, or inside the wall an arm's length from his cheek—someone said his name.

"Bram."

His own voice.

He stopped walking. His body knew it the way you know your own hand in the dark: the pitch of it, the slight break where his register turned, the way he swallowed the end of words because Grandmother was always telling him not to mumble. Nobody could copy that. Nobody would want to.

"Bram." Closer now. Warm. Glad. "There you are. We've been waiting."

He grabbed Cyrus's sleeve. She turned, saw his face, and lifted the map higher, angling herself between him and the wall. Good old Cyrus, who read contracts written on skin without flinching, who had lied to a witch-hunter's compass with nothing but raw terror and a flood of phantom roads. He saw her go still, listening. He saw her hear it.

"Do you hear that?" she whispered, which was foolish, because of course he heard it, it was the only thing in the world—

"Bram, come home," said the wall. "It's cold up there, isn't it? You always hated the cold. Come down where it's warm. Everyone's here. Mother's here. Da's here. We kept your place."

His mother had been dead nine years. He knew that the way he knew his own name, which was—apparently—a thing the walls knew too.

* * *

They sat on a step wide enough for two, backs against the opposite wall, as far from the talking one as the stairwell allowed. Cyrus lit no fire. There was nothing to burn and, he suspected, nothing she wanted to illuminate.

"It's your voice," she said quietly. "A piece of it. They don't keep the souls whole, I think. They spread them. Like—" she searched, snapped her fingers, "—like lard across bread. Thin. Everywhere. The mountain is paved with it."

He wrote on his slate, the chalk worn to a nub: **IT LIED ABOUT MY MOTHER.**

"Yes."

**IT SOUNDED HAPPY TO SEE ME.**

"I know." Her jaw worked. "That's the part I hate most."

Below them, patient as tide, the voice began again. Not calling now. Singing. A hymn he'd mouthed a thousand times in the valley chapel, standing in the second row where the acoustics were kindest, singing with everything in him while no sound came out—and now the sound came out, his sound, the tenor line he'd never once gotten to hear himself sing, rising up the stairs sweet and true and horribly wrong.

He hadn't known what his voice sounded like until this moment. He'd have given anything, once, to learn it. He'd given nearly everything, and the lesson arrived here, in the dark, sung back to him by stone.

He didn't cry. He was past crying, mostly. But he set the slate down flat on his knee and pressed both hands over his chest, over the hollow place, the way you press a wound.

Cyrus pretended not to see, which was its own kindness. Then she didn't pretend.

"Listen to me." She turned and took his wrist—not gently, exactly. Firmly. Cyrus-firmly, the grip of someone who hauled him out of a burning barn once and had never fully let go of the habit. "That is not you. Do you understand? That is an echo wearing your coat. An echo is the poorest, thinnest kind of lie. It can only say what it has already been made to say. It can't change. It can't choose. It doesn't even know we're sitting here. You do. You're the real one, Bram, and the proof of it is that you're scared. Echoes don't get to be scared."

He looked at her. In the faint glow of the map her face was all angles and stubbornness, and underneath the stubbornness something that had startled him since the valley: she meant it. All of it. Every reckless mile.

He picked up the slate. **WHEN WE GET IT BACK, WILL IT STILL SOUND LIKE THAT?**

She considered the question honestly, which he also appreciated and also hated.

"I don't know," she said. "I think... maybe it'll sound like that but belong to you. Which is different." A pause. "Like a house. Same rooms, your name on the deed."

He almost smiled. Almost.

Then the singing stopped, and in the gap it left, the walls around them—both walls, all the walls, the whole throat of the mountain—began to whisper. Not words yet. Just breath, hundreds of breaths, in and out, in and out, the rhythm of a choir settling before the downbeat. And threaded through it, unmistakable, his own voice again, no longer glad:

"You'll come back anyway. Everyone comes back. You always did exactly what they told you, Bram. That's why they picked you."

* * *

He tried to leave.

Not far. Not really. He got perhaps twenty steps up before his legs stopped, and he stood there in the dark with his hand on the wall, shaking, hating himself for shaking, hating the wall for being warm.

Because here was the thing he had never told anyone, the thing that had lived in him since the day his father signed the contract and the debt-collectors' men came with their scales and their polite voices: some small, starved part of him had wanted to be picked. Had been glad, in the worst corner of himself, that his silence meant something. That it bought something. That for once the boy who contributed nothing—not coin, not labor, not song—was worth a whole family's survival.

And the walls had found that corner. Of course they had. Walls like these were built of nothing else.

*You always did exactly what they told you.*

He was still facing upward, twenty steps from the light they'd left behind at the false door, when Cyrus's boots stopped beside him.

She didn't say anything at first. She just stood there breathing, letting him be a person having a bad moment instead of a problem requiring a speech. He loved her a little for that. He'd never told her that either.

"We're going up, then?" she said finally. Mild. Conversational. As though discussing the weather in a place that had never had any.

He shook his head.

"No?" She leaned on the wall next to him, shoulder to shoulder. "Funny. Because I counted, and going up is twenty steps, and going down is—I don't know, thousands, probably, my map won't commit—but going down gets your voice back. Going up gets us back to the valley, where there's a contract coming due at midwinter and a compass pointed at my head and absolutely nothing waiting except the same life we ran from, minus the part of you that made it bearable." She tilted her head. "So. Up?"

He didn't move.

"Up," she repeated, softer. "Bram. Look at me."

He looked at her.

"They will use everything you're ashamed of," she said. "That's what this place is. That's what it's *for*. It takes the worst hour of your life and builds a staircase out of it. And you can stand on those stairs forever, and they will hold you up forever, because that's all they know how to do." She took the slate gently out of his hand, and wrote on it herself, in her sharp cartographer's letters, and handed it back:

**THEY ARE LYING ABOUT YOU TOO.**

"The boy who walked into that court to save his family wasn't doing what he was told," she said. "He was defying them. Every step. With his whole heart. They've just spent years trying to convince him otherwise, because a boy who knows what he actually did might come back someday with fire in his hands, and they can't have that." Her voice cracked, just barely, and she pushed through it. "I need you to know what you actually did. Because I'm about to do something terrifying and criminal and possibly fatal, and I cannot do it alone, and you are the only person alive I trust in the dark."

He stared at the slate. At her handwriting. At her.

Then he turned around. Twenty steps down, past the whispering, past his own voice saying one last wounded thing (*you'll regret this*) that he no longer believed, and he kept walking, and Cyrus fell in behind him, and after a while—to keep his courage fed—he counted aloud in his head, and after a longer while, he realized he was tapping the count against his thigh with two fingers. One-two-three-four. One-two-three-four.

The hymn-rhythm. The pulse under every verse the valley ever sang.

Cyrus caught it. He felt her catch it, heard her step adjust to fall on the beat, and neither of them said anything, because some things were better carried than spoken.

* * *

They descended for what felt like a night and part of another. They slept in a niche where the wall mercifully held its tongue, curled back to back, her pack under his head, his coat over her feet. Once he woke to find her awake, staring at the map, frowning at a line that had appeared unbidden—a thin red thread running off the edge of the parchment and, somehow, continuing onto the stone beside it.

**WHAT?** he wrote.

"Nothing," she said, in the voice that meant something. "The map is drawing things I didn't ask for. Roads that aren't on the route." She rolled it up quickly, as if it were a mouth that might start talking.

At some indeterminate hour the stairs ended.

They opened into a gallery—long, vaulted, hung with lanterns of pale glass that burned without flame, each one flickering in a rhythm suspiciously like breathing. And the gallery was lined with doors. Hundreds of them, small as cupboard doors, close-set as honeycomb, and from behind every single one came sound. A hum. A phrase of melody, looped and looping. A laugh, the same laugh, at the same joke, forever. A woman saying a name—*Elowen, Elowen, Elowen*—with such tenderness that Bram's eyes stung.

Each door had a nameplate. Most were blank.

Cyrus walked the row slowly, reading the ones that weren't. Her lips moved. Her face did something complicated and terrible, and then shut like a book, and she turned to him with the map already in her hands.

"We're close," she whispered. "The map says the Choir keeps its voices in rows and its rulers below. There should be a way down past the gallery—"

Behind them, unhurried, from the top of the stairs they had just climbed, came footsteps.

And a voice. Not his. Not anyone's from a wall. A living voice, courteous and resonant, filling the gallery the way oil fills a lamp, and every door along the row went silent at once—all hundred humming, looping, grieving little doors, holding their breath.

"Ah," said the voice. "The cartographer. And her friend."

Bram's hand found Cyrus's.

Every lantern in the gallery flared brighter together, like an orchestra drawing its first bow.

"We've been so looking forward to hearing you both."
