## 12. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The map wanted us to walk into the mountain.

Not around it, not through some crack in its flank where a goat might have squeezed first. It wanted us to walk *in*, through a seam of rock that my living map insisted was a door and my eyes insisted was nothing at all. Bram stood beside me in the dark of the gorge, breath fogging, and looked from the blank cliff face to me with an expression I had learned to read fluently by now: *you're certain?*

"I'm certain it's here," I said. "I'm less certain about what happens after."

He tapped his chest twice—*we go*—and then, because he was Bram, tapped his own heart once more, slower. That one meant something I hadn't asked him to define. I was grateful for the omission. There are things you shouldn't name while you still have the option of running.

The seam wasn't visible until my fingers found it. The rock parted along a line no wider than a knife's edge, cool as the rim of a cup, and gave way to a passage that smelled of candle smoke and old rain. Behind us the night sky shrank to a coin, then a thread, then nothing. Ahead, the dark was not empty. I could feel it watching the way a page watches a pen.

My map had stopped drawing itself two miles back. I hadn't told Bram that. A guide that goes quiet at the threshold of the thing it's guiding you toward is either finished or afraid, and I no longer knew which possibility frightened me more.

We descended for what felt like hours, the passage widening by degrees until we were walking three abreast down corridors carved with such precision that no chisel had ever touched them—the stone simply *smoothed*, the way water smooths, the way a voice smooths when a hundred people say the same word together. And there were sounds, all that while. Faint ones. Under everything, like a heartbeat heard through a wall: singing. Vast, layered, patient. Not approaching. Not needing to. It was already everywhere, the way the sea is already in every drop of the tide.

Bram heard it too. I watched his hand drift to his throat and rest there, the way it did when the absence ached. He had told me once—in rhythms tapped on a tabletop, in the code we'd built between us out of hymn-meters—that he couldn't remember whether he missed his voice or only missed the idea of it, since the missing had started so young. But walking down into that singing dark, his face said plainly which it was. His voice was somewhere below us. It had been calling him home for eleven years.

Then the corridor opened, and I forgot how to breathe.

* * *

The cavern was a cathedral the way a wolf is a kind of dog—technically true, and built entirely wrong. Its vaulted ceiling rose higher than any human mason could have raised, ribbed with pale stone that caught and held light without any visible source. Lanterns hung in their hundreds from chains that vanished upward into shadow, and each lantern burned with a soft gold flame that did not flicker. As I looked closer—and I wish I hadn't; I wish to this day I'd kept my eyes on my boots—the flames moved wrong. They didn't dance. They *breathed*. Slow, rhythmic expansions, each lantern swelling and contracting on its own count, none quite in time with its neighbors, so that the whole cavern pulsed with a light like standing inside a chest of ribs.

Trapped breath. That was my first thought and I have never managed to make it my last. Every lantern was somebody's exhale, kept burning.

The floor was stone worn glassy by centuries of feet, and it sloped down toward the center of the cavern, where the space opened into a wide amphitheater of terraced benches rising in perfect rings—empty now, but arranged around a raised dais the way every bench in every hall is arranged around the thing that matters. On the dais stood a podium of black wood, and beside it a single chair, simple and high-backed, and beside *that*—

"You'll want to mind the third step," said a voice, warm as mulled wine. "It has opinions."

He came out of the wings of shadow as though the shadows had been holding him in reserve. Tall—not impressively so, just tall enough that you noticed it a beat too late—and dressed in dove-grey robes that moved like they remembered being wings. His hair was silver, but not aged silver; it was the silver of frost forming, deliberate and fine. His face was pleasant. That was the first terrible thing about him. He had the face of a man you would trust with your luggage, your secrets, your child. He was smiling before he finished walking toward us, and the smile arrived exactly on time, like a note in a score.

He bowed. To me. Not to Bram, though his eyes flicked over the boy with the brief proprietary glance of a man checking inventory. To *me*.

"Cyrus Vane," he said. "Cartographer of unmapped realms. Daughter of Marisol. Late of the valley that refuses magic." He straightened, hands folding neatly at his waist. "You cannot know how long we have rehearsed this meeting."

Every instinct I owned was screaming the same word: *run*. But running requires somewhere to run *to*, and the corridor behind us had gone very quiet in a way that corridors behind you should never go quiet. So I did what I have always done instead of running. I looked at the terrain.

"Third step has opinions," I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "That's a strange thing for a step to be."

"It's a strange thing for a court to be," the Maestro agreed pleasantly, "and yet here we are." He gestured up at the lanterns, the ribs of stone, the empty terraces. "Welcome to the Court Beneath the Mountain. You may call me the Maestro. Everyone does, eventually. Even those who arrive determined to call me nothing at all." His smile deepened by a fraction. "Especially them."

Bram had moved. I hadn't seen him decide to do it, but he was suddenly half a step in front of me, shoulders squared, chin up—a mute boy putting his body between me and a monster, which was either the bravest or the stupidest thing I had ever witnessed, and I had watched myself draw maps of cities that don't exist for twenty years, so I am a fair judge of both.

The Maestro regarded him with something almost like tenderness. "Bram. Eleven years, four months. You were our youngest acquisition that season." He tilted his head. "Your section misses you. The second sopranos keep a gap in the line where your voice used to sit. It's considered poor form to fill a departed seat too quickly. We are not barbarians."

Bram's hands curled into fists. I put my hand on his shoulder—not to hold him back, exactly. More like holding down a page in wind.

"Why the cartographer?" I asked. "You hunt the gifted across three kingdoms. You've had witch-hunters with compasses crawling up my road for weeks. Why greet *me* like—" I gestured at the bow he'd given, the warmth, the whole obscene courtesy of it. "Like a guest?"

"Because the hunters bring back voices," the Maestro said, "and voices are material. Lovely material. But a cartographer—" he spread his hands, and the lantern-light breathed over his grey sleeves "—a cartographer brings back *borders*. Do you understand what we do here, Cyrus Vane? Truly? May I show you rather than tell? Telling always sounds like madness, and I have been mistaken for a madman before, and it stung."

He didn't wait for permission. He raised one hand, and the cavern sang.

* * *

I don't have words for it, which is its own kind of humiliation, because words are my trade the way maps are. The Choir was there—I felt it before I saw it, the singing that had followed us down the passages swelling up out of the terraced benches as though the benches themselves had opened—and what rose into the light was not people, or not only people. Shapes of pale gold flame, each one breathing in a lantern rhythm, hundreds of them, arranged in ranks upon the risers, and every one of them wearing a face if you looked long enough, and I looked long enough because I am cursed with looking.

And the mountain *answered*. I felt it in my teeth. The stone above us thickened as they sang; the walls drew inward a hair's breadth, the way a body draws its borders tight against cold. The kingdom overhead—the passes, the roads, the crown's writ, the whole hard geometry of the realm that had hunted Bram and me across a hundred miles—it was being sung. Continuously. Right now. Had been sung without pause for longer than my family had kept records. The borders of the court beneath the mountain were not drawn. They were *performed*, nightly, eternally, by everyone who had ever been stolen to perform them.

"There," the Maestro said softly, beside me—I hadn't heard him move—his voice pitched under the music, private, confiding. "Do you see it now? Every kingdom is a song someone agreed to keep singing. Yours above, mine below. The difference between us, cartographer, is that I am honest about the arrangement." He let the Choir crest, hold, and fall silent, and the silence rang like struck metal. "Borders decay. Charters rot. Only song renews itself every time it's sung. Which brings us"—and he turned to face me fully, and his courteous smile finally showed its roots—"to why you are here."

"I'm here for his voice," I said. "That's all. That's the whole errand."

"Yes," the Maestro said gently. "So the contract stipulates. Voice of Bram, son of Edith, sold for the sum of his family's debts, payment due midwinter." He recited it the way a priest recites scripture, or a clerk reads terms. "I honor every contract that crosses my desk, Miss Vane. Ask anyone whose soul I keep. I am scrupulous. Which is why I offer you this before we discuss terms: sit. Eat. Rest—you have walked two hundred miles and descended into the earth, and you are swaying like a mast in weather. Nothing is decided tonight. Nothing is ever decided tonight." He gestured toward the wings of the amphitheater, where warm light spilled from arched doorways I would have sworn had not existed a moment ago. "Guest chambers. Real beds. My cook was a gift from a queen who owed me a favor, and she will be insulted if you don't finish the soup."

"We didn't come for soup," I said.

"No," he agreed. "You came for a voice. And I will give it to you—I promise you this, hand on heart, and my heart is older and stranger than it looks. All I ask in return is a conversation. A professional conversation, cartographer to cartographer." His eyes glittered, frost catching lantern-light. "You see, I have a problem, and your reputation suggests you are the solution. My court is expanding. The song holds what it held, but I have ambitions—new halls, new depths, realms beneath realms that do not yet exist." He paused, and the pause was masterful; it left room for exactly one word. "*Yet.*"

The word landed in my chest like a stone dropped down a well.

Places that do not yet exist. That is what I draw. That is the gift I have hidden under floorboards and behind false names my whole life: maps of things that haven't happened yet, ink that knows the future, parchment that charts itself. And here, in a cathedral of stolen breath, the most dangerous creature I had ever heard of was describing my secret as though reading it off an inventory slip.

"You can't mean—"

"I mean," the Maestro said, walking backward now toward the archways, unhurried, hostly, terrible, "that we have waited a very long time for you. Your mother was to have been the beginning of it. She was lovely material too—but that is a conversation for tomorrow, over breakfast, when you are rested and less inclined to look at me the way you're looking at me now." He reached the archway and turned, spreading his arms to encompass the lanterns, the terraces, the sleeping Choir, the whole breathing dark.

"The cartographer we have waited for," he said, warmly, formally, as though announcing her to the hall. "Welcome home, Miss Vane. Your rooms are ready. They have always been ready."

The lanterns nearest us flared brighter as he passed them—all of them, in fact, in a wave rolling away down the cavern, hundreds of trapped breaths inhaling at once—and I understood, with a calm that scared me more than screaming would have, that the doors behind us had closed without a sound, and that every kindness he had shown us, every soup and bed and promised bargain, was the trap closing *gently*, the way a hand closes around a bird that flew in on its own.

Bram's fingers found my sleeve and tapped, fast, urgent. Twice. Twice. Twice.

*I know,* I tapped back. *I know. I know.*

Tomorrow there would be a conversation about my mother. Tomorrow there would be a bargain I could not refuse shaped exactly like one I could. Above us, two hundred miles of stone, a kingdom went on existing because stolen souls were singing it into place—and somewhere in those ranks, the Maestro had said *your section misses you*, and Bram's jaw had gone white, and I had felt, for one horrible half-second, a harmony in that vast gold sound that fit a gap in my own chest I had never been able to map.

The Maestro had waited for me my whole life.

Standing in his glowing cathedral, guest of a court I had come to rob, I finally understood the worst part: I wasn't sure yet whether I had walked into his trap—or followed his directions home.
