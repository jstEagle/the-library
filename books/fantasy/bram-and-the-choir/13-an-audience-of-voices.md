## 13. An Audience of Voices

*Bram*

The Maestro's servants were not people. Bram had understood that since the door, but understanding became certain in the guest chamber, where the thing that brought them supper moved with the smooth wrongness of a puppet whose strings ran up inside its own skin. It set down a tray of bread and small birds roasted golden, bowed to neither of them, and left through a wall that opened like water and closed behind it without a seam.

He did not eat. Cyrus did, mechanically, tearing the bread and chewing with the fixed expression of someone who has decided hunger is a problem she can solve. Her hands were steady. Her eyes kept going to the wall.

The chamber was beautiful, which was the worst part. Someone had designed it to soothe—warm stone, a fire that burned without smoke, a bed wide enough for four. The ceiling was painted with constellations, and Bram, who had spent three years learning to read everything except sound, read them quickly: these stars were wrong. Arranged not as the sky looked but as the sky would look from somewhere else. From beneath. Everything here was built for an eye that stood below it.

He tapped his thumbnail against the tray's edge. Twice, pause, three times. *Are you listening.*

Cyrus didn't look up. But her finger, resting on the tablecloth, tapped back. Once. *Always.*

They had invented the rhythm-code on the road, badly at first, then better, the way two people learn anything when their lives depend on fluency. Most of what they could say was practical. Some of it was other things, which they did not say often, because saying it even in taps made it real, and real things could be taken.

The wall opened again before dawn—or before what the mountain claimed was dawn; there had been no windows, only a slow warming of the lanterns—and the puppet-thing gestured them out with a courtesy that made Bram's skin crawl precisely because it was so well done.

They were led through corridors where the stone sang underfoot. Not metaphorically. Each step drew a faint tone from the floor, pitched to the walker, and Bram realized after ten paces that the corridor knew him. It was singing a fraction of something he had sung as a boy. His own voice, worn thin, laid into the rock like a thread into cloth.

Cyrus heard it too. He saw her hear it—her jaw went tight, her stride lengthened—but there was nothing to do but walk, and be walked, deeper into the court beneath the mountain, toward whatever room held the thing that ruled it.

The audience hall was a cathedral carved by no human hand. Stalactites descended in tiers like the pipes of an organ, and between them hung lanterns of glass, each one glowing with a soft interior light that pulsed. Bram stopped walking when he understood what the light was. The pulse was too slow for flame and too irregular for enchantment. It was breathing. Each lantern held breath, and the breath held a voice, and the voices were quiet now, resting, the way a choir rests between movements.

At the far end of the hall, on a plain wooden dais—plainness itself a kind of arrogance, here—stood the Maestro.

Bram had expected robes, or horns, or some honest sign of monstrosity. Instead there was a tall figure in a grey coat, silver-haired, with the mild face of a schoolmaster and hands that never entirely stopped moving, conducting something inaudible even while standing still. When they approached, those hands gathered themselves and folded, and the Maestro smiled the way you smile at a letter you have waited years to receive.

"The cartographer," he said. "And the treble." His gaze touched Bram like a fingertip on the back of the neck. "You've grown. We heard you grow, you know. Every inch of you is written into the lower galleries. Children resonate beautifully. You did especially."

Bram's hands wanted to shake. He made them fists instead.

"You'll forgive us," the Maestro went on, gesturing, and chairs appeared—not conjured, Bram noticed, simply brought, by more of the puppet-things—"for standing audiences are for executions, and this is not an execution. This is a homecoming."

*Cyrus*

She sat because sitting let her hide how much the hall frightened her. The lanterns were souls. She had known it since the doorway—the map in her blood had told her, in its hungry way, *here is where the cargo is stored*—but knowing and seeing were different animals. Seeing meant counting. She counted past two hundred and stopped, because stopping was mercy.

"I'm not a cartographer," she said. "I draw pictures."

"You draw futures," the Maestro said pleasantly. "Do not be modest in front of me, Miss Vane. Modesty is a currency we do not accept." He turned a sheet of paper on the dais so it faced her, and her stomach dropped, because it was hers. The city that would not exist for fifty years—the one she had burned three nights finishing and hidden beneath her floorboards, or thought she had. Here it was, every line, every rooftop she had imagined into being, mounted and framed like a saint's relic.

"We found this in your valley fifty years from now," the Maestro said, and let the sentence sit, savoring it. "A figure of speech. We found it in your floorboards yesterday. Our riders are thorough, and our interest in you is long. Do you know what the court beneath the mountain does, cartographer? Everyone assumes we sing the kingdom. That is true, but it is the least of it. We sing the kingdom *outward*. Borders are arguments, and ours must win. Every year the realm grows, and every year the growth must be authored. Realms do not expand into empty space. They expand into unmapped space, and unmapped space resists. Unless one can chart it before it is claimed." His folded hands opened, palms up, an offering. "Unless one has a gift like yours."

"I want Bram's contract," Cyrus said. "That's why we're here. Nothing else."

"Yes. The boy." The Maestro glanced at Bram with genuine warmth, which was somehow more obscene than cruelty would have been. "You should know he sings magnificently. Or rather, the voice does. It has matured wonderfully in our keeping. When you leave—if you leave—it goes back into him, whole. I am not in the business of damaging instruments. Damaged instruments devalue the collection."

"Then return it."

"For services," the Maestro said. "Ten years of them. You will chart the court's expansion—the realms beneath the realm, the countries that do not yet exist, the spaces where the world is still deciding what it is. Your maps will tell us where to sing, and the song will make it true. In exchange: the treble's freedom, safe passage home, and the hunt called off. The witch-finders serve us, Miss Vane, though they don't know it. One word from me and the compasses that point at talent go quiet across three kingdoms." He smiled again. "One life traded for many, and none of them yours. Even the valley's arithmetic approves."

*Survival,* said a voice in her that sounded like her mother. *He's offering survival.*

*He's offering a bill of sale,* said another voice, which sounded like herself, if herself were braver.

Because she saw the shape of it now, the full architecture, and it was beautiful and vile in exactly the proportions of the hall around her. Ten years of drawing worlds into being so they could be swallowed. Ten years of being the pen for the hand that had hollowed out a choirboy in a market town. And Bram—Bram would walk free carrying the knowledge that he had been the price, that she had signed him over with ink made of her own talent, and he would spend the rest of his voiceless life doing the arithmetic of what he'd cost her, and no rhythm-code could carry that conversation.

Or she refused, and they both went into lanterns.

There was no third option. That was what the chairs were for, the courtesy, the roasted birds: to make her believe there was no third option, so that taking the first felt like wisdom instead of surrender.

She let the silence stretch. She let her face do the thing faces did when calculating—she had watched debt-collectors' wives do it at market, the small tightening around the eyes—and she asked, "Show me the work."

Something flickered behind the Maestro's mildness. Satisfaction? She couldn't tell, and hated that she couldn't.

"Of course," he said, rising. "But first—" and the courtesy dropped away for one instant, like wind lifting a carpet, showing the stone floor of the thing underneath, "—understand me, cartographer. I have waited a long time for you. Longer than you have been alive. The gift you carry is not an accident of birth. It is a loan, and loans return to their lender. Whatever you decide tonight, you were always going to stand in this hall. The only question ever in play was whether you stood here as our servant or our guest." The mildness returned, seamless. "Servants dine separately. Guests see everything. Shall we?"

*Bram*

Bram watched Cyrus rise, watched her arrange her face into the shape of a woman considering a bargain, and he knew—because of the code, because of the road, because of a hundred nights of reading her by firelight—that she was lying with her whole posture. She would not trade him. She also would not tell the Maestro that. So Bram did the only useful thing he could do: he played his part, the broken treble, the mute boy trailing his rescuer, harmless as furniture. He kept his eyes down and his fists loose and he listened.

The Maestro led them along a gallery behind the dais, and the lanterns thinned here, and the sound began.

It came up through the floor first, through Bram's boots, through the bones the boots covered. A chord, vast and sustained, held so long it stopped seeming like sound and became climate. Then, as they walked, it resolved into parts—basses under, tenors weaving, and above them all a line of sopranos so clear it cut him somewhere old.

He knew the hymn. It was the valley's harvest hymn, the one they sang every autumn when the warding stones were dressed with wheat, and the Choir was singing it, and that was wrong, that was *deeply* wrong, because the harvest hymn belonged above, to the valley, and hearing it here was like hearing your own name spoken by a stranger in a dark room.

The gallery opened onto a balcony, and the balcony overlooked the Choir.

Hundreds of them. Standing in ranked terraces carved from living rock, robed in grey, mouths open, faces lit from within by the same soft breath-light as the lanterns. They were not suffering, and that was the horror of it. They sang with the serene absorption of people deep in prayer, or deep in sleep, and their song poured outward into tunnels that Bran could feel pulling at the air—north, south, east—going out to push the borders of the kingdom another mile, another field, another village.

His eyes swept the ranks, hunting. He didn't know what he was hunting until he found it: a soprano near the front whose mouth shaped words he could almost read. Three years of watching choirs from the outside had made him fluent in mouths. And the mouth was singing *Bram*, singing it inside the hymn, over and over, gentle as a bell—

The Maestro spread his hands over the terrace like a farmer surveying wheat.

"Every kingdom needs an author," he said. "We are simply honest about the arrangement. Now." He turned to Cyrus, and his smile widened by one precise degree. "Before we discuss terms properly, there is someone you should meet. A senior voice. She harmonizes beautifully. She came to us—" a pause, delicately cruel, "—a long time ago. From the valley."

On the second terrace, one grey figure turned its head.

Not toward the Maestro. Toward Cyrus.

And Bram, watching her face come apart and reassemble into the bargaining mask a heartbeat too late, understood that whatever bargain was being offered in this hall, it had never really been about him at all.
