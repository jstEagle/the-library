## 14. The Choir Revealed

They came for us at the hour the lanterns dimmed.

I learned the court's hours by its breath. The trapped lights overhead—each one a jar of stolen air, each one sighing—dimmed in slow rotation, like the court itself was sleeping, or pretending to. When the dimming came, two servants of the Maestro appeared at our guest chamber door without knocking. They were tall and gray-robed and they moved the way water moves, which is to say without any decision visible in them, and they stood in the doorway with their hands folded and waited for us to understand that this was not an invitation.

Cyrus understood first. She always understood first.

"It's time," she said, and her voice was steady in the way I had learned to distrust—steady the way a rope is steady when something heavy is pulling the other end.

I stood. My legs had been restless for hours anyway, since the Maestro's courtesy at dinner, since the way he had looked at Cyrus across the candle flames and said, *tomorrow I will show you what we have built here*, in the tone a man uses to describe a garden. I had spent the night with my back against the wall, tapping the hymn-rhythms against my knee, counting the beats between the court's breaths. Four hundred and twelve. Then four hundred and twelve again. Whatever this place was, it kept time like a heart that had been taught to.

The gray-robed ones led us down corridors of raw stone that should have been cold but weren't. That was the first wrongness I could name. The mountain was warm. Warm like a body. The walls sweated faintly in places, and the air had a taste I couldn't get out of my mouth—copper and beeswax and, beneath it, something older, like the smell of a room where someone has wept for a very long time.

Cyrus walked close enough to me that her sleeve brushed my arm. She was doing the thing she did with her maps—reading. I could feel it coming off her like heat off a forge. Her eyes moved along the walls, the joints of the stone, the seams where human masonry gave way to something that had never been masonry at all, and I knew she was charting. Even without parchment. Even without her gift fully awake. She was drawing this place into herself, corridor by corridor, the way she had once drawn cities that didn't exist yet.

*Remember this*, I thought at her, uselessly. *Remember all of it.*

She glanced at me. For a moment I thought she had heard me, the way she sometimes seemed to hear the things I didn't say, and her mouth went thin and hard.

The corridor opened.

* * *

I had imagined the Choir, in the months since my voice was taken. I had imagined it as a room. A vault, maybe, or a cellar, some dark place where my voice sat in a jar with a label on it, waiting. I had imagined I would walk in and see it and know it, the way you know your own coat on a hook.

It was not a room.

It was a cathedral the size of weather. The cavern opened above us and below us and ahead until my eyes gave up trying to find its edges, and hanging in it—not from anything, just hanging, the way the lanterns hung—were the voices.

That is the only word I have. They looked like voices. They looked the way sound would look if you could catch it in your hands: ribbons of pale light, hundreds of them, thousands, drifting in slow orbits around a central dark, trailing long tails of fainter glow like comets or like the trains of dresses. Each one pulsed. Each one pulsed *in time*, and the time they kept was the time of the court's breath, four hundred and twelve beats, and when I understood that, I understood what I had been counting all night.

The whole mountain was keeping time with them. Or they were keeping time with the mountain. I could not tell which was the heart and which was the blood.

And they were singing.

I don't know how to write down what it was. It wasn't loud. It was barely audible at all, a sound like the one you hear when you press a shell to your ear, except the shell was the whole world. Hundreds of voices singing one held note, and out of that note, if you listened the way you listen for a name called across a field, came everything: the ring of hammers in the forges above, the creak of carts, the bleat of goats, the magistrate's voice reading proclamations, the bells, the rain. The kingdom. The song was the kingdom. The kingdom was the song, held in place note by note, voice by voice, the borders stitched in sound.

I had mouthed hymns for six years in a church where no one questioned why the choirboy made no noise. I had thought my voice was *gone*. Sold, spent, sitting in a jar somewhere, dead.

It wasn't dead. It was *working*. Somewhere in that slow wheel of light, a ribbon with my timbre in it was holding up a road, or a wall, or a border stone, singing me into the world's foundations without my consent, without my knowledge, without ever once letting me hear myself.

My knees went. Cyrus caught my elbow—quick, hard, her fingers digging in like she could hold me up by force—and I stood, because she was standing, and that was its own kind of command.

* * *

The Maestro was already there.

He stood on a spur of stone that jutted out over the abyss like a pulpit, and he did not turn when we arrived; he watched his Choir the way a shepherd watches a flock at dusk, with an expression I could only call tenderness. That was the worst of it, in those first moments. The tenderness. He loved them. You could see that he loved them, the way a man loves a thing he has made.

"Six hundred and forty-one," he said, without turning. "That is the current count. We were six hundred and forty until the spring, when a fiddler from the eastern valleys came to us by way of a debt." He said it the way a man reports a birth. "You are looking at the oldest working of human artifice in the world, and also the newest, because it is remade every hour. Kingdoms of stone crack. Kingdoms of law rot. But a song, maintained—"

He turned then, and his eyes found Cyrus, and the courtesy in his face was so perfect it was clearly practiced, the way a mask is practiced.

"—a song maintained is *eternal*."

Cyrus said nothing. She had gone very still, the stillness she got when she was frightened and refusing to be frightened, and her eyes were moving through the wheel of voices, ribbon by ribbon, and I thought at first she was counting them the way the Maestro had counted them, six hundred and forty-one, cataloguing, charting, because that was what she did with everything, that was how she loved things, by mapping them.

Then her hand found my wrist.

Not caught it. *Found* it, the way a drowning person finds a rope, and her fingers went white, and I turned to look at her and her face had changed into something I had never seen on it before, not at the ford, not in the valley, not when the riders came.

She was looking at a voice.

It hung maybe forty feet out from the spur, in the third orbit, and it was like the others—pale ribbon, slow pulse, trailing its faint tail—and I could not have told you what made it different. But Cyrus could. Cyrus was staring at it the way you stare at a face in a crowd that you have buried and prayed over and dreamed about for fifteen years, and her lips had parted, and no sound came out of her, and I understood before she ever said it, because I had seen that look in my grandmother's face when she read the contract, that exact look, grief arriving faster than the body can decide what to do with it.

"That one," Cyrus said. Her voice came out sanded down to almost nothing. "That one. Who—"

"Ah." The Maestro's head tilted, and something moved behind his courtesy—satisfaction, I thought, or hunger wearing satisfaction's clothes. "You recognize her."

*Her.*

"No," Cyrus said. "No, that's not—she *left*. She left, she went over the pass, she—" Her hand was crushing my wrist now and I let it, I would have let it break. "She got out."

"Did she?" The Maestro said it gently. Gently, the way you tell a child the dog has died. "The valley keeps no records of departures, cartographer. Only arrivals. And the court beneath keeps the only records that matter." He gestured, one long finger, at the ribbon of light. "Harvested nineteen years ago. A powerful voice. Untrained, but *rooted*—you can see it even now, how she holds her place in the harmony. She anchors the western border. Every stone of the western wall is sung by her."

Nineteen years.

Cyrus had told me once, on the road, in the dark of the hedge-witch's loft, in one of those flat voices people use for wounds that have scarred over wrong: her mother had gone over the pass when she was small. Gone, and never written, and the valley had said what valleys say—*she ran off, they always run off*—and Cyrus had built her whole life on top of that sentence the way you build a house on a filled-in well. She had hated her a little for it. I had heard the hatred under the grief, the way you hear water under ice. *She left us. She left* me.

The western border. The western wall of the kingdom. The wall between the valley and the rest of the world.

The wall that magic could not cross. The wall that had made her valley a place where her gift went quiet, where she had hidden for her whole life, unnoticed, unsought.

Her mother's voice was the wall. Her mother had not left her. Her mother had been *made into the thing that shut her daughter in*.

* * *

I have no voice. I have not had one since I was nine years old. I have spent six years learning every other way a body can speak—hands, eyes, the tap of fingers, the angle of a shoulder—and in all that time I thought silence was my affliction. My sentence. The thing that had been done *to me*.

Watching Cyrus stand at the edge of that abyss with her mother's soul singing forty feet away, I learned that silence has another shape. That it can be done to a person who can still speak. That you can have a voice and still be voiceless, still be standing in front of everything you need to say with the words turned to stone in your mouth.

That was Cyrus. Her mouth moved twice and produced nothing. Her hand on my wrist had stopped crushing and started trembling, a fine fast tremor like a plucked string, and I did the only thing I could do, the thing my grandmother used to do for me when the contract's memory came at night: I turned my hand over and held hers. Held it the way you hold a bird. Not to keep it from flying. To keep it from shattering.

She looked down at our hands. Then up at me. And I watched it happen—I watched the grief go through her and come out the other side as something else, watched it the way you watch a storm cross a valley, because I had seen this exact weather in her before, at the ford, when her gift flooded out of her in a panic and became a shield. Grief went in. Something with an edge came out.

"Which note," Cyrus said.

The Maestro raised an eyebrow, courteous, inviting.

"Her voice," Cyrus said, and her voice was level now, level the way the rope is level, and I understood that I had been wrong about the rope all along. It wasn't something pulling her. It was something she was holding. "In the harmony. Which note is hers. Show me."

"Curious," the Maestro said softly. "Most kin ask *when*. Or *why*. You ask for the music." He smiled, and for the first time the smile went all the way to his eyes, and I wished with everything in me that it hadn't, because his eyes were the abyss. "This is why we have waited for you, cartographer. Six hundred and forty-one voices, and every one of them given. But the song is incomplete. It has always been incomplete. There is a note missing—the keystone note, the one that would let us sing beyond the mountains, beyond the crown, beyond anything—and we have searched for it for three hundred years." He stepped closer, and the lanterns of trapped breath swung gently around him like courtiers. "And then a mapmaker's daughter walks into my court, and when I look at her, do you know what I see?"

He looked at Cyrus the way he had looked at his Choir. With tenderness.

"I see the last note," he said. "I have been listening to you since the moment you crossed my threshold, and you are *glorious*."

The cavern breathed. Four hundred and twelve beats. The ribbons of light wheeled, and among them, in the third orbit, a voice with a western timbre held its place in the harmony, holding up a wall between her daughter and the sky.

Cyrus's hand tightened around mine once—hard, deliberate, the way she tapped the hymn-rhythms, the way she spoke when she couldn't speak.

And I felt her counting.
