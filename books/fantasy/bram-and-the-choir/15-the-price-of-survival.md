## 15. The Price of Survival

The Maestro's guest chambers were the most beautiful prison Cyrus had ever seen.

They had been given rooms carved into the living rock above the cathedral cavern, high enough that the Choir's song came up through the floor as a vibration rather than a sound—a hum in the soles of her feet, a tremor in her back teeth. The walls were veined with quartz that caught the lantern-light and scattered it like frost. A bed with blankets soft as moth-wing. A basin of water that steamed without fire. A window—*a window*, three hundred feet beneath the mountain's crown—that looked out onto a garden of pale fungi glowing blue as drowned stars.

Someone had left fruit on the table. Someone had turned down the covers. Someone wanted them comfortable, and comforted, and here.

Cyrus did not touch the fruit.

She sat instead at the writing desk, which was the only piece of furniture she trusted, because it was the only one that served the thing she actually was. Her satchel lay open before her. Inside: six finished maps, two half-finished ones, her pens, her ink, and the sheet of parchment stained with her own blood from the ford—the map that drew itself, the map that had led them here, alive and hungry and *waiting*.

She unrolled it across the desk and pinned its corners with the fruit she hadn't eaten. Apples make good weights. She'd learned that at nine years old, mapping the valley orchards her mother used to walk.

Her mother.

Cyrus pressed her palms flat against the parchment and felt it breathe.

Since the audience chamber—since the Maestro's courteous voice saying *the cartographer we have waited for*, since Bram's hand tightening around hers hard enough to grind bone—she had been turning one question over and over like a stone in a riverbed, wearing it smooth, looking for what lay underneath.

*Why does the court know my work?*

That was the stone's surface. The Maestro had described her maps in detail no stranger could possess. The river-road she'd drawn at fourteen that appeared, three years later, exactly where she'd charted it. The bridge at Callowmere. The second market square of a town that didn't exist yet. The Maestro had recited them like a lover reciting letters.

But underneath that question was a colder one, and she had been avoiding it for hours, because she already knew its shape:

*How does a court beneath a mountain read maps drawn by a girl in a valley where magic cannot follow?*

She picked up her pen. Not to draw—to interrogate. She dipped it in ink and wrote, in her smallest hand, along the margin of the self-drawing map:

*Who taught me?*

The parchment shivered under her fingers.

Nothing answered. But the ink she'd laid down sank into the page the way water sinks into thirsty ground, and where it vanished, faint lines rose elsewhere—thin as spider silk, threading out from the margin toward the center of the map, toward the cathedral cavern below, toward the hanging lanterns of trapped breath.

Toward the Choir.

"No," Cyrus said aloud, to an empty room.

The lines kept rising.

* * *

She should have woken Bram. He slept in the adjoining chamber, door open between them because neither of them had suggested closing it. He slept badly, she'd noticed—on his side, knees drawn up, one hand curled near his throat as if guarding something that wasn't there anymore. Twice in the night he'd made a sound like a word trying to hatch.

But she didn't wake him. This was a thing she needed to do alone, the way you need to pull a splinter alone even when someone offers, because if anyone else touches it you'll flinch and leave half of it buried.

She wrote again: *Where did the gift come from?*

More silk-thin lines. More of them this time, spreading from her words like cracks in ice, all running the same direction. Down. Inward. Toward the song.

Her hands were steady. That frightened her more than shaking would have. Some part of her had always known how to be calm while reading terrible documents; she'd spent her life deciphering contracts written on skin, after all, and skin remembers what it was made to hold.

*Why the valley?* she wrote. *Why a valley where magic cannot follow?*

And the map answered—not in words, never in words, but in the oldest language she knew. It drew.

A valley took shape in the corner of the parchment, small and precise: the river's elbow, the warding stones, the orchard rows, the crooked lane to market. Her valley, rendered in strokes surer than any she'd ever made, because these were not her strokes. They were memory laid down in blood and ink, older than her hands.

And threaded through the valley, through every line of it, ran a single filament of light. It entered from outside the map's edge—from somewhere far away, somewhere beneath—and it pooled, and waited, and held its shape inside the borders of a place where magic could not follow.

A vault, the drawing said. That is what your valley is. A place built to keep a treasure safe from everything except time.

Cyrus stood up so fast her chair went over backward. It clattered against stone and the sound seemed enormous, obscene, in the humming dark.

The filament in the drawn valley pulsed once, patient as a heartbeat.

* * *

She thought about her mother then, properly, for the first time in years—which is to say she let herself think about her, instead of walking around the thought the way you walk around a grave.

Maren Vane. Dead at thirty-one, or gone at thirty-one; the valley had never agreed on which. There had been no body. There had been a cold morning, an empty house, a kettle still warm on the stove, and a seven-year-old girl who woke to find the world rearranged around an absence shaped exactly like her mother. The neighbors said *fled*. The neighbors said it kindly, mostly—*your mother loved you, but some people can't stay rooted*—and the kindness had been worse than cruelty would have been, because cruelty she could have hated, and hatred keeps a child upright.

So Cyrus had grown up hating nothing, grieving nothing, and drawing maps of places that did not exist, in a valley where such drawings should have been impossible, and she had called it a gift the way you call a scar a gift: because the alternative was asking what cut you.

She crouched now beside the fallen chair and did not pick it up. She looked at the map on the desk, at the filament of light coiled in the drawing of her home, and she asked the question with her whole chest, silently, the way you scream in dreams:

*What am I?*

The parchment bloomed.

Not lines this time. An image—crude, urgent, a child's drawing rendered in blood-ink. A woman standing in a doorway. Light pouring out of her chest, out of her mouth, out of her open hands, streaming upward and away in a long bright thread that ran off the edge of the map and down, down, down, into darkness at the bottom of the page where a shape waited to receive it: a cavern, hung with lanterns, filled with singing figures.

The thread did not snap when the woman fell. That was the worst part. The thread stayed attached, trailing from her emptied body like a fishing line, and at its far end, deep in the dark, the filament split into two—and one strand sank into the singing court, and the other strand traveled back up the map, back through the valley, back through the years, and ended in a cradle.

In her.

Cyrus Vane had been seven years old when her mother disappeared. She had been seven years old and full of something planted, something seeded, something that had been waiting inside her bloodline the way a seed waits inside winter—not dormant but *scheduled*. The valley hadn't hidden her from magic. The valley had been built to hide the magic until it ripened.

Her gift was not a gift. It was a fragment. A shard of the Choir, broken off and buried in a family line, generation after generation, thinned and passed down like an heirloom nobody knew they were keeping—until the court beneath the mountain decided the interest had compounded enough to collect.

Her mother hadn't fled.

Her mother had been *harvested*.

* * *

Cyrus did not remember crossing the room. She was at the basin suddenly, gripping its rim, and the water in it had frozen solid—frozen, in a warm room, in a court heated by breath-lanterns—and the frost was climbing the walls in patterns, and the patterns were *maps*, streets and rivers and coastlines of places that had never existed blooming across the quartz in white lace, her gift pouring out of her uncontrolled the way it had poured out at the ford, except there was no hunters' compass to confuse this time, no enemy to blind, nowhere for the power to go except outward and outward and—

She made herself stop. Breath by breath. The way you talk yourself down off a roof: not by leaping, not by lying down, but by finding the next handhold. In. Out. The frost slowed. Stopped. Began, very slowly, to weep down the walls.

In the basin, the ice cracked and melted.

She laughed. It came out wrong, a dry bark with nothing behind it, and she put both hands over her mouth because the sound reminded her of Bram—of the shapes his mouth made around sounds that wouldn't come—and that undid her worse than anything the map had shown.

Because he was down here because of her. Because her maps had guided the debt-collectors' road to his grandmother's door. Because the same court that harvested her mother had sold a boy's voice to buy flour and firewood, and the whole rotten architecture of it—the contracts, the hunts, the valleys built like vaults—all of it sang the same song, and she had been humming along her entire life without hearing the tune.

*The cartographer we have waited for.*

Of course they'd waited. You don't hunt a seed. You water it. You build a quiet valley around it and wait for it to come up green, and then you send witch-hunters into the valley—not to catch the gifted, never to catch the gifted, but to flush the bird from cover, to drive her out past the warding stones where her power would flood back into her like cold water, where it would ignite, where it would grow loud enough to be found.

Every step of their journey had been walked for them. Every road she'd charted had been charted first by the thing that made her.

Cyrus slid down the wall to the floor, knees to chest, and let herself shake now, finally, because the steadiness had done its work and there was nothing left to do with terror except survive it.

* * *

She didn't know how long she sat there. Long enough that the frost-maps on the walls had melted to clear streaks. Long enough that the Choir's hum through the floor had shifted key twice, moving through some vast slow music she couldn't parse.

When she finally moved, she moved with purpose, and the purpose surprised her by being cold and clean.

She returned to the desk. She righted the chair. She rolled up the self-drawing map and set it aside—she was done taking its testimony; she'd heard enough—but she pulled fresh parchment from her satchel, blank, ordinary, bought in a market town for two coppers, and she smoothed it flat with both palms.

Then she began to draw. Not the future. Not roads and bridges and towns fifty years unborn. She drew the past.

Her mother's face, from memory—she was startled how much of it remained, the strong jaw, the laugh-lines already cut deep at thirty, the way her hair went iron-grey early at the temples. Maren Vane, drawn in careful strokes. And around her, from memory again, the valley: the kitchen, the kettle, the door she'd walked out of on a cold morning and never walked back into.

Cyrus drew it all, and her hand did not shake, and nothing supernatural happened, because this map was not a prophecy. It was a record. It was the plainest kind of truth: *this person existed, and this is where she stood.*

"I'm coming for her," she said quietly, to the ink, to the room, to whatever fragment of Maren Vane still hung in those lanterns below, singing a kingdom into permanence with a voice that harmonized like home. "Whatever's left of you. I'm coming."

The word *coming* settled into the parchment and, faintly, in the corner where she hadn't yet touched pen to page, a new line began to rise—thin as spider silk, threading downward, charting a corridor she had not seen before. Not toward the cathedral cavern. Somewhere deeper. Somewhere the Maestro's tour had not gone, behind a door of black stone that the courteous voice had passed without pausing, almost too smoothly, the way a liar passes a subject.

The map was showing her where the harvested voices were kept.

Or it was showing her where she was meant to be kept.

Cyrus stared at the rising line for a long moment. Then she heard it: footsteps in the corridor outside, unhurried, courteous, stopping just beyond her door. And beneath the floor, the Choir changed key one final time—resolving, settling, going quiet as a held breath—as if the whole court beneath the mountain had paused to listen for her answer.

A knock. Soft. Patient.

"Cartographer," said the Maestro's voice, warm as lamplight. "You've been busy. Shall we discuss what you've learned?"

Cyrus looked at the blank page on her desk, where the secret corridor still traced itself, line by line, toward the dark.

She covered it with her palm, and opened the door.
