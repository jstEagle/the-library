## 16. A Bargain in the Dark

The guest chambers were beautiful, which was how Cyrus knew they were a cage.

They had been led through corridors of veined black stone to a suite that any duke would envy: a bed hung with grey silk, a hearth that burned without wood, a window of thick crystal that looked out onto the cavern's endless dark and showed nothing—no view, no stars, only their own reflections floating in the glass like drowned things. The door had closed behind them with the softness of a lid.

Cyrus tried it anyway. It didn't open. There was no lock to pick, no bar to lift. The door simply *was* shut, the way a mouth is shut, and when she pressed her palm against it she felt a faint vibration, deep and patient, like a held note.

"They're listening," Bram signed. His hands moved in the small, quick language he'd taught her on the road—the one borrowed from deaf millers in his grandmother's village, reshaped by two travelers who needed to speak without sound. "Or they're listening for sound. Maybe not this."

"Then we talk like thieves," Cyrus said aloud anyway, because her nerves needed the noise. She crossed to the crystal window and studied their reflections. "We have until midwinter. That's what the contract said. Payment due in full."

Bram shook his head. He sat on the edge of the silk-hung bed, spine straight, hands folded in his lap like a boy at prayer, and Cyrus understood suddenly that he was performing calm—that somewhere beneath the stillness he was screaming, and had been since the Maestro's courteous voice had welcomed them into the mountain.

*The cartographer we have waited for.*

She should have run then. She should have taken Bram's hand and bolted back up those impossible stairs, through the door that remembered her voice too well, into the cold honest daylight where the worst thing hunting them was a witch-finder with a compass. Instead she had stood in that cathedral cavern, surrounded by lanterns of trapped breath, and felt her gift lean toward the Choir like a plant toward sun, and she had known—with the sick certainty of a mapmaker reading terrain—that there was no road out of this place that didn't pass through its heart.

"We're not leaving," she said quietly. "Not the way we came."

Bram looked up. His eyes were wet but steady.

"So we don't leave," she said. "We break it."

* * *

They worked by the light of the hearth that burned without fuel, and Cyrus hated it a little, the way it gave warmth without cost, comfort without truth. Everything down here was like that. Songs without singers. Light without fire. A kingdom sung into being by people who had never agreed to sing.

Bram tapped the arm of the chair.

It took her a moment to understand what she was hearing. Then she went cold all over, because she knew the rhythm—of course she knew it, she'd grown up in a valley that sang it every sabbath morning. It was the opening phrase of the valley hymn, the old one, the one about the shepherd who counts his flock by knocking on each skull, gentle, gentle, so the souls wake laughing. Her mother used to hum it while kneading bread.

He tapped it again, slower, and pointed at her, then at himself, then made a shape in the air—a sphere, a whole.

"The hymn," she said slowly. "You're giving me the hymn."

He nodded, and tapped it again, and this time she heard what he was actually showing her: not the melody as she'd always heard it, but its *structure*. Four phrases. Each phrase built from the one before, each one folding the previous into itself the way a river folds its tributaries. And underneath the four phrases, holding them together, a fifth thing—not a note, not a rest, something between. A silence shaped exactly like a missing voice.

Her skin prickled. "That gap. That's deliberate."

Bram's hands flew: *Yes. Every song has one. The Choir is built around one.*

"A keystone," she whispered. The word arrived in her mouth before she could stop it, and she watched him flinch, because he'd heard the Maestro say it too. *The cartographer we have waited for.* Not a guest. A note.

He tapped again—faster now, urgent—and she forced herself to listen past her own dread. He was giving her the second phrase, but altered: one beat displaced, one note bent flat, just slightly, just enough that if you sang the phrase against itself the way the Choir did, layering voices in rounds, the harmony would grind. Not fail. Grind. Like a wheel with sand in the axle.

"You want me to bend it." She was thinking aloud now, pacing, her gift stirring under her skin like water remembering it was a flood. "One voice turned against the others. A discord planted inside the structure, where everything leans on everything else, so when it grinds—" She stopped. "The whole song carries the flaw forward. Into every border it draws. Every wall. Every hall."

Bram nodded once, slow and grave, and then he did something that undid her more than all the tapping: he reached across and took her hand, and pressed her fingers flat against the arm of the chair, and tapped the rhythm *through her bones*, so she felt the hymn's architecture in her marrow—the four phrases, the folded harmonies, the shaped silence waiting at the center like an empty throne.

And Cyrus Vane, who had spent her whole life hiding, who had never let another living soul feel the tremor of her gift, sat very still and let a mute choirboy teach her the anatomy of the thing that had eaten his voice.

"I need to know more," she said, when she could speak. "The Choir isn't one song. It's hundreds of voices. Hundreds of songs, braided. If I plant the discord in the wrong braid, they'll isolate it. Cut it out like a rot. They've done it before—I saw the village. The one that forgot its name." She met his eyes. "I need to know which thread holds the most weight. Which voice, if it turns, drags the most of the song down with it."

Something moved across Bram's face—grief, and fear, and under both of them a terrible resolve. He lifted his hand and began to tap again, and this time the rhythm was not the valley hymn.

It was slower. Older. A lullaby-structure, cradling and repetitive, the kind of song meant to be sung over and over until the singer forgets they are singing. And woven through it, unmistakable even in rhythm alone, was a second line—a descant, high and clear, threading above the cradle-rock of the lower part.

Cyrus knew that descant. She had grown up falling asleep to it.

"That's my mother's," she said. Her voice cracked on the second word. "That's her line. In the Choir."

Bram didn't look away. He tapped it again, gently, apologetically, and pointed to the center of the rhythm—to the place where the descant crossed the lullaby, where the two lines leaned hardest against each other, bearing the most weight.

*There,* his finger said. *That's your thread. That's the load-bearing voice.*

"She's been holding up this mountain," Cyrus said. The words came out flat and strange, as if someone else were saying them. "All these years. My mother's been down here singing the borders of the kingdom that harvested her, and I—" She laughed, and it came out wrong. "I drew maps. I drew maps of places that didn't exist yet, and I thought I was free, and the whole time I was just a seed they'd planted in the dirt outside the walls. Waiting to sprout. Waiting to walk home."

Bram caught her wrist—not hard, but firmly—and signed one-handed, fierce: *Not home. Ours. We choose.*

She looked at him. This boy who had lost his voice to pay debts he never incurred, who had every reason to curl around his wound and guard it, and instead he was here, in a cage of silk and warm air, teaching her how to break the prison with the only instrument he had left: rhythm. Memory. Defiance tapped out on a chair arm.

"Yes," she said. "We choose."

* * *

They planned until the false hearth burned low—or seemed to; Cyrus suspected the fire obeyed the Maestro's attention, dimming when the mountain listened harder.

The plan was simple, because simple plans survive panic. During the coronation concert—the Maestro had mentioned it with the offhand pride of a host describing a feast—the Choir would perform the great binding-song, the one that renewed the kingdom's borders, the one sung only when the court gathered in full. Hundreds of voices. Every stolen soul in the mountain, singing at once, and at the center of it all, the space they'd kept hollow for centuries: the keystone, the final note, the seat they believed she had come home to fill.

"I walk in as their cartographer," Cyrus said. "Their prodigal note. I let them think I'm considering it. The Maestro wants me willing—they said so, remember? *We do not harvest what can be given.* Courteous monsters. So I stand in the Choir's heart, and I listen, and I find her."

Bram tapped: *Your mother's line.*

"My mother's line. And when the song reaches the crossing point—the place you showed me, where her descant bears the most weight—I put the discord there. One bent note. Your phrase, the one you taught me, ground against itself." She flexed her fingers, feeling the gift coiled in them, vast and frightening and finally, finally aimed. "I don't attack the Choir. I *join* it, wrong. From inside. And the mountain sings its own fracture."

Bram was quiet a long moment. Then he signed, slowly: *And you? Where are you in the song after?*

Cyrus looked at the crystal window, at their reflections hanging in the dark like two small lanterns of their own.

"I don't know," she said honestly. "Maybe nowhere. Maybe the song takes me with it when it goes. But Bram—if it works, every voice walks free. Yours. Hers. All of them. That's worth whatever's left of me."

He shook his head hard, and crossed the room, and took both her hands, and pressed them against his throat—against the smooth, scarred hollow where his voice had been cut away—and held them there while he tapped the valley hymn against her palms, gentle, gentle, so the souls wake laughing.

*I'm already empty,* his hands said, when he released her. *Don't make us both be.*

She didn't answer. There wasn't an answer. She pulled him into a hug that was half embrace and half apology, and felt him hold on like she was the last solid thing in a dissolving world, and over his shoulder she watched the crystal window, and in the reflection she saw—

She went rigid.

Behind them, faint in the glass, the door of the chamber stood ajar. Not open. Ajar. A hand's width of darkness where solid stone had been, and in that darkness, watching, the pale suggestion of a face.

By the time she turned, the door was shut. The vibration in it had changed—deeper now, warmer, almost pleased.

Bram followed her gaze and signed, very carefully: *Did they hear?*

Cyrus looked at the door for a long time. Then at the hearth, burning its fuelless flame. Then at the boy who had given her his only weapon, note by tapped note, trusting her with the shape of his ruin.

"No," she lied, and smiled, and began to hum—softly, sweetly, the valley hymn, perfect and unbroken, the song of a woman preparing to come home. "But even if they did—all they heard was a cartographer learning her part."

Somewhere beyond the door, deep in the stone, something sighed with satisfaction.

The concert was tomorrow.
