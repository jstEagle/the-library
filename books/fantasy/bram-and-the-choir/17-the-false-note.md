## 17. The False Note

The robes they gave me were the color of drowned things.

I stood in the guest chamber while a servant with no shadow—no shadow, I had checked three times—buttoned me into grey silk that moved against the light instead of with it. Court dress, the Maestro had called it, when he'd sent it up with his compliments. *You will want to look your best for the concert, Cartographer. It is not every coronation that has an honored guest.*

Honored. Caged. The words had the same shape in this place.

Bram sat on the window ledge, watching me dress with the flat attention he'd worn since we'd been locked in. Two days now. Two days of meals that arrived already seasoned with sleep, of corridors that rearranged themselves when I tried to count my steps, of the Maestro's courteous voice drifting up through the stone like water through chalk. Two days of Bram tapping hymn-rhythms against my palm in the dark—*slow, slow, quick-quick, slow*—until I could read the Choir's architecture the way I read coastlines.

*Four voices hold each wall,* he'd tapped, over and over, until I understood. *The song is a room. A room has corners. Corners can be broken.*

I looked at myself in the mirror the chamber provided. The glass showed me a woman in drowned-grey silk, and behind her, faintly, the mirror showed something else: a road. Not the chamber. A road, running away into green country, the road I'd have drawn if my hands were free.

The mirror was trying to sell me something. Everything down here did.

"Stop it," I told it quietly, and the road faded, sulking.

Bram slid off the ledge and came to stand beside me. In the glass, our two reflections looked like a painting someone had started and abandoned—him in his borrowed choir white, me in my drowned grey, both of us wearing the faces of people who belonged here. That was the trap of it. The court didn't need chains. It only needed you to look like part of the composition.

He tapped my wrist. *Tonight?*

"Tonight," I said.

*Scared?*

"Terrified." I took his hand and tapped back, clumsy, the rhythm we'd built for *yes, and anyway.* "Yes. And anyway."

His mouth did the thing that wasn't quite a smile but lived in the same neighborhood. Then he straightened my collar with two brisk tugs, the way his grandmother must have done a thousand mornings ago in a valley I'd helped put on the map, and we went down to unmake a kingdom.

* * *

The cathedral cavern had been dressed for murder.

That was my first thought, stepping out of the ascending gallery into the vast dark, and I couldn't improve on it. Lanterns of trapped breath hung in their hundreds from the vaulted stone, and tonight they'd been hung in patterns—spirals, sunbursts, the long interlocking chevrons of the court's heraldry—so that the whole cavern glowed like the inside of a jewel. Banners of woven sound draped the pillars; I don't know how else to describe cloth that rippled when no air moved, that hummed if you came too close. The Maestro's court had turned out in its finery: the grey courtiers with their unfinished faces, the breath-lantern keepers, the tall silent ushers whose bodies bent like reeds in a wind I couldn't feel.

And above it all, tier upon tier rising into the dark, the Choir.

I'd seen them once, from a distance, in the Maestro's guided tour—his word for parading his collection. Now I saw them properly. Hundreds of them. Hundreds of stolen souls, standing in ranked rows on stone shelves that spiraled up the cavern walls like the inside of a shell, each one wearing the shape it had worn in life, each one singing. They had been singing since before Bram was born. They would have sung until the mountain wore down to a hill and the hill to a memory, holding the borders of this kingdom taut the way tent-ropes hold canvas, and not one of them had ever been asked.

I found her row before I meant to. Third tier, left of center, a woman with my jaw and hands that moved when she sang the way my hands moved when I drew. My mother. Or the shape my mother's soul wore now, worn smooth by decades of someone else's music.

*Later,* I told the ache in my chest. *Grieve later. Burn later. Right now: work.*

Bram's hand found mine and tapped once—*ready*—and then the ushers were moving us, gently, irresistibly, toward the places the court had decided we would occupy. Bram was seated among the human guests on the cavern floor, in a row of chairs that had grown, not been built, out of the living rock. I was seated one tier up, in a box of grey silk, close enough to the Choir's lowest rank to feel their song in my teeth.

The Maestro stood below on a dais of black glass, and when he raised his arms, the cavern inhaled.

* * *

I have heard the valley choir. I have heard the king's cathedral in Lowmark, before the witch-hunters made me stop going anywhere a register was kept. I thought I knew what a hundred voices could do.

I knew nothing.

The Choir began with a single note so low I felt it in the floor, in my chair, in the fluid of my eyes. Then a second voice joined it, a fifth above, and the stone itself seemed to lean. Then ten voices. Then fifty. The sound didn't build the way human music builds—it *assembled*, each voice clicking into place like a stone into an arch, and with every voice that joined, the cavern became more real. That's the only way I can say it. The walls grew more wall. The lantern-light grew more light. Somewhere above us, I understood, the whole mountain kingdom was being sung one more day into permanence: the farms, the fords, the ford where I'd bled my gift into a river to save us. The coronation concert wasn't a celebration. It was maintenance. The court beneath the mountain was re-singing its kingdom the way a housewife re-lights her hearth.

And at the center of the arch, holding every voice in tension, was the Maestro. He didn't sing. He *conducted*, and the Choir was his instrument, and the kingdom was his song, and I was—according to him—the final note. The keystone. The cartographer whose gift had been seeded into a bloodline generations ago so that the court could always, eventually, reclaim it.

My gift. The thing that had let me draw cities fifty years unborn. A fragment of this. A splinter of the Choir, lodged in my mother's mother's blood, waiting.

I watched him conduct, arms carving the air, courteous even in triumph, and I fed my rage down deep where it could bank into something usable. Rage is fuel, but it's a messy burn. What I needed was cold.

Bram, below, sat with his hands in his lap, his face tilted up toward the Choir, and even now—even now, with everything—there was hunger in it. He was watching three hundred people do the thing that had been cut out of him. I tapped my own wrist where he couldn't see, the rhythm we'd built for *soon*, and hoped some part of him felt it.

The concert moved through its movements. A march for the new monarch's ancestors. A lament for the old one—I gathered the old monarch had been *sung down*, retired into the Choir itself, which explained the extra voice in the second tier wearing a crown-shaped shadow. Then a long, gorgeous, terrible hymn of permanence, during which the lanterns of trapped breath brightened and I saw, in each one, the faint shape of a lung expanding. Breathing for the kingdom. Breathing *as* the kingdom.

And then the Maestro turned, mid-hymn, and looked directly up at my box.

*Now,* his posture said. *Now, Cartographer. Come and be counted.*

The ushers came for me before I could decide whether to look surprised. Two of them, reed-bent, courteous, hands like folded paper. They walked me down the tier, through a gate of woven sound that hummed against my shoulder, and set me on the dais of black glass beside the Maestro, facing the Choir, and the whole court turned its unfinished face toward me with the mild attention of people watching a keystone being lowered into an arch.

"Cartographer," the Maestro said, and his voice carried through the hymn without disturbing it, the way a knife slips through water. "You honor us."

"I was told there'd be a seat," I said.

"There is. There always was." He gestured, and the black glass at our feet softened, dimpled, offered up a shallow basin like a font. "You needn't sing, of course. The Choir will do the singing. You need only give it the note it lacks. One note. One small surrender, and Bram's voice returns to him by morning, and your mother—" his eyes flicked up, warm as a banked fire, "—your mother may stand beside you at the coronation feast. As a guest. Not a lantern."

Every word of it was probably true. That was the horror of the Maestro; he rarely lied, because he never needed to. The trap was always comfortable. The trap had cushions.

I looked at the font. I looked at the Choir, three hundred souls singing the world into shape. I looked at Bram, small and white and silent in his row of chairs, and I let my face do what it needed to do—let it soften, let it waver, let it show him the exact moment a cornered animal stops fighting.

"Will it hurt?" I asked.

"Less than you fear," he said, which was a Maestro sentence if ever there was one. "More than nothing. All true things cost."

I stepped toward the font.

* * *

Here is what I had learned in two days of locked chambers and tapped hymn-rhythms, and here is what the Maestro did not know I knew:

The Choir is an arch. Every arch has a keystone, yes—but an arch also has *stones*, and stones can be turned against each other. Bram had tapped it out against my palm in the dark, over and over, his only language and therefore his sharpest: *four voices hold each wall. The song is a room. A room has corners.*

And a room with corners can be broken at a corner.

The lowest rank of the Choir, nearest the dais, was held by four voices in tension with each other—Bram had heard them during our tour, had felt the structure of it in his chest the way I felt structure in a coastline. Three sang the permanence-hymn. The fourth, at the corner, sang the *counter*-hymn: the voice that gave the other three something to push against. Take away the counter and the arch sags. Turn the counter *against* its neighbors and the arch—

Well. I was about to find out.

The fourth voice belonged to a young man, second row from the bottom, left of the dais. He had died, I guessed, sixty or seventy years ago; his clothes were cut in an old fashion, and his face had the smoothed-over look of a soul long since worn to its function. He sang his counter-hymn faithfully, the way a nail holds a board, because that was all he was allowed to be.

The Maestro guided me to the font with a hand light on my elbow. "Whenever you are ready," he said. "The Choir will wait. The Choir is very good at waiting."

I looked down into the black glass. My reflection looked back, and this time the mirror showed me nothing but myself—no roads, no temptations. Even my reflections had given up selling to me.

"One note," I said.

"One note," he agreed, delighted, terrible, certain.

I put my hands on the rim of the font. I closed my eyes. And instead of pouring my gift *down* into the glass, into the waiting mouth of the court, I reached *sideways*—out through my fingertips, out along the floor of the dais, out into the living stone that the Choir's song had spent three centuries making exquisitely, perfectly, *conductive*.

That was the joke of it. That was the joke I would treasure for the rest of my life, however long it turned out to be. The Choir had sung the stone of this cavern into a state of perfect resonance. Every wall, every tier, every inch of the mountain above us: tuned. Tuned like a bell. And I was a cartographer of things that did not yet exist, a girl whose gift could chart roads that had never been walked, and I had spent two days learning the shape of this room from a boy who knew it in his bones.

I did not give the Choir my note.

I gave the stone a map.

It unrolled from me the way my maps always did—fast, hungry, joyous—but this time what I charted was not a road. It was a *dissonance*. A false route. I drew, in the resonant heart of the tuned stone, a note that did not exist: a pitch bent wrong, a counter-hymn sung a quarter-tone sharp, the exact sound the young man in the second row *would* be singing if his soul had been left to drift, unheld, the way souls drift when no one is conducting them. I drew the sound of a voice that had slipped its tether. And the tuned stone, which had been waiting three hundred years for exactly this kind of instruction, which had been made *eager* for instruction, took my false note and gave it to him.

The young man's mouth kept moving with the hymn.

The sound that came out was mine.

For one heartbeat, nothing. The Choir sang on. The Maestro smiled his courteous smile. The lanterns breathed their trapped breath.

Then the young man's voice—my false note, sharp and drifting and *free*-sounding—collided with the three voices that held his wall, and the wall of the arch *shivered*.

I felt it in my teeth before I heard it. A wrongness rippling outward, voice catching voice catching voice, the dissonance spreading the way fire spreads through a dry roof—because every soul in the Choir had been tuned to perfect tension, and perfect tension is only another name for perfect fragility. The counter-hymn faltered. The permanence-hymn lurched to correct it and overcorrected. Somewhere in the third tier, my mother's voice bent against its neighbor, and the sound she made was the sound of a rope beginning to fray.

The Maestro's arms stopped mid-gesture.

"Cartographer," he said, and for the first time since I'd met him, the courtesy in his voice had a crack in it, thin as a hairline in glass. "What have you—"

The mountain shuddered.

Not the cavern. The *mountain*. I felt it through the soles of my feet, a long rolling groan from the roots of the world, as if something vast had shifted its weight in its sleep. Above us, through a mile of stone, the kingdom that the Choir had sung into being for three hundred years felt its borders loosen. Dust sifted down through the lantern-light, glittering. One of the lanterns of trapped breath guttered—and in its flicker, I saw the shape inside it press against the glass, *pushing*.

The Choir was still singing. That was the terrible thing. Three hundred souls, trained past all resistance, sang on through the wrongness, trying to hold the arch together, and every note they sang now carried the fracture in it, the way a cracked bell carries its crack in every peal. The false note I'd planted was in all of them now. It would be in all of them forever. There was no unsinging it.

"No way back," I said aloud, to the Maestro, to the court, to myself. "You should have checked my maps more carefully. I've never once drawn a road that led where people expected."

He turned to me, and his face was still courteous, and his eyes were the eyes of something very old watching its life's work crack down the middle, and he said, softly, in the voice of a man closing a door on the last room he has left:

"You have no idea what you have loosed, Cartographer. The song is not a cage. The song is a *dam*."

The mountain shuddered again, harder. Overhead, far up in the vaulted dark, a crack of raw grey stone split the beautiful tuned rock from tier to tier, and through it I heard—not falling rock, not water—*voices*. Hundreds of them. The Choir's stolen souls, straining against three centuries of harmony, and the strain sounding, more than anything, like a held breath about to break.

Bram was on his feet in his row of chairs, white-faced, staring up at the spreading crack, and his hands were moving—tapping, tapping, frantic, the rhythm we'd built for *run*.

I looked at the Maestro.

The Maestro looked at the crack in his heaven.

And somewhere above us both, the first stone came down.
