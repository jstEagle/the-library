## 18. What the Song Unmade

The first thing to break was the light.

Bram had spent eleven days beneath the mountain learning the language of the lanterns—how they brightened when the Choir swelled, how they dimmed between phrases like lungs resting between breaths. Trapped breath, Cyrus had whispered, and he had believed her, because he had spent four years as a boy who could not sing and so had become a scholar of everything singing did.

Now the lanterns stuttered. All of them. Hundreds of pale globes strung along the vaulted dark of the great gallery, each one holding someone's exhaled last word, and every one of them guttered at once, like a congregation flinching.

Then the discord hit them.

It came up through the floor. Bram felt it in his teeth before he heard it—a wrongness in the Choir's endless anthem, one voice bent half a tone against all the others, and the whole vast harmony shivering around that wound like water around a cracked stone. He had taught Cyrus the Choir's structure in their locked chambers, tapping hymn-rhythms against her wrist with two fingers, and she had listened the way she listened to everything, wholly, hungrily, her eyes going somewhere past the walls. But he had not understood, until this moment, what she meant to do with what he gave her. She hadn't bent a note. She had planted a seed of unmaking in the middle of the song, and now it was growing.

*Good*, he thought, and then, immediately, *oh no.*

Because the court was coming apart.

He saw it happen to the nearest pillar first. It was marble—or had been marble, or had been sung into something that behaved like marble—and as the discord rippled through it the stone seemed to remember that it had never actually been decided upon. Its edges went soft. Its veins of grey unraveled like loose thread. For one heartbeat the pillar was raw stone, unshaped, a lump of mountain with no opinion about itself, and then it tried to be a pillar again and only half managed, listing sideways, weaving grit from its capital like smoke.

Around him, the court screamed.

They were beautiful things, mostly, the courtiers of the deep kingdom—people who had been paid in permanence, faces smoothed by centuries of borrowed harmony, robes that moved as if the air had been instructed to cooperate. Now the instruction was faltering. A woman near the dais clutched her own arm as the sleeve of it went translucent, then solid, then translucent, her mouth opening on a sound Bram knew too well: the shape of a scream with nothing behind it. He had made that shape for four years. Seeing it on another face, on a face that had helped hold his voice prisoner, did not make him merciful. It made him glad, and the gladness frightened him more than the falling stone.

Cyrus grabbed his wrist. Two fingers, three taps—the rhythm they'd agreed on, the one that meant *run*—and then she was moving through the collapsing gallery with her map-satchel hugged to her chest, and Bram ran beside her.

Behind them, on the raised conductor's platform, the Maestro stood perfectly still.

That was the worst of it, Bram thought. Not the unraveling halls, not the courtiers flickering like bad candlelight. The Maestro, standing amid catastrophe with his long hands folded, his head tilted as though listening to a performance he had already decided to forgive. His face never changed. His face had never changed, not once, not when he had welcomed them with wine and courtesy, not when he had offered Cyrus the world in exchange for her pen. Stillness that complete, in a place that was coming apart, was its own kind of violence.

Then his head turned, unhurried, and found them in the crowd, and Bram understood that stillness had been patience, and patience had run out.

* * *

The galleries of the court had been mapped by no one—that was why Cyrus's gift had frightened the Maestro, Bram suspected; she charted places that didn't exist yet, and the court existed only because a song kept insisting it did—but even unmapped places had architecture, and architecture had corridors, and corridors could be run.

They ran them.

The stone failed around them in slow waves. Doorways they had passed an hour ago were gone, replaced by rough cave mouths, the elegant arches dissolved back into whatever the mountain had been before the Choir told it a story about itself. In one hall, the floor simply stopped being flat: slabs tilted like ice on a thawing pond, and a courtier slid down the incline silently, arms out, dignity intact even as physics abandoned her. In another, the ceiling-high windows of glowing quartz went dark one after another, and in the darkness Bram saw, briefly, the raw vein of the mountain running through the wall like marrow.

Cyrus pulled him left at a junction, then right, then stopped dead, and he felt her go still in the particular way that meant she was reading something invisible. Her gift had burned back into her when they crossed the warding stones weeks ago, cold water and wildfire both, and since the ford he had learned to recognize its weather on her face. This look was different. This look was grief doing arithmetic.

"The Choir's breaking along the fault lines," she said, low. "The song held everything in one shape. Now every hall is remembering it used to be a hole." She glanced back the way they'd come. "Including us, maybe. I don't know what happens to bodies when the song that argued them into being stops arguing."

Bram tapped her wrist: *You're still here.*

"I'm not part of the song," she said. "I'm the note it never got." A muscle moved in her jaw. "Which is exactly where he wants me."

Three taps: *Then don't give it to him.*

"That," said a voice from everywhere, "is entirely up to her."

The corridor ahead lit—not quartz-light but a colder, whiter glow, sourceless—and the Maestro stepped out of it as if stepping out of a private room. He looked exactly as he had at their welcoming feast: tall, courteous, dressed in grey that might have been silk or might have been compressed fog. The unraveling had not touched him. Perhaps he held his shape by sheer insistence. Perhaps, Bram thought, there had never been anything under the shape to unravel.

"Cartographer," the Maestro said. Only to Cyrus. But his eyes touched Bram too, briefly, the way a man checks that his coat has both sleeves. "You have done something quite remarkable. I want you to know that I admire it. Sabotage is a form of composition. You've written a rest into my score that will outlast empires." He spread his hands. "I would prefer, however, that you unwrote it."

Cyrus shifted, putting half a step between Bram and the Maestro, which Bram found both touching and absurd. "The Choir is fracturing. Every stolen soul in it is tearing free a little more with every bar. You can hear it. You built it—you must be able to hear it."

"I hear everything," the Maestro agreed pleasantly. "That is rather the whole of me."

"Then you know I can't restore it."

"You can." And here the Maestro's voice changed—not louder, but closer, the way sound behaves in small rooms, intimate, inescapable. "You are the keystone note, child. The fragment seeded into your bloodline four generations ago, carried and carried, thinned and thinned, until it arrived in you whole enough to matter. Your mother carried it before you. Do you understand what that means? It means your gift was never yours. It means—" and here he smiled, gently, terribly— "it means your mother is still here."

The silence after that had texture. Bram felt it press against his skin.

"No," Cyrus said.

"She sings third from the left," the Maestro said. "In the second tier. She has sung there for nineteen years. Her voice is the one that makes the eastern borders hold in winter—the frost-line, we call her section, though of course she doesn't know. They don't know anything anymore. That is the mercy of it. That is the *gift* of it." He took one step forward, and the white light followed him like a loyal animal. "Restore the song, take your place as its final note, and I will release her. Fully. Voice, memory, selfhood—whole, healed, returned to the surface by morning. She will grow old in the valley. She will watch you draw maps. She will never know what she lost, because I will return what she lost with her. That is within my power. Few things remain within my power for much longer."

Bram watched Cyrus's face do something he had seen it do once before—at the ford, when her gift flooded the valley with phantom roads and she realized, mid-collapse, that she could reshape the world and unmake herself doing it. Hope and horror arriving in the same instant, wearing each other's clothes.

Her mother. Nineteen years. Third from the left.

He thought about his grandmother's kitchen. About the contract written on skin, folded in the bottom of her sewing box. About the debt clause: *payment due in full by midwinter, or the remainder of the family follows.* He thought about how the Choir worked—it only ever took one piece at a time, and it always let you believe the rest was safe.

He reached out and tapped her wrist. Slowly. Deliberately. Not their code. Something older, from the valley hymns, the descending phrase that ended every verse: *it is finished, it is finished, it is finished.*

Don't listen. It's already done. Don't trade us for a promise from a man made of other people's voices.

Cyrus didn't look at him. But her hand turned over, and caught his fingers, and held.

"There's a flaw in your offer," she said.

"There generally is," the Maestro said, with real appreciation.

"If I restore the song, the Choir needs a keystone. Me. Which means my mother goes free and I take her chair. Third from the left. Second tier." Her voice stayed level, and Bram marveled at it, at the cartographer's precision of it, laying out the map of the trap so they could both see its walls. "And Bram stays voiceless. And every other family's midwinter debt comes due forever. And the crown keeps its witch-hunters, and the valley keeps losing children, and the song keeps singing borders around a kingdom built on a ledger."

"Yes," the Maestro said simply. "That is what survival has always cost. I'm surprised it's taken you this long to notice. Most people learn it much younger, and far less comfortably." He extended one long hand, palm up, and in it—impossibly, horribly—light gathered, coalesced, took the shape of a single held tone made visible, warm gold, trembling. "Her voice, cartographer. Right now, in my hand. Say yes and it flies home to her body tonight. Say no and when the Choir breaks—if it breaks—she scatters with the rest of them. Some souls reassemble. Most don't. I have watched nine hundred years of most-don't."

The gold note trembled in his palm. And Bram, who had been voiceless for four years, who knew better than anyone alive what a single stolen sound was worth, felt the full weight of the temptation land on Cyrus like a dropped ceiling.

Because it wasn't a lie. That was the thing. He could see it wasn't a lie. The Maestro had no need to lie; lies were for creatures with less power. If Cyrus said yes, her mother would sing again. Would laugh again. Would stand in the valley kitchen and hum over the bread dough without knowing why the humming felt like resurrection.

*Take it*, Bram almost tapped. *Take it, take your mother, leave me—*

She squeezed his fingers hard enough to hurt, as if she'd heard the tap he hadn't made.

"No," Cyrus said.

The word fell into the corridor like a stone into a well.

"I watched you offer it," she said, "and I watched myself want it, and I'm going to tell you exactly why the answer is no, because you should have to carry the whole of it." She stepped forward, pulling Bram with her, and her voice took on the cadence of someone reading coordinates. "My mother spent nineteen years as a frost-line. My friend spent four years as a hole in his own throat. And you're asking me to keep the machine running so that one woman can be handed back out of it—paid for with everyone still inside. That's not rescue. That's just choosing whose turn it is to be harvested." Her grip tightened. "You seeded my bloodline so the court would always get its note back. Fine. Here it comes."

The Maestro's smile faded by one degree. In his face it was like a landslide.

"You misunderstand your position," he said softly.

"No," Cyrus said. "I really think it's you who—"

The floor opened.

Not dramatically. Politely. The stone beneath their feet simply reconsidered its commitment to being ground, and parted, and the last thing Bram saw before the dark took them was the Maestro's white-lit figure above, folding its long hands, and the golden trembling note of his mother's voice sinking down after them into the abyss like a lantern lowered on a string.

*Come and get it*, the light said, in a language older than words. *It's waiting for you at the bottom.*

They fell together, and the mountain closed over the sound of them, and somewhere far below—deeper than the Choir, deeper than the court, deeper than anything Bram had let himself imagine—something that was not a song began, very quietly, to hum.
