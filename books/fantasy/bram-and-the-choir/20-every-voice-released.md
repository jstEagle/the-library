## 20. Every Voice Released

**Bram**

The note Cyrus poured into the dark was not a note at all. It was an ending wearing a note's shape, and Bram felt it pass through him like a blade through smoke.

The Choir fractured.

He heard it before he saw it—a seam tearing down the middle of the vast sound, one voice bending wrong against the others, and then another, and then the whole cathedral cavern of the court beneath the mountain shuddered like a struck bell. The lanterns of trapped breath swinging overhead flared white, guttered, and burst one by one, and where each one broke, something rose out of the shattered glass.

Not light. Not exactly. Something that had been pretending to be light for a very long time.

They came out of the walls first. Out of the hanging cages of breath, out of the choir stalls carved from living rock, out of the Maestro's great silver organ whose pipes were, Bram understood now, made of people. They rose slowly at the beginning, like swimmers surfacing from deep water—wobbling, unformed, shapes of pale shimmer with faces flickering inside them—and then the discord Cyrus had planted spread its roots through the song and the floodgates went.

Souls streamed upward in their hundreds. In their thousands.

Bram stood in the collapsing gallery with his hands over his ears and his mouth open, and he watched them go like migrating birds, wheeling in spirals toward some ceiling that was no longer a ceiling but an opening, raw stone peeling back to reveal darkness above and then, impossibly far above, a coin of grey daylight. They called to each other as they rose. He couldn't hear the words—he would never stop being grateful for that—but he heard the tones: recognition, reunion, the particular pitch of voices finding voices they had lost. Whole families knotted together midair and climbed as one.

The galleries unraveled around him. Halls that had stood for centuries came apart into raw stone, then into gravel, then into nothing, the court's architecture forgetting itself room by room. A staircase he had descended an hour ago simply wasn't there anymore, just a soft rain of dust falling upward into the dark. The mountain kingdom above—the borders it sang into permanence, the roads, the walls, the crown's whole glittering claim—all of it was loosening, thread by thread, as the singers left their places.

And in the center of the ruin, on his raised dais of black stone, the Maestro still stood with his arms raised, conducting a choir that was no longer there.

His baton moved through empty air. His coat, that immaculate grey, was fraying at the seams like the halls themselves. But his face—his courteous, terrible face—was turned not to the fleeing souls but to the dais's edge, where Cyrus knelt with her hands flat against the stone, emptied, spent, her gift gone out of her like a lamp going out.

*Bram,* she had said, or shaped at him, in those last seconds before the note took everything. *Get us out.*

There was no way out. The ways out were dissolving. So Bram did the only thing left: he ran to her, hooked her arm over his shoulders, and hauled her upright, and began to run through a palace that was eating itself.

* * *

**Cyrus**

She had thought emptiness would feel like cold.

It didn't. It felt like weather. Like standing outside for the first time after a lifetime indoors—everything larger than her skin, everything moving, wind where there had only ever been the close warm hum of the gift she'd hidden beneath floorboards and behind silence her whole life. Her maps were gone. Not the parchment ones; those were in her satchel, inert, ordinary paper now. The living ones. The thing that had let her chart places that did not yet exist. She reached for it the way you reach for a stair that isn't there, and found nothing, and kept falling.

Bram carried her anyway. Through a hall whose pillars were crumbling into chalk dust, across a bridge that ended midair and grew itself ahead of his feet out of sheer habit, down stairs that softened under them like wet bread. She could tell him directions no longer. There was no direction. There was only up—the coin of daylight, growing.

Behind them, unhurried as a host seeing off guests, the Maestro followed.

She heard his voice before she saw him again, pitched to carry over the roar of the unraveling, still courteous, still terribly gentle:

"Cartographer. You have unmade the borders of a kingdom. Do you understand what comes next? War. Famine. Every neighbor who ever feared these mountains will smell the weakness. I offered you survival and you have chosen apocalypse."

Cyrus found her voice. It worked. Her voice had never been the thing she'd spent; only the rest of her had.

"Then let them build honest borders," she said. "Let them draw them on paper."

The Maestro smiled, and there was something almost human in it, which was the worst thing yet. "You are so much your mother's daughter."

And then he opened his hand, and her mother's voice came out of it.

* * *

**Bram**

It was a small sound, that voice. Smaller than it had any right to be, after everything. A single line of melody, rising from the Maestro's palm like smoke from a snuffed candle, and Cyrus went rigid against Bram's shoulder as though a hook had been set behind her ribs.

"I kept only the finest," the Maestro said, walking toward them across a floor that was mostly memory. "When the harvests came in. One voice from each generation of the seeded line, held in reserve. Insurance." He tilted his head. "You have destroyed my Choir, cartographer. You have freed every common soul in my keeping and left me a pauper in a collapsing house. But this one I can still carry out. This one I can keep—or give."

The melody turned in the air. Bram knew nothing of Cyrus's mother except what lived in the daughter's silences, but he knew recognition when he saw it. Cyrus was staring at the little ribbon of song the way starving people stare at bread.

"If you restore the keystone," the Maestro said, "if you take your place and sing the song whole again, I will give her back to you. Whole. You will hear her say your name. And the court will endure, and the boy's family debt dies with me anyway, and no one need know what it cost. Everyone lives. Isn't that what maps are for? Finding the way that spares the traveler?"

For three full seconds, Cyrus wavered. Bram felt it in her body, the lean of her whole soul toward that sound, and he tightened his grip on her and braced to hold her back by force if he had to.

Then she laughed. It was a wrecked, gasping sound, half sob, but it was laughter, and it stopped the Maestro mid-step.

"You still don't understand," she said. "You think she's in your hand." She straightened, pulling free of Bram's arm, standing on her own ruined legs. "Look up."

Above them, among the thousands streaming toward the daylight, one shape had paused. One shape had turned. One small bright presence hung against the current of the ascending dead, looking down—not at the Maestro's palm, but at Cyrus's face.

"She was never in your hand," Cyrus said. "You've been conducting an empty pipe for twenty years. She left the moment your song broke. Everything since has just been echo."

The Maestro looked up. And Bram watched certainty die on his face.

The ribbon of melody in his palm thinned. Frayed. Turned to motes, and rose, and joined the shape above, and the shape above flared once—bright, so bright, brighter than every lantern of trapped breath in the ruined court had ever managed—and began, at last, to climb.

The Maestro screamed then. Not in rage. In the pure, animal horror of a man discovering his life's work was a hole in the shape of a building. And as he screamed, the last of the court came down around him, and Cyrus grabbed Bram's hand and pulled, and they ran.

* * *

**Cyrus**

The daylight swallowed them.

That was how it felt—one moment the dark's cold throat, the roar of stone behind them, the Maestro's scream thinning into the general ruin; the next, grey morning air, actual wind, the smell of pine and snowmelt, and grass under her hands. They had come out of a crack in the mountain's flank halfway up its southern face, and below them the valley fell away in early winter brown and gold, and the mountain itself—

The mountain was singing. Or had been, one breath ago, and was now, note by note, forgetting how.

They sat in the grass with their backs against warm stone, both of them shaking, and watched the sky fill.

The souls came out of the mountain everywhere—from cracks, from caves, from the old mine mouths and the sealed wells of the valley towns—and rose. All of them. A migration such as the world had never seen, a river of pale brightness climbing the morning, spiraling, gathering into skeins like geese, and every single one of them passing overhead close enough to touch, and none of them stopping, because they were done being kept. Done singing someone else's permanence. Going home, whatever home meant now, to bodies long buried and names scraped off stones and families who had learned to live around a hollow.

Cyrus watched them and wept without noise, because she had no gift left even for grief's theatrics. Just water, running down her face.

Bram's hand closed over hers.

And then his hand left hers, and flew to his own throat, and his mouth opened—

The scream came out of him like something breaking a dam. Twenty years of sold silence, every hymn mouthed, every word swallowed, every laugh bitten in half, all of it at once, a raw animal shriek that scattered birds from the pines and cracked at the edges and doubled him over in the grass. Cyrus grabbed his shoulders and held on. She did not try to soothe him. Some things needed screaming whole.

The scream climbed, and crested, and somewhere near its peak it changed key.

It became a laugh.

He laughed until he choked, flat on his back in the frost-wet grass, arms flung wide, laughing at the sky full of souls, at the mountain forgetting its song, at his own voice tumbling out of him rough and unpracticed and *his*, and Cyrus started laughing too, helplessly, tears and all, because there was nothing else the morning could possibly ask of either of them.

"*Hello,*" Bram said. His first word aloud in her hearing. It came out rusty and too loud and perfect. "*Hello. Hello. That's—that's my voice. That's mine.*"

"It's yours," Cyrus said.

He tried her name. Got it wrong on the first attempt, cracked in the middle, got it right on the second, and said it a third time just to feel it work. Then he sat bolt upright as if remembering language was a tool and not a toy, pointed down the valley, and said, rapid-fire, half-giddy: "My grandmother. The contract—if the court is gone, the debt is gone, I have to—I have to tell her, I have to *tell* her—" His face crumpled. "I haven't spoken to her in six years."

"Then she's owed six years," Cyrus said.

He was already on his feet, and she moved to rise with him—

—and stopped.

Because among the river of ascending souls, one had fallen out of the current. One had drifted down instead of up, down through the pine-scented air, and come to rest an arm's length away, level with her eyes, a small shimmer with a face in it that she had seen only in reflections of herself and in dreams she'd never told anyone about.

Her mother's presence did not speak. Perhaps it couldn't; perhaps it chose not to; perhaps some things are said better without the machinery of words. It simply drew near, and Cyrus felt it the way you feel sun through a window on a winter day—warmth with no weight, arriving from very far away and meaning only kindness.

*I see you,* the warmth said, without words. *I see what you made of what they planted in you. It was never theirs. It was never a chain. It became your eye, your hand, your map of everything good. I am not sorry it was you. I was always sorry it had to be anyone.*

*I forgive you* —and Cyrus wanted to protest, *what is there to forgive, I'm the one who should—* but the warmth pressed gently against her cheek, once, like a thumb wiping away a tear before it fell.

Forgiveness. Not farewell. Farewell was for people leaving each other; this was something else, a tide going out, natural and complete.

Then the presence turned, and rose, and joined the river, and Cyrus watched her mother dwindle among the hundreds and the thousands, smaller and smaller and brighter and brighter, until the whole migration passed over the eastern ridge into the sunrise, and the sky was only sky.

* * *

**Bram**

They sat a long time after that. Bram did not count how long. His voice kept surprising him—he would think a thought and it would half escape him as a mutter, and each time he flinched, then grinned, then muttered something else on purpose. He was drunk on it. He suspected he would be drunk on it for years.

Cyrus was quiet beside him, watching the empty sky, and when he finally touched her arm she came back from wherever she'd been and looked at him with red-rimmed eyes and something new underneath them. Lightness, maybe. Or the space where a weight had been so long the body didn't know its own contours without it.

"We should go down," she said. Her voice was different too. Flatter at the bottom, like a house with a room cleared out. "Grandmother. My valley. Before anyone decides the mountain's fall means the land is free for taking."

"The hunters," Bram said. "Their compasses."

"Gone, I think. Whatever they pointed at." She flexed her fingers, slow, like a woman testing a scar. "There's nothing left in me for them to find. I'm—" She stopped. Started again. "I'm just a cartographer now. The ordinary kind."

Bram looked at her. At the woman who had walked into the dark with nothing but impossible maps and stubbornness, who had unmade a kingdom rather than buy her life with his soul, who had refused her own mother's voice at knife-point because she knew—knew before any of them—that love kept in a cage isn't love at all.

"Cyrus," he said, and his voice cracked on it again, and he decided he liked that. "There's no ordinary kind of you."

Below them, far down the southern slope, the first sounds of the world reassembling were drifting up on the wind: a dog barking in some village, a bell ringing with no one pulling it, the confused murmur of ten thousand people waking to find the mountain's ancient hum gone silent for the first time in living memory. Somewhere in that murmur was his grandmother. Somewhere was a valley that had never known magic and would now have to learn to live beside its absence.

Cyrus stood, brushed the grass from her knees, and looked down the long slope between them and home.

"There's no road," she said. Half to herself. Her hand went to her satchel—to the maps that were only paper now—and stopped. For the first time in her life, she would have to walk somewhere without charting it first.

Bram stood beside her, filled his lungs with cold morning air, and did the one thing he had spent twenty years dreaming of.

He began to sing.

An old valley hymn, the one they all mouthed at festivals, rough and unsteady and gloriously loud in the empty air. He took the first step downhill without waiting to see if she'd follow, singing the path ahead of them the only way he knew how, and after a moment Cyrus laughed—wet, exhausted, alive—and stepped down beside him into the unmapped country.
