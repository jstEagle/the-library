## 21. The Unmapped Country

I had spent my whole life making maps of places that did not exist, and now I was walking through one.

The mountain's guts had gone quiet around us. Where the Choir had filled every corridor with song—where the very stone had hummed itself into being, walls held up by harmony as much as mortar—the silence after was total, thick, the kind of silence that presses on your eardrums like water. Bram walked a half-step ahead of me, one hand trailing the wall to feel for cracks in a world that no longer knew it was real, and I followed the sound of his breathing because I had nothing else to follow.

My gift was gone. I want to write that plainly, because everything else about it is complicated: the thing that had lived under my skin since before I could name it, the warmth behind my eyes when I looked at blank paper, the sense that the world was always whispering its unfinished roads to me—it was not there. It was like losing a limb I'd never seen. I kept reaching for it. My hands kept coming back empty.

"You're doing it again," Bram said. His voice, three days old, still startled both of us. He spoke carefully, the way a man walks on a healed leg, testing each word before he put weight on it. "The reaching."

"How do you know?"

"Your face does this thing." He glanced back at me. In the grey light leaking down from some distant shaft, his face was thinner than when I'd met him at market, hollow-cheeked, but the hollowness had changed quality entirely. The hollow look of someone missing a piece of themselves—that had been the first thing I'd noticed about him, months ago, over a stall of winter apples. Now he was hollowed out the way a flute is hollowed: shaped for something. "Like you're listening for a knock at a door you already opened."

"That's exactly what it is." I laughed, and it came out wrong, half a sob wearing laughter's clothes. "Everything used to talk to me. Roads. Rivers. Cities that hadn't been born yet. Now—" I gestured at the tunnel ahead of us, featureless, unremarkable, ordinary stone that would stay stone whether or not anyone sang it into permanence. "Now it's just rock. It doesn't know I'm here. Nothing knows I'm here."

"I do," Bram said.

We climbed.

* * *

You would think the way out of an unmade court would be hard to find. You would be wrong, in one direction and right in another. Hard to find, yes—but I had spent eleven days in that place, and even without my gift, I had a cartographer's memory. I had mapped the court beneath the mountain the way a spy maps a house she intends to burn: every gallery, every stair, every door. What I hadn't counted on was how much of it would already be gone.

We came to the Gallery of Bells—or what had been the Gallery of Bells—and found a raw throat of stone where the polished floor had been. No arches. No lanterns of trapped breath swinging in their long chains, greenish and patient. Just rock, ancient and indifferent, as if the beauty above it had been a coat of paint someone had scraped off. The mountain underneath had been waiting a long time to be itself again.

Bram went first through the rubble, then turned and offered me his hand, and I took it, which was its own strange newness. For eleven days we had been two people holding one plan between them like a rope over a gorge. This was different. His hand was warm and calloused from the climb and it asked nothing.

"The stairs are ahead," I said. "Then the antechamber. Then the First Door."

"Then daylight." He said it like a prayer he wasn't sure he was allowed.

"Then daylight."

The stairs were bad. They had been made of silence once—I remember them, the way sound died on them, the way my own footsteps arrived late and apologetic—and whatever the Choir had poured into them was draining away even as we climbed, so that the steps flickered between solid and suggestion. Bram solved it the way he solved most things now: he sang.

His voice came back mid-scream, three days ago, in the collapse of the great hall. That is a thing I will carry and he will carry and neither of us will say aloud for a long time, maybe ever: that his first sound in six years was terror, ripped out of him as the Maestro's world came apart. But since then the voice had been settling, finding its registers, remembering itself. On the stairs he sang one of the old valley hymns—the one about the shepherd counting his flock by starlight, the one every child in our valley learns before they learn their own name—and where his voice touched the wavering steps, they firmed. Not magic, or not only magic. Memory. The hymn remembered the mountain before the mountain was a kingdom, when it was just stone and weather, and the stone listened to the oldest voice it knew and agreed to hold.

I climbed behind him, hand on the wall, weeping without drama, the way you cry when there's no part of you left surprised by grief.

"What?" he asked when the hymn ended, not turning around.

"My mother used to sing that," I said. "She thought I didn't remember. I was four."

He waited. He was good at waiting; six years of silence will teach you that.

"She's really gone," I said. "Not dead. Gone. Up and away, like birds. And the last thing she—" My voice failed, and I stood on a half-real stair in a half-unmade mountain and let it fail. "The last thing she did here was forgive me. She brushed past me like a hand on the back of my neck, the way she used to when I couldn't sleep, and she went *up*, Bram, and I don't even know what she forgave me *for*. For surviving? For being the seed they planted? For taking twenty years longer than her to break the thing?"

"For nothing," Bram said quietly. "People don't need a reason to forgive. Sometimes forgiveness is just love with nowhere left to land."

I had no answer for that. I put my hand on the wall and climbed.

* * *

The First Door stood open when we reached it. Of course it did. It had been built to open to a sung note, and the entire mountain had been singing until three days ago, and doors do not understand that concerts end. It hung against the cliff face like a mouth that had forgotten how to close, and beyond it the air moved—real air, cold and pine-sharp, smelling of snow and distance.

I stopped just inside the threshold and let myself feel it. This was the door I had faked us through, weeks ago, bending my gift into a stolen pitch while the stone memorized my voice too well. This was where the trap had started closing, though I hadn't known it. Standing here without the gift was like returning to the scene of a crime committed by a stranger wearing your face.

"He knew," I said. "Before we ever came down here. The Maestro knew. He said *cartographer* like it was my name, like he'd been reading my maps my whole life." I wrapped my arms around myself. "Because he had, hadn't he. Every map I ever drew. Every city I charted fifty years early. It all came from the fragment they seeded in my bloodline. I was drawing his blueprints and calling it art."

Bram leaned against the doorframe and looked out at the light instead of at me, giving me the privacy of not being watched.

"Here's what I keep circling," I went on, because if I didn't say it I would fold up around it like wet paper. "I lost my gift. And I should be devastated, and part of me is, and part of me also feels—" I hunted for the word. "*Lighter.* Like I set down a pack I forgot I was carrying. Twenty-three years of hearing roads whisper. Twenty-three years of knowing things I shouldn't, charting places that didn't exist yet, feeling like a fraud and a freak and a miracle all at once. And it was never mine. It was a leash dressed up as a talent. And now it's gone, and the hunt can't find me, and the court can't claim me, and I'm just—" I laughed, raggedly. "I'm just a woman who's good at drawing."

Bram turned then. "Cyrus Vane," he said, and my name in his voice, his actual voice, still did something to the air, "you were never just anything."

* * *

We came out of the mountain in the last hour of afternoon, into a world so aggressively ordinary that I nearly sat down in the snow and laughed until they carried me away.

Pine forest. A slope of scree and crusty snow. Clouds the color of dishwater going pink at the seams, and somewhere below, invisible, a river running the way rivers run when nobody has sung them into it—badly, wonderfully, according to no design at all. Bram stood beside me breathing like a man surfacing from deep water, and I realized I was doing the same, gulping the thin cold air as if I'd been underwater for eleven days instead of underground.

"We need a fire before dark," I managed.

"We need food before a fire."

"We need both before either of us cries again, and I'm close, so move."

He moved. And that was the other thing I hadn't planned for, the thing that kept ambusing me in small moments: freedom is heavy. Everyone tells you grief is heavy, chains are heavy, but nobody warns you that liberty weighs something too—that walking out from under a story that owned you means carrying every choice yourself, unassisted, forever. My whole life had been shaped by the fragment in my blood: my hiding, my fleeing, my descent, even my sacrifice at the abyss's edge. All of it had been the Choir reaching for its final note. And now the reaching had stopped, and I stood on an anonymous mountainside with no destiny at all, and I confess the openness of it frightened me more than the dark had.

But then Bram found a windbreak among the rocks, and hummed while he gathered wood, and I thought: *no destiny at all.* And I thought: *good.*

* * *

The crown's hunt collapsed the way rotten timber collapses—all at once, and from the inside, and with a noise that everyone heard and no one could describe.

We learned it in pieces, over the following week, from a trapper who shared his smokehouse with us and from a mail rider whose horse had thrown a shoe and whose opinions were freely given. The witch-hunters' compasses, which had pointed hungrily at talent across three provinces, had begun spinning the night the mountain sang its last note—spinning and spinning, useless, and then cracking, all of them, in the same hour, as if the instrument and the thing it measured had been one mechanism. Without compasses the hunters were just armed men knocking on doors, and the provinces had grown tired of armed men. Warrants were withdrawn. Assessments cancelled. The magistrate in our valley—who had read the proclamation with such relish that autumn morning while Bram stood in the square watching me understand what I was—resigned his office by letter and left town before anyone could ask questions.

And deeper down, though the mail rider didn't know it and the trapper only half-believed it: the evidence was buried. Literally. The court beneath the mountain had been the engine of the whole machine—the contracts written on skin, the soul-ledgers, the debt-collectors' roads that I had once, guiltily, unknowingly, mapped into existence. All of it had been sustained by the Choir's song, and when the song ended, the paper rotted. Contracts crumbled in strongboxes across the kingdom, we heard later, ink fading like breath on glass. Debts that had been paid in voices simply ceased to be owed. There was nothing left to enforce them with, and no one left who remembered why.

Bram's family was free. He'd gone to them the second morning, walked into their farmhouse with his voice restored and his pockets full of nothing, and I had stayed outside by the gate because some reunions are not mine to witness. But I heard the sound that came out of that house—a woman's cry, and then laughter, and then Bram's voice joining it, unsteady and unmistakable—and I sat on a fence rail in the frost and cried the good kind, the kind I'm told exists.

His grandmother found me there afterward. She was tiny and iron-spined and she took my chin in her hand the way old women do, as if faces belonged to them.

"You gave him back," she said.

"He gave himself back. I just broke the cage."

"Mm." She studied me with eyes that had signed a contract on her grandson's skin and lived with it. "Same thing, from where the bird stands." Then she kissed my forehead and dragged me inside to eat until I begged for mercy, which is what that family does instead of saying thank you, and possibly instead of saying everything.

* * *

Winter deepened. We went home.

Not dramatically. There was no triumphant return down the valley road, no crowd, no reckoning. We came back the way water comes back to a channel: because it was ours, and it was downhill. The valley lay under its first true snow, the warding stones furred white, the fields asleep, and the valley that refuses magic refused it still—though I wondered sometimes whether it had ever been refusing magic at all, or merely refusing the Choir, keeping the fragment in my blood quiet the way a splint keeps a broken bone quiet, protecting me for exactly as long as protection was possible. Maybe the valley had known. Maybe my mother's people had built it. I would probably never know, and I was learning to let not-knowing sit in my chest without trying to map it.

I unpacked my old life like clothes from a trunk that had traveled without me. The floorboard where I hid my impossible work. The drafting table with its worn hollows. The rolls of parchment, blank, patient, suddenly just parchment—wood pulp and sizing, promising nothing.

I tried to draw an honest map, the ordinary kind. Our valley, from memory: the river's bend, the market square, the road out east where the crown riders had come. My hand knew the work. It was slow and careful and completely silent, and the map was accurate and dull and real, and when I finished it I pinned it to the wall and stood looking at it for a long time.

It was the first map I had ever made that would not come true. It was already true. It would stay exactly as true as the day I drew it, no more, no less.

I touched the corner of it, gently, the way you touch a sleeping animal.

"All right," I told it. "Just us, then."

Bram was rebuilding the choir. That sentence still stopped me sometimes mid-task, wherever I was, chopping wood or hauling water: *Bram is rebuilding the choir,* and meaning something entirely new by the word. He had found three of the freed voices wandering home through the foothills—a drover woman who'd been missing since before Bram was born, and twin brothers who did not speak of where they'd been but sang like men drinking after a drought. They practiced in the meeting-house with its windows cracked to the cold, and the sound that came out of it on practice nights was rough and cracked itself, imperfect, human, nothing at all like the terrible perfection beneath the mountain. It was the difference between bread and a jewel. One of them feeds you.

Some evenings I stood outside in the snow and listened, giftless, hearing only what my ears could hear, and it was enough. It was more than enough. It was, I was learning to say, *everything.*

* * *

On the last night of the year, I sat at my drafting table with a candle guttering and a blank page in front of me, not because I intended anything by it but because my hands wanted the company of paper, the way an old sailor wants the smell of tar.

Snow ticked at the window. Somewhere across the valley, faint, the meeting-house choir was ringing in the new year with the shepherd's hymn, Bram's voice rising through the middle of it, sure and warm and his.

I looked down at the blank page.

And the page looked back.

It was the faintest thing—fainter than breath on glass, fainter than memory. A line, gathering itself along the bottom edge of the parchment like frost forming on a pane. Then another line crossing it. The ghost of a coastline, perhaps, or a road. A contour that had no business existing, drawn by no hand, in no ink I owned, moving with the slow confidence of something that has all the time in the world and knows precisely where it is going.

I did not reach for the gift. There was no gift to reach for.

I picked up my pen, and waited to see what the country wanted of me.
