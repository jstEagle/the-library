## 22. Bram and the Choir

**Cyrus**

The valley had never looked like much on paper. That had always been its charm, Cyrus supposed—too narrow for a highway, too poor for a garrison, too ordinary for anyone's ambitions. A fold in the hills where the river slowed and the warding stones stood in their patient circle and nothing, ever, happened.

She drew it anyway. Ordinary maps were harder than they looked.

No living lines. No roads that bent where her eye wanted them to bend. Just the river's honest meander, the market square with its crooked well, the mill, the bridge, the twelve farms that ringed the valley floor like seats around a hearth. She sat at the long table by her south window—the same table where she'd once finished a city that would not exist for fifty years—and made herself render every roof at its true, unglamorous angle.

Her hand did not tremble anymore. It had taken months for that.

The scar across her palm had faded to a pale seam, the last mark of what she'd spent below the mountain. Some mornings she woke reaching for the gift the way an amputee reaches for a missing limb, fingers closing on cold air. Other mornings—more of them now—she woke and simply reached for tea.

Outside, someone was singing. Badly.

Cyrus smiled without looking up. That was Bram's doing, all of it. A year ago the valley had been as silent as any place the crown's reach could make it; hymns had been sung in whispers, if at all, because singing attracted attention and attention attracted hunters. Now you could hardly cross the square without tripping over a melody. The freed voices had come home like swallows, drifting back down out of the hills one and two at a time, hollow-eyed strangers who remembered how to walk before they remembered how to speak—and Bram had stood in the market with his hands open and his throat full of his returned voice, saying, *Come. We'll teach each other.*

The choir practiced in the old tithe barn on Thursdays. It was less a choir than a congregation of the rescued: the cooper's daughter who sang only in thirds, harmonizing against herself; a grey-bearded man who could manage just four notes before weeping; a woman in her sixties who had come down from the high passes with no name at all and let the valley give her one. Bram conducted them with gestures borrowed partly from his grandmother and partly invented whole, because no one alive had ever taught a choir made of stolen things how to sing together again.

He was good at it. That surprised no one more than him.

Cyrus shaded the mill wheel, checked it against her memory, and decided the map was done. She blew the dust from the ink, set down her pen, and sat for a moment in the particular emptiness that followed finishing anything—a small grief, a small room.

That was when she noticed the blank page.

It lay at the corner of her desk, half beneath a weight-stone. She had cut it that morning from the same stock as all the others, plain and empty, waiting for tomorrow's work. Except it wasn't empty now. Faintly, in lines thin as spider silk, something was surfacing through the fibers—roads? Rivers? A coastline she did not know, curving off the edge of the page into nowhere?

Cyrus did not move. Her heartbeat counted five slow strokes.

Then the lines faded, sinking back into the paper like fish into deep water, and the page was blank again, and the only sound was the wind working at the shutters and, distantly, the choir beginning Thursday's warm-up scales.

*Ordinary maps,* she told her own hands. *Only ordinary maps, forever.*

But her hands, resting flat on the desk, were warm. And somewhere under her skin, in the place where the gift had lived and burned and finally poured itself out into a single ending note, something faint and patient had begun—very quietly—to hum.

**Bram**

Thursday evenings, the tithe barn smelled of hay dust and lamp oil and forty kinds of fear slowly learning to be something else.

Bram stood on the meal crates he'd nailed together into a podium, hands raised, and waited. He had learned patience in the court beneath the mountain, where he'd had nothing else to spend, and he spent it now freely. The choir settled by degrees: the cooper's daughter first, always, then the grey-bearded man (his name was Odo; he'd found it carved inside his own belt buckle three months after coming home), then the rest, shuffling onto the plank benches like the pews of a church that had never existed.

His grandmother sat in the front row, as she always did, though she couldn't carry a tune in a bucket and said so nightly. She was there to listen to him. She'd spent a year of debt-money buying back his voice and another year pretending she hadn't heard it yet, and now she listened to everything he sang the way other people prayed.

"From the top," Bram said. His voice came out whole and low and his, and still, after all these months, saying simple sentences felt like spending coins he'd thought were gone forever.

They ran the valley hymn first—the old one, the safe one, the one every child learned before children had stopped learning it. Forty voices took it up raggedly, and Bram shaped them with his hands, pulling the altos forward, gentling Odo when the fourth note came and his face began to crumple. The hymn rose into the rafters and hung there among the hay dust, and Bram felt the thing he had never had words for even when he'd had words: the sound of broken people making one whole, temporary thing together.

Afterward they stayed, the way they always stayed, drinking his grandmother's cider and telling the stories they had all told too many times, because telling them was the point. The woman from the high passes—Maret, the valley had named her—described the moment the Choir broke. Everyone did, eventually. It was their scripture.

"I was a bird," Maret said, cider-stained and certain. "I don't mean I dreamed it. I was a bird, or I was in a bird, or the bird was what was left of me. And the sky opened, and there was a note underneath everything, one note, like the world's spine, and it snapped."

"It was a girl," said the cooper's daughter quietly. "I heard it too. It sounded like a girl deciding."

Bram kept his face still. Only Cyrus, who wasn't there but who had heard this story forty times through his retellings, would have known what the stillness cost him.

Because it hadn't sounded like a girl deciding, not to him. To him it had sounded like Cyrus. He had been standing beside her above the abyss when she poured out everything she was, and the sound had gone through his bones, and he had known—even before the halls unraveled, even before the Maestro's courteous voice tore apart mid-syllable—that whatever came next, she would come out of it smaller. They both had. The mountain gave her back anyway, most of her: her dryness, her stubbornness, her terrible handwriting. But he had watched her climb toward daylight afterward with her eyes closed, following his voice because hers couldn't find the road anymore, and some part of him had never entirely stopped climbing beside her, listening for what was missing.

He said goodnight to the choir, banked the lamps, walked her grandmother home, and then did what he did every Thursday night: he crossed the dark lanes to Cyrus's house and knocked twice, because she refused to leave a door unlocked in a valley with no crime, on principle.

She answered with ink on her fingers and an expression he had learned to read at ten paces.

"You've seen something," he said.

"I haven't seen anything." She stepped back to let him in. "I've possibly seen a page do something a page shouldn't."

* *

**Cyrus**

She showed him the paper. It sat between them on the table, lamplit and innocent, blank as fresh snow.

"Perhaps I imagined it," Cyrus said.

"Did you?"

She hated that he asked plainly. He had earned plainness, though—he'd spent a year mute, watching everyone lie beautifully around him, and he had no patience left for softening.

"No," she admitted. "There was a coastline. Roads. It moved." She turned her palm up on the table, the pale seam catching the light. "I spent it, Bram. All of it. I felt it go. There is no more gift in me than in this loaf of bread." She tapped the bread in question. "So either I'm wrong about what I spent, or something has decided to give it back, and I want very much to know which, because the last time my gift charted something without my permission, it led us under a mountain."

Bram picked up the page. Held it to the lamp, angled it, tilted it the way you'd tilt a mirror to catch a face. His lips moved slightly—still, sometimes, when he was thinking hard, mouthing thoughts his year of silence had taught to travel without sound.

"It's not the same paper," he said.

"What?"

He laid it flat and pointed. "Your stock. You tear your sheets with the short grain running this way—" he mimed the tear, "—because you're left-handed and it's easier. This edge is torn the other way. And it's thinner. Feel it."

She felt it. He was right. She had been so rattled by the lines that she hadn't examined the page itself, and now, under her fingertips, it was unmistakable: not one of hers. Not paper from her stock, her desk, her house. Someone—or something—had put a blank page on her desk that knew how to draw.

The fire popped. Neither of them moved.

"Could the Maestro—" Bram started.

"The Maestro is stone and silence," Cyrus said. "We watched the whole song end. Every voice went free. I'd know if a fragment of that thing survived; it would sing, and I'd—" She stopped. She almost said *I'd feel it*, and realized, pressing her hand flat against her sternum, that she couldn't swear to what she felt anymore.

Bram pulled the second chair around and sat close, the way he'd sat close in guest chambers beneath the mountain, tapping hymn-rhythms against the wall while they planned a betrayal. Without quite deciding to, his fingers began tapping now against the tabletop—the old valley hymn, the safe one, the one they'd sung to keep each other brave in the dark. It was his way of asking: *are we still us?*

Cyrus answered the rhythm with her own hand, offbeat, imperfect, theirs.

"All right," she said. "Hypotheses. One: the gift isn't gone, only sleeping, and it's dreaming on my desk. Two: the gift is gone, and something else is leaving me maps. Three: the world has more courts beneath it than one, and word travels."

"And four," Bram said quietly.

"Don't say four."

"Four: your mother's out there somewhere, and she was a cartographer too."

Cyrus looked at the blank page for a long time. Her mother's presence had brushed her once in the breaking of the Choir—forgiveness, not farewell—and departed upward like a migrating bird, and Cyrus had built a whole quiet grave for that moment in her heart and visited it faithfully. She had not allowed herself to ask whether forgiveness meant *goodbye* or simply *go well.* She had especially not allowed herself to remember that her mother's body had never been found, only her voice taken, and that a freed soul must land *somewhere.*

"We could burn it," Bram offered.

"We could."

Neither of them reached for the fire.

Outside, far across the valley, the tithe barn stood dark and empty, but the night itself seemed to hold the hymn the way walls hold echoes—forty imperfect voices, stitched into one whole, temporary thing. The warding stones stood in their circle, keeping magic out, or keeping it in, or merely standing, depending on which stories you believed. Cyrus had believed, once, that the valley was a sanctuary. Then she had believed it was a cage. Now she suspected it was neither: just a place, ordinary and dear, with a door in it like every other place.

The page lay between them, blank and waiting.

Very slowly, the lines began to surface again—fainter than before, shy, like a deer at a treeline. A coastline. A river bending toward it. And at the river's edge, so small she might have invented it, a mark she recognized from her mother's surviving sketchbooks: the little sign her mother had used for *here be somewhere new.*

Cyrus reached out and did not erase it.

"Well," she said, and her voice was steadier than she had any right to expect. "It seems I'll need better paper."

Bram laughed—one short, bright note, his whole returned voice in it—and pulled the lamp closer, and the two of them leaned over the desk together into the small warm circle of light while the map drew itself, patiently, line by line, out of the dark.
