## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

There are three rules to keeping a ledger of impossible things, and I invented all of them, because no one else had bothered.

Rule the first: never write *impossible* in the margin. The word attracts attention, and attention is the one thing this guild cannot afford. Rule the second: date every entry by the moon's phase rather than the calendar, because calendars can be seized and moons cannot. Rule the third — and this is the one I am proudest of — leave one line blank after each entry, in case the impossible thing turns out to be larger than first reported. Impossible things are like cats that way. You think you've measured them, and then they expand into a space you didn't know existed.

I had been ledger-keeper for six years, which made me the youngest keeper in the guild's recorded history, a distinction I held mostly because no one else wanted the job and because I had once been foolish enough to have neat handwriting where an elder could see it.

That evening — a Tuesday, if Tuesdays still meant anything down here, where the only sky was the underside of the city's oldest bridge — I was cataloguing the week's acquisitions by lamplight, and the week had been a strange one even by our standards.

*Item forty-one,* I wrote. *One bottled echo, source unknown. Purchased from a fiddler who claimed he caught it in a stairwell on the night his brother stopped speaking to him. When uncorked, the echo says nothing. It simply waits, as though listening for the rest of the sentence.*

The echo sat on my desk in its green glass bottle, faintly warm, faintly embarrassed. I had tested it twice. Both times it had declined to perform, which I respected. Some things only echo when they believe no one is keeping score.

*Item forty-two.* This one required more care. *One debt, owed by the moon, to a washerwoman of Copperlane whose name was not recorded at the time of the loan. Nature of the loan: unclear. Collateral: unclear. The moon has not paid in four hundred years, and interest is, by any honest arithmetic, now ruinous.*

The debt itself was written on a scrap of vellum so old it had gone soft as cloth. It had come to us through three intermediaries, two of whom were dead and one of whom insisted he'd never touched it, which is how you know someone has touched it. I entered it carefully, because debts owed by celestial bodies are the guild's most dangerous inventory. Not dangerous the way knives are dangerous. Dangerous the way promises are dangerous — quietly, over years, until one day the whole shape of your life turns out to have been built on top of one.

I blotted the page. I left the blank line. I looked at the blank line longer than a competent person should.

Six years, and the ledger had eleven hundred entries in my hand alone. Eleven hundred impossible things, weighed, dated, shelved, protected. And not one of them, I sometimes thought in the small hours, was me.

Which was ridiculous. I want to be clear about that. I knew it was ridiculous even as I thought it. A person is not an item. A person does not go in a ledger. But there is a particular loneliness in being the one who writes everything down and is written down nowhere — who knows the guild's whole shape because she records it, and is known by no one because she records it. The elders trusted me with their secrets the way you trust a lock: entirely, impersonally, without ever once wondering whether the lock gets tired.

Above me — far above, past the false cellar and the bricked archway and the wine merchant who genuinely did not know what was beneath his shop — the city was closing up for the night. I could feel it the way you feel weather in a scar. Bells, cartwheels, the last arguments of shopkeepers. Then the long hush, and under the hush, the other sound, the one no one above ever noticed and everyone below pretended not to hear: the cold coming down out of the north like something with intent.

It had been coming down for nine years now. The elders called it the Long Winter and spoke of it the way you speak of a rich uncle's slow decline — regretfully, distantly, with no intention of doing anything that might cost anything. Down here we were insulated, or told ourselves we were. The guild's whole art was insulation. Doors that opened onto places doors shouldn't open onto. Trades conducted in the gaps between moments. An entire institution built on the principle that the safest thing an impossible thing can do is remain unobserved.

We had gotten very good at remaining unobserved. So good, lately, that I sometimes wondered whether we still existed at all, or had merely become another entry in some vaster ledger, filed under *forgotten*, with a blank line after us in case we turned out to be larger than first reported.

The lamp guttered. Somewhere in the deep shelves, the bottled echo shifted in its sleep.

* * *

The elders convened at the ninth hour, which was traditional, impractical, and — I suspected — chosen specifically because by the ninth hour even the most stubborn elder wanted the meeting over quickly.

Their hall was a low vaulted room at the heart of the undercity, lit by chandeliers of candle-stubs salvaged from churches that had burned down before either of us was born. There were seven of them, though on most nights you'd have sworn there were five, because Elder Corvane and Elder Maudle had developed the habit of falling asleep mid-session with their eyes open, a skill I intended to acquire before I died.

I sat at the side table with the ledger open, recording proceedings. This was my official function. My unofficial function was to be furniture that could write.

"The rolls," Elder Vess was saying. She was the eldest, a small fierce woman shaped like a question mark, with a voice that could strip paint. "Read the rolls."

I read the rolls. "Active members, thirty-one. Inactive but living, sixty-four. Deceased this year, nine." I paused. "Joined this year, none."

Silence, of the kind that has weight. Nine deaths and no births. Any merchant in the city above could have told them what that arithmetic meant. Merchants, however, were precisely what we were not supposed to be, and so the elders looked at the numbers the way people look at a diagnosis they intend to second-guess.

"Recruitment," said Elder Fenwick, who believed every problem was a recruitment problem, possibly including death.

"From whom?" said Elder Vess. "The city above doesn't see us. That's the point of us. We can't recruit people who don't know we exist."

"Then we tell them we exist."

"And within a season," said Elder Ossory, dry as old paper, "we exist in a magistrate's report instead. Serath Vane's office has been asking questions about irregular commerce for two years. You want to hand him our address?"

At the name *Vane*, something moved through the room — not quite a flinch. More like the way a house settles. I wrote the name down without thinking about it, and then thought about it, and then decided not to think about it, which is a skill, and mine needed work.

Elder Maudle woke up just enough to contribute. "In my day," he began, and stopped, because even Maudle knew that phrase had become the guild's true motto, carved over no door, sworn by no one, obeyed absolutely.

"In your day," Elder Vess said, not unkindly, "there were three hundred of us and a realm below worth trading with. Now the roads between are frozen and the ones who remember how to walk them are dying faster than we can embalm them." She turned her sharp little face toward the ledger, toward me, though not quite toward *me* — elders rarely managed the final inch of that journey. "What do the acquisition figures say, keeper?"

"Acquisitions are steady," I said. "Quality is declining. Fewer true impossibilities, more near-misses. Bottled echoes. Minor curses. One debt owed by the moon." I hesitated, because the next part was the part that mattered, and mattering made my voice do things I didn't authorize. "But trades require two parties. Our counterparties below the ice are fewer every year. The last genuine crossing came through eleven months ago. Before that, fourteen months. The intervals are lengthening. Whatever the winter is doing down there—" I stopped myself. *Down there* was not a subject. *Down there* was a rule violation. "—it is doing it to our trade routes as well."

"Then we wait," said Ossory. "Winters end."

"This one hasn't," I said, "in nine years."

Ossory looked at me with mild surprise, the look of a man noticing that the lock has developed opinions. "Keeper. Your function is the record. The record does not require forecasts."

"No," I agreed, and wrote down my own words in the minutes, verbatim, where they would sit forever in my neat, obedient handwriting: *the record does not require forecasts.* If I could not be heard, I could at least be archived. It is a smaller revenge than it sounds. It is also, some nights, the only kind on offer.

The debate went on for another hour and concluded, as our debates always concluded, with a decision to form a subcommittee, which is the guild's formal term for *nothing*. I closed the ledger. The seven elders rose, became five, became shadows on stairs, became gone.

I sat alone in the vaulted hall a while, with the candle-stubs burning down around me, and let myself feel the thing I kept so competently filed away during daylight hours: that I had given this place six years of perfect service, and this place had given me a desk.

Not a mentor. Elders didn't take apprentices anymore; apprentices grew up to be members, and members were liabilities. Not friends — the few keepers my age had transferred to the surface offices years ago, where at least you got to see weather. Just a desk, and a ledger, and eleven hundred impossible things that would outlast my tenure and never learn my name.

I blew out the lamps. In the dark, the bottled echo sighed — or I imagined it sighed — and I carried both of us back to my cell along the shelf-lined corridors, past the Door That Opens Inward (do not ask), past the cabinet of keys to houses that have not been built yet, past the framed first rule of the guild, inked by some founder whose name even the ledger had lost:

*WHAT IS HIDDEN IS SAFE.*

I had walked past that rule six thousand times. That night, for the first time, I noticed it didn't actually say safe from *what*.

* * *

I was awakened — if the shallow drifting I did could be called sleep — by the crow.

It had been on my windowsill for three nights running. This was unusual. Nothing came to my window. My window opened onto a ventilation shaft that opened onto a chimney that opened onto a street where nothing interesting had happened since the plague. Crows have better prospects. And yet there it sat, black against the grey dark, feathers ruffled up against a cold that was, I noticed with a small cold feeling of my own, noticeably colder than it had been at supper.

"Shoo," I whispered, because shouting in the undercity invites answers.

The crow looked at me. I want to be precise about this, because it matters later, though I didn't know it then: it didn't look away, or tilt its head the way birds do when they're performing birdness. It looked at me the way a person looks at you across a crowded room when they've mistaken you for someone they loved.

"Go home," I told it. "Wherever that is."

It made a sound. Not a caw. Something structured. Two notes and a pause, like the start of a word it couldn't finish, and then it pressed its beak briefly against the glass — *tap, tap* — soft, deliberate, almost polite, and stared at me with one eye the color of wet slate.

I should have gone back to bed. I was, by every measure available to me, a sensible woman. Sensible women do not hold conversations with crows through ventilation shafts.

But the cold was pressing at the glass, and somewhere far above, muffled by brick and earth and the whole careful architecture of our hiding, I heard the first snow of the year begin to fall on the city — early, too early, weeks too early — and the crow tapped again, and its eye was not a bird's eye, and I thought: *eleven months since the last crossing, and the intervals are lengthening, and something is coming down the trade routes ahead of schedule.*

I dressed. I took the ledger. I told myself I was going to check the threshold wards, which was even technically true, in the sense that a drowning man is technically going for a swim.

The guild's front door — our one door to the world above, hidden behind the wine merchant's deepest cellar — stood at the end of the Entry Hall, and it was already standing open when I got there.

Open one inch. Cold pouring through the gap like water finding a crack. Snowflakes, impossibly, impossibly alive, drifting down our own stairwell, melting on stones that hadn't felt weather in two hundred years.

And on the threshold, half in and half out, folded up like a letter no one wanted to finish reading, was a man.

Frost in his hair. Frost on his lashes. His lips grey-blue, his hands bare and blackened at the fingertips, and clutched against his chest with both arms, the way you clutch the one thing you were trusted with, the way you clutch it harder as you die because dying is the moment you can least afford to drop it — a roll of paper, stained dark at one corner with blood that had frozen mid-drip.

He was trying to speak. I knelt, and the cold came up through my knees, and I heard him spend his last warmth on a single word, hoarse as a rusted hinge, repeated twice — once for me, and once, I think, for himself, to prove he'd earned the right to say it:

"Bram."

His eyes stayed open. The snow kept falling inward. Behind me, down the corridor, I heard the bottled echo wake in its green glass and say, very softly, the ending of a sentence begun four hundred years ago.

I pried the map from his fingers. It came away easily, the way things do when the person holding them has finally stopped needing them.

And I stood there on the threshold of the only home I had, holding an impossible thing that had arrived without paperwork, without provenance, without a single elder awake to tell me what to do with it — and for the first time in six years, Rosalind Almeida, youngest ledger-keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things, understood that whatever happened next, no one was going to record it but me.
