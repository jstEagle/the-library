## 2. A Stranger at the Threshold

The first snow of the year came down through the grate above Copperlane like ash from a polite fire, and I remember thinking, as I inked the evening's entries, that it was the sort of night when impossible things come looking for shelter.

I should know. I keep their books.

The Guild of Impossible Things has no address. It has a threshold—a door that exists only to people who have lost something they cannot name—and that door had opened perhaps four times in my eleven years as ledger-keeper, and three of those were cats. The fourth was a debt-collector named Odo, who is another story and not mine to tell yet.

I was in the counting room beneath Copperlane, where the ceiling sweats candlelight and the shelves lean together like old conspirators. My desk sits under the vent that carries the smell of the city down to us: coal smoke, bread, wet wool, the iron tang of the river. I have catalogued eleven years of those smells without ever walking among them for more than an hour at a time. That is the bargain of my office. The ledger-keeper sees everything and touches nothing, because a record must be kept by someone the world cannot take hostage.

Or so Elder Corvane says, every time I ask about the sky.

That evening's entries were ordinary by our standards. A bottled echo, traded twice and now leaking faintly—it hummed *Amaryllis* whenever anyone walked past its shelf, some dead woman's name, and I had begun to think of it as ours. A promissory note against next spring, signed by a farmer who'd sworn his orchard would bloom out of season if the guild cured his son's silence. And one item I still can't classify: a debt owed by the moon, transferred three owners ago and currently gathering interest nobody knows how to collect. I wrote *status: dormant* beside it and felt, as always, the small dishonesty of that word.

The elders were arguing two floors up, in the hall with the seven chairs. I could hear them through the floor, which is how I hear most things—Corvane's dry rasp, Mother Quill's bell-clear certainty, old Fenwick wheezing agreement with whichever side was winning. The argument was the same one they'd been having since I was apprenticed: numbers. Ours dwindling. Nine members who remembered the guild existed, three apprentices, one ledger-keeper. "We are a secret no one wants," Corvane said, and the floor carried it down to me like a stone dropped through water.

*A secret no one wants.* I wrote it in the margin of my private notebook, where I keep the things elders say that they would deny saying. The margin is getting crowded.

I want to be fair to them. They are not cruel; they are careful, and care taken long enough hardens into something that looks exactly like cruelty from underneath. I was six when they took me in off a winter step not so different from the one I'm about to describe, and everything I am—the neat hand, the triple-checked arithmetic, the way I can hold a number in my head for days without dropping it—is theirs. I owe them a self. I've simply started wondering lately whether a self is all I get.

So: snow, candlelight, the bottled echo humming its dead woman's name, and me writing *dormant* in a book of miracles. If you want to know the exact moment my life broke open, it wasn't dramatic. It was a knock.

We don't get knocks. We get arrivals—people simply *find* themselves standing before the threshold, blinking, having walked past it forty times without seeing. A knock means someone came deliberately. A knock means someone knew.

Three strikes, spaced evenly, soft as knuckles on a coffin lid.

I sat very still. Upstairs the argument went on. The knock came again, weaker, and something in its weakness decided me—I took the lamp and went up the spiral stair, telling myself I was merely answering the door as any junior member might, as I had been trained to do, as procedure required. Procedure, I have learned, is the word obedient people use instead of *want*.

The threshold stood at the top of the stair, set into the brick behind a false wall of shelving. From the street above it looks like a coal chute. From inside, it is oak, black with age, carved with the guild's motto, which translates roughly as *What Is Kept Cannot Be Lost*—though Fenwick swears the older translation is *What Is Lost Comes Here*, and I privately prefer his version tonight of all nights.

I opened it onto the cold, and the cold came in wearing a man.

He was tall, or had been; frost and hardship had bent him into a question mark. Snow crusted his shoulders and eyelashes, and his lips had gone the grey-violet of old bruises. His coat had been fine once—good wool, silver buttons, the kind of tailoring that announces a man used to being listened to—but it was scorched down one side, burned and torn as though he'd been dragged through a fire backwards. He looked at me with eyes that were doing their last arithmetic, and what he saw there made him clutch at the doorframe harder.

"You're real," he said. His voice was wrecked, gravel over ice. "The stories said—under Copperlane—the ones who keep what shouldn't exist. You're real."

"We're closed," I said, because apparently my training extends to absurdity.

He laughed. It turned into coughing halfway through, a wet, tearing sound, and he sagged forward and I caught him—or caught enough of him to steer his fall into the entryway rather than the street, which is the most consequential thing I have ever done with my arms. He was heavy and cold as a cellar. Frost had gotten into his beard, into the seams of his gloves, everywhere but his right hand, which he kept fisted tight against his chest as though holding something alive in there.

"Listen." He gripped my wrist with the left hand. His fingers were freezing but his grip was furious. "They'll tell you to burn it. Listen to me—they will *tell you to burn it*—" Another coughing fit folded him nearly double. When he straightened, there was blood on his lip, shockingly red against all that grey. "The map. Take the map."

"I don't know what map—"

His right hand finally opened.

It was pressed flat against his ribs like a second heart, and when he drew it away there was blood on the palm and a folded square of paper, stained through in the shape of a handprint. He pushed it against my chest with the last of his strength—not handed, *pressed*, the way you'd give someone a coal to carry across a frozen river. The paper was cold and damp through my apron and I felt its weight entirely out of proportion to its size, the way you feel a key.

"Five," he whispered. "There are five. He only thinks he needs—no. No, listen. Tell them Bram sent—"

And then he said his own name, which struck me even then as strange, a man introducing himself to the dark: "*Bram.* Remember the name. Someone has to remember the—"

The sentence never landed. He slid down the doorframe, and I went down with him, and the last thing he did in this world or any other was look at the ceiling of our entryway—at the carved motto, *What Is Lost Comes Here*—and smile. Not bitterly. With relief. As though he'd been carrying that smile a very long way and could finally put it down.

Then the relief went out of him the way heat leaves a room, and he was just a stranger dead on our doorstep, and I was kneeling in the first snowfall of the year holding a bloodstained map I hadn't asked for, listening to my own heartbeat and the far-off murmur of an argument about dwindling numbers.

* * *

They came quickly once I called. Elders descend fast when death arrives uninvited; it's the living who make them hesitate.

Corvane examined the body with the expression of a man auditing an invoice. Mother Quill knelt and closed the stranger's eyes with genuine gentleness—I will say that for her, always—and Fenwick stood back wringing his hands and saying "a magistrate's coat, that's a magistrate's coat" until Corvane told him to be quiet.

"Bram," Mother Quill said softly, testing the name. "I don't know it."

"Neither do I," said Corvane, "and that is precisely the problem." He turned to me. "Rosalind. What did he give you?"

I had, by then, tucked the map inside my apron. I did this without deciding to do it, which should have told me something about myself. "His hand," I said. "He held my wrist. And then he died."

"That isn't what I asked."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

You could have heard the silence settle. Corvane is not a man accustomed to being answered sideways; he invented most of the ways. His eyes, pale as frost on glass, moved from my face to my apron and back with terrible patience.

"Child. Whatever he passed to you, it came to our threshold, and what crosses our threshold belongs to the guild. You know the law."

I did know the law. I had helped transcribe it. Article Four: nothing enters the guild unrecorded, and nothing recorded remains ungoverned. I produced the map because refusing outright was beyond me—obedience doesn't crack all at once, it cracks the way ice does, hairline by hairline, and I was only at the first hairline—and watched Corvane unfold it under the lamp.

It was beautiful. There is no other word. Drawn on paper thin as onion skin, in ink that shifted color as it caught the light—brown to blue to a green like deep water—a map of two countries overlaid. Our city, unmistakable, the river bending like a drawn bow. But threaded through its streets ran the lines of another city, a mirror-city, its avenues crossing ours at impossible angles, its districts named in an alphabet I didn't know and somehow almost read. At the center, where our Copperlane would sit, the cartographer had drawn a crown. Small, precise, rendered in five separate strokes, each stroke numbered.

One through five. Five marks. Five shards, though I wouldn't learn that word for weeks.

Blood had soaked the corner where the other country's edge began, obliterating whatever lay beyond it.

"A map of here," Fenwick breathed, leaning in. "And of—" He stopped himself, the way we all stop ourselves from saying the word below. *Underneath. The other realm. The place the guild pretends, in its official histories, not to trade with anymore.*

"Where did he come from?" Mother Quill asked me.

"He fell out of the snow," I said. "Elder. Look at him—he's been burning and freezing at the same time. Someone chased him across half of both worlds to take this from him, and he chose us to die in front of. Doesn't that count for anything?"

"Counting," said Corvane, "is your department, Rosalind. Mine is survival." He folded the map along its existing creases, gently, almost reverently, which somehow made it worse. "This mark"—his finger hovered over the crown—"I've seen its like before, in ledgers older than mine. Nothing good has ever been written near it. And now a dying man has carried it to our door, followed by whoever killed him. Do you understand what arrives with a thing like this? Questions. Searchers. Attention. We have survived three centuries by being nothing to everyone. This—" he lifted the map "—is the opposite of nothing."

"The man is dead," Mother Quill said quietly. "Whatever he wanted, he can't be argued with. But the map can be refused. Burn it, Corvane. Burn it tonight, before whatever hunted him learns where he stopped."

"And record it," Corvane added, turning to me, "in the ledger of destructions. Full entry. Provenance unknown; item received from a deceased stranger; destroyed by order of council to preserve the guild."

He held the map out to me.

I understood, in that moment, that he was testing me, or teaching me, or both—that handing the fire to the youngest keeper was a lesson dressed as a courtesy. *This is the work,* the gesture said. *This is what keeping costs.*

I took it. My hands were steady. I note that with shame rather than pride.

* * *

Here is what I did instead, and I have rehearsed confessing this so many times that the truth comes out clean now:

I burned a copy.

While the elders argued upstairs about burial—Fenwick wanting the river, Mother Quill insisting on consecrated ground, Corvane caring only that the grave be unmarked—I sat at my desk with the map spread beneath my lamp, and I copied it. Every line. Both cities. The shifting-color ink I couldn't replicate, so I used two pens, working wet-on-wet, and got a passable shimmer. The alphabet I copied stroke by stroke like a child tracing letters in a language she doesn't speak, five times over, until my hand knew shapes my mind didn't. The crown with its five numbered strokes. The burn-scarred corner, which I left blank and honest.

It took me until the third bell past midnight. My candle guttered twice. The bottled echo hummed *Amaryllis* from its shelf, and I found myself thinking of the dead man's face when he saw the motto—*what is lost comes here*—and I thought: he wasn't lost. He came home. And we're going to erase him.

Because that was the final order, the one that arrived with dawn. Not just the map—the man. Corvane stood in the hall with the ledger of destructions open and dictated: all record of the stranger to be expunged. No name in the registers. No cause of death entered. The grave unmarked, the arrival unwritten. "A man who dies at our door becomes our liability," he said. "A man who never existed cannot haunt us."

"But he existed," I heard myself say. "Someone has to remember the name. He said so. Someone has to remember."

"Then let it be someone else," Corvane said, not unkindly, which is the crueler thing. "That is what it costs to keep secrets, Rosalind. Someone must forget on purpose. You're better at it than you think."

I burned the original in the brazier while he watched, and I recorded the destruction in a fair hand, and I signed it, and my obedience held—outwardly, entirely, flawlessly. The elders went to bed satisfied. The guild remained a secret no one wanted.

But a copy is not a lie if no one asks about it, and no one ever asks the ledger-keeper what she keeps in her margins.

I locked the false page inside my private notebook, behind the entry for the moon's unpaid debt, and I blew out my lamp, and I lay in my narrow bed beneath the vent that smells of coal smoke and bread and the river, and I listened to the snow finish falling over a city that did not know I existed.

*Five*, the dead man had said. *There are five.*

And on my windowsill, where no window opens onto anything but brick, something small and black and patient had settled out of the storm—a crow, feathers ruffled, head cocked, watching me through the glass with one eye like a drop of midnight.

It did not leave when I pinched the candle out.

It was still there when I woke.
