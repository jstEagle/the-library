## 3. What the Elders Burned

The Guild of Impossible Things had a procedure for everything, which was ironic for an organization whose entire purpose was to make procedures unnecessary. There was a form for acquiring a debt owed by the moon (Form 12-C, *Lunar Indebtedness, Recurring*), a form for bottling echoes (Form 7-A, *Acoustic Impossibilities, Contained*), and a form for what happened when a stranger died on your doorstep carrying a map that could unmake the world.

That last one was Form 0. It had never been used before. It consisted of a single line: *Burn everything.*

I know because I was the one who fetched it from the archive, and I read it three times in the stairwell hoping it would say something else. It did not. Forms rarely do.

The vote was called the morning after the stranger died—Bram, though only I and the snow had heard him say it—and it was held in the Long Hall beneath the city, where the guild had met for four hundred years without once being noticed by anyone who mattered. The elders sat in their crescent of high-backed chairs like a jury that had already decided. Elder Corvane presided, as she always presided, with the air of a woman who considered consensus a personal favor she granted to the room.

"The matter before us," she said, "is contamination."

I stood at my ledger-stand at the edge of the hall, quill poised over the record book, because that was my place. Youngest ledger-keeper. Keeper of the record of things that were about to be ordered not to exist.

"An unknown man," Corvane continued, "carrying documents of unknown provenance, died at our threshold. He spoke to one of our members." Her eyes found me, briefly, the way a lighthouse finds a rowboat—not out of concern, but to mark its position. "He carried a map. The map has been secured. The question is what else must be secured."

"Secured," said Elder Fenwick, who was ninety if he was a day and had spent most of those days perfecting the art of saying alarming things gently, "is a generous word for what we are proposing."

"We are proposing nothing," said Corvane. "We are voting on whether the guild's existence remains secret, or whether it ends this winter in a magistrate's report."

Silence, of the kind that has weight. In the Long Hall, silence always had weight; the stones down there seemed to collect it, the way gutters collect rain.

I want to be fair to them. I have had four hundred years' worth of reasons since to understand why they voted as they did, and I will tell you now, since no one asked me then: they were old, and tired, and the guild had survived precisely because it never once chose bravery over continuity. Invisibility is a discipline. You practice it the way monks practice silence, until the day comes when someone bleeds on your doorstep and the discipline says *sweep it away*, and you sweep.

But I am getting ahead of myself. That morning I still believed the elders knew something I didn't. That is the great advantage of being young in an old institution: you assume the walls are load-bearing.

"The record," said Elder Maren, "should reflect that we acted with regret."

I wrote that down. *Acted with regret.* My hand was steady. I remember being proud of how steady it was, and I remember understanding, even as I wrote it, that pride and shame can occupy the same knuckle at the same time.

"All records," said Corvane. "Ledger entries. Correspondence. The page copied from the recovered document."

And there it was. My page.

I should explain about the copy. When the stranger's map came into our keeping, sealed in an evidence box by order of the elders, I did what any ledger-keeper does with a document of consequence: I made a faithful transcription for the archive. This was not disobedience. It was literally my job. The guild's whole architecture rested on the principle that anything worth hiding was worth recording first—*what cannot be unkept must at least be well kept*, as the founder wrote, in the only sentence of his anyone quoted anymore.

So I had copied the map. Every river-bend, every marginal notation, every smudge of blood where Bram's fingers had gripped it in his last hour. I had done it neatly, in archival ink, on archival paper, and filed it under *Documents Pending Classification*, because I am thorough, and thoroughness, it turns out, is just obedience with better posture.

"The copy," Corvane repeated, looking directly at me now, "will be surrendered for destruction along with all other traces."

"Yes, Elder," I said.

Here is what I did not say: *I memorized it.*

Not entirely on purpose. That's the truth, and it matters to me that it's the truth. When you transcribe a document line by line—when your whole craft is making your hand obey your eye—you cannot help but build the thing inside yourself. The map lived behind my eyes now, complete, indelible, in the same room of my mind where I kept the guild's founding charter and the recipe for preserving bottled moonlight. I had not decided to keep it. Keeping was simply what I was *for*. No one had ever thought to include a ledger-keeper in the category of *traces*.

I did think about telling them. I want that recorded too, in whatever invisible ledger tracks the moral bookkeeping of people who spend their lives keeping other ledgers. Standing there with my quill wet, I thought: *say it, let them strike you from the rolls, let them burn you out of the record too.* And then I thought of the hall above—the real hall, the little one where I ate supper alone among shelves—and I thought of what it would mean to be erased while still breathing, and my courage, which had arrived so promptly, quietly took its coat and left.

Some knowledge cannot be unkept. I learned that sentence that morning, standing in the Long Hall, watching nine old people decide that a dead man should never have existed. I learned it the way you learn the weight of something by carrying it home.

* * *

The burning happened at dusk, in the courtyard of cinders behind the guildhall, because the guild understood theatre even when it pretended otherwise. All eleven of us attended—all the guild numbered, in those days, eleven souls, counting me and counting Wren, who was fourteen and mostly kept company with locks. Eleven. We had been forty once, according to the older ledgers. We were becoming the kind of organization that fit around a single fire.

Corvane herself fed the papers to the flames. I don't think she trusted anyone else to do it thoroughly, or perhaps she wanted the responsibility visible, the way magistrates sign warrants in ink rather than having clerks do it. The evidence box opened. Out came the stranger's few effects: a cracked pipe, a letter half-written to someone named only *M.*, a bootlace, a stub of grey candle. Then the map itself, folded into eighths, bloodstained at two of the folds.

Then my copy. My careful, faithful, obedient copy, in my own handwriting, which looked strange to me in the firelight—as if a piece of my hand had been arrested and was being executed for crimes I'd taught it.

The paper caught. The ink went brown, then gold-edged, then gone. The rivers burned first, I noticed. The mountains held their shape longest, pale ghosts against the blackening vellum, and then they too curled and collapsed into the general dark.

Fenwick said something quiet—I never learned whether it was a prayer or an apology—and Wren stared at the fire with the expression of someone memorizing *this* instead, which is a thing apprentices do instead of crying, and which I recognized, because I was doing it too.

"There," said Corvane, when the last corner had gone to ash. "The guild remembers nothing."

And I stood there in the cold with ash beginning to drift down like filthy snow, and I thought: *You're wrong. I remember everything. I remember the fork in the blue river where the notation reads 'the door remembers kindness.' I remember the five marks circled in red at the map's edge, arranged like points on a crown. I remember the word written along the bottom margin in a hand that shook: 'IF I DO NOT REACH YOU — ROSALIND ALMEIDA — THE LEDGER KEEPS WHAT IT IS GIVEN.'*

Because that was on the map too. In his handwriting. Addressed to me. And I had never told a living soul, and now the only other copy of that sentence was smoke above the city, and the original was somewhere behind my eyes where no vote could reach it.

The guild remembers nothing. Well. The guild is not the only thing that keeps a ledger.

* * *

I did not sleep that night. Or rather, I slept the way you sleep in a house where someone has died—thinly, in instalments, surfacing at every sound. The guildhall's dormitory wing was nearly empty; Hesper's old room had stood vacant for two years since her disgrace, Odo was away collecting on a favor owed in the fishmarket district, and the elders kept their own quarters near the Long Hall. So it was quiet, the particular quiet of institutions, which is not peace but held breath.

Near midnight—or what passes for midnight underground; we kept clocks down there more out of habit than conviction—I heard wings.

Not the fluttering of pigeons, which is clumsy and apologetic. These were deliberate wingbeats, unhurried, the sound of something that had landed where it intended to land. Our dormitory had one high window, a slot of glass set into the vaulting, looking up through a lightwell toward the mortal city's sky, and it was to that window the sound came, and stopped.

I lay in the dark and listened to something settle on the outer ledge. Then—tap. Tap-tap. A beak against glass, patient as a creditor.

I should have ignored it. Institutions run on ignoring things; it's practically our charter. But I got up, because my feet had apparently seceded from the union of my judgment, and I climbed the iron stair to the window niche, and I looked out.

A crow sat on the ledge. Blacker than the night behind it, with frost melting on its shoulders where its body heat met the cold, and it looked at me through the glass with an attention no bird ought to possess. Birds look *at* you. This crow looked *into* me, the way Corvane did during votes, the way the stranger had—Bram—when he'd pressed the map into my hands and the world had narrowed to blood and breath and the weight of paper going damp in my grip.

"Caw," said the crow. And then, in a voice like a hinge that had learned language secondhand—a voice made of stolen sounds, gravel and memory—"Rrrr... almeida."

My spine performed a maneuver I would later describe, in the privacy of my own head, as *reorganizing itself*.

"You can't," I told it, reasonably, through the glass. "Crows can't do that."

The crow tilted its head, and I swear to you—it is in no ledger, and needs to be in none, because some facts stand fine on their own—it looked *wry*. Like it agreed with me. Like it had once argued the same point with somebody and lost.

It stayed there the rest of the night. I know because I watched it, off and on, between failed attempts at sleep, and every time I surfaced it was still there, feathers ruffled against the cold, keeping its post on the ledge like the world's smallest, most impossible sentinel. Once, near the grey end of the dark, it spoke again—two words this time, soft, almost tender:

"Five shards."

Then dawn came up through the lightwell, thin and colourless, and the crow spread its wings and dropped into the dark of the shaft, gone.

* * *

I spent the next day doing my job, which is to say I spent it lying fluently in ink. The official record required amendment: *All materials pertaining to the deceased stranger destroyed by order of the Council, this date. No further record exists.* I wrote it in my best hand. I dated it. I blotted it neatly. Somewhere in me a small clerk of conscience raised his hand and asked whether perhaps we might note the destruction was incomplete, and I informed him that the destruction was in fact perfectly complete, that the map had been destroyed exactly as thoroughly as a thing can be destroyed when it has been copied into the only archive no fire reaches, and he subsided, unconvinced, the way such clerks always do.

Wren found me at the ledger-stand that afternoon. Fourteen years old, sleeves too long, with that way of appearing beside you that Wren had—which is to say, silently, like a lock deciding to open.

"It wasn't scared," Wren said.

"What wasn't?"

"The bird. On the window ledge. I saw it this morning when I was oiling the east hinges." Wren picked at a sleeve-button. "Birds are scared of everything. That's how you know they're birds. That one just... waited. Like it was minding a shop."

"Wren."

"Mm?"

"Did it say anything to you?"

Wren looked up, and for a moment I saw past the apprentice-calm to the child underneath, who had come to the guild two winters ago because doors liked Wren and people didn't. "No," Wren said. Then, honestly, which is worse than lying: "Should it have?"

"No," I said. "Go oil your hinges."

Wren went. I sat with the record book open in front of me and the crow's voice lodged in my ear like a splinter working its way toward the heart. *Five shards.* Not *the map*. Not *follow me*. Five shards—which meant the map was not one destination but several, which meant Bram had not lost something but scattered something, deliberately, at cost, which meant the dying man on our doorstep had been not a messenger but a guardian, and guardians, in my experience of ledgers, only ever guard things worth killing for.

That evening I did something I had never done. I opened the forbidden drawer of my own desk—every keeper has one; ours is called the *unfiled*, and its contents are officially nonexistent—and I took out a fresh leaf of archival paper, and from the map behind my eyes I drew it again. Rivers. Mountains. The five red circles. The shaking words along the bottom.

When I finished, I sat looking at two impossibilities side by side: a guild that remembered nothing, and a page that proved otherwise. One of them was going to have to change.

Tap. Tap-tap.

I turned. The window of the scriptorium—three rooms and two locked doors from my dormitory window—had a crow on its ledge.

It regarded me through the glass with vast patience, frost steaming off its shoulders, and said, in that borrowed, rusted voice:

"Rosalind. Almeida. He knew... your name."

And far above, through the lightwell, snow began to fall again on the mortal city, and somewhere beyond it a door I had not yet learned to see was waiting to be opened—but all of that was still ahead of me. What I knew, sitting alone in the lamplight with a forbidden map and a dead man's name in my mouth, was only this:

The guild had burned the record. The record had declined to burn back.
