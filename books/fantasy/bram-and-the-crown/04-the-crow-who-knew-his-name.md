## 4. The Crow Who Knew His Name

The crow came back on the third night, which I know because I had taken to marking the nights in the margin of my ledger like a prisoner scratching tallies into a cell wall.

*Day one: no crow. Day two: still no crow. Day three: the crow is on my windowsill and it is looking at me with what I can only describe as intent.*

I should explain about my window. The guildhall sits beneath the city the way a heart sits beneath a ribcage—buried, essential, and entirely unremarked upon by the body it serves. My sleeping cell has one window, and it doesn't look out onto anything so generous as sky. It looks onto the Vent Shaft, a stone throat that carries the smell of the city down to us: bread in the morning, tallow at dusk, and once, memorably, an entire cartload of overripe plums. There is nothing to see from my window except the shaft's opposite wall, six feet away, slick with condensation and the slow green patience of moss.

And yet there was a crow standing on my sill, six feet above the void of the shaft, in a position no sane bird would choose, looking at me as though I were late for an appointment.

"Go away," I whispered.

It tilted its head. Crows do that; it's how they triangulate. But this one did it slowly, deliberately, the way a person tilts their head when they've heard something interesting and are deciding whether to pretend they didn't.

"You're the stranger's crow," I said, which was stupid, because crows don't confirm things, and also because I was talking to a bird through a window in a hidden hall beneath a city that didn't know we existed. This is the kind of sentence you learn to say without embarrassment when you keep ledgers for impossible things. *Item: one conversation, avian, unproductive.*

The crow said, in a voice like gravel poured through a cracked bell:

*"Burn it."*

I dropped my pen.

I want to be clear that I did not scream. A ledger-keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things does not scream at impossible things; she catalogs them. But my hand did shake for a full minute afterward, and I wrote *pen, loss of (1)* in the damage column because my hands needed something to do that wasn't trembling.

The crow watched me fumble. Then it said, again, *"Burn it,"* and I understood—not all at once, but the way cold water comes to understand a sinking ship—that it was quoting him. The stranger. Bram. Those had been the elders' words, shouted across the threshold while he died: *burn the map, burn the map.* The crow had been there. The crow had carried the words home like a coin picked up off the street.

"Burn what?" I asked. "The map's already ash."

The crow looked at me for a long moment. Then it said, in a different voice—a woman's voice, clipped and official, the voice of someone reading from a document:

*"By order of the Guild of Impossible Things, all record—"*

It stopped. It knew the rest. So did I. *All record of the stranger shall be expunged. His effects burned. His name struck from every page.*

That vote had happened two days ago. I had sat through it with my hands folded and my face arranged into obedience, watching Master Corvane feed the copied pages to the fire—my copies, the ones I'd made in secret, the ones I'd told myself I might need later. He'd burned them right in front of me, and I'd thought: *well. At least I have the original in my head.* And then I'd gone back to my desk and catalogued a bottled echo and felt the specific loneliness of someone who has just discovered that her own cleverness is a secret she must now keep alone.

The crow knew about the burning too. Which meant the crow had been listening. Which meant—

"How long have you been following me?" I asked.

The crow said, *"Bram."*

Just that. His name, in the crow's own croak, not stolen from anyone. It said it the way you'd set a key down on a table between two people who both know what it opens.

I pressed my palm flat against the cold glass. "I'm not supposed to know that name anymore. Officially it never existed. If anyone asks, a stranger died on our doorstep and left us nothing, not even a name."

The crow hopped once along the sill, closer to the gap where the casement didn't quite meet the frame—the same gap, incidentally, through which the city's smells crept in, the gap I'd been meaning to report to Maintenance for three years. Through it came the smell of snow and coal smoke and, faintly, the iron tang of the river freezing over.

Then it spoke again, and this time the voice was his. I recognized it immediately, though I'd heard it only once, half-swallowed by frost and dying. *Thank you for opening the door.* The crow gave it back to me whole, clear, terrible: *"You'll want to see where I lived. Before he finds it."*

I stood there with my hand on the glass for a long time after it flew off into the dark of the shaft.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about secrets: they are heavier than objects. A map can be burned. A page can be struck from a ledger. But once a dead man's voice has come out of a crow's mouth and asked you for help, that weight settles somewhere behind your sternum, and no amount of official expungement moves it.

I got dressed. I took the false-bottom drawer key. And I went to find out where a dead man had lived.

* * *

Getting out of the guildhall at night is not difficult, which tells you everything about the state of the guild these days. We are so few, so old, so tired of guarding a secret the world stopped trying to find, that our security consists mostly of habit. Hesper used to say—we don't speak much anymore, Hesper and I, since her disgrace—that a door only protects you if somebody believes in what's behind it. Nobody believes in us. Therefore nobody guards us. Therefore a junior ledger-keeper with a lantern and a guilty conscience can walk straight up the Back Stair and out through the Coal Door, provided she leaves the hinges oiled, because Wren notices things like squeaky hinges and asks questions like *why were you out at midnight* with those enormous guileless eyes.

The city above was wearing its first real snow. It lay on the rooftops like a held breath, and the lamps wore cones of falling white, and the whole vast machine of the city had gone soft-voiced and slow. I pulled my hood up and followed the crow.

Following a crow through a winter city is exactly as dignified as it sounds. It flew ahead twenty feet, landed on some appropriate perch—a signpost, a chimney, the shoulder of a saint whose statue stood in a niche outside a shuttered chandler's—and waited for me to catch up, radiating the patience of a tutor with a slow pupil. Once, when I paused to get my bearings, it croaked, in the clipped official voice of the guild proclamation: *"Proceed."*

"I'm proceeding," I muttered. "Some of us don't have wings."

It said, in a child's voice I hadn't heard before, high and bright and wrong coming out of that black throat: *"Faster, Rosalind, faster!"*

I stopped in the middle of the empty street.

Because here is what I understood then, standing in the snow with my heart going like a trapped moth: the crow wasn't just repeating things it had heard. It was collecting voices. It had mine—it had heard me whisper through the window, heard me mutter at my ledgers, and it had kept a piece. Everything it heard, it kept. And if it could carry my voice away in its black inventory, then it had carried his too. All of him that was left. Whatever the crow was—transformed, bound, cursed, or merely some impossible thing the guild had failed to catalogue—it was walking around wearing the last scraps of a man, the way you wear patches cut from a coat.

"Faster," I agreed quietly, and walked faster.

His lodgings were in Candlewick Row, in the crooked stretch behind the lamplighters' yard, where the buildings lean together overhead like conspirators. The crow landed on a drainpipe beside a door so narrow it seemed apologetic, and looked at me, and looked at the door, and looked at me.

"No key," I said.

It said, in the woman's clipped official voice: *"All doors are records. Ask them what they remember."*

Which is either profound or nonsense, and I genuinely couldn't tell you which, but I put my hand flat against the door anyway—I don't know why, except that when a crow quotes your own guild's founding principles at you in a dead man's borrowed tones, you humor it—and the door swung open. Unlocked. Or locked, and indifferent to it. I filed the question under *later*, which is where I file most questions these days. The drawer marked LATER is getting very full.

Inside was a room the size of a confession booth, containing a bed, a chair, a table, a candle-end, and the entire remaining biography of Bram.

I lit the candle stub with shaking hands and made myself look properly, the way you're trained to look at a ledger line: itemize, don't flinch. A cloak, frost-eaten, hanging on a nail. Boots by the bed, worn through at the heel. On the table, a wooden cup, a knife, and—my breath caught—a small tin of tea leaves, because even men carrying apocalypses in their pockets apparently still wanted tea in the morning.

And under the loose floorboard beneath the bed, because every man keeping secrets keeps them under floorboards; I've read enough confiscated diaries to know it's practically a law of nature—letters.

There were eleven. I read them by candlelight while the crow perched on the bedpost and watched me with one round eye, and I will transcribe here what matters, because this chapter of my life deserves an accurate record even if the elders would strike every word.

They were letters to no one—or rather, letters written the way you talk to yourself when talking aloud would be dangerous. Drafts. Discards. The handwriting started confident and got worse as the pages went on, the way handwriting does when the writer's hands start failing.

The earliest said: *The Crown of Covenants was never a king's bauble. Whoever taught you otherwise was selling you something. It is the hinge. It is the handshake. Two realms swore on it, and the swearing is what holds the weather, the borders, the light. Take it away and you do not merely unlock a door—you take the doorframe with it.*

Another: *Vane was a magistrate once. A good one, they say, before the winter made him cruel or the cruelty made him useful. He does not think of himself as a thief. That's the worst of it. Thieves can be caught. He thinks he is a surgeon.*

Another, the ink blotted, hurried: *I have scattered the shards. Five pieces, five keepers, five debts called in. He cannot reforge what he cannot gather. Forgive me for what this costs the ones who hold them. Forgive me for what it cost me.*

And the last one, barely legible, dated the day before he died on our doorstep:

*If you are reading this, I did not make it back. Go to the guild. Yes, the guild—they will deny knowing me, deny everything, that is what they are FOR—but the young one, the one who opened the door, the one who writes and writes and is never written about. Give her the map. She'll copy it. They always copy it. That's the whole point of her.*

I sat very still.

The candle guttered. Snow ticked against the window. On the bedpost, the crow said nothing, and its silence was somehow louder than all its stolen voices.

"That's not flattering," I said finally, because my mouth does that when my chest is too full. "Being predictable. Being *the type*. 'She'll copy it, they always copy it'—you make me sound like a bad habit."

The crow tilted its head.

"And what am I supposed to do? Hmm? I'm a ledger-keeper. Junior. I catalogue echoes and moon-debts and once spent six weeks determining whether an invisible cat had been fairly traded. I don't fight magistrates. I don't cross realms. I write things down and stay out of sight, and the staying-out-of-sight part is the only reason any of us are alive, and you want me to—" I stopped. Started again, quieter. "You want me to walk toward the thing everyone else is running from."

The crow hopped down onto the table, next to the last letter, and looked at me.

Then it said—in my voice. Mine, exactly mine, lifted whole out of that moment at the threshold, the moment I keep returning to like a tongue to a broken tooth: *"Thank you for opening the door."*

I closed my eyes.

Wonder is a strange thing. People talk about it like it's gentle, like it's warm. But real wonder—the kind that arrives when the world cracks open and shows you it was never the shape you thought—wonder sharpens. It gets edges. It stops being *oh, how marvelous* and starts being *oh no, oh no, it's all real, and I have seen it, and seeing is a debt.* I sat in that coffin-narrow room holding a dead man's letters, and the wonder went through me and came out the other side as dread, cold and clean as the ice on the river.

Because there was more. Under the letters, folded twice, a scrap I almost missed: a printed notice, official, bearing a seal I recognized from the city courts—a magistrate's writ. *Serath Vane, Magistrate of the Winter Assizes,* requesting information regarding persons entering or leaving the city by unusual means, inquiries to be directed to the Grey Court. And beneath the printed text, in Vane's own hand—precise, unhurried, the handwriting of a man who signs death warrants the way other men sign birthday cards—one line:

*The crow will lead you to the rest. Follow it.*

He knew. He had known the crow would grieve, would haunt, would lead someone back here. He had counted on it. Which meant this room wasn't a refuge.

It was bait. And I was sitting in it, holding the evidence, lit like a festival.

The crow went rigid on the table. Its head snapped toward the window—an instant before I heard it myself: boots in the snow outside, unhurried, several pairs, closing on the narrow door with the confidence of people who already know what's behind it.

"Back way?" I breathed.

The crow looked at me, and then—at the wall. And I swear on every ledger I have ever kept, it said, in the calm official voice of the guild's oldest proclamation, the one carved over the Coal Door: *"A door is only a wall that has agreed to move."*

Boots. Closer. Snow crunching.

I grabbed the letters, snuffed the candle, and put my hand against the wall.
