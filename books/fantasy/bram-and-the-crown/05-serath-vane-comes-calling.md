## 5. Serath Vane Comes Calling

The Guild of Impossible Things had survived nine hundred years of kings, plagues, inquisitions, and one regrettable incident involving a mayor and a wish-granting goose, and it had done so by following three rules: never be seen, never be named, never lie to a member of the guild.

The elders broke the third rule within four minutes of meeting Serath Vane.

I know because I was counting. I count when I'm frightened; it's how I keep my hands from shaking. Four minutes from the moment the knocker sounded—our knocker, which has no door attached to it, which exists solely so that guild members can knock on nothing in a meaningful way—to the moment Elder Corvane said, "A dead man? Here? I'm afraid you're mistaken, magistrate," in a voice pitched exactly like a man who was not mistaken about anything except his own innocence.

I was standing in the corner with the ledger cart, because that is where ledger-keepers stand: near the records, out of the light, functionally invisible. It is a role I have perfected over eleven years. I could have told you the exact weight of the stranger who died on our threshold. I could have told you the color of the blood on the map he pressed into my hands before the frost closed his eyes. But nobody asked me, because asking the ledger-keeper would mean admitting she existed, and the whole architecture of my life depended on no one doing that.

The man in winter-grey did not look at the elders when they lied to him.

He looked at me.

* * *

Let me describe him properly, because I have thought about that description many times since, turning it over like a coin I suspected was counterfeit.

Serath Vane was tall in the way that furniture is tall—he took up space with an air of having been designed for it. His coat was grey, but not the grey of storm or ash; it was the grey of a page on which nothing has yet been written, and somehow that was worse. He wore no ornament except a chain at his collar from which hung a small seal, and the seal was blank. I stared at it for a long time before I understood what unsettled me: every magistrate's seal bears the mark of the office that grants it. His bore nothing. He had been authorized by an authority that either no longer existed or preferred not to sign its work.

"Forgive the intrusion," he said, and his voice was courteous the way a scalpel is courteous. "I am conducting an inquiry into the death of one Bramwell Ashe, formerly of this city." He produced a card between two fingers as if it were a thing he had grown there. "He was seen entering this quarter on the night of the first snow."

"This quarter?" Elder Corvane laughed—a dry, papery sound. "Magistrate, nothing enters this quarter. There's nothing here to enter. We are a street of chandlers and a chapel with a leaking roof. You've wasted your morning."

"Mm," said Vane.

That was all. *Mm.* And then his gaze drifted, unhurried, around the room—the false wall behind which our hall actually opened, the candlesticks that had been burning since before my grandmother's grandmother (impossible things keep their own hours), the ledger cart in the corner where I stood very still among the records like a filing error made flesh.

His eyes were pale. Not blue, not grey—pale the way ice under snow is pale, the color showing only where something has pressed down hard enough to reveal it.

"You keep ledgers," he observed.

"We keep accounts," Corvane said quickly. "Chandler's accounts. Tallow in, tallow out. Terribly dull. You'd weep with boredom."

"I rarely weep," said Vane. "May I see them?"

And here is what I will say for Elder Corvane, may the guild forgive me for saying anything nice about him at all: he was a coward, but he was a coward with excellent instincts about paperwork. "They're being rebound," he said. "All of them. Moths."

Vane looked at the cart. The cart looked back, insofar as a cart can, and on top of it, in plain view, sat the current ledger with my handwriting on its spine and last Tuesday's date.

"Moths," Vane repeated.

"A vigorous infestation," said Elder Maren, who had apparently decided that if one lie was good, two lies stacked on top of each other would bear weight like masonry. They do not. Lies are arches; they need exactly the right shape or everything comes down.

There was a silence. In that silence I heard three things: the candles burning, which they do with a faint sound like distant applause; my own heartbeat, which had gotten above itself and was now somewhere in the region of my throat; and, faintly, from the direction of the window high above the hall, the tap of a beak against glass.

Bram. The crow. He had taken to haunting my window since the night the elders burned the copied map—since the night I watched my own careful hand go into the fire and discovered, watching it curl, that I had already committed every line to memory. Some knowledge cannot be unkept. That is not a metaphor. It is practically the guild's founding charter.

"Do you hear that?" Vane said.

"Hear what?" said Corvane.

"The bird."

"Birds," said Maren, with the confidence of a woman who had never once been right about birds, "do not come here. The chimneys are sealed."

Vane smiled. I want to be precise about this smile, because it was the single most frightening thing I saw that day, and I include in that tally the moment he turned to go. It was a smile that acknowledged a joke without sharing it. A smile that filed something away. "Of course," he said. "Sealed chimneys. Vigorous moths. A dead man who was never here." He inclined his head to each elder in turn, a small precise bow, like a man ticking boxes. "You have been most helpful."

"We aim to serve the city," said Corvane.

"No," said Vane pleasantly. "You really don't."

* * *

He left. Or rather, he walked to the false wall, paused, and said, "This opens inward, I think," in the tone of a man remarking on the weather, and then he left, and the door of nothing closed behind him, and the elders stood in the hall looking like a committee that has just remembered the fire.

Then they turned on each other, which is what committees do instead of catching fire themselves.

"He knows," Maren said. "Corvane, he *knows*. Did you see him look at the girl?"

Everyone looked at the girl. I raised my hand in a small wave. It seemed polite.

"The girl is irrelevant," Corvane snapped—but too fast, the way you say a thing when you have just realized it isn't true. "What matters is the stranger. We should never have let the body be buried in the yard. We should have rendered him down. Unrecorded, unremembered—"

"He had a name," I said.

The hall went quiet. Eleven years of invisibility, and I spent my entire budget of visibility on those four words. Worth it.

Elder Corvane regarded me over his spectacles the way one regards a stain on an otherwise acceptable document. "Ledger-keeper."

"Bramwell Ashe," I said. "Formerly of this city. That's what the magistrate called him. Which means the magistrate already knew his name before he walked through our door, which means this visit wasn't an inquiry. It was a confirmation. He came to see whether we'd flinch." I heard my own voice doing something new—steady, level, almost bored—and marveled at it, because inside I felt like a kettle. "We flinched."

"You forget yourself," said Corvane.

"No," I said. "That's rather the problem, isn't it? Nobody remembers me. That's why I'm the one who remembers everything else." I pulled the current ledger off the cart and opened it to the entry I had made the morning after the stranger died—not the entry the elders ordered, which read *no person of record passed this threshold*, written in the bland official hand of institutional amnesia, but the real one, kept in the back pages where I keep everything that matters, catalogued under headings the guild pretends don't exist. "Frostbite, advanced. Blood loss. A map, water-stained, drawn on vellum of a make I've never seen. Last words: *the crow remembers*. And a name he gave me, though the elders voted it struck from record: Bram."

"You copied the map," Maren said slowly. "After we voted to burn it."

"I copied it *before* you burned it. That's why I was able to burn it with such conviction." I closed the ledger. My hands, I noted with interest, had stopped shaking somewhere around the word *flinched*. Fear does that, sometimes. It burns down to coals and then it's just heat, and heat can be worked with. "Elders. That man is going to come back. Not politely next time. And when he does, he won't ask questions. He'll bring forms."

"Forms?" said Corvane.

"Judgments," I said. "I've been reading the old ledgers. All of them, the deep ones, the ones from before the Long Winter started. Nine hundred years ago the guild recorded a magistrate who went below—into the other realm—with a commission to 'order the covenant.' His seal was blank. His coat was grey. The entry ends mid-sentence, Elder. Mid-*sentence*. As if whoever was writing it stopped believing in what they were writing." I looked around the hall, at the candlesticks applauding softly to themselves, at the elders I had served and obeyed and loved in the way you love a house that has never quite had a room for you. "He didn't steal the Crown yesterday. He stole it nine hundred years ago, or he found where it fell, or he *is* what happened when someone tried to hold order alone. I don't know which. But I know he looked at me today the way a man looks at a loose thread. And I know that this guild's invisibility—which we have worshipped like a saint—is the reason he'll win. You cannot hide from a man whose whole creed is that hidden things are disorderly. Hiding is just surrender with better posture."

Silence again. Outside, faintly, the crow tapped the glass once more, insistent, like a creditor.

"What would you have us do?" Maren asked at last, and her voice had changed—it was smaller, older, genuinely frightened, and I hated that I had done that, and I hated more that it needed doing.

"Nothing," I said. "That's the point. You'll do nothing, because doing nothing is what this guild does, and maybe that was enough when the worlds were whole. But I—" and here my voice did the other thing, the frightening thing, the thing that sounded like a decision wearing a voice as a disguise— "I am going to find out what a dead man wanted me to carry. And I'd very much appreciate it if, when you erase *me* from the record afterward, you'd spell my name correctly. Almeida. Two i's' worth of dignity."

I pushed the cart toward the archive stairs. Behind me, Corvane began, "Rosalind—" using my name for perhaps the third time in eleven years, always, notably, when obedience was required rather than belonging offered.

But I had already reached the stair, and the dark of the archive swallowed me kindly, and on my shoulder—though I had not heard the window open, though no door in that hall had been unsealed—sat a crow, feathers smelling of snow and smoke, who leaned close to my ear and said, in a voice like stolen silver:

"*The ledger-keeper keeps the ledger. The ledger keeps the keeper. Ask the door-maker. Ask the debt-man. Ask the girl who talks to locks.*"

I stopped on the stairs.

"How long have you been able to talk?" I whispered.

The crow considered this with one bright eye. "*Since someone finally listened,*" he said.

And far above us, muffled by stone and secrecy, the knocker that hangs upon no door knocked twice—polite, patient, unmistakable—the knock of a man in winter-grey who had forgotten something in our hall, and had come back for it, and had brought the forms.
