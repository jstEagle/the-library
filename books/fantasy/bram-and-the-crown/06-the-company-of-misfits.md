## 6. The Company of Misfits

The elders did not burn me at the stake, which I suppose I should count as a professional courtesy. They did something worse. they thanked me.

"You've shown initiative, Almeida," said Elder Quillon, folding his hands atop the ledger like a man settling in for a long nap. "Initiative is valuable. In its place."

"And where," I asked, "is the place for the truth?"

"The truth," said Elder Maren, who had once traded her shadow to a stranger and never quite gotten it back, so that she cast only a grey suggestion of herself on the wall, "is that a dead man gave you a map, and a crow has taken an interest in you, and neither of those things is your fault. But the guild survives by not existing, child. You are proposing that we *exist*."

I had proposed no such thing. I had proposed that we at least acknowledge the Crown of Covenants was real, that Serath Vane was hunting it, and that pretending otherwise was like ignoring a leak because the cellar hadn't drowned us yet. It was, in hindsight, optimistic of me to expect arithmetic from people whose entire profession was denial with good penmanship.

The vote was four to one against. The one was Elder Tamsin, who is ninety-one years old, mostly deaf, and later told me she'd voted yes because she thought we were voting on dinner.

So that was settled. The guild would do nothing, record nothing, and know nothing, and I was instructed—gently, kindly, in the tone one uses for a promising apprentice who has shown a worrying interest in fire—to forget the whole affair.

I went down to my alcove in the cataloguing hall, sat among the impossible trades, and considered my options.

They were, as follows: obey, and spend the rest of my life shelving bottled echoes while two worlds quietly froze; or disobey, alone, with a memorized map, a grief-stricken crow, and no particular talent beyond knowing where everything was filed.

It was while staring at shelf nine—the shelf where we kept debts owed by celestial bodies, all of them delinquent—that I realized I was thinking about this wrong. The guild's great secret wasn't that it existed. Anyone could exist. The guild's great secret was that it had spent three hundred years collecting the people nobody else wanted, precisely because nobody else wanted them. Door-makers who made doors that shouldn't open. Collectors of debts that shouldn't be collectable. Apprentices who talked to locks. Every outcast the city produced eventually trickled down into our hall like water finding a cellar.

If I needed a company, I didn't need to recruit anyone. I needed only to go through the guild's own discard pile.

* * *

Hesper Vane-no-relation-thank-the-gods worked in the door-yard behind the guildhall, where disgraced door-makers were sent to repair hinges until they were either forgiven or forgotten, whichever came first. She had been the guild's finest maker in a generation—doors that opened onto yesterday, doors that only opened for the honest, one famous door that refused to open at all and was considered a masterpiece—and then, eleven years ago, she had built a door for someone she shouldn't have, and what came through it had cost the guild two members and most of its reputation among people who don't believe in it anyway.

She was re-hanging a cupboard door when I found her, which tells you how far the mighty fall. A cupboard door. For the kitchen.

"I need a door that isn't there," I said, because Hesper appreciated directness the way other people appreciate wine.

She didn't look up. "Little Almeida. Ledger-keeper. Come to order me around by the book?"

"Come to ask you to break every rule the elders just reminded me of."

That got her attention. She set down her mallet and studied me with the flat, assessing look she probably used on timber. "Talk."

I talked. I told her about Bram dying on the threshold with frost in his lungs, about the map, about the Crown, about Vane in his winter-grey asking polite questions with knives folded inside them, about the elders voting to pretend the world wasn't ending. She listened without interrupting, which unnerved me more than shouting would have.

When I finished she picked her mallet back up and knocked twice on the cupboard door, listening to the sound of it.

"You understand," she said, "that if I make a door into the other realm, I'm not just exiled again. Exile means they still think about you. This means they'll strike me from the ledger entirely. No record. For a door-maker, that's not punishment. That's erasure." She turned the mallet over in her hands. "Eleven years ago I made a door for a man who paid me well and lied beautifully, and I've spent every day since being sorry in a cupboard. So." She looked at me, and there was something in her face I hadn't expected—not eagerness, exactly. Relief. "Where do you need to go?"

"The other realm. Through whatever way is left."

"There's always a way left," Hesper said. "Doors are stubborn that way. Like their makers." She rolled up her sleeves. "Give me three days and access to the old foundations under the guildhall. And Almeida—if you're gathering a company, get Odo. You'll need favors, and he's the only one who knows where they're buried."

* * *

Odo kept his office in the gap between the tannery and the chandler's shop—a space so narrow that the city planners had officially declared it didn't exist, which made it perfect guild property. He collected debts, but not the kind you could pay in coin. Favors. Promises. The small, unpayable currencies people trade when money won't cover the damage. His office walls were papered floor to ceiling with slips of paper recording every favor ever owed to him, organized by debtor, date, and degree of desperation.

He was eating lunch—an egg, balanced delicately on his knee—when I walked in.

"Rosalind Almeida," he said. "You've come to settle a debt."

"I haven't."

"Everyone comes to settle a debt eventually. Or to incur one. There's no third reason to visit me." He finished the egg with terrible elegance. "Which is it?"

"Neither. I've come to offer you a job that pays nothing, risks everything, and will make the entire guild disown you."

Odo considered this with the seriousness other men reserved for contracts involving land. Then he smiled, and I remembered why people found him unsettling: the smile was genuinely warm, and it made you want to trust him, and he knew it.

"The last time someone offered me that," he said, "I took it, and it's why I live between a tannery and a chandler's." He stood, brushed egg from his waistcoat, and began pulling slips of paper off the wall with practiced fingers—here a favor owed by a ferryman, there a promise extracted from a locksmith, somewhere higher up something from a person he declined to name. "What's the debt?" he asked, and I realized he meant Bram. Everyone would mean Bram, eventually. Dead men incur obligations whether they mean to or not.

"He saved the Crown from a thief," I said. "Died doing it. Left the map to me. That makes it mine to finish, and I can't finish it alone."

"Mm." Odo tucked the papers into his coat. "You know what I like about you, ledger-keeper? You say 'mine' like it costs you something. Most people say it like it feeds them." He pulled on his coat. "I'll come. Not for the Crown. Crowns are just debts wearing jewelry. I'll come because—" he paused, and for one moment the warmth in his smile flickered and showed the structure beneath it, which was older and more tired, "—because there's a favor I owe that I've been avoiding for eleven years, and the road you're taking runs through the country where I incurred it. Call it killing two birds. Or one bird and one coward."

He didn't explain further. Odo never explained further. That was the arrangement.

* * *

Wren was the hardest, though she was also the smallest, which should tell you something about the guild.

She was fourteen, an apprentice of no fixed discipline because no master could hold her. She talked to locks. Not *at* locks, mind—everyone talks at locks, usually while swearing. Wren talked *to* them, and worse, they answered. A lock would take one look at her nimble fingers and simply give up any pretense of being locked. Doors throughout the guildhall had begun developing what the masters called "an attitude problem" whenever Wren was within fifty feet of them.

Currently she was confined to the apprentices' dormitory for the crime of having opened the elders' private archive, which contained nothing interesting except the guild's actual founding charter, which revealed that our solemn traditions were largely invented in 1712 by a guildmaster with a flair for drama.

"They're going to send me home," she said, sitting cross-legged on her cot with her chin on her fists. "To my aunt. Who is *normal*."

The horror in her voice was sincere.

"What if instead," I said, "you came somewhere locks are actually difficult?"

Her eyes went bright and quick as a bird's. "Difficult how?"

"Doors that don't want to be opened. Locks in another realm. Mechanisms made by people who've had three hundred years to get creative about keeping strangers out." I crouched to her level, which felt less condescending than it sounds and more conspiratorial. "And I can't lie to you, Wren, because you'll be talking to the locks anyway and they'll tell you. It's dangerous. People might die. The elders will erase you from every record we keep."

"That last part's already happening." She shrugged, fourteen years old and ancient about it. "Nobody writes down the apprentice who talks to locks except when they're writing down why she's in trouble." Then, quieter: "Is the crow coming too?"

I blinked. "You know about the crow?"

"He sits on my windowsill sometimes. We don't talk. Well—I talk. He does this thing with his head like he understands *everything* and finds most of it disappointing." She slid off the cot. "He likes you. He followed you home from the door, you know. The night the man died. I saw him from the window. He landed where the blood was and just—stayed."

Something tightened in my chest. I filed it away, the way I file everything, and put out my hand.

"Wren. Will you swear to something the guild has no record of?"

She shook it with the grip of someone who had clearly been practicing handshakes in secret. "Sworn," she said. "Whatever it is. Sworn."

"Don't swear blind—"

"You literally just promised me locks in another world. How much blinder could a person possibly swear?" And she grinned, and I decided that if we all died in a frozen wasteland, at least I'd die having heard that grin.

* * *

We met at midnight in the door-yard, because midnight is when misfits are at their best and everyone else is at their worst. Hesper brought tools wrapped in oilcloth. Odo brought a satchel of paper promises heavy enough to be a weapon. Wren brought the crow, who arrived on her shoulder with the air of one gracing us with his presence, and then immediately flew to mine, which settled several questions about loyalty and raised several about taste.

"So," Hesper said, striking a lucifer against her boot. The matchlight caught four faces and one pair of black feathered eyes. "Before we go anywhere. Before doors get made and debts get called in and children get erased. What exactly are we swearing to? Because I've sworn guild oaths before, and they were beautiful and meaningless, and I'd like to try the opposite this time."

We looked at each other in the small circle of light. None of us, I realized, had ever done this before. The guild had forms for everything—forms for confession, forms for exile, forms for trading a memory of your mother for a warm winter—but no form for five people choosing each other in a cold yard with no witnesses worth the name.

"No ledger," I said slowly, working it out as the words came. "Nothing written. Which means nothing can be struck out, either. If we write it down, the elders can burn it. So it has to live somewhere they can't reach." I tapped my own sternum, and saw Wren tap hers a moment later, copying me without embarrassment.

"Say it plain, then," said Odo. "Plain words. The plainest ones."

So we said it plain. Hesper first, because she was oldest and had the most to answer for: *"I'm done making doors for liars. I'll make them for you."*

Odo: *"I'll call in every favor I own and owe every one I spend. My debts travel with the company now—all of them."* And here he glanced at me, briefly, and I understood that some part of what he carried was about to become ours, and that this terrified him more than Vane ever could.

Wren, too fast, stumbling over the bigness of it: *"I'll open what needs opening and leave shut what needs leaving shut, and I won't open anything for anyone who isn't us."*

Bram said nothing, being a crow, but he stepped forward along my arm and pressed his beak, very gently, against my knuckle—which from Bram constituted an affidavit.

And then they all looked at me, because somebody had to go last and I was apparently the one holding the map.

I am not good at speeches. I am good at ledgers. So I gave them the only oath I knew how to give, the one I'd been rehearsing alone in the cataloguing hall since the night a stranger died on our doorstep.

"I was taught that the guild keeps impossible things safe by keeping them hidden," I said. "I think that's backwards. I think impossible things stay alive by being *carried*, out loud, in company. So: I swear to carry this—Bram's map, the Crown, the debt, all of it—until it's paid or I am. I swear the guild doesn't end where the elders' fear ends. It ends wherever we stop walking. And I swear"—and here my voice did something embarrassing, and I let it, because an oath with no cracks in it isn't load-bearing—"that none of us gets erased. Not by Vane. Not by the elders. Not by each other. Whatever happens below, everybody comes home *remembered*."

Silence, then, in the door-yard. The kind of silence that isn't empty.

"Well," said Hesper at last, snuffing the match. "That was either the stupidest thing I've ever agreed to or the first honest oath I've ever taken. Possibly both." She hoisted her tool-bag. "Foundations, little Almeida. Show me where you want your door."

We went down into the dark under the guildhall together—five of us, counting the crow, not counting whatever else had already attached itself to our names. Behind us the guild slept on, secure in its invisibility, its ledgers immaculate and its records complete, containing no mention whatsoever of anything we were about to do.

Ahead of us, three stories beneath the city, in a foundation older than the guild, older than the city, there was a stretch of blank stone wall that Hesper looked at for a long moment and then touched with both palms, the way you'd greet an old friend you'd betrayed.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you're still here. Good." She glanced back at us over her shoulder, and the lamplight made her grin look slightly feral. "Everyone breathe out. This is going to feel like falling."

It was.
