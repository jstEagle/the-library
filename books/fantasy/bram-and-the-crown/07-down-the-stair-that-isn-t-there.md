## 7. Down the Stair That Isn't There

I have made eleven hundred and forty doors in my life. I know this because I counted, the way disgraced people count things—obsessively, at three in the morning, with a candle guttering and no one to tell them to stop.

Every one of those doors was honest work. Oak and iron, hinges set true, latches that spoke when you asked them nicely. A good door is a promise: *on this side, warmth; on that side, weather.* I was proud of my promises. Then the Guild of Impossible Things decided that a door I had made for a widow in Candlewick Lane opened onto a room that did not exist, and rather than admire the craftsmanship, they struck my name from their rolls and told me to consider myself fortunate they didn't strike anything else.

So understand me when I say what I said to Rosalind Almeida in the frost-dead garden behind the tannery, with the wind coming off the river like a bill collector:

"I can make you a door anywhere. That has never been the problem. The problem is what's on the other side of it."

She looked at me the way young people look at old craftspeople—as though experience were a purse she intended to pick—and she said, "I'm not asking you to open it all the way."

Which is how four misfits and a crow came to be standing in the cellar of a condemned dye-house at midnight, while I drew a door on a wall that had no business holding one.

* * *

Let me tell you about my craft, since no one in the guild ever bothered to learn it before they exiled me for it. Doors are not wood. Doors are not stone. A door is an *agreement* between two spaces—a treaty signed in hinges. Any wall will hold a door if you can convince the wall that somewhere else exists and that it wouldn't mind a visitor. Most walls are lonely. Walls spend centuries pressed up against other walls, hearing footsteps through them, smelling other people's bread. You'd be amazed what a wall will agree to if someone finally asks it properly.

The trick is the asking. Chalk helps. So does knowing the true name of the space behind—the not-space, the between, the place where doors go when they're closed. And it helps, though I have never said this aloud to anyone, if your hands shake just slightly. A trembling hand makes an honest line. Steady hands build fortresses. Trembling hands build thresholds.

My hands were shaking plenty that night, so I had high hopes.

Rosalind stood by the single guttering lamp with the map folded against her chest like something she'd stolen from church—which, in fairness, she had, more or less, from her own elders. Bram the crow sat on her shoulder with his head cocked at an angle I had come to recognize as *opinionated*. Behind us, Odo leaned against a barrel of congealed dye with his arms crossed and his ledger of favors—actual ledger, actual favors, don't ask—tucked under one arm like a shield he'd grown tired of carrying. Wren crouched at the base of the cellar stair with her palm flat against the lock of the street door above, listening to it the way you'd listen to a seashell.

"It says the night watch passes in nine minutes," she reported. "It says we shouldn't worry. It likes us."

"Doors always like Wren," Odo muttered. "It's the rest of the world that's the difficulty."

"The rest of the world," Rosalind said, without looking up from the chalk lines taking shape under my fingers, "is currently trying to kill us, so I've stopped taking it personally."

She meant Serath Vane, the magistrate in winter-grey who had come asking his polite questions and left behind a silence like a held breath. She meant the elders who had disavowed her before Vane's boots were even out of the guildhall. She meant the cold that had been creeping down through the city all season, frosting windows from the inside, killing trees that had stood two hundred years as though they'd simply been waiting for permission to quit.

And underneath all of it, though none of us said it plainly, she meant the realm below—the dying country our guild had spent three centuries pretending wasn't there, whose slow starvation was now leaking upward through every crack in the world like water through a bad roof.

The map said there was a way down. Not a door, exactly. The map marked it with a symbol I recognized from my apprenticeship days, before the guild taught me to be ashamed of knowing it: a triangle with its point downward, the mark of a *made threshold*. Someone, long ago, had built a stair between the realms, and then—this was the part the map didn't say—someone had unmade it, or buried it, or agreed so hard with themselves that it didn't exist that it had stopped.

That was my job tonight. To remind a piece of forgotten city that it used to go somewhere.

* * *

"Here," I said, when the chalk was done.

The design wasn't pretty. It never is. A true threshold looks like nothing—an arch sketched wrong, a rectangle that leans, marks that seem to shift when you blink because they're not quite sitting on the wall but slightly *into* it. My master used to say a proper door drawing should look like a mistake a child would make. He also drank. Both of these things were probably related.

"That's it?" Odo said. "We're going to invade another realm through a scribble?"

"You're welcome to knock on the wall politely instead."

He shut up. Odo talks big about debts and obligations, but I'd noticed he goes quiet around anything that might cost him something real. Which, if you think about it, describes most of us.

Rosalind came to stand beside me. Close enough that I could see the chalk dust on her knuckles and the exhaustion under her eyes that she pretended was resolve. "What do you need?" she asked.

"Honesty," I said. "From everyone, for about ten minutes. It's the rarest material I work with." I touched the top of the drawn arch. "A door is a promise, and a promise only holds if both sides mean it. The other side has to want us to come through—or at least permit it. This stair was made by people from below, going up, long ago. Their agreement is still in the stones, sleeping. I need to wake it up and offer ours in exchange."

"And if it doesn't wake up?"

"Then we've wasted a perfectly good evening and some excellent chalk, and we go home and wait for the winter to eat us, which frankly has a certain convenience to it."

Bram croaked. It sounded like laughter. It sounded, if I'm being precise, which is my whole personality, like *coward*, spoken in a voice that wasn't quite a crow's—that borrowed, magpie quality he had, stitching words out of other people's sentences. He'd been doing that more lately. Speaking in scraps. Rosalind fed him scraps back, patiently, like a woman translating a letter written in a language she almost knew.

"Begin," Rosalind said.

I began.

* * *

You don't force a door. I want that understood by posterity, whoever ends up reading this or remembering it, because the guild got it wrong for three hundred years and it cost them everything. You don't batter a threshold into being. You *ask*. You put your hand against the world and you say: *there is somewhere else, and it is not so far, and I am willing.*

I said it in the old craft-tongue, which sounds like nothing—like a woman humming to herself in a cellar, like nonsense syllables—but each sound is a hinge-pin. I felt for the sleeping agreement in the wall the way you feel for a latch in the dark: fingertips first, then palm, then the whole flat of your hand pressing until the wall stops being a wall and becomes a question.

The cellar went quiet. Not silent—quiet. The difference matters. Silence is absence. Quiet is attention. The whole condemned dye-house, the whole street above it, maybe the whole city, leaned in to hear whether I meant it.

My hands shook. Good. Honest lines.

"There is somewhere else," I said. "It is not so far. I am willing."

And the wall—

The wall remembered.

I'll try to describe it, though describing a made-threshold opening is like describing a chord to a man born deaf. The chalk lines didn't glow, exactly. They *sank*, sinking into the stone like ink into wet paper until the wall itself was the drawing. The brickwork loosened—not crumbling, *unclenching*, bricks easing apart like fists relaxing—and in the gaps there was not darkness but depth. Air moved out of it. Old air, mineral air, air that had been waiting underground since before the city had a name, and it smelled of iron and snow and something green that has no business existing in winter.

Behind me, Wren made a small sound. When I glanced back, she had both hands over her mouth and tears on her face, and she said, muffled, "It's so *glad*. The stair is so glad somebody came."

Threshold terror, my master called the feeling that hit me then. Every door-maker knows it. There's a moment, on the far side of any new-made door, when you understand with total clarity that you cannot take it back—that the agreement is signed, the hinge is set, and whatever comes next comes next whether you're brave or not. Your body knows it before your mind does. Your knees know it. Some part of you older than names stands at every threshold and screams *turn around*.

And right beside the terror—this is the shameful part, the part that got me exiled, probably—right beside it sits exhilaration, bright and filthy as stolen wine. Because some part of you older than fear also whispers: *but what if?*

"What if," I said out loud, and stepped through.

* * *

The stair was there. Of course the stair was there; I hadn't made it, only reminded it. It descended in great worn steps of black stone, wide enough for six abreast once, carved with figures that time had rubbed down to suggestions—processions, handshakes, two figures crowning a third between them. The workmanship was better than anything our realm had produced in living memory, and it was everywhere chipped and frost-cracked, beautiful the way a scarred face is beautiful.

The cold came up to meet us like a host.

Not the sharp cold of the city above. This was older, patient cold, cold with an appetite, and it flowed past us downward-bound as we climbed down against it, the way a crowd flows past a man walking the wrong direction. Rosalind shivered and pulled her coat tighter and did not slow down. The map was in her hands now, unfolded, and I noticed she kept one thumb pressed flat over the mark of the stair as though pinning it in place. As though afraid it might change its mind about existing.

Bram rode her shoulder with his feathers fluffed into a grey ruff, and his eyes—crows' eyes are supposed to be flat black buttons, but his caught the lamplight with something almost like fire in them—his eyes watched the carvings as we passed them. At the figure of the crowning, he made a sound. Low. Not a word. More like the memory of a word, sunk too deep to dig out.

Odo came last, which surprised me until I realized why: he kept turning around, looking back up at the doorway I'd made, watching the light of our own world shrink to a coin, then a ring, then a thread. Counting, I'd have bet my best chisel. Counting the cost of the way home.

"Door stays where I leave it," I told him quietly, when I fell back beside him. "Nothing closes unless I close it. Nothing opens unless I open it. You'll get your retreat."

He looked at me with those collector's eyes, weighing whether I was a debt worth trusting. "And if something comes up the stair after us?"

"Then I'll introduce it to a door that bites. I told you—I make promises. I didn't say they were all polite ones."

Something that was nearly a smile crossed his face and died of loneliness. "Hesper," he said. "Why'd you come? Really. Rosalind asked and you said yes before she finished the sentence. Nobody does that."

I considered lying to him. Odo deals in truth as currency; lying to him seemed rude, like paying a moneylender in leaves. But the whole truth was heavier than I wanted to carry down that stair, so I gave him the middle of it.

"Because they erased me," I said. "Eleven hundred and forty doors, and they struck my name out for making one they didn't understand. Down here—" I gestured at the dark ahead, at the vast patient cold, at the country we hadn't seen yet and already belonged to more than I'd ever belonged to the guild—"down here nobody knows my name yet. Which means nobody's crossed it out. That's not noble, Odo. But it's honest."

"It'll do," he said, which from Odo was practically a love sonnet.

* * *

We walked down through the foundations of everything.

That's what the stair turned out to be—not a passage beneath our city but a spine running through both of them, stitched through cellars and catacombs and older things still: a stretch of Roman brickwork that predated Rome's business in our city, a hall of pillars that no architect of ours ever designed, a length of plain packed earth where the stair gave up pretending to be architecture and simply became a path through dark that hummed faintly, like a throat about to sing.

And the whole way down, the cold deepened behind us. I kept checking—I'm a door-maker; I check thresholds the way sailors check weather—and the doorway I'd made in the dye-house cellar was frosting over, rim by rim, white climbing the chalk-lines like frost claiming a windowpane. By the time the light of our world had faded entirely from view, the frost had sealed the arch edge to edge. Not closed. Frosted. As though winter itself had come along behind us and breathed on the glass.

"It's following," Wren said softly. She'd taken to walking with her palm trailing along the wall, reading it. "The cold. It's coming down after us. The stair says—" she paused, head tilted, listening to stone the way other people listen to gossip. "The stair says it's sorry. The stair says it tried to stay warm."

"Stairs apologize now," Odo muttered.

"This one does. This one remembers being loved."

Nobody laughed. That was the thing about our little company—we'd gone past laughing at Wren somewhere around the second hour, the way you go past laughing at a person who turns out to be simply, plainly telling the truth.

Rosalind walked at the front with the lamp and the map and the crow, and I studied her as we descended, because studying people is a hobby of mine; you learn a lot about what kind of door a person is by watching them in the dark. She was afraid. Don't let anyone tell you otherwise, whatever the ballads end up saying about her. Her jaw was set the way jaws get set when they're holding something back, and her free hand kept drifting up to touch Bram's feet on her shoulder, checking he was there, the way you check a wound hasn't reopened. She was twenty-something years old and carrying a dead man's map into a starving world, hunted by a magistrate who thought winter was a policy, disowned by the only family she'd ever had.

And she was *glad*. That was the strange part, the part I only understood later. Underneath the fear, glowing through it like a lamp through frosted glass, she was glad—because for the first time in her careful, hidden, invisible life, she was doing something that could not be entered in a ledger and filed away. She had become, briefly, an impossible thing herself.

I knew the feeling intimately. It was the reason for my exile.

* * *

Near the bottom—if it was the bottom; the stair had stopped promising anything—the carvings changed.

Up near the surface they'd shown processions and coronations, the two realms meeting like friendly neighbors. Here, the figures were different. Here they showed the making of something: many hands passing pieces of a circlet from one to another, dozens of figures, hundreds, each one touching the crown-shape only briefly before handing it on. No figure held it alone. No figure even tried.

Bram stopped Rosalind with a grip of his claws through her coat sleeve, hard enough that she gasped. He stared at that frieze for a long time, head tilted, and then he said—in his stolen, stitched-together voice, assembled from phrases he'd picked up who knows where:

"*Hand it on. Hand it on. Hand it—*" and then the word failed him, collapsed into a raw croak, and he hid his head under his wing like a bird in a storm.

Rosalind stood very still. "Bram," she said gently. "What did they do to you?"

He didn't answer. Crows keep their counsel. But Wren, still trailing her hand along the wall, said quietly: "The stair remembers a guardian. The stair remembers him *before*." And she looked at the crow with an expression I couldn't read, and didn't ask.

Below us, faint but unmistakable, carried up through the dark like smoke through a keyhole: light. Not lamplight. Something cooler, bluer, moving—many lights, moving together, the way a city moves when seen from a hill at dusk.

The last steps opened onto a ledge overlooking a valley that no map of ours had ever admitted existed, and the valley was full of glass towers lit from within by a thousand dimming flames, and above them the sky was the wrong sky, deeper, stranger, hung with constellations arranged in patterns like ledgers, like accounts being kept.

And rising from the towers, thin as thread, I heard it: singing. A city singing to keep itself warm.

"Well," said Odo, in the voice of a man totaling a bill he already knows he can't pay. "Here we are."

Rosalind took one step forward to the lip of the ledge. The blue light caught her face, and the map, and the frost-feathers of the crow on her shoulder, and I saw her mouth move—just once, just to herself, though in the silence of that place we all heard it.

"We're coming," she told the valley. And the valley, cold and starving and beautiful, seemed to lean toward her the way a wall leans toward a door-maker's hand.

Waiting to be asked.
