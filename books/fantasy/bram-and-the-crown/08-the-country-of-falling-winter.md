## 8. The Country of Falling Winter

The first thing I saw in the other realm was a city made of glass, and the second thing I saw was that it was dying.

I want to be honest about my first thought, because honesty is supposed to be the currency down here and I might as well start spending it early. My first thought was not *how terrible*. My first thought was: *the ledgers were wrong.* Every map, every margin note, every dry little footnote in the guild's oldest books described this place as a country of "diminished splendor," as though splendor were a candle someone had simply forgotten to trim. Nobody had written *starving*. Nobody had written *dying*. And I had spent eleven years trusting those margins the way other people trust their own mothers.

The stair Hesper had opened let out onto a hillside above a valley, and the valley held the glass city like a cupped hand holds water—carefully, and with the constant fear of spilling. Towers of blown and spun glass rose in colors that had no business existing: a blue like the moment before crying, a green like the memory of leaves rather than leaves themselves. Light moved inside the towers. Not lamplight. Something slower, more patient, the way light moves through ice or through a sleeping person's eyelids.

"It's beautiful," Wren said, and then immediately, in the small voice she used when she'd said something wrong: "It's beautiful, isn't it?"

"It's beautiful," I agreed. "And look at the edges."

Because that was the thing. The towers at the center of the city still glowed, faintly, stubbornly. But toward the outskirts the glass had gone grey—not dirty, not broken, just *grey*, the way a voice goes when a singer has been singing too long. Whole districts stood dark. Between them, streets of packed snow wound like veins, and along those streets moved figures. Small, from up here. Slow. The kind of slow you get from cold, or hunger, or both.

Bram shifted on my shoulder. He had been quiet since we came through the door—which was itself remarkable, because the crow had not stopped talking since chapter one of our acquaintance, if crows have chapters, which after three days of his company I am prepared to believe they do. Stolen phrases, mostly. *Mind the third step. Mind the third step. Bread and salt, bread and salt.* A vocabulary assembled from other people's lives, worn like a coat made of patches.

But now he went still, and then he said, very clearly, in a voice that was not stolen from anyone:

"I knew that tower."

I looked at him. His head was cocked toward the tallest spire, the one crowned with what looked like a lantern of white fire even at this distance, even through falling snow. His eye—the near one, black and wet and far too knowing for a bird—did not blink.

"You knew it how?" Odo asked. Odo asks questions the way debt-collectors ask them: politely, and with the understanding that an answer will eventually be owed.

Bram didn't answer. He did the thing he does instead of answering, which is to ruffle all his feathers at once so that he looks briefly like a fistful of anger wearing a bird costume, and then settle, and say nothing.

"Right," Hesper said. She had her door-maker's satchel slung across her back and her hands jammed in her armpits, because the cold here was not like the cold above. The cold above is weather. This cold had *intent*. "We're standing on a hillside in a dying world arguing with a crow. Downhill is shelter. Downhill is also every risk we came here to take. All in favor of downhill."

Nobody voted against. We went downhill.

* * *

The gates of the glass city were open.

That should have been the first warning, and to their credit, my misfits noticed it before I did. Gates in any realm are statements. Open gates say *welcome* or they say *there is nothing left worth locking*, and the difference between those two sentences is usually written in the faces of the people on the other side.

There were people on the other side. That surprised me more than it should have. I think some part of me, raised on the guild's careful indifference, had expected the other realm to be empty—a stage set, a rumor, a place that existed only so that ours could have a border. Instead there was a market square inside the gates, and the market was *busy*, and the busy-ness was the saddest thing I had ever seen.

Stalls lined the square under awnings of frosted silk. On the stalls sat goods—or what passed for goods here. Jars of light, actual light, decanted and stoppered. Bundles of summer tied with string. Small glass bells that rang notes I could feel in my teeth. And everywhere, on every stall, handwritten signs in a script like falling water, each one offering the same essential transaction in different words.

WARMTH FOR MEMORY, said the nearest sign. FAIR RATES. LONG MEMORIES PREFERRED.

A woman stood at the stall beneath it. She was old in the way that has nothing to do with years—young face, old eyes, the specific agedness of someone who has given away too much of herself and knows precisely which parts are missing. She held out her hands, palms up, and the stall-keeper held a small brass sphere over them, and the sphere drank something out of her palms that I could not see but could somehow *feel*, the way you feel a draft from a window in another room.

"What's she paying with?" Wren whispered.

"The sign says," Odo murmured back, but his voice had gone flat and careful, the way it did when he was doing arithmetic he didn't like.

"She's paying with her wedding," I said, because I don't always know how I know things but I usually do, and I knew it then, watching the woman's face. She was smiling as the sphere drank. That was the worst part. She was smiling like a person remembering something lovely, except the smile was the last of it, the final coin, and when the sphere finished she looked at her own hands with mild polite puzzlement, as though they belonged to a stranger who had recently done something nice.

She turned away from the stall and walked past us, humming a tune with no memory attached to it.

"That's the covenant," I said. My voice came out strange. "That's what breaking it costs. It's not just winter. It's—" I gestured at the market, at the jars of light, at the whole economy of self-cannibalization. "They're eating themselves to stay warm."

"The guild taught you about this realm?" Hesper asked quietly.

"The guild taught me this realm was *handled*." I heard the bitterness come out of me and didn't try to catch it back. "One paragraph in the founding ledger. 'The lower realm, bound by covenant, tended by the Crown.' Tended. Like a garden. Like something that couldn't be hurt." I looked at the woman who had sold her wedding. "Like something that couldn't starve while we kept our doors locked and our ledger balanced."

Bram made a sound. Not a word. A sound low in his chest, feathers pressed flat, staring at the stall where the brass sphere sat gleaming among its brothers. If a bird can grieve, that was grief. If a man can grieve inside a bird, that was a man grieving.

"We should find lodging," Odo said, gently, in the tone of a man throwing a rope to someone going under. "And information. Preferably lodging that provides information."

"There." Wren pointed.

At the center of the square stood a building that was not glass—stone, squat, ancient, with a sign hanging over its door that swung in the windless cold as though the wind were a courtesy being performed elsewhere. The sign read, in the falling-water script and, beneath it, in trade-tongue: THE INN AT THE END OF TRADE.

Underneath, smaller: ALL PRICES NEGOTIABLE IN TRUTH ONLY.

Odo read it twice. Then he laughed, once, without any humor in it at all. "Of course," he said. "Of course it is."

* * *

The innkeeper was a broad woman with forearms like dock rope and eyes the color of a frozen lake seen from underneath. Her name, she said, was Maren, and she said it the way people say names they've decided to keep using regardless of circumstances. She took one look at the five of us—four travelers and a crow—and said, "Truth first. What are you?"

And here is where I learned the second rule of the other realm, after *everything costs memory*: you cannot lie indoors. Or perhaps you can, but the room won't have it. I felt it happen. Hesper opened her mouth—I watched her, door-maker, professional liar of hinges and thresholds, begin to say *traveling merchants*—and the words died in her throat like moths in frost. Her expression was fascinating. It was the expression of a craftswoman encountering a lock she respected.

"Travelers," I said, which was true, and the room allowed it, and I felt the allowance like a hand briefly on my shoulder. "From the upper realm. Looking for something that was scattered."

Maren's eyebrows climbed. "Scattered," she repeated. "You'll want to be more truthful than that before long. Truth's the only coin here, girl, and small change gets you small rooms." But she jerked her chin toward the common room. "Fire's lit. Sit where you like. Payment at morning. And the bird eats nothing you give it, so don't waste your bread."

Bram, on my shoulder, gave her a look of profound affront and then ate nothing she gave him.

The common room was warm in a way that felt rationed, deliberate, like warmth was being measured out by the spoonful. Other guests sat at the tables: glass-folk, their skin faintly translucent at the wrists and throat, the way ours goes at the ears in certain lights. They spoke softly. Everything here was soft, I was realizing. A loud people would burn more fuel. Even their laughter was economical.

An old man at the corner table caught me watching him and, uninvited, told me his story. Maybe that's what truth-economies do to people; maybe they make everyone a merchant. "Third winter since the deep cold came," he said. "First year, we burned furniture. Second year, we burned the memory of furniture, which is warmer, oddly. This year—" he turned his hands over, translucent at the wrist, "—this year we're burning ourselves. Small things first. The name of a childhood dog. The smell of a particular rain. You'd be amazed how much heat is in a smell." He smiled at me, kindly, terribly. "You're from above. From behind the doors. Tell me—do they remember us up there? At all?"

And I, ledger-keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things, keeper of secrets, trained from childhood in the gentle arts of omission, found that the room would not let me lie.

"No," I said. "Not really. There's a paragraph."

He nodded slowly, and there was no anger in it, which was worse than anger. "A paragraph," he said. "We got a paragraph. Well." He raised an imaginary glass to me. "It's more than the Crown left us."

Bram's claws tightened on my shoulder, just slightly, and I reached up and touched his breastbone with one finger, and he leaned into it, the smallest weight in the world carrying the largest.

* * *

We took a room—two rooms, one for the others, one for me and the crow, because Bram had opinions about sleeping arrangements and his opinions involved my collarbone. Through the frost-feathered window I watched the glass city dim by degrees. Tower by tower, the inner light guttered. Where it went out, the dark that replaced it wasn't ordinary dark. It had texture. It felt *watched*.

"They traded tonight," Wren said from the doorway. She'd crept up the stairs; the girl talks to locks and apparently walks like one too, silently until she chooses otherwise. "In the market. I counted. Forty-one transactions before the stalls closed. Do you know what most of them bought?"

"Warmth," I said.

"Warmth," she agreed. "But do you know what they paid? I listened. Names. First kisses. The layout of their mother's kitchen. One man sold the memory of his daughter's laugh, Rosalind. He got one jar of light for it. One jar. And he haggled *down*—he asked for less, because he said he couldn't carry it another winter anyway." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she wiped her nose furiously with her sleeve, angry at herself for cracking. "The elders said this realm was handled."

"The elders," I said, "have never once in four hundred years gone downstairs."

Bram stirred against my neck. And then, in the dark, in a voice assembled from a hundred borrowed mouths but aimed, for once, at something entirely his own, he said:

"The lantern tower. In the middle. That was the Hall of Keeping." A pause. Snow ticked against the glass. "I hung the light there myself. It doesn't go out. It's not supposed to be able to go out."

"But it's dimming," Wren whispered.

"It's dimming," Bram agreed, "because what it burns isn't oil."

I looked at the tower, at its crown of failing white fire, and I understood—or began to—with a cold that had nothing to do with the season. The light was burning memory. The city's memory. Perhaps the realm's. The covenant hadn't merely broken; it was being *consumed*, and everything these people sold in the market, every jar of light, every bundle of summer, was drawn from the same shrinking account.

Somewhere out in that beautiful, starving dark, five shards of a crown lay hidden, and a magistrate in winter-grey was collecting them, and the ledger of this world was running toward zero.

"Bram," I said quietly. "Tomorrow, you're going to tell me about the first shard."

The crow was silent a long moment. When he finally answered, he used a phrase I'd never heard him use before, one that fit his mouth like it had been waiting in it for years:

"Yes," he said. "I suppose I owe you that much truth."

Below us, in the market square, the last stall closed its shutters, and a woman who no longer remembered her wedding walked home through the snow, humming.
