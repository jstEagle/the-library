## 9. The Inn at the End of Trade

I should explain something about favors before I tell you what I did at the inn, because you will not otherwise understand the size of it.

A favor is not a kindness. A kindness is free and dies quickly, like a mayfly. A favor is a debt wearing its good coat. When someone does you a service and you say *I owe you one*, you have just signed a contract in invisible ink, and somewhere — I am convinced of this, professionally — a ledger writes itself. I have spent thirty-one years collecting those signatures. I know where they live.

My name is Odo Fenwick, and before the winter took everything worth taking, I was the Guild of Impossible Things' collector of debts. Not money. Money is for people who lack imagination. I collected the other kind: the promise a clockmaker made to his dead wife's ghost; the debt a bridge owed to the river it had bullied for two centuries; the favor a mayor once promised to a beggar who turned out to be a saint on holiday. I kept them in a book bound in grey calfskin, and I kept them honestly, which is more than most collectors can say, and I kept them *all*, which is less forgivable.

The company had been walking through the falling country for six days when we saw the inn.

You could see it from a long way off because it was the only thing in that white waste that was lit from inside. Everything else in the realm of Falling Winter glowed faintly and wrongly, like a lamp with a dying wick, but the inn burned warm and gold against the snow, and above its door hung a sign that read:

**THE END OF TRADE**
*All things bought. All things sold. Coin accepted nowhere.*

"Coin accepted nowhere," Hesper read aloud, squinting. "That's either very honest or very rude."

"It's both," said Wren, who was fourteen and had opinions about doors the way other children have opinions about sweets. "Look at the hinges. That's honest work. But the sign is hung crooked on purpose, so you'll comment on it, so they can hear your voice before you come in. It's a listening door."

Hesper looked at Wren with the expression of a woman realizing she has brought a dangerous animal along by mistake. "Remind me never to build anything near you."

Rosalind said nothing. She had said very little since the river country, since Odo — since I — had gone down like a sack of turnips putting myself between Wren and a shard-trap that Vane's people had left in an abandoned chapel. My ribs were still strapped. My pride was still worse. Rosalind stood looking at the inn with her ledger-keeper's face on, which is the face of a person doing arithmetic about things that cannot be counted.

"Bram?" she said.

The crow on her shoulder ruffled his feathers. He had been quiet all day, quieter than usual, and he had been unusually quiet since the glass city, since the glassmaker had called him by a name none of us had spoken aloud. Now he hopped twice along Rosalind's shoulder and croaked: *"Warmth for truth. Warmth for truth. All things bought."* Stolen phrases, always stolen. The crow spoke entirely in things other people had said, like a magpie of conversation, and it should have been comic and usually was, but just then it sounded like a warning delivered by a bird who knew the establishment personally.

"We have no coin anyway," I pointed out. "We haven't had coin since the second day. I checked everyone's pockets while they slept, purely as a professional courtesy."

"You *what*," said Wren.

"I returned everything."

"That's not the *point*, Odo—"

"The point," Rosalind said, "is that we're out of food, the cold eats memories past the meridian, and that's the only light for forty miles. We go in." She paused. "And we tell the truth."

I want it noted, for the record I no longer keep, that I flinched.

* * *

The innkeeper was a broad woman with forearms like a blacksmith's and eyes like a woman who has heard every lie ever told and filed them alphabetically. Her name, she said, was Mother Quill, and she said it the way other people say *and I'll thank you to remember it*. The common room was low-beamed and crowded with travelers of the wrong sort of quiet — the realm's folk, glass-skinned and dim, trading warmth for memory in murmured lots, and here and there a mortal face gone grey with cold and homesickness.

She sat us at a table near the fire and set down five cups of something steaming without being asked.

"No charge," she said, "for the first cup. The first cup is how I learn what you are." She looked at each of us in turn, lingering on Bram, who pretended to be an ordinary crow with the commitment of a professional liar. "Then we trade. In my house, the only coin is truth. You pay for what you take, in things actually true, and I decide what your truths are worth. Lie to me and the fire goes cold. That's not a threat, dear. That's the architecture."

Wren, delighted: "What happens if we say something true by accident?"

"Then you've paid for your soup and don't know it. Happens constantly. Children eat free here." She smiled at Wren, who beamed, then visibly wondered whether that had been a trap.

Hesper paid for her bed with the truth that she had once built a door so beautiful her own guild expelled her for making the others look bad, and that she had hated them for eleven years and missed them every morning. Mother Quill nodded slowly and said the room upstairs was hers, and that the truth was worth more than the bed, and that Hesper shouldn't spend the change all in one place.

Wren paid for supper by admitting she talked to locks because locks were the only things that answered honestly — *you're open or you're shut, none of this maybe* — and that sometimes, back in the guildhall, when the elders forgot she existed for weeks at a time, the locks had been the only ones who noticed she'd come home. Mother Quill's eyes went soft and hard at once, the way a mother's do, and Wren got seconds of everything.

Rosalind paid last and least and most. She told the room that she had copied a dead man's map after her guild ordered it burned, that she had lied to her elders, that she had led four people into a dying world on the strength of a theft, and that if the company died out here it would be arithmetic she would owe forever. The fire flared bright enough to throw shadows on the rafters. Mother Quill looked at her a long moment and said, "Ledger-keepers make the best liars and the worst customers. Your truths are heavy, girl. Eat."

Which left me.

Mother Quill turned to me and folded her arms, and I understood that I had been saved for last the way a surgeon saves the difficult part.

"And you, collector. What will you give me for their beds?"

Now. Here is what I had planned to say. I had planned to say: *I collect favors, madam, and I hold one that is very old and very fine, and I will spend it gladly.* Which was even true. What I had not planned — what no amount of professional preparation could have planned for — was the way the whole room went quiet when she asked, the way the fire leaned toward me like a creditor, the way Rosalind looked at me with those grey eyes that had never once asked me for anything and therefore demanded everything.

Because here is the other thing about the favor I meant to spend. It was not mine to spend freely, and it was not a small one, and spending it would cost me more than a bed.

Thirty years ago — I was nine, and hungry, and my father had just been taken by the debt-men for owing the wrong person — a woman found me in the ash-market with my hand in her pie. She didn't call the watch. She sat me down, fed me, and said, "You steal well. Steal better." Then she taught me. For three winters she taught me what a debt was, what a promise weighed, how to find the true price of a thing. And when she left, she said: "One day I'll ask you for a favor, Odo Fenwick, and you'll pay it, whatever it is, however it costs. That's the tuition."

Her name was Maren Vale. And eleven days before I met a dying stranger on a guild doorstep, Maren Vale had sent me a letter from this realm, from this side of the meridian, asking me to come and bring nothing and tell no one.

I never answered. I joined a company of misfits instead, and told myself it was loyalty, and told myself it was adventure, and told myself every comfortable lie a collector tells himself about other people's debts while ignoring his own.

So when Mother Quill asked what I would pay, the truth came out of me sideways, the way truths do when you've been sitting on them too long and the fire is leaning in.

"I came here to escape a debt," I said.

The words came out ugly and plain. The fire crackled. Somewhere a glass-skinned traveler stopped mid-murmur.

"Not to finish the quest. Not out of love for any of you, though — well. Though. A woman named Maren Vale taught me everything I know and asked me to come to this realm, and I ignored her letter because answering it would mean admitting I owed anyone anything, and I have built thirty years on never admitting that. I collect favors because I'm terrified of owing them. Every signature in my book is a wall between me and the fact that I am the most indebted man in two realms. I joined this company to run away, and I've been running toward the same woman the entire time, and I don't know if she's alive, and I was going to spend her favor tonight to buy your beds without telling any of you why it hurt."

Silence. The bad kind. The kind with weight.

Wren was staring at me. Hesper had stopped chewing. Rosalind — Rosalind reached across the table and put her hand flat on my ledger-hand, the one that hadn't held a book in weeks, and said nothing at all, which from a ledger-keeper is the loudest possible thing.

Mother Quill let the silence sit until it had paid full fare. Then she said, quietly, "Maren Vale stayed here. Four months ago. Heading north, toward the meridian, alone." She refilled my cup. "Your beds are paid, collector. That truth was worth a palace. Don't ever sell yourself short again — it's the one debt nobody collects fairly."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat by the fire with the cup going cold in my hands, and one by one the company drifted down instead of up, the way people do when a thing has been said that needs sitting with.

Hesper came first, sat beside me, and said, "Eleven years I told myself I left the guild. Truth is they pushed and I fell and I called it leaving. So." She knocked her cup against mine. "Fellow debtor."

Wren curled up on the bench with her head against my arm, which she had never done, and mumbled, "Locks wouldn't lie about you. You're shut but you're not locked," and was asleep before I worked out whether it was an insult.

Bram landed on the table, looked at me with one eye and then the other, and produced, from wherever crows keep such things, a phrase I had never heard him use: *"Tuition paid in full."*

Rosalind came last. She sat across from me and studied the fire.

"Maren Vale," she said. "North. Toward the meridian. Toward the third shard."

"Toward the river frozen mid-song, if the map's honest. Yes."

"She went alone."

"She always traveled alone. Said companies were just debts with legs."

Rosalind almost smiled. "She sounds terrible."

"She was magnificent. It's the same thing."

"Odo." She waited until I looked at her. "Tomorrow we walk north, and we walk it together, and if she's alive we find her, and if she isn't—" She stopped, and chose her words the way she chose entries, precisely and without mercy. "If she isn't, then you owe her a finished quest instead of an answer. Debts don't die with the creditor. You taught me that."

"I taught you that?"

"In chapter and verse. You lectured Hesper for an hour about the bridge and the river."

"That was different. Bridges can't argue."

But I was smiling, God help me, and she saw it, and let me keep it.

It was near dawn when the trouble came — because there is always trouble, and in my experience it prefers to arrive just when a man has finally paid something honest. Boots on the stair, grey cloaks, the smell of magistrate's wax and cold iron. Mother Quill rose from behind her bar with the unhurried menace of a woman whose house runs on truth, and the lead figure stopped in the doorway, stamped the snow from his boots, and smiled at our table with recognition.

"Well," said the grey-cloaked man, consulting a paper he did not need to consult. "Odo Fenwick. Collector of favors. Your guild wrote to us about you — disavowed, I'm told. Which makes you, legally speaking, no one at all." He folded the paper away. "And no ones, Master Fenwick, are so much easier to arrest."
