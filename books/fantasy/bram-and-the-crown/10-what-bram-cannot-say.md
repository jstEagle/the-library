## 10. What Bram Cannot Say

The crow had been with us for three days by the time I admitted, privately and against every instinct of a trained ledger-keeper, that he was not an ordinary bird.

I want it noted that I resisted the conclusion. I am a professional. I have catalogued a bottled echo, a debt owed by the moon (outstanding, interest accruing), and a hat that remembers its previous owners' arguments, so I know what impossible looks like when it files itself properly. Crows are clever. Crows hold grudges and count better than most guild accountants. Crows do not, as a rule, say *run*.

But on the third night, camped in the lee of a fallen archway while Hesper's fire did its stubborn best against the falling cold, the crow had hopped onto my pack, fixed me with one yellow eye, and said — in a voice like pages tearing — "*Bram.*"

Not his name, I understood later. An instruction. A door held open. But at the time I dropped my tin cup into the snow and Odo laughed so hard he had to sit down, which for Odo meant sitting down on someone else's bedroll, which nearly ended our journey there and then in a dispute of favors.

"You knew," I said to Wren, who was feeding the crow crumbs with the unbothered serenity of a girl who talks to locks and considers talking birds barely worth remarking.

"Course I knew," Wren said. "He's been saying things since the guildhall. You just weren't listening."

"He's been saying things since—"

"Most people aren't listening," Wren said, with the gentle cruelty only apprentices can manage, and went back to her crumbs.

Hesper looked up from the fire. She is not a talkative woman — she makes doors, and doors mostly require patience rather than chatter — but when she did speak, it was to the point, as doors are. "Ask him what he wants."

"I have asked him."

"Ask him properly," she said, and put another stick on the fire.

That is how I came to be sitting cross-legged in the snow at midnight, facing a crow, with my company pretending very badly to sleep around me. The cold here was not weather. I want to be clear about that. In the other realm, the winter had stopped being a season sometime in the last century and become something more like an appetite. It leaned on us. It listened.

"Bram," I said. "If that's your name. Or his name. Or — whoever you were." I felt ridiculous. I had felt ridiculous for most of this quest and expected to continue. "You gave me the map. You died on my doorstep. Well — you fell off my doorstep, technically, and then died, but the doorstep was involved. And now you're following me across a dying realm in the body of a bird that says one word, and I would like — " My voice did something embarrassing at the end there, and I let it, because there was no one awake to hear. "I would like to know what I'm carrying. Please."

The crow regarded me for a long moment. Then he stepped off the pack, walked to the flat stone before the fire, and did something I will be describing in ledgers until I die: he scratched.

He drew lines. Deliberate ones. A circle broken into pieces. Five strokes radiating from it like a child's sun or a shattered wheel. Then a shape I recognized, because I had copied it in secret ink at two in the morning with a stolen candle and a guilty heart — the map's border, the winding road, the little mark at the end of it that I had taken for a mountain.

It wasn't a mountain. It was a forge. Though I didn't know that yet, not properly; I knew it the way you know a word in a language you don't speak, by its weight alone.

"Five," I said slowly. "Five of... those?"

"*Five,*" the crow agreed, in the voice of some long-dead clerk whose phrases he had swallowed whole, and then, in a woman's voice, warm and strange: "*the crown, the crown, remember the covenant*," and then in his own torn-page voice, "*broken. scattered. run.*"

Odo had given up pretending. He sat up, blanket around his shoulders like a disapproving magistrate. "It talks in quotations," he said. "Like a debt-collector reading back your file."

"Thank you, Odo, that's exactly the comparison I needed."

"You're welcome." He frowned at the scratched lines. "Five shards means five debts, if it's shards of something sworn. That's how covenants work. Every promise has a price attached and a place where it's kept."

"You collect favors, not theology."

"Favors are theology," Odo said, and lay back down, and I honestly could not tell if he was joking.

I sat before the scratched stone a long while after the others slept. The crow settled onto my knee, which weighed nothing and everything, and I talked to him the way lonely ledger-keepers talk — quietly, and about myself, and with apologies built into every third sentence.

* * *

The dream came that same night, though *night* is a courtesy; in the Country of Falling Winter the dark simply deepens without ever committing to ending.

I dreamed I was standing in a hall made of ice and gold — no, not made. *Dressed* in them, the way a bride is dressed, or a corpse. It was vast. It smelled of oaths. Down its length ran a table of black stone, and above the far end hung a crown that was not metal at all. I cannot describe it better than that, and I have tried. It was not metal, not light, not sound, but something that partook of all three the way a chord partakes of notes. When I looked at it, I felt every promise I had ever kept hum in my chest like struck bells, and every promise I had ever broken ache in my teeth.

Someone stood beneath it. A man, tall, grey-eyed, dressed plainly for all that the hall was gilded — dressed plainly the way rich men dress plainly, out of confidence rather than humility. His hair was dark going white at the temples. His hands were a guardian's hands, scarred along the palms.

He turned and saw me, and his face did three things too quickly for me to name them all. Grief. Recognition. Relief, terrible and immediate, like a drowning man seeing a rope thrown to someone else.

"You're the girl from the ledger-house," he said. His voice was wrong for the hall — too human, too tired. "The one with the ink on her fingers. Good. Ink-fingered girls are the only ones who listen."

"You're Bram," I said. It wasn't a guess.

"I was." He smiled, and the smile was older than the rest of him. "What's left of me is doing a poor job of explaining things to you from inside a feathered body, so forgive the intrusion. Dreams between the bound and their keepers run cheaper than words. And words, for me, are..." He flexed his scarred hands. "Spent. Mostly spent."

"What happened to you?"

"Vane happened." The name dropped through the hall like a stone through still water, and the crown above the table flickered. "Serath Vane. Magistrate of the Meridian Bench, Keeper of Judgments, and — once, in a life I try not to remember — my friend." He said the last word the way you'd say *gangrene*. "You've heard his name by now. The letters found it fast enough."

"They found it. They didn't explain it."

"No. Explaining was always my job, and look how well I did." He walked toward me down the hall, and I noticed as he moved that the walls were not decorated with carvings but with *writing* — thousands upon thousands of names, in a hundred hands, spiraling up into the dark. "This was the Hall of Covenants. Every name on these walls is a voice in the oath. Kings, yes, at the start — realms love kings, they're convenient furniture — but over the centuries, ordinary voices. Farmers. Glassmakers. Door-makers. Ledger-keepers." He stopped before me. "The Crown doesn't belong to anyone, Rosalind Almeida. That's the whole secret. It belongs to everyone, which is infinitely harder, and so everyone assumed it must belong to someone, and one day a man arrived who agreed with them."

"Vane stole it."

"Vane *reasoned* his way to stealing it, which is worse and takes longer." Bram's mouth twisted. "He believed — believes, don't imagine for a moment he doubts himself — that the covenant failed because authority was divided. Too many voices, too much mercy, too much argument while the winter came. He thought one crown and one judge could hold the cold back through sheer decisiveness. So he came for it in the night with writs and knives, and I was the last guardian on duty, because I had drawn the short watch, because even miracles run on bureaucracy."

I laughed. I couldn't help it. Something in the dream laughed with me.

"I couldn't stop him taking the Crown," Bram went on, quieter. "But I could stop him keeping it. A crown of covenants isn't one thing, whatever it looks like. Break the oath and it breaks with it. I shattered it — five shards, five anchors, five of the oldest promises in the binding — and I scattered them before Vane got his grey hands on more than frostbite and fury. Do you understand what scattering cost? Each shard took a piece of me to move. A memory. A year. A face." He raised his scarred hands, and I watched, in the way of dreams, as they shortened, darkened, feathered. "The last piece went into the map. The map knows where all five are, because the map *is* the fifth — no. Forget I said that. Not yet. Not yet."

"The map shows one treasure," I said. "One mark. One road."

"The map shows one road because roads are singular. Destinations are not." He was mostly crow-shaped now, and his voice was fraying into borrowed scraps: "*five, five, remember the covenant, broken, run.*" But the eyes stayed his. "Listen to me, keeper of impossible things. Vane has been hunting the shards for sixty years and found none, because they hide behind debts, and he refuses to pay debts — paying implies the system binds him, and binding is precisely what he intends to be exempt from. You and your misfits have something he doesn't. You can *owe*. Use that. The first shard sits with a glassmaker in the city of Ambrin, and she has been owed a protection long promised and never delivered, and she is angry enough to freeze hell over, and she is right to be."

"Ambrin. The glass cities." I had seen them from the ridge — a horizon of dimming spires like candle flames suffocating under glass domes.

"Go there. Pay her debt. And Rosalind—" the crow's voice now, entirely, except it wasn't, because crows don't grieve, "—when you find the others, when you learn what I was and what I failed to protect, don't carry it like guilt. Carry it like inventory. Guilt sits still. Inventory moves."

The hall was dissolving. The names on the walls were lifting off like ash in an updraft, and among them, absurdly, I saw my own name being written in fresh ink by no hand at all.

"Why me?" I called into the whitening air. "Why give it to me? You could have gone to the elders. The guild. Anyone with standing."

The answer came from everywhere, in the torn-page voice, in the dead clerk's voice, in a woman's warm voice, in his own:

"*Because the elders burned me. And you copied.*

* * *

I woke with frost on my eyelashes and the crow's feet pressed to my collarbone, and I lay still a long moment, doing what ledger-keepers do: reconciling accounts.

Here is what I had, entered plainly. One map that was not what it seemed. Five shards, not one treasure. A guardian reduced to borrowed words, bound by a sacrifice I could now name, and a thief who believed order was a thing one man could own. A company of four misfits and a bird, camped in the ruin of somebody's faith, in a realm starving because a promise made by many had been broken by few.

And here is what I owed, which was the longer column. I owed a dead man the truth spoken aloud. I owed a glassmaker I had never met a protection promised before my grandmother was born. I owed Wren honesty instead of protective silence, and Hesper answers instead of orders, and Odo — well, Odo kept accounts too, and he'd invoice me eventually either way.

"Grief is inventory," I said to the grey sky, testing it. It sounded like something a sensible person would embroider on a pillow and never once mean. But Bram hadn't said it to be comforting. He'd said it the way you reclassify a loss in a ledger: not erased, not forgiven, just *moved* — from the column of things done to you into the column of things you are responsible for carrying onward.

I got up. I woke the company, because the cold was leaning harder and because for the first time since Bram died on my doorstep, I knew where we were going and why, and knowing is a thing that goes stale if you hoard it.

"Change of understanding," I announced, over Hesper's banked fire. "The map doesn't lead to one prize. It leads to five — shards of the Crown of Covenants, scattered by the man our crow used to be, hidden behind debts that Vane won't pay and we can. The first one is in the glass city of Ambrin, held by a glassmaker whom the guild once swore to protect and never did."

Silence. Snow ticking down. Odo's eyebrows performing a small interpretive dance.

"So we're not treasure hunters," Wren said at last, delighted. "We're *debt collectors*."

"We're debt *payers*," I corrected.

"That's much rarer," said Odo gravely, and began packing.

Hesper kicked snow over the coals and stood, unfolding to her full imposing height, and looked east toward the smothered glow of the glass cities. "Then we'd best hurry," she said. "In my experience, people who've been owed a long time don't greet you warmly."

She was, as usual, right — though none of us, trudging toward the dim spires of Ambrin, could yet imagine just how coldly a righteous anger could receive the heirs of a broken promise.
