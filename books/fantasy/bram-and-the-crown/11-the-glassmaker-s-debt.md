## 11. The Glassmaker's Debt

I have always believed that locks are honest things. A lock tells you exactly what it wants: to be closed, or to be opened, and never both at once. People are not like locks. People will tell you they want one thing with their whole mouth while their hands are doing another entirely.

Take the city we walked into that morning, if you could call it walking and if you could call it a morning, since the sky above Spireglass was the color of an old bruise deciding whether to fade. The city was dying — everyone said so, in the way people say things when saying them is easier than screaming. Its towers were made of glass blown thin as breath, and in each tower lived a family, and in each family someone had sold a memory to buy fuel for the winter fires. You could see it in the glass itself. Grief-fogged panes. Windows gone milky where a wedding had been traded away, or a mother's face, or the smell of rain.

Wren, I told myself, keep your mouth shut. Wren, you are an apprentice who talks to locks, and locks do not need you narrating at them.

But I talk to everything, really. It's a habit. When you grow up the way I did — passed along like a mislabeled parcel until the Guild of Impossible Things finally lost track of me in a cellar full of doors nobody wanted — you learn that things listen better than people. And so as we came down the long causeway into Spireglass, I said hello to the gate, and the gate creaked back at me in a voice like a cracked bell, and what it said was: *too late, too late, too late.*

"Cheerful place," Odo muttered beside me. He had his ledger out, which he always did when he was nervous. Odo collects favors the way other men collect coins, and he counts them when the world frightens him. "No wonder nobody guards the shard. Nobody's left standing."

"Somebody's left," Hesper said. She pointed with her chin — she never wasted a hand on pointing — at the one tower at the city's heart whose glass was still clear. Not just clear. *Bright.* Every pane sang faintly when the wind moved through it, a sound like wet fingers on wet rims, like something remembered on purpose instead of by accident.

"The glassmaker," Rosalind said.

She'd been quiet most of the crossing, carrying the map against her chest under her coat, Bram the crow riding her shoulder with his head cocked like a weathervane in a mood. Now she looked at the bright tower the way she looked at ledgers, I thought — like something that could be read, if only she were brave enough and stubborn enough, and Rosalind Almeida was nothing if not both.

"She kept everything," Rosalind said softly. "Look at it. Everyone else sold. She didn't sell a single memory."

"That," said Hesper, "is either very hopeful or very expensive."

Bram said, from Rosalind's shoulder, in his stolen-phrase voice — fragments of other people's sentences, worn like borrowed coats — "*Honor the debt. Honor the debt. She'll ask.*"

We all stopped walking.

He didn't say much, our crow. When he did say anything, it came out wrong-shaped, phrases lifted from conversations he'd overheard decades ago, or maybe centuries. But those four words landed among us like coins dropped in a silent church.

"She'll ask," Rosalind repeated. "Who?"

Bram ruffled his feathers and would not say more, because of course he wouldn't. That was the arrangement with Bram: he gave you exactly enough to keep walking and never one word more.

* * *

The door of the bright tower was the finest piece of craftwork I had ever seen, and I include in that assessment every door in the guildhall beneath the city above, including the one that opens onto Tuesday. It was glass, but not glass the way we know it — this glass had been taught things. It knew about warmth; there was frost on every surface in Spireglass except this door. It knew about welcome; the handle shaped itself to my hand before I'd fully decided to reach for it, which is presumptuous in a door and also rather lovely.

"Hello," I whispered to it. "We've come about the shard."

The handle pulsed once under my palm, warm as a heartbeat, and the door opened itself — no lock at all, which unsettled me more than any lock ever had. A door without a lock isn't trusting. It's *decided.*

Inside, the tower was full of light that had nowhere to be. Glass hung everywhere: chandeliers like frozen fountains, windows within windows, a staircase of translucent steps going up into a brightness that had no business existing in this starving realm. And at the top of the stairs, working at a bench with tools that glowed faintly amber, sat the smallest old woman I have ever seen. Her hands were enormous in proportion to the rest of her, scarred and burn-speckled, moving with the unhurried certainty of someone who has made ten thousand of a thing and intends to make ten thousand more.

"You're loud," she said, without turning around. "All five of you, plus the bird. Loudest company to cross my threshold in forty years."

Rosalind went up the stairs first, because Rosalind always goes first, though she pretends it's accidental. "Madam Glassmaker. We—"

"I know why you've come." Now she turned, and her eyes were the pale grey-green of sea glass, and old, old grief had polished them smooth. "Word travels ahead of travelers in a small country. A crow, a map, five strangers asking after the Crown of Covenants." She set down her tool. "You want my shard."

"We want to *reforge* it," Rosalind said. "Not wield it. There's a difference. We think it's the whole difference."

"Do you." The glassmaker looked at her for a long moment. Then at Hesper, who stood with her arms crossed like a woman guarding a doorway even when nowhere near one. Then at Odo, who had instinctively hidden his ledger behind his back, which fooled no one. Then at me.

"And the boy talks to doors," she said.

"Locks, mostly," I said. "Doors when they're friendly."

That almost got a smile. Almost. Her mouth moved toward it and then remembered itself. "My name is Ilvane. I was the glasswright of this city before it was a graveyard with streetlamps. Sit down, all of you. If we're going to speak of debts, we'll do it properly, sitting, with tea I can't afford to serve you."

She served us tea anyway. It was thin and smoky and tasted faintly of pine, and I drank mine too fast because my hands wanted something to do.

* * *

Here is what Ilvane told us, while the wind played her bright windows and the dying city listened through its fogged panes.

Ninety years ago — before the winter, before Vane, before the covenant broke — the two realms still touched. Doors between them were rare but real, and the Guild of Impossible Things held the keys to most of them. And ninety years ago, the borderlands of this realm began to fail. Not dramatically. Slowly. Summers arriving late, then later, then not at all. The folk of Spireglass sent envoys up the stair that wasn't there, and the envoys found the guildhall, and the guild heard their petition.

"They promised," Ilvane said. She was looking into her cup, not at us. "Three elders came down themselves — I was an apprentice, I carried their coats. They stood in the assembly square and they swore it on their own ledger: *the Guild of Impossible Things will not let this realm freeze alone.* Their words. Sworn and entered in the book."

"What happened?" Rosalind asked, though I could hear in her voice that she already suspected.

"Winter came faster than anyone guessed. And the guild—" Ilvane set down her cup with terrible care, the way you set down something you might otherwise throw. "The guild sealed its doors. All of them. Every stair that wasn't there became a stair that truly wasn't. They called it *prudence.* They called it *protecting the guild.* Two hundred of my neighbors died of cold in a city that used to trade in light, and your guild called it prudence."

Nobody spoke. Bram shifted on Rosalind's shoulder, and made a low sound, not quite a word.

"My parents froze in the winter of the third year," Ilvane went on. "My brother sold his memories of them to buy heat, and then forgot he'd had a brother, and now he tends lamps in the fog-district and doesn't know my name when I pass. I stayed here. I kept my memories because somebody in this city had to remember what happened, and remembering is the last trade worth making." She looked up, and her sea-glass eyes fixed on Rosalind. "And I took a shard of the broken Crown, because the last guardian begged me to hide it, and because I thought — foolishly, perhaps — that one day someone would come who understood that promises don't expire just because they become inconvenient."

Rosalind had gone very pale. "You want the debt honored."

"I want the debt *acknowledged* first," Ilvane said. "Honored second. In that order, girl, and don't you dare reverse them. Anyone can pay a debt to make it go away. Paying it honestly means saying aloud what was done."

* * *

What happened next, I want to tell properly, because I watched Rosalind Almeida choose, and I have rarely seen anyone choose so completely.

Odo leaned forward first — of course he did; debts are his native language. "The elders who made that oath are dead or scattered," he said gently. "Legally speaking, a debt sworn by a dissolved party—"

"Odo," Rosalind said, and he stopped.

She stood up. She was the youngest person in that room by forty years and the least important member of a guild that hadn't yet noticed she existed, and she stood up anyway, and I saw Bram go very still on her shoulder, watching her the way you watch a fire catch.

"The guild broke its oath," she said. "There's no version of the story where it didn't. I can't tell you why they did it except that they were afraid, and fear is the only currency the guild has never run short of. But you're right that acknowledgment comes first. So: acknowledge. The Guild of Impossible Things promised your realm protection and abandoned you to die of it. That is in the ledger whether or not anyone wrote it down. I'm writing it down now." Her voice shook once and steadied. "And I can't honor it as the guild, because the guild disowned us the moment we climbed down that stair. But I can promise you this: whatever guild comes out of this quest — and one will, even if it's only five misfits and a crow — its first act will be to open doors here. Not sealed ones. Honest ones. We will come back, Ilvane, and we won't be prudence this time."

The wind moved through the tower, and all the bright windows rang together, one long chord, and I swear the whole city heard her.

Ilvane studied her for a long time. "You're nobody," she said at last. "No elder. No magistrate. No guardian of anything."

"Yes," Rosalind said. "That's rather the point, isn't it? Kings and magistrates broke the covenant. Maybe it takes nobodies to mend it."

Something in Ilvane's face let go — not forgiveness, not yet, forgiveness is a longer project than that, but the beginning of belief. She rose, joints cracking like green wood, and crossed to the far wall where a window hung that was different from all the others: smaller, older, its glass thick and slightly green at the edges.

"This shard has kept my city's memory alive," she said. "It's why my tower stays bright while everything else fogs over. Take it, and Spireglass starts forgetting again. That's the true price, girl. Not my grief. My *neighbors'*. They'll lose the last clear mirror they have." She drew a deep breath. "So I'll give it to you on one condition, and it's not payment. Payment implies it ends. This doesn't end. You carry this city with you. Every mile. When you swear your new covenant, you swear it with Spireglass in your mouth. Their names, their losses, their claim on you. Can you do that?"

Rosalind looked at the fogged towers beyond the window, hundreds of them, thousands of forgotten weddings and mothers' faces and smells of rain.

"Yes," she said. "Teach me the names."

* * *

It took an hour. Ilvane recited, Rosalind repeated, and I helped where I could — names stick better if you whisper them to something, so I murmured them into the tower's doorframe, and the glass held them the way glass holds light. Hesper learned them grimly, like drill commands. Odo wrote them in his ledger, in the front, before his precious favors, which for Odo is roughly equivalent to carving them on his heart.

When the last name was given, Ilvane opened the old green-edged window — no lock, of course, because it had never needed one — and reached inside the space between the panes, where no space should have been, and drew out the shard.

Oh, friends. I have picked locks since I was six and I thought I understood what small clever things were. The shard was the size of a knife-blade and it was not merely crystal, it was *covenant-stuff*, and even broken it hummed with the memory of an oath, and holding it near felt like standing next to someone about to make a promise they intend to keep forever. It was warm. Everything in that realm was cold except the shard and Rosalind's stubbornness.

Ilvane put it in Rosalind's hands and folded the girl's fingers closed over it with her huge scarred ones.

"Ninety years," she said quietly. "Don't take ninety more."

"We won't," Rosalind said.

Bram hopped down onto the workbench, and did something I'd never seen him do: he bowed. Low, wings spread, the bow of a man to a lady, and Ilvane looked at him a long moment with her sea-glass eyes and said, "Hello, Guardian. I wondered if you'd find your way back."

"*Found,*" Bram agreed sadly, in a voice that might once have belonged to a young man, and Ilvane did not ask further, because in this realm they know better than to press at wounds.

* * *

We left Spireglass at what the locals generously called dusk, the bruised sky darkening toward a darkness that never quite finished arriving. At the gate, the same cracked-bell voice said *too late, too late* — but slower now, almost thoughtful, and I patted the gatepost on the way past and told it: *not anymore.*

We made camp in the lee of the causeway wall, and I was on first watch, which suited me; locks and I understand night work. I was turning the shard's case over in my hands — Rosalind had trusted me with it, which I was trying very hard not to make a whole personality about — when Hesper appeared out of the dark at my elbow, silent as a door opening in socks.

"You did well in there," she said.

"I mostly whispered at architecture."

"You listened. That's the rare skill." She settled beside me, and for a while neither of us said anything, which with Hesper is practically a friendship ceremony. Then: "Look east."

I looked. On the horizon, where the meridian waste began, the sky was wrong. Not storm-wrong. *Ordered*-wrong — a vast grey rectangle of perfectly uniform dark, like a door laid flat against the world, like frost spreading in a straight line.

"That wasn't there yesterday," I said.

"No," Hesper said. "And there's a rider coming up the causeway road. Grey coat. Alone." She rose, already reaching for her tools. "Wake the others, Wren. Whatever that is out there —"

From somewhere beyond the hills came a sound I had only read about in the guild's oldest books: a horn, blown three times, in the cadence the old ledgers called *summons of the frozen court* —

"— I suspect it's looking for us."
