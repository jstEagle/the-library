## 12. Vane's Grey Court

I have been called many things since I took the Crown's shards for my own: thief, tyrant, the Winter That Walks. The last is my favorite, if only because it is nearly accurate. But the word that stings — the one the glass cities whisper behind their frosted shutters when they think the wind will carry it away before I hear it — is *unjust*.

Unjust. As though justice were a thing one could have too much of.

Let me tell you how a magistrate comes to steal a crown. It is not the story they tell in the taverns. In the taverns I am ambition with teeth, a man who looked at the throne of two realms and could not bear to be merely a servant of it. There is some comfort in that version, I suppose. Villains are simpler than remedies, and people have always preferred their medicine sweetened into poison.

The truth is this: I was the last honest judge in a realm that had forgotten what honesty cost.

* * *

The hall I hold court in was once the Meridian Assizes, back when the realm below still bothered with law at all. It is a beautiful room, or it was — vaulted ceiling of blue stone, pillars carved with the faces of the first covenant-signers, all of them solemn and bearded and certain. Now frost has taken the carvings. The signers wear masks of ice, which is an improvement. Ice, at least, does not lie about what it is.

I sit at the center on a chair of grey oak. Not a throne. I want that understood, even if no one else does. A throne is a claim; a chair is a duty. When the Crown is reforged and set where it belongs, I intend to stand beside it, not upon it. Order does not need a king. Order needs a judge — one judge, incorruptible, unwearying, who cannot be flattered or bribed or grieved into mercy's little exceptions. Mercy is how covenants rot. Mercy is the crack through which the cold first came.

Ah. You see the irony. Everyone sees the irony eventually. They usually mention it just before I sentence them.

"Bring in the next," I said, and my clerks — grey-robed, grey-faced, chosen precisely because they had nothing left to lose and therefore nothing to bargain with — opened the doors.

The frozen came in as they always did: slowly, in the shuffling procession of the starved. This was the docket for the day, forty-one souls from the glass city of Vell, each accused of the same crime, which is the only crime left worth prosecuting in a dying world: hoarding warmth against the common need.

* * *

You must understand what winter has done to law down here. Above, in the mortal city, they still have courts — little theatres of guilt and innocence, lawyers performing grief, verdicts bought like bread. Below, we once had something better: the Covenant Courts, where every judgment was witnessed by both realms, where a sentence passed in the glass cities bound equally in the mortal streets above. The Crown of Covenants sat at the center of it all, not as a treasure but as a *witness*. It remembered every oath ever sworn beneath it. It could not be deceived, because deception requires forgetting, and the Crown forgot nothing.

Then the Crown was stolen. Then the realms drifted apart. Then the winter began.

And here is the question nobody above has thought to ask, because asking it would require admitting an uncomfortable answer: *who kept judging?*

Someone had to. The Covenant Courts closed their doors, but disputes did not close theirs. Neighbors still stole from neighbors; cities still cheated cities; the strong still preyed on the weak, now with the added urgency of starvation. And into that vacuum stepped whatever remained of the old magistracy — which, by the end, was me.

I did not take the Crown because I wanted power. I took it because I watched a city of ten thousand people decide, over one particularly cruel winter, that law was a luxury, and I watched what they became inside of four months. I have the ledgers. I keep meticulous ledgers. You would not sleep well after reading them, and neither did I, and I have not slept well in eleven years, which I consider part of the office.

So: the Crown. Scattered by its last guardian into five shards hidden across the realm like a coward hiding coins from his own family. I have recovered three. The fourth sits in my stronghold, set into a throne of ledgers — yes, a throne, I am aware of what I said earlier, and I have made my peace with the hypocrisy, which is more than most men manage. The fifth eludes me. For now.

In the meantime, I judge. Because someone must, and because judgment deferred is judgment denied, and because a realm without law is already dead and merely hasn't lain down yet.

"The charge," I said to the first of the forty-one, "is hoarding."

He was a glassmaker's apprentice, or had been, before Vell's furnaces went dark. Young. Frostbitten at the ears. He stood in the witness circle with his chin up, which I noted in his favor. Most of them weep. Weeping is reasonable; I simply cannot let it move me.

"I was keeping coal for my sister," he said. "She's sick."

"How sick?"

"Sick enough to die if she gets colder."

"And how many others in your quarter are sick enough to die if they get colder?"

He stared at me. He had not expected the question to go sideways. None of them ever do; guilt is always so certain of its own uniqueness.

"Forty," he said finally. "Maybe sixty."

"And your sister's share of that coal, divided among sixty dying people, would keep each of them warm for how long?"

"An hour." His voice cracked. "Maybe less."

"Yes," I said. "That is the arithmetic of the age. One hour of warmth for sixty people, or perhaps three days for one. Choose."

"I've chosen—"

"You've chosen *for yourself*, which is precisely the problem. Sixty people chose for themselves, and now there is no coal at all, and everyone is equally frozen, and you call that fairness because at least it's shared." I leaned forward. "I am not angry with you, boy. Understand that. I am angry with the world that made your choice necessary, and I am doing something about it. Which is more than anyone else can say."

"What will you do to me?" he whispered.

"Nothing."

The hall went quiet. Even my clerks paused their quills.

"You'll do nothing," I repeated, "because punishing you changes nothing. Your sentence is this: you will carry the remaining coal to the quarter-house yourself, and you will watch it divided, and you will write down the names of the sixty, and you will bring me the list. Not so I can punish them. So I can remember them. Someone has to remember them. That is the whole of the law now — memory, arithmetic, and the refusal to look away."

He went out shaking. Whether from fear or relief I neither knew nor cared. Forty-one cases followed, and I judged them all the same way: no cruelty, no mercy, only the ledger and the long arithmetic of survival. By the time the doors closed I had filled nine pages in a hand so steady it might have been printed.

This is what they call tyranny. Very well. Let them. Tyranny is what the comfortable call the moment someone starts counting.

* * *

It was near evening — such evening as exists here, a bruised violet dimming — when my clerk Corvess entered with the particular stiffness he reserves for news he expects me to dislike.

"A prisoner," he said. "Taken on the Vell road. She claims to be a guildsman."

My quill stopped.

"Of which guild?"

"She wouldn't say. She says—" Corvess hesitated, and Corvess never hesitates; hesitation costs extra in this economy. "She says you'll know the guild when she names it, and she'd rather say it to your face."

I set down the pen. There is a superstition among the frozen that I never show surprise, and like most superstitions it exists because someone once saw me surprised and mistook it for a trick of the light. My heart, I will admit, moved. Not much. A half-step, like a man shifting his weight.

Because I knew. Of course I knew. A guildsman on the Vell road, alone, taken easily, claiming a name she'd rather deliver in person — I have spent eleven years studying the architecture of traps, mostly from the inside of them, and this had the unmistakable smell of bait. Fresh bait. Bait that had been *sent*.

"Bring her in," I said.

* * *

She was younger than I expected. They always are, the ones who come for me; youth is the raw material of every crusade, and someone should really warn them. Small, dark-haired, dressed in travel-worn wool with a crow perched on her shoulder like a familiar she hadn't asked for. Her hands were chapped and ink-stained. A ledger-keeper's hands. I know those hands; I used to have them.

She looked around my court — at the iced pillars, the masked signers, the clerks scratching in the gloom — with an expression I found genuinely difficult to read. Not fear. Not contempt. Something closer to *recognition*, as though she'd walked into a room she'd been dreaming about.

"Name," I said.

"Rosalind Almeida." Her voice was steady. Good. Steadiness makes the conversation shorter. "Ledger-keeper, Guild of Impossible Things."

There it was. The name dropped into the silent hall like a stone into a well, and I confess I listened for the splash.

The Guild of Impossible Things. Yes. I knew it — knew it better than its own elders did, I'd wager, because I had spent a decade mapping its doors while they pretended those doors didn't exist. The guild that kept itself secret from both realms. The guild whose founding charter, if you dug far enough into the ash of history, bore a clause I had never forgotten: *the Guild shall hold no crown, serve no crown, and answer to no crown.* The guild of the middle path, the locked door, the polite invisibility. The guild that had watched the winter come and concluded, in its collective wisdom, that the correct response was to remain unnoticed.

"You're a long way from your hall," I said.

"My hall doesn't exist anymore." Something flickered across her face — grief, quickly filed away. Interesting. "You burned it open. Or your people did."

"I don't burn things, Miss Almeida. Burning destroys evidence. I *open* things. What your elders did afterward — the disavowal, the letter, the pretense that you four were never members — that was entirely their own craftsmanship. I merely provided the draft." I folded my hands. "Did you come all this way to accuse me of theft?"

"I came to ask you a question."

"Ask."

"Why?" And she said it without heat, which unsettled me more than any accusation could have. "You were a magistrate. A good one, by every record I found. You sentenced a smuggler in Vell to repair the seawall instead of the gallows, and the seawall held for thirty years. You fed a famine town out of your own salary and recorded it as a loan to yourself. Why would *that* man steal the Crown?"

For a moment — a moment I did not enjoy — I considered answering honestly. It is a strange loneliness, being the villain in everyone's account of you. There is a whole speech I have carried for eleven years, polished smooth as riverstone, about the winter of the empty courts, about the boy who froze in a doorway thirty feet from a granary because no one had the authority to open it, about how I swore over his small grey body that authority would never again be absent when it was needed. About how the Crown was not a treasure but a *witness*, and witnesses were what the realm had lost, and I intended to give it back whether it wanted them or not.

But she was bait. Bait does not get the speech. Bait gets released, so it can swim home to the net.

"Power," I said instead, and smiled the smile they expect. "Is that not sufficient? It suffices in every ballad."

"It's a lazy answer."

"It's the true one. Lazy truths are still true." I rose, and the hall rose with me, forty clerks and one crow. "Here is what happens now, Miss Almeida. I find you guilty of trespass, conspiracy, and the theft of guild property — the map, specifically, which belongs to the dead man who carried it, and dead men's property falls to the Crown. To *the* Crown. You appreciate the distinction."

"You're letting me go," she said. Not a question. Clever girl. Too clever by exactly the amount I required.

"I'm releasing you," I corrected. "Letting go implies I had hold of anything that mattered. Go back to your misfits, Almeida. Find the shards you're looking for. Gather them up nice and neat, all in one place, and then — when you've done the work I have no patience for — I will take them from you, and I will reforge what should never have been scattered, and this realm will have one law, one witness, and one winter that ends *on my authority* rather than by accident." I stepped closer. Up close, she smelled of ink and cold iron and, faintly, of the mortal city above — rain on slate. Homesickness is a weapon; I deploy all weapons. "Do you know why I release you?"

"Because I'm useful."

"Because you're *inevitable*. Every rebellion needs its martyr or its messenger, and you haven't the constitution for a martyr. Deliver this to your company: the court remains in session. It will remain in session until every soul in both realms is accounted for, warmed, and judged — and I do mean *every* soul. Tell them the docket is long, and I am patient, and winter keeps excellent records."

The crow shifted on her shoulder and fixed me with one black eye, and for a moment — a half-second, gone like frost in sunlight — I thought I saw recognition there too. An old eye. A guardian's eye. I put the thought away in the drawer where I keep everything I refuse to feel, next to the boy in the doorway and the seawall and eleven years of unslept nights.

"Go," I said. "And Almeida — when you reach the forge, and you will reach the forge, remember that I wanted you warned. I am not a monster. I am a remedy. The difference is that monsters enjoy the hurting."

She went. The doors closed. The clerks resumed their scratching, and the frost on the covenant-signers' faces gleamed like a jury that had finally reached a verdict.

I sat back down at my grey oak chair-that-is-not-a-throne, drew a fresh page toward me, and wrote, in a hand steady as print:

*Prisoner released. Bait accepted. Estimated time to recovery of final shards: days.*

Then, after a moment, beneath it, smaller:

*She asked why.*

I looked at that line for a long time. Then I did what I do with everything that refuses to stay in its drawer.

I ruled it irrelevant, and kept writing.

Outside, the winter fell on, orderly as a ledger, patient as a court, waiting — as I waited — for the forge.
