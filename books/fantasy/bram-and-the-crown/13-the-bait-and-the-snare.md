## 13. The Bait and the Snare

There is a particular kind of silence that falls over a room when everyone in it has just realized they are being hunted, and no one wants to be the first to say it aloud. I had heard it once before, in the guildhall, the night the elders voted to burn a dead man's name out of our ledgers. This silence was worse. This one had teeth.

We had made it perhaps four miles from Vane's grey court before Hesper finally stopped walking and turned around.

"He let us go," she said. Not a question. Hesper doesn't ask questions when she already knows the answers; she just states them and waits for the world to argue.

"He let *me* go," I corrected, because apparently I still had energy left for pedantry. "The rest of you he never saw."

"He saw enough." Odo shifted Wren's weight on his shoulder—the boy had slept through our entire audience with the magistrate, which I chose to interpret as either profound trust or profound exhaustion—and squinted back down the white road we'd come by. "A magistrate doesn't release a suspect. He releases bait. There's a difference, and the difference is a hook."

"Thank you, Odo," I said. "I had gathered."

"No offense meant."

"None taken." I pulled my coat tighter and looked down the road myself. The snow here fell upward in places, little rebellions against gravity that the dying realm permitted itself now and then, as if it had forgotten which way things were supposed to go. Somewhere behind us, Serath Vane was sitting in his frozen tribunal, being very pleased with himself.

And the terrible thing—the thing I couldn't stop turning over like a coin with two identical faces—was that he was right to be pleased. He had looked at me across that grey courtroom and seen exactly what I was: a guild-keeper's apprentice playing at questing, carrying a map she'd copied in secret, leading three misfits and a crow toward shards of a crown she didn't understand. He'd weighed me and found me harmless. He'd let me walk away because I was more useful walking than sitting in his cells.

Bram shifted on my shoulder. The crow had been quiet since the court—quieter than usual, which for Bram was saying something, since his usual conversation consisted of stolen phrases deployed at tactically devastating moments. Now he pressed his cold beak against my ear and said, in a voice like wind through a keyhole: "*She is the remedy.*"

Vane's words. Bram collected sentences the way Odo collected favors, and he had carried that one out of the courtroom like a stone.

"I know," I murmured. "He thinks he's the remedy. That's what makes him dangerous."

"*Dangerous,*" Bram agreed, and ruffled his feathers, and said nothing else for a long while.

* * *

We made camp that night in the shell of a waystation—a trade-post once, by the look of it, its counters still standing though the roof had long since gone to glass-dust and snow. Wren built a fire from wood so old it burned green and smelled like the inside of a violin. We huddled around it, four people and a crow, and I laid out the situation the way I'd learned to lay out ledgers: plainly, in columns, with the losses where everyone could see them.

"First column," I said. "What Vane knows. He knows about me. He knows about the map—or he believes there's a map, which amounts to the same thing. He knows we're looking for the shards. He does not know how many we've found, or where the rest are."

"Second column," Hesper said, catching on. "What he thinks. He thinks you're a frightened clerk who'll lead him straight to everything if he follows her home."

"That's the hook," Odo said. "He's not chasing us. He's *herding* us. Every road we take, he'll already know it, because he expects us to take it."

I nodded. "Third column. What we actually have." I reached into my coat and brought out the second shard.

It sat in my palm like a sliver of frozen dawn—no bigger than a knife-blade, but heavy past all reason, the way important things always are. We'd taken it from the glassmaker's city, paid for honestly with Rosalind Almeida's promise, which I was beginning to suspect was the most expensive currency in either realm. When light touched it, it hummed. Not audibly. Somewhere under the skin, in the teeth, in the old hollow places. It wanted to be whole. All the shards did. You could feel them pulling toward each other across the miles like separated pages of one letter.

Wren leaned forward, firelight moving in his eyes. "It sings louder when Vane's shard-side comes near," he said. "I told you. At the court. It knew him."

"It knew its own stolen piece," I said. "Yes. Which brings us to the problem, and the problem is this: whatever we do, Vane will be watching the roads. If we run, he runs us down. If we hide, he waits, because he has an army of the frozen and we have a fire that smells like a violin. And if we go on—" I turned the shard in my fingers, watching it drink the light— "he follows us to every shard we find and takes each one the moment we've done the hard part. We are, functionally, his mining crew."

Silence around the fire. Even the green flames seemed to consider this.

"So we stop being useful to him," Hesper said.

"How?" Odo asked. "You can't un-be bait. Bait is what you are, not what you do."

And that was when Wren—sleepy, soft-spoken Wren, who talked to locks because locks listened—said the thing that changed the shape of everything.

"Then we should be *better* bait."

We all looked at him. He blinked, surprised at his own volume, and pushed on in a rush. "He expects you to carry the shards. He expects to follow you and take them. So—so let him follow. Let him take one. But make it a shard that tells us where he's going." He held up his hands, palms out, the way he did when explaining locks to people who thought locks were simple. "A lock isn't just a thing that keeps people out. It's a thing that decides *who* gets in. You build the lock, you choose the key. If we give him the shard—if we *let* him take it—then we're not prey anymore. We're the lock. And he's the key turning himself."

Nobody spoke. The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, the snow kept up its occasional rebellion against falling.

"You want us to hand Serath Vane a piece of the Crown of Covenants," Odo said slowly, "on purpose."

"I want him to *think* he took it," Wren said.

Hesper started to smile. It was not a nice smile. Hesper's smiles rarely are; they tend to have hinges in them, like her doors. "Cracked," she said. "You mean cracked. You can crack it, can't you? So it leaves a trail."

Wren's ears went red. "I've been listening to it since the glass city. Shards want to find each other—that's their whole nature, that's why the Crown held the realms together, because every piece was always reaching for every other piece. If I open a flaw in it, just a hairline, it'll reach *harder*. Desperate things call loudest. And the other shards—" he pointed at my coat, at the first shard hidden against my ribs— "will answer. And Vane's stronghold is full of the pieces he already has. Wherever he goes, the cracked shard will sing to them, and I can hear the song. We won't need the map anymore. We'll have *him* for a map."

"And Vane," I said, following it to the end, feeling something cold and bright kindle in my chest that I suspected might be hope wearing armor, "will believe he's won. He'll believe the frightened clerk dropped what she was carrying. He'll take the shard back to his fortress and bolt his doors, certain the hunt is over—"

"—and every step he takes," Hesper finished, "he'll be drawing us a picture of exactly where the rest of it lives."

Odo laughed. It was a short, startled sound, as if it had escaped custody. "That's either the best plan I've ever heard or the worst."

"With this company," Hesper said, "those are usually the same plan."

* * *

It took Wren two days.

I want to be honest about those two days, because ledger-keeping means recording losses as well as gains, and what it cost him deserves to be written down. Cracking a shard of the Crown of Covenants is not like cracking a nut. It is more like convincing a star to develop a flaw. Wren sat with the thing cupped in his hands for hours at a stretch, whispering to it—not spells, I don't think Wren knows any spells, unless listening counts, and I am increasingly willing to entertain the possibility that it does—telling it stories, apologizing to it, explaining what we needed and why. The shard resisted. Of course it resisted. It had spent an age holding two worlds together; it did not take instruction from apprentices.

On the evening of the second day, Wren began to cry without seeming to notice he was doing it, and the shard went quiet, and then—very softly—it sang.

"There," he whispered. His hands were shaking. There was a line across the shard's face now, fine as a spider's thread, visible only when the light moved. "There. It's not broken. It's *lonely*. I just—I gave the loneliness somewhere to go."

Bram hopped along the branch above him and said, "*Well kept,*" which was one of the stranger's phrases, and Wren smiled through his tears as if he'd been knighted.

We rested one more day, because the plan required a performance, and performances require strength. Then we packed camp, and I put the first shard—the true one, the sound one—against my heart where it had lived since the glassmaker's city, and I set the second shard, the cracked one, in the outer pocket of my pack. Where it would be found.

"You're sure?" Hesper asked me quietly, while the others broke camp. "Once he has it, we can't take it back. Whatever happens next happens because we chose it tonight."

I thought about the elders burning a dead man's page. I thought about how they'd called it prudence, and how it had felt like erasure, and how I had memorized the page anyway because some knowledge cannot be unkept. I thought about Serath Vane in his winter-grey, saying *she is the remedy* about himself, believing with his whole frozen heart that order was a thing one man could hold.

"He assumes we're powerless," I said. "So we'll fight him with his assumptions. They're the sharpest weapons he's ever handed us."

* * *

We let him catch us on the third day.

Not caught, precisely—overtaken. We walked the open road through a country of leaning pines and glass-frosted hills, making no effort at stealth, and at midday the temperature dropped the way it does when something vast moves behind the weather, and the crows—all the ordinary crows, the realm's real ones—went silent in the branches. Bram dug his claws into my shoulder and said nothing at all, which was its own kind of warning.

They came out of the treeline without hurry: a dozen figures in winter-grey, and then Vane himself, riding a horse the color of unfallen snow. He looked exactly as he had in his courtroom—composed, reasonable, a man perpetually about to render a fair judgment—and he reined in ten feet away and regarded our little company with the weary patience of a magistrate reviewing paperwork.

"Rosalind Almeida," he said. "Ledger-keeper. You travel poorly. No escort, no provisions worth the name, and you've doubled back twice, which suggests your map is imperfect." His gaze moved over Hesper, Odo, sleeping-Wren-on-Odo's-shoulder—he'd woken, I noticed, and was doing a masterful impression of sleep—and settled, with the weight of a stamp coming down, on my pack. On the outer pocket. "You have something of mine."

"The Crown belongs to no one," I said. "That's rather the point of it."

"The Crown belongs to *order*," Vane said, and there it was again, that terrible sincerity, that conviction worn smooth as a riverstone. "Everything else is sentiment. Give me the shard, keeper, and I will let you walk away. Again. I am not cruel. I am *necessary*, and necessity has no time for cruelty."

I stood very still. Behind my ribs, the true shard was singing softly, answering the cracked one in my pack, and I prayed to whatever faint gods still kept books in this realm that Vane could not hear harmony, only volume.

"You'll take it whether I give it or not," I said.

"Yes," he agreed pleasantly. "But one way, you keep your fingers."

So I shrugged the pack off my shoulder—slowly, a clerk surrendering evidence, every inch of me performing reluctance—and set it in the snow between us, and stepped back. One of the grey figures retrieved it. Vane drew out the second shard and held it up to the pale light, and I watched his face for the flicker of doubt that would undo everything.

There wasn't one. Why would there be? The shard hummed in his hand, hungry and reaching, exactly as a shard should. He had no reason to suspect that Wren had tuned its hunger like a lute-string. He turned it in his fingers, and I saw the moment he decided—saw certainty settle over him like frost forming, beautiful and bloodless.

"The frightened clerk," he said, almost gently, "drops what she carries. I expected better of the Guild of Impossible Things. Perhaps that is why your guild hides. Everything it touches comes apart in its hands." He tucked the shard away inside his grey coat, and something in my chest twisted to watch it go—but underneath the twist, faint and steady, I felt it: the song. The cracked shard calling to its brothers, and the brothers answering, a thread of sound running from my ribs toward Vane's stronghold, thin as spider-silk and strong as oath-iron. Wren, eyes still shut against Odo's shoulder, breathed one small deliberate snore.

"You may keep the other one," Vane said, wheeling his horse. "For now. Consider it collateral. When I have reforged the Crown and rendered final judgment on both realms, I will send for you, keeper, and we will settle accounts." He paused, and looked back at me, and for one unguarded instant there was something almost like pity in his face. "You could have been orderly. It would have spared you a great deal of grief."

Then the greys closed around him, and the winter swallowed them, and the road stood empty except for snow and the four of us and a crow.

Nobody moved until the cold-horse's hoofbeats faded. Then Wren opened his eyes, sat up, and grinned—a real grin, the first I'd ever seen on him, wide and crooked and young.

"He took it," he whispered. "He *took* it. Rosalind, I can hear it—I can hear it *already*—it's calling north-northeast, hard, like a bell ringing underwater—"

"Hush," Hesper said, but she was smiling her hinged smile, and Odo was laughing again, that startled escaped-custody laugh, and even Bram ruffled his feathers and produced, from wherever crows keep such things, a perfect imitation of Vane's voice: "*Perhaps that is why your guild hides.*"

"Yes," I said. I picked up the empty pack and slung it over my shoulder and looked north-northeast, where a thread only Wren could hear was pulling us forward through the falling snow. "Let him think the guild hides. Let him think I'm a frightened clerk who drops what she carries." I started walking, and the others fell in beside me, and behind my ribs the true shard sang its low answering note, guiding us after the thief like a compass needle made of promise. "By the time he realizes what we've done, we'll already be inside his walls."

North-northeast. The thread sang. We followed.
