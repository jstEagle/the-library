## 14. Across the Frozen Meridian

I have made doors my whole life. Doors are honest things. A door has a hinge-side and a latch-side, a warm side and a cold side, and if you build it true, it does exactly what it says. The meridian waste had no sides at all. It was the space between a door and its frame, stretched out for a hundred miles, and I hated it the way a craftsman hates shoddy work.

We stood at its edge on the morning we crossed, which was not quite a morning, because the sun in that country had been dying so long it had forgotten how to rise properly. It just hung there above the horizon like a coin someone had dropped down a well and couldn't reach.

"That's it?" Odo said. "That's the famous meridian? Looks like somebody's attic floor after a bad year."

"Attic floors don't eat you," Wren said.

"Most attics," Odo said, "have never tried."

Rosalind said nothing. She was looking at the map, or rather at the place where the map's ink had begun to behave strangely — the lines of the waste redrawn themselves every time she blinked, as though the paper couldn't decide where anything was. Bram sat on her shoulder with his feathers puffed into a grey ball, and he was making a sound I hadn't heard from him before: a low, clicking croak, over and over, like a man counting under his breath.

"What's he saying?" I asked.

"I think," Rosalind said slowly, "he's counting something he lost out here."

Nobody wanted to ask what. We walked.

* * *

The first day taught us the rules, though nobody announced them. Rules on the meridian were like weather — you learned them by being hit.

The rule was this: the cold didn't take your fingers or your toes first. It took your memories. You'd be walking along, minding your own frostbite, and suddenly there'd be a hole in your head where something used to be. Not gone exactly. More like a room in your house that you knew existed but couldn't picture anymore. You remembered *that* you had a mother. You could no longer remember her face, or whether she'd laughed at your jokes, or what her hands smelled like after kneading bread.

Wren noticed it first, because Wren noticed everything about locks and nothing about people. "I've lost the name of my street," she said, about an hour in. "The one I grew up on. I know it started with an S. Or a C."

"Silver Street?" Odo offered.

"Maybe. Or Candle Lane."

"Candle Lane doesn't start with an S."

"It might, if you're tired enough."

I kept my mouth shut, because I had already lost something and hadn't told anyone. It was small. It was the smell of the guildhall — the tallow and oak-dust and old varnish that had soaked into me across thirty years of door-making. I reached for it and found nothing. A whole lifetime of mornings in that hall, and the smell of them had been scooped out of me like the flesh of a fruit.

You would think I'd have grieved. Here is the thing I learned about myself on the ice, and I did not enjoy learning it: I felt relief. Thirty years in the Guild of Impossible Things, and they had demoted me for one mistake — a door I built too well, that opened onto a thing it shouldn't have, that cost two people their minds. Thirty years, and when I fell, not one elder spoke for me. The guild had taken my memory of belonging and replaced it with the memory of shame, and the cold had simply finished the job.

So when the waste came for my recollections of the guildhall — the layout of the workshops, the faces of the elders, the exact words of my censure — I let it have them. I walked and I did not fight, and I told myself that was strategy. Conserving strength. Keeping my warmth for things that mattered.

It was half a lie. But only half.

* * *

By the second night we had learned to sleep in shifts, pressed back to back in a hollow of blue ice that Hesper — that is, that I — judged stable. Rosalind took first watch. I lay awake listening to the wind, which on the meridian does not howl like ordinary wind. It whispers. It gets inside your head through whatever gap you've left and starts rearranging the furniture.

"Hesper." Rosalind's voice, low. "You're crying."

"I'm not. My eyes are freezing. That's a known phenomenon."

"All right." She shifted closer, sharing warmth without making a ceremony of it, which I appreciated. Then, after a while: "What did it take?"

"Nothing important."

"Hesper."

"A door," I admitted. "A door I made when I was nineteen. My first real commission. I can remember that I was proud of it. I can't remember the door."

She was quiet. Above us, the sky did its dead-sun trick, a faint bruise-colored glow that never fully became night.

"My grandmother's voice," she said. "It took my grandmother's voice yesterday. I still have her letters. I read them and I hear nothing." Her breath fogged. "Bram says — well, Bram doesn't say, Bram clicks, but I think he means the waste takes the loudest memories first. The ones you replay most. Because those are the easiest to find."

"Then it should be starving," I said. "Mine are mostly regrets."

"No," Rosalind said. "I've been watching you. Yours aren't regrets. They're the guild. Every time you talk about the elders, your jaw does a thing. You keep their voices even when you hate them. Especially when you hate them."

I wanted to argue. I was too cold and too tired, and also she was right, and being right she deserved better than argument. "They made me," I said finally. "Thirty years. Whatever else they were, they made me."

"And what will the waste leave," Rosalind asked, "if it takes everything they made?"

I didn't answer. Somewhere out in the dark, Bram clicked his slow count, and I wondered what a man counts when he's already lost everything worth counting.

* * *

The third day was the worst, and I will tell it plainly because a door-maker knows the value of describing a load honestly before she tries to carry it.

The wind rose at dawn — such dawn as there was — and it came at us not from one direction but from all of them, the way cold comes off a lake. And it was full of voices. That was the cruelty of the meridian: it had eaten so many travelers that it had learned to speak with their mouths. It called our names in voices we almost recognized. It offered back what it had stolen, just slightly wrong, like a counterfeiter who'd only ever seen money from a distance.

I heard my old master, Doorwright Callum, dead eleven years, telling me my work was good. He had never once said that while alive. That was how I knew it was a lie, and knowing didn't stop me from wanting it.

Odo heard a debtor. Of course he did. "She owes me a favor," he kept muttering, walking faster, "a big one, she promised, I have it written down—" and it took Wren and me both to haul him back into line. Whatever favor he'd spent at the inn, whatever debt he'd joined us to escape, the wind had found it and was wearing it like a mask.

And Wren — Wren stopped walking entirely, stood stock-still in the white, and said, in a perfectly pleasant voice, "There's a lock ahead. A beautiful one. It wants picking."

"There's nothing ahead," I said. "There's never anything ahead. That's the entire problem with this place."

"It's singing to me," she said, and her eyes had gone somewhere else, and Rosalind slapped her — gently, twice, the way you wake a sleepwalker — and Wren came back to herself and burst into tears and apologized to everyone including the wind, which I thought was excessive, but grief makes people polite to the wrong things.

We roped ourselves together after that. Waist to waist, arm's length apart, the way climbers do. And here is the craft-lesson of the meridian, the thing I'd tell any apprentice fool enough to cross it: rope helps, but names help more. A rope keeps your body together. A name keeps something else.

So we began to say each other's names. Out loud, at intervals, like a litany. Rosalind started it, because of course she did; she had the map-brain, the ledger-brain, the terrible responsible heart.

"Hesper," she'd call against the wind. "Hesper Almeida. Door-maker. You built the door that got us out of the city, and it held."

"Held," I'd answer, because answering was the point. "Hesper. Yes."

"Wren. Wren of the locks. You cracked the shard Vane carries, and he doesn't know it, and that crack is going to save us all."

"Cracked it," Wren would say, sounding surprised every time, as if she'd forgotten and was pleased to be reminded.

"Odo. Odo who pays his debts. You bought us passage with the truest coin there is."

"Pays them," Odo would mutter. "Pays them all."

And then each of us would say hers back, and mine, and the others', until the litany ran around the rope like blood through a body. It looked ridiculous. It probably was ridiculous. It was also the only reason four misfits and a crow crossed a hundred miles of memory-eating cold without coming apart, and I will defend that litany to my grave, if I ever get one.

* * *

On the fifth day I understood what choice the waste was offering me.

It happened like this. I stumbled — the ice had grown treacherous, ridged like ploughed ground — and went down hard, and for a moment I simply knelt there, because kneeling felt reasonable and standing felt like an opinion I could no longer support. And the wind came in close, close as a lover, and it said, in my own voice:

*You can keep one set. Choose.*

That sounds mad. I know it sounds mad. But the meridian was made of bargains — the whole other realm was made of bargains; trade was its bones — and the waste was simply the oldest trader of all, and every trader respects a customer who understands the terms. It showed me, briefly, both accounts.

In one account: the guild. Callum's workshop, the smell of fresh-cut oak, the pride of my first door, the long slow years of mastery, and threaded through them, bright as wire, the shame of my fall, the silence of the elders, the door that opened wrong. All of it. The good and the bad, inseparable, because that's how a life comes.

In the other account: five days. A woman with a map pressing a cup of melted snow into my hands because I was shaking. An apprentice talking to a padlock like it was a shy child. A debt-collector giving away his one treasure and pretending it was nothing. A crow's weight on a shoulder, light as a secret. A litany of names shouted into a wind that wanted to erase us, and the wind losing.

Five days against thirty years. Any sane accountant takes the thirty years.

But here is what I knew, kneeling in the ice, that no sane accountant knows: the thirty years had already failed me. They had made me competent and alone, skilled and unloved, a master of doors in a guild that had locked every one of them against me. The thirty years were a beautiful house with no way out. And I had spent my whole life building ways out.

"Keep the new ones," I said aloud, to the wind, to myself, to whoever keeps the ledgers of such trades. "Take the rest. Take all of it. I choose the company."

The wind paused. Even the waste, I think, was surprised; it doesn't get many customers who shop like that. Then it moved through me, thorough and impersonal as a clerk, and when it was done I knelt on empty ice with thirty years gone out of my head like water out of a broken jug.

Do you know what I felt? Warmth. Actual warmth, the first since the city. Because grief for a thing you chose to lose weighs less than grief for a thing taken, and I had carried the old guild's weight so long I'd mistaken it for my spine.

I stood up. The rope pulled, gentle, and Rosalind's voice came thin through the wind: "Hesper! Hesper Almeida!"

And I answered, and my voice was steady, and I meant it more than I had ever meant anything: "Here. Hesper. Here."

* * *

What I lost, I should say, I lost completely. When we made camp that night and Rosalind asked, carefully, what remained, I took inventory like a woman checking a storeroom after a flood.

I knew I had been a door-maker. The skill lived in my hands; hands are loyal that way. I knew I had belonged to some guild once, the way you know you've owned a coat you no longer have. I knew there had been a falling-out, though the shape of it was gone, and — this was the strange mercy — the sting was gone with it. You cannot ache over a wound you cannot find.

"You don't regret it?" Wren asked. She was watching me with those lock-picking eyes of hers, the ones that see the mechanism under the surface. "Losing all of it?"

"Do you regret the rooms you've locked and never reopened?"

"That's not an answer."

"It's a door-maker's answer. We don't regret closed doors, love. We just note where they are, in case we need them later." I fed a twig — our last twig, gods help us — into the little spirit-flame Odo carried. "Besides. Look at the ledger now." I nodded around our fireless camp: Rosalind bent over the map, lips moving, memorizing it again just in case; Odo rewrapping his feet with the tenderness of a man who had learned the hard way what frostbite cost; Bram with his head tucked under his wing, one eye open, keeping his endless count. "Five entries. Best inventory I've ever kept."

Rosalind looked up. There was snow in her hair and exhaustion in her face and something underneath both that I was beginning to recognize as the thing the elders never had and never missed: certainty that wasn't borrowed from a title. "Hesper," she said. "When this is over. If it's over. The new guild — the one I keep saying we'll build—"

"You'll want doors," I said. "Good ones. Honest hinges."

"The best."

"Then I'm already membered," I said. "Sign me in whatever book you like. I've given up my history for this company. I intend to get a future out of the bargain. I'm told I drive a hard trade."

Odo snorted. Wren smiled into her collar. Bram clicked once, which from him was applause.

* * *

We saw the far edge of the waste on the seventh day, and it was the cruelest sight of the crossing, because it was so close and so impossible.

A line of black rock on the horizon, maybe six miles off. Beyond it, faintly, a smudge of color — actual color, the deep glass-blues of the cities we'd come from, alive with some interior light. Six miles. Half a day's walk, in any sane country.

Between us and it, the ice had changed. It stood up in vast pale spires, a forest of frozen glass, miles deep, and the wind funneling between the spires made a sound I will hear until I die: it was singing. Not whispering now. Singing, in a thousand stolen voices, every traveler it had ever eaten, harmonies of the lost. And beneath the music, so low you felt it in your teeth rather than heard it, something else. A rhythm. Regular. Patient.

Like footsteps. Like something very large, very old, walking toward us through the spires, keeping time with the song.

Bram stood up on Rosalind's shoulder, all his feathers flat, and for the first time since the doorstep he made a sound that was not a click. It was a word. Ragged, rusted, half a crow's croak and half a man's voice, torn out of him by whatever walked in the glass:

"*Run.*"

Rosalind's hand went to the map. Wren's hand went to her picks. Odo sighed the sigh of a man whose debts have followed him across worlds.

I put my hand flat against the nearest spire, feeling for the grain of it, the way I'd feel for the grain of timber before cutting. Cold as a grave, yes. But ice is only water that's forgotten itself, and water, given heat and honesty, remembers how to move.

"Well," I said, and heard my own voice come out level, a craftsman pricing a difficult job. "Anyone got a match?"
