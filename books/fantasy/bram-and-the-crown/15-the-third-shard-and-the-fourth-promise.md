## 15. The Third Shard and the Fourth Promise

The river had been singing when it froze.

That was the first thing you noticed about the Meridian's western reach: the water hadn't stopped so much as been interrupted. It hung in mid-arch over its own banks, a thousand tons of silver held mid-leap, and inside the ice—this was the part that made Wren press her face against it until her nose went white—you could see the song itself. Not sound, exactly. The shape of sound. Ripples of pale light frozen in the current like pressed flowers, verse after verse of something that had been going on since before either realm learned to count.

"It's a mourning song," Bram said from my shoulder.

I had stopped being surprised when he spoke whole sentences. Mostly. "You can understand it?"

"I *was* it." He ruffled his feathers, which was his way of shrugging. "Guardians are sworn at the river. The river sings your name into the covenant. When I scattered the shards, I ran here first." A pause, long enough that I felt it in my collarbone where his talons gripped. "It was still singing then."

The third shard lay somewhere beneath all that interrupted music. The map—the true map, the one I'd copied and recopied until my original burned with everything else the elders fed to the fire—showed it as a small star caught in the river's deepest bend, below the arch of ice, below even the memory of flowing.

Hesper stood at the edge of the bank with her hands in her coat pockets, doing what she always did before opening a door: nothing. Looking bored. It was a professional affectation, she'd told me once. Doors could smell eagerness.

"Problem," she said.

"We've established there are several," Odo said. He was crouched over his ledger of favors, making one of his little marks that meant *asset located, terms unclear*. He'd made forty-one such marks since we crossed the meridian waste. I knew because he'd told me. Twice.

"The river's not dead." Hesper nodded at the great frozen arch. "Paused. There's a difference, and doors care about the difference. If I cut an opening into something paused, it might finish. All at once. On top of us."

"Then we go under," Wren said. She was already on her knees at the riverbank, talking to the ground in her low, coaxing voice—the one she used on locks, on hinges, on anything built to keep people out. "There's a culvert. Old. Older than the glass city. It runs along the riverbed like—" she cocked her head, listening to something none of us heard, "—like a promise someone made to the water and never took back. It'll let us in. It wants company."

"Everything down here wants company," Odo muttered. "That's how it gets you."

I should have said something wise just then. I'm telling you this story honestly, so I'll tell you that too: I said nothing. I was looking at the frozen song and thinking about Bram saying *it was still singing then*, and I was doing arithmetic I didn't like. Two shards in our keeping. Three still loose. One magistrate with a fortress full of judgments and a stolen map. And four of us—a disgraced door-maker, a favor-monger, a girl who chatted with locks, and a ledger-keeper who had never, in twenty-two years of meticulous record, once been trusted with anything that mattered.

The arithmetic said we were going to lose somebody. I just hadn't worked out who yet.

"Rosalind?" Hesper said. "Culvert or no?"

"Culvert," I said. And because it seemed suddenly important, I added, "Together."

* * *

The culvert was exactly as wide as a person and exactly as tall as a person's regrets, which is to say we went through bent double, in single file, in the dark, while the ice sang overhead in a voice like a struck bell that had forgotten how to stop ringing.

Wren led, palms flat against the stone, murmuring. Odo came behind her with the lantern. Then me, with Bram hunched under my coat, complaining in crow about the indignity of it. Hesper took the rear, because rear guards get eaten first and she'd decided years ago that if the world wanted her, it would have to take her facing forward.

The song got louder as we went. That was wrong—I knew enough by now to know it was wrong. Frozen things shouldn't amplify. But the deeper we moved under the river, the more the light-ripples in the ice above us gathered and brightened, verse stacking on verse, until the whole ceiling of our tunnel glowed like the underside of a thought.

"It knows we're here," Bram whispered. "Rosalind. It knows."

"Is that good?"

"The river has never once in its existence done anything good for anyone standing underneath it."

"Wonderful," said Odo.

Wren stopped so abruptly I walked into her. She put one finger to her lips, then pointed down.

The floor of the culvert wasn't stone anymore. It was ice—clear ice, thick as a cathedral window—and beneath it, maybe ten feet down, wedged in the river's deepest bend, something burned. White-gold, furious, the size of a fist and the size of a heart, throwing off light in jagged pulses that matched, beat for beat, the rhythm of the frozen song.

The shard. The third shard, singing to the river that had sung it into being, both of them refusing to stop even now, even frozen, even dying.

"Oh," Wren breathed. "Oh, it's *lonely*."

And that was the moment, of course. Because Wren has never met a lonely thing she didn't want to fix, and the rest of us were half a heartbeat too slow.

She knelt. She put both hands flat on the ice the way she put them on locks, and she said, in that low coaxing voice, "It's all right. We're coming. We're coming, you don't have to sing anymore—"

The ice answered her.

Not broke—*answered*. The whole floor lit up, and the song that had been muffled overhead came up through the ice and through our boots and through our teeth, and the culvert began, very gently, very politely, to finish.

"HESPER!" I shouted. "DOOR!"

"Working on it!" She had her knife out, scoring lines in the air the way she did, but the air down here was all river and river doesn't hold a line. "It won't *stay*—the whole place is one door and it's opening ALL OF THEM—"

The ice beneath Wren split like a struck bell cracking.

I have replayed the next three seconds perhaps nine hundred times. Here is what happened, as near as I can make the pieces fit: the floor opened. Wren fell toward the light. Odo—Odo, who counted everything, who kept a ledger of every favor ever done him because he believed debts were the only honest architecture in either world—Odo threw himself flat across the gap and caught her wrists.

And the river finished.

All at once, on top of us, exactly as Hesper had promised. Ten feet of interrupted water remembered its business, and the culvert became the riverbed, and the world turned white and loud and heavy beyond any weight I had words for.

The last thing I saw before the cold took me was Odo's hand, still locked around Wren's wrist, and Wren's hand locked around the shard, which she had caught, because of course she had, because Wren does not let go of lonely things.

* * *

I woke on the south bank with Hesper slapping my face with the flat, practiced efficiency of someone who has revived drowning colleagues before and resents each occasion.

"Up," she said. "Breathe. Good. Now look."

I looked.

The river was moving. Actually moving—unfrozen, unbound, roaring past us in the grey morning light, and the song was in it again, audible now, real, a vast slow melody that sounded like grief finally allowed to be grief. The ice was gone. The culvert was gone. Everything that had been under the river was now, technically, downstream.

So was Odo.

We found him a quarter mile on, snagged against a fallen tree at the river's edge, facedown in the shallows. Wren reached him first. She turned him over, and I heard her make a sound I hope never to hear again.

He was breathing. I want to be clear about that, because in the terrible arithmetic of that morning, breathing was the whole of the sum. His left arm was broken in two places—we could see the shapes of it through the skin, which is a sentence I will not elaborate on because I have eaten since. There was a gash over his eye. His lips were the color of the winter sky. And when Hesper peeled back his coat, we found the bruise already blooming across his ribs, dark as spilled ink, spreading as we watched.

"He turned," Hesper said quietly. "When the water hit. Turned his back to the current and curled around her. Took the tree-trunk strike on his own ribs instead of letting her take it." She looked at me. "Nobody does that by accident, Rosalind. That's a man who's done sums."

Odo's eyes opened. Just barely. Just enough.

"Ledger," he rasped.

"Don't you dare," Wren said.

"Favor... owed." Each word cost him visibly. "To the girl. For the... the catching. Mark it. Mark it down, someone has to mark it—"

"You absolute fool," Hesper said, and her voice cracked clean down the middle, and I understood that under all her boredom and all her craft, Hesper had been waiting her whole life for someone worth being afraid for. "You pulled her out of a river and you want a *receipt*?"

"Only honest way," Odo whispered. "Only... only honest..."

He went out again, and this time it was the deep kind of unconscious, the kind that doesn't answer to slapping.

* * *

We made camp in the lee of the fallen tree, because there was no making camp anywhere else, and because stopping felt like surrender and moving felt like murder and between those two, shelter at least let us pretend.

Hesper splinted the arm. Wren held him. I built the fire, badly, twice, and Bram perched on the tree trunk above us with his head tucked, silent in the way that meant he was watching everything and would say the worst of it later.

It was Wren who said it first. Of course it was.

"We should go back."

Nobody answered. The river sang.

"To the inn," she said. "The truth-debt inn. They'd take him. They'd trade passage for truth, and Odo's full of truths, he'd—" Her voice wobbled. "Or back up the stair. Home. The guild has healers, the guild has—"

"The guild disavowed us," Hesper said. Not cruelly. Just factually, the way she said most things. "Remember? Letter, wax seal, the word *expendable* used in an official capacity?"

"They're still *people*—"

"So is he." Hesper nodded at Odo. "And he's the one bleeding."

"He wouldn't want us to stop." Wren's chin came up. "He'd hate it. He'd write it in the ledger. *Favor squandered by committee.*"

"And if we go on and he dies?" Hesper's voice rose. "If we carry him into Vane's country, into the meridian, into whatever's waiting at the fourth shard, and he dies of a cracked rib and a fever because we needed our tally of shiny rocks? Is that the covenant, Rosalind? Is that the new guild? Weighing a man's life against a *count*?"

They were both looking at me.

Here is the thing you must understand about me, the thing I have hidden behind ledgers and competence and the careful art of being useful rather than central: I had never once in my life been the person everyone looked at. I was the youngest ledger-keeper. I was the one who copied maps in secret and memorized what she was supposed to burn. I followed. Following is a skill, and I was excellent at it, and every step of this mad journey I had told myself that I was following Bram, following the map, following the shape of a duty someone else had died starting.

But Bram was a crow on a log. The map was in my head. And two women I loved were asking me a question with no safe answer, and Odo was breathing in shallow, expensive increments, and the river sang on like it hadn't just nearly killed us all.

For the first time, nobody was ahead of me to follow.

"Both of you are right," I said.

"That's cowardice," Hesper said.

"It's arithmetic," I said, and heard Odo in it, and hated how much I loved him for teaching me. "We can't carry him back. Look at him. The stair is nine days behind us and the waste eats memories, and he hasn't got nine days of fever in him. So we don't go back." I held up a hand before Wren could speak. "And we don't leave him. Ever. So we change the shape of the thing. We stop being five travelers chasing three shards. We become something slower. Something that carries its wounded. Hesper—can you door us somewhere warm? Anywhere. A barn, a ruin, a cave with decent manners."

Hesper blinked. "I... yes. If I don't have to fight the local weather. Give me a destination that isn't a river."

"There's a town on the map's edge. Thornhollow. Half-abandoned, the map says. Stone buildings. Doors."

"Doors," Hesper repeated, like a prayer.

"We rest there until Odo can ride. However long that takes. Days. A week. The quest waits, because the quest isn't a race, it's a *promise*, and promises don't mean anything if you spend people to keep them." I looked at Wren. "You were right that we can't abandon him. You were right that he'd hate stopping. Both things. Hold both. That's the job now—not choosing what's true, carrying what's true."

Wren wiped her face with her sleeve. "Since when do you give orders?"

"Since about ninety seconds ago," I said. "I'm as surprised as you are. Someone should check whether it suits me."

"It doesn't," Hesper said. But she was already standing, already turning her hands over, already hunting for the seam in the air. "Thornhollow. Fine. But Rosalind—" She paused, knife halfway raised, and gave me a look I'd remember for the rest of my life, the look of a woman deciding to trust. "This leadership thing. You're keeping it. Don't hand it back when it gets heavy. That's how the old guild died—everyone holding the office at arm's length like it was someone else's turn."

The door opened onto darkness and the smell of cold stone and, faintly, woodsmoke.

* * *

We carried him through together—Wren at his shoulders, me at his feet, Hesper holding the door with her whole body, sweat freezing on her brow. Bram flew ahead into the dark, a black scrap against the blacker air, and just before we crossed, he landed on Odo's chest, looked down at the ruined face, and said, in a voice gone strange and soft:

"The river sang his name today. It does that for the ones who hold. I heard it under the ice."

Then he lifted off, and we stepped through, and behind us the third shard's light sank away down the moving water like a coin thrown into a well—still ours, still owed, waiting.

In the dark of Thornhollow's empty square, with the fever just beginning to climb in Odo's broken body and the fourth promise waiting somewhere north in Vane's gathering shadow, I sat down on a cold doorstep, took out the map that lived nowhere but my memory, and began, for the first time, to plan.
