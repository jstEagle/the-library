## 16. The Elders' Letter

I want it on the record—my record, since nobody else keeps one anymore—that I never wanted to be a hero. Heroes get chapters named after them. I would have settled for a warm room, a lock that talked back politely, and maybe once, just once, a full night's sleep.

Instead I got the meridian waste, Odo's snoring, and a pigeon.

Well. Not a pigeon. But when the messenger bird dropped out of the grey sky and landed on Hesper's pack like it owned the thing, my first thought was *pigeon*, and my second thought was *pigeons don't carry guild wax*, and my third thought was the kind of thought that arrives cold and sits down in your chest without asking.

The seal was ours. The Guild of Impossible Things—the real one, the one under the city, the one with the ledger I'd spent six years learning to touch only with clean hands—pressed into dark green wax with the sigil of the door that opens inward. I'd seen that seal maybe four times in my life, always on documents I wasn't allowed to read.

Hesper looked at it the way you look at a snake in your boot.

"Nobody sends sealed letters across realms," she said. "It's not done. It's *expensive*."

"It's also," Odo said, "addressed to us." He turned it over. In the elder-script, the cramped beautiful hand they used for things too important to lie about clearly, was written: *To the company formerly of this guild.*

*Formerly.* Odo said the word out loud, tasting it, and none of us liked the flavor.

Rosalind took the letter because Rosalind takes everything—not greedily, but the way a wall takes weight—and broke the seal with her thumbnail. Bram hopped from her shoulder to her wrist to be closer, head cocked, one glass-dark eye fixed on the paper as if crows could read. As if this particular crow couldn't.

She read it silently first. I watched her face do something complicated, like weather over a harbor, and then she handed it to me, because apparently I was the reader now, or because she couldn't say the words herself yet, or both.

I read it aloud. It seemed important that somebody's voice shake besides hers.

---

*"To Wren of the locks, Hesper of the doors, Odo of the debts, and Rosalind Almeida, ledger-keeper third class—"*

"Third class," Rosalind murmured. "They still had me at third class."

*"—let it be known that on the ninth night of the long frost, the Elders of the Guild of Impossible Things, in assembly and with heavy hearts, voted to disavow all knowledge of your expedition, your persons, and your purpose. You are struck from the active rolls. Your oaths are declared void. Your names are removed from the ledger."*

I stopped there. The wind was doing something mournful against the ice ridge behind us, and I needed a moment to hate it in peace.

"Keep going," Hesper said quietly. "Bad news is like a debt. Worse in installments."

*"This action was taken to preserve the guild entire. Serath Vane came to our doors in winter-grey and asked his polite questions, and we answered as we could, but he had already learned what you carried and who walked with you. When we refused to name your road, he did not threaten us. He simply waited. And then—"*

Here the handwriting changed. Got worse. Faster. Somebody writing by firelight, or by the light of things burning.

*"—he burned the doors open."*

I had to sit down on a rock for that one. You have to understand what that sentence means if you're guild-raised. Our doors don't burn. That's the whole point of doors; that's Hesper's whole *craft*. A guild door is a promise made of oak and iron and older words than either: *what happens inside stays inside; what is inside stays safe.* Vane hadn't broken in. He'd made the promise itself untrue, which is a different crime entirely, the way killing a man is different from killing an idea of a man.

*"The hall stands. The archives stand. Four elders do not, and nine of our number have gone above to live openly among mortals until this ends, which the Elders assure me is temporary, though I confess I no longer believe much that is assured to me."*

New hand again. Smaller. Almost apologetic.

*"Rosalind. If any scrap of this reaches you: they are wrong, and I helped them be wrong, and I am sorry. Do not come home. There is nothing here to come home to except the shape of home, and shapes can be worn by anyone. You were always better at impossible things than we were. We only kept the ledger of them. — M."*

M. Master Ellery-Quince, who taught me my first tumbler and told me locks were just doors that hadn't found their courage yet. His handwriting shook. I'd never seen it shake.

There was more—formal clauses, the disavowal repeated in triplicate the way guilds do when they're ashamed—but the rest was ash before I finished it. Not literally. Just the kind of words that stop meaning anything after a sentence like *he burned the doors open.*

*

Nobody spoke for a while. That's the thing about our company people wouldn't guess: we're loud in taverns and silent in griefs.

Bram made a sound I'd never heard him make. Not a word—he has hundreds of stolen words, filed away like coins—but a raw croak, low in his throat, and he pressed his head against Rosalind's jaw the way you press your forehead to someone's when language has failed you both.

"He burned the doors open," Hesper repeated. She said it like she was checking the math. "You can't. I've tried. I've *studied*. A guild door isn't wood, it's a vow with hinges."

"He didn't break the vow," Odo said. He was very still, holding the letter's wax seal up to the flat white light. "He made it true differently. Look—the sigil. The door that opens inward. He didn't force it from outside. He got someone inside to open it."

And there it was, sitting in the middle of us like a stone nobody wanted to pick up. Somebody home had opened the door. Somebody had stood in the guildhall, with the frost coming through the walls and Vane's polite voice on the other side, and chosen the living over the secret. I don't know who. I hope whoever it was has managed to forgive themselves, and I hope they never find out how much it cost, and I hope both those hopes aren't lies.

"So the elders sold us," Hesper said at last, "to save the furniture."

"No," Rosalind said. "The elders sold us to save the *guild*. That's the horrible part. It might even have been right, by their arithmetic." She looked down at Bram. "By anyone's arithmetic but ours."

"Their arithmetic," Odo said, "struck our names from the ledger."

"Yes."

"Then we have a problem of bookkeeping," Odo said, and there was something in his voice I'd heard exactly once before—at the inn where truth was currency, when he paid with his best favor and it cost him more than blood ever had. "Because a name struck from a ledger isn't gone. It's *unowned*. And unowned things, in my professional experience, belong to whoever claims them first."

*

We argued about it around the cookfire, because arguing is how our company prays.

Hesper said go back. Go back above, scatter, survive, wait out the winter like sensible criminals. "We're five people and a crow," she said. "Vane has a fortress, a philosophy, and apparently enough fire to unmake vows. This isn't cowardice, it's *arithmetic*."

Odo said push on, obviously, because Odo collects obligations the way other men collect scars, and somewhere between the inn and the frozen river he had quietly decided we were his.

Bram said, "Home. Is. A. Door." Three stolen phrases, stitched together, and then he looked at each of us in turn with that eye that sees too much, and nobody asked him what he meant, because we all knew: home isn't a building. Home is whatever you can walk through together.

And I—I said the thing I'd been chewing since the wax seal cracked.

"You know what strikes me," I said, "is that they did it wrong. The disavowal. Not cruelly—Ellery-Quince's postscript proves they hated every line of it. But wrong. Because you can't strike an oath that was sworn outside the ledger in the first place." I held up my hand, the one with the scar from the oath-night, the little white line across the palm where we'd each cut ourselves on the same knife outside any ledger at all. "Our oaths were never theirs to void. They were ours to make. Which means—" and here my voice did the shaking thing again, but I let it, because some sentences deserve to shake, "—which means they didn't exile us. They *ordained* us. We're not the guild's castoffs. We're the only part of the guild that was ever actually free."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere out on the waste, the cold went on eating somebody's memories, patient as rent.

"That," Hesper said finally, "was almost eloquent, for a girl who talks to locks."

"I talk to locks because they listen better than people."

"And yet." She sighed the sigh of a woman conceding a point she'd been fighting since chapter seven. "Fine. Fine. But if we're the guild now, we do it properly. No more sneaking. A guild has *works*. Deeds. Things you can point to." She jabbed a finger south, toward the pale smear on the horizon where Vane's stronghold sat on its throne of ledgers. "That. That's our first entry."

Rosalind had been quiet through all of it, turning the letter over and over, and now she smoothed it flat on her knee and read the last lines aloud—the ones I hadn't gotten to, the small ones at the bottom, in Ellery-Quince's shaking hand:

*"P.S. The map you buried beside the forge—we kept a copy. Vane does not know this. He thinks he holds the only road ahead. He is mistaken, and being mistaken is the one judgment he cannot render against himself."*

I looked up. Rosalind looked up. Even Bram went very still.

"He doesn't have the only map," she breathed. "The elders—cowardly, careful, self-preserving, *disavowing* elders—kept a copy hidden. Which means Vane's stronghold isn't the end of the road. It's the *middle* of it. And he's marching toward the forge thinking he's ahead of us."

Odo began, very softly, to laugh. It was not a kind sound. "He burned their doors open," he said, "and they lied to his face with a piece of parchment. Gods, I love bookkeepers."

*

We broke camp an hour later, moving south with the letter folded against Rosalind's heart and the old guild folded somewhere quieter than that—grief and inheritance sharing the same pocket, which is, I think, how most families actually work.

I walked at the rear, as usual, listening to the locks of the world. And I'll tell you what the locks said, because someone should: they said *open*. Every hinge in the frozen air, every seam in the ice, every door-shaped silence we passed—they'd all gone loose and listening, the way locks do when the key that fits them is finally walking toward them.

Exile, it turns out, is just ordination with worse weather.

Behind us lay a guildhall with its promise burned open. Ahead of us lay a fortress full of judgments waiting to be overturned. And somewhere between, in the space no ledger had ever claimed, five misfits and a crow were becoming the thing the elders had only ever kept records of.

The Guild of Impossible Things.

Population: us.
