## 17. Vane's Stronghold of Order

The fortress of Serath Vane did not look like a fortress. That was the first thing I noticed, and the first thing I hated about it.

It looked like a courthouse. A very large, very grey, very well-lit courthouse, rising out of the ice plain like a tooth from a gum of snow, its windows glowing with a light that had no warmth in it whatsoever—the light of a hundred lamps burning behind frosted glass, the light of offices that never close. Banners hung down its face, winter-grey, each one stitched with a single word in Vane's tidy hand: *ORDER*. *JUDGMENT*. *ACCOUNT*. There was no banner that said *MERCY*. I suppose he'd have considered it untidy.

"He built a bureaucracy," Hesper said flatly. We lay on our bellies on a ridge of wind-packed snow, half a mile out, watching the thing. "He stole the Crown of Covenants and he built a *bureaucracy*."

"Every tyranny is a filing system with delusions," Odo said. He was rubbing his bad shoulder—the river had given it to him, along with the third shard, and it hadn't stopped complaining since. "I've repossessed houses for gentler men."

Bram shifted on my shoulder, his claws tightening in my coat. He was looking at the fortress the way you look at a grave you dug yourself. I didn't ask. There were things Bram couldn't say, and by now I'd learned to read the shape of his silences the way Wren read locks: by feel, by patience, by the small tremors that meant something inside was about to give.

*Four shards,* I thought. *We have three.* The glassmaker's shard, green as a bottle's heart. The cracked one we'd let Vane steal, which was currently sitting in whatever strongroom he trusted most, quietly leading us to him like a compass needle made of our own treachery. And the river's shard, won at a cost Odo still wouldn't let me apologize for.

The fourth was in there. Set into a throne, according to the crack in the second shard, which sang to Wren at night in a voice like a struck wineglass. Set into a throne of ledgers.

"A throne of ledgers," I said aloud, because saying it made it slightly more survivable. "He actually did it."

"You sound surprised," Wren said. She was fourteen and talked to locks and had once picked the Guild's own front door as an act of philosophical protest. Nothing surprised her except cruelty done inefficiently.

"I'm not surprised. I'm *taking notes*." I pulled the copied map from my coat—my copy, inked in secret while the elders burned the original's twin—and looked at the fortress again. "All right. Doors."

Hesper smiled. It was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of a woman who had spent thirty years making doors and been disgraced for it, and who had recently remembered exactly how much she loved her work.

"How many ways in do you want?"

"All of them."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about infiltrating a stronghold of perfect order: it is easier than breaking into somewhere merely well-run, because perfect order cannot tolerate exceptions, and we were nothing but exceptions.

Vane's fortress ran on schedules. The guards changed watch at intervals so precise you could set a dying clock by them. The supply wagons came through the south gate at the same hour daily, were inspected by the same officer, and were logged—Wren nearly wept with joy when she learned this—in ledgers kept in a records room whose lock, she reported after listening to it through a wall for an hour, was "lonely and proud and desperate to be told it matters."

"You're going to talk to the lock," I said.

"I'm going to *compliment* the lock," Wren said with dignity. "There's a difference."

There was, in fact, a difference, and the difference got us inside.

We went in with the evening supply wagons, folded among crates of frozen grain, because Hesper had spent two days befriending the wagon-master—a hollow-eyed man named Pell who traded warmth-memories at the inn road and hated Vane with the quiet thoroughness of the long-oppressed. Hesper's craft, properly applied, is not picking doors. It is *convincing a door that it was always meant to open*, and apparently this extends to convincing a wagon-master that a loose panel has always been loose, and has always been exactly crow-sized, and human-sized, if humans fold themselves with conviction.

I folded myself with conviction. My knees will file a complaint later.

The records room smelled of paper and lamp oil and something underneath both—old cold, the kind that gets into stone and stays. Rows of shelves marched away into the dark, every ledger spine labeled in Vane's immaculate hand. Wren pressed her ear to the door-lock on the far side, murmured something I chose not to hear, and it clicked open like a sigh.

"That's the strongroom corridor," she whispered. "The cracked shard is close. I can hear it. It sounds—" she frowned, tilting her head, "—it sounds *smug*."

"It would," Odo muttered. "It knows something we don't."

Bram knew something too. As we crept along the corridor, past cells with their little judgement-slots in the doors, past a chamber where a tribunal was adjourning with the scrape of chairs and the rustle of sentences being handed down, the crow grew heavier on my shoulder. Not physically. But grief has weight, and he was carrying all of his.

Then we turned a corner, and I understood why.

The throne room was not hidden. Of course it wasn't; Vane believed in his own rightness, and rightness, he thought, should be displayed. The doors stood open at the end of a long gallery, and beyond them, under a vault of grey stone, rose the throne itself.

It was built of ledgers. Thousands of them, bound in grey, stacked and mortared and carved into a seat and a high back and armrests worn smooth by one pair of hands. Every book was full. Every page, as far as I could see from the gallery's shadow, carried judgments—names, crimes real or invented, sentences rendered, appeals denied. The whole history of a realm's suffering, bound and shelved and made into furniture.

And in the center of the backrest, where a crest should be, set in a cradle of grey iron like a star in a bruise: the fourth shard.

It was the largest of the five. It burned with a cold white light, and the light fell across the ledgers below it, and I swear on my guild oath that the pages turned by themselves sometimes, slowly, like a sleeper shifting under blankets.

"The Crown remembers everything it bound," Bram had written to me once, in the dream, in letters made of falling snow. "That is why it must never belong to only one pair of hands. One pair of hands reads only its own pages."

Vane had built himself a throne that read only his pages. I felt sick.

"Rosalind." Hesper's whisper, at my elbow. She'd found the gallery's service door—it had taken her eleven minutes and one argument with a hinge—and beyond it lay a maintenance stair climbing toward the vault behind the throne room. "There's a way up. Old builders' access. They never sealed it because"—her mouth twisted—"because no one builds doors into a room they believe is unbreakable. Arrogance leaves gaps. I've been saying it for years."

"Everyone says arrogance leaves gaps. Almost no one waits by the gap."

"That's why they're not here and I am." She glanced at the throne room. "Guards change at the top of the hour. Wren's counting. When she whistles, we move."

Odo pressed something into my hand before we climbed: a strip of black cloth. "For your hands," he said. "Shard-light shows everything. Even fingerprints of intent, if the stories are true. Don't touch it bare."

"What stories?"

"The ones I'm inventing right now, but convincingly."

I loved him. In the way you love people who have walked too far beside you to ever be strangers again—not a romance, nothing so simple, just the fierce particular love of the misfit company, sworn outside any ledger. I tied the cloth over my palms and we climbed.

* * *

The vault behind the throne room was a cavity of stone no bigger than a pantry, and it shook.

Not with noise. With *judgment*. Below us, Vane's tribunals ground on, and the whole fortress hummed with the frequency of verdicts, and the fourth shard—visible through a grille in the floor, blazing in its iron cradle—drank it all in. I could feel it pulling at me even through the stone, the way the meridian cold had pulled at memory. It wanted my account. It wanted my name, my debts, my failures, entered neatly in its books.

"Careful," Wren breathed. She had come up the stair with us, leaving Odo and Hesper at the gallery door to manage our exit. Her fingers hovered over the grille. "It's asking questions. Can you feel it? It's asking *who are you, what have you done, who gave you leave*."

"Yes," I said. My teeth were clenched. "It can put it in writing."

Below, boots on marble. The changing of the guard. Wren's whistle came—two notes, soft as breath—and Hesper moved.

I will describe what Hesper did, though I barely saw it, because describing it feels like lying otherwise. She laid both hands against the vault floor, against the seam where the throne's foundation met the stone, and she spoke to it. Not words I caught. Something older. The tone you use with a horse that's been beaten, or a child who's been told too often to be quiet. And the seam—this is the true part, I'd swear it anywhere—the seam *relaxed*. The iron cradle holding the shard tilted, gently, like a cup offered rather than a jewel defended, and the fourth shard slid free into my bandaged hands.

The light went out of the throne room below like a held breath released.

For one moment—one shining, stupid, glorious moment—I held four-fifths of the Crown of Covenants in my two ordinary hands, and I thought: *we've done it. We've actually—*

The map.

My blood went cold ahead of my mind. The map. The copied map, my copy, the one with all five shards marked and the forge beneath the ice circled in Bram's blood and my ink. I had carried it into this fortress because I could not bear to leave it behind, because it was the first impossible thing that had ever been *mine*—

"It's in my coat," I whispered. "Hesper. The map is in my coat and my coat is—"

Down in the throne room, a door I had not accounted for opened.

Serath Vane did not shout. Shouting, I understood in that instant, was for men unsure of their authority. He simply stood in the doorway of his own throne room, winter-grey and neat as a filed document, and looked up—at the empty cradle, at the darkened ledgers, at the grille in his ceiling with its loosened seam—and then, slowly, at the service stair, where four intruders stood frozen in the act of becoming legends.

"There you are," he said, in the tone of a magistrate confirming an appointment. "Ledger-keeper Almeida. I wondered when you'd come to me. You've saved me the trouble of a summons."

Everything happened very fast after that, and also very slowly, the way disasters do.

Hesper moved first—of course Hesper moved first—slamming her shoulder into the vault's old builders' door and *unmaking* its willingness to stay shut, and we tumbled through into the dark of the maintenance ways while Vane's voice followed us, unhurried, terrible:

"Keep the shard, if you like. Keep all four. A crown is not a collection, girl, it is a *verdict*—and I hold the map to the fifth, and the forge, and the law." A pause, and then, softer, worse: "You were always going to lead me there. Thank you for your diligence. It will be noted in the record."

We ran. Through walls built for builders and forgotten by judges, down stairs that Hesser coaxed open and Wren's locks sighed apart for, out through the south gate's shadow in the wake of the night wagons, across the ice plain with the fortress's grey light shrinking behind us and Bram a wild weight on my shoulder and Odo gasping curses about his shoulder and the universe generally.

We did not stop until the ridge where we'd lain that morning. Then we collapsed into the snow, four thieves with three-quarters of a stolen god, and I laughed—actually laughed, high and unhinged, until Wren looked at me with alarm.

"We did it," I said. "We took the shard. Out of his *throne*. Out from under his—"

"And he took the map," Hesper said quietly.

The laugh died in my throat.

I checked my coat anyway. Of course I did. Empty pocket, and a single sheet of grey paper left in its place, folded with terrible neatness. On it, in Vane's immaculate hand:

*Received: one map, one destination, one forge. Rendered: your passage, this night, unwitnessed. Consider us in negotiation. — S.V.*

"He let us take it," Wren said. Her voice was very small. "He *let* us."

"No." I stared at the note until the words blurred. "No, he didn't let us. We took the shard. That part was real—that was *ours*, we earned it, we bled for the other three." I stood up, knees screaming, and looked north, toward the ice waste and the ancient forge beneath it, where five shards waited to become one covenant or one chain. "But he has the map, and he has a head start, and he thinks a crown is a verdict."

Bram hopped down onto my knee. He looked up at me, and in his stolen-crow eyes was something I'd seen only once before, in a dream: the last guardian of the Crown, watching everything he'd scattered come back together in the wrong hands.

"So we don't follow the map," I said. The idea arrived whole, the way the best and worst ideas do. "We don't need it. *He* needs it. We go to the forge the way the covenant would want us to arrive—not as thieves chasing treasure, but as witnesses. With voices." I looked around at them: a disgraced door-maker, a debt-collector with a ruined shoulder, a girl who talked to locks, a crow who used to be a man. "The assembly. The realm's last free assembly guards the forge. If we reach them first—if we bring them the shards and the truth—then when Vane arrives, he won't find a treasure to seize. He'll find a court he can't preside over."

Odo groaned and got to his feet. "You're proposing we outrun a magistrate across the meridian waste to win an argument."

"I'm proposing we *arrive*," I said. "It's the one thing guilds have always done better than governments."

Hesper smiled that smile again, and pointed north, and somewhere behind us the fortress of order glowed grey and certain against the dark, already dispatching its wagons, its ledgers, its law.

He had the map. He had the schedule. He had every advantage but one.

He thought the path ahead belonged to him.

It belonged to whoever reached the end of it first—and for the first time since a stranger died on our doorstep, I intended to run like the guild I was going to build depended on it.

Because it did.
