## 18. The Forge Beneath the Ice

Bram woke us an hour before dawn by screaming a word into Wren's ear.

The word was *down*, and he screamed it in the voice of a fishmonger, which is how I knew it mattered. Bram's stolen phrases came in all registers — a child counting, a magistrate declaiming, once, memorably, a woman singing a lullaby in a language none of us knew and all of us wept at. But when he used the fishmonger, it was always urgent. Hesper said it was because grief lived loudest in the cheapest voices. She had never explained what she meant by that, and I had learned not to ask.

We broke camp in the blue dark. The meridian waste had ended two days past, giving way to a country of black stone and slow ice — glaciers that moved like sleeping animals, so gradually you could stand on one all day and swear it hadn't stirred, then find your footprints gone by morning. Odo walked with a stick now, his ribs still bound from the river, and refused with tremendous dignity to let anyone take his pack.

"The forge is under that," Bram said, perched on my shoulder, his beak aimed at the largest glacier — a wall of ice half a horizon wide, glowing faintly from within, the way a banked stove glows.

"Under *through*," Hesper corrected, squinting. "Nothing is under that. That's a mountain wearing a blanket."

"Under," Bram insisted, and then, in the fishmonger's voice: "DOWN."

* * *

Hesper found the door by lunchtime, because of course she did. It was set into the glacier's flank where the ice curved away from the stone beneath — a slab of grey metal no taller than a well-cover, unmarked except for a single line of script that ran around its rim like a sentence chasing its own tail.

Wren knelt and put her palm flat against it. She did this with locks the way other people did it with horses or harps; the lock would tell her things. Usually she reported bolts and counterweights. This time she sat back on her heels and looked troubled.

"It's not locked," she said. "It's *listening*. There's a difference. A lock wants to keep you out. This wants to know why you're in."

"Then we answer honestly," I said, and immediately heard how that sounded coming from a guild whose entire existence was a lie of omission three centuries deep.

Odo snorted. "Honestly. From us."

"We've been honest with each other since the inn," I said. "That has to count for something. It counted at the inn."

"That cost me my best favor," Odo muttered, but he said it without heat, the way you recite a scar.

I crouched at the door and spoke to it as if it were an elder of the guild, which is to say politely, clearly, and with the understanding that it might ignore me entirely. "My name is Rosalind Almeida. I was ledger-keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things. I am not that anymore — the elders disavowed us, and the guildhall burned, and whether any of them live I don't know. What I am now is one of five people who walked here carrying shards of the Crown of Covenants, and what I want is to see it reforged. Not kept. Reforged. And given away."

The silence went on long enough that I began composing apologies. Then the script around the rim brightened, letter by letter, like a wick catching, and the door swung inward on a stair of black stone descending into warm gold light.

"Well," said Hesper. "That's more than my doors ever gave me. Mine just opened."

"They were sulking," said Wren.

* * *

The stair went down farther than the city above had foundations. I counted four hundred steps and then stopped counting, because the walls had begun to change — the black stone giving way to seams of something luminous, veins of amber running through the rock like the memory of fire. The air warmed. Somewhere below, faintly, there was a sound I couldn't place until we were nearly at the bottom: many voices, low and steady, speaking all at once but somehow together, the way a choir sounds from outside the cathedral.

Bram pressed himself flat against my neck. His claws pricked through my coat.

"What is it?" I whispered.

"*They're real,*" he said, in the voice of a small boy, which was worse than the fishmonger. "*I thought they'd be dead.*"

The stair opened into a cavern that was not a cavern. Someone had built here, ages ago — vaulted halls of the same amber-veined stone, lit from within, and filling those halls, gathered in tiers upon tiers of seats carved from the living rock, were people. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Every kind the dying realm could offer: glass-folk translucent with hunger, stone-skinned quarry folk, tall figures robed in frost who might have been the frozen river's cousins, humans of both realms, and creatures I had no ledger-entry for. They were not watching us. They were talking — all of them, in that low choral murmur — passing words up and down the tiers like buckets in a fire-line.

At our entrance the murmur didn't stop. It *turned*, the way weather turns. I felt every voice bend toward us without a single conversation ceasing.

An old woman detached herself from the nearest tier and came toward us. She walked with a stick of black ice that steamed gently in the warm air, and her face was lined the way old ledgers are lined — every crease an entry. When she reached us she looked at each of us in turn, longest at Odo's bandaged ribs and shortest, curiously, at Bram.

"You are the ones the crow promised," she said.

"I don't remember promising anything," said Bram, in my own voice, which startled me badly.

"You promised before you were a crow." She said it without accusation, the way you state that water is wet. "I am Maren Ostreth. I keep the assembly's roll. Come. You are expected, and the forge does not wait well."

* * *

She led us down the central aisle while thousands of voices murmured around us, and I confess I spent most of that walk trying to understand what I was seeing, because it contradicted everything the guild had ever taught me about the realm below. We were taught they were subjects. Remnants. Folk who had once been bound to us by the Crown and had fallen into barbarism when it was stolen — that was the word the elders used, *stolen*, as though the Crown were a purse and not a covenant. We were taught their cities dimmed because they lacked the discipline to tend their own light.

What I saw instead was an assembly. Functioning. Arguing, by the sound of it, but arguing *together*, in a hall beneath a glacier, while the winter killed everything above them. They had no crown. They had no king. They had each other and apparently that had been enough to keep a parliament alive inside a mountain while the world froze.

"How long have you been here?" I asked Maren.

"Since Vane took the Crown. Four hundred years, give or take a bad decade." She tapped her stick on the stone. "When the binding broke, everyone assumed whoever held the Crown would become the ruler. So the wise ones hid, and the ambitious ones hunted, and the rest of us did the arithmetic. A crown on one head is a war. A crown held by many hands is a hearth. We chose the hearth."

"And the elders above? Did they know?"

Maren glanced at me sidelong. "Child, your guild signed the original covenant. Your guild was the witness. Where do you think the forge got its first fire?"

I stopped walking. The murmur of the assembly seemed very loud suddenly, and very close, like standing inside a bell.

"The guild *founded*—"

"The guild *witnessed*," Maren corrected. "There's a difference, and it matters, and it's the whole reason you're here. But yes. Your guild's founders stood where you're standing and swore the first covenant alongside ours. Then your side took the Crown home for safekeeping, and safekeeping became secrecy, and secrecy became forgetting. We kept the forge. You kept the map. Between us we lost the point entirely."

Hesper made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. "So the guild didn't fall. It *drifted*."

"Everything drifts," said Maren. "That's why covenants need renewing. Which brings us to the unpleasant part."

* * *

The forge lay beyond the assembly hall, at the heart of the glacier, and it was nothing like any forge I had imagined. I had pictured hammers and anvils, coals and bellows. What waited at the end of Maren's corridor was a chamber of clear ice, and in the center of the ice, suspended, a fire that burned without consuming — the same gold as the veins in the stone, the same gold as the light in the door's listening script. Around the fire, set into a ring of black basalt, were four shallow sockets. Three of them held shards.

Our shards. The glassmaker's, cracked and mended with a seam of silver Wren had poured; the second, recovered from Vane's own courier after the snare turned; the third, bought with Odo's blood at the frozen river. They sat in their sockets glowing softly, and the moment the last of us crossed the threshold, all three brightened like lamps recognizing a face.

"The fourth shard is in Vane's throne of judgments," Maren said. "Yes. We know. The assembly hears everything the ice hears, and the ice goes everywhere. He sits on it nightly and calls it justice." Her mouth thinned. "He will come here. He has always meant to come here. The forge is the only place the Crown can be remade, and Vane is not a man who tolerates unfinished work."

"Then we bring him the fourth shard ourselves," said Odo. "Take it off his throne while he sleeps."

"He doesn't sleep," said Wren quietly. Everyone looked at her. "I listened to his locks. At the stronghold. Locks know their owners. His don't sleep either. They just wait."

"Then we take it while he watches," said Hesper, "and we're quick about it."

"No," said Maren.

The single word dropped into the chamber and the fire itself seemed to lean back from it. She limped to the ring of basalt and laid her hand on the stone between two sockets.

"This is the part nobody told you, because Bram scattered the shards before anyone could write it down, and the ones who knew were mostly dead. Listen carefully, ledger-keeper, because this is your part." She looked at me. "The Crown cannot be reforged by force. It cannot even be reforged by possession. All five shards, yes — necessary, not sufficient. The forge will melt them. The forge will pour them. And the metal will cool into a circlet with no more power than a wedding band unless—" she paused, choosing, I realized, with a craftsman's care, "—unless it is sworn to."

"Sworn to," I repeated. "By whom?"

"Everyone." She spread her hand at the chamber, the hall behind it, the tiers of voices, the glacier, the two realms entire. "That was the original design. Not a king's crown. A covenant's crown. It was never meant to sit on a head. It was meant to sit in a hall, in the middle of an assembly, and hold the oath of everyone who chose to be bound by it. One wielder makes it a weapon. Many voices make it a hinge — the thing that lets two worlds swing open to each other. The founders understood this. Vane understands it too. It's why he stole it rather than claiming it openly, and it's why he's building a throne instead of a hall. He believes if he alone swears the forging — if one will is strong enough, disciplined enough, *certain* enough — the Crown will accept him as its whole covenant."

"Will it?" asked Wren.

"No," said Maren. "But it will *half-work*. It will bind the realms shut forever under one judge, cold and orderly and dead, and it will do it so thoroughly that no one after him will ever be able to open the door again. A half-made covenant is worse than none. It's a lock welded shut from the inside."

The fire burned on, patient and golden, and I looked at it and felt the whole shape of the last months turn over in my chest like a page. Every piece of it suddenly faced the right way. The inn where truth was currency. The glassmaker demanding a debt be honored rather than a price be paid. Odo spending his dearest favor. The company swearing an oath outside any ledger, five voices in the snow. Even the elders' betrayal — even the burning of the guildhall — fit. Everything we had done that the old guild would have called reckless, everything Vane would have called disorder, had been practice. Rehearsal. The covenant was not a treasure you carried. It was a thing you *did*, over and over, until doing it was what you were.

"That's why he released me," I said slowly. "At his court. He wasn't playing with me. He was *recruiting* me. He wanted the guild's ledger-keeper — the last witness of the old covenant — to be the one who brought him the shards. If I handed them over, the forging would carry the guild's authority in it. Legitimacy. He wasn't setting a trap for us. He was setting one for the *oath*."

Maren smiled for the first time, and it was not a comforting smile. "Now you see him clearly. Good. Because he sees you clearly too, and he is coming, and we have perhaps days."

"Then we swear it now," said Hesper. "We have three shards. We start the oath with three."

"A partial oath holds nothing," said Maren. "Worse — a partial oath, abandoned, teaches the forge refusal. It has happened before. There are ruins in the north that used to be kingdoms that tried."

"Then we need the fourth shard," said Odo, "and we need it fast, and we need it off the lap of the most dangerous man in two worlds."

"Yes," said Maren. "And there is a way. Not a good way. The assembly has argued it for four hundred years and hated every version of it." She turned, and the firelight caught the lines of her face, and for a moment she looked every year of her age and then some. "The truth-debt road. The old inn's road runs under the meridian and comes up inside Vane's stronghold — the builders of the inn were the builders of the throne room, in a more honest century. The door opens only for a debt of truth that has never been spoken aloud. Something true that the speaker has never told anyone. Paid willingly, at the threshold, by someone the stronghold's wards cannot foresee."

Everyone looked at everyone else. The fire murmured. On my shoulder, Bram shifted his weight, and his claws were trembling.

"There's a version of this," he said at last, in a voice I hadn't heard him use before — not stolen, not borrowed, but worn smooth and old, like the last coin in a purse, "where I pay that debt. There's a version where I walk in the front and give Vane what he actually wants, which was never the shards."

"Don't," I said.

"You don't even know what I was going to—"

"I know you," I said. "I've known you since you screamed *down* at me in a fishmonger's voice. Whatever you're about to volunteer, the answer is that we go together or the oath isn't worth the breath. That's rather the entire point of the evening, in case anyone missed it."

The assembly hall behind us had gone quiet. Thousands of voices, silent at once, and in that silence I understood that they had heard — that the ice carried everything, that we had been speaking our thesis into a hall full of witnesses, and that somewhere in the tiers above, hands were already being raised to vote on whether to believe us.

Maren Ostreth looked from me to the crow to the fire, and exhaled.

"So be it," she said. "The assembly will prepare the road. Rest while you can. Eat. Write your names in my roll — all of you, even the bird, *especially* the bird — because if this goes wrong, someone should know exactly who tried." She was already limping toward the corridor when she stopped, and without turning around, added:

"One more thing, ledger-keeper. The fourth shard is in his throne, yes. But the fifth—" and here her voice did something I had never heard an elder's voice do, which was hesitate, "—the fifth shard was never lost. The founders split it from the others on purpose and gave it to the one party both realms trusted to keep it hidden and keep it safe. Your guild has been sitting on it for four hundred years. In the ledger-hall. Under the floor of the counting room, where the youngest keeper sweeps."

The cold that went through me had nothing to do with the glacier.

The guildhall had burned. Vane had burned its doors open, and his men had walked its floors, and the elders had disavowed us to save themselves — and the fifth shard, the keystone, the piece without which every oath was a ruin in the north —

"—was under the broom closet," I finished aloud. My voice sounded very far away. "Where I swept every morning for eleven years."

Behind us, far above, through miles of stone and ice, something began to glow on the horizon of my mind's eye: a burnt hall, a broken floor, a magistrate in winter-grey kneeling in the ash with his pale hands in the rubble, smiling.

Vane hadn't needed the map to find the forge.

He had needed it to know the forge was empty.
