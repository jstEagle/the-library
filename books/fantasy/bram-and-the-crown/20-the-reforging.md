## 20. The Reforging

The cold that came off Vane's forging was not the ordinary cold of winter. I had walked through the meridian waste and felt winter eat my memories out of my head like moths in a coat pocket, and that had been bad. This was worse. This was cold with an opinion. This was cold that believed, sincerely and with citations, that it knew what was best for everyone.

We came up out of the truth-debt inn's old road into the forge's undercroft, and the first thing I saw was light—the wrong kind, the kind you get when something holy is being done badly. Above us, through a grate in the ceiling, Vane's voice rolled down like a sermon delivered from the bottom of a well.

"One crown," he was saying. "One law. One winter, ordered and therefore merciful. You have all suffered under a covenant of *many*. I offer you a covenant of *one*."

"He's actually giving a speech," Wren whispered beside me. "He's monologuing at a frozen crowd."

"Magistrates," Hesper said grimly, and put her shoulder to the grate.

Odo couldn't help lift it. He leaned against me instead, grey-faced, his arm bound where the frozen river had taken its toll for the third shard, and I felt him weigh more than he should have—a whole man becoming a debt I wasn't sure we could pay. "Go on," he said. "I'll hold the door behind you. It's what I'm for."

"It is not what you're for," I said. "It is one of many things you are for, and I am making a list."

"Ledger-keeper," he said, and smiled with cracked lips. "Always keeping accounts."

Hesper lifted the grate the way she did everything—quietly, expertly, as if doors were animals that only needed to be spoken to correctly—and we came up into the forge.

* * *

I had expected a forge to be a forge: fire pit, anvils, soot. The place where the Crown was first made was more like the inside of a promise. The walls were ice that burned without melting, gold-white, and in their depths moved shapes—hands, mostly, hundreds of hands, reaching toward each other across the stone. The old assembly's mark. Many hands joined at the wrist, forming a ring.

And in the center, suspended over a fire that gave no heat, hung the shards.

Five of them. Three ours by blood and bargain; two surrendered by Vane's own hand into the flames, because a crown cannot be forged from half itself even by a man who believes order forgives everything. They turned slowly above the fire like planets, and where their light crossed, I could see the shape they wanted to make: not a circlet, exactly. A ring of joined figures. A crown that was never meant to sit on anyone's head at all.

Vane stood beneath it with his arms raised, and he looked—this is the part I will never tell anyone but this page—he looked like a man praying. Winter-grey coat, frost in his eyebrows, that patient magistrate's face worn smooth by decades of judging other people's failures. Around the walls of the forge stood the frozen assembly, the realm's last free folk, locked mid-gesture in attitudes of witness. And before them all, Serath Vane was rebuilding heaven by himself, because he genuinely could not imagine why heaven had ever needed help.

The half-Crown flared. He cried out—not triumph; pain—and I saw blood on his fingers where the shards refused to knit. Of course they refused. You cannot be a covenant alone. It is not a failure of effort. It is a failure of category, like trying to carry water in a closed fist.

"You're hurting it," I said.

He turned. He did not seem surprised, which was somehow the most frightening thing in the room.

"The ledger-girl." He said it almost fondly. "I released you from my court precisely so you would come. Order requires a witness, Rosalind Almeida. Even mine."

"That's not what witnesses are for."

"Isn't it?" He gestured at the burning ring above him, and I watched the light gutter where his blood had touched the metal. "Your guild hid beneath a city for three hundred years, keeping accounts of wonders no one was allowed to see. Your elders erased a dying man rather than risk inconvenience. Don't speak to me of what things are for. I watched both realms rot under a covenant nobody remembered how to keep. I am the only one who kept faith with winter. I am the only one who *came*."

Behind me, very softly, a crow said: *"Bram."*

Just once. Just the name. But the whole forge heard it.

Vane's face changed. Underneath the conviction, for one heartbeat, there was something older and sadder—the look of a man who has been carrying a body a long way and has finally been asked to set it down.

"You," he said quietly. "You were there. That night on the doorstep. I thought I had—" His jaw tightened. "He scattered five shards across two realms rather than let me hold one. Do you know what that cost him? Do you know what it cost *me*, to hunt a friend across a dying world?"

"You call that friendship?" Hesper said, from my left, her hand already finding the seam of a door that wasn't there yet.

"I call it *consistency*," Vane said. "Bram swore to guard the Crown. I swore to perfect it. We have both kept our oaths. Only one of us kept his head while doing it." He raised his hands again to the burning ring. "Stand witness, ledger-girl. Or interfere, and stand judged. Either way, the winter finishes falling tonight, and after it, no one will ever have to choose anything again."

The shards turned. The fire rose. And Bram left my shoulder.

* * *

I want to record exactly what happened, because this is the guild's new ledger and ledgers exist so that memory can be trusted later. But exactness fails me here, the way it fails everyone eventually, so I will write what I saw and let the truth make of it what it likes.

A crow cannot carry much. Everyone knows this. What a crow carried, in the air between Vane's hands and the burning Crown, was himself—all that remained of himself, the last human fragment, hoarded through years of feathers and stolen phrases, spent in one motion like a man emptying his purse into a beggar's bowl.

Bram flew into the light.

There was a sound I have no word for. Not a shattering—shattering implies something broken past use. This was more like a bell struck so hard it forgets to be a bell. The half-forged Crown rang wrong, and then it rang *apart*, and Vane was thrown backward off his feet with his palms smoking, and where the crow had been there was—for less than a heartbeat, no longer, barely time enough to notice—

A man. Frostbitten. Familiar. Smiling at me the way you smile at someone from a great distance, or from the far end of a long life, and mouthing something I could not hear but understood anyway:

*Not one wielder.*

Then the shards fell, five bright pieces ringing off the forge floor like the last notes of a song, and the man-shaped absence where Bram had been folded up into snow, and the snow went out.

"Bram!" My voice cracked on it. I was on my knees among the shards before I'd decided to move. Nothing. Feathers, already cooling. The smallest drift of snow, melting in a room full of fire.

Vane was struggling up against the anvil-throne, his beautiful ordered world ringing around him. "Fool bird," he rasped. "Fool bird—he's *delayed* nothing—I still hold the map, I still know the rite, I will simply begin again—"

"No," I said.

I picked up the shards. All five. They were hot and they cut me and I held them anyway, because somebody once told me—well, cawed at me, technically—that some knowledge cannot be unkept, and some debts must be honored, and I was the youngest ledger-keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things, and I finally understood what our impossible thing was going to be.

"You think a crown is power," I said, standing, walking toward him, my hands bleeding light onto the floor. "That's your whole philosophy, isn't it? Somebody has to hold it, so it might as well be you, because you'd hold it *correctly*. But look at it." I opened my bleeding hands. Five shards. A broken ring of joined figures. "It was never meant to be held. It was meant to be *kept*. By everyone. Together. That's what a covenant is, Serath. That's what your tribunal never understood. You didn't steal the Crown. You stole the *we*."

"And what," he said, and there was frost coming off him now in sheets, rage feeding winter feeding rage, "do you propose to do about it? Reforge it yourself? Take up the throne? Go on, girl. Pick up the crown. Every hand that reaches for it proves my case."

And here is the thing I will be proud of until I die, which, thanks to everything that happened next, may take considerably longer than expected:

I turned my back on him.

I carried the five shards past the fallen magistrate, past the frozen assembly, past Odo holding the door and Hesper holding the wall and Wren holding their breath, to the center of the forge, where the fire waited—cold fire, old fire, fire that had been waiting three hundred years for enough hands.

"I don't propose to do anything about it," I said. "I propose we do. All of us. Right now. Assembly of the realm below—witnesses, judges, glassmakers and river-singers and whoever else is thawing out there—come and put your hands on this. Misfits of the Guild of Impossible Things, come and put your hands on this. Anyone who has ever been invisible, indebted, disgraced, disavowed, or dead and come back as a crow—come and put your hands on this."

Wren laughed, wet-eyed, and came. Then Hesper, swearing fluently in three dialects of door. Then Odo, leaning on the doorframe, one good hand stretched out. And beyond them, crackling like spring ice on a pond, the frozen assembly began—one figure, then ten, then the whole ring of them moving, stiff, astonished, alive—to come and put their hands on this.

"What are you doing?" Vane screamed from the floor. "*Stop.* One crown! One—"

"One *we*," I said.

We forged it together, and it took no time at all and also my entire life. The fire took the shards and took our oath with them, and the words I said weren't grand ones because I don't know any grand ones. I said the guild's true name, the one the elders had let gather dust: *the Guild of Impossible Things, refounded, doors open, both realms welcome, no member invisible, no debt unrecorded, no wonder unshared.* Wren said the locks would open. Hesper said the doors would answer. Odo said every favor owed would be paid. The assembly said—we heard it as one voice with a thousand cracks in it—the covenant, remade.

The Crown came out of the fire whole. Not a circlet for a head. A ring for a hall. And when it settled into the stone of the forge, set into the assembly floor where anyone could touch it and no one could wear it, the winter outside made a sound like a held breath finally let go.

Vane was gone by the time we turned around. Just a scorch-mark in the shape of a kneeling man, and frost running out under the door, thinning as it went—his order collapsing, thaw following him out into the dark like a tide coming in after him.

I didn't run after him. There would be tribulations; there always are. But I stood in the doorway a moment and watched the first true thaw of three hundred years come racing down the mountainside toward the glass cities, water singing over stone, and I thought: *he'll hate this. He'll hate every drop of it.* And I found, examining myself honestly the way a ledger-keeper must, that I felt no triumph about that at all. Only a tired, enormous hope, and grief underneath it, sharp as shard-metal.

Because the thaw was running down the mountain, and the realms were waking, and we had won—

and there was a small pile of snow on the forge floor that used to be my friend, and it was melting, and I knelt down next to it and stayed there a long time.

* * *

They gave us the morning. Realms are generous that way when they've just survived.

By dawn—which the realm below hadn't seen in living memory—water was running in the streets of the glass city and the river was trying to remember the middle of its song. The assembly hall filled with folk trading warmth for warmth instead of memories for it, which, Odo observed, was going to ruin the local economy and about time too.

I sat by the melt-spot until the sun came through the high windows and took the last of it. Wren sat with me. Neither of us said anything useful. There isn't any.

Then Hesper appeared in the doorway—the actual doorway, which she had spent the night teaching to open honestly—and her face had an expression on it that I had learned, over a hard road and a frozen waste, to trust the way sailors trust weather.

"Rosalind," she said. "You'd better come see this."

On the threshold of the forge, where the snow had blown in all night, lay a single black feather. Except it wasn't a feather. Feathers don't gleam like that. Feathers aren't warm.

And as I picked it up, from somewhere—above us, below us, inside the ring of joined figures set into the floor, inside my own ridiculous stubborn heart—a voice said, in stolen phrases, in borrowed words, in a grammar only one person in two realms had ever managed to make charming:

*"First entry. New ledger. Write it down."*

I stood there with the feather in my fist and the thaw roaring down the mountain, and I laughed until I cried, and then I cried until I laughed, and Wren said, very gently, "Rosalind. Is that—"

"Get my bag," I said. "Somebody get my bag. I have a ledger to open."
