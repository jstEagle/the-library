## 21. A Crown of Ordinary Voices

I have made a great many doors in my life. Doors of oak and doors of iron, doors that only opened for the right hand and doors that pretended to be walls. I was, once, the finest door-maker the guild had ever produced, which is precisely why they disgraced me: I made a door too honest. It opened for anyone who truly needed it, and it turned out the guild had never wanted a door like that at all.

So believe me when I tell you I know thresholds. And what we built in the assembly hall beneath the forge, in the days after the thaw began, was no throne, no pedestal, no locked case behind velvet rope. It was a threshold.

The last free assembly of the realm below — forty-odd survivors with frost-scars and stubbornness in equal measure — had voted on everything by voice for nine hundred years, and then someone had decided a crown would be more efficient. We all know how that went.

"We're not putting it up high," Rosalind said, when the question came. She had frost still fading from her fingertips, pale patches that were slowly remembering how to be pink. "We're putting it *in*."

"In what?" asked the assembly's speaker, a glasswright named Corvane whose left arm had gone translucent in the long winter and never quite come back.

"The floor," Rosalind said. "Where everyone stands."

* * *

It took us four days. Wren talked to the stone the way she talks to locks — low, patient, half-singing — until the assembly hall's floor consented to open a hollow exactly one pace inside the entrance, so that every single person who came to speak would have to stand over it. Odo laid the Crown in with his own hands. He was still using a crutch, and he refused help down the ladder, and swore at the rungs in three languages, two of them possibly invented, but he would not let another soul touch it.

"It's a debt," he said when I raised an eyebrow at him. "The biggest one outstanding. You don't hand off settling a debt. Bad craft."

The Crown did not gleam. I want to be clear about that, because songs will be sung about this eventually and songs are liars with good posture. The reforged Crown of Covenants looked, in that moment, like five shards of old metal that had been wept on and hammered and argued over, dull as a pot-bottom, warm — that part was true, it was warm, warm as a living hand — and entirely unimpressive.

Rosalind spoke the covenant into it. Not alone. That was the whole point, the thing Vane had died — or frozen, or dissolved; I saw what I saw and I'm still not sure — not understanding. She spoke first, because someone has to start, and then Corvane spoke, and the glassmaker who had given us the first shard spoke, and Odo, and Wren, and I, and then the assembly, voice by voice by voice, ordinary people saying extraordinary plain things: *I will keep the door. I will pay what I owe. I will not let the winter take what spring is owed.*

When the last voice finished, the Crown sank into the floor like a coin into deep water, and the stone closed over it, and there is now a faint circle in the assembly hall floor that glows very slightly when someone tells the truth standing on it.

Wren stands on it constantly. She claims this is coincidence. Nobody believes her.

* * *

The thaw reached the glass cities on the third morning after the forging, and I wish I could make you see it.

We had come through those cities in the dark of the long winter, when the towers were dimming like lamps running out of oil and the people traded their own memories for warmth. Now the light came back down the spires the way honey comes down a spoon — slow, thick, gold at the edges — and where it touched, frost didn't melt so much as *let go*. The glass rang. All of it, the whole city at once, a chord so low I felt it in my teeth and my bad knee and whatever it is in my chest that I keep meaning to have looked at.

People came out into the streets. Some of them were missing years of themselves, sold away piece by piece across the cold decades, and some of those years came walking home out of the thaw like dogs that had wandered off — memories returned to their owners in the melting ice, blinking and confused. A woman in the glass market got back the face of her mother after sixty years and sat down in a puddle and laughed until she cried, and I stood there watching with my arms crossed like a sensible woman and did some crying of my own, quietly, where the dignity couldn't see.

Odo found me there. He leaned on his crutch beside me and said nothing for a while, which for Odo is practically a eulogy.

"Going to collect on any of these favors people owe us?" I said.

"Half of them are dead," he said. "And the other half are going to owe me breakfast for the next fifty years. I've decided that's enough." He shifted his weight. "Hesper. The innkeeper sent word. There's a road opening. The old road, the truth-debt road. It runs up under your city."

"I know," I said. "Rosalind told us. Doors reopening both ways." I looked at him. "You're not coming above, are you."

Odo collects favors the way other men collect coins, and he had spent nearly all of himself on that frozen meridian, taking the blow that should have broken Wren, and I thought — I don't know what I thought. That he'd earned the right to walk away from all of it.

"Someone has to keep the ledgers honest down here," he said. "And someone owes an innkeeper a truth or two." He paused. "Also I can't climb ladders anymore. Have you seen the stairs to your guildhall? Who designed those? You? They're a menace."

"That door was my finest work," I said, "and I'll thank you to slander it properly."

He grinned. Then neither of us said anything, because there was a thing coming we both knew was coming, and favors and jokes are how men like Odo and women like me carry things without dropping them.

"Bram," he said finally.

"Yes," I said.

* * *

We buried him beside the forge, on a hillside that had been under snow since before any of us were born and was now, astonishingly, green.

There was a body. I'll say that plainly, because we argued about it among ourselves afterward and I know rumors travel faster than truth. There was a body: a man, tall, frost-burned past recognizing, dressed in the ragged remains of a guardian's coat, lying where Bram the crow had fallen in the shattering of Vane's forging. As if the crow had been the coat all along and something human had finally climbed out of it. Or as if the man had been the crow all along and the thaw had simply returned him to himself. The realm's oldest records say the Crown's last guardian scattered its shards and paid with his human shape. The records do not say whether the payment could ever be refunded.

Nobody knows. Rosalind says the story never fully explains itself, and stories shouldn't have to; that's how you tell a true one from a made-up one.

What we know is this: the crow had followed a stranger to our doorstep on the first night of snow. The stranger had pressed a map into Rosalind's hands and whispered his own name — *Bram* — and died. And the crow had known landmarks he should never have known, grieved over cities he'd never seen in that shape, and in the end thrown his last fragment of humanity into the forge to break a tyrant's crown before it could finish becoming one.

Whether he was the stranger come back small and feathered, or the guardian who had lent the dying man his name, or both, or neither — the hillside didn't care, and neither, finally, did we.

Wren carved the marker herself. She's the youngest of us and she cried the whole time and kept working anyway, which is the definition of grief done properly, if you ask me. The stone reads:

**BRAM**
**WHO CARRIED WHAT HE COULD**
**AND GAVE AWAY THE REST**

Underneath, smaller, because Wren insisted:

**HE TALKED MORE THAN MOST CROWS AND LESS THAN MOST MEN, AND WE LIKED HIM EITHER WAY.**

Rosalind buried the map. The original, bloodstained, creased into softness by five hands passing it over frozen wastes — she folded it one last time along its old lines and laid it in the earth next to the marker, and covered it, and stood there a long moment with her hand flat on the turned soil.

"He gave it to me to burn," she said. "Or to follow. He let me choose. That was the whole covenant in one gesture, wasn't it. He handed it over and stepped back."

"Sentimental nonsense," I said, because someone had to, and because my voice was doing something embarrassing. "He was a crow. Crows leave shiny things on windowsills. It's basically the same thing."

"Hesper," Rosalind said.

"He ate half my rations on the meridian," I went on, "and complained about the quality. A crow. Complaining. About rations. I fed him my best dried plums and he gave me that look, that *look*—"

"Hesper." Rosalind put her hand on my arm, and I stopped, and we stood together in the new grass while the wind came up warm off the thawing valley for the first time in nine hundred years.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about surviving something: the surviving is the easy part. The hard part is the morning after, when you wake up and the emergency is over and you have to figure out who you are when nothing is trying to kill you.

We had been a company. Companies exist because of quests. Take away the quest and you have four misfits and a crutch standing on a green hillside wondering why the world suddenly got so large.

Corvane solved it, in the way of practical people everywhere, by handing us paperwork.

"The assembly votes tomorrow," she said, "on the matter of the guild. Your guild. Above." She tapped the table with her translucent fingers, click click click. "They disavowed you, I hear. To save themselves. And then their hall burned anyway."

"It didn't burn," Rosalind said. "Vane's people opened the doors and took what they could carry. The hall stands. Empty."

"Then fill it again," Corvane said. "But hear the assembly's terms, ledger-keeper, because we voted on them last night and I drew the short straw for telling you. If your guild reopens, it opens for both realms. Doors here. Doors there. Members from below holding ledgers above, and the reverse. No more invisible guild. No more secret stair that serves one world and pretends the other doesn't exist. The covenant you swore on the floor — your guild signed it too. You just haven't noticed yet."

I watched Rosalind take that in. I want to record, for the ledger of history, that she did not look frightened. She looked like a woman being handed exactly the thing she'd wanted her whole life, in a shape slightly larger than her hands, and squaring her shoulders to hold it anyway.

"The elders will never agree," she said.

"You're not asking the elders," Corvane said. "You're the one who reforged the Crown. Ask yourself."

* * *

We went back up through the old road — the truth-debt road, the innkeeper's ancient passage that ran beneath the roots of both worlds — because it seemed fitting, though I'll admit I also wanted to test the acoustics of a tunnel that charges honesty for tolls. (It took a memory from each of us at the first gate: not stole, *took*, gently, held it up, and gave it back warmer. Wren's was of her first opened lock. Mine was of my disgrace. I suspect the road kept a little of mine anyway. Good. It can have it.)

The guildhall above was exactly as Vane's men had left it: doors hanging wide, shelves stripped of anything portable, ash ground into the flagstones, and the long tables bare except for dust and one elder's abandoned teacup with a fossil of tea in the bottom.

The elders themselves were nowhere. Scattered, cowed, or simply gone — the invisibility they'd spent centuries perfecting having turned out, in the end, to be indistinguishable from vanishing.

Rosalind walked the length of the hall. Bram's crow used to perch on that lintel; I caught her looking up at it, and looking away, and looking up again, the way you check a window for someone who isn't coming home.

"Well," I said, into the quiet. "It needs a sweep."

"It needs everything," Wren said. Her voice echoed. She'd brought a lock-pick out of pure habit and was fidgeting with it. "Doors, roof, records, everything. And we don't have a charter. And we don't have elders. And we don't have permission."

"Good," said Rosalind.

I looked at her.

"Every impossible thing this guild ever catalogued," she said, "and the members thought the impossible part was the inventory. Bottled echoes. Debts owed by the moon." She picked up the abandoned teacup, considered it, and set it back down with ceremony, as if it were a relic, which I suppose it was. "The impossible thing was always this. A guild with its doors honestly open. A ledger that belongs to both worlds. Ordinary people deciding they're allowed."

She turned around and looked at the three of us — a disgraced door-maker, a debt-collector on a crutch, an apprentice who talks to locks — and then at the empty hall, and then, briefly, at the lintel where a crow wasn't sitting.

"So," she said. "Fetch a broom. All of you. And then fetch a table. We start tonight."

"With what ledger?" Odo asked. "They took the ledgers."

Rosalind smiled. It was the same expression she'd worn pressing her copied map into her coat on the night the elders ordered it burned, and I understood, finally, that I had been following that smile across two worlds without once asking where it came from.

"They missed one," she said. "They always miss one. It's in my coat. It's been in my coat since the night a stranger died on our doorstep." She reached inside. "I've been waiting to find out what we were before I wrote the first line."

She set a small, battered, half-empty ledger on the table, in the dust, in the light coming honestly through the open doors.

"Now," she said. "Someone hand me a pen."
