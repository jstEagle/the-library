## 22. The New Ledger

The new guildhall has doors.

I want to be clear about that, because it took eleven months of argument, three collapsed walls, one visitation from a glassmaker who insisted on inspecting our mortar, and a formal apology read aloud by the entire surviving eldership before anyone agreed that the doors would be not merely present but *open* — propped wide, day and night, with an actual bell above them that anyone might ring.

The old guild's founding principle had been secrecy. *The world must never know we exist,* the first ledger said, in ink gone brown as old blood, *for what the world knows, the world wants, and what the world wants, it devours.* It was good advice for a very long time. It was also, I now believe, the exact shape of mistake that lets a magistrate steal a crown while everyone congratulates themselves on their invisibility.

The new principle is painted over the lintel in letters Hesper herself carved, because she said if her doors were going to stand open they were at least going to stand open beautifully:

**THE GUILD OF IMPOSSIBLE THINGS — BOTH REALMS WELCOME — PLEASE WIPE YOUR FEET.**

Wren added the last part. Nobody could prove it. Everybody knew.

* * *

I came down before dawn on the first morning of Thaw-month, because some things you want to do alone, or at least you think you do until you find Odo already sitting at the great table with two cups of tea poured, which is his way of being alone near people, which is the only kind of alone he trusts anymore.

"You're up early," he said.

"You're up earlier."

"I never went to bed." He pushed a cup toward me. "Couldn't stop reading the old ledgers. Did you know we once held in inventory a debt owed by the moon? Interest accrued for two hundred years. Nobody ever collected."

"What happened to it?"

"Rolled over." He shrugged. "Some debts don't want to be paid. They just want to be remembered."

Odo talks like that now. He came back from the frozen meridian with a limp and a scar across his ribs where Vane's grey knights cut him, and something else besides: a habit of saying true things without charging for them. The inn at the end of trade marked him, the way a forge marks iron. He still collects favors — he says you can take the debt-collector out of the ledger but not the ledger out of the debt-collector — but he collects them gently now, and forgives more than he records, and once a month he walks down to the river with his book of favors and burns one page, free, for whoever needs it most.

We drank our tea in the half-dark and listened to the hall wake up around us: Hesper hammering already somewhere in the east wing, because Hesper sleeps four hours and works fourteen and has decided, in her own words, to spend her disgrace making doors until every door in both realms is honest; and Wren's footsteps overhead, and the small metallic gossip of locks testing themselves, because Wren talks to locks and the locks, apparently, talk back, and the whole building greets her like a favorite aunt.

That was another thing the old guild got wrong. We had apprentices who learned doors and keepers who learned ledgers and nobody, in four hundred years, thought to ask whether a girl who spoke to locks might be not a curiosity but a *craft*. The new guild has no divisions. We tried to invent some, for tradition's sake, and failed, and I consider this our finest institutional achievement.

* * *

The old ledger sat on its lectern where it had sat for four centuries, and beside it, on a cushion of blue velvet that Hesper swore was ceremonial and Wren suspected was stolen, lay the Crown of Covenants.

It doesn't look like much. I have told this to ambassadors from the glass cities and to the assembly delegates who come through the under-door twice a season, and none of them believe me, so let me say it here where no one can argue: it looks like a circlet of dark iron with five seams running through it, faintly luminous, like cracks in ice that have decided to become rivers instead. It does not gleam. It hums — barely, only when more than three people are swearing something honest nearby. It was reforged in fire beneath the ice by many hands and sworn to by hundreds of voices, and its power consists entirely and exactly of this: it remembers promises, and it will not let the two worlds forget each other again.

Serath Vane wanted a crown you could *wield.* What he got, in the end, was a covenant you can only *keep.* There is a difference, and the difference is everything, and I watched the understanding of it come into his face at the last moment, in the light of his own burning forging, just before the thaw took his order apart like sugar in warm water.

They never found his body. I think about that more than I'd like. But the assembly keeps watch, and the meridian is green now, and there are worse fates than having to live in a world that refuses to obey you.

* * *

When the light came grey-gold through the open doors — actual weather, actual morning, things the old guild would have called exposures — the others gathered without being asked. That's the thing about a company forged on the road: you stop needing summons.

Hesper arrived wiping soot from her hands, followed by two of her new apprentices, door-made children from the lower city whom nobody else would teach. Odo brought his tea. Wren brought a lock — an old one, brass, from the first door she ever opened — cradled in both hands like a sleeping bird. And behind them, filling the doorway because doorways are her natural habitat, came Marisol of the Glass City, holding the shard-shaped lantern she'd promised to bring "when your guild stopped being a rumor," and behind *her*, spilling down the steps, a crowd of the curious, the hopeful, the impossible: glass-folk blinking in real sunlight for the first time in a generation, city children with snow still on their boots, an elder or two hovering at the edge pretending they weren't proud.

Bram's crow was on the lectern already.

I don't know how else to say it. The crow — Bram, or what was left of Bram, or whatever Bram became when he gave his last human fragment to shatter a tyrant's forging — had been coming and going all winter. He speaks in stolen phrases still, sentences assembled from other people's conversations like a magpie's nest of words. Sometimes he says *"mind the third step"* in my mother's voice, though my mother has been dead twelve years. Sometimes he says *"the guild thanks you"* in the old First Elder's dry tones, and then, immediately after, in a voice none of us recognize and all of us know: *"Well done, ledger-keeper."*

He did not attend the burial. Crows don't, I think, when it's their own grave. We buried the map instead, the stranger's map, the bloodstained original that started everything, folded around a lock of Wren's hair and a pressed sprig of the first thaw-grass from the meridian, in the ground beside the forge beneath the ice. Hesper made the marker. Of course she did. It's a door — a small stone door, closed, carved with a single word in both realms' scripts: *HERE.*

* * *

I opened the new ledger at the first blank page.

It was bound for me by a bookbinder from the glass quarter who charged me nothing and asked, as payment, only that the first entry be spoken aloud, "so the binding drinks it in." Old craft. Good craft. The cover is glass set in leather, because Marisol insisted the guild's memory should be transparent, and because she is the sort of person who makes metaphors physical and then dares you to object.

The hall went quiet. Even the locks. Even the crow.

I dipped the pen. And I found — I confess it here, in the record itself, since this ledger is pledged to truth — that my hand was shaking.

Four hundred years of keepers had written in the old books entries no one would ever read: trades of bottled echoes, inventories of moon-debts, the careful erasure of a dead man at the threshold. I grew up believing my name would never appear in those pages except as handwriting. Keepers don't get entries. Keepers *are* entries — a line of ink in service of a guild that will forget them by spring.

I looked up. Hesper nodded once, door-maker's shorthand for *the way is open.*

And I wrote.

---

*Entry the First, in the Year of the Thaw:*

*Be it known to both realms, and to whichever comes after: the Guild of Impossible Things is refounded this day, its charter amended, its purpose restored. The old guild kept impossibilities hidden. This guild shall keep them possible.*

*Its doors shall stand open. Its ledgers shall stand open. Its membership shall be drawn from any realm, any trade, any wanderer who has ever been told they do not belong — for it is the firm finding of this company, tested across winter and written in the loss of friends, that the impossible is not accomplished by the powerful acting alone, but by ordinary voices choosing one another, and keeping the choice.*

*The first members of the refounded Guild are these:*

*Hesper Vale, door-maker, who opened the stair that wasn't there, and lost her memories to the cold, and chose new ones, and chose us.*

*Odo Ferrenz, collector of favors, who paid truth like coin when coin was worthless, and taught this guild that vulnerability is not payment's opposite but its purest form.*

*Wren Ashlaw, speaker-to-locks, first apprentice of the new register, who cracked a shard against her own palm to save a company, and who heard the locks of Vane's stronghold say, in her words, "please."*

*Marisol Veyle, glassmaker, creditor of the guild's oldest and gravest debt, whose realm we failed and who forgave us into better than we were.*

*Bram, last guardian of the Crown of Covenants, who scattered five shards against a tyrant and paid his human shape for it, and who died at our threshold as a stranger and lived long enough, as a crow, to see the winter break. His name stands first among the honored dead of this ledger, and last in this entry, as he asked, "so I'm always the newest member."*

*Sworn by Rosalind Almeida, keeper, formerly youngest, now founding: who spent her life invisible, and gives that invisibility back gladly, and signs her own name at last.*

---

I signed it. Out loud, as the binder required, and my voice cracked on the last syllable, and nobody pretended not to notice, which is the other thing the new guild gets right.

The crow hopped onto the page before the ink dried — I yelped; the lock Wren was holding rattled indignantly — and studied the entry with one bright eye. Then he said, in a voice like frost forming on a window, slow and deliberate and entirely his own:

*"Keeper."*

Just that. One word. Not stolen from anyone. The first word I ever heard him make that wasn't borrowed.

Then he flew up through the open doors into the Thaw-month morning, and I did not cry, and if you consult the margin of the first page you will find a small inked footprint and next to it, in Wren's handwriting, the annotation *"evidence,"* and that is the official record and I am sticking to it.

* * *

By noon the bell above the door had rung forty times. A fishwife came in to ask whether we could settle a boundary dispute with her neighbor's chickens — Odo took the case. Two glass-children came to enroll, and Wren, aged nineteen and suddenly senior, interviewed them with terrifying gravity. An elder of the old guild arrived, hesitated on the threshold as though the openness itself were a high wind, and then stepped through and asked, quietly, whether there was still work for someone who knew the old cataloging systems.

"There's always work," I told him. "But you'll write your own entry, and it goes in the open book, and anyone may read it."

"That," he said, looking around at the light, the noise, the impossible crowd of us, "is the most impossible thing this guild has ever kept."

I gave him a pen.

At dusk I stood at the door myself and looked out at the street — the ordinary street, the mortal city, snowmelt running in the gutters toward a river that was singing again, if you knew how to listen — and above the rooftops, very high, very far, a crow turned once in the gold light and flew toward the place where, if you know the way, the world opens downward into spring.

The bell rang behind me. Someone needed a door.

I went to answer it.
