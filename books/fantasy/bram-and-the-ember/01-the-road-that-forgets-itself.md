## 1. The Road That Forgets Itself

The pass did not exist. Emrys Galloway had made certain of that eleven years ago, when they had walked its length with a fistful of mountain ash and a stolen name-sigil, unweaving it from every map, every ledger, every drover's song that mentioned the high road between Cinderhollow and the salt flats. It had taken a full summer of careful work, kneeling in ditches to scratch out charcoal lines, sitting by fires in wayside inns to murmur corrections into the ears of men who thought they were merely changing their minds. By autumn, no document in Thornfell admitted the pass was there. By winter, even the crows had stopped flying over it.

And yet here it was under their boots, narrow and grey and real as a scar, threading up through the shoulders of the Thornfell mountains like a vein of silver in dark stone.

Emrys preferred it that way: a road only they could find, because they were the only person alive who remembered how to look for it. There was a symmetry to that which they found almost comfortable, the way a prisoner finds the shape of a cell comfortable after enough years. The road forgot itself. Emrys had helped it. In return, it kept their secrets, or at least kept them company.

Behind them, the salt caravan came on in its slow, jangling order — fourteen mules, nine drivers, two wagons with wheels that complained like old men, and four dogs who had been uneasy since dawn without being able to say why. Emrys could hear everything: the crunch of hooves on frost-brittle scree, the creak of harness, Doss Halloway at the rear swearing softly at his mule in the resigned tone of a man who had been swearing at it for twenty years and expected to swear at it for twenty more.

They paused where the path bent around a spine of black rock and waited for the caravan to catch up, hood back, hands loose. This was the other half of the craft, the half people never understood. Being forgotten was not the same as being invisible. Anyone could wrap themselves in shadow; Emrys had met hedge-witches who could do that much. What Emrys did was quieter and far more tedious: they arranged themselves so that eyes slid off. They stood at the exact angle where a guard's gaze would naturally skip to the next figure in line. They spoke in a voice pitched to be half-heard and entirely dismissed. They dressed like a person you'd already met and hadn't liked, so you wouldn't want to look again.

The caravan drivers knew them as *Grey*, a guide who cost too much and was worth it, and even that was more attention than Emrys liked. But salt had to come over the mountains, and Cinderhollow's miners would riot without it, and the low roads had grown unsafe in ways nobody could articulate — carts arriving with one mule missing, drivers swearing they'd counted thirteen at the last ford and fifteen at this one, as if the mountains had started miscounting. So the salt factors paid, and Emrys took the caravans up roads that didn't exist, through passes that had been unwritten, and delivered them whole.

It was honest work, in the way that work done by someone who doesn't exist can be honest.

* * *

By midmorning they had climbed above the tree line, into country where the wind combed everything flat and the light came down hard and white off the snowfields. Emrys walked point as always, reading the mountain the way a clerk reads a ledger — noting where the frost had heaved a slab out of true, where the meltwater had undercut the path, where a fall of scree three days old said the slope above was restless. The mountain spoke constantly if you listened. Most people heard only wind.

Doss Halloway came up beside them at the next rest stop, working a strip of dried mutton between his teeth.

"Grey." He squinted eastward, where the peaks stacked themselves against the sky like pages of a closed book. "That notch there. The one shaped like a broken tooth. Where's it go?"

"Nowhere," Emrys said.

"Looks like it goes somewhere."

"It goes somewhere you don't want to be."

Doss chewed on that along with the mutton. He was a decent man, Doss, decent in the specific way of men who drove animals for a living — patient, unsentimental, attentive to suffering only when it threatened the schedule. He'd hired Grey four seasons running now and had never once asked where Grey went when the caravans disbanded. That was the arrangement working as intended. Emrys had built the arrangement out of exactly such absences: no questions asked, no curiosity kindled, nothing remembered past the settling of accounts.

"Strange year," Doss offered at last. "My sister's girl down in Ashford says the wells have gone talkative."

"Talkative."

"Bubbling. Singing, she said. Of a night." He laughed, but thinly, the way men laugh at things that aren't funny. "Plague winter did things to people's heads, I expect."

"Plague winters do," Emrys agreed, and turned away before their face could say anything else.

Because that was not nothing. Wells going talkative. Emrys had spent eleven years learning what the mountain sounded like when it was only stone and weather, and lately — a season, perhaps two — there had been an undertone beneath the wind that was not wind, a hum below hearing that made the dogs whine and the mules stamp and left a taste at the back of the throat like a coin held too long in the mouth. Old-timers called such years *listening years*. Emrys called them nothing, because naming them would mean admitting what they feared, and fear named is fear invited.

They gave the caravan water, checked the mules' feet, and moved on.

* * *

The shrine cave announced itself an hour before they reached it, though none of the drivers knew that. Emrys felt it first as a change in the quality of the silence — a thickening, the way air thickens before a storm — and then in the behavior of the light. The sun stood behind a rack of cloud, yet a faint warmth lay across the path ahead, sourceless, like the glow of a banked fire. Emrys slowed without deciding to slow. Their body knew this stretch of road. Their body had known it before they did, which was a sentence that made no sense and which they did not examine.

There should have been nothing here. Eleven years ago there had been nothing here — a shallow overhang where shepherds sheltered, nothing more. Emrys had seen to it that the shepherd shrine that once stood within was forgotten down to its last candle-stub.

But the mountain had other ideas now.

The cave mouth opened in the rock face like a held breath: tall, narrow, hung with icicles that caught the sourceless warmth and wept despite the cold. Inside, beyond the reach of daylight, something glimmered. Not firelight. Ember-light — a low red pulse, steady as a sleeping heart.

The dogs stopped first. All four of them, at once, as if a hand had been laid on their backs. Then they began to refuse — not barking, not bolting, just planting themselves, bellies low, hackles up, and looking at Emrys with the stricken, apologetic eyes of animals begging their people to be sensible. The lead dog, a grizzled bitch named Sorrel who had crossed these mountains thirty times and feared nothing that walked or crawled, pressed herself flat against the frozen ground and *shook*.

"What's got into them?" Doss called from behind.

Emrys didn't answer immediately. They were looking into the cave, at the ember-glow, and the glow was — they could not put it any other way — *looking back*. Not seeing. Attending. The difference mattered enormously. A trap sees you and springs. This was older than traps. This was the sensation of walking past a shut door and knowing, with animal certainty, that someone behind it has lifted their head.

"Grey?"

"They won't go in," Emrys said. "We'll rest here and lead the dogs through on short tethers. Keep your voice down past that rock."

"Why? There's nobody—"

"*Keep your voice down.*"

Doss looked at them properly then — the first time in four seasons anyone had looked at Grey properly — and Emrys felt the old alarm, cold and precise: *too visible, pull it back*. They softened their stance, dropped their chin, let their voice go flat and forgettable. Doss blinked and the moment passed over him like weather. He went back to the dogs, muttering.

Emrys approached the cave mouth alone.

Up close, the glow resolved into a niche cut deep in the back wall, and in the niche, a shape. Stone, very old, worn featureless by time and by hands — thousands upon thousands of hands, polished smooth by touching. Offerings lay at its base, fresh ones: a cup of milk, skin wrinkled with cold; a knot of black bread; three copper coins, green with age, arranged in a line. And on the wall beside it, scratched into living rock by fingers or a knife, words that Emrys read without wanting to:

*SHE ANSWERED.*

Below that, in a different hand: *IT SAVED OUR MARA.*

Below that, in a child's crude letters: *THANK YOU THANK YOU THANK YOU*

Eleven years of erasure, undone in a single winter. Someone had prayed at a dead shrine, and something dead had answered, and the news had spread the way news spreads — not through documents, which Emrys could unwrite, but through the one medium they had never managed to erase: gratitude. You could burn a name from every ledger in Thornfell. You could not stop a mother from saying *thank you* to the dark.

Emrys stood very still. The ember-pulse in the niche brightened fractionally — or seemed to; they refused to trust their eyes — and along the walls of the cave, in the oldest layer of soot, shadows shifted in a way that had nothing to do with any light source Emrys could identify.

And then it happened. Small. Terrible. Like the first hairline crack in a dam.

Something turned toward them. Not the shrine-shape, not the glow — something vast and slow and buried very deep, the way a whale is buried in the ocean, and it *noticed* the place where Emrys stood. Their erasure, so carefully maintained, so expensively purchased, rippled. For one heartbeat Emrys felt it the way a man feels a hand pass over his face in the dark: the sensation of being almost recognized. Almost. A great attention moved through the mountain like a swell moving through deep water, touched the hole in the world shaped exactly like Emrys Galloway, and paused there, puzzled, tasting the absence.

*That one,* the pause said. *We know that nothing. We loved that nothing once.*

Then it moved on, sinking back toward whatever furnace it slept above, and the cave was only a cave again, and the glow only mineral light, or nearly.

Emrys discovered they were gripping the doorstone hard enough to hurt their hand. They made themselves let go, finger by finger.

*No,* they told the silence, and the telling had no force at all, and they knew it. *I am no one. I spent myself becoming no one. Whatever you remember, it isn't me anymore.*

The silence received this the way mountains receive all prayers: without visible interest.

They went back to the caravan. The dogs came through the defile at last, hauled trembling on their leads, Sorrel whining continuously in a thin thread of sound like a kettle about to sing. The drivers muttered about wolves, about spirits, about the price of salt not being worth this. Emrys said little. Their hands were steady on the leads. Their hands were always steady; it was one of the few things about themselves they had never needed to forge.

But that night, camped on a shelf of stone above the cloud line, while the drivers slept and the salt glittered faintly in its sacks like a field of stars brought down and bagged, Emrys sat apart with their back against a boulder and did the thing they did perhaps once a year: they tried to remember their own name.

Not *Grey*. Grey was a coat you wore until it wore out. The name underneath — the one burned out of records, murmured out of memories, paid away and buried and denied — Emrys reached for it the way you probe the socket of a pulled tooth. And found, as always, nothing. Smooth absence. A room swept clean.

Except tonight, in the sweeping, one grain remained.

It arrived as a fragment of sensation rather than sense: the smell of rain on hot stone. A woman's voice, very tired, saying a word of two syllables that dissolved before it landed. And beneath both, faint as the ember-glow in that impossible cave, a feeling so enormous and simple that Emrys sat bolt upright against the boulder, heart slamming:

*grief*. Someone, somewhere, grieved them. Loved them and lost them and grieved —

They killed the thought. They were good at killing thoughts; they'd had practice. But it left a residue, the way crushed weeds leave green on the hands, and long after midnight Emrys still sat awake, watching the east, where — and they told themselves it was only dawn coming early, only weather, only nothing — the sky above the deepest of the Thornfell peaks had begun, very faintly, to glow.

Not sunrise. Sunrise came from the wrong direction.

This light rose from under the mountains, ember-red, pulsing slow, keeping time — Emrys realized with a cold that had nothing to do with altitude — with the beating of their own heart.

Far below, in the sleeping camp, Sorrel the dog lifted her head, looked at Emrys sitting alone against the stars, and began, very softly, to howl.
