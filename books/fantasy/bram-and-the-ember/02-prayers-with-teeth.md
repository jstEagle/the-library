## 2. Prayers With Teeth

The trouble with Cinderhollow was that it remembered things.

Not the people—the people were like any mining folk, quick to forget a face and quicker to forget a debt—but the town itself. The slate roofs held the shapes of every hailstorm they'd weathered. The mine-tailings kept the footprints of boys dead thirty years. And the shrine above the river, squat and moss-eaten at the bend where the water went loud over black stones, had been keeping something for a long time. Emrys had felt it the moment they crested the ridge with the caravan's last mule and saw the town smoking in its valley: an old attention, banked like a coal under ash, beginning—very faintly—to glow.

They had spent ten years learning to be a rumor. It was not a talent so much as a discipline, the way fasting is a discipline, or drowning. Emrys Galloway did not exist in ledgers. Did not exist in the memories of innkeepers who'd poured them wine the night before. The salt merchants they'd guided through the pass this season would wake in Lowmark tomorrow remembering only that their dogs had been restless and their route had been lucky. This was how Emrys preferred it. A name was a handle, and a handle was something a god could take hold of.

The caravan master, a barrel-chested woman named Odile with salt crusted white in the seams of her knuckles, paid them in coin pressed into a glove rather than skin to skin. She didn't know why she did that. She thought it was her own superstition. Emrys had suggested it three winters ago, gently, the way you plant a seed in someone's dream, and now it was simply *the way things were done*, and Emrys passed through the world one more degree unremarked.

"You'll winter in Cinderhollow?" Odile asked, squinting up at them against the low sun. Her eyes slid off Emrys's face the way water slides off oiled leather.

"Passing through."

"Strange season for passing through." She spat. "River's been wrong all week."

Emrys had noticed. Rivers ran wrong all the time in Thornfell—silt, landslides, the mountain's endless slow rearranging of itself—but this was a different kind of wrong. The Greywater had gone still at its bends. Not frozen; the air wasn't cold enough for that. Still the way a dog goes still when it hears something under the floorboards.

"I'll be gone before it matters," Emrys said, which was both a farewell and, as it turned out, a lie.

* * *

They took a room at the Waystone Inn because even ghosts need sleep, and because inns were where a town told on itself. Emrys sat in the corner with their back to stone—a habit, not a comfort—and listened.

Cinderhollow talked about tin prices and a widow's second marriage and whether the priest from Lowmark would bother coming up before the solstice. Ordinary talk, worn smooth as river pebbles. But underneath it ran a thinner thread, the way ore runs under rock, and Emrys followed it down through an hour of noise until they had the shape of it:

Three days ago, someone had left offerings at the old shrine by the river. The drowned shrine. The one dedicated to a god whose name nobody said anymore because nobody could remember it—there was a hollow in every prayer where the name used to go, like the gap of a pulled tooth the tongue keeps worrying. Fish heads and river-glass and a cup of small beer, the kind of offering children make when they're playing at religion, or grieving mothers make when there's nothing else left to give.

And this morning, the offerings were gone.

Gone, and in their place, the river had given something back.

Emrys bought a bowl of barley soup they didn't eat and turned the shape of it over in their mind. Offerings answered at a dead shrine. In Thornfell. After ten years of silence so complete that even Emrys, who listened for such things the way other men listen for wolves, had begun to believe it would hold.

It was possible—barely, dangerously possible—that it meant nothing. That a child had taken the fish heads as a joke. That the river's stillness was silt. Emrys had built a life out of explaining away omens, and they were very good at it, good enough that most nights they could almost believe their own explanations.

But the back of their neck had been prickling since the ridge, that old animal certainty of being watched, and no amount of professional skepticism could scrub it away.

* * *

In the morning, the whole town walked down to the river, and Emrys walked with them, hooded, half a step behind, one more gray body in the gray crowd.

The child stood at the shrine.

She was maybe seven years old, dripping though the morning was dry, her dress heavy with water that fell off her in beads and did not soak into the ground. Her skin had the blue-white cast of a body pulled late from cold water. Her lips were the color of the slate. And she was smiling—not the fixed grin of a corpse, but a living, wondering smile, the smile of a child seeing the world for the first time, which in every sense that mattered, she was.

"That's Maren Cooper," whispered the woman beside Emrys, a laundress with wet-reddened hands wrung together at her breast. "That's little Maren. She went in the flood two springs past. I helped carry her—" The woman's voice broke and reformed around something worse than grief. "I helped bury her."

The child turned her head. The motion was smooth, too smooth, like something underwater turning to look at a light. Her eyes were clear brown and utterly empty, and when she smiled wider, Emrys saw that behind the teeth, far back in the dark of her throat, there was a faint orange light. Not reflection. Emanation. A coal burning somewhere down inside her, warm and patient and *hungry*.

"Mama says thank you," the child said. Her voice came out layered, a girl's voice with something vast speaking underneath it, the way a bell's note has the strike hidden inside the ring. "Mama says the gifts were lovely. Mama says come again."

The crowd made a sound Emrys had heard only once before, long ago, in another life at the edge of another disaster: the collective intake of breath that is half awe and half the beginning of a scream. Someone dropped to their knees. Someone else grabbed their children and ran. The priest from Lowmark, who had arrived the evening before after all, stood frozen with his holy book open in his hands like a man holding a shield he'd forgotten how to use.

Emrys did not kneel. Emrys did not run. Emrys looked at the light in the child's throat and read it the way a smith reads a flame—by color, by character—and what they read turned their stomach to cold lead.

Fire that lives underwater. Warmth in a drowned thing. Gold light in a place of river-dark and stone. There was only one power in all the old pantheon of Thornfell that burned beneath the earth and reached up through water, one court whose signature was heat without light and gift without mercy.

The Ember Court.

The gods below. The quiet fire. They who had once taken worship in the deep places and paid for it in miracles that always cost more than they seemed. Emrys had hoped—had *arranged*, in years of careful, patient work—to never think that name again, and here it was, written in a dead child's resurrected flesh, glowing orange behind her teeth.

*Maren Cooper* blinked slowly, like a cat, and her gaze drifted across the crowd and snagged—for one terrible instant—on Emrys.

It should not have. Emrys stood wrapped in a decade of erasure; crows forgot them mid-flight, debts lost their page numbers when they passed. Nothing that hunted by name or memory should have found anything to look at.

But the thing wearing Maren Cooper looked at them anyway, and the smile widened, and the voice that came out was suddenly just a little girl's voice, sweet and puzzled, and it said:

"You smell like storms."

Then it blinked, and the vastness underneath receded, and the child swayed, and her mother was screaming her name and catching her as she fell, and the crowd surged forward into a chaos of weeping and prayer, and whatever thread had connected Emrys to the thing in the river was gone as if it had never been.

Except that it had been. Except that *you smell like storms* rang in Emrys's skull like a struck bell, and beneath the ringing was a sound they hadn't heard in ten years: the faint, far-off grinding of something enormous turning over in its sleep. Attention. Divine attention, moving through the mountain's roots like a lamp moving through a cellar, checking its stores.

Coming awake.

* * *

Emrys walked the riverbank that afternoon while the town celebrated, because celebration was exactly what worried them. They watched from the alder shadows as Maren Cooper—alive, warm, laughing, her mother refusing to let go of her hand—was carried up the hill on a chair like a bride. They watched the townsfolk begin, shyly at first, then eagerly, to gather offerings again. Bread at the drowned shrine. A ribbon. A copper coin, then a silver one. By dusk there was a small mound of gifts on the mossy altar, and the river ran past it, still and glassy, holding each offering's reflection a beat too long before letting it go.

Professional curiosity, Emries told themselves. That was all this was. A storm-caller's habit of assessing weather: pressure falling, wind shifting, something coming. You didn't have to *care* about a village to read its sky.

But their hands shook as they crouched at the water's edge, and they knew it wasn't fear for Cinderhollow. It was fear of what this meant for the life they had built, the beautiful invisible nothing they had spent ten years constructing stone by buried stone. If the old gods were waking—if the Ember Court was answering prayers again—then the whole balance of the mountains was changing, and things that slept through erasure might wake to it. Things that had let Emrys go might decide to look again.

There was a way to check. There was always a way to check, if you were willing to pay.

Emrys pulled off their left glove. Beneath it, inked into the skin of the palm in a script older than the kingdom, was a sigil they had cut into their own flesh a decade ago and never once used since: a listening-mark, tuned to the frequency of divine attention. It worked by sympathy—you opened your own name to the listening, offered it up like bait on a hook, and anything out there that remembered you would pull the line. Every time Emrys had considered using it, they had imagined the line going slack forever, the silence confirming the erasure complete, and the confirmation had been worth more than the risk of using it.

Tonight, they needed to know.

They pressed the inked palm flat against the river-stone and spoke the trigger-word in a language that predated Cinderhollow, predated the mine, predateed perhaps the mountain itself. The sigil warmed. The mark drank the warmth. And Emrys closed their eyes and waited in the darkness behind their eyelids for either silence or—

*Pull.*

The line snapped taut so fast it was nearly pain. Something on the other end had seized the bait instantly, greedily, the way a starving thing seizes food, and Emrys felt themselves *known*—felt attention pour along the connection like molten metal into a mold, filling every contour of them, weighing them, tasting them. Not recognizing. Recognizing takes a memory of what came before. This was something else. This was a hunger so old and so vast that it recognized the *shape* of what it wanted, the way a mouth recognizes food it hasn't tasted in a century.

A voice moved through the mark, through the bones of Emrys's hand, through the river-stone and up into the soles of their feet. It did not use words. It used *certainties*, whole meanings arriving fully formed, and the meaning was simple:

*You are owed. You are remembered. Come home.*

Emrys tore their hand from the stone. The sigil went dark. The river lapped on, innocent, and up the hill the shrine-mound glittered with bread and silver, and somewhere beneath all that stone and all that water something settled back with the satisfaction of a merchant who has confirmed his customer still lives.

Emrys sat back on their heels in the alder shadow and breathed, and found their breath wouldn't steady.

Come home. As if there were a home. As if Emrys had not burned every bridge, buried every record, salted every field of their own history. The court below was hungry and half-waking and it had smelled them the way the drowned child had—by storm-signature, by some essential flavor of the soul that no amount of forgetting could disguise—and it considered them *owed*.

Owed what? Owed by whom? To whom?

Ten years of questions Emrys had carefully never asked were now standing in a row outside the door, demanding entry.

They got up. They walked back toward the inn through streets full of candlelight and relieved laughter, past windows where families prayed aloud to a god whose name they couldn't say, and the prayers hung in the cold air like smoke from a fire just lit, rising, drifting, finding the cracks in the world and pouring down through them. Feeding something. Every grateful word a coin in a collection plate. Cinderhollow was going to love its new miracle to death, and the thing below was going to let it, and grow fat, and want more.

At the well in the market square, Emrys stopped. They didn't know why at first. Then they heard it: a thin sound, half-swallowed, coming from the direction of the well—a girl's voice, fourteen or so, talking low and fierce to the darkness between the well-stones.

"—and you keep your hands off him, you hear me? He's paid. We're square. You leave Tam alone."

The voice broke on the name. Emrys stood in the shadow of the baker's awning and watched a slight figure hunched at the well's rim, knuckles white on the stones, a fresh scar or mark visible even at this distance on her open palm—and around the mark, faint as breath on a window, an orange glow.

The same light. The same signature. The Ember Court's fingerprint, pressed into living skin.

Emrys had come to Cinderhollow to pass through. They had a room paid for one night, a route planned south, a decade of invisibility to protect. Every instinct honed over ten careful years said walk away, walk south, walk anywhere, let the girl and her brother and her glowing palm belong to the mountain that marked her.

But the mark glowed, and the girl bargained with the dark like someone who had already lost everything and didn't know how much worse losing could get, and Emrys—who knew better than anyone alive what it cost to owe the things below—heard themselves ask the dark in return:

"What did you promise it?"

The girl spun, wild-eyed, hand fisted shut around the burning mark. "Who's asking?"

"No one," Emrys said quietly, which was true, and also the whole problem.
