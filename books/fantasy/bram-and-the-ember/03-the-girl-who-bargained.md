## 3. The Girl Who Bargained

Bram knew the well was lying before she reached it, because the well had been dry since the winter the fever came, and yet that morning she'd heard water.

She heard it now as she crested the last rise above Cinderhollow—a soft, patient lapping, like a tongue against stone. The sound carried wrong through the thin mountain air. Water should echo in a shaft. This didn't. It sat in her ears like a hand pressed gently over them, and it made her want to walk faster and also never take another step.

She walked faster.

The village spread below her in its crooked rows of slate-roofed houses, chimneys bleeding thin smoke into the grey morning. From up here Cinderhollow looked almost whole again. That was the trick of distance; it smoothed everything over. Up close there were still the shuttered houses with their white crosses painted on the doors—nine of them, though only seven crosses were needed anymore—and the empty pens where the goats had gone to feed the fever's hunger, and the way people spoke to each other now, carefully, quietly, the way you spoke in a sickroom even after everyone in it was dead or better.

Her family had been spared. All four of them. Mother, Father, Tam, Bram.

*Spared.* She turned the word over the way she did every morning, feeling for the crack in it.

The well stood at the crossroads where the mine road met the church path, ringed by a wall of fitted stones older than the village, older than the church, older probably than the mountain's name. In summer children used to drop pebbles down it and count—one, two, three, *plunk*. Now it was dry as an old bone, and everyone knew it, and nobody drew from it anyway because drawing from it felt, these days, like reaching into a mouth.

The voice had come out of it on the worst night of the plague winter, when the snow was falling so thick the world seemed to be erasing itself, and her mother's breathing had gone shallow and rattling in the next room, and Father had sat by the fire not praying because he'd stopped believing in anything that could be prayed to, and Tam—

Tam had been asleep. Tam slept through everything. He was nine then, all elbows and questions, a boy who talked so much in his waking hours that his dreams must have needed the rest.

And Bram had been fourteen and terrified and standing at the well in the dark with snow in her boots, because old Nan Cottle had told her once—you don't pray to the dead shrines, girl, they're dead—but Bram had been desperate enough to try something else. Something lower. Something *down*.

*I'm sorry,* she'd said to the dark inside the stones. *Please. Not my mother. Please.*

And the dark had said: *What will you give?*

She hadn't understood, then, what kind of question that was. She'd thought it was a merchant's question. She'd thought about the copper candlestick, about her mother's wedding ring, about the good knife with the bone handle. She'd opened her mouth to offer the candlestick, and the dark had laughed—not cruelly, which was somehow worse—and said:

*Not things. Things are nothing here. What do you love most?*

And Bram, fourteen years old and shaking, had thought of Tam asleep in the loft with his feet hanging off the edge of the bed, and she had said, without meaning to say it aloud, without understanding that saying it aloud was the same as signing it:

*Anything but him. Take anything but him.*

The silence that followed had been long enough for her to believe the bargain refused. Long enough for hope to start crawling back up her throat. Then the voice had spoken again, gentle as a hand smoothing hair:

*Done.*

The snow had stopped falling. Just like that, mid-flurry, as if someone had closed a door on winter. And when Bram ran home with her heart hammering, the fever had broken in the night—her mother's first easy breath in three days, her father snoring by the banked fire, and Tam awake in the loft, blinking at her in the lamplight, asking why she was crying.

That was eleven months ago. Everyone else's family had lost someone or nearly lost someone. Hers had lost no one. And in the spring, when the fever came back for the outlying farms, it had passed around Coldbrook Farm the way water passes around a stone.

Spared. All four of them. The crack in the word was this: she hadn't paid for it with anything she could see.

* * *

Tam stopped dreaming in the spring.

He didn't stop sleeping—he slept like the dead, deeper than before, if anything—but he woke in the mornings with nothing behind his eyes. No stories about flying off the mill roof. No monsters under the bed worth describing at breakfast. No dreams at all, he said, when Bram asked, and he said it the way you'd report the weather. Flat. Unbothered. As if a boy could simply lose an entire country he used to visit every night and not miss it.

It wasn't just dreams. Bram noticed the rest slowly, the way you notice a room getting colder one degree at a time. Tam stopped humming. He'd hummed constantly before—the mine songs, the harvest songs, songs he made up himself, tuneless and endless. Now nothing. He stopped crying too, which should have been a mercy but wasn't; a nine-year-old who never cried was a boy with a hole where the crying used to come from. And sometimes, when she watched him across the table, she caught his eyes moving in a way that didn't match whatever his hands were doing, like a man reading a page someone else held up.

Worst of all was the shadow thing. She'd noticed it in high summer, hanging laundry, when the sun came slantwise through the yard. Her own shadow stretched long and sharp across the grass. So did Mother's, bent double over the basket.

Tam stood in full sun, and the ground beneath him stayed pale.

She'd made him stand still while she checked—walked around him twice, made him raise his arms, turn, hold up the bedsheet to prove it wasn't some trick of the light. Tam had cooperated with the patience of a child being tested by a madwoman, and the ground had stayed pale, and finally she'd dropped the sheet and run into the house and been sick behind the woodshed.

She never told anyone. Who would she tell? *My brother has no shadow and no dreams and I know why, I know why, I went to the well.* They'd have called it fever-madness, a girl cracked by the hard winter. Some of them would have called it worse. Old superstitions lived close to the surface in Cinderhollow, and a girl who bargained with the deep places was a girl you kept your children away from.

So she carried it. Guilt worn the way Tam used to wear his one good coat—buttoned tight, sleeves too short, visible everywhere.

* * *

In autumn, the shrines started answering.

Bram heard about the drowned boy the way everyone did, in fragments, each telling worse than the last. A fisherman's son from the river villages upstream, taken by the water back in the fever months. His mother had left offerings at the dead shrine by the ford—bread, milk, a lock of the boy's hair—and three nights later the boy had walked out of the river, dripping and whole and smiling, and climbed into her arms.

Whole. Smiling. Except that he stood in the sun and cast no shadow. Except that he never slept, or rather never seemed to wake, moving through the house at night with his eyes open. Except that when the priest tried to speak a blessing over him, the boy had turned his head very slowly and smiled wider and wider until his mother begged the priest to stop.

They burned the shrine afterward. It wouldn't burn.

Bram listened to all of it with her hands clenched in her lap and her face arranged into the proper village expression—fear, disgust, pious horror—and underneath it, a cold clear knowledge rising like groundwater: *I know what answered. I've spoken to it. It knows my name.*

Because that was the thing she couldn't say, couldn't stop thinking: the offerings weren't new prayers. The shrines were answering debts. Somewhere below the mountain, something had woken hungry, and it was collecting, and Bram Galloway owed it the dearest coin she had.

She went to the well again in late autumn. She hadn't meant to; her feet decided. She stood at the rim in the wind with her knuckles white on the cold stones and whispered down into the dry dark: *I want him back. What do I give?*

The water sound came again—that impossible lapping—and beneath it something that might have been laughter, or might have been the mountain settling. Then, faint and amused and terribly patient:

*Come due time, little borrower. The account is kept. Bring nothing.*

Due time. She asked when. She asked how. The well gave her nothing but the sound of water that wasn't there, and after that she stopped going, because going felt like knocking on a door she couldn't un-knock.

But she counted. Everyone in the village counted, those weeks, though most of them didn't know what they were counting toward. The days shortened. The light came later and left earlier. And Bram had grown up on her grandmother's stories, stories about the Ember Court below Thornfell, about the solstice when the veil between the deep fire and the thin world wore to gauze, about bargains called due *when the year turns darkest.*

Nineteen days. That was what she counted, on the morning the stranger came to Cinderhollow.

* * *

She saw them first at the market square, and she would have looked past them entirely—Cinderhollow got peddlers and tinker-folk all the time—except that nobody looked at the stranger. That was the strange part. A tall figure in a storm-grey traveling cloak moved through the crowd, and eyes slid off them like rain off slate. The baker handed over a loaf and stared through the stranger while doing it. Two miners argued about ore weights directly across the figure's path and neither glanced up.

Bram watched, and her skin prickled, because she recognized the trick. Not the shape of it—she'd never seen its like—but the *family* of it. Things that hid themselves belonged to the same country as things that took shadows. The stranger moved unseen the way the well sounded like water. Both wrong in a way the world pretended not to notice.

The stranger stopped at the corner near the old tithe-barn, and—there was no other way to describe it—*arrived* in her attention all at once, as though a curtain had been pulled aside. Grey cloak, travel-worn. A face hard to hold onto, weathered and ordinary and somehow memorable anyway, the way a landscape is memorable. Eyes the color of sky before hail. They swept the square once, twice, and settled on her with the weight of a hand on her shoulder.

On her. Out of the entire market.

Bram's first instinct was to run. Her second, older and stronger, was stubbornness—the same flinty thing that had carried her to the well in the snow, the same thing that let her eat breakfast across from her shadowless brother every morning without screaming. She lifted her chin.

The stranger crossed the square toward her, and people parted around them without knowing they were doing it.

"You're the Galloway girl," the stranger said. A voice like gravel under rain, genderless in the way of voices worn smooth by disuse. "Bram."

"I don't know you."

"No." Something flickered across the weathered face—not quite a smile. "You don't. Nobody does. That's generally the point." The hail-colored eyes dropped, briefly, to her right hand, where she'd curled her fingers into her palm without deciding to. Where, beneath the wrapped rag she wore for warmth, the mark sat—the thin spiral of ash-grey lines the voice in the well had pressed into her skin that snowy night, which no soap or scrubbing or prayer had ever faded. "May I see your hand?"

"Who are you?" Bram stepped back. "How do you know my name?"

"I know a great many names," the stranger said. "It's my trade, more or less. Names, and what they're attached to, and what they cost." The gaze came back up to hers, and there was something in it she hadn't expected—something tired and old and, absurdly, kind. "I know a voice spoke to you from the ground last winter. I know what you asked for and I know what was taken as payment, and I know, Bram Galloway, that you've spent eleven months pretending you don't know any of those things."

The words hit like a slap. Heat flooded her face, and the flint in her came up swinging. "You don't know anything. You come here with your—your *tricks*, making people look through you, and you think—"

"I think the mark on your palm is turning warm," the stranger said quietly. "I think it's been warmer every day this week. And I think you've noticed, because you've noticed everything, haven't you? The dreams. The humming. The—" the stranger paused, chose the word with care, "—the missing pieces. You've counted the days to solstice the same as I have."

Bram stopped breathing.

The market noise went on around them—the baker calling prices, the cart wheels, somebody's dog—and none of it mattered. Because the stranger was right, all of it, every piece, and no one was supposed to know, and hearing it spoken aloud in daylight, in the open square, made it real in a way eleven months of midnight dread never had.

"He doesn't dream anymore," she said. Her voice came out small and vicious, guilt dressed as anger, the only clothes she had for it. "My brother. He sleeps like a stone and wakes up empty and the sun goes straight through him onto the ground and it's my fault, I know it's my fault, I went to the well, I *asked*—" Her fist tightened over the hidden mark until the ash-lines bit. "I asked them to spare my family and they took him and I didn't understand, I didn't understand that's what *anything but him* meant, I thought—I was fourteen, I thought they wanted the candlestick—"

"I know," the stranger said.

"And the drowned boy, the one who walked out of the river—they're answering now, the old ones are *awake*, and the well told me to bring nothing when the time comes, and I know what 'bring nothing' means, it means they're coming for what they already have, they're coming for *him*, maybe for me, I don't—" She ran out of air. Eleven months of swallowed terror poured out in one breath and left her hollow, standing in the market square of Cinderhollow with tears freezing on her cheeks, glaring up at a person half the village had looked straight through.

The stranger crouched slightly, bringing that weathered face level with hers. Close up, their eyes held something like recognition—not of her, she realized, but of the situation. Of the shape of a debt. As if they'd stood in this exact place before, wearing different skin.

"My name is Emrys," they said. "And I'm going to tell you something no one in this valley will believe, so listen carefully. The court below the mountain keeps accounts, and yours has come due at the turning of the year. But accounts can be contested, debts can be traded, and contracts—" a pause, and the ghost of grim humor touched the grey eyes, "—contracts have loopholes. I've spent ten years learning the law of the deep places. I can get your brother's ember back, Bram. Possibly. At a price I'm still counting."

Bram wiped her face with her sleeve, hard, the way her father did when he refused a cold. "What price?"

"That's the problem," Emrys said. "I don't know yet. Which is why we leave tonight, before the solstice, down the roads that aren't on any map. You and me."

"Down." The word dropped through her like a stone into a well. "Down *where*?"

Emrys Galloway looked past her, then—at the ring of ancient stones around the dry well at the crossroads, at the faint, impossible sound of water rising from it into the winter air, patient as a heartbeat.

"Where the voice lives," they said. "Where your brother's soul sits on a shelf. Down into the Ember Court, girl—and may every god who's forgotten us both forgive what it costs."
