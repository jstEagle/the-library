## 4. A Debt Written in Ash

The girl's palm was worse than Emrys had feared.

They had made her sit by the fire in the back room of the tannery, where Cinderhollow's smell of lime and rotting hide could not quite be washed out of the walls. Bram had come without argument—had come, in fact, with the particular sullen willingness of a child who believes she is about to be scolded and has already decided to survive it. She sat on a stool meant for grown men, boots swinging short of the floor, and held out her hand as if offering it for inspection at market.

"Tam's got one too," she said. "Hers—I mean his—is on the back of the neck. You can't see it unless you push the hair around."

Emrys did not answer. They took her hand in both of theirs and turned it toward the firelight.

The mark occupied the whole of her palm, from the heel of her thumb to the base of her fingers, and it was not ink and it was not scar. It looked, at first glance, like ash rubbed into skin—a grey-black smudge that might have come from gripping a burnt stick. But when Emrys tilted the hand toward the light, the ash moved. Not smeared. *Rearranged.* The way iron filings move when a magnet passes beneath the table: reluctantly, all at once, in obedience to something below.

Lines surfaced in the grey. Fine ones, hair-thin, crossing and recrossing until they formed script in no alphabet Emrys had ever been taught—and they had been taught most of them, once, in another life they had paid handsomely to lose. The letters crawled like insects settling. At the center of the palm, where a coin would rest in a fortune-teller's reading, the lines converged into a single dense knot of darkness, and from that knot rose heat. Emrys could feel it without touching: a warmth like a stone pulled from a hearth hours after the fire died.

"Bram," they said carefully, "when did this start hurting?"

"It doesn't hurt." She said it too fast. "It's warm. It's been warm since—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Since Tam stopped dreaming."

*There it is,* Emrys thought, and hated how unsurprised they were. Ten years of erasing yourself teaches you a certain fluency in dread. You learn to read trouble the way a drover reads weather—not because you want to know what's coming, but because knowing is the only thing that has ever kept your bones intact.

"When did he stop?" Emrys asked. "Dreaming."

"Midwinter." Bram's chin came up, defensive. "That's not so strange. Lots of people sleep bad in plague winter. People die sleeping bad."

"And before midwinter?"

She hesitated. The stubbornness in her face flickered, and beneath it Emrys saw something younger and much more tired—the face of a girl who had been carrying a weight sized for an adult and had never once been allowed to set it down.

"Before midwinter," she said, quieter, "he dreamed all the time. Bad dreams. About the well." Her fingers twitched in Emrys's grip—an involuntary curl, protective, pulling the marked palm toward her body. "He said the water talked to him. He was only six, he makes up stories, we all thought—" Her jaw tightened. "We were starving. That's all you need to know. We were starving and the sickness was in the house and my mother had already buried my grandmother and the baby, and there was nothing left to eat but bark, and I went down to the well because old Marta used to say the deep wells remember the old days, and I asked it—"

She stopped again. This time the stopping looked physical, like hitting a wall at speed.

"What exactly," Emrys said, "did you ask it?"

"I didn't ask it anything." The words came out flat and rehearsed, worn smooth from repetition, the way a lie gets polished by handling. "I just talked. I said, please, please, let us live through the winter, take whatever you want, I don't care what it costs, just let us live. And something answered. It said—it said *done*, and it said my name back to me, and it said *we'll hold it safe until the sun turns.*" Her eyes came up, bright and hard. "That's not selling anything. That's just talking. Talking's free."

"Nothing down there is free," Emrys said. "And nothing down there forgets a promise. Especially one spoken over open water at midnight, in a place where the old shrines used to stand."

Bram yanked her hand back and cradled it against her chest, glaring with the full fury of someone who cannot afford to cry. "Then why'd it answer? If the gods are dead, if the shrines are dead, if everyone says there's nothing under the mountain but rock and dead men's tunnels—why'd something answer *me?*"

And that, Emrys thought, was the question, wasn't it. The drowned child walking out of the river two nights ago with riverweed braided in her hair and her mother's name spoken in a voice like water over stones. The offerings vanishing from the dead shrine by the tannery gate. The dogs refusing the shrine cave in the pass, three weeks ago now, and the tremor Emrys had felt through the soles of their boots—attention, vast and slow, turning over in its sleep like a whale sounding beneath a boat.

The old gods were waking. The Ember Court was answering prayers. And it had answered this one first—or this one loudest—because children in extremis make the cleanest contracts. No lawyers' hedges. No conditions. Just *take whatever you want.*

"You should eat," Emrys said, standing. "And then I'm going to look at your brother."

* * *

Tam Galloway was in the yard behind the tannery house, sitting on an overturned salt barrel in the last of the afternoon light, doing nothing at all. That was the horror of him, Emrys realized within moments of seeing him. Children do not sit still. Children fidget, scheme, poke things with sticks, conduct wars between pebbles. Tam sat with his hands in his lap and watched the light move across the wall with an expression of mild, patient interest, like an old man waiting for a kettle.

His shadow was wrong. Emrys saw it immediately, before they saw anything else. The low sun threw everyone's shadow long across the yard—Bram's mother's, sweeping as she carried washwater; Bram's own, sharp-edged and ordinary. But Tam's shadow lay thin and grey as smoke, barely darker than the packed earth beneath it, and its edges moved when he did not. It rippled gently, continuously, like the reflection of trees in slow water.

"Hello," Tam said, when Emrys approached. His voice was his own for the word, and then, on the next sentence, it wasn't. "You've come about the debt." The voice was deeper, unhurried, layered—two voices speaking in perfect unison, a child's and something vast underneath it, the way a bell's note contains the hum of the metal. "We would have preferred the mother. Softer clay. But the elder sister spoke the words, and words bind to the speaker's blood."

"Stop using him," Emrys said quietly.

"He is comfortable. He sleeps while we speak." The boy smiled, and the smile was a child's smile stretched over an abyss of patience. "Do you know what we are, storm-caller?"

The name landed in Emrys's chest like a dropped stone. They kept their face still. Ten years of practice, keeping faces still. But inside, every alarm they owned went off at once—because they had not been called that aloud in a decade, because they had burned the title out of every record and tongue they could reach, because *no one knew*—

"We remember many names," the thing in the boy said, watching Emrys with eyes that had gone the deep amber of banked coals. "Yours among them. Buried things keep best, down here. Cool. Dark." Tam's head tilted. "You erased yourself very thoroughly. Very thorough. But thorough is not the same as gone. Ash remembers being wood."

Emrys's mouth had gone dry. "I'm not here to talk about me. I'm here about the contract. What are the terms? When the debt comes due—what happens to her?"

"The vessel will open," the voices said together, gentle as a lullaby. "At the solstice, when the door between stands widest, our court will rise into the shape prepared for it. She will carry us up into the world of sun and bread and forgetting. Through her mouth we will speak. Through her hands we will take back what was ours." The boy's amber gaze drifted toward the mountains, northward, toward the mass of Thornfell looming black against the sky. "It has been a long hunger. A hundred years of silence. But they pray again, storm-caller. Did you notice? They kneel at the dead shrines and leave bread and coins and beg. Worship is rising like floodwater. We only need a door."

"And the boy? Tam. Does he get returned?"

The unison paused. For the first time, something almost like discomfort crossed the borrowed face.

"The ember is collateral. It stays in the vault while the vessel serves. A year. A decade. However long the body lasts." A small shrug, terrible in its casualness. "Bodies wear out, holding us. But the ember keeps. He will dream again someday. Perhaps."

Inside Emrys, something cold and old and furious began, very quietly, to wake up. They recognized the feeling with distant surprise. They had thought it burned away with everything else.

"Leave him now," Emrys said. "I want to speak to the sister."

"As you wish." The amber drained from Tam's eyes; the shadow firmed; the boy blinked, shivered, and looked around the yard as though surfacing from deep water. "Was I—" he began, and then saw Emrys's face and stopped, and for a moment he looked six years old and utterly lost. Then he slid off the barrel and walked away toward the wash-line without looking back, moving with the careful gait of a child who has learned not to trust his own feet.

* * *

Emrys found Bram in the kitchen, furiously scrubbing a pot that was already clean. They shut the door, and told her. All of it. The vessel. The solstice. The door between worlds opening through her body, wearing her voice, spending her years. They told it plainly, because plainness was a mercy, and watched her take it the way a seawall takes waves—standing, absorbing, giving nothing back, until the structure of her started visibly to crack.

"No." Scrub. "No, that's not—that's not how bargains work, you don't get to take the *whole person*, I only gave—" Scrub. The pot rang against the basin. "I only gave permission. I didn't give *me*."

"They're gods, Bram. Or near enough. Permission is the only currency they recognize, and you spent it in a single breath. *Take whatever you want.*"

"Well, take it *back!*" She spun, wet-handed, eyes blazing. "Un-say it! You're—you do magic, the dogs wouldn't go near your part of the pass, the caravans pay you gold to walk roads that aren't on maps—you call storms, don't you? Everyone whispers it. Call a storm. Wash it off me. Scrub it off like—like this pot!"

Emrys crossed the room and took her wrist, turned her palm up. In the dim kitchen the mark had spread since morning—another finger-width of ash-grey climbing toward her fingertips, the crawling script denser now, and at the center of the knot, faintly, unmistakably, a pulse of orange light, rhythmic, patient, like a heartbeat behind a door.

"Look at it," Emrys said. "Really look. This isn't written *on* you. It's written *into* you—in your blood, in the shape of you. It's a key cut to fit one lock. Your lock. And a key doesn't stop fitting its lock because you throw it in a river." They met her eyes. "I've seen marks like this before. Long ago. People ran from them—crossed seas, changed their names, had the flesh cut away. The mark always found them. Distance means nothing to it; it isn't traveling toward you, it's already *in* you, growing toward the solstice like a root toward water. Running would only spend the weeks you have left."

"How many weeks?" Bram whispered.

"Solstice is thirty-one days off."

The scrubbing cloth slipped from her other hand into the basin. She stared at her palm, and Emrys watched the defiance drain out of her face and leave behind something raw and nine years old, and hated themselves for putting it there, and hated the thing under the mountain more.

"My mum," Bram said. Her voice had gone very small. "She sets his plate every night. She thinks he's just—he's quiet since the winter, she thinks he's just quiet. If I run, does he—does the ember—"

"If you break the terms by fleeing, the court keeps the collateral and collects the vessel anyway, late and damaged. That's the usual reading of contracts like these." Emrys released her wrist. "Which means running saves nothing and costs your brother whatever's left of him."

Silence. The fire ticked in the stove. Somewhere outside, Tam laughed at something—one bright, ordinary, unguarded note of a six-year-old's laugh—and Bram flinched as if struck.

"So what do I do," she said. "Just stand in the square at solstice and open my mouth and let it climb in?"

"No," Emrys said.

The word came out of them before any plan existed to back it. They stood in the tannery kitchen with lime-smell in the walls and a dying god's heartbeat glowing in a child's palm, and understood, with grim clarity, that they had just done the thing they had spent ten years training themselves never to do. They had stepped into a stranger's story. They had become visible. Somewhere under the mountain, something ancient had called them *storm-caller* and remembered their name, and instead of running north tonight—as every instinct screamed—they had said *no* on a fourteen-year-old's behalf.

Reluctant. Furious about it. Responsible all the same. That was the shape of it, and there was no unshaping it now.

"Contracts have loopholes or they have leverage," Emrys said slowly, working it through aloud, watching the ash-script crawl. "A debt this old, this hungry—it won't have loopholes. Which leaves leverage. Something it wants more than a vessel. Or someone it fears more than it trusts its own law." Their mind turned unwillingly toward the pass, and the shrine cave, and the blind mapmaker who traded in secrets, whose name the caravans only spoke sideways: Vess. "I need to know the way down. The real way. The smugglers' road under Thornfell."

Bram wiped her nose on her sleeve, dragged in a breath, and when she spoke again the stubbornness had reassembled itself around her fear like armor around a wound. "Then I'm coming."

"You're staying."

"She talks to me through him," Bram said flatly. "You heard it. It uses his mouth when it wants to talk. You think I can sit here for thirty-one nights listening to my brother's voice say things with *that* under it, and do nothing?" Her fist closed over the burning palm, knuckles white, as if she could strangle the mark by grip alone. "Whatever's down there took my bargain and twisted it. It's my mess. I'm coming to watch you fix it, and if you won't lead me, I'll follow the smell of smoke myself. I found the well, didn't I? I find things."

Emrys looked at her a long moment—at the ash-marked hand, the cracked seawall, the guilt worn so long it had hardened into something like courage—and felt the last of their ten quiet years close behind them like a door.

"Dawn, then," they said. "Pack for cold. And Bram—" they nodded at her palm, where the orange pulse beat on, patient as a wick, "—from tonight, you sleep with that hand wrapped in wool, and you tell no one, and if your brother starts talking in voices again, you come get me before you answer. Because the next thing that thing will try is bargaining with *you* about the terms. And nothing it offers will be worth what it costs."

Outside, the sun went down behind Thornfell, and the mountain stood black against the last light—vast, silent, and, Emrys could not help feeling, awake.
