## 5. What the Storm Remembers

The mark on Bram's palm had begun to whisper.

Not aloud—Emrys would have preferred that, honestly. A voice could be argued with. A voice could be silenced with a hand over a mouth or a charm stitched into a sleeve. But this was worse: the mark whispered *inward*, into the girl's sleep, and Emrys heard it anyway, bleeding through the thin wall of the loft where Bram lay curled in Cinderhollow's only inn, because Emrys's own talent for silence worked both ways. They had spent ten years making themselves unhearable, and so they heard everything.

*Three weeks,* the mark murmured, in a voice like coals settling in a grate. *Three weeks and one turning of the sun.*

Emrys sat by the window with their back to the shuttered glass and did not sleep. They had not slept properly since the drowned child walked out of the river, and they suspected they would not sleep properly again until either Bram was free or the solstice took her. Those were the only two endings they were willing to consider. Everything else—the vessel-rite, the court wearing a fourteen-year-old girl like a glove—was simply a thing that happened to other people, to careless people, to people who bargained with voices in wells.

Except it wasn't anymore. That was the trouble with responsibility. It arrived without asking, the way weather arrived, and once it settled over you there was nothing to do but dress for it.

Bram came down before dawn, as she always did now, moving carefully past the room where Tam sat at the table his mother kept set for him. Emrys heard her pause at that doorway—hear the small hitch in her breathing, the way she looked at her brother's shadowless silhouette and then made herself look away—and something in Emrys's chest tightened in a way they resented deeply.

"You didn't sleep," she said, finding them by the window. She'd learned to find them in the dark; her eyes had gone sharp from years of plague-winter rationing, when every candle was counted like coin.

"Neither did you."

"I don't dream anymore." She said it flatly, the way she said everything now, as if stubbornness were a coat she wore buttoned to the throat. "Since the well. I figured I'd spent all my dreaming on Tam, so he should have some left." Her jaw worked. "He doesn't even use it."

"Bram." Emrys turned toward her. In the grey pre-light their face was just a shape, which suited them fine. "I'm going to try something today. It may be loud. It may frighten the town. I need you to stay indoors until I come back, no matter what you hear."

"What are you going to do?"

"Scour that mark off you," Emrys said, "with weather."

She stared at them. Even in the dark, Emrys could feel the weight of her stare, the particular gravity of a girl who had learned that adults lied mostly by being vague. "You can do that?"

"No," Emrys admitted. "But I can try. And if the storm refuses—" They stopped. Refused was the wrong word. Nothing refused them; that was the whole architecture of their life. Things simply failed to notice them, and storms most of all. A storm-caller whom storms ignored was a paradox they had built deliberately, stone by stone, over ten long years, and they had never once regretted the masonry.

Until now.

"If the storm refuses," Emrys finished, "then we'll know more than we know now. Knowledge is a kind of progress."

"It doesn't feel like progress," Bram said. "It feels like paying for things twice."

Emrys almost smiled. The girl had a merchant's soul under all that flint. "Go wake your mother. Eat something. And Bram—if anyone asks where I've gone, tell them you don't know. Tell them you've never seen me at all."

"That part's easy," she said. "I've been practicing forgetting you since the day we met."

This time Emrys did smile, briefly, and it felt strange on their face, like a key that no longer fit its lock.

* * *

They climbed above the tree line by midmorning, following a path that did not exist.

That was not poetry. Emrys had made it so, years ago—a shepherd's track unwritten from every map, unnamed in every ledger, its waymarkers pulled up and its existence quietly subtracted from the memories of the few who had ever used it. It wound up through the scree fields behind Cinderhollow to a shelf of bare granite that local superstition called the Old Man's Shoulder, and it was, as far as Emrys knew, the only place within twenty miles where they could work weather without an audience of any kind. No eyes. No ears. No names spoken over what they did.

Perfect conditions, in other words, for the kind of magic that required being forgotten.

The mountain was quiet in the way mountains were always quiet—not silent, but indifferent, which was quieter. Wind moved through the loose stone with a sound like slow applause. Far below, Cinderhollow sat in its bowl of smoke and slag-heaps, small enough to cover with a thumb. Somewhere down there, a boy who cast no shadow was eating breakfast in borrowed voices, and a girl was watching him and hating herself with the disciplined ferocity of someone who intended to keep hating herself until it accomplished something.

Emrys stood on the granite shelf and let out a breath they'd been holding, in one form or another, for a decade.

Then they reached into the sky.

Calling weather was not, despite what ballads claimed, a matter of shouting commands. It was closer to conversation—closer still to seduction. You found the tension already living in the air, the great slow argument between warm earth and cold height, and you leaned on it. You whispered along the pressure gradients. You asked the moisture to remember it had once been sea, and the clouds to remember they had once been young, and if you were very good, and very patient, and very much *not yourself*, the sky obliged you the way a cat obliges a stranger: reluctantly, magnificently, and only because it suits its own purposes.

Emrys had been very good at this once. Then they had spent ten years teaching the sky to look through them, and the sky, obliging creature, had learned the lesson perfectly.

So they began the way they always began: small. A thread of attention spun southward, where heat shimmered over the lowlands. A gentle suggestion to the wind, currently sulking in the western passes. A coaxing of the cold air crouched on the high peaks, inviting it down, down, into the warm valley air, where the two masses would meet and argue and produce, as arguments do, thunder.

For a long moment, nothing. The mountain breathed. The scree ticked in the sun.

Then the wind changed.

It came around out of the west all at once, not sulking but *eager*, and Emrys felt the hair rise on their arms and knew, with the professional unease of a craftsman whose tools have begun acting on their own initiative, that something was wrong. Storms did not arrive eager. Storms arrived the way debts arrived—slowly, inevitably, with plenty of warning. This one arrived like a guest who has been waiting outside the door for years.

Clouds boiled up over the western ridge with unnatural speed, stacking themselves into towers, going green-black at their bases. The temperature dropped so fast that Emrys's breath plumed white in midsummer. Below, faintly, they heard dogs begin to howl—all across Cinderhollow, every dog at once, the same note, the same complaint, as though the whole town's animals had agreed on a single sentence:

*It knows you're here.*

"Nonsense," Emrys said aloud, because saying things aloud made them smaller. "I am nobody. I am a rumor. I am a smudge on a window."

The first lightning strike hit the peak half a mile north, and it did not fork. It fell in a single straight line, vertical, precise, like a nail driven into the mountain—and the thunder that followed it did not roll. It *spoke*. Not words, exactly. Not yet. But rhythm, cadence, the shape of a voice clearing its throat after a very long silence, and Emrys felt the sound go through the granite under their feet and up through the bones of their feet and settle somewhere behind their sternum with a weight like recognition.

"No," Emrys said, and began, with shaking hands, to do the one thing they had sworn never to do again.

They called the storm *properly*.

Not the polite, deniable, half-hearted persuasion of a smuggler guiding weather. The real thing. The old grammar. The deep syntax of sky and stone that they had not spoken in ten years and had promised themselves, on the worst night of their life, never to speak again. Words rose in them unbidden, in a language they had thought burned out of them, syllables that tasted of ozone and iron and something older underneath—something that tasted, horribly, of *home*. Their mouth moved. The mountain listened. And the storm, which should have scattered at a command of that authority, instead gathered itself, drew itself up like a serpent rearing, and turned—

—and bent.

Every bolt, every tendril of charge crawling the cloud-undersides, every stray spark leaping between Emrys's own raised fingers: all of it curved away from the peaks, away from the valley, away from the mark three miles below that it had been summoned to scour, and bent inward, toward the granite shelf, toward the figure standing on it with its arms raised in a language it had sworn to forget.

Toward Emrys.

The next bolt struck close enough to blind them. When their vision cleared, they were on their knees on the fused glass of their own making, and the storm stood over them like a colossus, and the thunder was speaking now in earnest, and it was speaking *their name*.

Not Galloway. Galloway was a coat they had bought secondhand in a border town eleven years ago, a name belonging to a dead salt merchant, worn so long it fit like skin. Not the false names, not the working names, not the seven other identities buried in seven other valleys. Something else. Something the thunder pronounced with terrible familiarity, the way you pronounce a word you have said ten thousand times, a prayer-word, a household word, a word once spoken over a cradle—

"*Aurelian.*"

Emrys did not know the name. That was the horror of it. It passed through them like a blade through water, leaving no wound and no memory, and yet every nerve in their body answered it, the way a dog answers a whistle too high for human ears. Their hands shook. Their eyes stung. Somewhere beneath the terror, beneath even the vertigo, was a feeling they recognized with absolute clarity because they had spent ten years drowning it:

*Relief.*

The storm wanted them. The mountain wanted them. Something vast and patient and buried beneath the Thornfells had heard the old grammar spoken again after ten years of silence, and it had risen up joyfully, like a sleeper hearing a beloved voice, and it had called them by the name they had paid everything to lose.

"Aurelian," the thunder said again, gentler now, almost tender. "*Little storm. Little flame. Come home.*"

"I don't know that name," Emrys whispered, and their voice cracked on the lie, because their body knew it, their bones knew it, their raised hands knew it, still holding the last posture of a calling they had completed before their conscious mind caught up. "I am Emrys Galloway. I am no one. I am—"

The rain came down all at once, warm as blood, and in the rain the voice was everywhere—in the drumming on stone, in the rivers running off the shelf, in the pulse behind their eyes.

*You burned your name to hide it from us,* the storm said, sorrowful now, immense. *But fire does not forget what it burns. We kept it for you. We kept all of it. Every year of you, little flame. Come below, and take yourself back.*

And Emrys—who had built a life on the principle that nothing in this world should ever know their face, their art, their history, their *name*—knelt in the warm rain on a shelf of granite that existed on no map, and understood, with the calm clarity that arrives only at the exact center of catastrophe, three things at once.

First: the storm had not come to scour Bram's mark. The storm had been sent—or had risen, or had waited—to find *them*.

Second: whatever slept beneath the Ember Court had known them before, intimately, lovingly, and had mourned them.

Third: the mark on a fourteen-year-old girl's palm was not merely a debt. It was a *door*, and doors opened from both sides, and the thing on the other side had just used a stolen boy's soul and a desperate girl's guilt to knock.

The clouds broke apart as suddenly as they had gathered, peeling back to reveal a hard blue noon, and the mountain went quiet again, indifferent, as if nothing had happened. But on the granite where Emrys knelt, the fused sand had cooled into a circle of green glass, and in the center of the circle, written in letters of char that no rain would ever wash away, was a single word in the old grammar:

*Aurelian.*

Emrys looked at it for a long time.

Then they got up, wiped their face with a sleeve that smelled of ozone, and started down the nonexistent path toward Cinderhollow, walking quickly, like a person fleeing a house fire, like a person going home.

By the time they reached the town's edge, they had made themselves a promise, and the promise had teeth: *I will not go below. Whatever waits down there, whatever remembers me, I will find another way to save the girl. There is always another way. There has to be.*

Behind them, far above, on a shelf of bare granite, the charred word waited in the sun.

And in the loft of the inn, waking from a dreamless sleep, Bram sat up suddenly with her palm burning, and looked at her hand, and saw that the mark had changed. The coiled lines of the contract had rearranged themselves overnight into something new, something deliberate, something almost like writing.

Almost like a name.

* * *

Vess had told her once, in that dry cartographer's voice, that maps were just stories that had agreed to hold still. Emrys thought about that now, standing in Vess's workshop above the tannery, dripping rainwater onto floorboards that had absorbed worse, while the blind mapmaker's head tilted toward them with the slow precision of a compass needle finding north.

"You're wet," Vess said.

"It rained."

"On the Old Man's Shoulder." Vess smiled, and it was not a kind smile. "Interesting. There's no rain scheduled for the Thornfells this week. I would know. I keep the schedule." The blind woman's fingers found a fresh sheet of vellum and smoothed it flat, though she could not see it, and began, idly, to draw. "Sit down, Emrys Galloway. Or should I say—"

"Don't," Emrys said.

The word came out sharper than intended, and Vess's quill paused mid-stroke.

"Don't," Emrys repeated, softer. "Whatever you heard on the wind today—and I know you heard something, because everyone in this valley hears everything eventually—do not say it. Not that name. Not here. Names have weight, Vess. You of all people should know that. You trade in them."

"I trade in *maps*," Vess said mildly. "Names are just the labels people stick on places they're afraid of." She set down the quill. Her clouded eyes fixed on Emrys with unsettling accuracy. "But you're right that I heard something. Half of Cinderhollow heard it. The dogs have been howling since noon. Old Marta swears the river ran backward for the space of three heartbeats. And you come down off a mountain that isn't on any of my maps, smelling of lightning, asking me—" she lifted the fresh vellum, and read its surface with her fingertips, though she had drawn it blind, "—asking me, apparently, for the deep roads. The smuggler's spine. The way *down*, Emrys. Into the mountain."

Emrys said nothing. The rain on their shoulders was drying, and with each degree of warmth they felt the morning's terror receding and the morning's other feeling—that awful relief—receding with it, thank the absent gods, receding like a tide leaving something exposed on the sand.

Because that was the problem, wasn't it. The tide always went out. And it always left the same thing lying there.

*I will not go below,* they had promised. And then they had walked directly to the one person in Thornfell who knew the way.

"You want to reach the Ember Court," Vess said. It wasn't a question. "You want to go down into the vaults where they keep the souls like heirlooms, and you want to buy a boy's ember back before the solstice. And you want me to draw you a map to get there." She tilted her head. "Here's my difficulty, Emrys Galloway. Maps cost. And the price of this one—"

"I'll pay it," Emrys said. "Whatever it is."

"—is a memory," Vess finished, "and I haven't chosen which one yet."

The workshop went very quiet. Outside, the afternoon light lay thick and golden on the tannery roofs, ordinary as bread, and Emrys stood in it and felt the morning's crack in their identity widen by a hair's breadth, because they had just realized something about themselves that frightened them more than the thunder had.

They had said *whatever it is* without hesitating.

Ten years of careful erasure. Ten years of hoarding every scrap of self like a miser hoarding coin. And faced with the loss of one more piece—one more memory gouged out of the dwindling stock—they hadn't even flinched. Because some part of them, some part that spoke in thunder and wrote its name in char on mountaintops, had already decided that the memories were going to the court below regardless, and better to spend them buying a child's freedom than hoarding them against a rescue that would never come.

*Come home, little flame,* the storm had said. *We kept all of it for you.*

Maybe, Emrys thought, watching Vess's blind fingers hover over the blank vellum, the mapmaker would choose kindly. Maybe she'd take something small. A taste. A color. The smell of a harbor they'd loved in a life they couldn't recall.

Or maybe she would reach into the hoard and pull out the one thing Emrys had guarded most carefully without ever knowing they were guarding it—the memory at the very bottom of the stack, the keystone, the one that held up the whole architecture of *Emrys Galloway, no one, nobody, a smudge on a window*—

"Sit," Vess said again, gently this time. "This will take a while. And Emrys?"

"Yes?"

"When I choose, you won't feel it leave. That's the mercy of it. You'll only notice the hole later, when you reach for something that isn't there anymore." She smiled again, and this time it was almost kind. "Like reaching for a stair in the dark that isn't there. Now hold still."

Her fingers touched Emrys's temples.

And somewhere far below them both, beneath miles of stone, in a court of quiet fire, something ancient stirred on its throne of embers, and turned its attention upward, and *waited*.
