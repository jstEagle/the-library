## 6. The Cartographer of Small Mercies

The mapmaker lived where the maps ended.

That was the joke Vess liked to tell, Emrys had heard, back when there had still been people to hear jokes from—back in the first years of exile, when Emrys had still been learning the shape of their own disappearance. *I live where the maps end*, she'd say to clients who climbed the switchbacks above the salt-roads expecting a shop and finding instead a hut stitched to the cliff face like a burr to a wolf's coat. Below her door, the mountain fell away into cloud. Above it, the stone went on climbing into country no surveyor had ever troubled to name.

Emrys had not come in ten years. The trail remembered them anyway—or perhaps the trail simply forgot everyone equally, and that was its kindness. Lichen the color of old bruises marked the way where footprints could not survive. Wind came up the valley in long slow breaths, carrying the smell of cold iron and, faintly, underneath everything, the smell of a forge that had never been lit: the smell of the Ember Court waking beneath the world.

Three days from Cinderhollow, and Emrys could still see the mark.

Not Bram's mark—the girl was two days behind them now, left with her mother and a story about seeking a cure for Tam's sleeplessness, a story Bram had delivered with the flat defiance of someone who had learned early that lies were just truths wearing armor. No, what Emrys saw when they closed their eyes was the contract-seal on the girl's palm, the fine ash-gray script coiled there like a sleeping adder, and the way it had shifted in the night since Emrys had first looked at it. Grown by a hair's breadth. The court below the mountain was patient the way stone is patient, but it was not idle.

*Solstice,* Emrys thought, and the word sat in their chest like a swallowed coal. *Nineteen days.*

They climbed.

* * *

Vess opened the door before Emrys knocked.

"Salt-caravan man," she said, tilting her head. She was small, the way trees that grow in wind-shadow are small—all their mass gone sideways, into root and stubbornness. Her eyes were the pale gray of river fog, and they did not fix on anything; they drifted slightly, as if listening rather than looking. "Or no. Not anymore. You've changed your coat." She sniffed. "And your walk. And something else."

"Hello, Vess."

"Don't 'hello Vess' me in that voice." She stepped back from the doorway, an invitation that was also an accusation. "Ten years. Ten years, and you come at dusk, smelling of storm-ozone and other people's prayers. Come in before the mountain hears you. It's been *listening* lately. It's gotten rude about it."

The hut's interior was smaller than memory insisted and more crowded. Maps hung from every surface—not the great vellum surveys of kingdoms that hung in governor's halls, but working maps, worn soft at the folds: shepherd-paths and smuggler-cuts, the veins of abandoned mines, the migratory routes of goats, the places where avalanches liked to sleep. And everywhere, in every margin, in handwriting so small it seemed meant for ants or angels: annotations. *Untrue after the rockfall of the Red Year. Lies. Ask the river instead. This road eats names.*

A single lamp burned on the table, though Emrys could see no oil feeding it. The flame bent toward the door, toward Emrys, like a plant toward sun.

"You felt that too," Vess said quietly, closing the door with three separate bolts. "The attention. It started a month ago—a tickle, like something turning over in its sleep. Then Cinderhollow." She turned. Her fog-colored eyes found Emrys's face with unnerving precision for a blind woman. "You've seen it. The answered shrine. The child out of the river."

"I've seen it."

"And you know whose signature that is."

"The Ember Court." Saying it aloud made the hut feel smaller. "The gods under the mountain. The quiet fire. They're supposed to be dead, Vess. Or asleep past any waking. Everyone starved them out centuries ago."

"Everyone *forgot* them," Vess corrected. She crossed to the table and laid one hand flat upon it, fingers spread, as though reading the wood grain like braille. "There's a difference, and you of all people should know the difference. Starved things die. Forgotten things only wait." A pause, dry as paper. "You'd know all about waiting, wouldn't you? You've been doing it for ten years. Waiting to be forgotten thoroughly enough to be safe."

Emrys said nothing. Outside, the wind worried at the eaves.

"But that's not why you've come." Vess lowered herself onto her stool with the economy of movement of someone who had mapped her own home so completely she never needed to reach for anything. "You want the deep road. The Smuggler's Spine, down through the roots of Thornfell, into the court itself. You want to go *down*."

"There's a girl," Emrys said. "She sold her brother's soul to a voice in a well. She didn't know what she was selling. The debt comes due at solstice, and when it does, the court means to wear her."

Vess was silent a moment. In the lamplight her face did something complicated—grief and recognition tangled together, quickly folded away. "Bram," she said. "Wren's daughter. Yes. I heard about Tam. Half of Cinderhollow heard about Tam; they just didn't understand what they were hearing." Her mouth thinned. "The well-voice. I wondered who'd been bold enough to start collecting again."

"So you'll draw me the way in."

"Oh, I'll *draw* it." Vess laughed, short and without much humor in it. "Drawing is nothing. Drawing is ink and patience, and I have more of both than anyone living. The question is what you'll pay. Because you can't pay me coin, Emrys-of-no-surname. Coin I can get anywhere. What I deal in is rarer."

"Secrets," Emrys said. "So I've heard. Name your price and let's be done."

"That's just it." Vess leaned forward, and for a moment the drifting quality of her gaze stilled, and Emrys felt—absurdly, horribly—*seen*. "You don't understand the trade. I'm not a merchant, I'm a cartographer. I chart what isn't on any other map. And the deepest roads, the roads under the mountain—they aren't drawn in ink. Ink is for surfaces. To travel below, you need a map written in something truer." She tapped her own temple. "Memory. Memory is the only ink that holds its line down there. Every map I've ever sold of the deep roads, I paid for with a memory, and the memory *became* the map. That's the toll. That's always been the toll."

Emrys had known this. Some part of them had known it for ten years and kept the knowledge in the same locked room as everything else. "Then take one," they said, keeping their voice level. "Take any. There's little enough in this head worth keeping. Take my memory of my mother's face—it's half-gone already, rotted through with disuse. Take the taste of the wine they poured in some city I won't name. Take—"

"No."

The word landed like a bolt shot into a door.

"You misunderstand again," Vess said, gently now, which was worse. "You think you get to choose because you think everything in you that matters has already been cut out—that what remains is scrap, and scrap is cheap. But that's precisely backwards. Your scraps are the most expensive things in this whole mountain." She rose, and even small, even blind, she suddenly filled the hut. "You burned yourself, storm-caller. I've watched people burn themselves before—I make my trade among the scorched—but I've never watched anyone do it so *thoroughly*. You didn't erase your crimes. You erased your *self*. Every warm thing. Every ordinary thing. Do you know how rare that makes you? Do you know what a mapmaker could do with the interior geography of a person who amputated their own soul?"

"I'm not selling you my soul. I need it. All the ragged bits of it. It's the only currency the court will honor."

"And I'm not asking for your soul." Vess extended her hand, palm up—an echo, Emrys realized with a lurch, of the mark on Bram's palm, the same gesture, the ancient posture of transaction. "I'm asking for one memory. Just one. But *I* choose which."

The lamp-flame stood perfectly upright between them, as if afraid to move.

"That wasn't the bargain," Emrys said.

"It's the only bargain there's ever been. Ask anyone who's bought a deep-road map. The mapmaker chooses, because the mapmaker must chart true, and truth can't be dictated by the buyer. If you choose the payment, the map lies. And a lying map under the mountain doesn't kill you, Emrys. It delivers you. Straight to whatever's hungry."

Silence. The wind outside had stopped, Emrys noticed. The whole mountain seemed to be holding its breath, listening at the keyhole.

"What are you going to take?" Their voice came out lower than intended.

"I don't know yet." Vess smiled, and it was not unkind, which somehow made it unbearable. "That's the honest answer. I'll know it when I touch it. There's always one—the memory you didn't know you had, the one that survived the burning because you never knew it was flammable. Something hidden so deep even you couldn't find it to destroy it. Everyone has one or two. Yours..." She tilted her head. "Yours are *heavy*. I can feel them from here, sitting in you like stones at the bottom of a well. One of them wants to be taken. It's been wanting for years."

"This is extortion."

"This is cartography." 

* * *

Later—minutes or hours; the hut kept its own time—Emrys sat across the table from Vess with their bare right hand palm-up between them, and tried not to remember anything at all, which is the hardest remembering there is.

Vess had wrapped her fingers around theirs. Her hands were cool and dry and precise, a surveyor's hands. Her fog-pale eyes had rolled shut. And Emrys felt the strangest sensation: not of being searched, but of being *read aloud*. Somewhere inside, shelves were being walked. Somewhere inside, a blind woman was running her fingers along the spines of everything Emrys had chosen to keep, and humming softly, the way scholars hum.

"Mm," Vess said. "Mm. No. No—you kept that one deliberately; it's a decoy. Clever. No. Oh—" a pause, and her breath caught, "—oh, *there* you are."

Her grip tightened.

"What is it?" Emrys asked. "What did you find?"

"A morning," Vess whispered. Her face had gone strange—rapt, young, lit from within by borrowed light. "A small one. Nothing happened in it. That's why you missed it. There was no crime in it, no god, no storm. Just—rain on a window. Someone laughing in another room. Someone whose laugh you knew better than your own heartbeat. You were happy, Emrys. For one entire unremarkable morning, years before any of the rest of it, you were simply *happy*, and you never once thought to burn it, because you never once knew you had it." Tears had begun moving down her cheeks; she did not seem to notice. "Do you understand what I'm holding? This is the last morning of your old life. Everything else you fed to the fire. This one piece of kindling survived because it was buried too deep for you to reach. And it is the single most beautiful thing I have ever charted."

"Take it," Emrys said, and hated how steady the word came out. "If that's the price. Take it and draw me my map."

"It will hurt," Vess warned. "It hurts more when it's small. Big griefs tear. Little joys *unpick*."

"Draw the map."

Vess drew a long breath. And then she pulled.

There was no sound. That was the terrible part—no thunder, no tearing cry, though Emrys's mouth was open and something was coming out of it that might have been a scream or might have been wind. It was like watching a page lifted out of a book while the book stays bound: the morning departed, the rain-on-glass, the laugh in the other room, the warmth of tea in both hands, the feeling—*the feeling*, gods, the feeling of being a person who expected good things—lifted clean away. What remained was the hole it had occupied. A morning-shaped absence. Emrys would spend years afterward reaching for that absence in idle moments, the way a tongue worries a missing tooth, and finding only smooth blank enamel, and wondering vaguely why that particular nothing ached.

When it was over, Vess sat back, gray-eyed and wet-faced, holding nothing visible in her cupped hands, and wept openly.

"Get out," she said thickly. "No—wait. Wait. Let me finish crying and then I'll finish your map, because if I stop halfway the roads will close behind you, and I will not send anyone down there with a map that closes." She wiped her face with her wrist like a child. "*Gods.* Ten years I've wondered what was under all that ash. Now I know. It was a morning. You paid a decade of exile and a lifetime of loneliness to protect *one rainy morning*, and you didn't even know it."

"Is that pity?"

"That's professional admiration." She was already pulling fresh vellum toward her, already grinding ink, her hands moving with sudden ferocious purpose. "Now hold still and tell me everything about the girl. Every detail of the mark. The exact wording the well-voice used, if she remembers it—wording matters below, wording is *law* below. You're going to need a perfect map, Emrys Galloway."

Emrys went very still. "I never told you my surname."

"You didn't?" Vess didn't look up from the vellum. Her pen moved in tiny, certain strokes. "Huh. Then it must have been in the morning. It must have been in the laugh." She paused—and for just a moment her pen stopped, and her fog-colored eyes lifted, and something in her face was frightened in a way that had nothing to do with blindness.

"Because here's the trouble," she said softly. "Memories don't leave a body empty, they leave it *open*. And I only took the one. So you should know, before you go down there, what else I read on those shelves while my fingers were walking." She set down the pen. "There's another stone at the bottom of your well. Bigger than the morning. Older. And it's been *stirring* ever since I touched you—and Emrys, it knows the way to the Ember Court better than any map I could ever draw. It's been there before. Whatever you burned out of yourself—"

Outside, far below in the darkening valley, thunder rolled. Clear sky. Dry air. Thunder anyway.

"—it's starting to remember you back."
