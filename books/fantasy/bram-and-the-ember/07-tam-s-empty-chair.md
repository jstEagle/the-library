## 7. Tam's Empty Chair

The chair had always been the third one from the window.

Bram could not have said why that mattered, only that it did—that in eleven years of suppers, through lean winters and fat ones, through the year their father coughed himself into the churchyard and the years after when it was just the three of them and the smell of tallow and boiled barley, Tam had sat with his back to the window and his elbows on the scarred wood and complained about everything. The porridge was too thin. The bread was too dark. The fire smoked. He complained the way other boys whistled, constant and tuneless, and their mother would swat at him with a spoon and tell him gratitude was a muscle he'd never exercised, and Tam would grin, because being scolded was its own kind of warmth.

Now the chair was full again, and Bram could not stop looking at it.

Tam sat where he had always sat. His hands were folded neatly on the table—too neatly, the way a doll's hands are posed—and his face wore an expression she recognized from mirrors: mild, attentive, waiting to be told what to feel. Their mother had ladled out the stew, three bowls, and set the good spoon at Tam's place, the one with the handle worn smooth by their father's grip, and Tam had said thank you in a voice like a well echoing back a dropped coin.

It was his voice. That was the worst of it. It was exactly his voice, pitched exactly as he used to pitch it, and if Bram shut her eyes she could almost believe—but her eyes were open, and open eyes were a punishment she kept choosing.

"More turnip?" their mother asked, hovering, radiant, terrible.

"Yes," Tam said. "Please."

He didn't eat it. That was another thing. He lifted the spoon, carried it to his mouth, and the stew went in, but nothing changed in him—not the swallow, not the small satisfied sound he used to make, not the second helping he'd always angle for before their mother even offered. The food went into him the way rain goes into a dry stone: accepted, unaltered, gone.

"You're eating well," their mother said, and her voice cracked down the middle with joy, and Bram had to look at the table because looking at her mother's face was worse than looking at Tam's. "You're eating so well, my love. I knew you'd come back to us. I knew it. A mother knows."

*He never left,* Bram thought. *That's what you don't understand. He never went anywhere. Something just moved in.*

She had tried to explain it once, three nights ago, standing in the doorway of the room they shared while Tam lay in his bed staring at the ceiling without blinking, without breathing, without doing any of the hundred small animal things a sleeping boy does—no shifting, no mumbling, no turning toward the door when it creaked. She had gotten as far as *"Mam, listen, the night of the fever, there was a voice in the well, and I—"*

Their mother had slapped her.

Not hard. That was somehow worse. A quick, stinging, desperate slap, the kind you give someone to wake them from a faint, and then she'd gathered Bram against her chest and held her so tightly Bram's ribs ached, and whispered into her hair: *"Don't. Don't say it. Whatever you think happened, whatever you dreamed—the fever took him and gave him back, and we are not going to pick at the miracle until it bleeds."*

So Bram had stopped saying it. She carried it instead, the way she carried everything now, pressed down under her breastbone like a stone in a sack of grain. The voice in the well. The cold breath rising out of it smelling of ash and honey. The words she'd spoken—she could still feel them in her mouth, still taste them, sweet and metallic, like sucking on a copper coin:

*I'll pay anything. Take anything. Just let him live.*

And the voice had said, gentle as a grandmother: *Anything is such a big word, little one. Let's start smaller. Let's start with what you love best.*

She hadn't understood. That was the thing she couldn't make anyone see, the thing that made her want to scream at the whole stupid world: she had been nine years old and starving and her brother was dying and she had thought *anything* meant the goat, or the silver buttons off Da's coat, or her own two hands if it came to that. She had stood at the lip of the well in the dark with sleet needling the back of her neck and offered up her goat and her buttons and her hands, and the voice had sighed with something like pity and said, *No, child. Not the things around the love. The love itself.*

And then Tam had woken up.

The village called it a miracle. Old Mother Hesk, who buried more people than she married, had crossed herself and said the saints had mercy in them after all. The priest had rung the bell. And their mother had wept for a day and a night and then begun setting the table for three again, and for five years—five years of Tam going to the mine with the other boys, of Tam laughing at festivals, of Tam complaining about porridge—it had almost been possible to forget the well entirely.

Almost. Because Tam had stopped dreaming.

He'd told her that himself, once, in a rare unguarded moment, frowning at his own hands like they belonged to a stranger. *I don't dream anymore, Bram. Isn't that strange? I lie down and it's dark and then it's morning.* He'd laughed about it. But Bram had remembered the well. Remembered the voice. And some cold, patient part of her had understood, even then, what she had actually signed away—not his body, not his breath, but the secret warm country inside him where a soul goes walking when it thinks no one is watching.

She had sold the dreaming. She had sold the inside of her brother and kept the outside, and everyone had clapped.

* * *

Tonight the stew was venison. Real venison, dark and rich, from a haunch their mother had traded half their wool stores for, because Tam had mentioned—once, idly, in that borrowed voice—that he liked venison.

Except Tam had never liked venison. Tam had hated venison. Tam had once picked every shred of it out of a pie at the harvest feast and eaten the crust alone, declaring that deer tasted like sad old boots, and their mother had been there, she had laughed, she had—

"Mam," Bram said. "Tam hates venison."

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight and grain, like the air before lightning.

Their mother's hand paused over the pot. When she turned, her smile was still on her face, but it had gone strange, stretched thin as ice over moving water. "What a thing to say."

"He does. He always has. Ask him."

"Bram—"

"Ask him, Mam."

They both looked at Tam. Tam looked back at them with those mild, patient eyes—his eyes, exactly his eyes, hazel shot through with green, the eyes she'd known since before she'd known anything else—and smiled, and said, in his voice, in his perfect, hollowed-out voice:

"I like venison now."

And something in Bram's chest tore along a seam she hadn't known was there.

Because that was the answer, wasn't it? That was the whole horrible truth laid out plain on the table between the bowls. *I like venison now.* Not *I've changed my mind.* Not *I don't remember hating it.* *Now.* As if tastes were garments to be put on. As if the thing wearing her brother had consulted some ledger of what a boy ought to enjoy and amended its records accordingly. It spoke in borrowed voices—all of them, she'd heard it use the priest's cadence, the miller's laugh, once, horribly, their dead father's low rumble humming through Tam's throat while he slept—and it got them nearly right, nearly perfect, and the *nearly* was a knife you only felt on the second look.

"It's delicious," Tam added helpfully. "Thank you, Mam."

Their mother sat down slowly. Her hands were shaking. Bram watched her look at her son—at the son, at the shape of the son, at the beautiful returned miracle of the son—and watched her decide, right there at the table, in real time, not to ask. Bram saw the question rise in her like a bubble through deep water and saw her press it down, press it down, press it down, until her face smoothed over and she said, "Well. Good. Eat, then. Both of you. Growing children need meat."

*Mam.* Bram gripped her spoon until her knuckles went white. *Mam, he casts no shadow.*

She had noticed it a week ago, in the lamplight, and had spent days convincing herself she'd imagined it. She hadn't. The lamp sat center-table; it threw shadows of all three of them against the wall, hers long and wavering, her mother's broad and stooped. Tam's place on the wall held only the wall. Firelight poured past him as though he were a window, a hole cut in the world, and their mother had hung a blanket over that stretch of plaster last week and claimed it was to stop the draft.

The blanket was still there. It would be there tonight, when they went to bed. It would be there forever, Bram understood suddenly, until one of them found the courage to say the word *shadow* out loud, and it was not going to be her mother, and God forgive her, it might not be her either.

"I saw Vess today," their mother said brightly, into the silence. "Down by the bridge. She says there's strange doings in the high passes. Caravans coming through routes that aren't on any map. And the shrine by the river—you know the drowned one, the old one—" Her voice did the thing it did when she was filling space, tumbling forward too fast. "People have been leaving offerings. And this morning the Aldermans' girl came up out of the water. Drowned in spring thaw, poor mite, and there she was on the bank, wet as a new babe, asking for her supper. Whole town's talking. Whole town's lighting candles."

"That's nice," Tam said.

"It's a *miracle season*, is what it is." Their mother's eyes shone wet and fierce. "First our Tam, now the Alderman girl. The old powers are waking up, Vess says. Waking up hungry and generous both. Well, I say it's about time somebody down there remembered us."

Bram's blood went cold and colder.

*Hungry and generous both.* The words snagged in her like a fishhook. Because she knew that arithmetic. She had learned it kneeling in the slush at the lip of a well with her heart in her mouth: that below the world there was a court that paid in miracles and collected in love, that generosity was just hunger wearing its good coat, that every answered prayer was a debt with the interest already accruing. The Aldermans' girl walking out of the river wasn't a gift. It was bait on a hook the size of a town. And her mother—her exhausted, grieving, hopeful mother—was calling down blessings on it, was feeding it candlelight and gratitude, was setting the table for it the same way she set the table for the thing in her son's chair.

Three places. Every night. Plate, cup, the good spoon.

Bram looked at the third place setting and finally, finally understood what she was looking at. It wasn't grief. It wasn't hope. It was *payment*. Her mother was keeping a seat warm at the family table for the court below, the way you keep a lamp lit in a window for a traveler, the way you leave out milk for the fair folk—not because you love them, but because you're afraid to stop. Somewhere in the five years since the well, without ever knowing it, her mother had become a keeper of shrines, and the shrine was her own supper table, and the offering was the daily theater of a family that no longer existed.

Love was the currency. It had always been the currency. The voice in the well had told her so, and she hadn't listened, and now the bill was arriving course by course, and her mother was paying it gladly, gratefully, with both hands, and thanking it for the privilege.

"May I be excused?" Tam asked.

"You haven't finished your—"

"May I *be excused*?" Still polite. Still mild. But underneath the borrowed voice, for just a heartbeat, Bram heard something else—a flatness, a patience, the tone of a clerk repeating a question to a slow customer. The lamp guttered though there was no draft. The stew in Tam's untouched bowl had gone cold, frost feathering its surface, in a warm kitchen, in June.

Their mother flinched. "Yes. Of course, love. Of course."

Tam rose, folded his napkin into a precise square, thanked her for the meal in their father's voice—*thank you kindly, missus, fine spread*—and walked out of the kitchen with his back very straight, and the firelight poured through him all the way to the door.

Bram heard him climb the stairs. Each footfall landed at exactly the same interval, like a clock. At the top, the footsteps stopped, and a voice drifted down—not Tam's, not anyone's, a voice like many voices speaking together under the floorboards of the world, soft as ash falling:

*"Five years, little one. The interest compounds. Come solstice, we'll discuss the principal."*

Then nothing. Just the ordinary sounds of the house settling, and her mother gathering bowls, and outside, far off in the mountains, a light flickering orange against the night sky where no fire should burn—ember-colored, patient, bright as an opening eye.

Bram sat at the table with her cold stew and her hammering heart, and beneath the terror, rising through it like a spring through snowmelt, she felt something new take root: not guilt this time, not the old sick shame, but a hard little coal of purpose.

She didn't know how yet. She didn't know what or whom or at what price. But she knew this much: whatever she had sold, she was going to buy her brother back—and this time she would read the contract first.

There was, she remembered, a storm-caller passing through Cinderhollow. A salt-caravan guide the miners talked about in the tavern, who moved through Thornfell unseen and asked no questions and owed no gods.

Tomorrow, Bram decided, she was going to find them.

Tonight, quietly, while her mother slept, she was going to take the good spoon off the third place setting—and throw it in the well.
