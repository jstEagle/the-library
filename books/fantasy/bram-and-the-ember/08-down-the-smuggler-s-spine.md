## 8. Down the Smuggler's Spine

*Emrys*

The deep road began where the mapmakers' courage ended.

Emrys had told Bram this the night before, standing at the mouth of a crevice so narrow that the mountain seemed to have swallowed a doorway and forgotten to leave one behind. She had looked at it the way most people looked at open graves, and Emrys had said nothing to comfort her, because comfort was a currency they no longer carried. Instead they had handed her a hooded lantern and said, *Breathe slow. The mountain hates fast breathers.*

Now, three hours in, Bram was breathing like a woman being born and dying at once, and Emrys found themselves almost—almost—amused.

"Stop," they said, raising a hand. The passage ahead forked around a fallen pillar of black stone, and the air moved wrong through it: too warm on the left, cold as wellwater on the right. "Which way feels worse?"

Bram stared at them. "That's not a question anyone asks."

"It's the only question that matters down here. The mountain lies to your eyes. It lies less to your skin." Emrys crouched, pressing their palm flat against the stone floor, letting the faint tremor of moving water travel up through bone. "Left side's breathing. There's a hollow behind it, something old exhaling. Right side is dead stone all the way to the heart of Thornfell."

"So we go right."

"We go left."

"Why?"

Emrys stood, brushing grit from their knee. "Because whatever breathes back there has been breathing for ten thousand years and hasn't eaten anyone yet. Dead stone means the road's collapsed somewhere past it, and I'd rather share a tunnel with an old god's lung than dig my way out with my fingernails."

Bram considered this with the flat, unblinking seriousness that Emrys was beginning to suspect was her native weather. Fourteen years old and she had already survived a plague winter, a bargain she didn't understand, and a mark on her palm that burned when she dreamed. Fear had stopped surprising her somewhere along the way; now it was just another chore, like hauling water.

"You've done this before," she said. It wasn't a question either.

"I've done everything before," Emrys said, and turned down the left-hand passage before she could ask what *everything* meant.

The smuggler's spine ran beneath three ranges and above two kingdoms' maps, and for a decade it had been Emrys Galloway's whole country. They knew its grammar the way priests knew liturgy: which echoes meant open cavern and which meant hollowed-out bones of some pre-human thing; which drips were water and which were the court below, leaking upward. The deep roads had been cut by hands older than any living nation, roads for people who needed never to be seen—and Emrys had needed that more than anyone alive.

Or so they had believed. Lately the belief sat differently in their chest, like a coat that no longer fit across the shoulders.

Behind them, Bram's boots scraped. She was learning to walk quietly—that much was true—but she hadn't yet learned that quiet wasn't silence. Silence was a thing you made with your whole body, your weight distributed into the stone, your breath timed to the mountain's own long respiration. Emrys kept meaning to teach her properly and kept instead simply walking, letting her copy them the way water copies the shape of its channel.

It occurred to them, somewhere past the third mile marker—a notch carved into the wall by smugglers dead two centuries—that they had not spoken this many words to another person in six years.

They decided not to think about that.

* * *

*Bram*

Bram counted things to keep from screaming.

Steps. Turns. Times the lantern guttered and steadied. Times Emrys stopped without explanation and she had to stop too, pressed against warm stone, listening to something enormous shift in the dark ahead until Emrys's hand came up—*wait*—and then down—*now*—and they moved again.

She was up to four hundred and eleven steps since the last rest when she finally asked the question that had been sitting in her chest since Cinderhollow.

"What did you do? Before. To end up down here."

Emrys didn't break stride. "I walked."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the truest one I have." A pause, then, grudgingly, like a coin pried from a fist: "I lived in the lowlands. Things went wrong. I came to the mountains and made sure nothing here would ever know my name. That's all."

Bram chewed on that. Her mother said you could tell a lie by how smooth it was, and this answer was smooth as river stone, worn down by telling or by time. But there was something underneath it, some grain of truth that caught.

"My gran used to say the mountains remember everyone who hides in them," Bram said. "She said Thornfell keeps a list."

"Your gran sounds wise."

"She also said turnips cured fever, and she died of fever, so." Bram ducked under a shelf of rock, feeling it graze her hood. "Maybe don't quote her."

Something happened then that Bram would think about for weeks afterward: Emrys laughed. It was a small sound, rusty, startled out of them like a bird flushed from cover—and they immediately looked annoyed about it, jaw tightening, pace quickening. But Bram had heard it. She filed it away next to her other treasures: Tam's laugh, before the well; the smell of bread on the one good morning last winter; the exact blue of the sky the day the plague finally broke.

People thought she was hard because she didn't cry. But Bram knew the truth: she just kept everything. Every feeling, every moment, stacked and hoarded like a miser's coins. You couldn't take from someone who never spent.

"How far to the gate?" she asked, because asking about the destination was safer than asking about the person.

"Three days, if the spine holds. Two, if we're lucky and stupid."

"Which are we?"

"Currently lucky," Emrys said. "Ask me again tomorrow."

* * *

*Emrys*

They camped in a smugglers' cache Emrys had used perhaps forty times over the decade: a bubble of cavern off the main road, walled with dry-stone, floored with old planks, stocked once upon a time with oil and hardtack by men whose names were lost even to the mountains. Emrys lit no fire. Firelight traveled in the deep roads like gossip in a village.

Bram ate her ration slowly, watching Emrys check the cache's entrance, reset the loose stone that disguised it, and lay down fully clothed with their back to the wall and their face toward the dark.

"You sleep facing the door," she observed.

"I sleep facing every door."

"What are you afraid of?"

Emrys was quiet long enough that Bram might have thought them asleep, if their breathing hadn't been so carefully awake. "Nothing specific," they said at last. "That's what makes it efficient."

Bram snorted. In the lantern light her face was all angles and ash-brown stubbornness, and there was a smudge of cave dust across her cheekbone that she either didn't know about or didn't care about. Ten days ago Emrys had found her trying to claw the contract-mark off her own palm with a kitchen knife, and had taken the knife away and said, *You'll want that hand intact where we're going,* and she had looked at them with eyes old as the mines and said, *Fine. But if it comes down to me or Tam, you cut it off yourself.*

Emrys had agreed, because lying to children was crueler than the truth.

Now they watched her tuck the lantern close like a hen over an egg and pull her knees up, and felt the strange, unwelcome weight of responsibility settle onto shoulders built for carrying nothing but their own secrecy.

"Tell me about him," Emrys heard themselves say. "Your brother."

Bram's chin lifted. For a moment Emrys expected defiance—the girl had a hedgehog's relationship to sympathy—but then something in her simply... unlocked.

"He's twelve. Was twelve. He used to dream out loud, did you know that? Whole stories, every night, talking in his sleep. Da said he'd be a bard. Ma said he'd be a liar." Her voice went thin and steady, the way voices go when they're holding a great deal. "When the sickness came, half of Cinderhollow was coughing blood into rags. Ma was already gray. And I went out to the well because Old Marta said the deep places still answered if you asked right, and I was so angry, and I said—I said *take anything.*"

The lantern flame leaned, though there was no draft.

"And it took him," Bram said. "Not his body. His body still eats supper. But he doesn't dream anymore, and he doesn't cast a shadow, and when he talks it's like hearing someone else's words come out of his mouth. And Ma sets his place at the table and smiles at him and I can't—I can't tell her, because she'd rather set a place for a ghost than grieve a son." Her fists closed. The mark on her palm glowed faintly through her fingers, ember-orange, patient. "So yes. Tell me what we're walking into. Tell me exactly what it costs to buy him back. Because I've already paid once and I'd pay again, whatever it is. Whatever it *is.*"

Emrys looked at her for a long moment.

"The court deals in memory," they said quietly. "Souls, mostly, but souls are just memories wearing a body's shape. When we get there, we will not be buying. Buying tells them what we value. We will be trading—giving them something they want more than the boy, so the boy becomes worthless stock." They paused. "And Bram. If it comes to it—if the trade requires something of mine—you let me make it. That's the price of my company. Not your hand. Mine."

"That's a stupid bargain."

"It's the only one I'm offering."

Bram glared at them across the little circle of light, fourteen years old and armored in guilt, and Emrys recognized the armor because they had forged a set just like it, years ago, and worn it until it grew into the skin.

"Sleep," they said. "Tomorrow I teach you the echo-language. If you're going to walk the spine, you'll walk it like you belong to it."

* * *

*Bram*

The echo-language, it turned out, was real.

Emrys taught her at dawn—or what passed for dawn, a gray thickening of the dark that Bram chose to believe was morning—and by midday she was reading the tunnels the way she read tracks in snow. A sharp double-report off the left wall meant open space beyond. A swallowed, woolly echo meant the road narrowed. And a sound she had no name for, a kind of wet resonance that made her teeth ache, meant the Ember Court was near enough to leak.

"Again," Emrys said, and clapped twice, sharply, twenty paces down the passage.

"Open on the right, tight on the left, and—" Bram tilted her head, hating the sound, "—something's dripping that isn't water."

"Good." Emrys's voice held no praise, but no correction either, and Bram had learned in ten days that this was the highest grade they gave. "You learn faster than the salt-caravan men. Faster than most."

"Most people aren't running from anything."

"Everyone's running from something. Most are just bad at it." Emrys shouldered their pack. "Come on. We cross the Whisper Gallery before the mountain wakes proper."

The Whisper Gallery took two hours to traverse and Bram would remember it her whole life, however long that proved to be. The ceiling rose into darkness like the inside of a throat, and the walls were covered—*covered*—in names. Tens of thousands of them, scratched and chiseled and burned into the stone, in scripts dead and living and some that hurt to look at directly. Smugglers' marks, Emrys said. Names given freely to the road, the old toll, from the days before the gate below formalized the payment.

"They gave their names away?" Bram whispered, and even whispering felt loud.

"To the road, not to the court. There's a difference. A name given freely stays yours. A name *taken*—" Emrys touched one inscription, gently, the way you'd touch a grave-marker. Their fingers lingered a half-second too long. "A name taken belongs to whoever holds it."

Bram watched them. Something in Emrys's face had gone distant and careful, the look of a person standing at the edge of a hole they couldn't see the bottom of.

"Yours isn't up here," she said. It wasn't a question. She'd looked, actually—scanned the walls while pretending to study them—for *Galloway*, for anything. She'd told herself it was practical. Knowing your guide's name seemed useful. But she knew, in the hoarding place inside her where she kept true things, that it was more than that. She wanted to be able to say it. To give him—her—*them*, whatever Emrys was—to give them back something.

"No," Emrys said. "Mine isn't anywhere."

And Bram, keeper of small hoarded truths, understood suddenly and completely that this was not a boast. It was a wound, self-inflicted, and very old.

She didn't ask. Some debts you don't call in early.

Instead she pulled the stub of charcoal from her pocket—the one she'd been using to mark their turns—and, before Emrys could stop her, scratched four letters into the wall among the ten thousand dead names:

**BRAM**

"There," she said, cheeks hot. "Now the road knows me. Freely given. So it stays mine, right? That's how it works?"

Emrys stared at the letters. Then at her. And Bram saw something move behind their eyes—something that looked almost like grief, and almost like envy, and almost like hope, three things that shouldn't fit in one face but did.

"Yes," Emrys said softly. "That's how it works."

They walked on. And that night, at the second camp, when Bram woke shivering, she found Emrys sitting awake beside her—not guarding the doors for once, but sitting close, radiating heat like a banked stove, saying nothing, expecting nothing.

She pretended to stay asleep, and hoarded that too.

* * *

*Emrys*

On the third day the spine began to descend in earnest, switchbacking down through strata of stone the color of old coals, and Emrys knew the gate was close because the whispers started.

Not the gallery's echoes. Real whispers. Thin as wire, threading up from below, and Emrys walked faster, and Bram matched them, and neither spoke of it.

At the last bend before the tollgate's antechamber, Bram caught their sleeve.

"Emrys." First time she'd used the name aloud; it sounded borrowed, a placeholder, and they both knew it. "Whatever happens down there. If it goes wrong. I wanted—" She stopped, jaw working, a girl trying to spend feelings she'd never once spent. "You didn't have to come. Nobody made you. I know what that's worth. I just wanted you to know I know."

Emrys looked at her—this plague-winter child with a god's brand on her hand and her brother's soul in hock—and felt the decade of erasure strain against something it had no defense against.

Gratitude. Aimed at *them*. From someone who *knew* them, however little there was to know.

"Bram," they said carefully. "When we get down there, do exactly as I say. Speak when I speak. Be silent when I'm silent. And whatever the Archivist offers you—" the word came out of Emrys's mouth before they knew it was in it, *Archivist*, and they frowned, because they had never heard that title before and could not say why it tasted familiar— "whatever it offers, refuse. Refuse twice. Refuse loudly."

"The Archivist," Bram repeated. "You've been there before?"

"No," Emrys said.

But their feet, carrying them down the last slope toward the ember-light rising from below, seemed very sure of the way.

And far beneath them, in a vault lined with shelved and sleeping lives, something that had waited ten years for a nameless storm-caller to come home opened one lid, one eye, one ledger—

and began, with great tenderness, to write.
