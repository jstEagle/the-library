## 9. The Tollgate of Names

The deep road ended where the mountain's ribs closed like a throat.

Emrys had known it would. Vess had drawn it for them in charcoal on cured hide—the smuggler's spine narrowing at last into a fissure no wider than a coffin, and beyond it, the threshold. *The gate is old*, she had said, her blind eyes aimed somewhere past Emrys's shoulder, as if she could see through stone to what waited below. *Older than the court itself. The gods built it before they learned to lie, back when names were still heavy things, still worth carrying.* She had tapped the hide with one cracked fingernail. *It will ask you what everything under the mountain asks. And you will have nothing to give it.*

Now, standing before it with Bram's breath quick and shallow behind them, Emrys understood that Vess had not been warning them. She had been mourning.

The gate was not stone. Or rather, it was not only stone—it was stone that remembered being something else, some vast joint of the world before the world cooled. It filled the fissure wall to wall, floor to ceiling, and its surface moved the way a sleeping animal's flank moves: slowly, with heat beneath. Veins of ember-light crawled through it in patterns that almost resolved into letters, almost resolved into script older than any alphabet Emrys had ever stolen or sold or burned. Where the light pooled thickest, the rock had gone smooth and dark as cooling glass.

And it was listening.

Emrys felt it the way they felt weather coming—a pressure behind the eyes, a taste of iron at the back of the throat. Ten years of practice had taught them to sense attention the way sailors sensed wind: by its absence, mostly, by the blessed nothing that followed them from town to town. But this was the opposite of absence. This was attention like a held breath, vast and patient, coiled in the rock and waiting for them to speak.

"Is it alive?" Bram whispered. She had been brave for six days now—brave through the collapsing stair, brave through the gallery of hanging lanterns each one lit with something that used to be a person—but bravery sat strangely on her, like a coat cut for someone taller. Her hand, the marked hand, was pressed flat against her chest as if she could hold the debt in place by force.

"No," Emrys said. Then, because lying to her had begun to feel like a weight too: "I don't know."

They set down the packs. There was no sense carrying anything further; whatever happened at this gate, packs would be irrelevant. Emrys unwrapped the last of Vess's provisions—hard salt bread, a twist of dried plum—and made Bram eat half. She chewed mechanically, watching the gate, and Emrys watched her watch it, and thought about how much easier this would have been alone. That was the trouble with guiding people. You started measuring your own death by what it would cost others.

*You will have nothing to give it.*

Vess had been right, as she had been right about everything, and her rightness had cost Emrys a memory they still couldn't name—only feel, the way you feel an amputated limb, an ache shaped exactly like something missing. Whatever she had taken from them, it left a hole in the shape of a word.

Because here was the arithmetic of the gate, plain as a knife on a table: every traveler must surrender a true name. Not a given name, not a use-name, not the false syllables Emrys had worn across a decade of villages—*Aldous, Merrin, the quiet salt-factor with the limp, the deaf carter, nobody, nobody, nobody.* A true name. The sound the world made when it meant you specifically. The thread that tied a soul to a body so that gods could find it, courts could claim it, debts could come due against it.

Emrys Galloway did not have one.

They had spent ten years making certain of it. They had unpicked themselves from parish rolls and ledger books, from mothers' mouths and lovers' letters, from the memory of every witness who had ever watched them work a storm. They had paid cartographers and priests and one very expensive thief in Greywater to carry their name away into forgetting. What remained was a competent shell that walked quietly and slept badly and owned nothing that could be confiscated. It had seemed like wisdom. It had seemed like survival.

Standing before the gate, Emrys understood—for the first time, with the whole force of the thing—that it might instead be damnation. Because a soul without a name was not invisible to powers like these. A soul without a name was *unclaimed territory*. And unclaimed things, under old law, belonged to whoever seized them first.

The gate would not let them pass as a guest, offering tribute. It would let them pass as property.

Unless.

Emrys turned the problem over, the way they'd once turned storms over—looking for the seam in the thing, the flaw in its architecture. Gates demanded names. Very well. But demand was not discernment. The gate was old, Vess said; old as the gods' honesty, before they learned to lie. Which meant it was built for a world where everyone *had* a true name, where the question had only one honest answer. It was not built for a creature who had spent a decade manufacturing silence.

It could not miss what had never been there to find.

But it would try. Oh, it would try. Emrys could feel it already reaching toward them, patient as root-growth, tasting the air around their skin for the flavor of a self. And when it found nothing—when its ancient certainty collided with their manufactured void—it would do what all hungry things do with an empty dish.

It would take something else.

Blood. The thought arrived with terrible clarity. Names lived in blood—every hedge-priest knew it, every curse-writer, every god. Blood was the ink the body signed itself with. If the gate could not have Emrys's name, it would settle for the medium of one. It would write its own entry in them. And whatever it wrote—

Whatever it wrote, Emrys would carry into the court below, stamped and legible. Property of the threshold. They would walk into the Ember Court already branded.

Behind them, Bram finished the plum and wiped her mouth on her sleeve. "You're doing the face," she said.

"What face?"

"The one where you've decided something bad and you're deciding whether to tell me." She came to stand beside them, small and grim in her dead brother's coat. Six days of deep roads had aged her past fourteen and back down again into something harder. "Tam used to make that face. Right before he told me the truth about something awful, because he said I'd rather be scared knowing than safe not." Her chin came up. "I'm owed truths. I paid for them."

The mark on her palm had begun to glow faintly in the gate's presence—two thin lines of ember-light, like a seam of fire stitched under the skin. Emrys looked at it, and at her, and gave her the truth.

"It wants my name. I don't have one. So it will take payment in kind." They flexed their right hand. "There will be blood, and I won't know until afterward what it bought. When we go through—if we go through—you keep walking. No matter what I look like. No matter what I say. You keep walking toward the firelight, and you don't stop until we're inside. Do you understand?"

"You're scared," Bram said. Not a question. She was studying their face with the frankness of the young, cataloguing fear the way other children collected river stones.

"Yes."

"Good." She said it fiercely, and for a moment she was not a girl with a debt but something older, some ancestor of hers standing in a plague winter refusing to die politely. "Scared people check their knots twice."

Emrys almost smiled. Almost.

* * *

They approached together, because Emrys had learned in six days that Bram bolted if left alone with her thoughts, and because—though they'd have denied it under torture—some stubborn animal part of them wanted another heartbeat beside theirs at the end.

Up close, the gate's surface was worse. The ember-veins pulsed in slow rhythm, and Emrys realized with a lurch that the rhythm matched their own pulse exactly, had probably matched it since the moment they entered the fissure. The gate was not merely listening. It was *attuning*—learning the tempo of them, reading the small drum of their heart the way a scholar reads a page.

When they were three paces away, it spoke.

Not in words. Words would have been merciful. It spoke in the space behind words, a pressure that became meaning directly inside the skull, the way pain does:

***NAME.***

One syllable of absolute demand. Emrys's knees nearly buckled—not from volume but from intimacy. The gate had spoken *into* them, past skin and skull, into the vaulted dark where a name should have lived. And it found—

Nothing.

The silence rang. For one horrible instant Emrys stood inside their own emptiness and heard it echo, the way a robbed house echoes, the way an empty cradle echoes. Ten years of careful erasure, and here at the bottom of the world it did not feel like armor. It felt like a wound the gate was considering licking.

***NAME,*** the gate asked again, and this time the pressure carried something new. Curiosity. Hunger sharpening into interest. ***WHAT ARE YOU, THAT THE WORLD DOES NOT SPEAK YOU?***

"I'm a broker," Emrys said aloud, and their voice sounded absurdly small, a match struck in a cathedral. "A trader in souls. I've come to deal with the court."

***BROKERS HAVE NAMES.***

"Not mine."

The silence that followed stretched so long that Emrys heard Bram's breathing speed up beside them, heard the drip of water somewhere far up the fissure, heard their own heart—still keeping perfect time with the gate's slow pulse, traitor organ. The ember-veins brightened. The smooth dark patches began to warm, radiating heat like a forge door swinging open, and Emrys understood that the gate was deliberating, weighing them on scales older than mercy.

Then the heat focused.

It came off the rock in a single directed wave, a lance of warmth that found Emrys's right hand and *pulled*. No—that wasn't right either. It didn't pull. It *asked*, in the oldest grammar there was, the grammar of sacrifice, of altars, of every fire that had ever been fed. Give what lives in the blood. Give the ink of the self. We cannot have your name, little hollow thing, so give us the paper it should have been written on.

"There it is," Emrys breathed.

"Don't—" Bram started.

"It's the price. It's always a price." Emrys drew the belt-knife. Their hands were steady, which surprised them; ten years of fear had worn grooves deep enough that terror could flow along them smoothly, almost calmly, a river in a canyon of its own making. "Keep walking when I do it. Promise me."

She promised. Her voice cracked on it.

Emrys pressed their left palm flat against the gate. The heat bit immediately—not burning, worse than burning, a heat that seemed to reach *through* the skin and take inventory of what lay beneath. And then, holding the gate's gaze in whatever way a thing of stone and ember can be held, Emrys spoke.

Not their name. They had none. Instead they reached down into the deepest shaft of themselves, past the stolen memories and the purchased silences, past even the ache-shaped hole Vess had left, and grasped the one truth about themselves that no erasure had touched. The one fact that survived every fire they'd put it to.

"I am the one who owes nothing," they said. "That is my name. Nothing. Nobody. I am the gap in the record. I am what remains when a person is subtracted. Take that to your masters. Tell them the broker Null has come to trade."

Even as the words left their mouth, Emrys knew it was a gambit that should fail—that a true name could not be *declared*, that identity was not a garment you could simply claim. But the gate ran on old law, and old law had a clause older still: *a freely offered name binds as truly as a birth-given one.* Hedge-priests took new names at initiation. Brides took husbands' names. The dying sometimes gave their names away to cheat devils. A name was not what you were born with. A name was what you answered to, at the bottom of yourself, when nothing else remained.

And at the bottom of Emrys Galloway, after ten years of excavation, nothing else did remain.

They dragged the knife across their right palm.

The pain was white and total and strangely clean. Blood welled hot and immediate, and Emrys pressed the bleeding hand to the gate beside the first, and the rock *drank*. There was no other word for it. The blood sank into stone as if into sand, and where it went, the ember-veins flared blinding, racing outward from the point of contact in spreading branches—writing, Emrys realized dimly, *writing*, the gate inscribing their offered name into its own living surface in characters of fire and gore.

***NULL,*** the gate pronounced, and the word went through Emrys like a nail through a plank, fixing them to something. Fixing them to *nothing*, which was somehow worse. ***SO BE IT WRITTEN. SO BE IT OWED. ENTER, HOLLOW ONE. WHAT YOU CARRY, THE COURT WILL COUNT.***

The gate opened.

Not swung—*dissolved*, the stone going soft as tallow at the center and parting around a corridor of low red light and air that tasted of copper and candle smoke and something sweeter underneath, something rotten-ripe, like fruit left too long in a sealed room. Heat rolled out over them, carrying distant sounds: music played very slowly and slightly wrong, voices in conversation that never quite reached the level of words, and beneath it all, faint and ceaseless, a whispering like pages turning, like thousands upon thousands of pages turning.

Emrys swayed. Their palm throbbed, and when they looked down at the wound they saw the cut had already scarred—not healed, *scarred*, silver-white in the shape of the gate's strange script, a single character of the old alphabet etched into their flesh forever.

A brand. As predicted. But looking at it, feeling it pulse in time with their heartbeat, Emrys felt something colder than the expected dread settle into their bones.

Because the brand itched with meaning they almost understood. The character was not the symbol for *null*. It was not the sign for nothing, for nobody, for the gap in the record. Now that it was written in them, Emrys could feel what it actually said, the way you can feel a splinter's shape under skin.

It read: ***PAID IN FULL.***

As if the blood had settled a debt. As if somewhere, in some ledger older than the court, older than the gods, an account bearing Emrys's erased name had just been marked closed.

Vess had taken a memory as toll. The gate had taken blood. And both payments had landed in the same account—one Emrys hadn't known existed, in a name Emrys had spent a decade burying.

"Emrys." Bram's voice, very small. "Look."

Beyond the threshold, the corridor opened into vastness. Terraces of fire descended into the dark like inverted amphitheaters, and on every level, figures moved—tall, elegant, wrong, their skins the color of banked coals, their eyes like the moments before lightning strikes. The Ember Court, glittering and awake. And everywhere among the terraces, displayed on shelves of black glass, embers glowed in their hundreds and thousands. Souls. Heirlooms. Inventory.

Somewhere down there was Tam.

Somewhere down there, the Archivist waited with a name no living person should know.

Emrys flexed their branded hand, felt the scar-pulse beat in time with the turning pages of the dark, and stepped through the gate with Bram close behind—into the smell of candles and rot, into the light of the quiet fire, carrying nothing but a false name, a fresh scar, and the growing, vertiginous suspicion that they had not erased themselves ten years ago at all.

They had *paid*. And the court, it seemed, kept excellent records of what it was owed.
