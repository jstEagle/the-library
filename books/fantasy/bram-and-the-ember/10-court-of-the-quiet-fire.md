## 10. Court of the Quiet Fire

The gate had taken its toll and let them pass, and now Emrys Galloway walked into a court that should not have existed, lit by fire that gave no heat.

They had expected darkness. What they got was splendor.

The road beneath the mountain opened like a throat and then widened into a cavern so vast that its ceiling vanished into a haze of drifting sparks, thousands upon thousands of them, rising slow as snow in reverse. And the light—Emrys stopped on the threshold of the great gallery and made themselves look, because looking was the first discipline of survival. The light came from the walls. From shelves. From cases of black glass arranged in tiers that climbed out of sight, and behind every pane burned something small and orange and patient.

An ember. Each one an ember.

Bram stood close at their shoulder, her breath coming quick and shallow. "What are they?" she whispered, though Emrys could see by the way her fist closed around the mark on her palm that she already knew. Fourteen years old, plague-wintered, sharp as a flint chip—and she knew.

"Lives," Emrys said quietly. "Kept."

"Kept how?"

"Like heirlooms." They started walking, because standing still in this place felt like standing in a mouth. "Like silver you polish but never use. Look at the cases—the hinges are oiled. The glass is clean. Someone tends these. Someone *cares* for them." That was the part that turned Emrys's stomach, more than the embers themselves. Not cruelty. Cruelty would have been simpler. This was *curation*. Somewhere below, hands that might once have been kind dusted the shelves where souls sat burning in the dark, unspent, unfinished, forever.

The gallery floor was veined with channels of faintly glowing stone, rivers of ember-light running between the shelves like streets between houses. Emrys counted archways as they passed—seven visible, each carved with a sigil they half-remembered from shrines gone cold above: the coiled serpent of the deep roots, the antlered thing of the high passes, the weeping face over the wells. The old gods of Thornfell. The ones who had slept for a century while their people forgot them, and who had woken hungry.

And here was the pantry.

"You're doing the thing," Bram murmured.

"What thing?"

"The counting thing. Your eyes go side to side like a cat's." She hugged her arms around herself. The borrowed cloak Emrys had given her at the gate hung off her shoulders; she'd pulled the hood up, and beneath it her face was pale and set. "Are there guards? Should I be doing something?"

"There are no guards," Emrys said. "That's worse. Nothing here needs guarding. Everything that matters is already owned." They adjusted the satchel at their hip—the soul-broker's kit they'd assembled from Vess's instructions, from things bought and things stolen: a ledger bound in grey leather, a set of brass weights, a broker's seal pressed from wax Vess had sworn was genuine. "You're my apprentice. You speak only when spoken to, and then as little as possible. If anyone asks about your hand—" Bram's fingers twitched toward her palm, where the contract-mark had settled since they crossed the threshold, quiet as a banked coal— "you say it's a debt you're carrying to sell. Debts are currency here. No one will question it."

"And if someone asks your name?"

"No one will." Emrys said it flatly, and did not explain. There was nothing to explain. That was the whole architecture of their life: no one asked Emrys Galloway's name because Emrys Galloway had spent ten years making sure there was no name to ask for. In Cinderhollow they were the salt-trader with the forgettable face. On the caravan roads they were the guide whose employers could never quite describe them afterward. Even Vess, blind Vess, who had taken a memory as payment and chosen one Emrys hadn't known they possessed—even Vess had called them only *the storm-caller*, and even that title had sounded like a guess.

Here, in a court built on memory, Emrys was the most invisible thing in it.

They walked deeper.

* * *

The vaults were organized, and that was the horror of it.

Not chaos—not a dragon's hoard of snatched souls heaped in glittering disorder—but order, taxonomy, care. Emrys moved through the galleries posing as what they claimed to be, a broker taking inventory before a sale, and the court let them, because the court believed in commerce the way other powers believed in prayer. A steward met them at the third gallery: a tall figure whose face was smooth as riverstone, features suggested rather than fixed, wearing robes the color of cooling ash. It had looked at Bram for three long seconds—Emrys had felt the girl stop breathing beside them—and then looked away, satisfied, as if a debt walking freely through the vaults was simply another item properly shelved.

"Inventory for the solstice reassessment," Emrys told it, in the bored drawl they'd practiced against their own reflection in still water. They opened the grey ledger to a page of columns Vess had taught them to fake. "I'll need access to the mid-tier collections. My client is particular."

"The mid-tier opens to brokers of standing," the steward said. Its voice was like wind through a chimney. "Your seal is old."

"My client is older."

Something that was almost amusement moved across the smooth face. The steward gestured, and a case of black glass swung open at Emrys's touch.

Inside, on a cushion of woven silver, lay an ember the size of a robin's egg. It pulsed. Slowly. Like a heart that had learned patience.

Emrys lifted it with both hands, feeling warmth seep through their gloves, and held it up to the ember-light, the way a jeweler holds a stone. And the ember *responded*. It brightened. It leaned toward them, if light could lean, and somewhere inside all that small burning there was a pressure, a reaching, a—

A person. A whole person, folded down to the size of a coal. Someone who had laughed, and owed money, and been afraid of the dark, and loved somebody badly or well. Someone whose mother had set a place for them at the table.

Emrys set it back very carefully and closed the case.

"You're shaking," Bram whispered.

"I'm angry. It looks similar." They moved to the next case. And the next. Because anger was useful, and inventory was the mission, and if they stopped moving they would have to stand in this light and feel what they were feeling.

The ledgers were worse than the shelves. Each case bore a placard, and each placard a name, and each name a date, and some of the dates went back two hundred years. Generations. Whole family lines filed under the sigils of the gods who had taken them—farmers who'd prayed for rain during drought years, mothers who'd begged the well-god to spare a feverish child, soldiers who'd promised anything, anything, for one more day of life. The Ember Court had never stolen a single soul. That was the thing Emrys understood as they walked the galleries, copying sigils and shelf-marks into the grey ledger with a hand gone steady and cold. Every ember here had been *sold*. Freely. Desperately. By people at the end of their rope, to gods too starved to refuse the payment.

The old gods hadn't woken cruel. They'd woken *hungry*, and hunger made everything honest.

"Emrys." Bram's voice, very small. "This one has a date from last winter."

They crossed to her. Her lantern-light—it was the only flame in the court that wasn't a soul, and Emrys had insisted she carry it—fell across a case near the gallery's inner wall. The placard read: *TAMMAS COLE. Taken in trade, the winter of the grey sickness. Condition: dormant. Provenance: offered by kin.*

Tam. Tam's ember, sitting in a case like a brooch, like a keepsake, like something inherited rather than inhabited. Small. Cold-burning. Still faintly, stubbornly orange.

Bram didn't touch the glass. Emrys watched her hands hover near it and then pull back, watched her jaw work, watched fourteen years of guilt rearrange itself into something harder. She looked at the ember the way you look at a wound you gave yourself.

"That's him," she said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"He's so *small*."

"Bargains shrink things," Emrys said. "That's how they're enforced. You gave a piece of him; the court kept the rest." They touched her elbow, gently, guiding her back from the case. "We don't take it. Not yet. The moment his ember leaves its shelf without provenance papers, every bell in this mountain rings. We came to map the vaults, remember? To find where they keep the vessel-rite records. We do this right or we do it never."

She nodded. But her eyes stayed on the glass a moment longer, and Emrys saw her lips move—*I'm coming back for you*—and thought, with a pang they didn't permit themselves to examine: *so was I, once. Something. Someone.*

The thought arrived uninvited and left the same way, like a fish surfacing in dark water and vanishing before you could name it.

* * *

It happened again in the fifth gallery.

Emrys was cataloguing—a row of embers from the high passes, antlered-sigil stock, mostly shepherds by the placards—when their hand paused over a case and refused to move forward. Not hesitation. Refusal. Their body simply stopped, the way a compass needle stops when it finds north.

The ember inside was larger than most. Old, by the patina of its silver cradle. Its light was different too—not the steady banked glow of the others but a flicker, restless, almost storm-colored at its edges, blue threading the orange like lightning caught in amber.

The placard was worn nearly blank. Emrys bent close and read what remained:

*Taken in trade, the year of the [illegible]. Condition: [illegible]. Provenance: offered by [illegible].*

And beneath, in a different hand, older, sharper, almost gouged into the metal:

*THEY CAME VOLUNTARILY.*

Emrys stood there a long time.

Around them the court went on glittering—thousands of lives burning quietly in their cases, stewards gliding between the shelves with their oil and their cloths, the whole vast machine of keeping humming along beneath the mountain. Bram was three cases down, reading placards with fierce concentration, cataloguing in her own head the way Emrys had taught her. The ember-light ran in its channels along the floor. Everything was beautiful. Everything was terrible.

And Emrys Galloway, who had erased themselves from every record and memory they could reach, who had no name to give gates or gods or strangers, who had built a decade of safety out of being forgotten—Emrys looked at that storm-colored ember and felt something turn over in their chest that had no name either.

Recognition.

Not of the ember. Of the *shelf*. Of the act of being put away, folded down, kept where no one would reach for you. Of choosing it. *They came voluntarily.* Whoever this had been had walked into this court and handed themselves over, the way Emrys had walked out of their own life and handed it to the dark. Two different vaults. Same currency. Same terrible arithmetic: *if no one remembers me, no one can ask me to burn.*

Their hand rose toward the glass.

Stopped.

Because underneath the recognition, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, something else stirred—an answering flicker in the ember itself, blue threading brighter, reaching toward their glove the way the robin's-egg soul had reached, except this time the reaching felt less like a stranger's hope and more like—

Like being *known*.

"Broker." The steward's voice, behind them, wind-in-a-chimney. "That collection is closed to reassessment."

Emrys withdrew their hand. Turned. Bowed the shallow bow of a merchant corrected. "My error. The lighting misleads."

"The lighting is honest." The smooth face regarded them, and for a moment Emrys felt seen in a way they had paid ten years to make impossible—felt the steward's attention move over them like fingers checking a lock. Then it passed. "The solstice approaches. Complete your inventory by tomorrow's dark, or your seal will be reviewed."

"Of course."

The steward drifted away. Emrys exhaled slowly and found Bram watching them from down the row, her young face tight with a question she was too disciplined to ask aloud in the open.

Later, in the shadow between two galleries, she asked it anyway. "That case. The one that stopped you. What was it?"

"I don't know."

"You knew it. I saw your face."

"You saw a broker making an error," Emrys said, and heard how thin it sounded even to their own ears. They crouched, pretending to check the ledger's bindings, buying a moment. "Bram. There's something wrong with me that I've been ignoring since the gate. This place runs on memory. Memory is its coin, its light, its law. And I walked in here with ten years of myself burned away—and nothing in this entire court has looked twice at me. Not the gate. Not the stewards. Nothing that feeds on remembering can see me at all." They looked up at her. "Do you understand what that means?"

She frowned. "That you're safe here?"

"That I'm *familiar* here." The words came out before Emrys could weigh them, and once out, they rang true in a way that frightened them. "Erasure leaves a shape, Bram. A hole has edges. Everything in this place is organized, catalogued, kept—and somewhere in these vaults there may be a record of what filled my shape before I emptied it. The Archivist keeps the deep records. Vess said so. Tomorrow, while you're—"

While you're what? They hadn't finished deciding. The plan was half-built: reach the Archivist, learn the vessel-rite's terms, find the flaw. But standing in the ember-light with a storm-colored soul flickering behind them like a hand raised in greeting, Emrys felt the plan tilt toward something else entirely. Toward the shelves. Toward the worn placard. Toward whatever waited in the deep records under their own absence.

"Tomorrow," they said instead, "we find the Archivist."

Above them, impossibly far up through the mountain's bones, the first tremor of the solstice made itself known: a sound like a great door being unbarred, deep and slow, and every ember in every case along the galleries brightened at once—as if the whole court had drawn breath.

And in the closed case behind them, unnoticed by either of them now, the storm-colored ember burned a little bluer, and a little closer to the edge of its silver cradle, as if it had begun, after all these years, to wait for someone specific.
