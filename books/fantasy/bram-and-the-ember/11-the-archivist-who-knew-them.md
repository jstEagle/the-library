## 11. The Archivist Who Knew Them

The vaults of the Ember Court did not smell of smoke.

That was the first wrongness Emrys noted, and they clung to it the way a drowning sailor clings to driftwood—because cataloguing wrongness was what kept a liar alive in a place like this. A court built on fire should have reeked of char and tallow, of scorched hair and hot iron. Instead the air tasted of cold candlewax and old paper, of dust that had never been disturbed, of something faintly sweet like fruit left too long in a dark room. The vaults smelled, Emrys realized with a slow curl of unease, like a library. Like preservation. Like things being *kept*.

They walked the long gallery with a broker's ledger under their arm—a prop Vess had pressed into their hands along with the map, its pages filled with invented valuations in a hand so beautiful it could only be false—and let their eyes do what their hands could not: count.

Shelves rose on either side, floor to ceiling, carved from stone the color of cooling coals. And upon the shelves, in niches lined with grey felt gone soft with age, sat the embers.

Souls. Hundreds of them. Thousands.

Each one no larger than a robin's egg, each one burning with a light that had nothing to do with heat. Some glowed steady as hearth-coals. Some guttered, dimming and flaring in rhythms Emrys recognized with a lurch of the stomach—the rhythm of sleep, of dreaming, of a mind turning over its own small histories somewhere inside the flame. Little brass plaques were set beneath each niche, engraved in a script that predated any alphabet Emrys knew, though they found, disturbingly, that they could almost read it. Almost. The way one can almost remember a language spoken in childhood.

*Heirlooms*, the broker's guide had called them, when Emrys had paid for the tour in forged coin and a stolen accent. *Family holdings.* As if a soul were a silver tea service, passed down through generations of gods who had outlived the people the souls had once belonged to.

Emrys paused before a niche where an ember burned low and blue, and made themselves look at it properly. Made themselves see it not as inventory but as what it was. Someone's grandmother, perhaps. Someone's child. A life reduced to a coal in a drawer because some god had loved it—or owned it—or simply refused to throw away what it had been given.

*You are here for Tam,* they reminded themselves. *One ember among thousands. Find him, find the terms of his debt, and get Bram out from under it.*

But even as they thought it, their gaze snagged on the plaque beneath the blue ember, and the almost-readable script resolved for half a heartbeat into words they understood:

*KEPT IN LOVING MEMORY OF WHAT WAS FREELY GIVEN.*

Freely given. Emrys turned the phrase over and found it rotten at the center. Nothing down here was free. Everything was bought, bargained, or taken—and the taking was always dressed up in the language of gift.

"You're new."

The voice came from behind them, and Emrys did not startle, because startling was a luxury they had trained out of themselves a decade ago. But every muscle under their borrowed coat went quietly, professionally rigid.

They turned.

The figure standing in the gallery's central aisle was small—smaller than Bram, perhaps, though it was hard to judge, because the Archivist seemed to occupy space the way a rumor occupies a town: everywhere at once and nowhere you could point to. They wore robes the grey of long-settled ash, and their face was pleasant and unremarkable and utterly forgettable, which Emrys recognized instantly as a professional courtesy between colleagues. Their eyes, though. Their eyes were the orange of banked fire, and they did not blink often enough.

"I'm conducting an appraisal," Emrys said, and let the broker's drawl settle over their tongue like a familiar coat. "On behalf of a client with significant holdings. I wasn't aware the vaults required escort."

"The vaults don't." The Archivist smiled. It was a kind smile. That was somehow worse. "I do. I greet all serious collectors personally. It saves time later, when they come back wanting more than they came for. They always want more than they came for." The orange eyes traveled over Emrys—coat, ledger, face—with the unhurried attention of someone reading a very old letter. "And you, I think, will want more than anything."

"Then your greeting has wasted both our time. I'm here for a single acquisition. Small piece. Family matter."

"Mm," said the Archivist. "Yes. The boy. Tam of Cinderhollow. Cold little coal, third gallery, shelf nine, niche forty-one. His sister's mark is already warming; you've noticed, of course. You notice everything. You always did."

The ledger nearly slipped from Emrys's fingers.

They recovered it—smoothly, they hoped—but the Archivist's smile deepened by a single degree, and Emrys understood that the recovery had been seen, catalogued, and filed.

"You have me confused with someone else," Emrys said.

"No," said the Archivist, gently, terribly. "I really don't."

* * *

They walked together down the gallery, because refusing would have been worse. The Archivist moved without seeming to take steps, and the embers brightened faintly as they passed, the way dogs lift their heads when a familiar footstep sounds on the stair. Emrys watched this happen and felt the dread in their chest change shape—from the fear of being caught lying to something older and less nameable. Recognition, radiating outward from the shelves. The court itself knew them. Not the broker. Not the stranger with the forged ledger.

Something underneath.

"You erased yourself well," the Archivist said conversationally, as though discussing weather. "Better than most. Better than anyone, actually. Most people who burn their names miss a corner somewhere—a birthmark in a story, a scar in a song. But you were thorough. You had practice being thorough." They glanced sideways, fire-eyed. "Ten years, was it? Eleven? Time moves strangely when no one is holding your shape for you."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Of course you don't. That was rather the point." The Archivist stopped before a door Emrys had not noticed, though they had passed this stretch of gallery twice already. It was black stone, unmarked, handleless—and yet the Archivist laid one grey sleeve against it and it swung inward on a breath of air that smelled, again, of that terrible sweetness. Old paper. Kept things. "Come in, storm-caller."

The word hit Emrys like a hand flat against the sternum.

Not *Galloway*. Not the name they'd worn like a borrowed coat through ten years of salt roads and forgotten passes. *Storm-caller.* A title. A function. Something a person was *called* because it was what they *did*—and the fact that the word existed in the Archivist's mouth, whole and undamaged, meant that somewhere in this court of preserved things, the person who had done that calling was preserved too.

"I told you," Emrys said, and heard their own voice go thin, "you have me confused—"

"With someone else. Yes. You said." The Archivist gestured them through the door. "Indulge me. Appraisers verify provenance, don't they? Consider this a verification."

* * *

The room beyond the black door was round and low-ceilinged and lit by no lamp Emrys could find, and yet every surface was perfectly visible. There were no shelves here. No embers. Instead, the walls were lined with drawers—thousands of them, shallow and narrow, each fitted with a small card-catalogue frame—and in the center of the room stood a reading table with two chairs, as if the Archivist had been expecting company for a very long time.

"This is the personal collection," the Archivist said, closing the door behind them with a click that sounded final as a coffin lid. "Memories surrendered voluntarily. The rarest category. Souls we keep because they were owed to us. Memories we keep because someone *paid* us to hold them." They moved to the wall and trailed their sleeve along the drawer frames, not searching—strolling. A host showing off a wine cellar. "Do you know how seldom people choose forgetting, rather than having it chosen for them? Grief does it. Guilt does it, sometimes. But deliberate, systematic, line-by-line erasure—" they tapped a drawer, once, affectionately, "—that takes a very particular kind of person. Someone disciplined. Someone frightened. Someone with something enormous to bury."

"I'm leaving," Emrys said.

"There's a drawer in this room," the Archivist continued, as if they hadn't spoken, "with your handwriting on its label."

Every sound in the world stopped. Or perhaps only every sound in Emrys's body. Their heartbeat, their breath, the whisper of blood in their ears—all of it suspended, the way the air goes still before lightning.

"That's impossible," they said. "There is no label. There is no drawer. I didn't leave anything. I *burned*—"

"Burning leaves ash," said the Archivist. "Ash keeps. You paid us in fire, and fire is honest—it destroys nothing, it only changes what a thing is made of. Your name went up in smoke, yes. But smoke rises, and everything that rises comes down somewhere, and down is where we live." They turned from the wall at last, and their forgettable face wore an expression of such gentle sympathy that Emrys wanted to strike it. "We never threw anything away, storm-caller. We are archivists. Throwing away is the one sin we don't forgive."

Emrys's mouth was dry. "What's in the drawer?"

"Ah." The Archivist's smile returned, and now there was a merchant's gleam beneath the kindness, and Emrys understood—finally, fully—that they had been walking toward this moment since the moment they'd entered the court. Perhaps since the moment they'd been born. "Now we arrive at price."

"I haven't agreed to buy anything."

"No. But you're going to ask what's in the drawer anyway." The Archivist waited. The silence stretched, elastic, patient, and Emrys hated them for it, and hated themselves more, because the Archivist was right.

"What," Emrys said, "is in the drawer."

* * *

The Archivist drew it out with two fingers, reverent as a priest lifting a relic.

It was shallower than the others, and longer—a tray rather than a drawer—and inside, on a bed of grey felt, lay objects that should not have fit inside any drawer at all. A strip of storm-light, coiled like a ribbon, still flickering with the green-white pulse of a sky about to break. A handful of rain that fell upward, droplet by droplet, back into a small cloud that hovered an inch above the felt and drizzled eternally onto nothing. A scrap of cloth, scorched at the edges, embroidered with a name in thread gone brown with age.

And a memory. Just one, visible the way the court rendered such things: a small sphere of amber light, warm as a held hand, turning slowly on its bed of felt. Inside it, shadows moved. Figures. A village, maybe. Firelight. Screaming, though no sound escaped the glass-bright surface—only the shapes of mouths opening, over and over, silent as fish.

"Your collection," the Archivist said softly. "Catalogued the night you paid. Every year of yourself you surrendered, bound and shelved. The name your mother gave you—we kept the sound of it, in case you ever wanted it back. The face you wore before you learned to be forgettable. The teacher who taught you to call the storms, and what she made you promise. The god who—" the Archivist paused, delicately, "—who gave you your orders."

"I don't take orders," Emrys said. The words came out automatic, reflexive, and hollow as a drum.

"No," the Archivist agreed. "Not anymore. That's rather the tragedy, isn't it? You were the most obedient instrument the old gods ever held, and then one night you decided to stop—and instead of running, or fighting, or dying, you did the one thing none of them expected. You made yourself unownable. You deleted the debtor to escape the debt." They set the tray on the reading table, between the two chairs, and pushed the second chair out with one grey sleeve. An invitation. "Very clever. Very thorough. And now here you stand, ten years free, and you cannot walk past this table without looking. Because that's the flaw in your cleverness, storm-caller, the crack running through all that beautiful discipline: you erased the memories, but you couldn't erase the *wanting to know*. Wanting isn't stored in the head. It's stored lower than that. In the marrow. In the scar tissue. In whatever's left when a person has been scraped clean."

Emrys looked at the amber sphere. The silent screaming figures. The village-shaped shadows.

They knew—knew with the certainty of the marrow the Archivist had spoken of—that this was the memory. The one. The night. Whatever had happened in whatever village those shadows belonged to, it was the hinge their whole exile had swung upon, the load-bearing horror their decade of invisibility had been built to conceal. If they took it back, they would know why they ran. And they would become, in the same instant, someone who could never run again.

If they left it, they would walk out of this court still hollow, still safe, still a stranger wearing their own skin—and Bram would pay the price of their ignorance at the solstice, because the court's terms, the vessel-rite, the mark on her palm—all of it would remain a locked room to them, and locked rooms favored the house.

"How much," Emrys said.

The Archivist tilted their forgettable head. "For the lot? More than you have. For one?" They considered, and their fire-orange eyes did something almost tender. "For one, I'll take something small. A trifle. The memory of this conversation, say—so that when you leave my collection, you'll leave it as you entered it: certain you were never here, certain you are no one, certain there is no drawer with your handwriting on it." They smiled. "Or—"

"Or?"

"Or you may pay in the usual currency of this court. Worship. Attention. Acknowledgment." The Archivist leaned across the table, and the amber sphere between them pulsed once, like a heart remembering its work. "Say your name, storm-caller. Your real one. Say it aloud, in this room, to me—and the memory is yours. Free. A gift." Their voice dropped to a whisper that filled the whole round chamber anyway. "We've missed hearing it. We've missed it *so much*."

The trap was perfect, Emrys thought distantly. Beautifully, horribly perfect. The name was the key to everything they'd built—the name spoken aloud would unravel ten years of erasure in a single syllable, would make them visible to every listening thing in the mountain, would call the old gods' attention down upon them like a struck bell. And the Archivist knew it. Was offering it. Was *hungry* for it.

Which meant the name mattered more than the memory. Which meant the memory was worth having, if only to learn why.

Emrys pulled out the second chair and sat.

"Not tonight," they said, and their voice was steadier than they had any right to expect. "But I'll take the memory. Name your real price—and know that if it's the girl, or the boy, or anything touching Bram of Cinderhollow, I walk out of this room and burn this court's map on my way up."

The Archivist studied them for a long moment. Somewhere in the walls, in the thousands of drawers, a faint rustling stirred—like pages turning, like sleepers shifting, like a vast attention leaning closer.

"Oh, storm-caller," the Archivist said at last, and there was something in their voice that Emrys would spend a long time afterward trying to name, and would finally identify, much too late, as pity. "You misunderstand the transaction entirely. I'm not selling you the memory." One grey finger came to rest, feather-light, on the amber sphere. "I'm selling you the *choice*. The memory is already yours—it has been yours since the night you brought it here, weeping, and begged us to take it away. All I sell is the door back into it." The fire-orange eyes lifted. "So. Shall I open it? Or would you like to stand outside yourself a while longer, wondering what's behind the glass?"

The sphere turned on its felt bed. Inside it, the silent figures opened their mouths.

Emrys reached out—and touched the light.
