## 13. Bram Among the Heirlooms

The Archivist's gallery swallowed Emrys whole.

One moment Bram was standing at their shoulder, watching the tall figure in ash-grey robes bow over a ledger of names; the next, a curtain of woven smoke had drifted between them, and when it thinned, Emrys was gone. Not walked away. *Gone*, the way a candle is gone when someone pinches the wick.

"Emrys?" she whispered.

The gallery answered with silence—the particular kind of silence that has furniture in it, and listeners.

She stood very still, the way Emrys had taught her on the deep roads. *When you lose your footing, don't run. Feel for the ground.* But there was no ground here to feel for. The floor beneath her slippers was warm and slightly yielding, like skin that had been sitting too near a fire, and the walls rose up on every side into a dark that wasn't empty but *full*—full of small lights, thousands upon thousands of them, drifting at the height of held breath.

Embers. The court kept its embers here.

Bram had known, in the abstract way you know a thing before it has teeth. Emrys had told her: *the vaults are where they store what they've collected. Souls, mostly. Bought, bargained, won at dice from things that gamble.* Knowing it and standing inside it were different animals entirely. Standing inside it felt like standing inside a church where every prayer ever whispered had been hung up on hooks to dry.

The nearest ember hovered an arm's length away, no bigger than a hazelnut, burning a slow gold that never flickered. As she watched, it pulsed—once, twice—and something moved behind her eyes that wasn't hers. A smell of cut hay. A woman's laugh, low and startled. A summer that belonged to somebody else.

She stepped back. The sensation let go of her like a hand opening.

*Don't touch anything,* Emrys had said, back when saying such things still seemed useful. *In this place, touching is taking, and taking is telling.*

But Emrys was gone, and the smoke-curtain had closed behind her too, sealing off the way they'd come, and the only direction left was deeper, along a corridor lined with shelves that receded farther than any cellar under Cinderhollow had any right to be. So Bram did what she had gotten good at doing since the plague winter: she stopped waiting for the world to make sense and started moving through it anyway.

* * *

The vaults were organized by nothing she could understand at first, and then by everything.

Not by name—the court didn't seem to care much for names, or perhaps names were too cheap to bother shelving. Not by date, not by place. She passed alcoves grouped by *longing*: embers that burned amber and leaned, all of them, toward some invisible south. An alcove of *firsts*—first words, first snowfalls, first times standing up after falling down—these burned white and small and fierce as struck flint. One whole gallery was given over to grief, and those embers were the strangest: they didn't burn at all, exactly. They smoldered, patient as peat, and the light they gave off made her want to sit down on the warm floor and weep for people she had never met.

She kept her hands clasped tight behind her back, fingers laced so hard they ached.

Somewhere in this mountain was a fourteen-year-old boy who had stopped dreaming.

Tam. Her brother. The word still worked on her like a splinter working toward the heart. Tam, who used to sleep with his mouth open and wake up narrating—*I dreamed I could breathe underwater, Bram, I dreamed the sheep could talk but only insults*—Tam, who after the night at the well had gone quiet in a way that no fever explained. He ate. He walked. He sat at the table while their mother piled food on his plate and told him about her day, and he looked at her with eyes like windows painted shut, and sometimes his mouth moved and a voice came out that was almost his voice but pitched wrong, borrowed, the way a coat fits differently when it belongs to someone else.

*I gave him,* she thought, walking the endless warm corridors. *I stood at the well in the dark with my hands bleeding from the rope and I said take him instead of us, take him instead of Mam, and the voice in the water said done, child, done, so gently, like it was doing me a kindness.*

It had been a kindness. That was the rot at the center of it. The plague had passed their house over. Their mother lived. Bram lived. And Tam—

Tam was on a shelf somewhere, the size of a hazelnut.

Her eyes stung and she blinked hard and kept walking, because crying in a place like this felt like leaving coins on a table.

* * *

She found the children's wing by accident, following a sound.

It was faint—a rhythm, soft and irregular, like rain on a far-off roof—and it pulled at her the way her mother's humming used to pull her home through the dusk. The corridor narrowed, then opened into a round chamber with a ceiling lost in dark, and there she understood what she'd been hearing.

Laughter. Dozens of embers, hundreds, each one pulsing with captured laughter, and the sounds overlapping into that soft rain-roof rhythm. A nursery of moments. Here an ember that glowed the exact pink of a newborn's fist; there one that flickered with the green-gold of a first loose tooth. Little brass plaques beneath each shelf, engraved in letters that seemed to shift if she looked straight at them, refusing to be read—or perhaps refusing to be read by *her*.

She walked the rows slowly. She didn't know what she was looking for. That was a lie, and she knew it was a lie even as she let herself keep thinking it. She knew exactly what she was looking for. She simply didn't believe it would work, because believing it might work meant believing the rest—that Tam was here, that Tam was *this*, that the thing eating supper at their table was a coat with nobody in it.

And then she heard him.

Not laughter. Something smaller. A hum, tuneless, wandering—the sound Tam made when he was concentrating, mending a net or whittling, the sound their mother called his bee-song. It came from a low shelf near the chamber's far wall, apart from the others, in an alcove that was colder than the rest of the room. Colder, and darker, and the single ember resting there burned so faintly it was less a flame than a memory of one.

Grey. Grey as winter ash, grey as the well-water at midnight. And around its base, wrapped tight as a bandage, a thread of warmth that was *hers*—she knew it the way you know your own name in someone else's mouth. When she'd spoken her bargain into the well, something of her had gone down the rope with the words. A knot of her voice. A loop of her guilt. It had come back tied around her brother's soul, and it was the only reason the ember still had any color in it at all besides grey.

"Bram," she said out loud, just to hear a real voice. "That's him. That's Tam."

The ember pulsed. Once. Twice. The bee-song stuttered, caught, and—for three heartbeats, four—it resolved into something almost melodic, almost *awake*, and she felt it reach for her across the space between shelf and girl the way a drowning man reaches for a rope thrown years too late.

Her hands came unclasped from behind her back without her deciding anything.

*Touching is taking, and taking is telling.*

Let it tell, then. Let the whole mountain hear.

She lifted Tam's ember off the shelf with both hands, the way you lift a bird with a broken wing, the way you lift bread out of a shared loaf because everyone's hungry and there isn't enough. It weighed nothing. It weighed everything. The thread of her own warmth wound around her fingers and tightened, gentle as a cat's tail, and the cold of the grey part sank into her palms and she didn't care, she would carry all of it, she would carry the cold part forever if the warm part got to keep living.

"I'm sorry," she whispered. "I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I'm sorry." Three sorries, one for each year he'd sat silent at the table. It wasn't enough. Nothing would be enough. But sorry aimed at *fixing* instead of sorry aimed at hiding—that was new, and it filled her chest like a lungful of cold clean air after a winter spent breathing the sickroom.

She tucked the ember against her sternum, inside her shirt, where it lay against her skin like a second heartbeat. Small. Cold. Still faintly hers.

Then the alarms began.

* * *

They didn't sound like bells. They sounded like *names*.

Everywhere at once, from the walls themselves, from the warm yielding floor, from the drifting smoke-curtains, voices began speaking—not shouting, nothing so honest as shouting—reciting. Names of the stolen. Names of the sold. Thousands of them, layered over each other, a tide of ownership rolling through the vaults, and Bram understood with animal clarity what the recitation was *for*. The court was counting. The court had noticed a subtraction. Somewhere in that vast arithmetic, a gap had opened where a grey ember used to be, and now every voice in the mountain was reading through its ledger trying to find which entry had grown legs.

Which entry had grown a *girl*.

She ran.

The round chamber spat her into corridors she didn't remember crossing, and the walls were changing as she fled—shelves swinging aside like doors, galleries rearranging themselves, the whole vault flexing around her the way a body flexes around a swallowed stone. The ember against her chest had gone from cold to cool to a faint stubborn warmth, and with every pulse she felt Tam's bee-song buzz against her ribs, frightened now, or maybe hopeful, and she held it tighter and ran faster.

A wall of smoke descended ahead of her. She put her shoulder down and went through it and came out gasping in a gallery of grief, all those patient smoldering embers leaning toward her as she passed, drawn to her panic like moths. Behind her, the reciting voices had stopped listing names.

Now they were repeating one.

*"Bram of Cinderhollow. Bram of Cinderhollow. Bram of Cinderhollow."*

Her own name, in her mother's voice, in Tam's borrowed voice, in the well-voice, in a dozen voices she didn't know, sweet and reasonable and terribly patient, the way the well had been patient. *Done, child, done.* The court wasn't angry. That was the worst of it. The court sounded like a shopkeeper who has caught a child with her hand in the till and intends, kindly, to discuss it.

She burst through another smoke-curtain and nearly fell over Emrys.

They stood in a junction of five corridors, ash-grey coat torn at one sleeve, face lit from below by the glow of a hundred embers, and their expression when they saw her was not surprise. It was something older and wearier—the look of someone watching a prophecy keep its appointment.

"You took it," Emrys said.

"He's mine," Bram said. Her voice cracked down the middle and she hated it and kept talking anyway. "He's my brother. I paid for him wrong and I'm paying for him right, and if the court wants to discuss it, the court can discuss it with my boot."

For one heartbeat Emrys stared at her. Then—there was no other word for it—they *grinned*, quick and fierce and gone again in an instant, like lightning in a bottle. "Good," they said. "Terrible. Perfect. Both. Listen—" They caught her arm as the voices swelled again, closer now, coming down two of the five corridors at once. "It knows what you carry. It will offer you trades. It will offer you *anything*. Your mother's health. Your own future. It will sound fair. It will sound kind."

"And if I say yes?"

"Then you'll be standing at a well for the rest of your life, making bargains." Emrys turned, coat swirling, already choosing a corridor—the one that sloped downward, toward heat, toward the glittering heart of the court. "So don't. Run."

The voices found them together then, pouring into the junction from both sides, and the last thing Bram heard clearly, pressed against Emrys's back as they plunged into the smoke, was her own name spoken in her *own* voice—which she had never given this place, which it should not have had—asking her, gently, reasonably, whether she was quite sure she wouldn't rather put it back.

She ran faster.

Behind them, deep in the vaults, every ember in the children's wing began to burn at once, and the solstice mark on her palm woke like an eye opening, and the mountain began, softly, to sing.
