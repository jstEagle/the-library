## 14. The Hunt Beneath the Mountain

The alarm was not a sound. That was the worst of it, Emrys thought—years of imagining what a stolen soul's theft might sound like, and the court answered with something worse than bells or horns. It was attention. A vast, slow turning of regard, like an eye the size of a cathedral rotating in its socket beneath the mountain, and every gallery of names lighting at once as it swept toward them.

"Run," Emrys said, and did not wait to see if Bram obeyed.

They ran.

The vault corridor stretched ahead in both directions, and Emrys chose by instinct—the smuggler's grammar of the deep roads, the language of load-bearing dark and air that moved where it shouldn't. Left, where the shelving leaned away from the wall. Down three steps that had no business existing in a place of polished galleries, into a service passage where the ceiling dropped low enough that they had to run bent double, Bram's breath rasping hot against their shoulder.

Behind them, the light came.

It poured along the corridors like spilled honey, amber and slow, and where it touched, the names began to burn. Not the quiet ember-glow of heirloomed souls in their niches—this was open flame, crawling along inscriptions, licking up the shelves, and each burning name made a noise. A syllable. Half a word. The first note of a life interrupted, repeated over and over as the fire ate it.

Emrys had spent ten years erasing names. They knew what a name weighed. They had never heard so many of them screaming at once.

"Bram." They caught the girl's arm at a junction, pressed her back against cold stone. "Your hand."

She held it up without being asked. She had wrapped it in the strip of linen torn from her sleeve hours ago, back when the mark had merely glowed through her skin like a coal held under cloth. Now smoke threaded between her fingers. The linen had scorched through in two places, and beneath it Emrys could see the contract-signature pulsing—not red, not orange, but the white-hot color of iron just pulled from a forge.

"It doesn't hurt," Bram said, which was a lie, and they both knew it, because her jaw was set so hard he could see the muscle jumping. "It just—it wants something. It pulls. Like a fish on a line."

"That's the rite finding you." Emrys tore a strip from their own cloak, doused it from the waterskin, and bound it over her palm. The hiss of steam was obscene in the running silence. "The solstice is close. Hours, maybe less. When it crests, the pull becomes a door, and you become the doorway."

"Then we don't stop." Bram's eyes were too bright, fever-bright, but steady. She was fourteen and she had survived a plague winter and she had traded her brother's soul and she had walked into hell to steal it back, and none of those things had broken her yet. "Which way?"

Emrys listened. Above them—far above, a thousand feet of mountain—the alarm-attention wheeled and settled, and beneath it, faint as a memory of weather, there was air. Moving air. Drafts meant shafts, and shafts meant the surface road.

"That way." They pointed down the left-hand passage, into the dark. "And Bram—when I say hide, you get small and you stay small. Whatever you hear, whatever calls your name in my voice—you stay hidden until my hand finds you. Understood?"

She nodded once. Then, quieter: "You'll come for me."

It wasn't a question, but it wasn't quite trust either. It was the voice of a girl who had learned that promises cost, who wanted to believe one anyway.

"I have come this far for a stranger's mistake," Emrys said. "I won't lose you to furniture."

Something almost like a laugh cracked out of her, sharp and startled, and then they were running again.

* * *

Bram counted her footsteps because it was the only thing that kept the panic from eating her whole.

Two hundred and eleven since the last junction. Two hundred and twelve. The passage here was narrow as a chimney, and the walls were different—not the smooth gallery-stone of the vaults above but raw mountain, veined with something that glowed faintly red, like the mountain itself had a banked fire in its heart. Which, she supposed, it did.

Emrys went ahead of her, moving the way water moves, never hesitating, one hand trailing the wall as though reading it. Bram had watched them for days now, through the deep roads, through the tollgate where Emrys had bled rather than give a name, and she still couldn't decide what frightened her more: how much she trusted them, or how little she actually knew.

"You're limping," she said, because if she didn't fill the silence the burning names would. Their echo still came down the shaft behind them, thin and terrible, half-syllables repeating.

"A stone in my boot."

"Liar."

A pause ahead of her. In the dim glow of the vein-light she saw Emrys glance back, one eyebrow raised, and for a moment the mask of the unshakeable guide slipped and showed her something underneath—exhaustion, maybe, or amusement.

"The floor back there took a bite out of my heel," Emrys admitted. "The court doesn't like thieves. It rearranges itself."

"Can it do that? Rearrange the whole mountain?"

"It's awake now. Awake things move." Emrys turned forward again. "Keep counting your steps. If we get separated, count backward from wherever you stopped, and you can find the junction again."

"Why backward?"

"Because the court counts forward. Everything in it runs toward the solstice. If you move against its grain—" Emrys shrugged. "Sometimes the world forgets to look at what's going the wrong way."

*I would know about that,* Bram thought, and almost laughed, and didn't.

Her hand throbbed inside the wet bandage. The pulling sensation had gotten stronger since they stole Tam's ember—it sat against her breastbone now, sewn into a pocket of her coat, small and cold and *hers*, and every step toward the surface made it shiver like a struck bell. Every step also made the mark burn hotter. She understood the arithmetic without wanting to: the closer Tam's soul came to freedom, the tighter the court gripped its collateral. Her body was the ledger, and the ledger was coming due.

"Emrys." She said it low. "If it comes down to it—if the door opens and there isn't time—"

"Don't."

"—you take the ember and go. You don't stop for me. You bring him back to my mother and you tell him—" Her voice caught, and she hated it, and pushed through anyway. "Tell him I paid for him fair. Tell him I didn't run."

Emrys stopped. Turned. In the vein-light their face was all shadows and one lit edge, and their eyes were very old and very tired.

"When I was young," they said, "I made bargains about who deserved saving. I ranked lives. I did the arithmetic you're doing right now, and I did it fluently, and I will tell you what it bought me." They held up their hands, empty. "Nothing. Ten years of nothing, and a name even I can't remember. So no, Bram. We don't split up. We don't sacrifice. We walk out of this mountain together or not at all, and if you argue with me again I will put you over my knee like the child you are pretending not to be."

For a heartbeat Bram was furious—and then the fury broke against something else, something in Emrys's voice that wasn't sternness at all. It was fear. Not for themselves. For her.

"Fine," she said thickly. "Together."

"Together." Emrys started walking. "Now come on. Three hundred more steps and there should be a shaft."

There was a shaft. But there was also, rising out of it like smoke given ambition, the smell of hot metal and old incense—and standing at its mouth, patient as a held breath, a figure made of ember-light wearing the shape of a courtier, its face a smooth blank page waiting for a name to be written on it.

"Found you," it said, in no voice at all.

* * *

Emrys had fought exactly twice in the last decade, and both times had ended with running, because fighting meant being seen, and being seen meant being found, and being found was death by a slower name. Old habits screamed at them now. Go invisible. Slip sideways. Let the thing take the girl and vanish into the mountain's blind spots forever.

Instead they picked up a length of fallen scaffolding-iron from the shaft's edge and swung it at the courtier's blank face with everything they had.

The iron passed through ember-smoke and rang off stone behind it—but the creature flinched, and the flinch told Emrys everything: it was not made to be resisted. Nothing in the court was. Souls didn't fight. Debts didn't fight. It had no practice at this.

"Bram! Backward—count!"

She was already moving, dragging at his sleeve, hauling them both back down the passage the way they'd come, against the court's grain, while the courtier reassembled its composure behind them like slow fire. Emrys ran with their heel screaming and their lungs full of glass and thought, absurdly, of chapter-things Vess had told them years ago over maps drawn in tallow-light: *the court is a hunger, and hungers only know forward. Feed it sideways. Confuse the palate.*

"Here!" They shoved Bram into a niche behind a buttress of vein-rock and spun to face the passage alone, raising the iron bar, and when the courtier came flowing around the corner Emrys did the single most dangerous thing they had done in ten years.

They stood still. And let themselves be seen. Fully, deliberately, standing in the open like a person who existed, like someone who could be remembered, and shouted:

"HEY. YOU WANT A NAME? COME TAKE MINE."

It worked better than they expected and worse than they feared. The courtier froze—and then the whole corridor *leaned*, the attention of the mountain pivoting toward Emrys like a compass needle finding north, and for one vertiginous second Emrys felt the vast hunger below take notice of them specifically, individually, *by appetite*—

—and in that second, Bram grabbed their arm and yanked them sideways through a crack in the rock that hadn't been there a moment ago, a smuggler's squeeze, barely wider than her shoulders, going the wrong way, against the grain, into a space the court had forgotten to look at.

Then darkness, and the rasp of their own breathing, and the distant confusion of the attention sweeping past them, hunting, missing.

* * *

They lay in the crack together, pressed chest to back in the absolute dark, neither daring to whisper. Bram's heart hammered so hard Emrys could feel it through her coat, against the pocket where the ember slept.

Minutes passed. Or hours. The heat of the searching light washed over the crack's mouth twice and slid away both times, baffled, like a tongue probing for a tooth that had been pulled.

At last Emrys dared it: a breath, hardly a sound. "Still there?"

"Yes." Bram's whisper shook—with adrenaline, with cold, with something else. "That thing you did. Standing out. Calling to it." A pause. "That was the stupidest bravest thing I've ever seen anyone do."

"I've had practice being stupid. The bravery was incidental."

Another silence. Then, in the smallest voice: "It noticed you, didn't it. Really noticed you. I felt it turn."

Emrys stared into the black and felt the truth settle over them like ash. Yes. The court had looked at them and *tasted* something—an old flavor, a familiar vintage. Somewhere in these vaults was a hoard of memories with their name on the label, and the court was beginning to remember that it liked the taste.

"It did," Emrys said. "Which means we have very little time before it stops hunting blindly and starts hunting specifically. The shaft to the surface road is close—I can smell pine resin. Real air. We make for it now, fast, and we don't stop for anything."

"And then? The solstice is still coming. The mark's still burning. Running just means it opens the door somewhere with witnesses." Bram unwound the soaked bandage; the cloth fell away smoking, and beneath it the signature had begun, horribly, to rise off her palm like a brand lifting from hide—flame without fuel, a small standing fire in the shape of a contract.

Emrys looked at it and felt the last comfortable lies fall away. There was no outrunning this. There was no trick of grain or darkness. The debt would find its bearer, and the bearer would be opened, unless someone gave the court something it wanted more.

Something the gods remembered.

Vess's words surfaced, unbidden, spoken over a map of the deep roads with her blind eyes fixed on nothing: *The court runs on worship, child. And worship can be turned. But you'll need a lightning rod it remembers loving.*

"We go up," Emrys said quietly. "And then I need you to do something much harder than running."

"What?"

"Believe in a storm."

Above them, far above, through a mile of stone, the mountain shuddered—and somewhere in the shining dark, the Archivist opened a vault whose label had been blank for ten years, and began, gently, hungrily, to read.
