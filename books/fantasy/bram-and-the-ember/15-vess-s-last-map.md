## 15. Vess's Last Map

Vess felt them coming the way she felt weather coming—long before the door moved.

Two sets of footsteps on the switchback above her tower, one heavy and stumbling with exhaustion, one light and trying very hard to be quiet. The light one was a child. The heavy one walked like someone who had spent ten years unlearning how to walk like anything at all: no rhythm, no signature, a gait sanded down to nothing. Vess had sold enough secrets in her life to know what it cost to move that way. She had never met anyone who managed it so completely.

She set a third cup on the table before they knocked.

The knock came anyway—two raps, then nothing, as if the knocker had lost their nerve halfway through. Vess crossed her workroom by touch and memory, past the hanging maps that were not maps of any place a cartographer would admit to, past the jars of ink ground from things better left unnamed, and opened the door onto the cold.

"You took the Smuggler's Spine," she said. "Both of you. Alive. That's either luck or talent, and I don't sell to lucky people."

A pause. Then Emrys Galloway's voice, roughened by days underground: "You know who we are."

"I know who *you* are, which is more than you do." Vess stepped back from the doorway. "Come in before the mountain notices you're here. It's been noticing things lately. It's gotten greedy about it."

They came in. The girl first—Bram, fourteen years old, thin as a winter branch, with a bandaged palm held close against her chest the way a person holds a wound or a secret, and in Vess's experience the two were usually the same thing. Behind her came the storm-caller, hooded, faceless, moving through Vess's workroom like a draft that had learned manners.

"Sit," Vess said. "Drink. The tea is willowbark and something I won't name. It will make your hands stop shaking, which matters, because I'm going to ask you to show me something, and shaking hands make for poor showing."

Bram sat. Emrys did not—not immediately. Vess heard the hesitation in the way the floorboards didn't creak, the deliberate stillness of someone deciding whether a room was safe, arriving at *no*, and sitting down anyway.

"Your mapmaker is blind," Bram said quietly. It wasn't a question. It was the particular bewilderment of a child who had been told stories about Vess of Thornfell and hadn't believed half of them.

"My eyes went to pay for my daughter's life, twenty-two years ago," Vess said. "The gods took them. Or the plague did—the story changed depending on who needed to hear which version. But I'll tell you what I've learned since: there is more than one way to see, and most of the ways that matter are worse." She turned her face toward Emrys, toward the silence where a face should have been. "Show me your hand, storm-caller. No—show me your *palm*. There's a difference tonight."

* * *

Emrys's palm was a ruin of old scars, fine white lines crossing each other like a script no living language claimed. Vess ran her fingertips over them without asking permission, because asking would have taken longer and the night was already too short. The scars were old. Decades old. And beneath them, faint as heat off a stone at dusk, she felt the residue of something enormous.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you poor fool. You didn't just erase yourself. You wrote yourself out in *their* alphabet."

"What does that mean?" Bram asked.

"It means this isn't the work of hedge-magic or mortal forgetting." Vess withdrew her hand. "Every name burned out of every record leaves a scar somewhere, and scars have grammar. This grammar is divine. Old-divine. Ember Court." She leaned back in her chair. "You didn't buy your invisibility from a broker, storm-caller. You paid the court itself. Which means they've been holding your change in trust all these years. And courts—" her mouth twisted, "—courts always keep the change."

The silence from across the table had a texture to it. Vess had spent twenty-two years listening to silences; this one was a man or woman or whatever Emrys Galloway had become, standing at the edge of an abyss they'd already fallen into once, being told the fall had been arranged.

"The Archivist offered to sell them back," Emrys said finally. "In the vaults. My memories. All of them."

"And you bought one instead. One memory, and then the alarms went up, and now you're here." Vess smiled without warmth. "Word travels in the deep roads faster than feet do. The stones talk to each other. They talked to me."

"How?" Bram again. The girl leaned forward. "How do stones talk?"

"The same way everything talks, child. By being listened to." Vess rose and crossed to the far wall, where her oldest maps hung in their protective oilcloth. Her fingers found the one she wanted by its smell—she'd treated the cloth with juniper the day she made it, and juniper kept. "But we haven't time for lessons. Sit both of you down properly. What I'm going to tell you has no second telling, because after tonight I may not be alive to give it, and if I am, I intend to charge for it, and neither of you can afford me anymore."

* * *

She unrolled the map across the table and weighted its corners with cups and a jar of salt.

It was not a map of Thornfell. Or rather, it was—but drawn the way a physician draws a body, skin peeled back, organs exposed. The mountains were rendered in cross-section, and threaded through them, in ink that shimmered faintly even to Vess's blind sight, were veins. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Thin gold lines running down from every peak, every pass, every village shrine, converging like rivers toward a single chamber deep beneath the roots of the range.

"What is it?" Bram whispered.

"Worship," Vess said. "Drawn as it actually flows. Every prayer ever muttered at a roadside stone, every offering left out of fear or habit or love—it doesn't vanish. It runs downhill. Everything runs downhill eventually. And at the bottom of Thornfell, it pools." She tapped the central chamber with one finger. "That is the Ember Court's heart. Not a throne. A *reservoir*. The court doesn't rule the old gods, girl. The court *is* the plumbing. It catches what the gods are owed and stores it, and doles it back out in miracles when it wants something—a drowned child walking home, a family spared through plague winter—to remind the living that prayer works, and should therefore continue."

"That's why the shrines woke up," Emrys said slowly. "After ten dead years. It wasn't the gods returning. It was the court turning the taps back on."

"Now you're thinking like a soul-broker. Good. You'll need it." Vess traced a vein upward, from the reservoir toward Cinderhollow. "Here's what matters. The court is glutted right now—ten years of stored worship, hoarded, untouched, and suddenly it's spending. It answered prayers it shouldn't have been able to afford. It bought a child's brother. It's preparing a vessel-rite at solstice, which is the most expensive working there is, because a god needs a body, and bodies need to be *held*, and holding requires power poured out continuously, like light through a lamp." She looked at Bram, though of course she couldn't see her; she looked at where the girl's breathing came from. "The mark on your palm is the wick, child. At solstice they light it."

Bram's breath went ragged. "Then we run. We run until—"

"You can't outrun a mark that lives in your skin. You know that. Your storm-caller told you, and your storm-caller was right." Vess lifted her hand before the girl could speak again. "But listen. Listen, because this is the part worth dying for. A reservoir can be drained. Worship flows *downhill*—that's its nature, its law, older than the court, older than the gods themselves. The court holds its power only because everything flows toward it. Redirect the flow, and the reservoir empties. Empty the reservoir, and the vessel-rite gutters out like a candle in wind. The mark goes cold. The boy's ember goes unclaimed. The gods go unfed, and starved gods sleep, and sleeping gods are the closest thing this world has to mercy."

"How?" Emrys's voice had gone very quiet. Very controlled. Vess recognized the control of someone gripping a ledge. "Redirected how? Worship doesn't listen to people. It goes where it goes."

"It goes where it's *drawn*," Vess said. "And drawing it requires a ritual—an old one, older than the court, from back when worship was given freely and could be given elsewhere freely too. A redirection. You stand at a high place, above the veins, and you offer the gods something they want more than what the court is feeding them. You give them a reason to look away from their reservoir." She paused. "There's a condition. Of course there's a condition. There's always a condition; I've built a career on conditions."

"Name it," Emrys said.

"The ritual requires a focal point. A soul the gods *know*. Worship isn't a current you can redirect with wire and cleverness—it's attention, and attention must be caught, held, *recognized*. The working needs someone the old gods remember. Someone whose name is written in their oldest ledgers. Someone they loved, once, hard enough that even ten years of erasure couldn't fully burn it out." Vess reached across the table, found Emrys's scarred hand, and laid it flat upon the map, over the place where all the golden veins converged. "Someone like you."

* * *

The silence that followed lasted long enough for the tea to go cold in three cups.

"No," Bram said first. "No. Find someone else. There have to be others—the gods loved people before, they must have—"

"They loved thousands of people, child, over thousands of years. And nearly all of those people are dust, and the ones who aren't are forgotten, and forgotten is the same as dust for this purpose." Vess kept her hand where it was, over Emrys's, feeling the fine tremor that the willowbark tea had not been able to still. "I've spent four days searching my records since the stones told me you were coming. Four days, and I found exactly one soul in all of Thornfell whom the old gods remember well enough to turn their heads for. One. And that soul is sitting at my table pretending to be nobody."

"You knew," Emrys said. "Before we knocked. You knew why we'd come."

"I knew you'd come. I hoped you wouldn't need to." Vess withdrew her hand. "Let me tell you what I see, storm-caller, since seeing is my trade and I'm going to be honest with you for once in my mercenary life. You have spent ten years building a person out of absence. You erased your name, your history, your debts, your loves—I can feel the shape of the hole you left; it's the largest empty space I've ever touched in a living creature. And it worked. It kept you safe. Nobody could find you because there was almost nothing left to find." She tilted her head. "And now I'm asking you to unmake the one thing that's kept you alive, in exchange for two strangers. A girl who bargained stupidly and bravely, and a boy you've never met."

"Not strangers," Emrys said.

"No," Vess agreed. "Not anymore. That's how it works. That's the trap of other people—they stop being strangers precisely when they become expensive."

"There has to be another way." Bram's voice cracked. "Take my memories. Take mine—all of them, I don't—I don't remember much good anyway, take the plague winter, take all of it—"

"Child." Vess gentled her voice, which she resented having to do and did anyway. "Your memories aren't currency the gods recognize. They never loved you. They *used* you—that mark on your hand is a leash, not a bond. Only love draws their eyes, and only old love, and there is exactly one ledger entry that qualifies." She stood, joints cracking, and began rolling up the map with hands that knew every crease. "I won't pretend I feel nothing about this. I've watched a great many people walk into that mountain. I drew the maps that got some of them there. I have charged for every secret I ever sold because charging was the only way I could sleep afterward—if knowledge has a price, then the buyer carries the weight of choosing it, and I carry only the weight of naming it." She tied the roll shut. "So hear the price plainly, storm-caller, and know that I name it without pleasure. To redirect the worship, you must stand above the veins at Cinderhollow at solstice and call the storm—and while you call it, you must speak your true name aloud. Fully. Openly. In the hearing of every god and every mortal within the reach of the thunder."

* * *

Bram made a small sound, like something breaking quietly.

"They'll all know," she whispered. "Everyone. Everyone will know who you are."

"Everyone will know *something*," Vess corrected. "A name is a door, not a house. But doors, once opened, cannot be shut by the person who opened them. Your erasure ends at solstice, storm-caller. Whatever you were, whoever you burned—you become findable. Memorable. *Known*." She turned her blind face toward the window, toward the distant smudge of ember-orange light bleeding up from behind the peaks, brighter tonight than last night, brighter every night as solstice approached. "I won't dress it up. It may be the worst trade anyone has ever offered you. You'd be giving up the only fortress you have, and the siege outside hasn't even started yet."

"And if I refuse?" Emrys asked.

"Then the girl becomes a door at sundown on solstice, the court walks into the world wearing her, and the drowned child in Cinderhollow was only the first miracle of many." Vess let that sit. "Those are the options. I've checked them twice. I checked a third time hoping I was wrong, because I liked you, and liking people has never once improved my margins."

Across the table, the sound of a chair pushing back. Footsteps to the window. Vess tracked them by ear—Emrys standing at the glass, looking at the burning sky above the shrine-mountain, saying nothing for a long time.

When Emrys finally spoke, the voice was stripped of everything but its plainest timber. No performance. No brokerage. Just a person, asking a question.

"When I spoke my name in the vaults—when I bought that memory back—I remembered a village. A storm. A god giving the order." A pause. "Was it my fault? The records. Do your records say whether it was my fault?"

Vess considered lying. She had lied beautifully for decades; it was, in its way, her native tongue.

"Yes," she said. "And no. The records say you obeyed. The records also say you were the only servant of the court who ever stopped." She folded her hands. "The gods loved you, storm-caller, because you were the best of what they made. That's usually how it goes with the ones who leave."

Another silence. Longer. Outside, the ember-light pulsed once, twice, like something breathing in its sleep.

"All right," Emrys said at last, and the words came out level and quiet, without drama, the way a person agrees to carry something heavy—not because the weight is bearable, but because someone smaller is standing nearby, and the road is long, and the weight has to be carried by somebody. "Tell me where to stand."

Vess unrolled the map one final time and put Emrys's finger on the summit above Cinderhollow, where the oldest shrine stood, where the first vein of gold began its long descent into the dark.

"High ground," she said. "Solstice dawn. Speak loudly."

* * *

They slept in her workroom—Bram curled in the chair, refusing the cot, one bandaged hand closed around nothing as though it might still hold something if she gripped tightly enough. Emrys sat awake by the cold hearth, and Vess, who did not truly sleep anymore, listened to the two kinds of breathing and thought about prices.

Near morning, footsteps crossed to her desk. Paper whispered. Something small and heavy was set down beside her inkwell.

"Payment," Emrys said. "For the map."

"I told you neither of you could afford—"

"It's not coin." A pause. "It's a memory. A small one. The taste of the tea in your workroom, and the sound of your voice saying my debt was real. I've carried it since we walked in. Take it. I want what happens tomorrow to be a purchase, not a charity. I've been given too many things for free lately. It makes a person feel *known*."

Vess reached out and closed her fingers around the small warm weight of it—a memory, loose and unguarded, offered like a pebble from a pocket.

"Storm-callers," she said, when she could speak steadily, "were always terrible negotiators. It's why the court liked them."

She heard Emrys return to the hearth. She heard Bram stir and murmur a name—Tam, surely, Tam—and settle again. And outside, above the shrine-mountain, the ember-light gathered itself for the last day of the old world, and Vess of Thornfell, blind cartographer of small mercies, sat in the dark and memorized the sound of two people breathing, because she suspected that soon, one of those sounds would belong to someone entirely new.
