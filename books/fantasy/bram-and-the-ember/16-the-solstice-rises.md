## 16. The Solstice Rises

Bram had never seen Cinderhollow so full of light.

It came from everywhere and nowhere—from the dead shrine at the river's edge that had begun to glow like a banked hearth, from the well in the square where she'd once pressed her palm against cold stone and made her terrible bargain, from the windows of houses whose owners swore they'd lit no candles. The whole town had turned amber, as though the sun had decided to set directly into the streets instead of behind the mountains.

And the people were smiling.

That was the worst part. That was the part Bram couldn't stop looking at, the way you can't stop pressing a bruise. Old Marta Kell stood at the river shrine with her hands raised, laughing—actually laughing—as the water glowed around her ankles. The miller's boys knelt in the mud with their foreheads touching the stones. Someone had brought bread and honey and laid it out on the altar slab where nothing had been offered in forty years, and the offering was *sinking*, slowly, into the rock, the way water sinks into thirsty ground.

"They're feeding it," Bram whispered. She was crouched behind the tannery with Tam beside her, or what passed for beside her—the space he occupied never quite matched the space his body seemed to take up. "Emrys said they'd come to the shrines when the gods started answering. They don't know what it costs."

Tam turned his head toward her. His eyes were wrong tonight. They'd been wrong for days now, but tonight they were worse—there was firelight in them that didn't match any of the fires in town, a deep orange glow like coals seen through a grate. When he spoke, it wasn't quite his voice either. It was his voice with something underneath it, something vast and patient and *hungry*.

"You should go home, little sister," he said. "You shouldn't be out on the solstice night."

"Stop using him." Her voice cracked on the second word. She hated that. She'd promised herself she wouldn't crack, not here, not where half the town could see her talking to the boy who cast no shadow. "Tam. If you're still in there anywhere—you have to fight it. Tonight. Whatever they're doing with me, you have to—"

The thing wearing her brother smiled, and for one horrible moment the smile was too wide, the jaw unhinging slightly past what a face should do, before snapping back into Tam's familiar crooked grin. "He isn't fighting," it said gently. "He's sleeping. He sleeps so beautifully now. No dreams at all. Isn't that what you wanted?"

Bram's hand closed into a fist, and the mark on her palm flared.

She gasped. It wasn't pain yet—not exactly. It was heat, a spreading warmth like holding your hand over a stove, and beneath the warmth a pulling sensation, deep in her chest, as if something had hooked a line behind her ribs and was testing its weight. She looked down at her palm. The contract-mark—the spiral of fine black lines Emrys had examined with such grim care—was glowing now. Not metaphorically. It was actually giving off light, a thin ember-orange radiance that leaked between her fingers no matter how tightly she clenched them.

*Come home,* the mark seemed to say. *Come down. Come be poured.*

"No," Bram said aloud, to the mark, to the mountain, to whatever was listening. "No. I'm not coming."

Behind her, across the square, the crowd at the well began to sing.

* * *

It was an old song. That was the strange thing—Bram recognized it from her grandmother's funeral, from harvest nights, from every gathering where the old people wanted to feel the past settle over them like a shawl. It was a song about the mountain keeping them, about the fire under the stone that never went out, about how the valley's children were all children of the ember. Nobody had sung it in years. Now the whole town sang it, voices rising together in the amber light, and with every verse the glow in the windows brightened, and the pull behind Bram's ribs tightened another notch.

She understood then, with a clarity that felt like falling, what Emrys had tried to explain on the road: worship wasn't just prayer. It was attention. It was love pointed in a particular direction. And an entire town's worth of it, gathered and focused on the mountain on the longest night of the year, was a feast.

They were eating her neighbors' devotion, and they were using the strength of it to open *her*.

She ran.

Not away—she'd learned that lesson weeks ago, learned it in her own legs and lungs when she'd tried to outrun the mark and found it waiting wherever she stopped, patient as weather. She ran toward the one place in Cinderhollow she hadn't tried yet: the old chapel on the hill, the one dedicated to the new god, the quiet god, the one whose faith had come over the passes a century ago and never quite taken root in the valley. Its doors were iron-banded oak and its foundation stones had been quarried from the mountain itself, which everyone agreed was a mistake, because the mountain's stone remembered what it belonged to.

But it was stone. It was walls. It was somewhere to put her back.

She got halfway up the hill before the sky changed.

* * *

Later, Bram would struggle to describe it. The best she ever managed—and she managed it only once, years afterward, to Emrys, who listened without blinking—was this: *the sunset came back.*

Because the sun had already gone down. The solstice night had settled, stars out, the winter dark lying blue and clean over the valley. And then the western horizon lit again, ember-orange, and the light climbed—not spreading like dawn spreads but *rising*, pouring upward off the horizon like smoke from a fire, staining the clouds, staining the snowfields on Thornfell's shoulders, until the whole sky above the mountain burned the color of the inside of a forge.

The singing in the square faltered. Then resumed, louder, with something ecstatic in it now. The villagers had seen the burning sky and understood it as blessing. Of course they did. What else would you think, if you didn't know? A miracle. An answer. The old god showing its face at last after forty silent years.

Bram knew better. She stood on the chapel hill with her shadow stretched long and trembling behind her, and she watched the light pour upward, and she understood that it wasn't light at all. It was *attention*. The court beneath the mountain was opening its eye, and everything it looked at caught fire with the fact of being seen.

The mark on her palm screamed.

There was no other word for it. The warmth became white heat, the pulling became a yank, and Bram dropped to her knees on the frozen grass with her arm clutched to her chest. Under her skin, the spiral was moving. She could feel it turning, unwinding, the black lines crawling up her wrist like ivy in fast-forward, and where they touched, her veins lit orange beneath the skin, a network of embers mapping themselves along her arm toward her heart.

*No,* she thought. *No, no, no—*

She thought of Emrys. She thought of the storm-caller's flat, careful voice saying *the debt comes due at the solstice; the court means to wear you.* She thought of Vess's blind eyes turning toward her like compass needles. She thought of her mother setting Tam's place at the table, night after night, refusing to stop even after Bram begged her, because hope was the only thing keeping her mother upright and Bram had poisoned it at the source.

She thought: *I did this. I opened the door. Me. Fourteen years old and scared and so tired of watching people cough blood into rags.*

And then, kneeling in the burning grass under the burning sky, Bram Galloway felt something shift in her chest—not the mark, not the pull, but underneath them, in the place where she kept the things that were actually hers. Guilt had lived there for a year. Guilt and stubbornness, packed tight together like two stones in one fist. She had carried them everywhere. She had let them make every decision: the bargain, the silence, the running, the shame of Tam's empty dreaming face at breakfast.

The stones turned over. And what was underneath them, what had been underneath them all along, was anger.

Not the hot kind. The cold kind. The kind that doesn't shout. The kind that looks at the thing hurting you and says, very quietly, *you picked the wrong girl.*

"I sold my brother's soul," she said to the sky, to the mountain, to the vast patient hunger pouring its attention down onto her. Her voice shook. She let it. "I know what I did. I'm not proud of it. But I paid for my family's lives with the only coin I had, and I've spent a year paying *again* every single day, and I will spend the rest of my life paying if I have to." She got one foot under herself. Then the other. She stood, swaying, her arm a column of fire. "But you don't get to collect twice. You don't get to take Tam *and* me. You don't get to walk around in my skin and smile at my mother with my mouth. I am not a door."

The mark flared so bright it threw her shadow backward, sharp-edged on the chapel wall.

"I am not a *door*," she screamed, and drove her burning palm against the chapel's iron hinge.

* * *

The metal seared. She smelled her own skin and didn't care. And through the contact—flesh to iron, iron to stone, stone to the bones of the mountain—she felt the pull stutter.

Just for a heartbeat. Just a hitch, like a cart wheel hitting a rut. But she felt it, and she understood something in the same instant, the way you understand things in dreams: the court needed her willing. Or near enough to willing. It needed her to *open*, and a door slammed shut was a door it couldn't use, not tonight, not with the ritual already in motion, not with the whole town's worship flowing through it like a river through a millrace. There were rules. Even hunger had rules.

So she held on.

She wrapped both arms around the iron hinge like a drowning woman around a spar, and the metal burned her palms and forearms and she held on anyway, cheek pressed to the freezing oak, tears freezing on her lashes, and every time the pull surged she pushed back with the only weapon she had left, which was *refusal*, pure and stupid and magnificent. Below her, the singing swelled. Above her, the sky churned orange. Inside her, something enormous leaned its weight against the door of her and found a fourteen-year-old girl standing in front of it with her feet planted and her teeth bared.

*Little vessel,* the voice said—not in her ears but in the mark, in the marrow, in the spaces between her thoughts. It sounded almost fond. *Why do you fight? We gave your brother peace. We spared your house. We answered every prayer this valley forgot to pray. We are generous, Bram Galloway. Open, and be generous with us.*

"Generous," she panted. The word came out a laugh, ragged and disbelieving. "You want generous? Here's generous." She lifted her head from the door and looked down the hill at the glowing town, at the singing crowds, at the well where she'd made her bargain, and her cold anger crystallized into something with edges. "You keep them. You keep every scrap of their love, every song, every candle—they're yours, drink deep, choke on it. But this—" she hammered her burned fist against the iron, once, twice, three times, "—this is mine. My brother. My mother. My hands. You had a year of me and it ends tonight."

The pull surged again, harder, furious now, and Bram's vision went white at the edges. Her knees buckled. The iron was the only thing holding her up, and she could feel the heat of the mark winning, line by line, inch by inch, the ember-veins creeping past her elbow now, climbing toward her shoulder, toward her heart, and she was so tired, she was fourteen and she was so *tired*, and the door of her was bowing inward—

And then the wind changed.

It came down off the mountain in a single long exhalation, cold and mineral and smelling of deep stone, and it carried with it a sound that Bram felt in her teeth before she heard it with her ears: thunder. Far away. High up. On the shrine-mountain, where the oldest dead shrine in Thornfell stood alone against the burning sky.

Thunder, in winter. Thunder, on a cloudless, burning, impossible night.

The pull hesitated. The whole valley seemed to hesitate—the singing wavered, the amber light flickered, the court's vast attention turned by a degree, like a great beast lifting its head from its meal to sniff the air.

Something else was on the mountain. Something the storm remembered.

Bram pressed her forehead to the scorched iron and laughed—a small, cracked, ferocious sound that no one heard.

"Hold on, Tam," she whispered. "Hold on. They're coming."
