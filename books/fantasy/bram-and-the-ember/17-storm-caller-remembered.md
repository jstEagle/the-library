## 17. Storm-Caller, Remembered

The mountain had a name once. Everything in Thornfell had a name once—Emrys had made a decade's work of forgetting that fact, and now it rose up to meet them like an old debt.

The villagers called it the Shrine-Mountain because of what crowned it: a ring of standing stones older than any kingdom, older than Cinderhollow, older perhaps than the habit of naming things at all. For ten years those stones had been silent, lichen-eaten, ignored by shepherds who gave the summit a wide berth out of some animal wisdom no one could articulate. Dead shrines. Dead gods. That was the story everyone agreed on, and Emrys had clung to it like a drowning man clings to driftwood, because if the shrines were dead then nothing beneath the mountain remembered *Galloway*.

The shrines were not dead. The shrines had never been dead. They were only sleeping, and tonight they were waking hungry, and Bram was strapped to the altar-stone at their center while her blood turned to fire in her veins.

Emrys climbed.

The path was not a path. It was a goat-track gone feral, switchbacking up through scree and black pine, and Emrys took it at a pace that would have killed anyone whose lungs were merely human. The air grew thin and strange. Below, Cinderhollow glowed—a scatter of hearth-light in the valley's cup—but above the town the sky was wrong. It burned ember-orange from horizon to horizon, as though sunset had gotten stuck there, as though the whole dome of the world had been fired in a kiln and left to cool too slowly. No stars survived that glare. The solstice moon hung in it like a coal in ash.

And the light pulsed. Slowly. Rhythmically.

Like breathing. Like a heartbeat. Like the mark on a fourteen-year-old palm being opened one degree wider with every pulse.

*Faster,* Emrys told their legs, and their legs obeyed.

They had left Vess at the tree line with a map drawn in charcoal on birchbark and a warning delivered in that flat, sightless voice that somehow carried more weight than shouting: *"Once you speak it, there is no unspeaking. Every ear in this mountain will know you. Every eye. There is no hiding after, Galloway. Not ever again."*

"I know," Emrys had said.

"You don't," Vess had said, with terrible gentleness. "You can't. You built the hiding so deep you've forgotten you're inside it. But I'll say it anyway, for whatever it's worth: I'm sorry. And thank you."

Emrys hadn't trusted their voice to answer. They had climbed instead.

Now the pines gave way to bare stone, and the stone gave way to the stones—the ring itself, vast and tilted by centuries of frost-heave, each monolith taller than three men. They were carved. Emrys had never seen the carvings up close; from the valley they were only shadows on shadow. Up close, in the ember-light, they resolved into figures: robed and crowned shapes, faces worn smooth by weather but still unmistakably *attentive*, all of them facing inward toward the altar at the ring's heart.

All of them watching Bram.

She lay on the slab with her wrists bound in cord that wasn't cord—it moved like smoke, tightened like a promise—and the mark on her palm had opened. What had been a sigil inked in ash was now a seam of living flame, running from the heel of her hand to the tip of her middle finger, and from that seam light poured upward in a column that fed the burning sky. Her eyes were open. She was screaming, but quietly, the way people scream when they've been screaming long enough to learn economy—small sounds, wrung out, between clenched teeth.

Her head turned as Emrys crossed the threshold of the ring.

"Don't," she rasped. "Don't—you said run, I told you to *run*, you stupid—"

"I know," Emrys said. "I never listen."

The column of light between her hand and the sky thickened. Through it, faintly, came a sound Emrys knew from ten years of nightmares: voices layered under voices under voices, a choir heard through a wall, the Ember Court singing its slow rite of taking. *Vessel. Vessel. Open the door. Open the door.* The words weren't in any language. They arrived directly behind the eyes.

Bram's back arched off the stone.

There was no more time for climbing, for circling, for the careful approach Emrys's decade of caution demanded. There was only this: the center of the ring, the oldest stone, the one the carvings loved best—a monolith carved with a figure whose face alone had not been worn away, because it had been defaced deliberately, chiseled into anonymity by hands that feared it.

Emrys pressed both palms against the cold granite and did the thing they had sworn on their own erased soul never to do again.

They called the storm.

It did not come easily. Ten years of disuse lay over the talent like scar tissue, and the first pull was agony—a hook behind the sternum, hauling on something rooted deeper than muscle. Emrys pulled anyway. Pulled until their vision spotted and their knees buckled against the stone. Above, the ember-orange sky *shuddered*. A single cloud, gray and ordinary and impossibly beautiful, dragged itself across the burning dome like a hand smearing wet paint.

The choir faltered.

*What,* said the voices behind Emrys's eyes, in a tone that was almost curiosity, almost recognition, almost hunger. *What is that.*

More clouds. Emrys hauled them in from every quarter of the sky, tearing them loose from horizons, and the wind came with them, shrieking down the mountainside, whipping Bram's hair across her face, guttering the column of light so that it flickered like a candle in a draft. The temperature dropped. The first raindrops hit the altar-stone and hissed to steam where they touched the flame-seam.

"Let her go," Emrys said aloud, to the sky, to the stones, to the court beneath the world. Their voice cracked on it. "Take me instead. I'm the one you want. I'm the one you *made*."

The choir laughed. It was not a kind sound. *You are nothing,* the voices said, with the infinite condescension of things that had watched empires compost. *You are a hole shaped like a person. You cut yourself out of every ledger. You are unremembered, unmarked, unwritten. We cannot take what we cannot see.*

And that was true. That was the whole architecture of Emrys's survival, load-bearing truth, the wall they had built their life inside: the gods could not claim what they could not remember. Erasure had been armor. Invisibility had been salvation.

Which meant there was only one weapon left.

Emrys looked at Bram. She had stopped screaming—not because the pain ended, he realized, but because she was watching him with an expression he'd seen once before, on the smuggler's road, when she'd asked him why he helped people who couldn't pay him back. He hadn't answered then. He answered now, without words, by turning back to the storm-darkened sky and doing the unthinkable.

He spoke his name.

Not *Galloway*. Not the trade-name stitched into his coat, not the half-dozen aliases scattered through ten years of careful, forgettable life. His *true* name—the one given at birth, the one spoken over him by his mother in a language of the old country, the one he had paid a fortune in blood and years to burn out of every record, every memory, every mouth. He had not heard it spoken aloud since the night he fled. He had not let himself *think* it whole. Now he opened the vault where he'd buried it, took it in both hands, and shouted it into the storm:

"*I am Emrys Galloway!*"—and the true name rode underneath it, ancient and syllabic and *loud*, louder than thunder, louder than the choir, a sound that did not travel through the air so much as through everything, through stone and bone and the roots of the mountain and the roots of the world—"and I am the one who called your storms! I am the one your altars were raised for! I am the last of your storm-callers, and I am HERE!"

The world broke open.

Lightning struck the ring—not near it, not around it, but *into* it, six bolts at once, one for each monolith, and the carved figures lit from within like lanterns, their worn faces suddenly vivid, suddenly *awake*, all of them turning—stone grinding against stone, a sound like continents clearing their throats—to look at him.

Every god in Thornfell looked at Emrys Galloway.

He felt it happen. That was the part no one could have warned him about, the part Vess's blind eyes had tried to convey: visibility was not a metaphor. It was a physical event, a tearing, as though he had spent ten years pressed flat against the underside of the world and now the world peeled him off and held him up to the light. Every ward he'd woven unraveled at once. Every memory he'd planted in strangers' minds to blur his passage bloomed and died. Somewhere in Cinderhollow, he knew, heads were lifting from supper tables. Somewhere in the valley, Vess stood motionless at the tree line, face turned toward the summit, whispering his name to herself just to feel the shape of it. Every prayer ever muttered in his direction, every curse, every kindness—he felt them all arrive at once, a tide of attention with the weight of an ocean, and it should have crushed him.

Instead, it felt like setting down something he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was heavy.

*Liberation in self-destruction.* He understood the phrase now, understood it in his marrow. To be seen was to be vulnerable. To be vulnerable was to be *real*. He had mistaken disappearance for safety, and safety for peace, and peace had been neither. This—this naked, catastrophic exposure—was the closest thing to peace he had known since the night he ran.

The choir stopped singing.

In the silence, the column of light above Bram's hand wavered, thinned, began—slowly, grudgingly, like a beast denied its meal—to bend. Away from her. Toward him.

"No," Bram whispered. She had torn one wrist free of the smoke-cord, or it had loosened, or the court simply didn't care anymore, and she was pushing herself up on the altar-slab with her burned hand cradled against her chest. "No, no—that's not—we came to save *me*, you don't get to—"

"Bram." Emrys didn't turn. Couldn't; the attention of gods is a current, and swimming against it would drown them both. "Listen to me. The mark needs a vessel. It doesn't care which. It wanted you because you were small and desperate and you owed it. But it *knows* me. It has always known me. I am worth a hundred of you to it—a thousand. Let it look at me."

"That's not fair."

"No," Emrys agreed. The rain was falling properly now, cold and clean, and it plastered his hair to his skull and ran down his face and he laughed, actually laughed, at the feel of it. When had he last stood in the rain? When had he last dared stand anywhere the sky could find him? "Nothing about this is fair. Fairness is for stories. This is just arithmetic."

The light reached him.

It poured over the altar-stone, over his outstretched arms, and where it touched, the mark bloomed—not on his palm, he noticed distantly, but across his whole body, sigils surfacing along his forearms and throat like fish rising in a pond, the old god-script of the Ember Court recognizing its own. The choir resumed, but changed: no longer the rite of taking, but something older, hungrier, almost tender. *Storm-caller. Storm-caller. Ours. Ours. Come down. Come home.*

The ground shuddered. Between two of the monoliths, the bedrock split—not violently, but with the inevitability of a door that had always been a door—and stair-steps descended into a glow that was not firelight and not sunlight but the color of banked coals, of embers kept alive for centuries by patient tending.

The Ember Court, opening to receive him.

"Emrys." Bram was on her feet now, swaying, her burned hand smoking gently in the rain. Her face was a ruin of fury and terror and something worse, something that looked unbearably like love. "You come back. You hear me? You come *back*."

Emrys looked at her—at this furious, stubborn, guilt-ravaged child who had sold her brother's soul to survive and had spent every day since trying to buy it back—and thought, with a clarity that felt like the lightning had passed through him too: *This is what I forgot. This is what erasure cost. Not the name. This.*

"I'll do better than that," he said. "Wait for me at the bottom. Both of us. Tam too."

Then he stepped past the monoliths, past the watching carven faces, onto the first stair, and the mountain closed the door behind him with a sound like a held breath finally released.

The stairs went down farther than the mountain was tall. The walls were lined with niches, and in each niche sat an ember—a soul, he understood, a life reduced to its warmest remaining particle—and as he descended, the embers turned toward him in their niches, hundreds of them, thousands, a corridor of small fires all bending like grass in wind, all *watching*.

At the bottom, in a chamber floored with cooled glass, something vast unfolded itself from the dark and regarded him with attention that had weight and texture and heat.

"There you are," said the Archivist, and its voice was almost gentle. "We wondered how long you'd make us wait. Ten years, storm-caller. You kept us waiting *ten years*."

Behind Emrys, far above, the storm raged on—and somewhere in it, small and stubborn and alive, a girl with a burned hand started down the mountain to keep a promise.

* * *

The chamber of cooled glass stretched in every direction, and Emrys understood, with the calm of a man already falling, that *every direction* was a lie told by a place that did not believe in distance. The floor reflected not his face but his name—both names, the shout and the secret, written in cooling light—and the walls were shelved to a height that mocked geometry, and the shelves were full.

Souls. Thousands of them. Each in its niche, each a coal-sized knot of warmth, and the court wore them like a miser wears rings: not to use, only to *have*.

The thing that had unfolded from the dark resolved, the way the court's servants always did, into something almost shaped like courtesy. Tall. Robed in ash-gray. Its face was a page that had been written on and erased so many times the surface had gone soft, features blurred into suggestion—and yet its voice, when it spoke again, was one Emrys knew.

Knew the way you know a room you grew up in.

"You spoke it beautifully," the Archivist said. "After all these years. All that practice forgetting. And when it mattered, you still said it like you meant it." It drifted closer, robes whispering like turning pages. "Do you know what we felt, up there, when the name hit the sky?"

"I imagine you felt hungry."

"We felt *grief*." The word landed strangely, heavily, in that vast cool room. "You were ours, storm-caller. Our best. Our loudest. And then one night the village burned, and you blamed us, and we blamed you, and you did the one thing no god can forgive and no mortal should be able to do." The blurred face inclined. "You made yourself impossible to mourn."

"I didn't come for a reconciliation."

"No. You came for the boy." The Archivist gestured, and far up the endless shelves, one niche among thousands brightened—a small ember, colder than the rest, flickering with a stubbornness Emrys recognized, because he'd spent a winter teaching its owner to build snares and swear properly. Tam. Still hers. Still faintly, defiantly, his sister's. "And we will discuss the boy. But first—" the soft face turned fully toward him, and behind the blur, for one instant, Emrys saw eyes he had prayed to as a child, "—you should understand what speaking your name has done. You are visible now. To us. To the valley. To every ledger in the world. And visible things can be *collected*."

The storm overhead cracked like a whip. Through miles of stone, Emrys felt Bram's footsteps on the descent path, small and fast and refusing to stop.

"So bargain," Emrys said, and spread empty hands. "That's why I came down. Name your price."

The Archivist smiled—or the blur arranged itself into the shape a smile leaves behind.

"Oh, storm-caller," it said. "We're going to ask for *everything*."
