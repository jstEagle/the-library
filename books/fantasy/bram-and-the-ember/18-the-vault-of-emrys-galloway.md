## 18. The Vault of Emrys Galloway

They dragged Emrys through the court by chains of spoken sound—not iron, nothing so crude—each link a syllable that tightened around their wrists when they struggled. The gallery of burning names streamed past on either side, thousands of small flames guttering in their niches, and Emrys understood now, with the terrible clarity of a man reading his own death warrant, that every one of those flames was a life. A whole person, folded down to the size of a coal. Somewhere behind them, Bram's screams had gone hoarse and then silent, and the silence was worse.

The throne room—if it could be called that—opened like the interior of a furnace seen from within. No walls, only heat given architecture. The Ember Court did not sit at the room's center; it *pooled* there, a shape of slow orange fire that never quite resolved into faces, though Emrys's eyes kept trying to make them. Mouths. Eyes. Hands. Suggestions of an audience that had starved for so long it had eaten its own outline.

The chains hurled Emrys to their knees before it. The floor was warm as skin.

*Storm-caller.* The voice did not come from the fire so much as from everywhere the fire was not. *The mountain remembers you. We remember you.*

"You don't," Emrys said. Their voice came out steadier than they felt, which surprised them. Perhaps terror had burned through everything else and left only the frame. "That's rather the point."

*We held your prayers once,* the court said, ignoring them, or savoring them. *You were loud in your devotion. You burned brightly. Do you know how long we have hungered since you went dark?*

Emrys kept their eyes on the middle distance, where the fire thinned enough to suggest a face. "I want to make an offer."

The court rippled. Laughter, maybe, or indigestion, or the sound a fire makes when it finds something new to eat.

*You have nothing,* it said. *You are a hollow thing wearing a name you stole back through a gate with blood. We have the girl. We have her mark. At solstice—and look, storm-caller, the ceiling is already brightening—we will have her hands, her voice, her years. She will open every shrine in Thornfell and pour the worship back into us, and we will be fed for an age.*

"The girl is fourteen," Emrys said. "She made one bargain on the worst night of her life because her brother was coughing blood into a bucket and her mother was dying and nobody else would answer. That's who you're building your eternity on. A child's desperation." They let the pause stretch. "No wonder you're starving."

The heat around them sharpened. The floor warmed another degree. But the court did not strike them down, and Emrys knew why, and the knowing sat in their chest like swallowed coal: gods could not resist being needled about their hunger. Hunger was the one prayer they still answered.

"I know what this court values," Emrys said. "Memories. Names. Selves. I've been walking through your galleries cataloguing them for days, posing as a broker, and you let me, because you thought I might be useful—or because you recognized the merchandise." They raised their head fully now, meeting the suggestion of eyes in the fire. "Because you've smelled it on me since I came down the smuggler's spine. Haven't you? You've been drooling for a week."

*Speak plainly, hollow thing.*

"A vault," Emrys said. "Below the galleries. Your Archivist told me about it, or told me around it, which is the same courtesy you extend everyone. There's a vault in this mountain holding everything I burned away. Ten years of erasure. Every record, every recollection, every whispered mention of the name Emrys Galloway—gathered, hoarded, filed. You didn't destroy any of it. You never destroy anything." Their mouth was dry. They made themselves keep going. "You keep it because somewhere deep in your embers you believe memory is the only food that doesn't run out."

The court had gone very still. Fire does not go still; this was the exception that proved the horror.

"You want worship," Emrys said. "Worship is just memory with reverence attached. And no memory in Thornfell burns hotter than mine. A decade of a self, deliberately buried, deliberately starved—do you know what that tastes like? It tastes like the opposite of a prayer. It has the *shape* of devotion. All that attention, all that remembering, bent for ten years toward forgetting. You could feed on the negative space for a century."

*You would give us yourself,* the court said slowly.

"I would give you the vault. All of it. Every year of me that I paid to forget." Emrys spread their chained hands—a gesture of offering, or surrender, or simply showing the merchant the goods. "Tam's soul returned whole. The girl's mark dissolved. Her family forgotten by your court so thoroughly that no shrine ever answers them again. In exchange, I walk out of here empty."

*Empty,* the court repeated, tasting the word. *You do not understand what you offer.*

"I understand better than you think."

And that was the truth of it, or near enough. They did not remember what was in the vault—that was the entire architecture of the thing—but they remembered the weight of it. Ten years is not nothing. Ten years is a childhood's worth of mornings, a hundred storms called and a hundred storms weathered, faces, failures, loves if there had been loves, the smell of a house they must have lived in, a teacher's voice, a mother's laugh or a father's or neither. All of it sat beneath this mountain like ore in a seam, and Emrys had spent a decade walking around on top of it without falling through, and now they were proposing to hand over the deed.

They thought of Bram on the deep roads, learning the mountains' grammar, laughing once—at Vess's expense, at some joke about contour lines—and how the laugh had changed her face entirely, made her look four years younger and infinitely more breakable. They thought of Tam's empty chair, and the boy who sat in it wearing borrowed voices. They thought: *this is what the last ten years were for. Not escape. Storage. I was saving myself for something, even if I didn't know what.*

Perhaps that was even true.

The court turned its attention inward—or seemed to—and the temperature of the room swung wildly, hot then cold then hot, a fever deciding. When it spoke again, the Archivist was beside Emrys.

They hadn't heard the old creature arrive. They never did. The Archivist stood in robes the color of cooled ash, ink-stained fingers folded, lamp-eyes lowered in something that on a human face would have been called pity and on this face was merely its best available imitation.

*The terms are acceptable,* the court said.

"Then we're agreed—"

*But,* the court continued, and Emrys's stomach dropped, *the withdrawal must be witnessed. The Archivist will conduct it. You will remain conscious throughout.*

"That wasn't the bargain."

*That was never discussed,* the court said, with the serene cruelty of entities for whom omission is a sacrament. *Consciousness was not part of the price you named. Consider it a gift. Most souls beg to watch themselves be consumed. It is the closest they come to immortality.*

* * *

The vault was below everything.

Emrys had imagined shelves, rows of glowing embers like the galleries above, but the reality was older and stranger. The descent took them down stairs that had not been built but *grown*, spirals of black stone veined with faint orange light, and at the bottom opened a cavern where the ceiling should have been and instead was darkness going up forever. And in that darkness: names. Not embers. *Words.* Thousands upon thousands of words suspended in the air, written in no script Emrys knew and yet each one legible on sight, hanging at every height like snow that had stopped mid-fall and decided to stay.

Galloway. Galloway. Galloway.

Their name filled the cavern. Spoken forms of it, written forms, carved forms, sung forms—the way a mother says it, the way an enemy spits it, the way it looked on a ledger in a customs house in a town they could not picture. Every instance anyone had ever set down or sounded out, harvested from the world over ten patient years and stored here in the dark, and the mass of it hummed at a frequency Emrys felt in their teeth, in the scar on their palm from the tollgate, in the place behind their sternum where a person keeps whatever it is that makes them continuous from one day to the next.

"There are more than I thought," Emrys said. Their voice echoed strangely, and each echo came back altered, as if the cavern were trying the name on.

*There are always more,* the Archivist said. *Forgetting is a lazy craft. Mortals misremember, misfile, repeat. A name spoken once in a tavern in a country you've never visited—that is enough to seed a new instance. We have been collecting yours since the night you first prayed for erasure.* The lamp-eyes regarded Emrys without judgment. *You should know it worked. Almost completely. What remains up there—"a gesture toward the surface"—is less than a candle-end of what you were.*

"Then take the candle-end too."

*That is not—*

"Take all of it. Whatever's left. Whatever fragments are still rattling around loose in my skull—I'm not doing this halfway, Archivist. If I leave here owning even one hour of my own history, I'll spend the rest of my life trying to buy it back, and I'll be back down your stairs inside a year, and you'll get it anyway, but you'll have had to deal with me twice." Emrys smiled, and found to their surprise that it was almost real. "Consider it efficiency."

The Archivist studied them for a long moment. Then, with two ink-stained fingers, they reached out—not toward the floating names, but toward Emrys's forehead—and drew something out. It came away like a thread of smoke with weight to it, curling between the old creature's fingertips, faintly warm.

*One hour,* the Archivist said. *Recent. The girl, laughing, on the deep road.* They held it a moment longer than necessary. Then they set it adrift among the others, and it joined the constellation of Galloways, and Emrys felt the absence immediately—not as loss, exactly, but as a question with no asker. Something had happened on a road once. Someone had laughed. The space where the knowing had been was smooth and clean and utterly unmarked.

"Oh," Emrys said quietly. "So that's what it feels like."

*You may stop,* the Archivist said. *At any point. The court will honor the bargain struck so far, for the portion already surrendered. Gods are literal creatures. It is their one virtue.*

"Start at the beginning," Emrys said. "Wherever that is."

The Archivist raised both hands, and the cavern moved.

It did not come all at once. The names descended in order—chronological, Emrys realized, the Archivist filing even this—and the earliest ones were soft, almost tender. A diminutive. *Rys.* Someone had called them Rys. The word touched Emrys's shoulder and passed through, and with it went whatever it carried: a kitchen, woodsmoke, a woman's hands flour-dusted to the wrist, a voice saying *Rys, come in from the rain* —and then it was gone, and Emrys stood in the same spot, but the spot was emptier, and they could not have said what had just been taken except that their cheeks were wet.

They did not stop.

The names came faster. Schoolroom instances. Ledgers. A first lover's murmured version, taken with a whole evening attached. Storms—so many storms—their name in the mouths of villagers praying for rain and cursing it, and threaded through all of them, growing louder as the chronology climbed, a word that was not their name but kept company with it: *chosen, chosen, chosen.* The gods' word for them. Each instance of it passed through Emrys like a coal dropped down their throat, and each time they swayed, and each time they stayed standing.

Somewhere in the middle of it, they became aware that they were speaking. Reciting, really—the memories leaving words in their wake, the way a wound leaves blood.

"—there was a village," they heard themselves say, voice flat as a read inventory. "In the valley below Thornfell. The god asked for a sign and I gave it one. Rain. Then lightning. Then the fires. Two hundred people. I was twenty-three. I ran here and I burned my own name because if no one remembered me, no god could find me, and if no god found me, I could never be used that way again." A breath. "It didn't occur to me until right now that I could have simply refused."

*Could you have?* the Archivist asked softly, feeding another name into the dark.

"No," Emrys said, and was startled by the certainty. "That's why I ran instead."

The hours lost meaning. The last names were the heaviest—the ones from the decade itself, the careful ones, the erasure-work, each spell and unspelling done in caves and crossroads with salt and blood and stubbornness, each one signed *Emrys Galloway* in the moment of casting and unsigned forever after. Those came out of them like splinters from a wound ten years deep, and with the final one, Emrys felt something in their posture change—a settling, a lightening, a body putting down a load carried so long the muscles had grown around it.

Then the Archivist drew out the thread of what remained. The candle-end. The residue. Everything loose in Emrys's skull that still answered to the name, all the unfiled hours, the texture of being somebody—and it left gently, almost kindly, and the cavern full of Galloways flared orange-gold, brighter than the galleries above, brighter than the court itself, a decade of a self igniting all at once.

Emrys watched it burn and felt nothing, which was, they understood dimly, the entire point of everything they had ever done.

*It is done,* the Archivist said. The lamps of their eyes had dimmed to something almost gentle. *The soul returns to the boy tonight. The mark will cool before dawn. The girl will not remember the court's face, per our terms—but she will remember you. Someone must.* A pause. *Do you wish to know what happens next?*

Emrys opened their mouth and found no words. They searched for their own name, out of habit, out of ten years of habit, and found the search come back empty-handed and empty-hearted and—free. That was the thing. Underneath the hollow, underneath the nothing, something in them was standing up straight for the first time in a decade, unbent from a burden whose weight it had already forgotten.

"I don't," they managed. "I don't know anything anymore. I don't know who I am."

*No,* the Archivist agreed, and helped them to their feet with surprising strength. *But you are about to become extremely well acquainted with the girl who does.*

Behind them, the vault blazed with the name Emrys Galloway, ten years of a self burning steady and bright, and above, far above, the mountain began, very slowly, to cool.
