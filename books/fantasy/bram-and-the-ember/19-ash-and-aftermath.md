## 19. Ash and Aftermath

Bram felt the court die the way you feel a fever break—not all at once, but in stages, each one leaving her colder and more herself.

The first sign was the mark on her palm. It had been a wound of light for hours, an open seam of ember-orange that showed something moving beneath her skin like a fish under ice. She had stopped screaming about it somewhere on the deep road, not because it stopped hurting but because screaming took breath she needed for running. Now, standing in the mouth of the smuggler's tunnel with Cinderhollow's rooftops visible far below—small and gray and impossibly ordinary under a sky still stained the color of banked coals—she watched the seam close. Not heal. Close, like a purse drawn shut, like a mouth deciding to keep its own counsel. The light dimmed from within, folding inward, and then there was only her palm again: chapped, dirty, the old scar from the well-rope running across it, and nothing else.

She pressed her thumb into the center of it anyway. Pressed until it hurt. Nothing answered.

"It's done," she said, and her voice came out strange, flattened by the tunnel's acoustics, by exhaustion, by the sheer unbelievable fact of being alive and unclaimed.

Behind her, Emrys stood with their back against the tunnel wall, and Bram did not look at them yet. She wasn't ready. She had seen what the court took—had stood in the vault as the Archivist lifted the lid off Emrys Galloway's life like a lid off a jar of preserved fruit, had watched shelf after shelf of glowing embers pour themselves down the court's hungry throat. Every memory Emrys had burned away. Every year of the self they'd buried. All of it gone now, swallowed, and the person leaning against the stone behind her was wearing Emrys's face and Emrys's coat and did not know why any of it mattered.

So she looked at the sky instead.

The ember-orange was fading. It drained out of the clouds the way color drains from a bruise, unevenly, in patches—the west still smoldering while the east went first to violet and then to honest dusk, and then the first stars pricked through, and they were just stars, cold and indifferent and belonging to no one. Below the mountain, at the answered shrines where the villagers had gathered all day without knowing what they were feeding, Bram could see lights beginning to move. Torches coming out. People emerging from their houses to look up at a sky that had, without ceremony or explanation, simply stopped burning.

Somewhere beneath her feet—far beneath, deeper than the vaults, deeper than anything mapped—the Ember Court went quiet.

Not silent. Bram would carry the sound of it for the rest of her life: a vast settling, like a great house whose family has died, like a hearth going out room by room. The quiet fire that lit every gallery guttered. The heirloom-shelves full of stolen souls stopped humming their thin, ceaseless hunger-song. And the gods themselves—she had heard them, in the last minutes before Emrys's surrender, voices like rockfall and riverbed, vast and starving and *awake*—those voices sank away downward, away from the surface of the world, back into the dark stone they'd slept in for a thousand years. Glutted. Fed. Full of one person's entire remembered self.

They went back to sleep the way a wolf does after a slaughter: sated, slow, and utterly without gratitude.

* * *

They came down the mountain in the dark because Emrys's body still knew the paths even if Emrys didn't.

That was the thing Bram couldn't stop noticing. The storm-caller walked ahead of her through the switchbacks above Cinderhollow with the same uncanny sureness they'd always had, feet finding the safe stones, shoulders turning a half-second before a low branch arrived, pausing at each fork as if listening for which way the mountain wanted them to go. Their hands still moved when they talked. Their voice still had that dry edge, that habit of understatement. But when Bram said, "Careful, the scree slides here," Emrys looked at the scree like it was the first scree they'd ever seen, and thanked her politely, and Bram's chest did something complicated and painful every single time.

"You don't have to walk me home," she said, on the third switchback. "I know the way."

"I know," Emrys said. Then, after a moment: "Do I know the way?"

"You knew it this morning."

"This morning I was someone else." They said it without drama, the way you'd report the weather, and kept walking. "This morning I apparently had ten years of reasons for walking these roads alone. This morning I knew what I traded and chose it." A pause. "Tonight I only know that my legs remember more than I do, and that seems worth following."

Bram didn't answer. What was there to say? *You climbed a mountain and gave yourself away so a boy you'd known for two weeks could dream again.* She'd started the telling already, up near the shrine, and Emrys had listened the way you listen to a story about a stranger—attentive, courteous, unmoved—and she understood suddenly that she would be telling it for years. That she was, now, the keeper of a life its owner had spent.

Her mother was in the doorway before Bram reached the lane. Of course she was; half of Cinderhollow was in the street, staring at the cooling sky, and her mother had been doing arithmetic of dread since dawn. She caught Bram by the shoulders, turned her around once as if checking for damage, found the empty palm, and made a sound that wasn't quite a sob.

"The shrines went cold," she said. "All of them, all at once, an hour past sundown. The river shrine too—the one that—" She couldn't finish it. She never could finish it. The one that gave Tam back wrong.

"Is he awake?" Bram asked.

"He's been sleeping since noon. Real sleep, the kind where he—" Her mother's face did something terrible and hopeful. "He casts a shadow now, love. He's been casting it since the hour the sky changed."

* * *

Tam lay in his bed by the window looking, for the first time in eight months, entirely asleep.

Bram stood in the doorway of the little room a long time before she let herself cross it. She had learned caution the hard way. For months her brother had occupied this bed like an heirloom himself—present, polished, wrong. He ate what was put in front of him. He answered questions in voices: the neighbor woman's voice, the miller's dead father's voice, once, horribly, Bram's own voice from three winters ago saying words she didn't remember saying. His eyes were right and his shadow was missing, and at night, when the house creaked, he would sit up and watch the door with the patient stillness of something waiting to be collected.

Now he was sprawled the way he'd slept when he was small—diagonal, one arm hanging off the mattress, mouth open, blanket half-kicked away. The moon through the window laid his shape out on the floor beside the bed, gray and complete, shadow attached at the heels like it had never left. His chest rose and fell. His brow was creased, actually creased, over a dream Bram couldn't see but which was, unmistakably, *his*.

She sat down on the edge of the bed, very carefully, and touched his wrist.

"Warm," she whispered, because she needed to say it aloud to make it true.

"Mm." Tam stirred. His eyes opened halfway, unfocused, and then focused, and it was his eyes—eight months of borrowed glances falling away like a shed skin—and they were bleary and annoyed in exactly the way a little brother's eyes should be at being woken. "Bram. Why're you crying?"

"I'm not."

"You are. You've got the whole face going." He yawned hugely. "Something happened. Everyone downstairs is whispering. Ma keeps touching my head like I'm going to bite her." His eyes sharpened then, some animal memory surfacing from the long wrong sleep, and his hand found hers and gripped. "It was you, wasn't it. Whatever it was. It was you."

"How do you know that?"

"Because things were taken out of me and things were given to me and none of it was ever your fault, no matter what you think." He said it sleepily, flatly, the way children state enormous truths and then ask for water. "I could hear you, sometimes. Down there. You were very loud."

Bram laughed—a wet, broken sound—and pulled him upright and held onto him hard enough that he complained about it, and complaining was so perfectly, ordinarily *Tam* that she had to hold on harder, and he let her, patting her back with the embarrassed rhythm of a fourteen-month-younger brother who would deny forever that he'd cried too.

Through the window, over the roofline, she could see the mountain. It stood black against the stars, ordinary, just a mountain. The faint orange glow that had pulsed at its crown for weeks—the glow the miners refused to name and the priests wouldn't discuss—was gone. In its place, snow. Just snow catching starlight.

Somewhere up there, a person she owed everything to was sitting alone in the dark, holding a coat full of pockets they didn't recognize.

* * *

The whole village came out the next morning, because that was what Cinderhollow did—it processed catastrophe through crowds, through shared bread and shared staring. They stood in the square by the dead fountain and listened to the priest attempt a blessing that trailed off uncertainly, and they looked at Bram when they thought she wasn't looking, and they looked longer at the stranger in the borrowed coat who sat on the fountain's rim eating porridge with the careful concentration of someone consulting their body for instructions.

Emrys had come down with her at dawn. Bram hadn't asked them; they'd simply appeared at the door when she left, saying, "Your legs are shaking. Someone should walk behind you in case you fall," which was, she was learning, how this version of Emrys offered friendship: sideways, disguised as logistics.

"They're staring," Emrys murmured, not looking up from the porridge.

"You saved their children. Half this town has somebody the shrines gave back wrong, and now everyone's shadows came home overnight. They want to know who to thank."

"Don't let them thank me." Something flickered across Emrys's face—quick, unreadable even to Bram, who had spent days learning to read it. "I don't know what they'd be thanking."

"That's not how thanking works."

"Then explain it to me sometime." But they said it lightly, and Bram saw the villager nearest them—a quarry widow whose son had come back from the river shrine speaking in her drowned husband's voice, and who had woken that morning weeping and whole—reach out and touch Emrys's sleeve, just touch it, briefly, the way you touch a shrine. Emrys went very still. Then they nodded to her, awkwardly, and the widow smiled through her tears and moved on, and Emrys looked down at their sleeve as though it belonged to someone braver.

Later, on the walk back up toward Vess's cabin—because Emrys had said, in that same sideways way, that they needed to see a mapmaker about a map, that a person with no past should at least know where it had happened—Bram finally asked the question that had been sitting in her chest since the vault.

"What was it like?" She kept her eyes on the path. "When they took it. The memories."

They walked a while in silence. Wind combed the pines below the trail.

"Like being robbed by someone very thorough," Emrys said at last. "I knew things had been there. You can feel the shelves, even when they're empty. There's a shape where a life used to be, the way an amputee feels weather in a missing hand." They paused. "And underneath that—this is the part I haven't told anyone, so I suppose you're the keeper of it too—underneath it there was relief. Whoever I was, whatever I did, the person paying that price seemed glad to be rid of it. The Archivist said something like pity when they accepted. I understand now that the pity wasn't for losing it. It was for wanting to."

"Do you regret it?" Bram asked. Then, quickly: "You don't have to—"

"No. It's the right question." Emrys considered the mountainside, the cold blue distance, the smoke rising thin and domestic from Cinderhollow's chimneys. "Ask me again when I know who's answering."

At the bend in the trail, where the path dropped toward the mapmaker's valley, Emrys stopped. Bram almost walked past before she noticed, and turned, and followed their gaze.

Below, half-hidden in the pines, stood a ruin she'd never noticed before, though she must have passed it a dozen times: tumbled walls, a collapsed roof, the black stub of a chimney. Old. Decades old, maybe more. And around its doorstep, half-buried in moss, stones set in a pattern—she squinted—a spiral, the kind carved on the oldest shrines, the kind that meant *a god was welcomed here.*

Emrys was staring at it with an expression Bram had no name for. Their hand had risen halfway to their chest, to the hollow of the throat, an old instinct reaching for something no longer there.

"I dreamed about that house," they said slowly. "Last night. First dream I've had that I know belongs to me. There was a storm coming off the pass, and someone was calling my name from inside—but it wasn't the name you told me. It was an older one." They looked at Bram, and their eyes were steady, and afraid, and awake. "Bram. If I sold my memories down there—then whoever lived in that house remembers me. And it isn't in any vault."
