## 20. A Stranger in Their Own Life

Bram found them at dawn, half-buried in the scree below the shrine-mountain, like something the storm had spat back out and immediately regretted.

She had been climbing since the sky went grey, Tam's ember warm against her sternum where she'd sewn it into her coat lining, her boots crusted with the ash that still fell in slow, tired flakes from the peak. The mountain had gone quiet in the night—not the quiet of sleep but of a fire burned down to its bed—and Bram had told herself, all the way up the switchbacks, that she would find Emrys Galloway standing at the summit, arms crossed, already annoyed at how long she'd taken.

Instead she found them here. Lying on their back in the loose stone, eyes open, watching the clouds with the flat, patient attention of someone who had never seen a cloud before.

"Emrys?" she said, and her voice cracked on the name.

The person on the ground turned their head. It was Emrys's face—the same sharp, weathered features, the same scar splitting the left eyebrow, the same ink-stain birthmark at the jaw that Bram had memorized over weeks of deep roads and shared fires—but the look behind it was wrong. Empty in a way that wasn't blankness. Blankness was a room with nothing in it. This was a room that had been swept so thoroughly even the dust remembered being furniture.

"Is that my name?" they asked.

The words went through Bram like a pick through river ice.

"You don't—" She stopped. Started again. "You climbed this mountain last night. You called down a storm. You said your name out loud in front of every god left listening." Her hands were shaking; she pressed them flat against her thighs. "That was you. That was *you*."

They sat up slowly, wincing, and looked at their own hands as though checking them for damage. There was damage—burns across both palms, the skin raw and weeping where the lightning had passed through them on its way to the sky—but they examined these with a stranger's clinical interest, tilting them toward the light.

"That must have hurt," they said.

"It did. I watched it."

"Did you?" A faint frown. "I'm sorry. I don't remember you."

*Don't remember you.* Four small words, and each one landed like a stone dropped down a well that never gave back an echo. Bram crouched in the scree, keeping her distance without quite deciding to, and studied them the way she'd once studied her mother's face during the plague winter, looking for signs of the fever before the fever announced itself.

"I'm Bram," she said. "Bram Halloway. Of Cinderhollow. You came through our town in the spring. You were guiding salt caravans through Thornfell, and everyone said no one moved through Thornfell unseen except you, and I found you because I was desperate, and you helped me even though you didn't want to." She swallowed. "We went under the mountain together. Into the Ember Court. You got Tam's soul back. You paid for it with—" Her voice snagged. "With everything. With yourself. That was the price. All the memories anyone ever erased of you, everything you'd buried, the whole hoard of who you were. The Archivist took it. And it worked. Tam woke up. He casts a shadow now. He dreams again."

The stranger listened to all of this with the grave, courteous attention people give to a story about someone else's family.

"That's quite a tale," they said at last. Not unkindly. That was the worst part—that it wasn't unkind. "And you're telling me I'm in it."

"You're not *in* it. You're the whole of it."

They looked out at the valley then, at Cinderhollow's chimneys smoking small and ordinary against the morning, at the river, at the dead shrines along the road whose stones had gone cold and grey overnight. Bram watched them take it in and saw nothing move behind their eyes. No recognition. No grief. Just a person meeting a landscape for the first time.

"There was a storm," they said slowly. "I think there was a storm. My hands say there was a storm." They flexed the burned fingers, hissing softly at the pain. "It's strange. I know what things are. I know that's a kestrel, and that's rowan, and if I needed to, I could read a map or mend a boot or tell you which mushrooms will kill you. But I don't know why I know those things. It's like walking through a house in the dark. Everything's familiar under my hands and none of it is mine."

Bram's throat ached. She thought of the vaults beneath the mountain, the shelves of embers, each one a life reduced to a coal that someone else owned. Somewhere down in that dark, in a court that was now only ash and silence, sat a shelf labeled with a name that no longer belonged to anyone. A shelf full of storms called and villages mourned and a decade spent teaching the world to forget.

*You did that,* she wanted to say. *You chose it. You stood in front of the Archivist and you didn't hesitate, not for one breath, and I hated you for it and I'll never be able to thank you enough.*

What she said was: "Are you hungry?"

*

They ate hardtack and dried plums from her pack, sitting shoulder to shoulder on a flat stone while the sun climbed and the ash stopped falling altogether. The stranger ate neatly, gratefully, asking questions between bites—what was the town called, how far, who lived there—and each question was a small knife, because Bram knew the answers were ones Emrys Galloway had carried for years. Cinderhollow. Three days' walk. Two hundred souls, give or take the plague winter's taking.

At some point the stranger noticed her staring and smiled—a crooked, self-deprecating smile that Bram recognized with a jolt, because it was *his*, it was exactly the smile he'd given her the day she'd first begged him for help, the one that said *I am going to regret this* right before he did the thing anyway.

"You keep looking at me like I'm a ghost," they said.

"You're the opposite of a ghost," Bram said. "A ghost is dead and still remembered. You're alive and nobody remembers. Including you."

"Ah." They considered this. "Which are you? Remembered or remembering?"

"Both, I suppose."

"Then you have me at a disadvantage." They brushed crumbs from their coat—the same salt-stained caravan coat, missing the same button—and looked at her directly for the first time. Their eyes were brown. Bram had always thought of them as storm-grey, but maybe that had been the power showing through. "You said I saved someone. Your brother."

"Tam. He's eleven."

"And what did saving him cost?"

Bram looked away, out at the mountain. "Everything you had."

They were quiet for a long moment. When they spoke again, their voice was careful, the way people speak when they suspect the floor is rotten. "This person. This Emrys. Were they good?"

The question hit her sideways. She'd expected *was I brave*, or *did it work*, or even *do I want to know*. Not *were they good*. Because Emrys hadn't known either—that had been the whole wound of them, the thing they'd carried under all the erasure: the terror that whatever they'd burned away might have been monstrous, that the exile had been deserved. And now the question came back around, asked by the same mouth with no memory of ever asking it before.

"They were stubborn," Bram said. "And grumpy. And they complained constantly about my cooking. They pretended they didn't care about anything, and they were terrible at pretending." She picked at a thread on her sleeve. "They were the best person I've ever met. And I think—I think they spent ten years hoping nobody would ever be able to say that about them."

"Why?"

"Because it's easier to be forgotten if you deserve it."

The stranger absorbed this. Bram watched them do it—watched the information settle into them like rain into dry ground, finding channels that had been carved long ago even though the water itself was gone. Whatever the Archivist had taken, it hadn't taken the shape of the person. The shape remained. Only the contents were missing.

"All right," they said finally. "So. You're the only person in the world who knows who I am."

"Yes."

"What happens now?"

And there it was. The weight she'd been carrying since dawn, since before dawn, since the moment she'd seen them lying in the scree and understood what her brother's waking life had cost. Because she could do two things, and both of them were terrible.

She could tell them everything. Every night of the deep roads, every lesson about the mountains' hidden grammar, every moment the alliance between them had softened into something like trust. She could hand them back their own life piece by piece, testimony instead of memory, and watch them carry a stranger's history like a pack that didn't fit. Or she could say nothing. Let Emrys Galloway stay dead and let this new person walk into Cinderhollow clean, unbought, owing nothing to any god or girl. Start fresh. Be free in the way Emrys had spent ten years clawing toward and never reached.

But that would be another erasure. And Bram had learned, down in the dark under the mountain, what erasure cost and who ended up paying it.

"I don't know," she admitted. "I've been trying to decide since I saw you."

"Decide what?"

"Whether to tell you."

They tilted their head. "Why wouldn't you?"

"Because it hurts," Bram said. "Knowing what you lost. I watched you know it, before. You used to wake up some mornings with your fists clenched like you'd been holding onto something all night and couldn't remember what. You said—" She stopped. Her eyes were burning and she made herself go on. "You said forgetting was the only peace you'd ever managed to build, and you'd built it with your own hands, stone by stone, and you'd burn down anyone who tried to dig into it. And then you dug into it yourself. On purpose. For Tam. For me." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, rough, angry at the tears. "So if I tell you everything, I'm just knocking down the wall you died building. And if I don't tell you, then you're nobody, and you didn't get to be anybody, and that's not fair either."

The stranger was silent through all of it. Then, gently: "Did I die?"

"No. That's the thing. You're the only one who didn't die. Everyone else did, a little. Everyone who knew you."

"Hm." They looked at their burned hands again, turning them over. "Can I ask you something, Bram Halloway of Cinderhollow?"

"Yes."

"When you found me just now—before you said my name, before any of it—what were you going to do? If I'd been dead?"

"Carry you home," Bram said. "Bury you next to the shrine your storm killed. Tell everyone you were a salt merchant. Nobody would have questioned it."

Something flickered across the stranger's face—too fast to name, gone before it landed. But Bram caught it, and her heart lurched, because it had looked almost like grief, and grief needs a memory to stand on.

"A salt merchant," they repeated. "That's very practical of you."

"Emrys taught me practical."

"Emrys," they said, tasting the name. "It doesn't fit in my mouth yet. Like a word in a language I used to speak." They exhaled. "Tell me."

Bram blinked. "Tell you what?"

"Everything. All of it. The parts that hurt especially." They turned to face her fully, and there was something in their expression now that Bram recognized from the deep roads, from the tollgate, from the top of the burning mountain—the same stubborn set of the jaw she'd followed into hell. "You said I built a wall out of forgetting. Well. I can't build it anymore, can I? The stones are gone. So I'd rather have the truth than the hole where it should be. Even if the truth belongs to somebody else." A pause. "Even if I'm somebody else."

"It's not somebody else," Bram whispered. "That's what nobody understands. You're still you. The shape of you. I'd know you anywhere."

"Then tell me who I am," the stranger said, "and I'll try to grow into it."

*

They started down the mountain in the late morning, Bram leading, the stranger following in her footsteps through the ash-drifts. She began at the beginning, because Emrys had taught her that too: *always start where the road starts, never where you wish it did.*

She told them about the pass that shouldn't exist, and the dogs that wouldn't enter the shrine cave. About the drowned child walking out of the river at Cinderhollow, dripping and smiling. About the voice in the well, and the bargain she'd made when she was thirteen and terrified, and the brother who stopped dreaming. She told it all in order, and the stranger walked beside her and listened, and twice they laughed in the right places, and three times they went pale in the right places, and Bram realized somewhere past midday that the story was doing something to them—that the shape of Emrys Galloway was filling in, detail by detail, the way a drawing fills in when you work the charcoal back and forth.

Once, near the tree line, the stranger stopped walking.

"The well," they said. "The voice in the well. What did it sound like?"

Bram frowned. "Like embers settling. Like a fire that talks."

They stood very still, head cocked, listening to something Bram couldn't hear. Then they shook themselves and kept walking, and when she asked what it was, they said, "Nothing. A bird." But their burned hands had curled into fists, and they didn't open them for a mile.

Below them, Cinderhollow spread out in the afternoon light—smoke rising straight from the chimneys, the river running clear for the first time in a season, and on the road into town, a small figure running toward them, casting a long shadow on the packed earth, shouting a name that wasn't Bram's.

It was Tam. And he was shouting *Emrys, Emrys, Emrys*, and the stranger beside her stopped dead, watching the boy come, and Bram heard them breathe:

"Why does that child know me?"
