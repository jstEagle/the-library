## 21. The Telling

The mountain let them go slowly, the way a fist opens finger by finger.

Bram walked first, because she knew the way down and Emrys did not—or rather, Emrys's feet knew it and the rest of them did not. She kept glancing back over her shoulder at the tall figure following her through the pines, stepping exactly where she stepped, pausing when she paused, as if her path were the only thread holding them together. Which, she supposed, it was.

The stranger wore Emrys Galloway's face the way you wear someone else's coat. The bones were right. The storm-grey eyes were right, though they had lost something Bram couldn't name—some banked light that used to live behind them, like coals under ash. But the person inside moved wrong: hesitant where Emrys had been certain, startled where Emrys had been unsurprisable. Ten days since the solstice. Ten days since the storm had come down off the shrine-mountain like a judgment and taken everything Emrys was and poured it into the hungry dark below Cinderhollow.

Ten days of searching the scree fields and the pine slopes before Bram found them sitting at the tree line, watching the sunrise with the polite interest of someone seeing their first one.

"Careful here," Bram said now, pointing at a shelf of wet stone. "There's a drop on the left. Emrys nearly broke an ankle on it last autumn, coming up in the dark."

"Did I?" the stranger said. There was no irony in it. They said their own name the way you'd repeat a word in a foreign language—carefully, testing whether it would hold weight.

"So you say." Bram picked her way across the shelf. "You told me once you knew every goat track in Thornfell. That was before, though. Before you—" She stopped herself. She'd been stopping herself for ten days.

"Before I gave it away," Emrys finished quietly. "You can say it. You're the only one who can."

* * *

They made camp that night in the hollow beneath a leaning boulder, a place Bram knew because Emrys had shown it to her on the climb up, a lifetime ago—eleven days ago, which felt like two different lives measured against each other. Bram built the fire the way Emrys had taught her, feathering the kindling into a bird's nest, feeding it breath instead of blowing at it like a fool.

"You're doing that wrong," she heard herself say, and then laughed, because there was no wrong anymore. There was only what she remembered.

Emrys watched her hands work. "Show me again."

So she did. And while the fire took, she began the telling—not for the first time, but the first time all the way through, without interruption, without the stranger flinching away from some part of it. She started where it started for her, which was not where it started for Emrys at all.

"It was the plague winter," she said. "Two years back. You know that part—you've heard it twice already, so stop me if I get dull."

"I won't get bored," Emrys said. "I don't have anything to be bored with."

That was the terrible thing, Bram thought, the thing she kept having to look at sideways like a bright coal: this person was new. Brand-new, eleven days old, wearing forty years of somebody else's history like clothes that hadn't been tailored yet. Everything they knew about themselves came out of her mouth. If she lied, they would believe the lie. If she forgot, it would never have happened.

She resolved, again, not to lie.

"There was a voice in the well behind our house. It came up warm, like bread smell, like the oven my gran used to keep. It said it could spare us—all four of us, Mam and Da and Tam and me—if we gave it something to hold. Something precious. I thought—I was twelve, I thought it meant a tooth. A lock of hair. Some coin." Her hands had gone still around a stick she didn't remember picking up. "It asked me to say Tam's name three times and give it with love. That was all. Love was the currency. I didn't know that until later, when I saw him at the table and he wasn't—"

She stopped. Breathed.

"He wasn't there anymore. His body was. He ate and slept and walked to the mine with Da, but he cast no shadow, and when he spoke it was other people's words, things our neighbors had said days before, played back wrong. And he never dreamed. Mam said a boy his age should dream, should talk in his sleep, should wake up telling stories. Tam just woke. Like a lamp going on."

Across the fire, Emrys listened with an intensity that hurt to look at. Not pity. Something more careful than that—the attention of someone trying to memorize a story they'd been handed the responsibility for keeping.

"And then you came to Cinderhollow," Emrys prompted gently. "With the salt caravan."

"With the salt caravan. You had mud on your boots and a face like you were apologizing for existing. Nobody noticed you but me. I don't know why I noticed you. Maybe because I was already half-erased myself, by then. Guilt does that." She fed the stick to the flames. "You looked at the mark on my palm and went white as bone. And then you said—" she closed her eyes, retrieving it, wanting to get it exact— "you said, 'Who told you it was allowed to be your fault?'"

Emrys said nothing. The fire popped.

"That's when you decided," Bram went on. "You tried everything else first. You called a storm to scour the mark off me, and the lightning bent toward you instead and whispered your own name back at you—that shook you, I could see it shake you, you went and sat alone on the ridge for half a day. Then you found Vess, the blind mapmaker, and paid a memory to learn the deep road down. You never told me which memory. Vess told me after. It was—" Bram hesitated. This part she carried like a stone in her pocket, turning it over and over. "Vess said the memory was of a woman teaching you to whistle through cupped hands so the wind would answer. Your mother, maybe. Or someone who stood where a mother stands. You didn't know you had it until it was gone."

The stranger's hand drifted to their chest, an unconscious gesture Bram had seen a dozen times in ten days, as if checking whether anything was still kept in there.

"Then we went down," she said. "Through the smuggler's spine. You taught me the mountains' grammar—which stones ring hollow, which echoes are honest. You taught me how to sleep cold and walk tired and lie to a tollgate. At the gate of names you cut your palm open because you had no true name left to surrender, and you lied to a god's face with blood on your teeth, and it let us through." She smiled despite herself. "You swore the whole way. Terrible language. I wrote some of it down."

"Did you," Emrys said faintly.

"In my head. It seemed important not to lose any of you." The smile faded. Inside the court, she told him then—the galleries of burning names, the shelves of embers, each one a soul reduced to a keepsake, heirlooms of a hungry family of gods. She told him how she'd gotten lost and found Tam's ember, small and cold and still somehow hers, still faintly warm in the shape of her brother's laugh. How she'd stolen it and rung every alarm in the dark beneath the world.

And she told him the rest. The hunt. The mark opening like a wound on her palm as the solstice rose. Vess's last map, pressed into her hands by blind fingers: *the court runs on worship, girl, and worship runs uphill to whoever the gods loved best.* The climb to the shrine-mountain while the sky burned orange and something ancient began pulling her apart at the seams, using her body as a door.

"And you," Bram said, "climbed the other side."

* * *

Emrys listened to their own life the way you listen to a song in a tongue you almost remember.

The strange thing—the thing they could not say aloud, because saying it might make it truer than the story—was that none of it felt like anything. Bram spoke of storms called down, of gods defied, of a name shouted from a mountaintop into the ears of every living soul in Thornfell, and the words landed on Emrys like rain on glass. True, probably. Coherent, certainly. Told with the stubborn, furious honesty of a fourteen-year-old who had decided that accuracy was the only gift she had left to give. But it was a story about a stranger named Emrys Galloway, and the stranger named Emrys Galloway was standing right here, empty-handed, holding nothing but the shape of it.

*I climbed the mountain,* they thought. *Why?*

Because Bram said so. Because Bram said the court wanted her body and Emrys had chosen to stand in the doorway instead. Because Bram said the old gods had once loved them—had *loved* them, the way you love a favorite child—and that Emrys had burned their own name to escape that love, long ago, before the exile, before the salt roads, before all of it. A storm that killed a village. A god who ordered it. A sentence passed on oneself.

None of it ached. That frightened Emrys more than anything else. A person ought to ache at their own tragedy, even secondhand. Instead there was only a vast, clean quiet, like a room after the furniture has been sold.

But here was the other thing, the thing Emrys turned over in the dark after Bram's fire had sunk to embers and her breathing had gone slow and even:

They believed her.

Not because the story rang true—though it did, horribly, in its details, in the way she described the well-warm voice, the borrowed words in her brother's mouth. They believed her because of how she told it. Nobody invents a sacrifice this specific. Nobody fabricates the detail about the whistling memory, or the blood at the tollgate, or the exact oath-word Emrys supposedly shouted when the storm answered. Those details cost something to carry. Bram carried them like water through a desert, hoarding every drop, and she had walked this mountain up and down for eleven days looking for a stranger she owed nothing to, purely so the story would not die with her.

If that was what being remembered meant—if this fierce, stubborn girl was what Emrys Galloway had purchased with forty years of self—then the trade, whatever it had bought, had bought something good.

*Choose it,* Emrys thought, lying awake under the leaning boulder with the stars moving slowly over Thornfell. *You cannot feel it. So choose it.*

It was not memory. It was not even faith, quite. It was more like taking a hand offered across a chasm: you cannot know the other side will hold, but you close your fingers anyway, because the alternative is the fall.

Tomorrow, they decided, they would ask her to tell it again. From the beginning. All of it. And the next day, and the day after, as many times as she could stand, until the story wore grooves in them, until it fit, until *Emrys Galloway* stopped being a coat and became skin. Identity built from testimony, plank by plank, nail by nail. Other people had their pasts given to them whole; this one would have theirs assembled, lovingly, by a girl with a guilt-shaped hole in her heart and the bravest way of telling the truth Emrys had ever heard described.

They closed their eyes. For the first time since waking on the mountainside, the quiet inside them did not feel like absence.

It felt like room.

* * *

They reached the lower slopes on the twelfth day, where the pines thinned and the smoke of Cinderhollow's chimneys stood up straight and grey against the morning, and Bram's steps slowed without her meaning them to.

Tam was down there. Whole now—she'd seen it with her own eyes, the ember going back into him the moment the court glutted itself on Emrys's surrendered years, the shadow snapping onto his heels like a dog come home. He dreamed again. He talked in his sleep, nonsense and wonder, and Mam cried into her apron, and Da set an extra place at the table out of pure habit and then laughed at himself and kept it anyway—for Emrys, though Emrys wasn't there, though nobody knew where Emrys was except a girl walking down a mountain.

"They'll know you," Bram said. She had stopped at the edge of the trees, where the road began. "That's the thing I have to warn you about. Everyone. The night you spoke your name, it went into everybody. The miners, the miller, old Widow Marsh who forgets her own sons' faces—they all remember you now. Every prayer anyone ever whispered at those shrines, they'll remember you answering. You'll never be invisible again. Not for one hour. Not anywhere in Thornfell."

Emrys stood very still. Bram watched them take that in—the death sentence of it, or what would have been a death sentence, once, to a person who had spent a decade scrubbing themselves out of the world. Their face did something complicated. Settled.

"Good," they said.

Bram blinked. "Good?"

"I'm told I chose this." The corner of their mouth moved—not quite a smile, but the ground where a smile would build. "I'd like to find out why."

They walked down into Cinderhollow together, and the town turned out along the road the way it does for a funeral or a festival, nobody sure which, everyone staring, every eye full of a person Bram had spent weeks knowing and they had known for eleven days and somehow all of them knew better: *that's the storm-caller, that's the one the gods loved, that's the one who paid.* Widow Marsh pressed a heel of bread into Emrys's hand. The miller's boys gaped. Somewhere a dog barked itself hoarse.

And Tam pushed through the crowd—her brother, her whole brother, casting a long afternoon shadow—and stopped in front of Emrys, and looked at them with the grave attention of someone examining a debt.

"You're smaller than the storm," Tam said.

"So I'm told."

"Good." He nodded once, the way boys do when the world has been put back in order. "Storms shouldn't be people."

Bram laughed until she had to sit down on the roadside wall, and Emrys sat beside her, bread in hand, remembered by everyone, powerful in nothing but presence, and watched the sun go gold over Thornfell.

"Mam's set you a place at the table," Bram said, when she could speak. "Every night since. You could—well. You could stay. If strangers are allowed to stay."

"I'm not a stranger," Emrys said. "You told me who I am. That makes me whatever you say I am." They turned the bread over in their hands, considering, and then added, in the careful voice of a person testing new ground: "Tell me again about the storm. The part where I shouted the name. I want to hear it while there's still light."

So Bram began the telling again, there on the wall, with the whole town listening, and Emrys Galloway leaned in to receive their own life, one testimony at a time—and in the lantern window of the house at the end of the lane, unseen by either of them, a single coal from the dead court sat glowing faintly in the dark, as if somewhere far below, something had turned over in its sleep.
