## 22. Ember, Kept

The first frost of autumn came to Cinderhollow on a morning when Emrys was mending a fence.

That was the kind of thing their life was made of now: fences, and the mending of them. Splinters and wire and the particular gray light that came down the valley before the sun cleared Thornfell's shoulder. They worked with bare hands because their hands had forgotten how to be anything but ordinary, and there was a strange honesty in that. A splinter did not care what name you used to carry. It went in just as deep for storm-callers as for anyone.

"Emrys!" Bram's voice carried up from the lane, breathless in the way of someone who had run the whole way from town for no reason worth running for. "Emrys, the Hendersons' roof is off, and Mrs. Colm says the shrine-stone's gone warm again—"

"It's not warm," Emrys said, without looking up. "It's never warm. It's just that everyone puts their hands on it expecting warmth."

Bram reached the fence and leaned on it, sixteen now and taller than she wanted to admit, her braid coming loose the way braids did when their owners climbed hills at a dead sprint. She was still, at heart, the girl who had sold her brother's soul to survive a plague winter—but the guilt had changed shape over the seasons, the way grief does. It had stopped being a stone she swallowed and become something more like a joint that ached before rain. Part of her. Useful, in its way. It told her when storms were coming, or when strangers lied, or when someone at market needed feeding more than they needed to admit it.

"The stone's warm," she insisted. "I put my hand on it myself."

"You put your hand on it after four hundred pilgrims did the same. Everything's warm if enough people touch it." Emrys set down the pliers and finally looked at her. "Show me."

She held out her palm—the left one, where the contract-mark had once burned like a brand. The skin there was smooth and unremarkable now, healed clean the day the court went quiet. But she turned it over the same way she had when she was fourteen and terrified, and Emrys felt the old ache of responsibility move through them like weather.

They took her hand. The stone was not warm. Nothing was warm. The old gods slept so deep now that even their dreams had stopped leaking up through the mountain's bones, and Cinderhollow had spent two years arguing about what to do with its answered shrines. Some wanted them pulled down. Most, in the end, had simply started leaving flowers on them, the way you might tend the grave of something fierce and beautiful that had once, briefly, loved you back.

"It's cold," Bram admitted. "I just—it felt like it should be warm. With the frost coming and all. Like an ending wants marking."

Emrys released her hand and picked the splinters from their palm, considering. "Endings don't need marking," they said. "They mark themselves. That's rather the problem with them."

* * *

The lantern hung by the door of Emrys's house, and inside it sat a coal.

Not firewood, not char—a coal the size of a walnut, black-red at its heart, taken from the vaults beneath Thornfell on the day everything ended. Tam's ember had gone home to Tam; the rest of the court's hoard had guttered out like a thousand candles snuffed by one long exhale, souls dispersing into whatever waits beyond keeping. But this one coal had remained on the Archivist's desk, still faintly lit, and Emrys had picked it up without knowing why, and the Archivist—with something like pity, always something like pity—had let them.

It gave no heat. It gave no light you could read by. But it glowed, very softly, in the dark hours before dawn, and sometimes—if you watched it long enough—it pulsed. Slowly. Patiently. Like something breathing in its sleep.

Emrys had learned not to watch it too long.

The house itself was nothing much: two rooms and a loft, stone walls, a roof Bram's father had helped re-thatch in exchange for Emrys fixing the well pump nobody else could make sense of. That was the economy of being known. You couldn't pay people in secrets anymore, or in fear, or in the careful currency of being overlooked. You paid in labor and honesty and showing up, and somehow it was enough. Somehow it was more than enough.

Marta Colm brought bread on Thursdays whether Emrys needed it or not. Old Henderson called them *the weather-witch* in a tone that had shed its bitterness years ago and become pure affection. Children trailed them through the market asking for stories about the mountain, and Emrys gave them carefully wrong ones—tales of giants and goats and a great stone badger who guarded the pass—and the children knew the tales were wrong and loved them anyway, because the truth was that Emrys Galloway had stood on the shrine-mountain two winters past and spoken a name aloud, and the sky had answered, and every soul in Cinderhollow remembered it.

Every soul in Cinderhollow remembered *them*.

That was the miracle and the wound of it, wound together so tightly Emrys could no longer tell which strand was which. When they walked into the Wool and Whistle, heads turned, and voices said their name—*Emrys, over here, Emrys, did you hear about the weather-witch*—and each time it happened, some small extinct thing inside them flinched toward shadow that was no longer there to receive it. They had spent ten years teaching the world to forget them, and the world had forgotten, perfectly, completely, and then in one night of storm and sacrifice they had unmade all of it, and now they were the most remembered person in the valley.

And they had no memory of doing any of it.

That was the trade, in the end. The court had taken the hoard—all of it, every erased year, the whole buried archive of who Emrys Galloway had been before the burning—and Emrys had woken on the mountainside with frost in their hair and nothing behind their eyes but the present tense. Bram had found them there. Fourteen years old, half-frozen herself, her brother's soul tucked against her chest like a stolen egg, and she had looked down at this stranger who had saved her family and seen exactly what they could not: the size of the price.

She had been telling them the story ever since.

* * *

Winter settled in properly by the week's end, and with it came the long evenings, and with those came the ritual that had become as fixed in Emrys's household as bread or prayer: Bram arrived after supper, took the chair nearest the stove, and talked.

Tonight she came in shaking snow from her shoulders and carrying a jar of Tam's honey—the real Tam, who was seventeen now and apprenticed to the cooper, who cast a proper shadow and dreamed his own dreams and had only the vaguest, kindest memory of a winter he'd spent somewhere else. He never spoke of it. Once, in the first spring, he had come to Emrys's door and stood there a long moment, and then said, quietly, "I remember glowing. Just that. Like being a word somebody was reading." And then he'd gone home and never mentioned it again, and Emrys had understood that this was mercy, both directions, and let it lie.

"He says hello," Bram reported, setting the honey on the table. "He says your bees are dying of gossip and you should split the east hive before the thaw."

"Your brother takes an unusual interest in my apiary."

"My brother takes an unusual interest in everything lately. I think—" She paused, settling into her chair, and Emrys watched her choose her next words with the care she'd learned from two years of telling a stranger their own story. "I think some of him remembers what it was to be kept. On a shelf. So now he keeps things. Bees, barrels, the whole town's business. He likes knowing where everything is. That it's safe. That it's *his*."

Emrys nodded slowly. They had noticed. They suspected Tam had noticed them noticing. There existed between the three of them an understanding too large and too tender for words, and so they mostly discussed bees.

Bram's eyes went, as they always eventually did, to the lantern.

The coal pulsed in the gathering dark. Slow. Patient. Breathing its long sleep-breath.

"Do you ever think about throwing it out?" she asked. She asked this perhaps twice a year, always in winter, always when the coal seemed brightest.

"Often."

"But you don't."

"No."

"Why not?"

Emrys rose and crossed to the lantern. This close, in the quiet, you could see the coal's light shift—not steady, but cycling, dimming and brightening across some interval too long to hold in the mind. A tide. A heartbeat slowed almost to stopping.

"Because I traded away my memory of the price," they said. "All of it. I know the shape of what I paid because you've told me, and I believe you, and believing isn't nothing—but I can't *feel* it. Do you understand? You've described the storm on the mountain until I dream about it, but I dream it like a story heard from a stranger. There's no weight under it. Somewhere in me there ought to be ten years of exile and a dead village and a god I served and betrayed, and instead there's—" they gestured at their own chest, at the strange clean emptiness of it, "—room. Just room. The coal is the only thing I own that remembers what I forgot. Throwing it out would be finishing the job. Making the erasure total. And I won't." Their voice stayed level, but barely. "Somebody should keep account. Even if the somebody is a lump of dead god-fire in a lantern."

Bram was quiet for a while. Snow ticked against the window. The stove breathed its small warmth into the room, and outside, somewhere up the valley, a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"Mrs. Colm asked about you again today," she said at last, in a different tone entirely, and Emrys laughed despite themself.

"Mrs. Colm has been asking about me since spring. She wants me to fix her millwheel, not court me."

"She wants both, and you know it. Widow Colm with her good bread and her flint-gray eyes. Half the town's betting on it."

"The whole town should find better entertainment than a weather-witch with no weather."

But Bram wasn't smiling anymore. She leaned forward, elbows on knees, and looked at Emrys with the directness that had terrified gods and brokers alike—that flat, stubborn, plague-winter steadiness that no amount of growing up had softened.

"I want to ask you something and I want a real answer."

"All right."

"Do you regret it?"

The question filled the room the way questions do when they've waited years to be spoken. Emrys turned back to the lantern. The coal breathed.

They thought about what they actually knew. Not believed—knew, in the body's ledger, where the only honest accounting lived. They knew mornings: waking in a house where neighbors waved, in a valley that needed them, with a name that people said warmly and often and to their face. They knew Bram at sixteen, laughing, alive, her family whole around a table that had long since lost its habit of setting extra places. They knew Tam's shadow falling long and ordinary across the cooperyard snow. They knew the shrines cooling, the mountain sleeping, the terrible hungry attention of the old gods withdrawn from the world like a hand drawn back from a flame.

Against that, they knew—an absence. Ten years of it. A village they had drowned, a god they had served, a self they had burned, all of it gone where the court's glutted fires went. They could not miss what they could not remember. Grief requires a shape, and theirs had been confiscated.

But Bram remembered it for them. That was the arrangement, unspoken these two years: she carried the story, and they carried the life, and between them they made up one whole person.

"No," Emrys said. And then, because it deserved better than a word: "Listen. Survival was never meant to be free. Nobody tells you that when you're young—they tell you about heroes and happy endings, and nobody mentions that staying alive, *really* staying alive, costs something every single day. Before, I paid in silence. In being nobody. It felt cheap because I'd already paid the true price once and forgotten the receipt. Now—" they touched the lantern glass, cool beneath their fingers, the coal pulsing against their palm like a second heartbeat, "—now I pay in being known. In being *this*. A woman who mends fences and gets courted over millwheels and can't call a cloud, much less a storm. It cost me everything I was. And what I am now is—" they searched for it, honestly searched, because Bram deserved honesty and had earned it, "—happy. Small, and known, and happy. So no. I don't regret it. I regret that I can't feel the size of what I gave. That's different. That's the one wound that didn't close. But regret needs a *before* to weigh it against, and my before belongs to the coal."

Bram wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, as if annoyed at them.

"You're going to make me cry in your kitchen again," she said. "Second time this month."

"Third."

"Shut up." She laughed, wetly, and then grew serious in the way that was hers alone—the gravity of a girl who had bargained with gods at fourteen and never quite lost the habit of weighing souls. "There's one more thing. Why I ran up here tonight, besides the stone and the gossip."

"Go on."

"Marta Colm's millwheel," Bram said. "It's not jammed. I looked. Someone's wedged iron in the sluice—deliberate—old iron, and it's got marks on it. Marks like the ones that used to be on my palm." She met Emrys's eyes. "And down by the river this morning, Emrys, there were footprints in the frost that started nowhere and went somewhere. I counted them twice. They start in the middle of the empty field, like whoever made them *arrived* out of the air." She pulled something from her coat pocket and laid it on the table between them: a sliver of rusted metal, etched faintly with a spiral that Emrys had never seen and yet recognized the way you recognize your own handwriting in someone else's book.

"And that's not all," Bram said quietly. "When I touched it—the coal in your lantern flickered. From clear across the square. I saw it happen."

On its shelf by the door, the lantern pulsed. Once. Twice. Faster now, its long sleep-breath quickening toward wakefulness, and in its glow Emrys Galloway—who could not remember the price of survival and had chosen to pay it anyway—felt something stir in the room's dark corners that was almost, almost a voice.

Somewhere beneath the mountain, very far down, something that had been fed ten years of one woman's entire past opened one ancient eye.

And began, softly, to say their name.
