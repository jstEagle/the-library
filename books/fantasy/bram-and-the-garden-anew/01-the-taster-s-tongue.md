## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The poison tasted like winter.

Not the clean cold of first frost, which Zephyrine Marchetti might have forgiven—it was the other kind of winter, the one that crept under doors and into lungs, the damp rot of a season overstaying its welcome. It sat on her tongue beneath the honeyed quince paste like a whisper beneath music, faint as a coin dropped in another room. Bitterroot, she thought. Aged in iron. Someone had been patient with it.

She kept her face still. Seven years of practice lived in that stillness: the small muscles around her eyes, the set of her jaw, the careful nothing of her expression. She swallowed, bowed her head as the protocol demanded, and waited out the twenty heartbeats that would tell the world whether Zeph Marchetti lived or died.

Her heart beat twenty times. Twenty-one. She did not die.

"Proceed," said Mistress Halvard, the taster-mistress, from her post by the servants' door, and Zeph lifted the little silver spoon again and took a second measure of the quince paste, because the first taste was never enough to be certain, and because certainty was the only currency a food-taster carried into the Hall of Mirrors on the night of the Autumn Court.

Around her, the banquet assembled itself the way weather assembles—gradually, then all at once. Footmen in livery the color of deep water moved between the long tables with platters balanced on their palms like sleeping birds. The chandeliers threw light down through a hundred cut crystals, and each crystal caught it and broke it again, so that the whole ceiling seemed to rain small bright fragments that never quite reached the floor. The Hall of Mirrors earned its name honestly: every wall was glass upon glass, and everywhere Zeph looked she saw herself multiplied—a dozen small women in a dozen grey dresses, all of them holding silver spoons, all of them pretending not to be afraid.

She was good at pretending. She had made an art of it, the way other people made art of embroidery or lying.

The poison troubled her more than the fear did. Not its presence—poison at court was as common as candle smoke—but its *shape*. She could taste things other people couldn't; she'd known that since she was a child licking rain off slate roofs in a village whose name she no longer said aloud. Where others tasted salt or sweetness, she tasted... more. The ghost of what a thing had been. The intention folded inside it, faint as breath on glass. Bread baked in grief tasted different from bread baked in joy. She had learned early that saying such things aloud got you looked at, and being looked at was the beginning of the end, and so she had spent seven years teaching her tongue to lie.

But this poison had a signature she almost recognized. Beneath the bitterroot there was something else, something green and old, like the smell of a garden after rain—and that was impossible, because nothing green had touched the royal kitchens in years. The crown did not permit living plants within the palace walls. Hadn't for generations. There was a law about it, one of those laws so old nobody remembered why it existed, only that it did.

Zeph put the thought away the way she put everything away: carefully, in the dark, where it couldn't grow.

* * *

Queen Isolde entered at the ninth bell.

Zeph knew the queen the way everyone in Vellamare knew her—from proclamations nailed to doors, from coins pressed into palms, from the portrait in the customs house where Her Majesty's painted eyes followed dockworkers and merchants alike with a gaze like a ledger being balanced. In person she was smaller than her portrait and colder than her coin. She wore white, because white was the color of the dynasty's purity, they said, though Zeph had always thought it looked more like the color of things bleached of everything they used to be.

The queen took her seat beneath the canopy of state, and the banquet began in earnest, and Zeph began the work that would keep her alive and invisible for one more night.

It was simple work, in theory. Taste everything meant for the queen's table before it reached the queen's table. Wait the proper interval. Report. In practice it was a performance stretched across four hours, because the real skill of a taster was not detecting poison but detecting it *without seeming to look*, without hesitating over the wrong dish, without letting your attention snag anywhere the courtiers might notice and wonder at. Wonder was dangerous. Wonder turned into questions, and questions turned into the Pale Tower, and the Pale Tower turned people into names on lists that were never seen again.

So Zeph moved through the courses like water finding its level. The oysters in vinegar—clean, briny, honest. The pheasant glazed with rowan-berry—rich, unspoiled, though the glaze carried a thread of melancholy she chose not to examine. The wine from the southern terraces, decanted with ceremony—she held it on her tongue longer than she should have, chasing something, and caught herself, and let it go.

The quince paste came to the queen's place third among the sweets, on a dish of blue porcelain that some ambassador had gifted and some chamberlain had approved. Zeph had already tasted it. She stood now at her station along the wall with the other palace staff, hands folded, spine straight, wearing the particular blankness that servants learn to wear—the face that says *I am furniture, I am wallpaper, I am nothing worth remembering.*

She watched the dish travel toward the queen anyway. She couldn't help it. That was the flaw in her art, the crack down the center of her: she could make her face lie but her attention always betrayed her, drifting toward the thing that mattered like a plant bending toward light.

The dish reached the table beside the queen's elbow. A footman lifted the lid. The blue porcelain gleamed.

And Zeph felt it happen—not with her tongue this time but somewhere behind her sternum, in the place where her hidden magic lived, the place she kept walled and dark and fed on nothing. The wall shivered. Something in the hall's air leaned toward that little dish the way a crowd leans toward a falling man.

Bitterroot aged in iron. Enough to stop a heart in the space between one course and the next. Enough, if the timing was right, to be blamed on the ambassador who had gifted the porcelain, or the chamberlain who had approved it, or the cook, or the taster.

Enough to start a war, if someone wanted a war. Or a purge. Purges were fashionable this season.

Zeph's mouth went dry. She ran the arithmetic of survival. If she said nothing and the queen died, the investigation would land on the kitchen, and the kitchen landed on the tasters, and tasters who failed were hanged with a swiftness that passed for mercy. If she spoke and named the dish, she saved the queen—and drew the eyes of the entire court onto a grey-dressed girl nobody had ever bothered to remember, which was its own kind of death, slower but just as certain. Gifted persons were being registered now. Registered today, summoned tomorrow, taken the day after. Everyone in the night market whispered about it. Everyone pretended the whispering wasn't happening.

There was a third path. There was always a third path, narrow as a knife's edge, and she had walked it a hundred times in smaller rooms: report the poison, claim instinct, claim luck, claim anything but the truth.

She was moving before she finished deciding. That was how she survived, mostly—by not giving herself time to be afraid properly.

"Mistress Halvard." Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "The quince. There's something wrong with the quince."

Halvard turned. She was a broad woman gone to iron-grey, with forearms like a laundress and eyes that had watched forty years of banquets and buried three tasters. "Explain."

"I can't explain. It's—" Zeph let a beat pass, let uncertainty show, because uncertainty was believable and precision was not. "The smell. When they lifted the lid. There was a smell underneath the honey. Like well-water gone bad. I've tasted it before, in—" she cast, quickly, "—in a fish stall that lost a customer last spring. Instinct. But my instincts keep me employed."

It was a good lie. It had a body to it, a history, a dead customer. Lies worked best when you dressed them in true clothes.

Halvard looked at her for a long moment. Then she raised two fingers, and a footman intercepted the dish four feet from the queen's hand, and the Hall of Mirrors did what halls of mirrors do when something breaks the pattern: it noticed.

Conversations thinned. Faces turned. Somewhere a fork paused above a plate with a small ringing note. Queen Isolde did not turn—queens did not turn for servants—but her gaze slid sideways along the table, unhurried, and settled on the commotion with the mild interest of someone watching weather approach from a very great height.

A man detached himself from the wall near the canopy. He wore the dove-grey of the Queen's Vigilance, and his face had the smoothness of a thing sanded down on purpose. He took the dish, uncorked a vial from his belt, and let three drops fall onto the quince paste.

The paste turned the color of old bruises.

The hall exhaled all at once, a thousand small gasps braided into one sound. The footman went white. The ambassador from the neighboring kingdom—whoever he was, whoever had planned this—began, with impressive speed, to look confused and concerned, arranging his features into the mask of a man learning terrible news.

And Zeph stood against the wall with her hands folded and her heartbeat slamming against her ribs, and understood that she had just done the most visible thing she had done in seven years.

* * *

They thanked her, in the way of courts, which is to say they punished her for it.

First came the chamberlain, brisk and sweating, who told her she had rendered a great service to the crown and would be compensated, and whose eyes never once rested on her face—they skated past it, cataloguing her, the way a man catalogs a horse he is considering buying or putting down.

Then came the Vigilance officer, the smooth-faced one, who asked her name and wrote it down. Zeph Marchetti. Age twenty-four. Employed nine months in the palace kitchens, previously at the Gilded Perch tavern, previously—here she gave him the careful fiction she maintained, a village in the hill country, parents dead of fever, no trade but tasting, no talents but a good nose.

"A good nose," the officer repeated. His voice was pleasant. Everything about him was pleasant, the way ice is pleasant to look at from indoors. "You smelled bitterroot through sealed porcelain and a honey glaze."

"The lid wasn't sealed properly." She shrugged, small and apologetic. "Honey holds smells. Anyone might have caught it."

"Anyone," he agreed. "Yet anyone didn't. You did." He smiled, and the smile reached nowhere near his eyes. "You have a remarkable gift, Mistress Marchetti."

The word *gift* went through her like a needle finding the gap between ribs. She kept her face soft, her shoulders rounded, her whole self arranged into harmlessness. "My mother called it a curse. Said I'd taste my own funeral one day."

"And did she have the same... sensitivity?"

"She had a good nose too." Let the dead carry the suspicion; they could bear the weight better than the living. "Dead these ten years."

He wrote that down as well. She watched him write it and understood that none of it mattered, that the page was a formality, that her name had already traveled wherever names traveled when the Vigilance took an interest. Registration decrees didn't need proof. They needed only interest.

When he finally dismissed her, the banquet had resumed around the empty space where the poisoned dish had been, conversation climbing back to its former pitch like water filling a footprint. The queen ate the next course without apparent concern. From a distance, Isolde's composure looked like courage. Up close, Zeph suspected, it would look like appetite.

Mistress Halvard found her in the corridor outside the hall, where the heat of the chandeliers gave way to the cold stone breath of the service passages. The older woman pressed a purse into her hand—heavier than a normal bonus, lighter than her life was worth.

"You saved the queen," Halvard said. "They'll say that in the morning. And by evening they'll say you knew beforehand, and by week's end they'll say you planted it yourself, because a story needs a villain and a grey mouse from nowhere is easier to hang than an ambassador." She said it flatly, without drama, the way one reports the tide. "Take the money. Take the night shift off. And girl—" she gripped Zeph's wrist, hard, her laundress fingers biting in, "—whatever it is you actually have, whatever it is you're hiding behind that good nose of yours—bury it deeper. The ground is shifting. Can't you feel it? Even I can feel it, and I've got nothing in me but soup and grievances."

"I don't know what you mean, mistress."

"No," Halvard said. "You don't. That's the point of you." She released Zeph's wrist and walked away, her keys chiming, and did not look back.

* * *

Zeph walked home through the city at midnight, because walking was thinking and thinking was necessary, and because the alternative was to stand in the tasters' dormitory listening to the other apprentices breathe and imagine the officer's pen scratching her name into lists.

Vellamare at midnight was a different country. By day the city shouted—fish-cries and bell-towers and the harbor's endless argument of gulls and rigging. By night it murmured. Lamplighters had done their rounds; the flames sat in their glass houses like tame spirits. Fog came off the canals in slow white tongues. Somewhere a drunk sang something mournful in the old dialect, the words worn smooth as river stones.

She took the long way, through the Terraced Gardens—or what remained of them. Once, the histories claimed, the terraces had bloomed year-round, flowers in winter, fruit out of season, a wonder that had made the founding kings famous across two seas. Now the beds lay fallow under crown decree, the soil raked bare, the trellises empty, the fountains dry except for rainwater and the black shine of algae. Living plants near the palace, forbidden. She had never questioned it. You didn't question laws that predated your grandmother's grandmother.

Tonight, passing the lowest terrace, she stopped.

Something was growing in the corner where the wall met the drainage grate. A single flower, pushing up through packed earth that hadn't been turned in years—white-petaled, open, absurd in the fog and the cold. It had no business existing. Nothing had planted it. Nothing watered it. And yet it stood there in the lamplight, trembling slightly, either from wind or from the effort of being impossible.

Zeph crouched. She didn't touch it. She looked at it the way you look at a word in a language you almost remember.

And the wall inside her—the one she had built brick by brick since childhood, the one that kept her small and safe and unnoticed—shivered again, the way it had shivered near the poisoned dish. Not warning this time. Recognition. The flower smelled like the poison's under-note, that green old rain-smell, and it seemed to lean toward her, and her chest ached with something that had no name she permitted herself.

*You did this,* said a voice that was not a voice, somewhere below thought. *Walking past. Wanting. The ground remembers what you are.*

"Don't be stupid," she whispered to herself, to the flower, to the fog. "I'm a taster. I have a good nose. That's all."

The flower, naturally, did not answer. But as she straightened and walked away, she could feel it behind her like an eye opening, and she pulled her cloak tighter and told herself the shiver down her spine was only the cold.

At her lodging above the chandler's shop on Rope Walk, she locked her door, sat on her bed, and counted her money by candlelight. Enough for a month. Perhaps six weeks if she was careful. Odile had been saying for weeks—*fly, Zeph, take the coast road, be nobody in some fishing village*—and Zeph had laughed and said invisibility had held for seven years, it would hold a while longer.

But Halvard had felt the ground shifting. The Vigilance officer had written her name twice. And somewhere in the dark of her chest, behind a wall she had built to keep the world out—or perhaps to keep herself in—something green and patient had begun, very quietly, to grow.

In the morning, she decided, she would go to the night market. Buy a forgetting-charm from old Sefa, even a fake one, even just to hold it. And after that—

After that, the ground would decide.

She blew out the candle. In the terrace garden below the palace, in the dark, a white flower opened a little wider, and no one saw it bloom.
