## 2. Charms for the Fearful

The night market of Vellamare woke the way a cat does—one eye at a time, wary, then all at once.

Zephyrine knew its rhythms better than her own heartbeat. By the third bell after dusk, the fishwives had gone home and the lamplighters were still working their way down the Spindle Rows, so there was a quarter hour when the lanes belonged to no one but moths and smugglers. Then came the soft percussion of awnings being unrolled, the strike of flint against wick, and the market opened like a flower that only bloomed in shadow: oyster shells heaped with river-pearls of dubious origin, tallow candles carved into the shapes of saints whose names had been sanded off, roasted chestnuts, stolen silk, love philtres made of vinegar and optimism.

And, at the end of Candlemakers' Row, under an awning patched with three different colors of canvas, a folding table where a small woman with ink-stained fingers sold charms that did almost nothing.

Zeph laid out her stock with the care of a jeweler. Knots of red thread for colicky babies—these she knotted while murmuring, though what she murmured was mostly nonsense syllables, because the thread itself did the work and her magic barely brushed it. Dried lavender sewn into muslin for sleepless nights. Copper coins hammered thin and pierced, to be worn against the throat against fever. A dish of salt from the Grey Marshes, sold by the pinch, *for keeping out what shouldn't come in*.

None of it was illegal. That was the point. Charm-selling was a trade as old as Vellamare's walls, tolerated the way beggars were tolerated—as long as it stayed small, stayed dim, stayed beneath notice. The crown's censors had long ago decided that a woman selling knotted thread was a merchant, not a threat.

Zeph had spent seven years making certain they went on deciding it.

She arranged the last item—a sprig of rosemary bound with blue wool, *for remembering what grief makes you forget*—and looked up into the face of her first customer of the night, who was already standing at the table even though she hadn't seen him approach. They always did that. The desperate moved quietly.

"Mistress Marchetti." He was a cooper, she thought, or a cooper's widow; his hands were banded with old splinter-scars. "My daughter. She's got the wasting cough. The physiker wants four silver a visit and we haven't got four copper."

Zeph's own hands were already moving before he finished speaking, because they always were. She took down a twist of dried thyme, a knot of red thread, a pinch of the marsh salt wrapped in wax paper. "Boil the thyme with honey," she said. "Breathe the steam, morning and night. The thread goes around her wrist—not tight, just resting. And this—" she tapped the salt packet against the table twice, a little ritual that meant nothing and cost nothing and made people feel cared for, which was most of what magic had ever been, in her experience, "—this goes in the corners of her room."

"And it works?" His voice cracked on the word. "Truly works?"

Zeph met his eyes. He wanted her to say yes. He needed her to say yes, the way a drowning man needs a rope even if it's frayed.

"The thyme works," she said honestly. "The honey works. The rest is so you can sleep, and she needs you sleeping more than she needs anything else I could sell you." She pushed the bundle across the table. "Two coppers. If two coppers is too much, it's nothing, and don't come back until she's well, because I'll know if you spent your bread money on me."

He paid the two coppers. They always insisted on paying something; pride was the one coin everyone in Vellamare still carried. He clutched the bundle to his chest like a relic and melted back into the lane, and Zeph watched him go and felt the familiar ache behind her sternum—the ache that was half compassion and half exhaustion, because every charm she sold took a grain of herself she didn't have to spare.

That was the secret nobody had ever guessed, and she intended to keep it that way until she died of old age in a warm bed: the magic wasn't in the thread or the thyme or the salt. It was in her. A whisper of it, a candleflame behind smoked glass—but real, and real things cost. Every knot she tied with intent drew a little warmth out of her blood. By the end of a market night her fingertips were always cold, always faintly numb, as if she'd been holding them in winter water.

Small magic. Harmless magic. Magic so faint that even she had spent years telling herself it might be imagination.

She rubbed her hands together and smiled at the next customer, and the next, and the next.

* * *

By the sixth bell the market was in full voice, and Odile arrived the way she always did—loudly, beautifully, and slightly late.

"You look terrible," Odile announced, setting a paper cone of candied ginger on the table as tribute. She was tall where Zeph was small, gold where Zeph was brown, a seamstress with a laugh that carried across three lanes and a talent for making poverty look like a fashion choice. Tonight she wore a shawl the color of pomegranates that Zeph strongly suspected had been *acquired* rather than purchased. "Don't argue. You've got shadows under your shadows. How many knots tonight?"

"Eleven. Two fevers, a birth, a drowned-sailor's-wife who wanted to know if he suffered." Zeph bit a piece of ginger and let the sweetness cut through the cold in her fingers. "He didn't. I told her the truth about that one. Some things you can't charm."

Odile's laughter dimmed. She glanced up and down the row—quick, professional, the glance of someone checking sightlines—and then leaned over the table, lowering her voice beneath the market's noise. "Have you heard?"

"I hear everything. It's my second-worst talent."

"The proclamation boards." Odile's hand found Zeph's wrist across the table, grip tighter than the joke deserved. "They went up at dusk. All of them, all at once—that's the part that frightens me, Zeph. Not the decree. The *coordination*. Scribes don't work that fast unless the orders came down days ago and they were waiting for dark."

"What decree?"

"Registration." The word dropped between them like a stone into a well. "All gifted persons to present themselves at the district houses within the fortnight. Names taken, gifts recorded, a mark issued." Odile's thumb pressed hard against Zeph's pulse point, as if she could check the truth of her friend by touch alone. "*For their protection,* the board says. You know what that phrase means. It means the way a cage protects a bird."

Zeph kept her face pleasant and her hands busy, straightening the lavender sachets that didn't need straightening. Around them the market churned on—someone haggling over eels, a fiddler tuning, the smell of frying onions and tar smoke—and she made herself match its rhythm, unhurried, unremarkable. Seven years of practice. You learned to let your heartbeat stay slow even when the words landed like knives.

"People register," she said quietly. "People have always registered. It's a list, Odile. Lists are paper. Paper is patient and harmless."

"This isn't a census." Odile's voice had an edge Zeph had never heard from her before, and it frightened her more than the decree had. "I read the whole board. Twice. There's a clause at the bottom, fine print, the kind they think seamstresses can't read. *Those whose gifts are deemed disruptive to public order may be conveyed to the Pale Tower for assessment.* Assessment, Zeph. Nobody comes back from assessment. You remember Marot the glassblower? Gifted with heat, never hurt anyone in his life? He went for assessment eleven years ago. His wife still sets out his supper."

"I remember."

"Then hear me." Odile came around the table, close enough now that they were two women gossiping over a stall, which is exactly how it would look to anyone passing, and both of them knew it. "I've been saving. There's a boat out of Saltgate on the tide after next—the smugglers' tide, Corvane's people run it. There's room for two. We go south, past the crown's reach, to the free towns. You sell your charms openly there. No smoked glass, no pretending, no—" her voice caught, and for a moment the beautiful armor slipped and Zeph saw underneath it, which was fear, plain and enormous, "—no waking up every morning wondering if today's the day they read your name off a list."

Zeph looked at her friend for a long moment. The lamplight caught the pomegranate shawl and turned it to embers.

"Odile," she said gently. "Look at me. What do you see?"

"A stubborn woman about to make a mistake."

"No. Look properly. What am I?"

Odile hesitated. "A charm-seller."

"A *harmless* charm-seller. A woman whose gift couldn't light a candle if her life depended on it—which, incidentally, I once tested, and it didn't so much as flicker." Zeph held up her hands, turning them in the light, ordinary hands with ordinary ink stains. "The registry will take my name and write *negligible* beside it, and the clerk will yawn, and that will be the end of it. That's been my armor for seven years, Odile. Invisibility. It has held through two purges and a plague quarantine and the time I accidentally served soup to a tax collector. It will hold through a list."

"You're not hearing me—"

"I'm hearing you perfectly." Zeph reached up and touched the edge of the pomegranate shawl, smoothing it, an old gesture between them, almost sisterly. "You're hearing yourself. And you should listen, because you're right—for *you*. Your gift isn't negligible, Odile. I've seen you take a bolt of cloth and know, without touching the scissors, exactly where the flaw runs. That's sight. That's real. Registering would be handing them a map to you." She held her friend's gaze. "Take the boat. Take the tide after next. I mean it—I'll help you pack, I'll carry your trunk down to Saltgate myself."

"And you?"

"I'll be here. Selling thread."

For a moment Odile looked at her the way you look at a wall you're deciding whether to beat your head against. Then she laughed, short and unhappy, and stepped back around the table. "You are the most infuriating person in Vellamare, and I include the queen."

"That's the kindest thing anyone's said to me all week."

"I'll keep asking. Every night. Until you say yes or until they drag you, and I want you to remember, when they drag you, that I asked." But her voice had softened, and she picked up the cone of ginger and pressed it back into Zeph's hands. "Eat something. Your fingers are grey again. I can always tell when you've been spending yourself—you get grey at the edges, like paper left in rain."

She swept off down the row toward her own stall, trailing pomegranate-colored light, and Zeph watched her go and felt the ache behind her sternum change shape. Because here was the thing she hadn't said, the thing she'd buried so deep even she only dug it up on nights like this, alone with the lamp and the cold tips of her fingers:

Her gift wasn't negligible.

She had known it wasn't for a long time now. She'd known since the banquet last spring, when the poison in the queen's wine had sung to her across forty feet of marble like a struck bell—known it since the flowers came. But knowing a thing and admitting it were different countries, and Zeph had built her whole life in the space between them. Small magic. Harmless magic. A candleflame behind smoked glass.

If she opened that door—even a crack—the glass would shatter, and she didn't know what was on the other side of it, and she was afraid.

So she ate the ginger, and straightened the lavender, and called out her prices in a voice pitched to be forgettable, and told herself the night was going well.

* * *

It was near midnight when the bells changed.

Vellamare's bells spoke a language every citizen learned in the cradle: the hour, the fire alarm, the flood warning, the call to assembly. What they had never spoken, in Zeph's lifetime, was the pattern that began at the twelfth bell and rolled outward from the citadel through the sleeping city—three strokes, a pause, three strokes again. Slow. Deliberate. The sound of a door being locked from the outside.

The market went quiet in patches, like lights going out. The fiddler stopped mid-phrase. Down the row, faces turned upward toward the dark hills where the citadel stood, and Zeph felt the silence move through the crowd like cold water through cloth.

"They're posting riders," said the eel-seller, an old man with a voice like gravel in a bucket. "Night proclamations. Haven't heard that pattern since the succession riots. My grandmother told me about those." He spat, carefully, away from his own eels. "Nothing good ever gets read by torchlight."

Zeph's hands had stopped moving. She noticed it distantly, the way you notice your own heart skipping. Around her the market was packing up—not dispersing, not yet, but folding inward, vendors bundling goods with quick economical motions, the whole row contracting like a fist. Fear traveled fast in Vellamare. It was the one commodity that never lost value.

"Zeph." Odile was back at her elbow, breathless, shawl clutched at her throat. "Saltgate. The tide after next. Please. I'm begging you, and I don't beg, it wrinkles the bodice."

"Go home, Odile." Zeph heard her own voice and was surprised by how calm it sounded, how steady, when inside her something was pacing like a trapped animal. "Lock your door. Sleep. Tomorrow the boards will say what they say, and the day after that the city will decide it's business as usual, because that's what cities do. They survive by forgetting quickly. I'll be here. Selling thread."

"You stubborn, blind, brave idiot—"

"Go *home*, Odile."

Odile went. At the corner of the row she stopped and looked back, and the look said everything her mouth wouldn't: *when you're ready, I'll be waiting, and God help us both if you wait too long.* Then the pomegranate shawl vanished into the dark, and Zeph was alone with her table of harmless charms and the bells still tolling their locked-door pattern overhead.

She packed slowly. Thread into the basket. Lavender. Salt. Last of all the rosemary bound with blue wool, *for remembering what grief makes you forget*, and her fingers lingered on it longer than they needed to.

The walk home took her up through the Spindle Rows, past shuttered windows and the occasional candle burning behind shutters—people awake, people listening. The city smelled of woodsmoke and low tide and, underneath, something else. Something she couldn't name. A tension in the air like the moment before a summer storm breaks, when the pressure changes and dogs begin to whine.

At the mouth of her own narrow lane, she stopped.

There was a rider at the far end of the street. Crown livery, dark on dark, a lantern hooded to throw its light only downward. He was nailing something to the proclamation post beside the well—the same post where wedding announcements and grain prices hung—and the hammer's sound was very loud in the empty lane. Three strikes. A pause. Three strikes again. Even his hammer spoke in the bells' new language.

Zeph pressed herself into a doorway and watched him finish and ride away, hooves muffled with rags, which was itself a message: *we do not want to wake you; we want you to find this in the morning, already done, already true.*

She waited until the hoofbeats faded. Then she crossed to the post.

The paper was fresh, the ink still shining. By lantern-spill she read it twice, the way Odile would have, hunting the fine print, hunting the clause. *By order of Her Majesty Isolde, Queen of Vellamare and its Territories.* Registration. District houses. Fortnight. And at the bottom, smaller:

*Persons concealing gifts, or persons found to have made false declaration, shall be conveyed to the Pale Tower for assessment.*

Below that, the seal. Below that, nothing. No appeal, no exception, no line marked *negligible*. The list did not distinguish between a hedge-witch who could blister paint and a woman who tied knots in thread. The list did not believe in small magic. The list believed in names.

Zeph stood in the empty lane for a long time. The wind had come up off the harbor, carrying salt and storm-smell, and somewhere above her a shutter creaked—someone else awake, someone else reading by memory what they'd see officially at dawn.

*It will hold,* she told herself. *Invisibility has held for seven years. One name among ten thousand, one gift too faint to measure. The clerk will yawn.*

Her hands, hanging at her sides, were trembling.

She walked home. She locked her door. She lay down in her narrow bed with her clothes on, and stared at the ceiling beams, and listened to the wind worrying at the tiles, and somewhere in the small hours she finally slept—

—and dreamed of her mother's garden, which she had not dreamed of in years. Green light through leaves. Her mother's hands, earth-dark to the wrists, pressing a seed into soil that seemed to breathe. *Everything that grows,* her mother was saying, in the dream where voices carried clearly from years that should have blurred, *everything that grows must first break open. Remember that, little root. When the time comes, don't be afraid of breaking.*

When Zeph woke, gray light was seeping under the shutters, and her room was full of the smell of roses.

She lay very still. The smell was impossible—it was nearly autumn, and she owned no roses, and the florist three streets down grew only funeral lilies. But it filled the room, thick and sweet and unmistakable, rising up through the floorboards as if from the ground itself, as if from the bones of the city.

Slowly, Zeph sat up. And looked down at her hands, lying open on the blanket, palms up, the way they rested when she worked her smallest magic.

Along the creases of both palms, faint as veins in a petal, ran lines of green.
