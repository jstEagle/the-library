## 3. The Proclamation

The bells began at dawn.

Not the usual bells—Vellamare had a bell for everything, for fish landings and funerals and the changing of the harbor watch—but this was the great bronze voice in the Saltspire, the one that rang only when the crown had something to say that it wanted pressed into every skull in the city at once. Zephyrine heard it through the floorboards of her rented room, felt it in her teeth, in the water standing in her washbasin, which shivered into concentric rings as though something enormous had struck the world beneath it.

She lay still and listened, and her body knew before her mind did what the ringing meant. Seven years of living small had taught her body to read danger the way sailors read clouds. Her pulse went thin and quick. Her hands, folded on the blanket, went cold at the fingertips.

*Registration,* Odile had said, three nights ago, gripping her wrist across the market stall until the bone ached. *They're not stopping at registration, Zeph. Registration is how they make the list.*

The bells rang nine times, then stopped. In the silence after, she could hear the whole district holding its breath—doors not opening, kettles not singing, the particular hush of ten thousand people pretending not to be awake.

She dressed in her plainest grey, braided her hair back tight, and pinned the little tin badge of the Tasters' Guild to her collar where it would catch the light. A guild badge was a kind of armor. It said: *this one belongs to something official; this one has been inspected.* She had worn it so long she sometimes forgot it was a lie of its own kind—that the Guild had never once tested her gift, because her gift had never once shown itself to them. They believed her sensitivity was a trained palate, an unusually fine tongue. Let them believe it. Belief, carefully tended, was the strongest charm she owned.

Outside, the morning was the color of pewter. Fog off the harbor had climbed the terraced streets and settled in the lower quarters like a guest who intended to stay. Zeph joined the slow current of neighbors drifting toward Saltspire Square, and no one spoke, and the silence had a texture to it, woolly and close, like the fog itself.

In the square, criers stood on the fountain's rim in royal livery—white and iron-grey, the colors of Queen Isolde's house—and unrolled their scrolls with a snap that made Zeph flinch though she'd been expecting it.

"By decree of Her Majesty Isolde, First of Her Name, Protector of Vellamare—" the crier's voice was pitched to carry over water, over crowds, over screaming if it came to that, "—all persons possessing unregistered magical gifts shall present themselves at the Hall of Records within seven days. Henceforth, all gifted persons are wards of the crown."

A pause. The fog dripped from the eaves.

"For their protection," the crier added, and there was something in his voice—a hairline crack, a hesitation he papered over with volume—"and for the protection of the realm."

Zeph stood very still among the crowd and did the arithmetic she'd been refusing to do for weeks. *Wards of the crown.* The Pale Tower had stood empty at the city's heart for sixty years, since the last royal mage had died and no successor had been named. Empty towers did not need filling unless someone intended to fill them. And the phrase *for their protection* was the oldest sentence in the language of cruelty; every cage in history had been described that way by the person holding the key.

Around her, the crowd began to move—not dispersing, exactly, but rearranging itself, people angling away from neighbors they'd known for decades, eyes sliding sideways. Zeph watched a fishwife take a half-step back from the woman beside her, a woman she'd clearly walked to market with, their baskets still knocking together. She watched the fishwife notice herself doing it and flush with shame, and not step forward again.

That was the decree's first harvest, gathered before a single door was kicked in: neighbor turned carefully away from neighbor, like flowers tracking a sun that frightened them.

Zeph pulled her collar higher and went to work.

* * *

The palace kitchens received the news before the square had finished emptying, because the palace kitchens received all news first—it traveled down from the queen's council chamber through corridors of runners and under-stewards and landed finally among the scullions like weather.

"They're saying the Tower's being refitted," murmured Bettine, the pastry cook, rolling dough with more force than the dough deserved. "Iron doors. New locks. My cousin's a mason; he's been hired for the upper floors. Says they're building cells with no windows."

"Cells," repeated the under-steward, "for their protection."

No one laughed. Laughter had become a dangerous currency in Vellamare, spent only in the smallest denominations, behind closed doors.

Zeph worked in silence at her station, tasting the evening's courses against the master taster's ledger—white wine from the southern coast, a quince sorbet, the roast lark pie that Lord Chancellor Merric insisted on despite the season. Nothing was poisoned. Nothing was ever poisoned anymore; the last attempt had been four years ago, and she'd caught it in a heartbeat, bitter almond blooming under the honey like a snake under flowers, and had reported it as "an off taste, perhaps the storage" and let the guards find the rest. The poisoner had been hanged. Zeph had been given a silver mark and a sleepless month.

Now, as she lifted the tasting spoon from the sorbet, a runner burst into the kitchen, breathless, and the room stilled around him the way water stills around a dropped stone.

"The proclamation," he gasped. "Her Majesty's issued a second one—an addendum. The Guild masters are to submit full rosters. Every taster, every charmer licensed or otherwise, every hedge-healer, every—everyone." He swallowed. "They're saying the Pale Tower will begin receiving the registered within the fortnight. For assessment."

Bettine's rolling pin stopped.

Zeph set down the spoon with a hand that did not shake, because seven years of practice could hold a hand steady through almost anything, and thought: *rosters.* Names. Her name, written in the Guild's careful ink, delivered to the Hall of Records, filed beside her address, her landlord's name, the description of her gift as the Guild understood it—which was wrong, mercifully wrong, *sensitive palate, no active talent*—but filed nonetheless, in a building that was about to become a hunting blind.

She finished the shift. She tasted everything put before her and found it clean, and signed the ledger, and thanked the under-steward, and walked out through the servants' gate into the fog with her face arranged into the mild blankness of a woman thinking about dinner.

Only when she reached the mouth of Tanner's Lane, where the fog thickened and the lanterns swam like drowned moons, did she allow herself to stop and press her back against the wet brick and breathe.

*Seven days,* the criers had said. The addendum said a fortnight for the registered. But lists preceded arrests; lists were the soft part of the fist. And somewhere in the Hall of Records, in a hand that wasn't hers, her name already waited like a seed in frozen ground.

*You should run,* said a voice in her that sounded like Odile. *Tonight. Now. Take nothing.*

But run where? The roads were watched; the ports were worse; her magic was too faint to shield her even if she'd known how to use it—and she didn't, she truly didn't, it had never been anything but a warmth in her palms and a sweetness on her tongue and flowers that lived a day longer in her presence than they should. No one fled Vellamare over *that*. No one hanged a woman for making cut roses last.

She would stay invisible. She had always stayed invisible. It had worked for seven years.

She told herself this all the way home, in the tone of a prayer whose god had stopped answering.

* * *

Mira lived two doors down.

She was seventy-one years old and stooped as a question mark, and everyone in the lane knew she was gifted, because everyone in the lane had at some point brought her a sick child or a fevered husband and received from her some small working—bread that stayed fresh through a week of damp, a poultice that drew fever out through the soles of the feet. Mira's gift was the humblest kind, hearth-magic, grandmother-magic, the kind that had kept the poor of Vellamare alive through plagues and famines while the crown's registered mages served the wealthy behind walls. Everyone knew. Everyone had always known. It had never mattered before.

It mattered now.

Zeph heard the boots first—the specific percussion of them, too many, too synchronized for any honest purpose—and then the light, torchlight swinging up the lane through the fog, turning the wet cobblestones to a river of dull fire.

She was at her window. She would never be able to explain afterward why she went to the window instead of away from it, except that Mira had once sat up all night with Zeph through a winter fever, when Zeph was new to the city and alone and half-convinced she was dying, and had held her head and hummed and told her, *you'll live, little one, you've got spring in you yet.* You did not look away from a thing like that. Looking away was how the darkness won its arguments.

They dragged her out into the lane in her nightdress.

Four of them, in white and iron-grey, and a fifth with a ledger book, and Mira's thin arms were pinned and her feet left the ground and her slippers came off, one, then the other, and lay in the middle of the lane like small dead animals. She was not screaming. That was the worst of it. She was saying, in a reasonable voice, the voice of a woman explaining something to children who ought to know better:

"I registered. I registered on the first day, I have the paper, it's inside, it's on my table—"

"It's been reviewed," said the one with the ledger, without looking up. "You're summoned for assessment. Protection protocols."

"I'm seventy-one years old." Mira's voice cracked at last, not into screaming but into something worse, something bewildered. "What is there to protect me *from*?"

No one answered. The fog took the sound of the boots going away, and the torchlight shrank to a smudge, and then the lane was dark again except for the two slippers lying in the road.

And Zeph stood at her window with her hand pressed flat against the cold glass, and felt something break.

Not loudly. Not all at once. It broke the way ice breaks on a pond in early thaw—one crack, running fast and silent under the surface, and then another, and then the whole sheet groaning, and then the black water showing through.

Because she had told herself a story for seven years. The story was: *my gift is too small to matter. The hunt is for the powerful, the visible, the dangerous. If I keep my hands closed and my tongue quiet, the storm will pass over my roof.* And Mira had kept her hands open her whole life—open to every fevered child in the lane—and the storm had taken her anyway, gently, in her nightdress, for protection.

There was no small enough. There was no quiet enough. The story was a lie, and she had built her life on it the way a bird builds a nest on a branch that is already falling.

She turned from the window and her own hands were glowing.

Faintly—oh, faintly, a pale green-gold shimmer along the creases of her palms, light like the underside of new leaves—but unmistakable, undeniable, the first time in her life the light had come *out* instead of staying hidden in. And underneath her feet, under the floorboards of her rented room, something answered.

She felt it as a pull, deep and tidal, the way she imagined a river must feel toward the sea. Seven years of suppression, seven years of holding the lid down on a boiling pot, and the pot had finally found the seam. The warmth that lived in her palms poured out of her like water finding a crack in a dam, and she did not know how to stop it, had never known how to *start* it, and it went down through the floor and into the stone of the old house and into the ground beneath the stone—

—and the cobblestones of Tanner's Lane began to bloom.

She heard it before she saw it: a creaking, a grinding, the sound of ancient stones shouldered aside by living things. She stumbled to the window again and looked down into the lane, and the fog seemed to glow, and where the fog glowed the street was flowering.

Crocuses first—they were crocuses, she knew them, her mother had grown them—forcing up between the joints of the cobbles in violet and white and a yellow like struck matches, dozens of them, then hundreds, splitting stone that had lain undisturbed for two hundred years. Then moss, spreading in velvet rivers. Then something taller, a shoot unfurling with obscene speed into a sapling, a whitebeam, budding, blossoming, all in the space of a minute, its petals shaking loose into the fog like snow falling upward. The lane smelled of spring. In the dead of autumn, in the poorest quarter of Vellamare, the street outside her window had become a garden.

And the light of it, the green-gold light rising off the blossoms, lit her room like a beacon.

*Stop,* she begged her own hands. *Please. Stop.* She gripped them, crushed them against her breastbone, and the glow soaked through her fingers and the blooming slowed, faltered, and sank—flowers closing, sapling wilting back into the stone, moss greying—all of it sinking down below the cobbles as though the street were swallowing its own miracle. In a minute there was nothing but a lane full of loose petals and two abandoned slippers, and the fog, and the dark.

But the light had been seen. Light like that was always seen.

Down the lane, a shutter opened. Another. A child's voice, sleepy and wondering: "Mama, the street's all flowers—"

Zeph stepped back from the window into shadow, her heart slamming, her hands still warm, still faintly luminous, like coals that would not die. She looked at them and felt the truth arrive in her body the way the bells had arrived that morning, felt it in her teeth, in the water trembling in her basin:

She was not too small. She had never been too small. Whatever slept in her was vast, and old, and it had just woken, and it did not care at all about her seven years of hiding.

Somewhere across the city, she knew, in a tower of pale stone, someone was writing down the reports of a street that bloomed in autumn. Somewhere, a nameless quarter was becoming a search grid.

She had until they came. Hours, perhaps. A night, if she was lucky.

Zeph blew out her candle, though the candle was the least of her lights now, and stood in the dark of her small room listening to the petals of Tanner's Lane rustle in a wind that touched nothing else—and understood, with terrible clarity, that invisibility was over, and the only question left was whether she would choose what came next, or it would choose her.

Then came the knock at her door.
