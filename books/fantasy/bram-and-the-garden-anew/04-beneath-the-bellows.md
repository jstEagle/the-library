## 4. Beneath the Bellows

The undercity did not smell like a city at all. It smelled like the inside of the earth—wet stone and old iron and something faintly sweet that Zeph could not name, a sweetness like fruit rotting in a cellar no one had opened for a hundred years.

She had come down through the mouth of an old drainage culvert behind the tanneries, guided by nothing but panic and the memory of a rumor: *if the hunt takes you, go down where the bellows breathe.* The foundries of Vellamare's lower terraces pumped air into their furnaces through great leather lungs, and beneath those lungs, it was said, ran passages the crown had never mapped. Smugglers' country. Hunt-blind country.

Her feet knew more than she did. They carried her along channels slick with condensation, past walls furred with white mineral bloom, while above her—muffled by forty feet of stone—the city went on shouting her name. Or not her name. She had no name yet, officially. But there would be posters by morning. There were always posters by morning.

*Flowers through cobblestones.* She could still see them when she closed her eyes, which was often, because the dark demanded it. A whole spillway of color cracking up through the street outside Widow Anselma's door, roses and feverfew and things she had no names for, blooming out of her own terror as if the ground itself had decided to answer her screaming with beauty. The neighbors had gathered. Someone had fetched a lantern. Someone else had fetched the watch.

She had run before they finished arriving. Barefoot, half-dressed, her charm-satchel still slung across her chest because some animal part of her had refused to leave even that much of her old life behind. The satchel bumped against her ribs now with every step, full of harmless little braided charms that suddenly felt like evidence.

*Harmless.* That word had been a door she kept locked from the inside. And last night the lock had broken, and everything she'd shoved behind it came pouring up through the stones.

Her ankle turned on a loose flagstone and she went down hard, palms slapping wet rock, breath knocked out of her in a white gasp. For a moment she simply knelt there in the dark, tasting copper and mineral water, listening to her own heartbeat and, beneath it, another sound.

Air. Moving in long, slow breaths.

She lifted her head. Far down the passage, a faint grey light pulsed—not steady like a lamp but rhythmic, swelling and fading, swelling and fading, like the glow behind a sleeping thing's eyelids. Warmth rode on it too, and with the warmth a smell that cut through the cellar-rot: green. Actual green, growing things, cut stems and crushed leaves.

There should be no growing things forty feet underground.

Zeph got up. Her options were simple and terrible: back toward the hunt, or forward toward whatever breathed in the dark. She went forward, one hand trailing the wall, because her body had apparently decided it trusted the smell of leaves more than it trusted her judgment.

* * *

The passage opened without warning into a cavern so large the grey light didn't reach its ceiling. Stalactites hung like the teeth of something patient. And filling the middle distance, tier upon tier of wooden shelves and hanging racks rose toward the darkness, laden with crates, casks, bundles wrapped in oilcloth, cages whose contents shifted and chirred. A warehouse built inside the earth's own throat.

And everywhere, impossibly, plants.

They grew out of cracks in the stone, out of seams between shelving boards, out of the mouths of abandoned casks—pale, spindly things, mushrooms and mosses and vines with leaves like coins, all of them leaning toward the pulsing grey light as if it were a sun. Some of them Zeph recognized from her mother's old herb-book. None of them should have lived here.

"You're bleeding on my floor."

The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, low and dry as sand shifting. Zeph spun. Between two shelving tiers stood a man she would have sworn hadn't been there a heartbeat before—a big man gone soft at the edges, with a smuggler's layered coat and a face like a map of old weather, seamed and faded and marked by routes no one traveled anymore. His eyes were the problem. They were pale, almost colorless, and they looked at her the way a jeweler looks at a stone someone has been using as a doorstop.

"I'm not—" Zeph started, then looked down. Her palms were scraped, and a thin line of red threaded the water running off the flagstones toward a drain. "It's nothing."

"Nothing." He said the word like he was tasting it, and finding it poisoned. He stepped closer, and she caught his smell under the coal-and-leather: rain again, that same impossible rain-smell from the greenhouse district, from the derelict glass house everyone said was empty. "You came down the tannery culvert alone, barefoot, at night, with the whole city howling above you. And you're telling me about nothing."

Zeph's hand crept toward her satchel. It was a stupid gesture; the charms in it couldn't stop a determined cat. But the man watched the movement with something that wasn't quite amusement.

"Keep your trinkets," he said. "I know what you are."

The words landed in her chest like a dropped stone. Seven years of smallness, of *just instinct, just a feeling, just a trick of the tongue*—and this stranger in the dark peeled it off her in four words.

"You don't know anything about me," she said, and was proud of how steady it came out.

"No?" He tilted his head. Behind him, deeper in the cavern, figures had begun to emerge—smugglers, a dozen of them, drawn by some signal she'd never seen given. Women and men and one enormous youth with a crossbow held loosely, all of them watching her with that same jeweler's attention. "Then tell me what happened on Tanner's Row tonight. Tell me why the stones bloomed."

The silence stretched. Somewhere far above, muffled almost to nothing, a bell began to ring—the hunt-bell, the one they rang when the watch moved into a quarter. Everyone in the cavern heard it. No one moved.

"Your magic is loud," the man said quietly. "That's the joke of it, isn't it? You spent years making yourself quiet, and it turns out you were never quiet at all. You were a held breath. And last night, you finally exhaled."

Zeph's eyes stung. She hated him for it. "Who are you?"

"Corvane." He said it the way other men said their debts or their sins—flatly, having made peace with the weight. "I move things the crown doesn't want moved. People, mostly, lately. Sometimes other things." His gaze flicked, briefly, to the pale vines climbing the shelving, and something crossed his weathered face that looked almost like grief. "You'll want to sit. What I have to tell you doesn't go down well standing."

"I haven't agreed to stay."

"No," Corvane agreed. "But the hunt-bell is ringing, your feet are cut, and everything above us right now wants to put you in the Pale Tower. So." He spread one broad hand toward a circle of stools around a brazier whose coals glowed the same pulsing grey as the light in the passage. "Stay until it makes sense to leave. That's all anyone here can offer you. That's all any of us ever had."

* * *

The tea they gave her was made from something that tasted of honey and pine resin and, underneath, faintly of the same green sweetness that filled the cavern. It warmed her cut feet better than any bandage. She sat wrapped in a borrowed blanket among strangers who did not ask her name, which was somehow kinder than asking, and Corvane sat across the brazier from her with his huge hands laced over one knee, and told her a story.

Not the way storytellers tell stories. He told it the way a man tells you the location of a wound.

"Before Vellamare," he said, "there was the Garden. Not a garden the way you mean—a garden the way the sea is water. Everything green, everything growing, everything enchanted in this world came out of it. It fed the world's magic the way the sun feeds the fields. And once, the world paid it back. That was the covenant. Memory for vitality. Remembrance for renewal. Two realms keeping each other alive."

"And then?" Zeph asked, though she already dreaded the answer.

"Then men built a city on the border of it and called themselves founders." His voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing about it—it never rose. "They learned they could take instead of trade. Drive pipes into the Garden's roots and draw its lifeblood up into their halls, into their bloodline, into the dynasty. Why pay for magic when you can steal it? So they broke the covenant. Shattered it. Deliberately, carefully, the way you'd break a sword—into three pieces, three keystones, buried where no mender would ever find them. And the Garden began to die. Slowly. Over centuries. The way a tree dies when you girdle it—green for a while, still standing, still looking almost alive, while everything underneath starves."

The brazier pulsed. One of the coin-leaved vines near Corvane's shoulder had drooped, and he reached out without looking and touched it with one finger, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping child.

"The city feels it, even if the city doesn't know it," he went on. "Why do you think Vellamare forgets things? Old streets, old names, old graves. Why do you think the dead get remembered less every year? Memory is the currency, girl. The founders stole the Garden's side of the exchange and never paid theirs, and now the whole account is coming due. This autumn. The equinox. When the Garden goes, it won't just be flowers. Every enchantment in the realm unravels—and every memory the Garden ever held, which is most of them, goes with it. Your queen will rule a city that can't remember its own name."

Zeph stared into the coals. Something in her chest was moving, some long-sealed door groaning on its hinges. "Why are you telling me this?"

Corvane leaned forward. In the pulsing light his colorless eyes had a depth to them, like wells.

"Because of what you are," he said. "Renewal. Do you understand how rare that is? Fire-gifts, shadow-gifts, tongue-gifts, a hundred little magics—all of those are *spendings*. They use power up. Renewal is the other direction. It gives back. It's the covenant's own signature, written into a person. The Garden's magic recognizes itself in you." He paused. "Last night, terrified, untrained, half-asleep—you didn't burn anything. You didn't break anything. You made dead stones *bloom*. That's not a talent. That's a summons."

"I don't want a summons." Her voice cracked on it, and she pressed on anyway. "I want—I had a life. A small one. I was good at it. I tasted food and sold charms and nobody ever looked at me twice, and that was—that was the whole point—"

"Yes," Corvane said, and for the first time his voice softened. "And look what it bought you. The hunt at your heels and flowers giving you away. Smallness was always a lie we told the frightened, Zephyrine Marchetti. The world was never going to let you be small. It only waited until you had something the world needs."

She hadn't told him her name. She was almost certain she hadn't. When she looked up sharply, he met her eyes without apology.

"The plants talk," he said, shrugging. "Everything growing in this cave heard you crying in the culvert twenty minutes before you arrived. They have opinions. Mostly they wanted tea made for you." A ghost of dry humor crossed his ruined-map face and vanished. "Get some sleep. There's a cot behind the seed racks. Tomorrow night I take you somewhere. There's a man you need to meet—a beggar who smells of rain, down by the old greenhouse district. If anyone can tell you what your gift is actually *for*, it's him."

"Why him?"

Corvane was silent a long moment. The brazier breathed. Above them, forty feet of stone and a whole lying city.

"Because he was there," he said finally, "when it broke."

* * *

The cot was narrow and smelled of cedar and soil, and Zeph lay on it in the dark listening to the cavern breathe—the bellows far overhead sighing through their leather lungs, the plants rustling though there was no wind, the slow pulse of that strange grey light against her eyelids.

She should be planning an escape. That was the sensible thing. These people were smugglers; Corvane knew her name without being told; trust was a luxury that had gotten gifted folk loaded into wagons bound for the Pale Tower. Odile's voice was in her head, sharp with fear: *you can't hide anymore, Zeph, why won't you understand that—*

But when she closed her eyes, she didn't see the wagons. She saw the cobblestones splitting open with color. She felt again that terrifying, gorgeous loosening in her chest, like a fist she'd been making for seven years finally opening—and the world rushing in to fill her palm.

*Smallness was always a lie.*

Her hands, in the dark, were still faintly warm. She turned them over and thought of her mother—of dirt under her mother's fingernails, of seeds pressed into spring soil with such particular gentleness, of the things her mother used to whisper over the garden beds that Zeph had always assumed were prayers.

Maybe they hadn't been prayers.

Sleep took her mid-thought, deep and dreamless as planted ground. And in the morning, when she woke to the smell of rain in a place where rain had never reached, Corvane was waiting with a lantern and a route map, and the first thing he said was:

"Ready to meet the beggar who remembers everything?"

She was. That was the terrible part. She was.
