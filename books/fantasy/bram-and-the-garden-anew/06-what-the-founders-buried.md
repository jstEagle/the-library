## 6. What the Founders Buried

Bram remembered rain before he remembered his own name.

That was the cruelty of exile—it did not take memory all at once, the way drowning takes a body. It took it the way a slow drought takes a well: a hand's breadth at a time, a season at a time, until what remained was not emptiness but sediment, layers of silt where clear water had been. He remembered rain because the Garden had been made of rain, in a way. Because every living thing there had been a form the water had agreed to take, for a while, out of love.

He sat in the derelict greenhouse with his hands around a clay cup of barley tea that Zeph had brewed over a smuggler's spirit-lamp, and he let the memories come the way he had learned to let them come—sideways, in fragments, like light through a cracked pane. She had asked him a simple question. *What broke?* And the answer was not simple, and the answer was not one answer, and so he began where memory began: with rain.

* * *

In the first years of the world's green age—though *years* is a human word and the Garden kept no such accounting—there was a gate, and the gate had no lock.

Bram had been its keeper. He said that now in the plain words humans used, though the truth was stranger and softer: he had not guarded the gate so much as *been* the place where the gate paid attention. The covenant between the realms was not a wall with a door in it. It was a conversation, ongoing, between everything that grew in the hidden Garden and everything that hungered and built and grieved in the human world. The Garden made enchantment the way an orchard makes fruit—not by decision, but by nature, by abundance overflowing its own borders. And the human realm received it the way soil receives seed: sometimes gratefully, sometimes not, always changed.

The covenant had three keystones because a conversation needs three things to endure. A stone of *giving*, planted where the two realms touched, so that the flow between them would never be dammed by greed on one side or fear on the other. A stone of *remembering*, sunk in the place where the human dead were honored, so that each generation would know what the last had received and what it owed. And a stone of *returning*, rooted in the Garden's own heart, so that everything that flowed out would someday flow back, and nothing would be taken that was not also given.

Bram had carried the stone of returning to the heart-tree himself. He remembered the weight of it—warm, heavier than its size, humming like a hive of patient bees. He remembered the way the heart-tree had folded it into its own wood, accepting the burden, and the way the whole Garden had sighed afterward, the way a house sighs when the last guest is settled and the fire is banked and everyone is safe.

He remembered all of that. What he could not remember, no matter how he dug through the silt, was the moment it ended. There was a hole where that memory should have been, smooth-walled, deliberate. Something had been taken out of him. Something with hands.

* * *

The first humans to come through the gate had been farmers, mostly. Pilgrims. A woman with a burn-scarred arm who had wept over the sight of a river that ran silver because it was remembering being a waterfall. Bram had loved them in the diffuse, unparticular way a garden loves its gardener—not one face, but the whole laborious tenderness of the species.

Then came the ships. Then came the men with ledgers.

He remembered a man named Alderic Vellamar, because it was a name that had been carved into things, and carved things resist the drought of exile longer than spoken ones. Alderic had come as a guest. He had walked the grey-and-gold orchards with his hat in his hands and asked questions in the tone of a man who already knows the answers and is only being polite about it. *And this vitality, this flowing-out—it has no end? It renews itself? And your people do not... trade in it? Do not hoard it? How curious. How wasteful.*

Bram had shown him the stone of giving, half-buried at the threshold where the realms touched, and Alderic had looked at it for a long time with an expression Bram would spend three centuries learning to name. It was not greed, exactly. Greed wants things. This was something colder: the look of a man seeing a machine where others see a living creature, and already turning in his mind the question of who may own the machine.

*We could end sickness,* Alderic had said. *We could end famine. Your abundance, joined to our need—think what we could build together.*

And Bram, who was young in the way of spirits who have never watched anything die, had said: *Then build it with us. Give as we give.*

Alderic had smiled. Bram remembered the smile. He had spent three centuries trying to forget it and failing, because it was not a cruel smile, and that was the horror of it. It was the smile of a man who has heard an offer, weighed it, and found it charming but impractical—like a child's suggestion that the sea be made of honey.

* * *

They came back with soldiers. That was the part Bram's memory kept whole, perhaps because it was the part that needed no interpretation.

He remembered the sound first—the Garden had never heard iron, and iron has a voice, a flat ringing voice that cuts through birdsong like a blade through stem. He remembered the grey orchards going greyer where the soldiers walked, the grass browning in bootprints, not from malice but from simple incompatibility, the way a fish dies in air. He remembered the wardens—his fellow spirits, the ones who had been the Garden's attention at its edges—rushing toward the gate to sing it shut, and he remembered the singing failing, because the gate had no lock, because he had never thought it needed one, because trust had been the whole architecture.

Alderic's mages had not come to fight. They had come to *tap*.

They drove great needles of cold iron into the roots of the heart-tree, and they bound those needles with chains of written law—the first laws of Vellamare, Bram understood much later, had been plumbing. Contracts. Siphons. A dynasty's founding charter written in the language of extraction, dressed in the language of covenant so that no one would ever see the difference. *The vitality of the Garden shall flow to the crown of Vellamare and its heirs forever.* Not shared. Not exchanged. *Forever*, the way a wound is forever if you keep it open.

And the Garden, which had been made of giving, did not know how to refuse.

Bram had stood at the heart-tree with his hands pressed to its bark, and he had felt it happening: the lifeblood of his home drawn up and away, channeled through iron and law into a city being raised on the human side of the gate, a city that would be called Vellamare after the man who stole it. He had felt the rivers slow. He had felt the orchards' gold go grey. He had felt the other wardens fade—not dying, spirits did not die, but *thinning*, their attention scattering like a flock with no wind to ride.

And he had done the only thing left. He had sung the gate shut himself.

* * *

Here was the fragment that came to him in the worst hours, the one he had never told Corvane, never told anyone: the gate had not shut cleanly. A covenant three keystones deep does not simply close. It *shatters*, because it was never a wall—it was a living thing, and living things, when they are broken, break the way bone breaks, jagged, along lines no one intended.

The stone of giving had cracked at the threshold, and its shards had sunk into the human world, into the foundations of the city rising above it—down, down, into the deep places, and one fragment, the largest, had come to rest beneath the tower the founders were already building to house their stolen power. The Pale Tower. They had built their prison of magicians directly over the wound and called it protection, and the keystone lay beneath it still, humming its broken song of giving into the stone foundations, and no one had ever thought to look, because who looks for the heart of a covenant under the seat of its murderers?

The stone of remembering had broken in the flooded chapel where the human dead were honored, and its shards had scattered into the water, and that was why the dead of Vellamare were forgotten so easily, why grief there slid off the living like rain off glass. The founders had not only stolen the Garden's vitality. They had stolen the city's memory of the theft. Every generation born in Vellamare was born downstream of a forgetting, and the forgetting renewed itself, and that was the dynasty's true magic: not the vitality they siphoned, but the silence they maintained.

And the stone of returning—his stone, the one he had carried to the heart-tree—had fused itself into the heart-tree's own wood, sealing itself into the last living place, the deepest room of the dying house. It could only be reached by the tree's own consent. And the tree, Bram knew, had learned in the long grey centuries to be very, very careful with its consent.

*Three keystones,* he would tell her. *Three shards of a sentence that once read: what flows between must flow both ways, and be remembered, and be given back. The founders broke the sentence into three and buried each piece where it could do no good. And they set a warden over the ruin.*

*You?* she would ask.

*Me,* he would say, and not explain, because the explaining was the hole in his memory, the smooth-walled absence, and he was afraid of what he would find if he ever reached into it.

* * *

Because that was the last fragment, the one that came only when the tea was cold and the smugglers' lamps had burned low and Zeph had fallen asleep in the greenhouse's one dry corner with a living vine—impossible, stubborn, the last of his tending—curled near her hand as if it had already decided whose gardener she would be.

He had not been exiled. He had *left*. That was the shape of the thing the drought had taken from him and that he was only now, in this rusting greenhouse that smelled of rain and rot and stubborn green, beginning to feel the edges of.

When the gate shattered, the Garden had needed a keeper on the far side. Someone to watch the wound. Someone to wait for the mender the covenant would surely call—because covenants, even shattered ones, call their menders; that was written into the stone of returning, into the wood of the heart-tree, into the oldest water of the oldest rain. Someone had to stay in the human world, in the grey city, in the rain that was only rain, and wait, and weaken, and remember as much as remembering could survive.

And Bram had volunteered. He remembered that much. He remembered raising his hand—or whatever a spirit raises, in the council of the fading wardens—and he remembered the heart-tree's voice, which was not a voice but a season, an autumn of assent, and he remembered the price being named. *You will forget the moment of the breaking. You will forget what you were. You will keep only enough to know what you are waiting for.*

He had said yes. He had said yes because someone had to, and because the others were too thin, too scattered, and because he had been the one who trusted Alderic Vellamar's smile, and so the debt of the failure was his to carry. He had walked out through the last sliver of the closing gate into a city of wet stone and woodsmoke, and he had forgotten his name, and he had kept only the rain, and the waiting, and the shape of a promise: *the mender will come with renewal in her hands, and she will be small, and she will be tired of hiding, and she will not know what she is.*

And then, three hundred years later, a food-taster with flowers in her wake had stood in his greenhouse doorway, dripping, terrified, alive, and every root he had left had turned toward her like a leaf toward light.

* * *

Bram set down the cold cup. Across the greenhouse, Zeph slept with her brow furrowed even in dreams, and the vine near her hand had put out a new leaf since sundown—a green so fierce it hurt to look at, the first new leaf in the greenhouse in a century. Her gift reached out even in sleep, quiet and vast, renewing what it touched, and she still believed it was nothing, a faintness, a flaw.

The founders had buried the keystones. But they had buried something else too, something they had never counted on: a mender, seeded into the world like a seed into winter soil, and the winter was ending.

He looked east, through the broken glass, toward the city, toward the Pale Tower rising over the rooftops like a white finger pointed at nothing. Beneath it, humming its broken song, the first keystone waited in the dark—and between it and the woman sleeping in his greenhouse stood wards, and soldiers, and a queen's hunger, and a trial of chained magicians that would fill the tower's halls in three days' time.

*Three days,* Bram thought, and the old rage rose in him like sap, and he let it, and then he let it settle into sorrow, which was heavier and more useful. *Three days, and she will want to go down into that place. And I will have to let her.*

The vine uncurled another leaf in the dark, reaching for her hand.
