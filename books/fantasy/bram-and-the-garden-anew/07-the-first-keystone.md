## 7. The First Keystone

The summons came on a morning that smelled of iron and wet stone.

A boy in palace livery—too young for the uniform, too solemn for his years—found Zeph at her stall in the night market's empty aftermath and pressed a folded paper into her hand as though passing contraband. *By order of the Crown, the taster Zephyrine is required at the Pale Tower, third bell after dawn. Refusal will be noted.*

*Refusal will be noted.* Four words that meant: we know where you sleep. Four words that meant: you are already a name in someone's ledger.

She read it three times by the grey light leaking under her door, and each time her eyes snagged on the same detail—the seal was not the queen's running stag but a smaller mark, a tower with a keyhole where the door should be. She had seen it once before, stamped on the wrist-cuffs of the men who had dragged Marta Ellery from her loom two streets away, who had taken her still wearing one shoe because there hadn't been time to find the other.

They wanted her tongue. Of course they did. A trial of magicians required proof of poisoning, or the pretense of it, and no one in Vellamare could taste a lie in a cup better than the woman whose gift hid so well even she had spent seven years pretending it wasn't there.

Corvane would have told her not to go. Bram would have told her something stranger—that the tower itself might want her there, that keystones had a way of calling to the hands meant to hold them. But Corvane was somewhere in the undercity moving smuggled families through the drainage tunnels, and Bram was fading by inches in his greenhouse, his skin going the color of old paper, his voice thinner every day she left him alone. So she decided alone, which was how all her worst decisions had always been made.

She went because the keystone was beneath the Pale Tower. Corvane had said it like a curse when he'd finally traced the first fragment's location—a thing buried in the roots of the very building where the crown caged its gifted, wrapped in wards and stone and the accumulated dread of everyone who had ever been marched through its gates. *The founders were never subtle,* he'd said. *They hid their theft inside their power. Who would think to dig beneath the prison?*

Who indeed. Only a taster, perhaps. Only someone whose whole life had been an education in what people swallow without looking.

Zeph dressed plainly, bound her hair back, and put on the plain grey apron of her trade like armor. In her sleeve, sewn into the seam where no searcher had ever thought to look, she tucked the charm Odile had given her a lifetime ago—a knotted thread that dulled the scent of magic, that made a gifted person read as merely tired. It had never been tested against anything stronger than a market guard's bored suspicion. Tonight it would meet the Pale Tower itself.

She tried not to think about Marta Ellery's single shoe.

* * *

The Pale Tower did not look pale. That was the first lie of it. Up close it was the color of bone left too long in weather, yellowed and faintly translucent, so that in certain light the upper stories seemed to glow from within—as if the building had swallowed something luminous and could not digest it. Zeph felt it before she reached the gate: a pressure against her senses like deep water, like standing at the edge of a well and hearing, far below, something breathing.

Her magic stirred in answer, unbidden. Seven years of holding it down, of keeping it small as a candleflame cupped in two hands, and here it rose toward that pressure like a plant toward light. She crushed it flat. Sweat prickled along her hairline.

Inside the gate, the world changed texture. Sound went strange—footsteps landing soft as if on wool, voices arriving slightly after the mouths that made them. The corridors were lit by lamps that burned without flickering, and the air tasted of cold metal and, beneath it, something older: green things rotting sweetly, sap, rain in a closed room. Zeph's tongue, traitorous instrument, catalogued it all. *Underneath,* she thought. *Whatever they buried, it's underneath, and it's still alive.*

A steward met her in the second corridor—a narrow woman with ink-stained fingers and the courteous blankness of someone who had learned that curiosity was a disease the tower cured permanently. "You'll taste for the trial chamber," she said. "Three sittings. The accused are given bread and water between sessions; you'll clear each portion. The Lord Justiciar is anxious about contamination." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile. "There have been attempts."

"Attempts," Zeph repeated carefully.

"The gifted are resourceful." The steward said it the way one says *rats are clever*, and led her down, and down, past cells whose doors had no bars but only seams, seamless doors of grey wood that breathed faint warmth, and Zeph understood with a lurch that the doors were not doors at all but mouths, that the tower fed somehow on what was kept behind them, and she made herself stop understanding, made herself be only a taster, small and dull and safe.

Almost safe. Because at the end of the cell row, one seam stood open a hand's width, and through it came a smell she knew—lye soap and wool and the particular herb blend Marta Ellery boiled for her cough—and a voice, cracked and low, humming a spinning song that every woman in the Weavers' Row knew before she knew her own name.

Zeph did not turn her head. Turning her head was a luxury purchased with other people's lives. She walked on with her tray and her apron and her dead calm face, and behind her the humming continued, steady as a loom, and she carried it up the stairs like a stone in her chest.

* * *

The trial chamber sat near the tower's crown, which surprised her until she thought about it. Of course. The founders had built upward and downward both, the courtroom suspended between the prisoners below and the sky above, so that judgment fell in every direction. Tall windows let in a light that seemed reluctant, filtered through glass thick as palm-bread. At the chamber's center stood a table of black wood, and on it, the evidence: cups, ewers, plates of bread, a roasted bird glazed until it shone like lacquer.

The Lord Justiciar was a heavy man with a scholar's stoop and a headsman's eyes. He looked at Zeph the way a man looks at a tool he has rented. "You are the one from the banquet," he said. "The poison at the queen's table. Instinct, you called it."

"I have a sensitive palate, my lord."

"Mm." He watched her a moment too long. "The Tower pays for sensitivity. Remember that."

Three sittings. Three accused brought up in chains from the depths, each given the plate, each watching her eat from it first. This was the theater of it: the taster swallows, the court waits, nothing kills her, therefore the food is innocent, therefore whatever crime remains belongs wholly to the prisoner. She had performed this ritual a hundred times in smaller rooms. Here it had the quality of liturgy, grim and precise, and she understood that her body was being used as an argument for the gallows.

The first accused was a boy of maybe sixteen, a lamplighter's apprentice, accused of charming his flame to burn colors. He watched her chew his bread with an expression she recognized from mirrors: the careful blankness of someone hiding something enormous behind a very small face. When she swallowed and nodded, his shoulders dropped—not with relief, she saw, but with resignation. He had known the tasting proved nothing except that she was permitted to live.

The second was an old man, a hedge-healer from the river districts, hands stained brown to the wrist with yarrow and comfrey. His charges ran longer than her arm. He ate nothing; he stared at the window light as if memorizing it.

The third sitting began at dusk, and the guards brought up Marta Ellery.

She had lost weight in eleven days. Her wrists were ringed with healing scabs where the cuffs had bitten. But her spine was straight as a warp thread, and when her eyes found Zeph across the black table, there was a flicker—recognition, then something worse, hope—and Zeph watched it happen and could do nothing, absolutely nothing, except pick up the bread and tear it and place a piece in her own mouth and chew.

Marta's hope died politely, the way poor people's hopes die, without fuss. She lowered her eyes. And Zeph tasted the bread—stale, harmless, ordinary as dirt—and swallowed it past the knot in her throat, and gave the Justiciar her nod, and heard the sentence read in the same flat voice that had read the others': *transport to the Pale Tower's custody pending the equinox judgments.*

Pending the equinox. Everyone in the room heard it and pretended not to. After the equinox, the sentences would become real, and real, in Isolde's Vellamare, meant the quiet wagons that left before dawn and did not arrive anywhere anyone could name.

*After the equinox.* The deadline Bram had given her. The night the Garden died, the night the city forgot itself. The crown had built its calendar of cruelty atop the calendar of catastrophe, whether it knew it or not.

*Later,* she told herself, the oldest lie in her repertoire. *I'll come back for them later. Once I have the stone. Once I understand what I'm doing. Later.*

She was already learning how heavy that word could be.

* * *

The steward dismissed her after the third sitting—"payment at the gate, standard rates"—and Zeph walked down and down again, ostensibly toward the exit, letting her feet learn the tower's grammar. Staircases spiraled counterclockwise, always counterclockwise, as if the whole building wound itself shut. Guards stood at landings in pairs, bored, armored, human. The wards were elsewhere. She could feel them now that she'd stopped pretending she couldn't: a hum in the masonry, a taste like copper and moonlight layered over everything, strongest below.

Below. Always below.

At the ground floor she asked leave to relieve herself before the long walk home, and the guard, a boy with chapped lips and a soldier's contempt for tasters, jerked his thumb toward a passage marked with a faded sign. She thanked him in a voice pitched to be forgettable, and went the wrong way instead.

The passage narrowed. The lamps thinned. The tower's noise fell away behind her until her own pulse was the loudest thing, and the green-rot-sweetness in the air thickened until she could nearly see it, a haze like pollen. Her magic rose again, harder this time, straining against her suppression like a dog against a chain, and the knotted thread in her sleeve grew warm, then hot, working to smother her scent while the tower's wards cast about for exactly such a scent.

She passed a door of grey wood, seamless, breathing. Another. Then the corridor ended in a wall that was not a wall.

It looked like plaster and stone, but her tongue—her impossible, honest tongue—tasted the truth of it: hollow, and behind the hollowness, water. Old water, living water, water that remembered rain. The founders had built their prison over a spring. Over something more than a spring. She set her palm against the plaster and felt, faint as a heartbeat through a floor, a rhythm. Slow. Enormous. Patient.

*Here,* said every sense she owned. *It's here, it's sleeping, it's waiting for you, hurry, hurry—*

Footsteps. Above, and coming down.

Zeph's mind went white and then, blessedly, went cold—the cold of the banquet hall, the cold of seven years' practice. There was no hiding the wall; there was no time to dig. But there was the tray she still carried, and the ewer of wine from the trial chamber that no one had thought to reclaim, and there was the fact that a taster found wandering was suspicious but a taster *sick* was merely pathetic.

She drank two fingers of the wine, held it, and forced herself to gag—genuinely, horribly, doubling over the tray, retching up wine and bread and the knot in her throat. By the time the boots arrived she was on her knees, shaking, grey-faced, a picture of a woman whose nerves had finally failed her after a day of swallowing for condemned strangers.

"She got lost," the chapped-lipped guard said, disgusted. "They cry half the time, these tasters. First trial-day."

"Get her out," said the older one, wrinkling his nose at the smell. "And dock her pay for the ewer."

They walked her up the spiral, one on either side, steering her by the elbows like something breakable and worthless, and Zeph kept her eyes wet and unfocused and let her body tremble on cue, and all the while, beneath the performance, her mind laid the corridor out in perfect memory: forty-one steps past the second breathing door, the false wall, the rhythm beneath it, the ward-hum's weakest point where two lamp-brackets met and the copper taste thinned to almost nothing.

Forty-one steps. She counted them twice on the way out, just to be sure.

* * *

Outside, evening had come down blue and cold. The payment—eleven silver bits, insultingly exact—sat in her palm like a joke. She walked six streets before she let herself stop shaking, ducked into the recessed doorway of a shuttered dye-shop, and pressed her back against the wood and breathed.

She had gone in expecting to take the stone tonight. She came out having only found its grave. The wall was warded—she'd felt the hum wrap around that stretch of corridor like a bandage—and wards of the Pale Tower were not charms to be slipped past with a knotted thread and a nervous smile. They needed unraveling from within, or drowning, or—

Or forgetting, said a small voice in her that sounded uncomfortably like Bram. Everything can be forgotten, little taster. Even towers. Especially towers.

And underneath the planning, underneath the cold competence she wore like her grey apron, the guilt sat where it had settled during the third sitting and refused to move. Marta's face when the hope went out of it. The boy lamplighter's resigned shoulders. The old healer memorizing the light. *Later,* she had promised them, silently, without their knowledge or consent. *Later I'll come back.* But later was a currency she was spending on their behalf without asking, and the interest was accruing in a place with no windows, behind seamless doors, in cells that breathed.

She thought of Odile, who had chosen registration, who had chosen survival with her eyes open, who would look at this arithmetic and say: *you saved yourself to save everyone, that's not betrayal, that's strategy.* And Odile would be right, and it wouldn't help, because Marta Ellery had once given her a heel of bread on a bad market day and asked nothing for it, and love does not accept payment in strategy.

A shadow detached itself from the deeper shadow beside the dye-shop's rain barrel, and Zeph's heart lurched before she saw the gait—the rolling, patient gait, the smell of rain that had no clouds above them.

"You're limping," Bram said.

"I'm not."

"You are. Inside, where I can't see." He studied her face in the dark, and whatever he found there made him go still. "You found it."

"I found where it sleeps." Her voice came out scraped. "Behind a wall. Under wards. Forty-one steps past the second door, and I walked away, Bram. I walked right past it, and upstairs they were reading sentences, and after the equinox—" She stopped. Pressed her knuckles to her mouth. "I tasted her bread. I stood there and I tasted it and nodded, and my nod helped hang her."

Bram was quiet for a long moment. Rain-smell and patience.

"Do you know what the heart-tree does," he said at last, "with everything the Garden takes in? Every leaf that falls, every branch that dies?" He turned his ruined, gentle face toward the distant tower, its bone-yellow crown catching the last light. "It doesn't throw any of it away. It pulls it down into the root and keeps it, and turns it into next year's growth. Grief isn't the price of the work, Zephyrine. Grief is the *material*. The covenant was built on it—memory freely given, sorrow turned to soil." His voice frayed, steadied. "So carry her. Carry all of them. But don't you dare put the stone down to do it. There are more behind those doors than your neighbor, and if the Garden falls on equinox night, the tower won't release them. It will simply forget them. Do you understand? Not kill. *Forget.* No name, no grave, no one left who ever knew they existed. That is what we are racing."

Zeph looked at the tower a long time. Its upper windows glowed faintly, that indigestible light, and she thought of the breathing doors, and the spring behind the false wall, beating its slow enormous heart.

"Then I need to get back inside," she said. "Past the wards. Past the guards. Down forty-one steps."

Bram smiled, and there was something in it that was older than his beggar's rags, something that had once tended orchards under a different sky.

"No," he said. "You need to get *under* it. The founders weren't subtle, little taster—they stole the Garden's spring to fill their tower's moat, and water remembers the way home." He held out his hand, and in his palm lay something small and damp and impossibly green: a seed that glowed, faintly, like a coal refusing to die. "Smugglers' tunnels run beneath the whole quarter. Corvane owes me a favor, and you owe him a visit anyway. We dig up through the water."

She took the seed. It was warm as a held hand.

"And the prisoners?" she asked. "Marta? The boy?"

Bram's smile faded into something harder and kinder.

"One grave at a time," he said. "But hear me—we come back for them before the equinox, or everything we're doing means nothing. That's not strategy. That's the covenant." He turned toward the darkening street, toward the undercity, and the rain-smell moved with him. "Come. Corvane will want to know what you tasted."

She followed him into the dark, counting in her head, all the way down: forty-one steps, forty-one names, and eleven days left to keep a promise she had made in silence to a woman in chains who didn't know yet that anyone was coming at all.
