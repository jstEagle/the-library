## 8. Hunted

The bells told her before anyone did.

Zeph was in the smugglers' kitchen below the Bellows, hands wrapped around a chipped bowl of barley soup, when the noon bells began to ring wrong—too many, too fast, the pattern the city used only for fire or treason. Corvane's people froze mid-motion. Somewhere above them, the great bronze throats of the Cathedral of Tides poured their alarm down through stone and sewer-water and the bones of the old drowned city, and Zeph felt the sound settle into her chest like a stone dropped into a well.

She didn't need to hear the crier. She already knew.

The keystone was gone from beneath the Pale Tower, and the tower had woken up angry.

"Finish your soup," Corvane said quietly. He was mending a net by the stove, though no net had been mended in that room in years; it was what his hands did when his mind was working. "You'll need your legs."

"They know it was me."

"They know *something* was taken." He didn't look up. "What they know about *you* is another matter. The tower's wards were keyed to blood and gift. When a renewal-talent touched that vault, the wards would have sung your nature to whoever was listening." Now he looked up, and his dark eyes were unreadable in the stove-light. "Hunt-Captain Voss listens very well."

The name went through Zeph like a fishhook. Sarel Voss. She had seen the Hunt-Captain twice from a distance—once at a royal banquet, standing behind the queen's shoulder like a drawn blade given a uniform; once in the night market, walking alone through the crowd of charm-sellers and fortune-tellers with her hands clasped behind her back, not arresting anyone, just *watching*, the way a heron watches water. There had been something in her stillness that Zeph had recognized without wanting to. The stillness of a person holding something down.

"She's gifted," Zeph said. It wasn't a guess. It had sat in her since the market, unexamined, the way you don't examine a splinter.

Corvane's net-mending stopped. "Careful," he said softly. "That kind of seeing is what got your neighbor taken."

*Marisol*, Zeph thought, and the guilt arrived on schedule, punctual as the bells. She had left Marisol in a cell beneath the Pale Tower with a stolen stone in her pocket and a promise she hadn't been able to keep: *I'll come back for you. I'll come back for all of you.* The promise had felt true when she made it. Promises made in vaults always did.

"Where's Bram?" she asked.

"Gone out at dawn. He goes out every dawn." Corvane's voice did something careful around the words. "The greenhouse keeps him alive. Every hour he spends outside it costs him. He doesn't like it mentioned."

Zeph thought of Bram as she had last seen him—sitting among his impossible plants in the derelict greenhouse, his grey coat smelling of rain, his eyes the color of the space between leaf and light. He had laughed at her the first day, a sound like water finding a new channel. He had also, she now understood, been *dimmer* each time she saw him. The edges of him softer. A candle burning down in a house with no more candles.

Every hour outside the Garden. And he spent them walking her through the city, teaching her the old names of streets, showing her where the keystones slept.

"Soup," Corvane reminded her. "Legs."

She drank it in four swallows, scalding her tongue, tasting—because she could not help it, because her tongue was a taster's tongue and would work even at the end of the world—barley, bay, a ghost of smoked pork, and under it all the faint iron tang of fear that had gotten into the kitchen's air. Then she pulled her hood up and went up into the city that was hunting her.

* * *

Vellamare in alarm was a different city, the way a face in fury is a different face.

The streets had that tightened quality, that held breath. Criers at every corner: *By order of Her Majesty, the Hunt-Captain's warrant extends to all persons harboring the thief of the Tower vault. Reward: two hundred crowns. Mercy for the harboring, if the harbored are given up.* Two hundred crowns was a fisherman's lifetime. Zeph kept her head down and her gait ordinary—she was good at ordinary, she had spent seven years practicing ordinary—and made for the night market district, where Odile's stall stood shuttered, where the narrow streets gave a hundred exits and the rooftops nearly touched.

She was three streets from the market when she felt it: a pressure at the back of her neck, faint and cold, like the draft from a door opening in a distant room.

Her gift had been flaring since the night it bloomed flowers through the cobblestones—unruly, untrained, responding to her fear like a dog to thunder. But this was not her gift. This was *other* magic, moving through the city, and it was looking for something.

She slipped into an alley, pressed her back to wet brick, and let her senses open the way Bram had taught her—not wide, never wide, but a crack, a keyhole's worth. Through the keyhole she saw the city's magic the way you see a current in dark water: by what it moved. And moving toward her, three streets away, unhurried, was a thread of cold silver light, disciplined as a plumb line, and at the end of the thread—

Sarel Voss stood at the mouth of the street, alone, no soldiers, her grey cloak beaded with drizzle, and she was not looking at the alley. She was looking at the *air* above the alley, at the place where Zeph's opened senses must have shown themselves like a lit window.

"Renewal-talent," Voss said. Her voice carried without effort, the way a blade carries its edge. "I can hear you breathing. It's a lovely gift, you know. The wards sang for an hour after you left. I've never heard anything sing like that." A pause. "Come out, and no one else gets hurt. I know you left people in the tower. I know you think you're coming back for them. Help me understand you, and maybe you can."

*She knows about Marisol.* Zeph's heart slammed. *She's using them as bait, or she's using them as truth, and I can't tell which, and that's the whole trick of her.*

"I didn't steal anything," Zeph called back, and was almost surprised to find her voice steady. Seven years of tasting poison at a queen's table had taught her this much: the steadier the danger, the steadier the voice. "I took back something that was stolen first."

Something moved through Voss's stillness—Zeph felt it through the keyhole of her senses, a tremor in the silver thread, like a plumb line brushed by a hand. "That's what they all say," Voss said, and there was something under the words, something tired. "That's what *I* said, once."

Then the silver thread *struck*.

It came down the alley like a harpoon of cold light, and Zeph threw herself sideways on pure taster's instinct—the same instinct that had pulled a poisoned cup from the queen's lips a heartbeat before the old ambassador drank. The thread took the brick where her head had been and the stone *frosted*, crackling white, and Zeph ran.

* * *

The rooftops of the Tanner's Quarter were built for smugglers and cats, and Zeph was neither, but terror is an excellent teacher and she had been taught all night by the sound of boots below her.

She went up a drainpipe, over a drying-shed, along a ridge of slate slick with drizzle, the city dropping away on either side into lanes that smelled of tanning acid and wet wool. Behind her, impossibly, Voss came too—no scrambling, no sliding, the Hunt-Captain moving across the rooftops with the terrible economy of someone whose gift was *precision*, each step placed as if the roofs had been measured for her. The silver thread of her magic flickered ahead of her like a hound's nose, tasting the air for Zeph's trail.

*She's gifted and she hunts her own kind.* Zeph vaulted a chimney, felt her ankle turn, kept going. *And she said "that's what I said, once." What did you do, Sarel Voss? What did they hold over you?*

The thought arrived at the worst moment, in the middle of a jump between the tanning-shed and the dye-works, and it cost her the jump. Her foot came down on a loose slate. The slate went one way; she went the other.

The fall was only one story onto a lower roof, but the roof was old and its lead flashing was rotten, and she went through it shoulder-first into darkness, into dust and pigeon filth and the collapsed beams of an abandoned loft, and pain went up her arm like a struck bell. She heard, above and behind her, Voss land on the broken edge of the roof. Heard the Hunt-Captain's breathing. Heard her stop.

"Renewal-talent." Voss's voice came down through the hole, and it had changed—it was quieter now, almost gentle, and the gentleness was the worst thing Zeph had heard all day. "I can hear your blood. Do you know what that is? My gift. I hear bodies. Hearts, breath, blood in the vessels. The crown calls it a hunting gift. It's very useful for hunting." A pause, and the drizzle ticked on the broken roof. "Do you know what it was before the crown named it? My grandmother had it. She was a midwife. She could hear a child's heart before the mother could feel it kick. She could hear when a fever broke. She heard the moment her own heart began to fail, and she had time to say goodbye to everyone she loved." Another pause, longer. "They registered her. Then they took her. Then they used her. So I know exactly what I'm doing, girl, and I hate it, and I'm going to do it anyway, because the alternative is my brother in the Pale Tower. Come out. Please. Don't make me come down there."

Zeph lay in the dark with her arm screaming and her blood loud enough that she could almost hear it herself, and she thought: *she's telling me the truth. That's the cruelest part. She's telling me the truth so I'll come quietly, and if I come quietly, Marisol rots, and the Garden dies, and every gift in this city dies with it.*

"I'm sorry about your brother," Zeph said. "And your grandmother. I mean it. I'm a taster—I know what it is to be useful and owned." She rolled onto her feet, cradling her arm, and looked around the dark loft. Broken beams. A rotted skylight at the far end. Rain coming through in threads. "But I can't come out."

She ran for the skylight.

The silver thread took her mid-stride.

It caught her across the back—not the killing strike Voss could surely have made, but a binding, cold light wrapping her torso like a band of ice, and it slammed her into the floor and the world went white and then grey and she tasted, of all things, *frost and iron*, the taste of Voss's magic, and through the roaring in her ears she heard boots on broken beams, coming closer, unhurried, and a voice saying, "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. Hold still—"

And then the loft filled with the smell of rain.

Not drizzle. *Rain*—deep rain, warm rain, rain on stone in summer, rain on roots, the smell of the greenhouse, of Bram's grey coat, and with it came a light that was not silver but green, green as the first leaf of a stubborn spring, and the binding on Zeph's back *loosened* like a fist unclenching, like ice remembering it was water.

Voss stopped. Zeph, cheek against the filthy floor, saw the boots stop three paces away, and saw—beyond them, at the mouth of the loft where no door stood open—a figure that had not been there a moment before.

He was dimmer than she had ever seen him. That was the first thing. Bram stood in the broken doorway with the rain-light coming off him in slow pulses, and where a week ago he had been a candle, now he was a candle at the end of its wick, the flame thin and blue and fighting. His coat hung on him as if he had lost weight since morning. His eyes, though—his eyes were the same, the color of the space between leaf and light, and they were fixed on Sarel Voss with an expression Zeph had never seen on him: recognition, and beneath it, grief.

"You," Voss said. Her voice had gone strange. "You're— I heard stories from my grandmother. The green man. The warden of the—" She stopped herself. "You're supposed to be a story."

"I'm supposed to be a great many things," Bram said. His voice was hoarse, worn thin, but it filled the loft the way water fills a cup. "A story. A beggar. A shadow. Once, a guardian." He took a step forward, and where his foot fell, a seam of green moss woke in the rotten boards and then, piteously, faded, the ground too starved to keep it. "And you are a daughter of a line that used to sing to gardens, Hunt-Captain. Did you know that? Your grandmother's mother sang to the orchards outside the old north gate. The city has forgotten. I haven't. Forgetting is the crown's trade. Remembering is mine."

"Stand away from her." But Voss's silver thread had gone slack, coiled at her side like a whip someone had lost the will to crack. "She's under warrant. She robbed the tower."

"She took back a stone that was stolen from the world," Bram said. "And you know it. You heard the wards sing. Tell me, Hunt-Captain—when the renewal-magic sang through that vault, what did it sound like to you?" He tilted his head. "You've heard a hundred gifted people taken. Did any of them ever make the wards sing like *that*?"

Silence. The drizzle ticked. Zeph's arm throbbed in time with her heart.

"Like a bell," Voss said at last, very quietly. "Like a bell being rung after a hundred years of silence." And then, with a violence that was almost despair: "It doesn't matter what it sounded like. It doesn't matter what she is. My brother is in that tower, and the price of his life is her in chains, and I will pay it, because I am the only person in this city who pays what things cost." The silver thread began to rise again. "I'm sorry, old one. Whatever you are. Run, and I'll pretend I lost you. Stay, and I take you both."

"Then take us," Bram said, "and lose everything," and he raised one hand, and Zeph felt the greenhouse come into him—the whole dying Garden, the grey orchards, the sleeping rivers, all of it gathered into that one thin flame of a man—and the rain-light *bloomed*.

It was not an attack. That was the terrible beauty of it; Zeph understood that even with her face in the dirt. It was a *showing*. For one heartbeat the loft was an orchard in spring, blossom-thick, the air warm and loud with bees, and Zeph saw Sarel Voss stand in the middle of it with her face undone, her gift wide open, hearing what a living Garden sounded like—ten thousand hearts that were not hearts, sap rising, roots drinking, the slow green pulse of a world—and heard her make a sound Zeph had never heard a hunt-captain make.

Then it was gone. Bram staggered. The light guttered down to almost nothing, and he caught himself on a broken beam, and Zeph saw with horror that his hands had gone translucent at the edges, like paper held to a window.

"Bram—"

"Go," he said, and his voice was nearly gone now, a thread of itself. "The hole in the wall behind you, girl. The tunnel. Corvane's people know it. Go *now*—she can't take us both and she knows it, and I—" he smiled, and it was the water-finding-a-channel smile, worn down to the bedrock of itself, "—I am very hard to arrest. Ask anyone. No one has managed it in three hundred years."

Voss stood with her thread raised and her face wet—not the drizzle, Zeph realized; her face was wet with something else—and for a long moment the loft held its breath.

Then the Hunt-Captain did the last thing Zeph expected. She lowered the thread. She looked at Bram, at his fading hands, and she said, in a voice like a woman stepping off a ledge: "The Garden. It's real. It's *dying*." And then, before either of them could answer, before Zeph could decide whether this was mercy or a trap more intricate than any she had escaped: "I have until the equinox to deliver a thief, or the queen sends someone who doesn't listen to what the wards sing. Three days. Whatever you're doing—whatever this is—" her jaw tightened, and the silver thread coiled itself away into nothing, "—you'd better be doing it fast."

She turned and walked out into the drizzle, and her boots on the roof-boards were steady as a metronome, and she did not look back.

* * *

The tunnel behind the wall was exactly where Bram had said, a smuggler's crawl smelling of river-mud and lamp oil, and Zeph dragged them both into it because Bram could no longer quite walk a straight line.

She got him two hundred paces in, to a dry shelf of stone where Corvane's people kept a cache of lamps, and got him sitting, and got a lamp lit, and then she made herself look at his hands.

The edges of him were still faintly translucent. The green pulse under his skin had slowed to something like a tide very far out.

"You spent what you didn't have," she said. Her voice cracked in the middle. Her arm was screaming, her back was frostbitten where the binding had caught her, and she found she didn't care about any of it. "You said every hour outside the greenhouse costs you. What did that cost? What did showing her the Garden *cost*?"

"Everything I had," Bram said simply. "Which is why I spent it. Girl—Zeph. Listen to me, because I may be a little unclear by morning." He leaned his head back against the stone and closed his eyes, and the lamp-light moved on his face like water. "That woman will come for us again. She has to; her brother's life is the coin. But she has *heard* the Garden now. You cannot unhear a thing like that. When the moment comes—the real moment, the one at the equinox—she will not be standing where the queen thinks she's standing." He opened his eyes, and they were dim, but they were still the color of the space between leaf and light. "I heard her heart, when the orchard showed itself. It broke a little. Things that break a little can break the right way."

"You need the Garden. You need to *go back*—" Zeph stopped, because the next words were the ones she'd been circling for days: *and we only have two keystones, and the third is inside the Garden itself, and every day you fade, and I don't—* She didn't know how to finish it. She found she was holding his translucent hand, her small warm fingers over his fading ones, and that some part of her gift had risen without being asked, that faint renewal-warmth flowing out of her and into him like water finding a dry root.

Bram looked down at their hands. Something moved through the translucency—a deepening, a green thread waking along his fingers where hers touched—and he made a small sound, half laughter, half something older.

"Well," he said. "There it is."

"What?"

"That. What you're doing. You don't even know you're doing it." He turned his hand gently under hers, and the green thread brightened. "The first covenant was made exactly like this. Not with stones. Not with words. One hand on a fading thing, giving without being asked." His eyes came up to hers, and for a moment the dimness in them burned away and she saw what he must have been, three hundred years ago, before the founders and the theft and the long grey exile. "The keystones will mend the covenant, Zeph. But *this* is the covenant. This is what it's made of. Remember that, when the heart-tree asks you its price. Remember what you just did, and what it cost you, and what it didn't."

Zeph looked at their joined hands—his fading, her flaring, the green thread between them pulsing slow as a sleeping heartbeat—and somewhere above them, muffled by earth and stone, the bells of Vellamare rang the hour, and beneath the bells she heard another sound, faint and far off, that she recognized with a cold drop in her stomach.

Dogs. And behind the dogs, a whistle she knew from the night market: three notes, repeated, the crown's signal for *quarry sighted, all hunters converge*.

Not Voss. Voss had walked away. This was the rest of the hunt—the hunt that didn't listen to bells, didn't hear what wards sang, didn't break a little when it saw an orchard.

Bram heard it too. He tried to stand and couldn't, and Zeph caught him under the arm, and his weight against her was horrifyingly slight, like carrying a bundle of dry sticks.

"Tunnel forks ahead," he whispered. "Left goes to the river. Right goes to—" he paused, and his fading face did something complicated, something that was almost apology, "—right goes to the night market. To your friend Odile's street."

Zeph stood in the dark with a fading spirit on her shoulder and hounds coming, and thought of Odile's face the last time they'd argued, *invisibility will hold, Odile, it has to*—and chose, with her heart hammering, the right-hand fork.
