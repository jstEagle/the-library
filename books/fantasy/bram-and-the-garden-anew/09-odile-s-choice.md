## 9. Odile's Choice

The right-hand tunnel smelled of tallow and river-mud, and then, faintly, of cinnamon—Odile's cinnamon, the cheap spiced oil she wore in her hair, the one Zeph had teased her about for seven years. Her heart lurched toward it like a dog toward a familiar hand.

Bram was nearly weightless now. She had stopped being able to feel the difference between his arm and her own sleeve. Every few steps he murmured directions—*left at the broken sluice, mind the third stair, it lies*—in a voice growing thinner, as though he were being spoken through a wall from very far away. The hounds had fallen behind at the fork; she could no longer hear their baying, only her own blood, loud and stupid in her ears.

The tunnel ended at a rusted grate, and beyond the grate was the underside of the night market: the cellar-doors of the spice sellers, the drains where the fishmongers' meltwater ran, the smell of a hundred suppers she would never taste again in innocence. Above her, muffled by boards and cobblestone, the market was waking for the evening. She could hear the charm-sellers crying their wares. Once, that had been her own voice.

"Odile's stall is the third from the fountain," Bram said. He slid from her shoulder and stood on his own, barely, swaying like a reed. His eyes, when they found hers, were terribly clear. "You should know what you're walking into before you walk into it."

"I know Odile."

"You knew Odile before the Proclamation." He said it gently, which was worse. "People under fear are not the people they were. I have watched a city learn that lesson for three hundred years."

Zeph pressed her palms against the grate. "She wouldn't give me up."

"No," Bram agreed. "She'd give up everyone *around* you. It's a subtler arithmetic, and it feels like loyalty right up until it doesn't." He coughed, a dry sound like leaves. "Go. But go as a stranger. Go as someone she's never met. And Zeph—" his hand caught her wrist, cold as well-water, "—whatever you find in her face, don't let her read yours."

* * *

The night market had changed in the weeks since Zeph had last worked it, and the change was not in the stalls but in the silences between them. Where the charm-sellers had once called out boldly—*wardings, warmings, charms for the fearful*—now they murmured, and glanced at the corners where the queen's criers had nailed their notices. The registration decree hung there in three copies, its wax seal fat and red as a wound. *All gifted persons will present themselves at the Pale Tower for assessment and protection.* The word *protection* had been underlined twice by some clerk's careful hand, and someone else had scratched beneath it in charcoal: *the way a spider protects a fly.*

Odile's stall was the third from the fountain, exactly as it had always been. She sat behind it wrapped in her grey shawl, older-looking than her thirty years, her dark hair oiled with that cheap cinnamon, her hands sorting dried lavender with the brisk competence of a woman who had decided the world would be survived by pretending it was ordinary.

Zeph had planned to walk past. She had planned it in the tunnel, rehearsed it: pass the stall, mark the street, find Odile later, somewhere private. But her feet betrayed her, or her heart did, and she found herself standing at the fountain's rim with a copper in her hand like any customer, buying time.

"One charm for safe travel," she said, in a voice pitched low and strange.

Odile didn't look up. "No travel charms tonight. Wardings only. The crier's been round." Her hands kept sorting. "Wardings are permitted. Travel charms suggest *flight*, and flight suggests guilt, and the watch has started asking questions about who buys what."

The voice was Odile's. The rhythm was not. Zeph stood very still.

"Then a warding," she said.

"Four coppers." Odile looked up then, and Zeph watched the recognition move through her friend's face like a lamp being carried through a dark house—first the eyes, then the stillness, then the terrible controlled blankness of a woman remembering that the whole street could be watching. Odile set down the lavender. She wrapped a sprig of it in brown paper with hands that did not tremble, because Odile's hands never trembled, they only went very precise. "Four coppers," she repeated. "And take it wrapped. Don't open it in the street."

* * *

They met in the drying loft above the tannery, a place they had used a hundred times—Zeph remembered laughing there once until her ribs ached, sharing stolen honey-cakes, Odile braiding her hair and saying *you worry like an old woman, Zeph, no one is looking at you, you're the smallest thing in Vellamare.* The loft smelled of hides and lye and, underneath it, the river. Moonlight came through the slatted roof in pale bars.

Odile arrived ten minutes after her, alone, having taken three turnings Zeph did not follow. She came up the ladder and stood at the top of it for a long moment, looking at Zeph as if confirming something to herself.

"You look terrible," Odile said.

"You look like a registered woman."

The words came out sharper than Zeph intended. She saw them land. She saw Odile flinch and then, worse, saw her *settle*—saw her friend put on the expression of someone who has already rehearsed this argument alone, in the dark, and armed herself for it.

"Someone had to stay above ground," Odile said quietly. "Someone had to keep the stall. Keep the customers. Keep listening. Do you know what I hear in that market, Zeph? Every night. Every fear. Every name the watch asks after. I hear things that keep people alive."

"Is that what it's called now."

"Don't." Odile's voice cracked and reassembled itself. "Don't you dare stand in a tannery loft in stolen boots and judge me for surviving. You think I wanted this? They came for Maren Voss's boy first—you remember the cooper's boy, the one who could light candles with a thought. Fourteen years old, Zeph. They took him to the Pale Tower *for his protection* and his mother got back a sealed letter and a lock of hair. A *lock of hair.*" Her hands were shaking now, finally, and she seemed furious about it, as though her own fear were a betrayal. "I registered because a registered woman can walk. Can listen. Can stand in a market and hear which names are being asked after, and warn—"

"Warn whom?" Zeph stepped closer. The moonlight bars fell between them like the bars of something. "Odile. Who did you warn about the tunnel under the bellows? Because the hounds found us there. The hounds knew where to look."

The silence that followed was the longest of Zeph's life.

She watched Odile's face and saw everything move across it—shock first, genuine shock, and Zeph clung to that, *please let that be genuine*—and then something worse, something that was not shock at all but calculation, the quick flicker of a woman deciding what could be safely admitted.

"I told them about the smugglers' route," Odile said at last. "The route. Not the people on it. I told them the underground was moving someone through the bellows district because they already knew, Zeph, they already had it from a cooper's apprentice they turned last month, and if I said nothing they'd have known I knew, and then I'd be in the Tower and there'd be *no one* in the market listening—"

"And the people on the route?"

"I didn't know it was you." Odile's voice broke clean through this time. "I didn't know. I swear it on my mother's grave, on the salt and the lavender, I didn't know it was you until you spoke at my stall just now and my whole soul fell through the floor." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "But if it *had* been a stranger, some smuggler's runner with a family, some child—would I have told them anyway? Yes. Yes, Zeph, I would have, and I have to live in the same skin as that answer, and it's the worst thing I own."

Zeph had imagined this confrontation a dozen ways in the tunnel—rage, tears, Odile begging forgiveness, Odile spitting at her. She had not imagined this: Odile standing in the moonlight telling the truth like a woman laying out her own organs on a table, saying *look, look what surviving has made of me.*

"You informed," Zeph said. It wasn't a question and it wasn't forgiveness. It was just the fact, set down between them like a stone.

"I informed." Odile lifted her chin, and her eyes were streaming, and she did not wipe them. "And I have kept eleven people out of the Tower this month with what I've heard. And I would do both again tomorrow. That's what the queen has made of us. That's the arithmetic. You wanted invisibility to hold. Well, it held for everyone but you, and now I'm the one standing in the light so you can stand in the dark, and you hate me for it, and I understand, and I'd still do it."

"I don't hate you." Zeph's voice came out small. "I wish I did. It would be simpler."

"Nothing about you was ever simple." Odile laughed, wet and broken. "Seven years I've loved you like a sister and you never once let me see all of you. Even now. Even standing here. There's something behind your eyes you're not telling me—some mission, some spirit, something the smugglers saw in you. I can smell it. I always could smell your magic, Zeph, even when you hid it, even from yourself. Did you know that? All those years selling charms beside you, and I thought: *she has more than she says.* And I said nothing. So don't talk to me about secrets."

The moonlight had shifted. The bars of it lay across the floor between them, and Zeph realized they had drifted to opposite sides of them without either choosing to.

"I can't tell you," Zeph said. "Not because I don't trust you. Because the fewer people who carry it, the fewer people they can take it from."

"That's not protection," Odile said softly. "That's just a smaller cage."

* * *

They stood a long time without speaking. Below them the tannery vats breathed their chemical breath; somewhere a night bird called from the eaves, one note, repeated, like a question no one would answer.

It was Odile who moved first. She reached into the breast of her dress and drew out a folded square of paper, soft with handling, and held it out.

"What is it?"

"What you'll need and won't ask me for." Odile's hand stayed steady. "The Pale Tower. Cell map. I copy it in pieces from what the registered hear—the rotation of the wardens, which stairs are warded, which cells face the river side where the old foundations are thin. There's a woman on the fourth level, the one they took from your street—the dyer, Sera, with the ink-stained fingers. She's in the cell marked with a cross. She's not well, Zeph. The assessments—" Odile's throat worked. "The assessments aren't assessments. You understand what I'm telling you. Whatever you're going to do, and I can see that you're going to do something, you should do it before the next intake. Before the equinox. After the equinox there won't be anything left in those cells to free."

Zeph took the map. The paper was warm from Odile's body. She wanted to say a hundred things—*come with me, run with me, forgive me, I forgive you, let's burn the arithmetic*—and every one of them died on her tongue, because she could hear Bram's voice: *people under fear are not the people they were.* And because some cold, clear part of her, the part that had survived seven years as a taster, understood that the kindest thing she could do for Odile was to leave no thread between them that the watch could pull.

"Odile—"

"Don't." Odile stepped back, up against the slatted wall, moonstriped. "If you thank me, then I've chosen. If you curse me, then I've chosen. Leave it unchosen. Leave it so that tomorrow I can stand in my market and not know what I am." She pulled her shawl tight. "But Zeph. One thing. One thing I'll say as the girl who braided your hair, and then I'll be the registered woman again."

"Say it."

"When they come for you—and they will come, the hunt-captain Voss has your description in her hand, I've seen it, they call you *the flower-girl* now, did you know that, they're telling stories about cobblestones blooming—" Odile's voice dropped to a whisper. "When they come, don't run to me. Whatever you do. Don't run to me. Because I don't know what I'd do, and I can't bear to find out."

She went down the ladder without looking back. Zeph heard her boots on the tannery floor, the door, the street, the market sounds swallowing her—cinnamon and lavender and lies, all of it, all of it love of a kind.

Zeph stood alone in the moonlight and unfolded the map. The tower rose off the paper in Odile's neat, precise hand—every cell, every stair, every warded door marked in ink, and beside the cell with the cross, in letters so small they were nearly an apology, a single line:

*The river-side foundations are thin. I'm sorry it has to be you. —O.*

Zeph pressed the map to her chest, and above her, through the slats, the moon was sliding down toward the west, and there were eleven nights left before the equinox.

Behind her, from the shadow at the loft's edge where he had waited unheard, Bram said quietly, "Now we know how to get in." A pause. "The harder question, Zeph, is what we do once we're inside—because the first keystone isn't kept in a cell. It's kept in the throne room, under the queen's own chair, and she sits on it every day of her life."
