## 10. The Drowned Chapel

Bram had known the chapel when it was dry.

That was the cruelty of Vellamare's memory: the city forgot its own drowning. The district of Saint Aveline's had once stood on a hill above the harbor, and the chapel had crowned it, whitewashed and warm with candle-smoke, where the families of the old quarter came to speak the names of their dead into the listening dark. Then the founders had needed somewhere to sink the second keystone—somewhere grief already pooled, because grief was a door—and they had broken the levees by night and let the sea come in slow and patient, and within a generation the maps had redrawn themselves, and no one living remembered that the water in the Sunken Ward was not natural at all.

No one but Bram, who remembered everything and could hold none of it safely, memory being a thing that required a place to put it down, and he had been unplaced for three hundred years.

He stood now at the edge of the flooded street where the ward began, and the water lay black and still beneath the moon, so smooth it might have been glass poured over cobblestones. Lamplight from the surviving upper windows of drowned houses wavered across it in long amber ribbons. Somewhere below all that stillness, forty feet down perhaps, the bell-tower of Saint Aveline's held its silence, and in the chapel beside it, behind an altar the founders had thought suitably obscure, the second keystone waited like a swallowed heart.

"You're doing the thing again," Zeph said beside him. "Where you stare at something like you're reading it."

"I am reading it."

"What does it say?"

Bram considered lying. It was his oldest habit, older than his beggar's rags, older than his hunger—the small dissimulations of a creature that had learned to survive by being overlooked. But she had been watching him fade for two days now, and she deserved better than habit.

"It says I've been here before," he said. "When there was air."

* * *

They went in at slack tide, before dawn, when the canals of the Sunken Ward ran shallowest and the drowned streets rose closest to the surface. Corvane's smugglers had lent them a boat—one of the flat-bottomed skiffs that slipped through the ward's channels carrying untaxed brandy and unstated histories—and a boy named Pell who poled them through streets where rooftops stood at eye level and doors opened directly into water.

Bram sat in the bow and felt the city's forgetting press against him like cold fog. The Sunken Ward was thick with unquiet remembrance; even humans felt it, he knew, the way they felt a draft from a shut room. The dead here had not been properly mourned, because mourning required the truth, and the truth had been purchased away. Their names hung in the water like silt.

Zeph sat across from him, hood up, one hand trailing near the gunwale—not touching the water, he noticed, but near it, the way a person rests a hand near a sleeping animal. Her magic had been restless since the tower. Since the first keystone, wrapped now in oilcloth against her chest, humming faintly through the fabric. He could feel it too, a low note under the world, like a struck bell still deciding whether to ring.

"It's beautiful," she said quietly, as the skiff slid between two drowned houses, their facades furred with pale weed, a shutter swaying in some current far below. "I know I shouldn't say that. People died here. But it's beautiful."

"They would have liked to be told so," Bram said. "No one says it. That's part of why they linger."

Pell brought them under a broken archway—once the portico of a merchant's house, now hung with moon-jellyfish that drifted like lanterns lit by no one—and into the open flooded square where Saint Aveline's had stood. The chapel's roof broke the surface in a single black ridge, barnacled, with one stone crossbar still upright against the sky. Around it the water deepened from green-black to nothing, to absence.

"Here," Bram said. "Tie off. We go down from the tower door."

Zeph looked at the water, then at him, and what he saw in her face made something twist in his old hollow chest: she was not afraid of drowning. She was afraid for *him*. She had been studying him the way he studied buildings—with an archivist's eye, noting every change.

"Bram." Her voice was very steady, which he had learned was how she lied. "How long since you slept? Truly slept, not dozed sitting up in that greenhouse?"

"I don't sleep the way you do."

"How long?"

The moon-jellyfish pulsed. Far below, he felt the keystone feel *him*—a cold attention rising through forty feet of salt water, recognizing, as the first stone had recognized her, the signature of the covenant's intended mender. And beneath that recognition, answering it without his consent, his own light stirred. His spirit-light, the last of it, the guttering remainder of what he had been before exile. It wanted to go home. Every day outside the Garden thinned him further; every day the world drank a little more of what remained, and he let it, because the alternative was to hoard himself into nothing.

"Three days ago I could have flown," he said, which was not an answer, which was a worse confession than any answer. "There was enough of me, three days ago, to make light. There is barely enough now to make shadow."

"That's why you've been grey." Her voice cracked on it. "I thought it was tiredness. I thought—" She stopped. In the skiff's small space her hands closed around each other. "You were going to spend yourself getting this stone and not tell me."

"The stone needs spirit-light to be reached. The wards the founders laid answer to nothing else. It has always been meant to cost me." He said it gently, the way one states weather. "It is arithmetic, Zeph. I am spending less than the Garden will lose if we fail."

"That's not arithmetic. That's just you deciding your life is the smallest number in the sum."

Pell cleared his throat, pointedly examining the jellyfish.

* * *

They went down together, because she refused otherwise and because, though he did not say it, he was no longer certain he could make the descent alone.

The tower door stood open six feet beneath the surface, and the water inside the chapel was different from the water outside—it moved. Slow currents circled the nave like breath, stirring the silt, and in the silt Bram saw the arrangement of bones that was not arrangement at all: the dead of the founding flood, left where they fell, never gathered, never named. The founders had sunk the ward with its people in it. That detail had been among the first things edited from the chronicles.

His light showed them the way—or what remained of it. He cupped his hands and coaxed the last silver from himself, and it came grudgingly, in flakes and threads, illuminating ten feet of water and then failing. He drew deeper. There was a sensation like unwinding a bandage from a wound, except the wound was everything he was, and each turn of the bandage revealed less of him rather than more.

Down through the narthex, where a carved saint gazed out with eyes long dissolved. Down the nave, their boots finding the floor tiles, each step raising a soft bloom of disturbed memory—he felt the dead notice them, a pressure of attention like fish turning in dark water. Zeph kept her hand on his arm. Not for guidance. For anchor, though she could not have said of what.

The altar was ahead, a great slab of grey granite furred white with something between coral and frost, and above it, embedded in the reredos wall behind a lattice of iron that three centuries of salt had eaten to lace, the second keystone burned cold.

It looked, as the first had, like nothing—a fist-sized stone the color of storm-light, veined with gold that was not gold but covenant-script, the old binding-language of the Garden rendered mineral. It sat in its niche the way a heart sits in a chest, wrong to be seen, essential to be touched.

The iron lace came away in Bram's hands without resistance. Of course it did. The founders had never feared theft; they had feared only discovery, and they had not imagined a garden-spirit returning, or a taster with renewal in her blood, because they had flattered themselves that they had killed both possibilities.

He took the stone.

And the chapel woke.

* * *

Not violently. That was the horror of it. The dead did not rise shrieking; they simply *arrived*, the way the drowned arrive in dreams, standing in the currents throughout the nave—shapes of grey-green light, hundreds of them, men and women and children, faces indistinct but postures eloquent. Some had their arms raised against a wall of water three hundred years fallen. Some knelt. One small shape stood apart, holding something to its chest, and Bram knew—it arrived in him whole, unbidden—that it was the sacristan's daughter, who had been carrying the chapel's reliquary when the sea came, and had not put it down since.

The current reversed. It pulled toward him, toward the stone in his hands, because the keystone was a piece of the covenant and the covenant was memory given structure, and all this unremembered grief wanted, with the simple terrible want of the abandoned, to be *held*.

They pressed in. Not touching—but the pressure of them, the weight of three hundred years of unmourned names, came down on Bram like the sea itself, and he understood in one bright instant of clarity what the founders had truly done here. They had not merely hidden a stone. They had made these dead into wardens of it, unwitting and unpaid, their grief the lock. And a lock made of grief opens only for someone willing to carry the grief.

He was willing. That was the trap of willingness; it always felt like virtue right up until the moment it emptied you.

He began to give them back their names.

It was not a decision so much as a surrender. His own memories rose and offered themselves—the chapel in sunlight, the congregation, each face a name: Aveline Odile-Not-Your-Odile, first of that name, who funded the bell. Magister Ruven, who argued with the bishop and won. The sacristan Colm, whose daughter's name was Issa, who carried the reliquary because her father told her to run and she mistook running for duty. Name after name after name, and each name spoken—even silently, even only in the language of spirit-light flickering through water—cost him. He felt himself thin with each one. Felt the grey deepen at his edges. The shapes in the nave grew brighter as he dimmed, their outlines firming, faces assembling out of the murk, mouths opening in the universal shape of the remembered.

*Bram,* he thought distantly, ironically, at the bottom of the sea, *you are doing the thing where you read something until there is nothing left of you to read with.*

The keystone in his hands was singing now, gold veins pulsing in time with the naming. His vision narrowed to a coin of silver light. His knees found the altar step. Somewhere very far away Zeph was shouting his name, and the water ate the sound, and he thought with something like peace: *this is arithmetic. This is only arithmetic.*

Then her hands closed over his.

* * *

Later—hours later, or a lifetime, on the chapel roof beneath the paling stars with the keystone wrapped beside them and Pell asleep in the skiff—she would demand to know why he hadn't stopped her, and he would not have an honest answer except that he had been beyond stopping anything. But in the moment, all he knew was warmth.

Her hands over his, her palms against the keystone, and through her skin something moving into him that he had no word for because the word had died with the covenant. Renewal. Her gift, the faint little gift, the harmless charm-seller's trick, and it came through her not as a trickle but as a tide—green-gold, tasting of rain on turned earth and new leaves and his own lost orchards—and it poured into the hollowed channels of him and did not fill them so much as remember them into shape.

The dead of Saint Aveline's received their last names. The last shape—the girl with the reliquary—turned once, her face briefly and perfectly clear, a child's face, ordinary and beloved, and then the whole congregation went out like candles in a gentle wind, not banished but *finished*, grief discharged at last into the keeping of someone who had agreed to carry it.

And Bram came back.

It was like surfacing. It was like being born, again, badly, gasping. He was aware first of the green-gold light everywhere in the water, pouring off Zeph's hands in ribbons he could see and she apparently could not; second of the keystone blazing between their joined fingers, its gold veins running molten; third, absurdly, of the taste of plums, though there were no plums within a hundred leagues.

She was saying his name. Over and over, underwater, wasting precious air on it, bubbles streaming upward past her furious, terrified, luminous face.

*I'm here,* he tried to say. What came out instead was light.

For a moment—they would argue forever afterward about how long—the two of them and the stone made a single burning shape in the drowned chapel, visible, Pell swore later, from the surface, a glow in the black water like a lamp lowered into a well. The green-gold of her gift and the silver of his spirit twined, and where they twined the water sweetened, and the dead salt of three centuries turned briefly fresh, and on the drowned altar, in the space of one heartbeat, white flowers bloomed across the granite and released their seeds and vanished, all in the same breath.

The first act of the covenant in three hundred years. Neither of them performed it. Neither of them chose it. That, he would come to understand, was precisely what the old texts had meant by *covenant*: not a contract seized, but a wholeness stumbled into by two depleted creatures who happened, in the dark, to reach for each other.

* * *

On the roof, in the air, shaking, wrapped in every scrap of cloth the boat possessed, Zeph held the keystone and glared at him with red-rimmed eyes and said, "Never again."

"Never again what?" Bram asked. His voice sounded strange to him. Full. There was more of him than there had been an hour ago; he could feel his edges, crisp and defined, and beneath his ribs a warmth he had not owned since exile. She had not merely refilled him. She had repaired the leaking. He did not understand how. He suspected she did not either.

"Never again arithmetic. Never again deciding alone what you're worth." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "I didn't know I was doing it, the—the healing, whatever it was. I felt it leave me like it knew where it was going. Like it had been *waiting* to go somewhere, my whole life, and finally had an address." Her voice dropped. "What was that?"

Bram looked out over the black water, at the drowned ward gone quiet now, truly quiet, its ghosts finished, its grief spent and carried. The first grey light of dawn was finding the rooftops.

"That," he said slowly, feeling his way along the truth like a man in an unfamiliar corridor, "was the covenant working. As it was meant to. Memory given where it's needed, life given where it's needed, neither side keeping accounts." He turned the words over, testing them, and found they held weight. "The old texts called it *the shared table*. Everyone eats; everyone brings something. The founders replaced it with a siphon because a table requires trust."

Zeph was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had changed—something settled in it, some question answered that she had been asking since the flowers came up through the cobblestones.

"My mother used to say I gave my warmth away too easily." A small, wet laugh. "She had no idea."

The sun crested the eastern warehouses and struck the water, and the whole Sunken Ward caught fire with reflected gold, and for a moment the drowned city looked the way Bram remembered it: alive, lit, named. He breathed the morning in. It tasted of rain and plums and possibility.

Then Zeph went rigid beside him.

"Bram." Her hand found his arm, gripping hard. "The first keystone. Under my coat."

He turned. Against the dawn, through the oilcloth, the first stone was glowing—faintly, unmistakably, its pulse matched beat for beat to the second stone beside it. And beneath the pulse, threading through both, he felt something else: a summons, thin and urgent as a plucked wire, rising from the east, from beyond the harbor, from the direction of the Garden itself.

The stones knew each other now. And something in the Garden—something that had been sleeping, or waiting, or counting—had felt them wake.

"It knows we're coming," Bram said quietly. "And Zeph—we are not the only ones it called. Something else just heard it too. Something on this side of the gate."

Far off across the waking city, from the direction of the Pale Tower, a bell began to ring—though no ringer had touched it—and did not stop.
