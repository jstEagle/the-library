## 12. Corvane's Price

Bram had learned, in his long exile among humans, to read grief the way other men read weather. It came off people the way heat came off cobbles at dusk—visible only if you knew to look for the shimmer. Corvane's grief had shimmered for years. Bram had seen it in the way the smuggler touched the little tin medallion he wore beneath his shirt, a habit so constant it had worn a groove in the leather cord. He had seen it in the way Corvane never spoke of the eastern quarter of Vellamare, never walked through it, though his route to the harbor would have been shorter if he had. He had seen it in the man's ledger-books, where certain pages had been torn out so cleanly that the stubs remained, like amputations.

So when the bells of the Pale Tower began to ring at an hour when bells should sleep, Bram was not surprised. Grief, denied a grave, will dig one for itself out of whatever lies nearest. Sometimes what lies nearest is the people who trusted you.

* * *

The safehouse beneath the Bellows district had been a wine cellar once, in the years before the river shifted and drowned the lower streets. Now it smelled of brine and tallow and the green, impossible scent that clung to Bram wherever he went—the last perfume of a Garden the city had forgotten it had stolen. He sat in the corner where the damp stone let him feel, faintly, the ghost of roots that had once threaded through this ground, and he listened to Corvane argue with the map.

"The east gate," Corvane was saying, his blunt finger stabbing at the parchment Zeph had brought back from Odile. "The tower cells drain through the old cistern galleries. If we take the magicians out through the cisterns, we surface in the tanner's yard on Mire Street, and from there—"

"From there we are nine people, three of them half-starved, walking through a quarter that Voss has locked down since the theft was discovered," Zeph said. She was sitting on a barrel with her bandaged arm cradled against her chest, and the lamplight made her look younger and more exhausted than her years. "Odile's map shows the cells. It doesn't show us how to get nine people across a city hunting for us."

"Then we go in smaller groups. Three and three and three."

"And if one group is taken?"

Corvane's jaw worked. "Then two groups survive."

Bram watched Zeph look at him—really look, the way she had begun to look at things since the Garden, since the grey orchards, since she had pressed her palm to the heart-tree and paid it a memory she could no longer name. There was something in her face that was not suspicion. It was worse than suspicion. It was the particular tenderness of someone who has begun to love a person and cannot yet name what is wrong with them.

"Corvane," she said quietly, "you've been to the east gate twice this week. You told me you were checking the guard rotations."

"I was checking the guard rotations."

"Alone? You never go anywhere alone. You taught me that the first night, when you pulled me out of the drain. *Nothing in the undercity moves alone.*"

The silence that followed had weight, the way water has weight when it rises past your chest. Bram felt it pressing against his ribs. He had lived through the breaking of a covenant; he knew what it sounded like when something that had held for a long time began, very quietly, to fail.

Corvane laughed. It was a terrible sound, too loud, the laugh of a man throwing a stone down a well to hear how deep it went. "You've been learning to think like a smuggler, taster-girl. Next you'll be telling me not to trust anyone with a kind face."

"I'm telling you," Zeph said, "that the kind face is the one I'm worried about."

* * *

Later, when Zeph had finally slept—she slept badly now, curled around her wound like a snail around its broken shell—and the cellar had settled into the dripping quiet of the drowned streets, Bram came and sat beside Corvane at the long table where the map lay under the guttering lamp.

The smuggler did not look up. His hands were flat on the parchment, as though he could hold the escape plan down by force, keep it from flying apart.

"You should sleep," Corvane said.

"I don't sleep the way you sleep." Bram folded his hands. In this light, in this human shape he had worn so long it had grown into him like a tree grows into a fence, his fingers looked like a beggar's fingers, cracked and dirt-stained. Only in certain lights, at certain hours, did they show what they truly were: green-veined, patient, old. "I keep watch. It's what I was made for. Watching over things."

"Then watch." Corvane's voice was rough. "Watch the door. Watch the water. Watch anything but me."

"I watched you take the medallion out tonight. First time in three days. You held it a long while."

Corvane's hands curled slowly into fists on the map. "You see too much, old man. That's the trouble with spirits. You see too much and understand too little."

"Perhaps." Bram let the silence breathe. Somewhere above them, water found its way through stone, drop by drop, the city's slow heartbeat. "Tell me their names."

The smuggler went still. Utterly still, the stillness of a hunted animal that has heard the hound's voice and knows that running will only make the ending quicker.

"Whose names," Corvane said at last, and his voice had gone flat and careful, a man walking across ice.

"Your family. The ones whose pages you tore out of your own ledger because you could not bear to read them. The ones from the eastern quarter." Bram kept his voice low and even, the voice he had once used to sing seeds awake. "The ones whose names the city has forgotten, because the founders took memory itself to feed their dynasty, and what the dynasty does not remember, it never mourned. Tell me their names, Corvane. I would carry them. That is what guardians are for, even failed ones. Even now."

For a long moment Bram thought the man would strike him, or weep, or both. Instead Corvane bowed his head, and the lamplight caught the wet shine on his cheekbones, and when he spoke his voice was the voice of a much younger man, a man from a quarter of the city that no longer existed except in torn ledger pages and a tin medallion.

"Miretta," he said. "My wife. She dyed cloth. Indigo—her hands were blue at the knuckles for the whole of every autumn, and she'd laugh and say she was married to the sky." His breath caught. "The boys. Teo and Anselm. Teo was seven and collected river-glass because he said it was the sea sending him messages. Anselm was four. He was afraid of pigeons." A sound came out of him that was not quite a laugh and not quite anything else. "Afraid of pigeons. In a city drowning in them. And my sister Vessa, who took them in when—" He stopped. Started again. "When the registration came. When I told them to sign the rolls, because signing meant safety, signing meant the crown knew you and the crown protects what it knows. That's what the criers said. That's what I believed."

"The tower took them," Bram said. It was not a question. He had seen the Pale Tower's appetite in another century, in another shape.

"Took them for testing. And the testing found what it found, and there was a trial, and the trial was held in a hall so grand and so cold, and they read the sentence, and then—" Corvane's hands opened and closed. "And then the worst of it. The *worst of it*, Bram. They were remembered wrong. The clerk wrote down their names wrong. Miretta became 'Marit, dyer, deceased.' Teo and Anselm were entered as 'two minor children of the accused.' *Two minor children.* Not their names. My sons were a number in a column. And Vessa—" His voice broke clean through. "Vessa was entered under her husband's name, and her husband had died two years before, and so the record of her trial is filed under a dead man, and no one will ever find it, no one will ever know she existed, and I have gone to the record halls, I have *begged* clerks, and they tell me the entry cannot be amended without a writ from the crown, and the crown does not amend the records of traitors."

The lamp hissed. Bram did not move. Grief like this was a living thing, and sudden movement could spook it into silence forever.

"So when the crown's man found me—three weeks ago, in the tanner's yard, a soft-spoken clerk with a writ already drawn up—he was not offering me gold." Corvane lifted his head, and his eyes were terrible, red-rimmed and clear. "He was offering me their names. Spelled right. Filed right. *Miretta, Teo, Anselm, Vessa Corvane*, entered in the rolls of the city as persons who had lived and died, mournable, memorial, real. A family restored to the memory of Vellamare. Do you understand? The city took them from me twice—once with the tower, once with a clerk's bad pen—and this man, this *clerk*, was offering to give them back. For a price." He laughed again, that awful well-stone laugh. "The price was a taster-girl and a beggar and two stones of a broken covenant. That's all. That's all I had to sell."

Bram looked at him for a long time. And what he felt was not the rage he might once have felt, in the years when he had been the covenant's first guardian and had believed that vigilance was the same as love. What he felt was the ache of recognition. He had watched kings do this very thing—take a people's memory and hold it hostage, sell it back to them piecemeal, at ruinous interest. The founders of Vellamare had built an empire on that trade. The clerk in the tanner's yard had merely learned from masters.

"They would have found another way to buy you," Bram said gently. "If not the names, then something else. Men like that clerk can smell a wound through a wall."

"I know." Corvane pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "I know. That's the filth of it. They didn't corrupt me, Bram. They just—*watered* what was already growing."

* * *

Bram did not sleep, because Bram did not sleep. He kept his watch in the corner, and he watched Corvane sit at the table through the dead hours, and he watched the man's hand drift, again and again, to the medallion at his throat. And Bram understood—because he had failed a covenant once, and understood the architecture of failure from the inside—that Corvane had already done the deed. The map-argument, the east gate, the guard rotations: those had been the last performances of a man trying to find a version of the story in which he had not already sold them. But the clerk had come three weeks ago. Three weeks. The tower's bells had rung at the wrong hour tonight because the tower already knew where they slept. The escape plan had been dying since before Zeph had drawn it.

He could wake her. He could take her hand and pull her into the cisterns now, while the soldiers were still mustering, while there was still a margin. Every hour he sat still was an hour the noose drew tighter, and Bram had spent four hundred years learning what happened when guardians hesitated.

But he watched Corvane instead. He watched the man take out a stub of charcoal and, on the back of the map, in a hand that shook, write four names: *Miretta. Teo. Anselm. Vessa.* He wrote them slowly, carefully, like a man planting something he did not expect to see grow. And when he had finished, he sat looking at them, and his shoulders were those of a man carrying a door on his back.

*They will erase even this,* Bram thought. *When the tower learns what he has done—when the clerk decides a traitor's family is not worth the paper—the writ will be torn up, and the names will vanish a second time, and Corvane will have sold everything for nothing.* The crown traded in memory, and the crown did not honor its debts; that was the whole of its history. Corvane must know it, somewhere beneath the grief. He had bet his dead on the honor of the people who had killed them, because the alternative was to believe they were gone in a way that nothing could ever mend.

Bram knew that belief. He had lived inside it for four hundred years.

So he did the only thing a guardian could do, even a failed one, even now. He rose from his corner, quiet as a shadow leaving a wall, and he crossed the cellar, and he stood over the map, over the four names written in shaking charcoal.

"Miretta," he said aloud.

Corvane flinched. "What are you—"

"Miretta, who dyed cloth, whose hands were blue at the knuckles. Teo, who collected river-glass, who believed the sea wrote to him. Anselm, who was four, and afraid of pigeons. Vessa, who took in her brother's family when the world grew dangerous, which is to say: Vessa, who loved well." Bram's voice was low, but it carried in the stone room the way water carries, filling every crack. "I remember them. I will remember them whether the crown's writ stands or burns. Memory freely given is the only kind the covenant ever honored, Corvane. The founders stole theirs. Isolde hoards hers. But this—" he touched the parchment, beside the names, not over them— "this is the old magic. The true kind. You have already paid the price the crown asked. Let me pay the one that matters."

Corvane stared at him. And Bram saw the moment the man understood that he had been forgiven—not excused, not absolved, not spared what was coming, but *forgiven*, which is a different and harder thing, a thing that cannot be revoked by any clerk. Something in his face broke open, and for a moment he was not a smuggler or a traitor or a seller of his friends; he was only a widower in a drowned cellar, holding four names in two cupped hands.

"You old fool," Corvane whispered. "You green old fool. I've already sent word. Tonight. The east gate. They'll come at the change of the watch, and there will be a captain with them, and she has your description, and the girl's, and—" His voice cracked. "Run. Take Zeph and run through the cisterns, the *west* cisterns, the map is wrong, I drew the east route for them—run now, while the watch is changing, it's the only hour there is."

Bram was already moving, waking Zeph with a hand on her good shoulder, pressing his finger to his lips before her eyes had fully opened, before the question could become a sound. She read his face the way she had learned to read everything since the Garden—quickly, wholly, with her whole frightened heart. Her eyes went to Corvane. Whatever she saw there, she did not waste breath on it. She took the map, she took her knife, she took Bram's hand.

At the cellar door she stopped. Turned back.

"Corvane. The names you wrote. Keep the page. However it ends—keep the page."

And they were up the ladder and into the dripping dark of the west cisterns, Bram's old heart hammering with a rhythm that was not quite fear and not quite hope, while behind them, above, the bells of the Pale Tower began to ring the hour—and kept ringing, past the hour, past all reason, ringing the alarm that meant the hunt had already begun.
