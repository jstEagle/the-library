## 13. The Pale Tower Falls Silent

The Pale Tower smelled of lye and lilies, and Zephyrine hated how well she remembered both.

Lye from the scrubbing of cells that had held gifted folk for three generations—scrubbed so often the stones had gone soft as soap. Lilies because Queen Isolde believed white flowers purified a place of magic, and had them carried up the tower stair in silver buckets every morning, their sweetness drowning the sourer truth beneath: iron rust, old sweat, the copper ghost of fear that no amount of scrubbing ever lifted.

Zeph stood in the tower's lower kitchen with a taster's satchel on her hip and a guard captain's writ in her pocket, and she let the smell of the lilies wash over her like a tide she was choosing to drown in.

*One last time,* she told herself. *One last service to the crown.*

The lie tasted better than most.

They had summoned her through official channels this time—not the kitchen steward with his greasy smile, but a sealed writ bearing the queen's own seal: *By order of Her Majesty, the taster Marchetti is seconded to the Pale Tower for the duration of the trials.* The trials. Three days of tribunals where captured magicians would be measured like fish at market—gift weighed, gift catalogued, gift sentenced—and then the wagons would roll east toward whatever the crown did with people whose only crime was being what the world had made them.

Somewhere in those cells was Marta of the lamplighters' row, her neighbor, the woman who had once traded Zeph a jar of honey for a charm against nightmares. Marta who could coax warmth out of dead coals. Marta who had been dragged down her own stairs by men wearing the queen's white, while her children screamed from the window.

Zeph tied back her hair, checked the little clay vials nested in wool in her satchel, and began her work.

* * *

A taster's trade was patience. Everyone thought it was courage—the swallowing of the suspect spoonful, the long still moment when the whole room watched your throat—but courage was cheap. Courage lasted a minute. Patience lasted weeks.

For six days now she had been patient.

She had learned the tower's rhythms the way she'd once learned Vellamare's night market: by walking it slowly, asking small questions, letting her hands look busy while her eyes did the real work. She knew which guards took their supper at which hour. She knew the ward-keeper, Master Hennet, drank his chicory bitter and his wine sweet and trusted no one's pour but his own—which was why she never touched either. She knew the wards themselves were not drawn on paper or carved in stone but *brewed*, steeped in great copper kettles in the cellar kitchen, and fed to the tower the way one fed a sick child: morning draught, evening draught, poured into channels cut beneath the thresholds until the whole building drank suppression down to its bones.

It was, she had realized on the second night, the largest charm she had ever seen. A working built to smother magic across nine floors of cells—a covenant of forgetting in miniature, the founders' theft shrunk down to fit inside a kettle.

Which meant it could be poisoned. Not with anything that harmed flesh—she would not, even now, even for Marta, become the thing the crown said she was. But Bram had spent three evenings teaching her, his voice low over the greenhouse bench while impossible flowers turned their faces toward him like sun-starved children, how to brew a charm of unmaking. A charm of forgetting.

"Every binding remembers itself," he had said, grinding dried rain-lily petals—rain-lilies, of course; he smelled of rain, and everything he touched did too—"the way a scar remembers the wound. You don't break a binding. You make it forget what it was for." He had looked up at her then, and his eyes were old and green and terribly kind. "You taught me that, though you don't know it. The first day we met, you made me forget I was afraid."

Now, on the sixth evening, the kettle room stood empty except for her.

The ward-keeper's apprentice had gone to supper. The head cook was drunk on festival spirits smuggled in from the city above—equinox eve tomorrow, and even jailers celebrated. Zeph set her satchel on the scarred oak table, unwrapped the wool, and counted her vials one final time. Seven. Enough for seven kettles, if her arithmetic held, and her arithmetic always held; it was the one gift she'd never needed magic for.

The first vial went into the evening draught with a steady wrist, a pale shimmer dissolving into the grey-green liquor like sugar into tea. The second. The third. Each kettle murmured as the charm sank in—a sound almost like contentment, like a dog settling at its master's feet—and Zeph found herself whispering an apology to each one, because that was the strange grief of her work: everything that bound could also be kind, once, before someone bent it cruel.

The last vial she kept apart. This one was different—Bram's own brewing, thick as honey and dark as loam, meant not for the kettles but for the threshold-stone of the cell block door. She pressed her thumb against its wax seal and felt it hum against her skin, faint and familiar, the way a struck bell hums after the sound has gone.

*For Marta,* she thought. *And all the rest.*

She poured it into the channel-cut beneath the door and watched it vanish into the stone like water into thirsty earth.

Then she washed her hands, straightened her satchel, and walked upstairs to taste the queen's justice, wearing her small harmless face.

* * *

The trial hall occupied the tower's third floor, and it was beautiful in the way that cruelty is always beautiful when it can afford good architects: vaulted ceiling painted with clouds, tall windows full of dying light, the queen's white banners hanging still as frozen waterfalls. Isolde sat upon a chair that was almost a throne, and beside her, at a smaller desk, sat Hunt-Captain Sarel Voss with her ledger open and her face closed like a door.

Zeph stood at her tasting station near the queen's elbow, as she had every trial-day, sampling the queen's wine and the little ceremonial cakes, finding nothing, reporting nothing, being nobody. It should have been easier now. She had a plan in motion beneath their feet; she had purpose. Instead each swallow felt heavier than the last, because the woman in the dock this afternoon was Marta.

Marta looked smaller than Zeph remembered. They had starved her carefully—not enough to kill the gift's evidence, just enough to keep it quiet—and her hands, those warm brown hands that had coaxed life from cold hearths for half the street, lay folded in her lap like birds with broken wings.

"The accused," intoned the magistrate, "is charged with the practice of unauthorized arts. How does she answer?"

"I warmed my own stove," Marta said. Her voice was hoarse but level. "In my own house. In winter."

"Warmth drawn by art is stolen warmth," the magistrate recited. "Drawn from the realm's vitality, from the Crown's—"

"From *what*?" Marta laughed then, and the laugh cracked something open in the hall; several guards shifted uncomfortably. "What exactly do you think I stole, my lord? There was one coal left in the grate and two children shivering, and the fire *answered me*. That's all magic ever was, before your laws. Something answering."

Zeph tasted the queen's wine. Nothing. Bitter, oaky, nothing. She set down the cup and did not look at Voss, though she felt the hunt-captain's gaze slide across her like a lantern beam.

"Sentiment," said the magistrate. "The court finds—"

And that was when the lights went out.

Not all at once. Like breath leaving a body. The chandelier-globes guttered amber, then blue, then grey, and the painted clouds overhead seemed to darken as though a storm had entered the room. Someone shouted. A guard's boots clattered on marble. And through the silence that followed—through the queen's sharp intake of breath and Voss rising from her desk with her hand already on her sword—Zeph felt it rise beneath her feet like water coming up through a well:

*Release.*

The tower was exhaling. Six days of suppression, seven kettles of binding, decades of stolen quiet—all of it unraveling at once, gently, the way frost lets go of a windowpane in spring. She felt the wards forget themselves floor by floor, cell by cell, and she felt something else too, something that made her press her knuckles to her lips so no one would see her mouth tremble: the magicians waking. All of them. Gifts unfurling in the dark like roots remembering rain.

"Captain," Queen Isolde said, very quietly, in the grey dark. "Find out what has happened to my tower."

Voss was already moving. She passed within arm's reach of Zeph's station, and for one suspended heartbeat their eyes met—grey eyes, quick and terrible—and Zeph saw something flicker there, some question forming behind the discipline—

Then the doors blew open.

Not with force. With *light*. Every cell below had emptied itself upward at once, forty-some magicians climbing the tower stair in the dark, guided no doubt by whoever among them could feel stone and air, and they came into the trial hall trailing radiance like people wading out of deep water. Marta's hands burned first—warm gold spilling between her fingers, the first fire she'd made in months—and she raised them high and said, clear as a bell across the stunned hall:

"There's your stolen warmth, my lord. Take it back."

Chaos, then. Beautiful, deliberate chaos. The freed magicians didn't fight; they *unmade*—doors unlocking themselves, chains sighing open, the guards' torches flaring so bright the men dropped them and blinked blind. Someone had planned this part too, and Zeph, slipping backward along the wall toward the servants' stair, sent a silent prayer of thanks to Corvane and his smugglers, who had gotten word to the tower's underground in ways she hadn't dared ask about.

She reached the stair. She took it downward, two steps at a time, heart slamming, and she was nearly at the ground floor when a hand caught her sleeve and pulled her into an alcove.

Odile.

Her friend stood in the alcove's shadow dressed in a guard's spare cloak, her dark hair stuffed under a helmet two sizes wrong, her face pale and furious and terrified and resolute all at once, which was the most Odile expression possible in the world.

"You absolute *idiot*," Odile whispered. "Seven kettles? I count seven vials missing from your kit and I think, no, surely Zeph wouldn't—"

"How did you—"

"I've known since the night market. Since before. I know you, Zeph." Odile's jaw tightened. "That's why I'm here. The west postern is unwatched for the next quarter-hour—I bought that quarter-hour with a forged rotation order, and it cost me more than you want to know. Your friends are heading for it. Go with them."

"You're coming too." It wasn't a question. Zeph grabbed her friend's wrist. "Odile. After tonight they'll tear this tower apart looking for who—"

"After tonight," Odile said gently, peeling Zeph's fingers away one by one, "someone respectable needs to still be standing inside the walls. Someone with a clean registration seal and a spotless record and no reason at all to be suspected." She smiled, and it was the old smile, the night-market smile, wry and bright and a little bit broken. "I registered so I could survive. Let it be worth something."

"They'll suspect everyone. Voss isn't a fool."

"No," Odile agreed. "But fools are what I'm best at pretending to be." She pushed the helmet back off her brow and, quick as a pickpocket, kissed Zeph's forehead—once, hard, like sealing a letter. "West postern. Quarter-hour. Don't look back, don't wait, don't be brave. Be *gone*."

"Odile—"

"*Go.*"

Zeph went. It was the hardest obedience of her life. She fled down the last flight and out through kitchens that stank of spilled supper and abandoned duty, past the lily-buckets tipped and spreading white petals across the flagstones like fallen stars, and out into the equinox-eve dark where the wind off the sea tasted of change.

Behind her the Pale Tower blazed with confusion, windows lighting one by one, voices rising. Ahead, in the shadow of the western wall, forty-some freed magicians moved through the dark like a procession of lanterns, and Corvane's smugglers folded around them, and somewhere in that river of escaping light was Marta, laughing softly, her hands still glowing.

Zeph counted heads as they slipped through the postern gate into the undercity's throat. Counted again. The last of them passed, and the gate sighed shut, and the quarter-hour ended.

They did not look back. That was the agreement, and they kept it, running hard through tunnels that smelled of salt and secrets, and no one saw what happened at the tower's western wall.

But Zeph felt it. Even leagues below the streets, even wrapped in stone and distance, she felt it when the pursuit finally organized itself—felt the net close on the one soul who had stayed behind to buy them their lead. Felt, unmistakably, the flare of a small registered gift being used one defiant time: a forgery of orders, a misdirection of guards, Odile spending the last coin of her clean record to hold the gate a few seconds longer.

Felt her get caught.

Zeph stopped in the tunnel so suddenly that Bram, waiting for her in the dark ahead with his rain-and-earth smell and his fading spirit-light, put out a hand to steady her.

"What is it?"

"She's taken." Zeph's voice came out strange, flattened, like sound heard through water. "Odile. Voss has her."

Bram said nothing for a long moment. Around them the tunnel dripped, patient as everything else in the deep places of the world. Then he stepped closer, and his hand—cool as riverstone, warm as loam—closed over hers.

"Then we have no time left to waste," he said quietly. "Two keystones sleep between here and the Garden, Zephyrine, and the equinox comes with tomorrow's moonrise. Whatever price Odile paid tonight, she paid it to buy us this road." His green eyes caught what little light there was. "We cannot save her by standing still. We can only make certain her sacrifice buys what she meant it to."

Zeph looked back the way they had come, toward the distant, invisible tower, toward the friend who had chosen the walls so that Zeph could choose the road. Triumph and grief arrived together, twins, inseparable—the tower silent, the gate open, forty souls free and one soul lost—and she understood, with a clarity that felt like a wound, that this was what the covenant had been trying to teach her all along:

Everything given freely costs exactly what it's worth.

She turned forward. Above them, faint through fathoms of stone, the bells of Vellamare began to ring the eve of the equinox.

"Then we walk," Zeph said, "and we don't stop."

And into the deep roads of the undercity, toward the drowned chapel where the second keystone waited, they went.
