## 14. Where the Garden Sleeps

The gate opened the way a wound opens—slowly, and with a smell of old iron.

Bram had not crossed the threshold in three hundred years. He had imagined it a thousand times, in the long grey centuries of his exile: imagined the orchards green, the rivers singing, the heart-tree lifting its crown into a sky the color of new honey. What he had not imagined, what no exile permits himself to imagine, was the truth of it now—the gate swinging wide on hinges of fused stone and living light, and beyond it, nothing green at all.

Zeph stepped through first. Of course she did. She had been doing that since the greenhouse—walking first into rooms that frightened her, holding her small charms up like lanterns, insisting on being the brave one when bravery was his debt to pay, not hers.

"Bram," she said quietly. "Oh, Bram."

He came through beside her and the Garden's air closed over him, and it was like being held by a mother who had forgotten his name.

The orchards stretched away to the horizon, row upon row of them, and every tree was grey. Not dead—grey. That was the cruelty of it. Dead things he could have borne; dead things had an honesty. These trees stood in perfect rows like mourners at a vigil, their bark the color of ash, their branches bare but not brittle, and here and there a single fruit clung to a bough, grey as river-stone, waiting. Waiting was worse than dying. Waiting meant the trees still believed.

The light came from everywhere and nowhere, sourceless and dim, like the last hour of a winter afternoon that had refused, for three centuries, to become night or morning.

"The rivers," Zeph said. She had gone ahead a few steps, to where the ground dipped, and she stood looking down at a riverbed of pale gravel. "Bram, there's no water."

"There is," he said. "It's sleeping. Look closer."

She knelt. He watched her lay her palm flat against the riverbed, and he watched the faint gold of her gift rise in her skin like warmth through a window. For a moment—only a moment—the gravel shivered, and something moved beneath it, deep and slow, a pulse of silver that traveled the length of the dry channel and vanished around a bend. A sleeping thing turning over in its dream.

Zeph snatched her hand back as if scalded. "It felt me."

"Everything here feels you," Bram said. "You are the first new thing it has touched since the covenant broke. You're a stranger's footsteps in a house where everyone has been asleep."

She stood, brushing gravel-dust from her knees, and looked at him with that directness she had, the one that made lying to her a kind of small violence. "You've been here before. You knew the way to the gate. You knew which of the stones would open it." She turned her grey keystone over in her fingers—the drowned chapel's stone, still cold, still weeping faintly. "You're not just a guide, are you. You never were."

The orchard wind moved through the grey branches. It made no sound. That was the thing he had forgotten to dread—the silence of it, the way wind could pass through a whole world and stir nothing, because there were no leaves left to speak with.

"No," he said. "I'm not."

He began to walk, because it was easier to confess walking, easier when the confession could be scattered along a road instead of delivered whole. She fell into step beside him, as she always did, and the grey orchard received them the way the sea receives two drops of rain.

* * *

They walked through the orchards until the rows began to fail—trees leaning at drunken angles, some fallen altogether, their roots lifted from soil that had gone hard as fired clay. Bram spoke as they walked, and his voice came out of him like water from a cracked jug, in fits and starts, in a shape he had never once given it in three hundred years of silence.

"I was the first guardian," he said. "Before there was a Vellamare. Before there were founders, before there was a crown to steal anything. The covenant was not a wall, Zeph. People think of it as a wall, a border, a locked door. It wasn't. It was a handshake. The Garden gave, and the human realm received, and the receiving was itself a kind of giving—memory, attention, care. A farmer who sang to his wheat was feeding us. A child who believed the hedge at the field's edge was alive was keeping us alive. We flowed into you, and you flowed into us, and neither side was ever poorer for it."

"And you kept the handshake?"

"I *was* the handshake." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, rusty. "That's what a guardian is. Not a soldier at a gate. A joint. A place where two things meet and move together. I was made for it—grown for it, out of the heart-tree's oldest root, given a body so I could walk in both worlds. I tended the boundary. I taught the first villages which springs were holy and which were merely wet. I sat with dying people and carried their last memories back into the Garden so they could become rain. It was—" He stopped. The word he wanted was *joy*, and he found he could not lift it. "It was work I loved."

Zeph said nothing. She had learned, in their weeks together, when silence was the kinder gift.

"The founders came later. Not conquerors—not at first. That's the part no one remembers, because remembering it would require the crown to remember its own shame, and shame is the one crop Vellamare has never learned to grow. They came as pilgrims. They came with gifts. And the Garden, being a garden, gave freely, because giving is what we are." His hand closed around the second keystone, the one from beneath the Pale Tower, warm and sullen as a banked coal. "And then they asked a question no pilgrim should ask: *what if the giving had no need to be returned?* What if we could take the flow and dam it? Keep it. Own it. Pour it into a bloodline instead of a world."

"The siphon."

"The siphon." He could still see it—the night it was built, the night the first keystone cracked. He had felt it in his body before he understood it in his mind, the way you feel a bone break in someone else's arm. "They didn't come with armies, Zeph. They came with engineers and priests and very reasonable men with very soft voices, and they broke the covenant the way you break anything precious—not all at once, but at its joints. Three keystones held the flow between the realms. Three, because three is the number of a promise: one for what was given, one for what was received, one for what was owed. They took all three. They hid one beneath their tower, where its power could feed the Pale Wardens. They drowned one in the chapel of the dead, because the dead remember longest and the founders wanted the memory buried deepest. And the third—" he touched his own chest, briefly, an old habit, as if the heart-tree's root still ran through it— "the third they left in the Garden, fused to the heart-tree itself, because they knew no one would ever come home to claim it."

"Because they'd killed everyone who could."

"Because they'd *forgotten* everyone who could," Bram said. "That was the genius of it. Not slaughter—forgetting. They siphoned memory along with vitality. Whole villages woke one morning unable to recall why they'd left offerings at the hedge. Mothers looked at their own children and felt a fondness with no story attached. The Garden faded from the world's mind not because it was hidden but because the world was *made* to forget it had ever been seen." He gestured at the grey rows, the sourceless light, the dry river. "This is what a theft looks like three hundred years later. Not ruins. Ruins would be honest. This. Everything still standing, everything still waiting, nothing still living."

They came over a low rise, and below them lay a river valley—or the memory of one. The channel was broad and graceful, carved by millennia of running water, and it was dry as a dead man's mouth. But along its banks stood willows, and the willows were not entirely grey. Deep in their bark, in the creases and knots, ran veins of the faintest green, like a pulse kept under a blanket.

Zeph stopped walking. "Bram. Your hands."

He looked down. His hands, which had been beggar's hands for three centuries—dirt in the creases, nails like horn, the skin of a man made of shadow and habit—were changing. The grey was lifting from them. Not to health. To translucence. He could see the orchard light passing through his own fingers, and beneath the skin, faint as veins in a leaf, the old green of him was showing through.

"I'm coming apart," he said, and was surprised at how calm it sounded. "I told you—exile thins a spirit. Outside the Garden I was a shadow wearing a man's shape, and it cost me to hold the shape. But here—" he flexed his fingers, watched the light go through them— "here the shape has nothing left to hold it *with*. The Garden can't keep me together. It can barely keep itself. Every hour I'm home, I'll fade faster. I knew that when I came. I'd have come anyway."

"Don't say that."

"It's only the truth, and the truth is the one thing this place has never been given enough of."

She grabbed his wrist—his translucent wrist, and her fingers pressed against veins of fading green—and her gift rose in her before she could stop it. He felt it pour into him, that warm gold flood, and for one dizzying moment the translucence retreated, the skin firmed, the green deepened. Then it ebbed, because her gift was not endless and he was a very old leak.

"Stop that," he said gently. "Ration yourself. You'll need everything you have for the heart-tree."

"You'll need—"

"I need to finish confessing," he said, "while I still have a mouth to do it with. Come. There's a place I want to show you, if we're going to speak of failure. It should be spoken of at the scene of the crime."

* * *

The guardian's cottage still stood at the meeting of two dry rivers, where the valley forked like a decision.

It had been a small house once—stone, thatched, with a garden around it that had held every healing herb in either realm, planted in patterns that were themselves a language. Now the thatch was gone, the stone was grey, and the garden was a field of ash-colored stalks standing in their rows like the orchard trees, like everything here, still planted, still tended in shape, still waiting.

Bram stood at the gate and could not, for a long moment, go through it.

"You don't have to," Zeph said.

"I do. That's rather the point of confession." He pushed the gate. It swung without a sound, and that was the worst of it—three hundred years, and the hinge still moved smoothly, because the Garden maintained even its griefs perfectly.

Inside, the cottage was as he had left it. That was the unbearable part. A bowl on the table, waiting for a supper never eaten. A cloak on its peg. His staff in the corner, grey now, but still standing straight, still faithful. On the table, under a fine skin of dust, lay a ledger—the guardian's ledger, in which every exchange between the realms had been recorded for a thousand years. Given. Received. Owed. He had written in it the morning of the last day, and the entry was unfinished, the pen still lying across the page where he had set it down when he felt, in his body, the first keystone crack.

He picked up the pen. It crumbled in his fingers like a moth's husk.

"I ran," he said.

Zeph looked up from the ledger.

"That's the confession. The one I've been circling for three hundred years, the one I let you believe was only about the founders. Yes, they betrayed the covenant. Yes, they stole. But Zeph—I was the guardian. The flow broke at *my* joints. And when the keystones shattered, when the siphon opened and I felt the Garden's life being dragged out of it like marrow from a bone—I ran. I came back here, to this house, and I gathered what I could carry, and I went out into the human realm to *hide*. I told myself I was scouting, gathering strength, finding a way to fight. Three centuries of telling myself that." He set down the ruined pen with great care, as if it could still feel. "I was a guardian whose whole nature was to stand at the meeting place of two worlds, and when the meeting place broke, I chose one world and hid in its gutters. I let the beggars' fires warm me. I let the city forget me. I let *myself* forget me. Do you know what it is, to be made for one purpose and to fail it, and then to have three hundred years to do nothing but fail it more thoroughly, one day at a time, one act of cowardice at a time?"

"Yes," Zeph said quietly. "I know what it is to hide."

He looked at her. She stood in the doorway of his old house with the two keystones in her hands and the sourceless light behind her, and she was not comforting him—she was *witnessing* him, which was a different thing and a harder one, and which was, he understood suddenly, the entire meaning of the covenant. Not the flow of power. The flow of attention. Someone standing in the doorway of your ruin, seeing all of it, and staying.

"The founders stole the Garden," he said. "But I stole something too. I stole the ending. It should have been: the guardian fails, the guardian dies at the broken gate, the covenant falls. Instead it was: the guardian fails, the guardian hides, and the covenant falls *slowly*, over three hundred years, so slowly that no one ever had to name the moment it died. I didn't just fail the Garden, Zeph. I failed it *indefinitely*."

"Bram—"

"The ledger." He touched it. "Read the last entry. Read what I was writing when I ran."

She bent over the page. The guardian's script was old, formal, the letters rooted like trees, but she read it aloud, slowly: "*Given this day: the spring flood to the eastern valleys, the first warmth to the lambing folds, one dream of green to a child in the fishing villages. Received: a song, a wedding garland, the grief of a good woman, freely offered. Owed: nothing. The accounts are—*"

She stopped.

"Balanced," Bram said. "The accounts were balanced. A thousand years of giving and receiving, and on the last morning, the ledger said *owed: nothing*. And then the founders made it say *owed: everything*—and I set down the pen and ran, and for three hundred years the ledger has been waiting for someone to finish the sentence." He turned to her, and his translucent hands were shaking. "That's why it has to be you. Not because of your gift. Because you're the first person in three centuries to walk through that gate *owing something and willing to pay it*. The founders never paid. I never paid. I hid. You came back to a dying house and you're standing in its doorway and you haven't left."

Zeph looked down at the ledger for a long time. Then she did something he did not expect. She took the pen's successor—a stub of grey charcoal from the hearth—and beneath the unfinished entry, in her small, careful taster's hand, the hand that had written a thousand discreet reports of *nothing amiss*, she wrote:

*Owed: everything. Payment begun.*

She set the charcoal down. "There," she said, unsteadily. "Now it's not only your failure in this house. Now it's mine too. We'll fail it together if we have to, but we'll do it in the ledger, where it can be seen. That's what I know how to do, Bram. I can't fight. I can't rule. But I can *record the truth*, and I have been doing it in secret my whole life, and I am so tired of the secret."

Bram found that his eyes were leaking, which was absurd, because he had not been sure for some years that he still had eyes, in any sense that mattered. The green in his hands pulsed once, faintly, like something remembering.

* * *

They slept that night in the guardian's cottage, under the cloak from the peg, in a house that had waited three hundred years to be warm again and made do with two bodies' worth of warmth, which was, the house seemed to agree, better than nothing.

Bram did not sleep. He stood at the window watching the grey orchards, and he felt himself thinning, hour by hour, the translucence climbing his arms now, past his elbows, the orchard light passing through him more easily with each passing bell. By morning, Zeph would see it. By evening, she would begin to bargain with him about it, and he would have to refuse her, because her gift was needed for the heart-tree and he was spent currency already, and they both knew it, and neither of them would say it.

Near dawn—by the old reckoning, though the Garden had no true dawn, only a slow brightening of the sourceless light—he saw it.

Far off, past the failing orchards, past the dry fork of the rivers, the land rose into hills, and on the highest hill stood a tree. Even at this distance, even grey, it was vast—a crown like a storm frozen mid-swirl, a trunk broad as a watchtower. The heart-tree. And around its base, faint but unmistakable even in the dim light, ran a thread of gold.

Not the grey of the Garden. Not the green of what remained. Gold—warm, living, moving gold, winding up the trunk like a vine of light.

Zeph woke and came to stand beside him, and he felt her go still.

"What is that?" she whispered.

"That," Bram said, and his voice cracked on it, "is the third keystone. Fused to the heart-tree, just as the founders left it." He did not look away from it. He could not. "But that light—Zeph, that light is *new*. The keystone has been dark for three hundred years. Something has woken it."

The gold pulsed, slow and patient, like a heartbeat keeping time for a world.

"Something is waiting for us," Bram said, "and it knows we're coming."
