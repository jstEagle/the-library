## 16. Equinox Eve

The keystones did not want to be assembled.

Zephyrine learned this in the hour before dusk, kneeling at the roots of the heart-tree with her palms pressed flat against the three stones as though she could hold a conversation with them by touch alone. The first—pulled from the dark beneath the Pale Tower, still cold with the memory of iron—fought her the way a drowning thing fights. The second, raised dripping from the drowned chapel, hummed with a low grief that vibrated up through her wrist bones and settled somewhere behind her sternum. The third had come out of the heart-tree itself only after her mother's memory was paid into it, and it was warm, unbearably warm, like a stone left all afternoon in sun that no longer existed in this grey place.

She had arranged them in a triangle around the tree's exposed root-crown, and now they would not stay.

Each time she set them down and stepped back, they drifted apart by inches—not sliding across the soil but *turning*, slowly, the way a sleeping person turns away from a lamp. As if even shattered, even half-dead, the pieces of the covenant remembered what had been done to them and wanted no part of being whole again.

"They're not refusing you," Bram said from behind her. "They're grieving."

He sat against the trunk of the heart-tree with his back to its bark, and Zeph tried not to look at him too long or too often, because looking meant measuring, and measuring meant knowing how much of him the last weeks had taken. His edges had gone soft in a way that had nothing to do with kindness. The light under his skin—the spirit-light he had poured out in the flooded chapel until the water glowed like drowned stars—came back thinner each day, a candle burning down to its final finger of wax. His hands, which had once been quick and green-fingered among impossible plants, lay still in his lap now, and where his shadow should have fallen there was only the faintest smudge on the grey grass, like breath on glass.

"Bram." She kept her voice level. She had gotten good at that, seven years of tasting poison with a courtier's smile. "Tell me what happens when I put them together."

The orchard around them held its silence. Grey trees in grey rows, fruit hanging heavy and colorless, rivers asleep under skins of pale silt. Above, through a sky the color of old pewter, the first star of evening had appeared—a real one, sharp and white, because the Garden's dying sky still opened onto the true heavens, and Zeph found that detail almost unbearable. Everything here was fading except the things that had never belonged to the Garden at all.

"You know," Bram said gently. "You've known since the tree took your mother's face from you. You felt it then. The covenant is not a machine, Zeph. It's a promise. And promises need someone to keep them."

"Say it anyway."

"A warden." He said the word the way one says the name of the dead. "Bound to the Garden. Not visiting. Not tending. *Bound*. Your life braided into the root of this place for as long as it stands—which, if the covenant is mended, could be longer than any human life has ever been. You would feel every season. Every seed. Every death of every small green thing in both realms. You would never walk out of these orchards again except as the Garden wills it, and the Garden—" his mouth twisted, "—the Garden has been starved for so long that when it finally feeds, I don't know if it will know how to be gentle."

Zeph looked down at her hands. They were a taster's hands, small and unremarkable, nicked at the knuckles from kitchen work, stained faintly silver at the fingertips from years of holding other people's food against her tongue and listening for the whisper of harm. Those same hands had pulled a keystone out of a living tree at the cost of her mother's laugh, her mother's smell, the particular way her mother had said her name with the *ph* soft as a moth's wing. She couldn't remember any of it now. She remembered that she had given it. The giving remained; everything given was gone.

"And there's no other way," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"There's always another way," Bram said. "That's what no one told me, the first time."

*The first time.* He had said it once before, in the ruined greenhouse, in a voice so quiet she'd almost missed it: *I was the covenant's first guardian.* She had not asked him to explain. Some confessions you let a person carry until they're ready to set them down. But tonight, with the equinox less than a day away and the keystones turning their backs to one another in the dirt, she thought perhaps she needed to hear it.

"Tell me about the first time," she said. "All of it. Not the version you gave Corvane's smugglers. Not the version you gave me when you were trying to make me brave."

Bram was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere far off in the grey orchards, a river shifted in its sleep—a sound like a great body turning over under blankets.

"I was young," he said at last. "Younger than you are now, though we don't age the way you do. When the covenant was new, the Garden chose a keeper from among the spirits—one who could walk both realms, who could stand at the gate and say *this much flows and no more*, who could remember on behalf of everyone else. It was an honor. Everyone said so." He smiled, and it was the saddest thing Zeph had seen since the neighbor's door had been broken open. "I believed them. I stood at the gate for what your kind would call centuries, and I was proud, and I was diligent, and I never once wondered why the covenant needed a jailer instead of a doorkeeper.

"When the founders came with their siphons and their stolen keystones, I fought them. That part is true, whatever else isn't. I fought them and I lost, and here is what I have had a very long time to understand, Zeph: I lost because I was guarding the wrong thing. I guarded the *power*. The flow, the gate, the treasure. I should have guarded the *promise*—the idea that two realms could share without either one owning. A promise doesn't need a warden. A promise needs witnesses. People on both sides who remember why it was made." He lifted his fading hands, turned them over. "Instead they made me a lock, and locks can be broken. I broke. And I have spent—I don't know how long—wandering your city as a beggar's shadow, smelling of rain, watching the dynasty spend my Garden coin by coin, telling myself that next year I would find a way back. So believe me when I tell you what I'm about to tell you."

He looked at her then, and his eyes were the green of the very first leaf of spring, the green that exists nowhere else, and they were full of something fierce and pleading.

"*Refuse.*"

The word fell into the silence between them like a stone into a well.

"The covenant will offer you the warden's bond because that is how it was written—by people who thought power must be held by someone, forever, or it would be taken by someone, forever. But you already proved them wrong once, didn't you? In the tower. You poisoned nothing and freed everyone. You found the third way before you ever saw the trap." He leaned forward, urgent now, the candle-flame of him guttering brighter with feeling. "If you bind yourself tomorrow, you will save the Garden and lose everything the Garden is *for*. What good is renewal locked in one pair of hands? What good is magic that belongs to a warden instead of to the world? I watched a dynasty steal enchantment by hoarding it. Don't let the covenant teach you to hoard it out of love. Love hoarded is just theft with better manners."

Zeph's throat was tight. "And if I refuse? If there's no third way this time?"

"Then we fail," Bram said simply. "We fail together, honestly, having refused to become the thing we were fighting. There are worse ends." He paused, and his voice dropped. "But I don't believe we'll fail. I've watched you for weeks, little taster. You taste truth the way other people taste poison. If anyone can find the flavor the covenant hasn't offered yet—it's you."

She wanted to answer him. She wanted it so badly that her chest ached with it—and that was when the ground shivered.

Not the deep, slow shudder of the Garden's ongoing collapse, which she had grown to know the way one knows a sick relative's breathing. This was sharper. Closer. A vibration in the grey soil like a drum struck once, hard, and the sleeping rivers all along the horizon stirred at once, silt rising off them in slow ghosts.

Bram was on his feet faster than she'd seen him move in days, and for one heartbeat the old light flared in him, bright enough to throw a real shadow. "That's not the Garden," he said. "That's boots. Ten thousand boots, keeping time."

Zeph scrambled up the low rise at the orchard's edge, Bram beside her, and looked back the way they had come.

The gate between realms stood behind them—a wound in the air, a shimmering seam above the threshold-stone, wide enough for two people to pass if they walked close together. Beyond it, impossibly far and impossibly near, lay the terraced hills above Vellamare, and those hills were moving.

Torches. Hundreds of them, thousands, snaking down the switchback roads like rivers of fire pouring toward the threshold. Banners she knew too well—the white hart of House Isolde on black—and the glint of pikeheads catching torchlight, rank after rank after rank. Drums. She could hear the drums even here, even through the seam between worlds, a heartbeat with no love in it.

At the head of the column, riding a grey horse whose breath steamed in the autumn cold, rode a figure in hunt-black with a sword bare across her saddle.

"She brought her whole army," Zeph whispered.

"No." Bram's voice had gone very quiet and very old. "She brought her whole army because she's afraid. Look at the rear ranks—supply wagons, siege gear, priests. Queens don't march on gardens, Zeph. They march on *threats*. Isolde knows what night it is. She knows that if the covenant wakes whole, the siphon dies, and the dynasty dies with it. She isn't coming to capture you." He swallowed. "She's coming to burn the orchards and salt the roots and end this forever. And Voss—"

"Voss is at the front." Zeph's stomach turned over. She thought of the hunt-captain's face in the throne room, the flicker of something hunted behind her eyes when Zeph had spoken of the Pale Tower's cells. *Herself secretly gifted.* Corvane had said it; Odile's map had confirmed it in its careful way. "Maybe she'll turn. Maybe—"

"Maybe," Bram agreed. "But hope is not a plan, and equinox is not waiting."

Below them, at the threshold-stone, the seam in the air trembled. And Zeph felt it then—felt it in her teeth, in the silver-tipped fingers, in the place where her mother's memory had been and where something else now lived, something vast and waking and desperate. The Garden knew. The Garden had known for hours, maybe days, and it was reaching for her the way a drowning person reaches for a rope, and its reach felt like warmth and like chains all at once.

*Bind yourself,* the warmth whispered, in a voice made of sap and rain and every seed that had ever split open in the dark. *Bind yourself and I will protect you. Bind yourself and no army can touch you. Bind yourself and live.*

There it was. The trap within the gift, laid out plain as a banquet table. Not cruelty—never cruelty. Just need, enormous and ancient and utterly without malice, offering her the only salvation it understood: herself, forever, nailed to the root as its keeper, its lock, its warden. The same bargain Bram had accepted an age ago. The same bargain that had broken him.

Zeph stood on the rise with the wind coming through the grey orchards, carrying the smell of torch-smoke from a world away, and she made herself look at it clearly. At the army. At the keystones lying turned-away in the dirt. At Bram, guttering beside her, who had urged her to refuse while standing in a garden that would die without someone to hold it together.

Courage, she thought, was not choosing to leap. Courage was seeing the whole trap—the bars, the bait, the beautiful door swinging open—and walking toward it anyway with your eyes open, because someone had to, and because the alternative was letting fear choose for you.

"We put them together," she said.

Bram turned to her sharply. "Zeph—"

"Not as warden. Not yet. Maybe not at all." She was already moving down the slope, fast, her voice steadying with every step as seven years of hiding burned off her like morning fog. "Isolde thinks she's marching on a garden. Let her arrive and find a *negotiation*. But the keystones have to be whole before her torches reach the threshold, because a dead covenant can't argue, and a living one might listen." She dropped to her knees at the heart-tree's roots and reached for the first stone, the cold one, the one from under the tower. "Help me hold them. They trust you. Or they did."

"They did," Bram said softly. He knelt opposite her, and his fading hands closed over the second stone, the grieving one, and where his skin touched it a thin thread of green light woke and ran around the keystone like a vein. "Zeph. Whatever happens when they join—if the bond comes for you, if the Garden takes your yes before you've finished saying it—you call out. You *taste* it first, the way you taste everything. Promise me."

"I promise to taste it," she said. "I promise nothing else."

It would have to be enough. He knew it; she saw him know it, saw him accept it with a nod that cost him more than any battle had.

Together they turned the stones to face one another. The cold one. The grieving one. The warm one that held her mother's name in a language she could no longer read. As the three points of the triangle drew close, the air between them began to sing—a note below hearing, felt in the marrow, in the sleeping rivers, in the roots of every grey tree—and the heart-tree's bark split along an old scar and began, very slowly, to glow.

Far behind them, at the edge of the world, the drums stopped.

In the sudden silence, Zeph heard the one sound she had dreaded and longed for all at once: the threshold-stone singing back, as the first torches of Queen Isolde's army crested the final hill, and the seam between the realms began, with a sound like tearing silk, to widen.

* * *

They worked through the night.

There was no other way to say it and no grander way to live it. The keystones resisted, and Zeph coaxed, and Bram held, and bit by bit the triangle became a ring, the ring became a lattice of green fire woven through the heart-tree's roots, and the Garden began—faintly, grudgingly, like an old woman persuaded to dance—to breathe.

Color came first to the smallest things. Moss on the north side of stones blushed from grey to a green so tender it hurt. One apple, high in the nearest tree, flushed from ash-white to a red that seemed lit from within. A river woke all at once with a gasp of rushing water and ran clean and loud through the orchard, and Zeph laughed out loud at the shock of it, the sheer *noise* of a living world, and then pressed both hands over her mouth because the sound carried, and sound carried to armies.

Bram changed too. Slowly, in increments, the way frost leaves a window. Color crept back into him from the places where he touched the land—his hands going brown and sure, the light under his skin thickening from candle-glimmer to lantern-glow. By the deepest hour of the night he could stand without leaning on the tree, and when he smiled at her across the lattice of fused keystones, his shadow fell long and solid and real across the grass, and Zeph had to look away because her eyes were stinging.

"This is what it was," he said quietly. "Before. I'd forgotten. Isn't that the joke of it? The guardian forgot what he was guarding."

"It's not a joke," Zeph said. "It's a warning."

The hours wore on. She tasted the covenant the way Bram had asked—constantly, carefully, one inner tongue pressed to the flow of power like a fingertip to a suspect broth. She catalogued flavors. Renewal, obviously, green and endless. Memory, salt-bright, flowing both directions now through the half-woven lattice. And underneath, deeper, older: the warden's clause, coiled at the covenant's heart like a hair-thin wire of iron in a loaf of bread. Waiting. Patient. *One must be bound. One must remain. Say yes, daughter of renewal, and be home forever.*

She did not say yes. She did not say no. She kept tasting, and kept weaving, and waited for the third flavor, the one the covenant hadn't offered yet, trusting it would come the way she had once trusted her instincts over a duke's wine cup—because they always had, and because the alternative was unthinkable.

Dawn came grey, then gold. Real gold. The first true sunrise the Garden had seen in an age, pouring through the orchard rows and setting every dewdrop burning, and Zeph stood in the middle of it with dirt to her elbows and exhaustion in her bones like lead, and thought: *whatever happens today, I was here for this. No one can take this morning away from me. Not even me.*

Then the horns sounded at the threshold, and the morning stopped belonging to anyone.

They came through in formation. Of course they did—Isolde's army did nothing unformed. Rank upon rank stepping through the widened seam onto Garden soil, boots finding the grass, pikes leveled, and the strangeness of it struck Zeph physically: soldiers marching into a place of growing things with weapons meant for breaking them. Behind the vanguard came the priests in bone-white, chanting the rite of severance, and behind them the supply wagons, and behind them, on foot now, leading her horse, Queen Isolde of Vellamare in armor the color of winter dawn, her crown bound to her helm with a strap like a common soldier's.

And ahead of them all, ten paces past the front line, walking alone with her sword drawn and pointed at the ground, came Hunt-Captain Sarel Voss.

Zeph watched her cross the meadow before the orchard and read her the way she read anything that might contain poison. Voss's stride was steady. Her jaw was set. Her eyes swept the grey-gold orchard with a hunter's precision—and then stopped, caught, snagged on something. On the river running clean. On the single red apple. On the color.

Zeph saw the exact moment the hunt-captain understood what she had been sent to burn.

Voss halted. Turned. Looked back at the ranks behind her—at the priests, at the wagons, at the queen—and Zeph, watching from the orchard's edge with Bram's hand suddenly tight around hers, saw the hunt-captain's sword arm tremble, just once, like a plucked string.

"Whatever you're going to do," Bram breathed, "do it soon."

Queen Isolde raised one gauntleted hand, and ten thousand soldiers stopped as one, and her voice carried across the meadow, clear and cold and absolutely certain:

"By the authority of the crown of Vellamare, in the name of the founders' blood and the dynasty's survival—burn it. Burn it all."

The archers raised their bows. The flame-priests began the words of kindling. And Zephyrine Marchetti, taster no more, stepped out of the orchard into the open meadow with the light of the waking covenant blazing behind her like a second sunrise, lifted both silver-tipped hands so every soldier could see they were empty, and called out in a voice that carried further than any queen's:

"Before you burn a garden—someone should taste what's in it!"
