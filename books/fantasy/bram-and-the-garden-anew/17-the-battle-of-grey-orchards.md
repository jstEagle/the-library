## 17. The Battle of Grey Orchards

I had forgotten what it was to have hands that could shape the world. For three hundred years I had shaped only small things—a wilted leaf coaxed green, a beggar's fever broken in the night, rain called down over a single rooftop when the city's poor were dying of thirst and no one watched. Small magics, smuggled magics, the leavings of an exile. But standing at the threshold of the Garden with the grey orchards spread before me like a held breath, I felt my old strength stirring in my marrow, faint as dawn through fog, and I understood: the Garden knew its keystone had come home. It was pouring what remained of itself into the ground beneath our feet, and some of that last flood was seeping into me.

It would kill it faster. I knew that too. The Garden was spending its final coin on us.

Zeph stood beside me at the gate where the three keystones had been laid out on a slab of living stone, waiting for her hands. Her face was pale in the colorless light—this close to the heart of things, even sunlight arrived drained, like a letter copied too many times—and there was a hollow around her eyes that had not been there before the heart-tree took her mother's memory as payment. She did not speak of it. She carried the absence the way one carries an amputated limb, reaching for what is no longer there.

"They're coming," she said.

I heard them before I saw them: the drum of boots on the causeway, the creak of wagon-axles, the thin bright sound of drawn steel. Isolde's army spilled from the tree-line at the Garden's edge like ink poured into water, rank upon rank of it, and above their helmets flew the queen's banners—white lilies on grey, the sigil of a dynasty built on stolen soil.

At their head rode Sarel Voss.

* * *

I want to say something true about Voss, because the songs will get her wrong. They will make her either a traitor or a hero, and she was neither. She was a woman who had spent her whole life sharpening herself against a lie.

She wore no helmet. That was the first thing I noticed—the Hunt-Captain of Vellamare riding bareheaded into battle, her dark hair braided tight against her skull, her scarred face bare and turned toward the Garden the way a starving man turns toward a lit window. Behind her came two hundred soldiers of the Pale Tower, and behind them, in wagons, came the things the Tower had been building all autumn: iron cages, ward-chains thick as my arm, and barrels of salt-lime for burning what the queen had decided could not be permitted to exist.

"Zeph." Corvane appeared at my shoulder, silent as ever despite his size, though today he had put aside his smuggler's rags for armor scavenged from the undercity's forgotten armories—mismatched plate, a breastplate too big for him, a helm he carried under one arm because he refused to have his hearing muffled. His eyes were fixed on the banners. On the lilies. "How many can we hold?"

"Fewer than are coming," I said.

"Then we hold fewer. We hold what we hold." He said it lightly, but his knuckles were white around the haft of his axe. I had learned, in the short brutal weeks of knowing him, to read Corvane's grief by how gently he spoke. The gentler the voice, the deeper the wound. Today his voice was almost tender.

The freed magicians numbered sixty-one. They had come out of the Pale Tower's breached cells still gaunt and grey-skinned, still wearing the look of people who did not quite believe doors opened for them anymore. Some of them Zeph had known only as faces behind bars during her theft of the first keystone; others Odile's map had delivered. They had no banner. They had whatever they had carried into their arrests—kitchen knives, hairpins, the raw untrained gift that had gotten them condemned in the first place—and they had Zeph's promise, shouted up the tower stairs as they fled into the night: *the Garden needs you, and if the Garden lives, everything changes.*

They arranged themselves along the hedgerows of the first orchard, among trees that had once borne fruit heavy enough to bend silver branches, now grey and bare as drowned men's fingers.

And Voss rode forward alone.

* * *

She stopped her horse twenty paces from the gate, in the dead grass between armies, and for a long moment she only looked. That was Voss—I had seen it in the tower, in the rooftops of Vellamare, in every hunt she had led: she looked harder than anyone, as if looking were a discipline, a devotion. A woman built entirely of attention.

Then she dismounted, and walked the last ten paces on foot, and knelt down, and put her bare hand flat against the grey grass of the Garden.

I heard her breath go out of her. Even across that distance, I heard it—the sound of a certainty breaking.

Because the Garden does not hide from those who touch it honestly. That was always its nature; that was why the founders had needed chains and keystones and a dynasty of lies. Touch the living Garden and you feel everything it is: the memory of every spring it ever gave, every seed it ever woke, the whole long generosity of the world's first green place. And touching it now, feeling it dying, feeling *why* it was dying—who had been drinking from it for nine generations—Voss knelt in the grey grass with her hand spread wide, and the truth went into her like a blade going in.

She rose slowly. She turned to face her own soldiers. And I watched her draw her sword—not toward us—and hold it point-down beside her like a mourner holding a candle.

"Hunt-Captain!" someone called from the ranks. An officer, young, confused. "The queen's orders—"

"The queen," Voss said, and her voice carried the way voices carry over graveyards, flat and enormous, "has fed you to her garden."

Silence. Two hundred soldiers and sixty magicians and one fading spirit, all listening.

"I have spent eleven years hunting gifted people for this crown," Voss said. "I told myself it was order. I told myself the Tower protected them. This morning I touched the ground these creatures stand on—" her hand gestured at us, at the orchards, and I heard the word *creatures* turn over in her mouth and become something else, become *neighbors* — "and the ground showed me what your taxes buy. What your oaths water. Nine kings of Vellamare, drilling holes in the world's heart so their line could live forever. Every famine the Garden couldn't stop. Every child born magicless in a district the siphon had bled dry. That is what you are ordered to defend."

She turned her horse aside with a touch—sending it walking back toward the ranks, riderless, a message in itself—and drew her sword properly then, and set her feet in the grey grass facing her former army.

"I am done defending it."

For a heartbeat, nothing. Then a soldier in the third rank threw down his spear. Then two more. Then a sergeant with a face like a closed door unbuckled his sword-belt and let it fall, and after that it moved through the ranks like fire through dry grass—not all of them, never all, perhaps half—but half of Isolde's vanguard laying down arms while the other half stared at them in horror, and the officer who had first shouted climbed back onto his horse and galloped away toward the causeway to fetch the rest of the army, screaming orders no one followed.

Voss walked to our gate. She stopped before Zeph, and sheathed her sword, and said, in the voice of a woman handing over her whole life in a single sentence:

"Tell me what to hold. I'll hold it until I'm pulled off it."

* * *

What followed was not a battle in the way songs mean battles. There was no charge, no glorious clash. What followed was a siege compressed into an afternoon, ugly and close-walled, fought along the hedgerows and drainage channels of a dying orchard, and I will not dress it up.

Isolde came herself within the hour. Of course she came. She had ridden all night from Vellamare at the head of four thousand soldiers, and she did not send captains ahead of her because Isolde had never once in her reign allowed another person to be the first to see anything. She arrived in a carriage drawn by six white horses that looked wrong against the grey landscape, over-painted, gilded, absurd—heraldry made flesh—and stepped down from it in armor of chased silver that no smith had ever intended for actual use, and surveyed the field where a third of her vanguard stood with the enemy.

"Captain Voss," she said, and her voice did not need to be raised; it had the flat carrying quality of a bell. "You are relieved of command. Kneel, and I will permit you to die a soldier."

Voss, spattered with mud at the second hedge-line, did not kneel. She laughed once, briefly, without any humor in it at all, and went back to fighting.

And then the afternoon went to ruin in earnest.

I will tell the parts I saw, because the parts I did not see belong to other tellers. I held the eastern hedgerow with twelve tower-magicians and a boy of perhaps sixteen named Fenn who could make light but had never been taught to make anything else; we made light anyway, blinding bursts of it that turned the grey orchard to strobing noon and bought us seconds, always just seconds. I felt the Garden feeding me and I felt it dwindling as it fed, a well lowering drop by drop, and I rationed myself like a man rationing his own blood. Zeph was everywhere—or it seemed so—moving along the lines with her renewal-gift blazing openly for the first time in her hidden life, pressing her palms to torn ground so that thorn-hedges rose where soldiers meant to ride through, mending broken bones with a touch, dragging exhausted fighters back from the lip of death. She had stopped being invisible somewhere between the tower and the equinox, and watching her I understood that invisibility had never been her nature, only her cage.

Corvane held the causeway.

He chose it himself, because the causeway was the throat—if it fell, the reserve regiments poured straight through to the gate and the keystones, and everything died: the Garden, Vellamare's memory, all of it. So Corvane planted himself at the narrowest span of that ancient stone road with his mismatched armor and his axe, and behind him he placed thirty of the freed magicians, and for two hours he made the throat of the Garden uncrossable.

I saw it in glimpses, between crises at the hedgerows. I saw him take a lance meant for a boy with frost-gifted hands and break the lance-shaft with his bare grip. I saw him laughing—that terrible light laugh of his, the gentle voice gone entirely, grief and joy fused into one white-hot thing. He fought like a man settling accounts. Because he was. Everything Corvane had done since the undercity—every smuggling run, every risk, even the betrayal, even selling our route to the crown for the promise that his family's names would be carved back into the memory-stones of Vellamare—everything had been one long argument with oblivion. His wife and daughters had been taken by Isolde's father twenty years ago, and not merely killed: *erased*, struck from record and remembrance, so that even their ghosts had nowhere to stand. Corvane had spent two decades trying to force the world to say their names again.

Now, on the causeway, the world was saying them.

"Bram!" he roared across the din, at some moment when the press of soldiers recoiled and he stood heaving in a drift of bodies, alive, impossible, bleeding from a dozen cuts. "Bram! Tell the stones! Tell the Garden! Adelisa! Little Corva! Little Maren! Say them, old ghost, say them so the ground hears!"

And the ground heard. The Garden heard. I shouted the names back at him, all three, my ruined voice cracking on them, and behind me the grey orchard stirred—just barely, just once, a shiver running through the dead branches like a sleeper turning over—and I saw Corvane see it. Saw him understand that the realm he had betrayed and bled for had, at last, in the only currency that mattered, paid him back.

The reserve regiments came at dusk.

Four thousand became two thousand at the throat of the causeway, wave after wave, and Corvane's thirty became nineteen, and then thirteen, and the light was failing and the keystones were still unfused on their slab because fusing them required Zeph's full undivided gift and she could not leave the walls or everyone inside them died. I fought my way to the causeway through a twilight full of torch-smoke and shouting, and found Corvane at the narrowest span, alone now but for five, holding a gap in the line with his body because his axe had broken an hour past and he had not stopped.

A crossbow bolt took him through the chest while I was still forty feet away.

I reached him as he went down. I knelt in the blood-slick stone and pressed my hands to the wound and poured everything left in me into it—spirit-light, Garden-strength, the dregs of my exile's hoard—and he caught my wrist with a hand already going cold, and stopped me.

"No," he said. Gentle as anything. Gentlest I ever heard him. "Spend it on the wall, old ghost. I've bought what I came to buy."

"Corvane—"

"My name." His eyes found mine, and there was no pain in them, or rather pain so old it had become furniture. "Not Corvane the smuggler. Not the undercity name. My mother named me Aurelian Corvane Drosselmeier, and my wife was Sera, and my daughters were Adelisa and Corva and Maren, and I want—" his breath hitched, and steadied, because he had spent twenty years learning to steady it— "I want it said here. Now. Where the ground can hear. All of it."

So I said it. All of it, loudly, into the smoke and the torchlight, my voice carrying over the causeway where soldiers had stopped fighting to stare at the big man lying in their midst—said his name and hers and the children's, each one, twice, and the fifth remaining defender took it up, and then a sixth voice, and then—God forgive them, or thank them—a soldier of Isolde's own ranks, weeping, saying *Maren, Maren,* like a man remembering his own daughter, and the names went into the grey ground of the Garden the way rain goes into drought-cracked earth, and the whole orchard shivered, and somewhere far off a river that had slept for centuries moved in its bed.

Corvane smiled. He said, "Paid in full," and died with his hand on the stone of the causeway he had held, and I closed his eyes and stood up and turned to the advancing reserve line, empty of everything, ready to be the next body in the gap.

Which was when the sky over Vellamare, far behind the enemy lines, began to glow.

* * *

It was not firelight. I have seen cities burn; this was nothing burning. It was *memory*-colored—gold and rose and the deep blue-green of the Garden in its living centuries—rising over the distant towers of the capital like dawn arriving from the wrong horizon, and spreading, and flowing outward across the land in slow luminous tides.

The battle stopped. Four thousand soldiers stopped mid-stride, mid-swing, and stared south, because every one of them was feeling the same thing I was feeling: the tide going out of the siphon. Nine generations of stolen vitality, loosed from the dynasty's hoards all at once, flooding back toward the Garden—and passing *through* everyone in its path on the way, showing each of them, in a single unbearable instant, exactly where their kingdom's splendor had come from and what it had cost.

Some soldiers fell to their knees. Some dropped weapons and simply stood weeping. A colonel tore the lily-badge from his own breastplate and cast it into the mud. And away down the causeway, in the failing light, I saw the royal carriage surrounded by its guard, wheeling, retreating, fleeing the tide it had spent nine reigns damming—

—and I saw, at the gate, Zeph turn toward the heart-tree with the three keystones lifted in her arms, and I saw her look back at me across the ruined orchard, across the bodies and the hedges and the kneeling thousands, and mouth one word.

*Anew.*

The battle was over. What came next would not be won by any blade. I ran to her through the grey grass, through the rising gold tide, to help her carry the covenant home.
