## 22. Taster No More

The hall had been the Pale Tower's great chamber once. Zeph still remembered it differently—remembered standing in its shadows with a silver spoon and a lie in her mouth, tasting for a queen who would have fed her to the wards if she'd known what Zeph truly was. Now the tower's black stone had been dressed in autumn: garlands of late grapes, sheaves of barley tied with red thread, lanterns of woven willow that threw warm light across walls where the old iron sigils of the crown had been pried away, leaving pale scars like healed wounds.

They had debated whether to keep the scars. Zeph had argued for keeping them.

"You don't sand down a memory," she'd told the council, "just because it aches."

So the scars stayed, and tonight they wore garlands too.

She stood at the long table's head beside Bram, who was trying very hard to look like he belonged among chairs. Garden-spirits did not, as a rule, sit. He had spent the last hour discovering that benches were a human invention with teeth, shifting his weight, his bare feet finding the floorboards the way roots find water—patiently, at an angle no one else could have managed. His coat was the same beggar's rags he'd worn in the undercity, though Odile had taken a needle to them, and now they were *mended* rags, which somehow made them stranger and more dignified at once. Around his throat hung a sprig of heart-tree leaves, green as spring's first promise, because Bram refused anything finer.

"They're staring," he murmured.

"They're curious," Zeph said. "It's different."

"Curiosity is just staring that hasn't found its manners yet."

She laughed—actually laughed, out loud, in front of two hundred people—and felt the sound go through her like sunlight through water. A year ago she would have swallowed it. A year ago she had swallowed everything: her gift, her grief, her name half-hidden behind the smallness of her trade. *Taster*, she had called herself, as if a life could be reduced to what it survived.

Tonight she tasted for no one's protection. Tonight she tasted because eating together was the oldest covenant there was, older than keystones and kingdoms—the one that said *I will not poison you; I trust you not to poison me.*

* * *

The dish came out on the shoulders of four bearers, and the hall went quiet enough to hear the lanterns crackle.

It was simple, deliberately so—a barley pilaf, dark bread torn rather than cut, roasted gourds split open to their seeds. But threaded through it all, unmistakable even before the platter reached the table, was a scent that made half the room close their eyes. Green and bitter-sweet, like rain remembered by a stone. Like the first breath after a fever breaks.

Garden-grown herbs. The first harvest ever carried openly across the gate.

Zeph watched the platter approach and thought of the grey orchards she had walked with Bram—trees that bore ash-colored fruit into air that tasted of forgetting, rivers asleep beneath their banks. She thought of the heart-tree, and of the price pressed into her palm there, and of the seed that now slept in the public garden below the hill, wrapped in winter, waiting for its own season the way all true things wait.

The bearer set the dish before the council. The council was a strange creature: three voices from Vellamare's guilds, two from the undercity smugglers' rings, one from the freed magicians who had lately become something between an order and a family—and facing them, the Garden's own delegates, dryad-kin and river-speakers who had crossed the living gate for the first time that morning, blinking at the city's noise like owls dragged into noon.

Sarel Voss sat among them, out of uniform, which looked almost indecent on her. She had brought her own knife anyway. Some habits were vertebrae; you did not argue them out of a body. When the platter reached her, she took a portion without ceremony and ate it looking straight ahead, and only Zeph saw the way her jaw went loose afterward, the way her eyes shone with something she would deny to her grave.

Hunger, maybe. Or homecoming. With Voss it was hard to tell the difference, and wiser not to ask.

The first spoonful was offered to Zeph—not by rank, but by request of the council, because this was her office now, the one she had chosen when she refused every crown they tried to set on her. *Keeper of Tastings*, the charter called it, and Odile had laughed until she cried when she heard the title. *You spent seven years tasting in secret,* she'd said, *and now they've made the secret itself an office.* But it wasn't a joke, not really. Someone had to stand at the boundary between realms and take the first bite, someone whose tongue knew both worlds, and there was exactly one person in Vellamare whose mouth had learned its craft in the queen's kitchens and its truth in a dying garden.

Zeph lifted the spoon.

The steam rose past her face. Beneath the herbs she caught barley and woodsmoke and the particular sweetness of gourds grown in honest dirt, and beneath all of it—faint, but there, the way a heartbeat is there if you hold still enough—the taste of the covenant itself. Renewal. The flavor of things that end and begin again.

She ate.

For a moment nothing happened, and then everything happened, quietly, the way spring happens: a warmth starting behind her sternum and spreading outward, a loosening in the room, a murmur running along the tables as the magic in the food touched every tongue present. Not power. Nothing anyone could use or hoard. Just recognition—the old covenant's new voice saying *you are part of this* to farmer and smuggler and spirit alike. Down the length of the hall, a wilting garland of ivy lifted its leaves. A candle flame leaned toward the platter like a listener. One of the river-speakers wept openly into her hands, and no one thought it strange.

Bram's hand found hers under the table. His fingers smelled of loam and rain, always, no matter how many times Odile scrubbed them.

"Well?" he asked softly.

"It's good," Zeph said. Her voice cracked on the second word, and she let it. "It tastes like being forgiven."

* * *

Later, when the council had eaten and the hall opened into general feasting—when fiddlers tuned up in the corner where the ward-irons used to hang, and children from the undercity raced smugglers' daughters between the tables, and a dryad attempted her first mug of cider with catastrophic results—Zeph stepped out onto the balcony to breathe.

The night was October-clear. Below the hill, the public garden glowed faintly where the seed slept under its mulch of heart-tree leaves. Beyond it, Vellamare sprawled in its ten thousand lights, and she could see, even from here, the places where the returning tide of memory had changed the city's shape: the founder statues pulled down in the square, yes, but also smaller things. New shrines in doorways. Charms hung openly in windows instead of hidden under eaves. A whole city learning, street by street, to stop apologizing for what it was.

Her mother had lived in a city that couldn't do any of that.

The thought arrived the way it always arrived now—not as a blade but as a door opening onto an empty room. Zeph stood with her hands on the cold balustrade and went looking, as she did some nights, for her mother's face.

She knew the facts the way you know entries in a ledger. Dark hair. A gardener's build. Hands that had taught small hands to press seeds into soil. She knew the facts, but the face itself—

Gone. Not torn away; paid away, freely given, and that distinction mattered to her more than breath. Somewhere inside the heart-tree, held safe in living wood, was the memory of her mother's face, and Zeph had traded it for a realm, and she would make the trade again, and knowing this did not make the empty room less empty.

But here was the thing she had discovered about the shape of loss, here in the months since the equinox: the ledger was not the woman.

She closed her eyes and her mother's hands rose up in her, vivid as the moment they'd happened—soil-cool, cracked at the knuckles, guiding Zeph's small fingers around a seed no bigger than a fleck of pepper. *Not deep,* her mother had said, and Zeph could hear the voice's music even where the face behind it had gone soft as fog. *Seeds aren't buried, little love. They're tucked in. There's a difference, and the seed knows it.* And then the press of earth over the seed, gentle as closing a hand over a secret, and the watering can passed down, heavy, hers to carry.

She hadn't known, at five years old, that she was being handed her whole future in miniature. That she would spend her life tucking lost things into soil and waiting for them to green.

Zeph opened her eyes. Her cheeks were wet, and she didn't wipe them, because the balcony was dark and grief deserved better than secrecy—it had spent too many centuries locked in towers already.

"I can't remember her face anymore," she said aloud, to the garden below, to the night, to whoever listened. "But I remember her hands. I remember what she taught them to do." She laid her own palm flat against the balustrade, against the cold stone, as if pressing a seed. "Maybe that's the truer remembering. Maybe faces are just the wrapping."

"She'd say the same," said Bram, from the doorway. "If she were here. Which—" he paused, weighing honesty the way he weighed everything, in both hands, "—she is, a little. Everything given to the tree stays in the tree. And the tree's seed is sleeping right down there in your garden. So somewhere in the root of it, a woman is teaching hands to plant, and doesn't know whose hands they are, and it doesn't matter, because teaching is the part that survives."

Zeph turned. In the doorway's lantern-light, Bram looked more solid than she'd ever seen him—the exile's translucence gone from him since the covenant's renewal, since magic flowed both ways and the Garden's spirit could walk the human world without fading. He had filled in the way dawn fills in a landscape: gradually, and then all at once, and then so completely you forgot he had ever been a shadow.

He crossed to the balustrade and stood beside her, shoulder almost touching hers, watching the city with the expression of a man reading a letter he'd waited three hundred years to receive.

"Do you miss it?" she asked. "Being a shadow. Being small."

Bram considered this with his usual terrible patience. "Shadows don't get cold," he allowed. "Or hungry. Or embarrassed by cider." Behind them, faintly, came a crash and a peal of laughter that was probably the dryad's second mug. "But a shadow can't plant anything. Can't be seen planting anything." He glanced sideways at her, and his eyes had that green depth the heart-tree lent them, old and young at once. "You taught me that. Seven years of making yourself invisible, and the first thing you did with your freedom was stand in a square and let everyone look at you."

"Somebody had to show them it wouldn't kill us."

"It nearly did."

"Yes," Zeph said. "That's generally the price of the true version of things." She thought of the false choices she'd been handed at the heart-tree—warden forever, or abandon the Garden entirely—and how the real answer had been to refuse the menu altogether, to write a third option in a language neither side had spoken yet. She wondered if that was the actual secret of covenants, of marriages, of friendships, of selves: that whenever you're offered only two doors, there's always a wall between them that isn't a wall at all, if you look closely. Just a curtain someone hung because they couldn't imagine anything beyond it.

Below, in the garden, the mulch over the heart-tree's seed shifted—or seemed to. A trick of wind, surely. It was far too early. Winter hadn't even—

No. Not a trick. As she watched, a single point of green parted the leaf-mold, delicate and absurd and utterly impossible, the way all true things are impossible right up until they happen. The seed was sprouting. Months early. On the night of the first shared harvest, at the moment its mother-tree's herbs touched a hundred trusting tongues.

"Bram," she breathed.

"I see it."

"That's not possible. It shouldn't wake till spring."

"Neither should most things worth waking." He was smiling, and she realized his eyes were wet. "Covenants kept tend to hurry the seasons, little keeper. You should know—you wrote that clause yourself."

* * *

They went down together, past the feasting hall, out through doors that no longer needed guards, into the October night where the grass was cold and the stars were sharp as taster's needles. The garden path glowed faintly where their feet fell—renewal leaking joyfully out of them both, marking their passage in tiny blooms of crocus and starflower that would baffle the gardeners at dawn.

At the seedbed, Zeph knelt. The sprout stood no taller than her thumb, two perfect leaves unfurled against the dark, and when she cupped her hands around it she felt warmth rising from the soil like breath, like welcome, like the beginning of an answer to a question she hadn't finished asking.

Her mother's hands moved in her memory—*not buried, tucked in*—and Zeph understood, suddenly and completely, what the tree was telling her. What it had perhaps been telling her since the equinox.

Payment made freely is never lost. It's planted.

And some seeds come back early.

Beside her, Bram knelt too, and together they watched the sprout turn its leaves toward the city lights, toward the tower-turned-school on the hill, toward the feast where two realms were learning each other's flavors—one small green thing, tucked into the dark, growing toward morning.
