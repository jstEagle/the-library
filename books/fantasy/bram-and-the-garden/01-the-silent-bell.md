## 1. The Silent Bell

The bell at Greyfall Waystation had not rung in eleven years, and Casper Lockridge liked it that way.

She felt it the way she felt everything now—not as sound, but as absence, a hole in the world shaped like a toll. The bell hung in its crooked frame above the station door, green with mountain rot, its clapper seized by rust and superstition both. The old-timers said it had been cast for the god Bram, back when the Thornfell villages still dared to ring for him, back before the crown's edicts turned every bell into evidence. Now it hung silent, and Casper passed beneath it every season like a woman walking under a blade that had forgotten how to fall.

*Still there,* she thought, the way she always thought it. *Still nothing.*

That was the trick of it. Anyone could hear a bell. Only Casper could hear a silence—could walk through the mountains and feel the place where Bram's voice had been, the way a tongue finds the gap where a tooth used to sit. The old god of Thornfell had died a hundred years ago, or so the priests said, and his silence lay over the high passes like snowmelt, cold and constant and everywhere. Casper had been born into it. She had learned to read it the way other guides read weather: the silence thickening before a rockfall, thinning near running water, pooling in the hollows where old shrines had been smashed and buried. It made her the best guide in the Thornfells, and it would hang her if anyone in a crown coat ever learned why.

So she kept it folded away, small and secret, the way she kept everything.

"Casper." Doss Hallow's voice carried up the switchback, roughened by cold. "You going to stand there admiring the view all morning, or are we getting this cargo over the shoulder before the weather turns?"

She turned. The mule train stretched below her, eight animals loaded with bolts of undyed wool and, tucked in the middle of the third load where the toll-wardens never looked, six kegs of Thornfell whiskey bound for lowland tables that preferred their sin unrecorded. Doss stood at the train's head with his collar up and his breath steaming, a broad, patient man built like something the mountain had decided to keep. Behind him, four hired hands stamped and muttered. Behind them, the pass fell away in gray steps toward the lowlands and the world Casper preferred not to think about.

"View's free," she said. "Everything else costs. Move them along."

The shoulder of the pass took them up through scrub pine and scree, the wind coming off the high snowfields with a knife's honesty. Casper walked point, as she always did, and let the silence wash over her. It was thicker here than it had been in spring. She noticed it the way she noticed a door left open in winter—not alarming, not yet, just wrong. Bram's dead silence had a texture to it, a settled quality, like ash after a fire has gone cold. This was different. This was ash *stirring*.

She pushed the thought down where she kept all the others.

They made the high col by midafternoon, and that was where the weather found them—not snow, not yet, but a wall of cloud boiling up from the western peaks with the smell of iron in it. Casper called the halt at the shelter cairn, a drystone hollow big enough for the mules and miserable enough for the men, and they huddled through the worst of it chewing dried venison and complaining with the particular relish of men who had nothing better to do.

"Heard a thing in Lowmark," one of the hands said—Ferren, a lowlander with more curiosity than sense. "Crown's posted new writs. Gift-hunt, they're calling it. Witch-hunters with silver badges, going village to village. Asking after anyone who *feels* things."

"Everyone feels things," Doss said. "That's what a belly's for."

"Funny. The writ says otherwise." Ferren leaned toward the fire he wasn't allowed to light. "Says there's folk born with the old god's touch in them. Mark-bearers. Says the crown wants them found. Says there's a reward."

Casper kept her eyes on the mule she was re-saddling and her hands steady, and both cost her something.

"How much of a reward?" Doss asked, and Casper could have kissed him for the boredom in his voice.

"Enough," Ferren said. "Enough that a body might start looking at his neighbors different."

The wind took the conversation apart after that, and Casper finished with the mule's girth strap, and her hands were steady the whole time, and she hated how much the steadiness cost. Ten years she had walked these mountains with her secret folded flat against her ribs. Ten years of reading silence and saying nothing, of finding lost children and drowned climbers by the pull of Bram's absence and calling it *instinct*, calling it *experience*, calling it anything but what it was. She had built a life the way you build a wall in scree country—low, wide, nothing that would catch the eye. And now some clerk in Lowmark had written a writ, and the wall was suddenly a shape on the horizon.

*You're not a mark-bearer,* she told herself, and the lie fit her mouth like an old boot. She had never seen her own mark. It sat below her left shoulder blade, she'd been told—she'd never dared a mirror long enough to look—small as a coin, shaped like a bell. Her mother had covered it with ash and oath and gotten a decade of quiet out of the bargain before the fever took her. *You're not anything, Casper Lockridge. You're a guide with a good nose for weather, and that's all anyone will ever say of you.*

The storm broke at dusk, and they moved.

* * *

Greyfall Waystation sat in the throat of the pass like a swallowed stone: one long room, a turf roof, a stove that had been lit so continuously for forty years that the turf above it had never grown back. The keeper was a woman named Maren Ost, sixty if she was a day, with forearms like hawser rope and a memory for debts that outlasted most religions. Casper had been trading through Greyfall for nine years, and Maren had never once asked her a question she didn't already know the answer to.

That was why it sat so badly, an hour past midnight, when Maren set down the pot she was scrubbing, looked at Casper across the stove, and said, "You should not have come this run."

Casper looked up from the manifest she was copying. Doss and the hands slept in the loft, snoring in ragged chorus. The stove ticked. Outside, the wind worried the eaves.

"That's a strange way to greet a paying customer."

"I'm not greeting you. I'm warning you." Maren came around the stove and sat, and Casper understood, with a slow cold settling, that the old woman had been waiting up for this. "There was a man through here four days ago. Crown man. Not a soldier—worse. One of the quiet ones. Silver badge under his glove, and he paid for his soup with a coin so new it still had the mint shine on it. He asked about traffic through the pass. Asked which guides ran cargo regular. Asked—" Maren's jaw worked, as if the words had to be chewed before swallowing—"asked if I'd ever heard of a Lockridge."

The stove ticked. Casper made herself breathe.

"And what did you tell him?"

"I told him Lockridge was a common enough name in the low country, and that guides come and go like weather, and that I keep books, not gossip." Maren's eyes, pale as river ice, did not blink. "He wrote it all down anyway. Then he asked me if I'd ever heard a bell ring in these mountains, and I said the bell's been dead since before my mother's time, and he smiled at that. Smiled like a man who'd caught a fish and was letting it tire itself out on the line."

Casper looked down at the manifest. Her own hand, her own false name, her own careful letters. *C. Lockridge, guide, contracted by D. Hallow, cargo: wool.*

"He's coming back," Maren said. "Quiet ones always come back. They like to arrive when you've decided you're safe."

"I'm not anything," Casper said. "You know that. I'm a woman who reads weather and keeps her mouth shut."

"I know what you are." Maren said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. "I've known for years, girl. You think I don't watch you walk under my bell and flinch? You think a woman keeps a waystation forty years without learning to read her guests?" She leaned forward. "I never said a word. I'll never say a word. But I'm old, and my silence only stretches so far, and there are men now with writs and silver badges who can make even honest silence crack. So I'm telling you what I'd tell my own daughter, if the crown had left me one. Finish this run if you must. Take your pay. And then *vanish*, Casper Lockridge. Vanish so thoroughly that even the mountain forgets which way you went."

Casper sat very still. Under her ribs, in the place she never touched, the silence of Bram lay thick and cold and, for the first time in ten years, *listening*.

"There's no vanishing from a writ," she said at last. "I've got contracts. I've got Doss. I've got—" She stopped, because the list had run out, and they both knew it. A decade of staying unseen, and here was the arithmetic of it: she had built a life so small that leaving it would cost almost nothing, and the nothing was its own kind of grief.

"Vanish anyway," Maren said. "Small lives are cheap to carry. That's their whole virtue."

Casper copied out the rest of the manifest with a steady hand, said her goodnights like a woman with nothing on her mind, and lay down in the loft between the snoring hands and stared at the rafters until the grey of morning came through the turf. She ran routes in her head. Southern passes were watched since the toll-war; the eastern scree was suicide in this season; the north—

The north was where the silence was thickest. The north was where the old shrines were buried. The north was the one direction she had spent ten years not looking, because looking north meant looking at the hole in the world, and the hole in the world had a way of looking back.

*Vanish anyway.*

Below, in the yard, the mules stamped. Doss was up, cursing the cold, calling her name. The run was half done; the whiskey still had to cross the shoulder and come down the far side; there was pay waiting and a reputation to keep and a wall of small quiet habits to maintain. Everything in her said: finish the run, take the pay, be nobody one more season.

Casper rolled out of her blanket, shouldered her pack, and went down the ladder into the grey morning light.

And stopped.

Because the bell above the waystation door—rust-seized, clapper-frozen, silent for eleven years—was ringing.

One soft, cracked note, no louder than a spoon against a cup, hanging in the cold air like something remembered rather than heard. Maren came out of the station and stood beside her, and neither of them spoke, and Doss looked up from the mules with his mouth open, and the note faded away into the wind off the high snowfields.

"That's not possible," Maren whispered.

Casper's heart was slamming against the folded secret under her ribs, and the silence all around them—the great dead silence of Bram that she had walked in for thirty years—felt, for the first time, less like an absence than like a held breath.

"Something's coming down out of the north," she heard herself say. "And I think it's looking for me."
