## 3. Hollow Covenant

Wren had never seen a house burn before.

She had heard of it—everyone had, in the way children hear of things that belong to the wide and dangerous world beyond their own doors—but hearing was a soft thing, a rumor shaped like a story. This was not a story. This was a smear of orange light against the underside of the clouds, three ridgelines east of the shepherd's hut where Casper had carried her, half-frozen, two days ago. This was smoke tasting of pitch and wool and something sweeter underneath that Wren's stomach understood before her mind would name it.

She stood at the hut's small window with her blanket around her shoulders like a cloak she hadn't earned, watching the glow pulse and gutter. The mark on her wrist—the sigil, the burning antlered thing the hedge-wives had gasped over when she was born, the reason her mother had wrapped her in a shawl and put her on a road alone—was warm. It was always warm now. Since the ravine, since Casper had pressed her bare hands to Wren's blue fingers and muttered something like a prayer or a curse, the mark had stopped being a birthmark and started being a *presence*. It sat under her skin like a coal banked for the night, breathing.

*They're burning them because of me,* she thought, and the thought did not feel like hers. It felt like something whispered up through the floorboards of the world.

Behind her, in the corner nearest the hearth, the old man coughed.

* * *

His name was Aldous Merrow, and he had been dying when they found him.

Casper had wanted to leave him. Wren had seen it in the guide's face—that swift, cold arithmetic smugglers did, weighing a stranger's weight against a stranger's risk—and Wren had done the only thing she knew how to do. She had knelt in the snow beside the collapsed figure on the track and taken his hand, and the mark on her wrist had flared so bright it lit the drifts blue-white, and Casper had sworn in three languages and said, *fine, fine, gods' teeth, help me lift him.*

Now he lay on the pallet by the fire, propped on sheepskins, a stick-thin man with a beard gone yellow at the edges and eyes the color of water left standing too long. He was a hedge-priest; he'd admitted it freely, which Wren learned later was either very brave or very foolish, because hedge-priests were what the witch-hunters burned first. He kept a little wooden antler on a cord around his neck, worn smooth as a riverstone, and he touched it constantly, the way other men touched charms against fever.

He had looked at Wren exactly once, when Casper hauled him inside, and then he had begun to weep without any noise at all, tears sliding down into his beard while he stared at her wrist.

"He knows you," Casper had said flatly, from the doorway, arms crossed. "Don't you."

"I know the mark," the old man had answered. "Not the child." His voice had been papery, full of holes. "The mark I have known my whole life the way a blind man knows a door. And it is *lit.* After all these years. Bram's mercy—it is *lit.*"

That had been yesterday. Today he was worse. The cough had settled deep in his chest like a stone dropped in a well, and each breath came with a wet rattle, and still he talked, because he said he had spent forty years keeping a secret that was killing the world and he would be damned—his words—if death took it out of him unspent.

Wren turned from the window and went to sit beside him. She didn't ask anymore if she could. He liked her near; she could feel that he liked it, the way she could feel the warmth of the mark, a faint pressure of *yes* at the edge of every thought. That frightened her more than the fire on the ridge did. She was nine—no, ten now, she'd decided, since nobody alive could tell her otherwise—and she had learned early that knowing things about people before they said them made mothers go quiet and neighbors find reasons to be elsewhere.

But the old man already knew everything there was to fear about her. There was nothing left to hide behind.

"Tell it again," she said. "The part about the kings."

Aldous smiled with most of his teeth. "You like that part."

"I like that somebody *did* it," she said. "I thought—" She stopped. She had thought, all her life, that the mark was a sickness, or a punishment, or a joke the world had played on one unlucky baby in a village of lucky ones. She had thought she was alone with it in a way no word covered. "I thought I was broken."

"You are a *half,*" the old man said, with terrible gentleness. "There's a difference. Broken things are finished. Halves are waiting."

He shifted against the sheepskins, wincing, and began again, in the cadence he'd worn smooth over two days of telling: the story Wren now carried around inside her chest like a second heartbeat.

Before the first kings, he said, before the crowns and the roads and the tithing-rolls, the mountains were Bram's. Not ruled by him—nothing so tidy as ruling. The god had simply been *in* everything the way water is in a sponge: in the thaw and the black ice, in the antler sheds and the wolf-tracks, in the silence between bells. The old peoples had needed no temples. You didn't build a house for someone who lived in your walls.

Then came the first kings, out of the lowlands with bronze and grain-law and a hunger for a world that could be counted. And Bram could not be counted. So they did not try. They wrote a covenant instead—not a treaty between equals, whatever the crown priests taught, but a cage drawn in oath-forms. They bound the god's waking self into a vessel of flesh and blood, a bearer, marked with the antlered sigil, generation after generation, so that the wildness of him would always wear a human face, answer to a human will, kneel to a human crown.

"But that wasn't enough," Wren recited, because she knew this part, and saying it made it realer than hearing it.

"That wasn't enough," Aldous agreed. "Because a bound thing strains, child, and a straining thing cracks its bonds. The covenant held only while it was whole. So the kings split it. One bearer to hold the god's waking strength—" he raised one trembling finger toward her wrist— "and one to hold the memory. The grief. The knowledge of what was lost and how it was lost. Two halves of one lock, set far apart, each incomplete without the other, so that neither could ever turn the key alone."

"And the memory-bearer," Wren said slowly, "wouldn't even know what she was carrying."

"Silence," Aldous said. "The memory-bearer hears the god as silence. A hole in the world shaped exactly like him. That's how the kings built it—a lock that guards itself. What kind of fool goes searching for the key to a door she doesn't know exists?"

Wren looked across the small room, to where Casper stood at the second window with her back to them, arms folded, watching the eastern glow the way she watched everything: as if measuring it for escape routes.

"She knows," Wren said quietly. "She's known all along. She just didn't know she knew."

* * *

That evening the wind changed and brought the smell straight to the hut, thick enough to taste, and Casper went out onto the shelf of rock above the door and stood looking east for a long time. When she came back in, her face had done the thing it did—gone smooth and closed, a shutter pulled down over whatever moved behind it—but her hands were shaking slightly, and she warmed them at the fire without meeting anyone's eyes.

"Corren's Ford," she said. "That'll be Corren's Ford burning. It's the only settlement big enough to see from here."

"Corren's Ford," Aldous repeated. Something in his voice folded inward. "The miller's wife there kept the spring rites. Quietly. As we all did. As everyone does who has ever felt the mountains breathe." He laughed, a small awful sound. "As if feeling the world breathe could be made a crime. They will burn the priest last, do you know? First the elders, then anyone who resists, then the priest—last, because he takes longest to break."

Wren's stomach clenched. "Why?"

"Because a frightened flock needs a shepherd's example," Aldous said, "and a dead god makes better law than a living one. Understand, child—the crown doesn't hate Bram. The crown *needs* him dead. Every king since the first has built his throne on the covenant. Every tax, every border, every rope and gallows—all of it rests on the claim that the old wildness is safely locked away, that order is natural and wilderness is crime. If the god wakes—" he spread his hands, and they trembled, and not only from the fever— "if the god wakes, then the world was never theirs to sell."

The fire popped. Outside, the wind worried the eaves.

"Then why," Wren asked, and her voice came out smaller than she meant, "does the crown hunt us? If we're the lock. Shouldn't they want to keep us safe?"

Aldous looked at her for a long moment, and the water-colored eyes were full of an old, tired sorrow.

"Because a lock is useful only while it stays shut," he said. "And because the kings learned, somewhere in those first generations, what I am about to tell you now, and it has frightened every crowned head since." He drew breath, and the rattle in his chest rose like water. "The covenant requires a death. To open the lock—to reforge the binding, renew it, make the cage strong again—one bearer must give her life to feed the other. The elder mark dies into the younger. That is the price written into the oath the kings swore in Bram's own name. And the reverse, child, if the bearers should ever stand in the Garden together and choose *otherwise*—"

He stopped. Coughed. Went gray around the mouth.

"Choose otherwise?" Wren whispered.

"The Garden," Aldous said. "Where it was sworn. Under Thornfell, under the roots, under the mountain's heart. If both bearers come to the Garden—if the waking half and the memory-half stand before the covenant-stone together—then the choice belongs to *them*, not the crown. Reforge the bond with a life given. Or shatter it entire, and let the god out of the world's cage forever—unbound, ungoverned, no longer anything any king can hold or use or blame." His hand found hers, cold and dry as paper. "Do you understand what I am telling you? You are not a curse, child. You are a *door.* And doors frighten the men who built the walls."

Across the room, very quietly, Casper said, "Stop."

Wren turned. Casper hadn't moved from the fireside, but her whole body had changed—coiled, braced, the way she'd looked in the ravine when the ice cracked under them.

"Stop telling her this," Casper said. "She's a child. She doesn't need to be a—"

"A door?" Aldous said mildly. "Neither do you, guide. Yet here we both are, standing in the draft."

The silence that followed had weight. Wren felt it press on her from all sides, and beneath it—fainter than breath, fainter than the fire's crackle—she felt something else. The mark on her wrist pulsed once, twice, like a heart remembering its rhythm. And for the first time it was not warmth alone. It was *direction.* A pull, gentle and immense, downward and northward, into the bones of the mountains.

*Aldous,* she almost said. *It's pointing. It knows where the Garden is.*

She didn't say it. Because at that same moment Casper's head lifted, sharp as a hound's, toward the western dark, and the old man's eyes went wide and fixed past Wren's shoulder, and the night outside filled with a sound Wren had heard described all her life in stories and never once believed she'd hear for herself:

Bells. Many bells. Coming up the valley.

"Witch-hunters don't ring bells," Casper breathed. "They're not trying to be quiet."

"No," Aldous whispered. His hand tightened on hers, and his voice was almost happy, almost laughing, as the rattle in his chest finally drowned him. "No—they're ringing because they've stopped hunting a smuggler and a child. They've realized what walks the mountains now." His eyes found Wren's and held them, bright and drowning. "*They've heard the door opening.*"
