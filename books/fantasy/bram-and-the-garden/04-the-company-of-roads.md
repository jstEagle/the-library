## 4. The Company of Roads

The Hollow Stair smelled of tallow and wet stone, as it always had, and Casper hated how much it smelled like home.

She had told herself the whole climb down from Thornfell's shoulder that she was not going back. That the network was a debt she had paid off years ago, in blood and silence, and that walking into Sella's den with a marked child on her hip was like carrying a lit lantern into a powder store. But Wren's cough had gone wet overnight, and the passes above were closing with the first real snow, and pride, Casper knew from long experience, was a luxury purchased by people who were not being hunted.

So she knocked the old pattern on the iron-bound door—three, pause, two—and stood in the cold while her heart argued with itself.

The door opened on Brannic, wide as a doorway himself, his beard crusted with frost. For one heartbeat he simply looked at her, and she watched ten years of careful forgetting fall off his face.

"Lockridge." He said it softly, the way you say the name of someone at a funeral. "Gods' teeth, girl. Get inside before the mountain sees you."

Inside, the Stair was dimmer than she remembered, or perhaps her memory had gilded it. The long table where smugglers had once argued over routes and rations now sat half-empty, its scarred wood holding only three men playing at dice they weren't watching. The maps on the walls had been taken down. That detail caught in her chest like a fishbone. Sella had always kept the maps up—the whole network lived on those maps, inked and re-inked over forty years of running cargo through the crown's blind spots. Bare walls meant fear. Fear meant someone had been asking questions.

Sella emerged from the back corridor wiping grease from her hands, small and silver-haired and sharp-eyed, moving with the same economy that had once seemed to Casper like the closest thing to magic the world contained. She stopped when she saw Wren bundled against Casper's chest, saw the sigil peeking above the child's collar before Casper could tug the wool closed.

Sella's face did something very small and very final.

"Casper." Not Lockridge. Never Lockridge, not from Sella; the surname was for strangers and soldiers. "You brought that into my house."

"I brought a child into your house. She's sick, she's nine, and she hasn't done anything except be born wrong according to the crown." Casper set Wren down by the hearth, keeping herself between the girl and the room. "Two nights' shelter. Hot food. Then we're gone south through the Undercut and you never saw us."

"And when the witch-hunters come asking?" One of the dice players had gone still. Another had slipped out the back door already, and Casper noted it with a coldness she didn't let reach her face. "You know what Vey pays for word. You know what happens to the families of anyone who harbors a bearer. I've got eleven people who sleep under this roof, Casper. Eleven. And you walk in with the one thing in these mountains worth more than all of them."

"You raised me," Casper said. It came out quieter than she intended, stripped of argument, just the bare fact laid on the table between them like a knife neither wanted to pick up. "I was thirteen. I had nothing and nobody and you taught me the passes. You fed me off this table. You told me the company of roads was worth more than any family blood ever bought."

Sella looked at the fire for a long moment. When she turned back, her eyes were dry and old.

"That was before the pyres started burning every month instead of every season. That was before Vey took Greywater Pass and posted bounty sheets in every market from here to the coast. Things change, child. People survive by changing with them."

"Then say it plainly," Casper said. "Say you won't help us. Say it to her face." She nodded at Wren, who was watching them both with the terrible attentiveness of children who have learned that adults' voices carry their futures in them. "Don't hide behind arithmetic."

For a moment—one breath, maybe two—Casper thought the old woman would break. Something moved behind Sella's eyes, some grief-shaped thing, and her hands curled at her sides as though reaching for a rope she'd already cut.

Then Sella said, "Brannic, get them food for the road. Blankets. The good ones." And to Casper: "One night. You're gone before light, and you go alone—I'll open no doors for you after this, and I'll deny your name if it's spoken under this roof again."

It wasn't help, exactly. But it was more than refusal, and Casper took it because Wren was shivering, and because some stubborn root of her still believed that Sella's arithmetic had an error in it somewhere—a remainder of love that wouldn't quite divide away.

* * *

They ate stewed barley and salt mutton by the hearth while Brannic fussed over them with the gruff tenderness of a man who had carried Casper on his shoulders across three flooded fords when she was small. Wren fell asleep mid-sentence, her bowl tilting, and Casper caught it and eased the girl down onto a pallet near the fire, pulling the good blanket to her chin.

"You should take her and go now," Brannic murmured, crouching to bank the coals. His voice was pitched under the room's noise. "While the watch is changing. Now, Casper."

"She said dawn."

"Sella said dawn because Sella wants you here at dawn." He wouldn't look at her. "A rider came up from the valley an hour ago. Crown colors. They're paying for word of two travelers, one grown, one small, bearing the old mark. Paying more than the network cleared last year." His jaw worked. "I don't know what Sella's decided. But I know what fear does to her sums."

The cold that went through Casper then had nothing to do with the draft under the door. She looked at Wren sleeping in firelight, at the sigil rising and falling with each small breath, and understood with a horrible clarity that she had known this ending since she'd walked in—that some part of her had counted the dice players, noted the missing maps, catalogued the exits, because ten years of staying alive had taught her that the people you loved were the ones most capable of burying you.

Still, she waited until the house settled. Still, she gave Sella until the middle watch, lying awake with Wren's hand curled in hers, listening to the mountain creak, telling herself that the old woman would come down the corridor and wake them herself, that loyalty was a muscle that only needed using.

At the third hour past midnight, she heard horses in the yard.

Not one horse. Six.

Casper moved without deciding to move. She had Wren up and wrapped before the girl finished waking, one hand sealed gently over the child's mouth, her lips at Wren's ear: "Quiet as the dead, little bird. Now." Through the crack of the storeroom shutter she saw them dismounting below—crown livery, grey cloaks, and leading them, tall in the torchlight with snow melting on her shoulders, Captain Ilsa Vey.

Ten years, and Vey still stood like a drawn blade.

Behind Casper, in the main room, she heard Sella's voice, steady and unhurried, offering wine. Offering words. Buying eleven lives with two names, and the worst of it—the part Casper knew she would carry longer than any wound—was that she could not even hate her cleanly. Because Sella was right about the arithmetic. Because Casper would have done the same, once, for the network. Because survival was a religion and Sella had only ever been devout.

She went out the drying-loft window into the falling snow, Wren strapped to her back, and did not look at the door she might have knocked on.

* * *

The Undercut was a smuggler's road, which meant it was barely a road at all—a shelf of stone cut along the throat of a gorge, half a league of footing narrow enough that a misstep dropped you sixty feet into black water. In summer it was treacherous. In winter, with ice glazing the rock and snow erasing the handholds, it was a coin flip whether the mountain let you keep your life.

Casper took it anyway, because behind her there were torches in the yard and hounds being loosed, and ahead of her there was only cold and distance, and she had always preferred her odds honest.

Halfway along, the mountain made its choice.

She heard it before she felt it—a deep groan under the wind, the sound of frozen scree letting go of itself—and then the shelf shuddered beneath her boots like the deck of a storm-tossed ship. Rock dust puffed around her knees. Somewhere above, a boulder the size of a cottage shrugged loose of its millennia and began to come down.

"Wren—" she shouted over her shoulder. "Run. Don't look, just run!"

They ran. The shelf disintegrated behind them in thunder, slabs the size of doors peeling away into the gorge, and Casper felt a whipcrack of pain across her left side as something spinning—stone, ice, a chunk of the world itself—caught her above the hip and flung her forward. She hit the rock face, tasted blood, kept moving, because stopping was dying and the far end of the shelf was twenty strides and then thirty and then ten and then—

—then they were through, stumbling onto the broad stable slope beyond, and behind them the Undercut poured itself into the gorge in a rolling white roar that erased the road, the hounds, the torches, everything.

Casper got them another quarter mile downslope on legs that barely answered her, until a leaning granite overhang offered shadow and shelter, and there she finally allowed herself to fold down against the stone with Wren beside her, gasping, shaking, alive.

Her side was bad. Not gutted, but bad—a long tear above the hip, welling dark in the moonlight, the flesh around it already going cold and stupid. She tore a strip from her shirt, packed the wound with snow until her vision swam, bound it tight with hands she refused to let tremble.

Wren was crying, silent tears freezing on her cheeks, and Casper pulled the girl against her good side without thinking about it, the way her body remembered things her mind tried to forget how to do.

"They sold us," Wren whispered.

"Yes."

"The people who fed us."

"One of them did. One of them warned me." Casper stared out at the snow falling into the gorge, at the buried road where ten years of belonging lay under fifty tons of fallen mountain. "That's the shape of things now, little bird. Nobody's all one thing anymore. Maybe nobody ever was."

"She was your mother. Sort of."

"Sort of." Casper's throat tightened around something hot and useless, and she swallowed it down where it belonged. Grief was a cargo like any other; you carried it; you didn't open it on the road. "And she chose her eleven over my one. I can't even swear she was wrong. That's the filthiest part."

Wren was quiet a long time. Then, in the small voice children use when they have understood something too large for their years: "Where do we go? There's nowhere left. Everyone sells."

Casper looked down at the sigil on the girl's wrist, faintly warm in the dark, faintly glowing, like an ember refusing the snow. And beneath her own ribs, in the place where Bram's silence had lived for ten years—a silence that had lately begun to feel less like death and more like something waiting—she felt, for the first time, a flicker. Faint. Fractured. Almost like a voice.

Almost like an answer.

"There's one place left," Casper said slowly, the words arriving from somewhere deeper than decision. "The hillfolk. The antlered ones. They keep rites the crown burned the churches to erase." She rose, gritting her teeth against the fire in her side, and pulled Wren up with her. "If any door in these mountains is still open to the marked, it's theirs. Come on, little bird. We've a long way to walk, and I think—" she pressed her hand flat against her sternum, frowning at whatever moved beneath it, "—I think something means to guide us."
