## 5. Ash and Antlers

They climbed for three days with the smoke of Hollowmere at their backs.

Casper measured distance in the old smuggler's way—by the ache in her boots, the sourness of the snowmelt they drank, the number of times Wren stumbled and caught herself before Casper could reach her. The girl was learning the mountains faster than she should have. There was something in her that read stone the way Casper read silence, some inheritance waking up under her skin, and it made Casper's teeth ache to watch it.

Behind them, somewhere below the cloud line, Captain Ilsa Vey was climbing too.

Casper knew it the way she knew weather coming. Vey did not lose people on trails. Vey lost people because she decided, coldly and correctly, that the trail would bring them back around—and then she waited where the mountain did her thinking for her. Ten years ago Casper had watched her do it to a bandit company in the Grey Saddle, starving them out of a canyon over nine days without spending a single arrow. *Patience is a weapon,* Ilsa used to say, sharpening her knife by the fire. *It's the only one that never rusts.*

So Casper did not take the passes. She took the places that were not passes at all—the goat-ribs of Thornfell, the shattered shoulders where the rock had come down a thousand years ago and no one had bothered to put it right. She hauled Wren up chimneys of scree with her hands bleeding inside her gloves, and when the girl cried in the night from the cold or the fear or whatever was burning under her mark, Casper lay awake listening and told herself it was only strategy, keeping the child alive until she could hand her off to someone better equipped for miracles.

She was lying. She had been lying since the ravine. The lie had gone thin as ice over moving water, and every night it creaked a little more.

On the fourth morning, Bram spoke.

Not to her. Not exactly. They had descended into a fold of the high country where the pines grew twisted and black-barked, and the air changed—thickened, went green-gold at the edges, like light through honey. Wren stopped so suddenly Casper nearly walked into her. The girl's marked palm was raised, fingers spread, and her whole body trembled like a struck bell.

"He's saying something," Wren whispered. "He's—he's trying to say my name."

Casper felt nothing. That was the shape of her life: everyone else got the voice, and she got the silence, the dead-weight quiet where a god should have been. Ten years of it. She had learned to call the silence a gift, the way you learn to call a scar a map.

But then—

*...ssst...*

It came not through her ears but through the soles of her feet, through the marrow of her, faint as a word spoken underwater. A sound like an antler dragging across stone. Like breath drawn through a broken reed.

*...the garden... the gar... den...*

The world tilted. Casper put her hand against a pine trunk and the bark bit her palm and still the voice came, threadbare and vast, a king's voice worn down to a beggar's whisper, and beneath the whisper she heard something that undid her worse than any shout could have:

It was pleading.

"Stop," she said aloud, and didn't know if she meant the god or herself. "Stop it. You don't get to—you don't get to *ask* me things."

Wren turned, eyes wide. "Casper?"

"Not now." Her own voice sounded wrong, scraped. "Keep walking. We're close. There are people ahead who can hide us, and after that—" She pushed off the tree and walked, fast, downhill, away from the sound. "After that we'll see."

But the voice followed her all day, thinner each hour, like a lamp running out of oil, and she could not stop listening. That was the cruelest part. Ten years she had starved that listening thing in her chest, and now it turned its face toward the sound like a plant toward sun, shameless, greedy, and Casper hated it, hated him, hated the imprisoned god who had let a twelve-year-old girl carry his waking half into witch-fires while he whispered *garden, garden* at the woman who'd spent a decade pretending she couldn't hear him scream.

By dusk they smelled woodsmoke—not the reek of burned villages but honest hearth-smoke, and under it something resinous and strange, like sap and juniper and old blood.

"The antlered folk," Casper said. "Stay close. Do what I do. And whatever you see tonight—don't name it."

* * *

The hillfolk found them before the firelight did.

They came out of the trees without sound, six figures wrapped in hides stitched with bone buttons, and their headdresses rose above their brows like crowns grown rather than carved—great branching antlers, some real and yellowed with age, some carved of rowan wood and hung with ribbons that had once been red. Their faces beneath the antlers were ordinary, lined, watchful. One of them was very old, and one of them was a child no older than Wren, and both looked at the two travelers with the same expression: recognition, and grief about it.

"You carry the split," said the old one. Her voice was dry as seed husks. "Both halves. Under one roof of sky." She looked at Wren first, then at Casper, and her gaze snagged on Casper's gloved left hand and stayed there like a bird on a wire. "The silent half. We wondered if there were any of your kind left."

"There aren't," Casper said. "I'm a guide. I'm paid to know trails, not gods. We need shelter from riders—a crown patrol, witch-hunters, maybe thirty armed. Two nights, and we're gone."

"They will not find you here," the old woman said simply. "This valley does not appear on maps. Maps are how kings think." She stepped aside, antlers dipping. "Come and eat. The god is thin tonight. He'll want witnesses."

"I don't want anything to do with him."

"No," the old woman agreed, not unkindly. "That is always how it begins with your kind. Come anyway."

The village sat in a bowl of the hills, a scatter of turf-roofed longhouses around a fire-pit wide as a threshing floor. As they came down into it, Casper saw the rites she'd only ever heard about in whispers at waystations—the antler poles planted at each door, ribboned and stained; the lintels cut with branching marks; a woman grinding something in a stone basin while humming a tune with no beginning and no end, a tune that seemed to go on being hummed whether or not her mouth moved.

Wren made a small sound. Casper glanced at her and saw the girl staring at the fire-pit, at the flames, which were burning green at their hearts. Green like the honey-light in the forest. Green like the color Casper had privately, secretly, over ten years of denial, come to think of as *his*.

"Sit," the old woman said, when they reached the fire. "Eat. Then listen. The listening ones among us say he has been calling for three days—calling down the mountain, following you like a dog follows blood."

"He doesn't follow me," Casper said. "He can't even finish a sentence."

"He cannot finish a sentence," the old woman repeated, and something in her tone made it a rebuke, gentle and terrible, "because the first kings took a hammer to him, child, and what is hammered does not speak whole. But a broken man drowning in a well still calls to whoever stands at the rim. It is not eloquence. It is thirst."

Casper sat. She ate the barley and venison they gave her and tasted none of it.

* * *

The rite began when the stars came out hard and close.

The hillfolk ringed the fire, and the drummers—three old men with drums painted in white spirals—began a rhythm that was less a beat than a heartbeat heard from inside another chest. The dancers wore the great carved antlers, and they moved slowly, turning, arms rising like branches in wind that wasn't there, and the green heart of the fire pulsed with them, brightening, dimming, brightening.

And into that pulse, Bram spoke—or tried to.

He came to the whole circle at once, Casper understood that much; every hillfolk face lifted at the same moment like flowers finding sun. But he came *hardest* to her. The voice poured through the drum-skin and the ground and her own ribs, and it was ruinous, a cathedral of a voice with its roof fallen in, words collapsing mid-shape:

*...bearer... my bearer of... of remembering... I kept... I kept the names, I kept them all, every name sworn to me, I kept yours—*

"Stop." She was on her feet. She didn't remember standing. The dancers turned and turned and did not stop, and the fire breathed green. "You didn't keep anything. You know what I got? Silence. Ten years of silence while your priests burned and your name became a crime. Where were you? Where were you when they came for the hedge-priests, for the hillfolk, for—for anyone stupid enough to love you?" Her voice cracked and she hated that most of all. "I hid. Do you understand? I hid so well I forgot what my own voice sounded like when it wasn't lying. And you want to tell me you *kept my name*?"

*...I could not reach... the binding... they bound my hands in the... in the...*

"In the Garden," Casper said bitterly. "Yes. I've heard. Everyone keeps telling me about this Garden like I should be grateful to be invited."

*...not grateful...* The voice guttered, dropped almost below hearing, and what remained was so small and so old that it went through her armor of resentment as if the armor were mist. *...sorry. Little bearer. So sorry. I heard every year of your hiding. I heard and could do nothing but keep your name safe where they cannot burn it. It was... it was the only thing my hands could still hold.*

The fire pulsed. The drums went on. And Casper stood in the ring of antlered dancers with tears cutting cold lines down her face, longing and fury knotted together so tightly in her chest that she could no longer tell which was which—wanting him, wanting the voice, wanting ten years given back, and hating him for needing her to be the one who climbed down into whatever dark held him, hating herself for already knowing she would go.

Wren's hand found hers in the dark. Small, fever-warm, marked.

"He sounds like he's sorry," the girl whispered.

"He sounds like everyone's sorry," Casper said. "Sorry doesn't unburn anyone."

But she did not pull her hand away.

Later, in the longhouse they were given, wrapped in wool that smelled of juniper, Casper lay awake while Wren slept, and the god's whisper came again through the packed earth beneath her, patient, threadbare, repeating the only whole sentence he seemed to have left:

*Come to the Garden. Come to the Garden. Come—*

And then, far down the valley, past the antler poles and the sleeping village, a sound that was not wind and not drum: the far-off barking of dogs, many dogs, moving upslope.

Casper was out from under the wool before the second bark faded, hand already on her knife, looking at Wren's sleeping face and understanding, with terrible clarity, exactly how few nights they had left.
