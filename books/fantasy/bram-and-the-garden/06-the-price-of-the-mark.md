## 6. The Price of the Mark

Wren had never seen a god's name spoken aloud before.

She sat cross-legged on the floor of the hillfolk longhouse, knees drawn up under Old Maren's too-big sweater, watching the antlered elders argue in their low rolling voices. The firepit smoked between them, and in its smoke—she was almost sure, though she kept checking and losing it—shapes moved that were not smoke. Antlers. Hands. A mouth opening on words no one else seemed to hear.

It was Bram they were arguing about. It had been Bram since the moment Casper pulled back her sleeve by firelight and showed the gathered elders her mark: the same broken circle-and-root that burned on Wren's own skin, except Casper's was pale as an old scar, cold to the touch, while Wren's still throbbed with warmth like something newly born.

"The elder bearer," Maren said, and her voice carried the flat weight of a stone dropped down a well. "The covenant names the order of it. The waking half cannot be loosed unless the memory-half is given. That is the price written into the binding itself. One bearer's life to unbind the other."

"But which one?" someone demanded from the shadows near the door. "Which is the memory?"

Maren turned her clouded eyes toward Casper, who stood at the edge of the firelight with her arms crossed and her face doing what it always did—which was nothing at all.

"Ten years of silence," Maren said. "The girl carries the living flame. The woman carries the ash." She spread her hands, gnarled and certain. "The covenant asks for the elder. It has always asked for the elder."

The longhouse went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when everyone has understood something and no one wants to be the first to say it plain.

Wren looked at Casper. Casper was looking at the fire, jaw set, and she did not look surprised. That was the worst part. She'd heard it before, then—or guessed it, or known it all along, the way she seemed to know everything about the mountains and nothing about how much Wren needed her not to know things like this.

"No," Wren said.

Heads turned. She hadn't meant to speak. Her voice came out smaller than the word deserved, so she said it again, louder. "No. That's wrong. You don't even *know* if it's true, you're guessing from a song—"

"The songs are older than your crown, child," Maren said gently. "Older than the kings who broke him. The songs remember what the covenant cost."

"Then the songs are wrong." Wren was on her feet now, hands balled, and she could feel her mark heating under her sleeve like a coal cupped in her palm, responding to her fear the way it always did, making her more visible exactly when she most wanted to disappear. "Casper found me. Casper carried me out of a ravine when I was already dead-cold, she gave me her coat, she—" Her throat closed. She made herself push through it. "She doesn't get to die for it."

For the first time all evening, Casper looked at her. There was something in her face Wren couldn't read—not pity, not gratitude. Something more tired than either.

"Nobody's dying tonight," Casper said. "Go to sleep, Wren."

*You first,* Wren almost said. But Casper had already turned away to speak with Maren in low tones, and the conversation closed around her like water over a stone, and Wren stood there in the smoky light feeling eleven years old and utterly unheard.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She lay on a pile of sheepskins near the wall and listened to the longhouse breathe—the elders' slow snoring, the wind worrying the thatch, somewhere far below them the thin continuous hiss of snow sliding off pines—and she thought about dying.

She thought about it more calmly than she expected to. That frightened her a little. She was eleven; she should have been terrified. But death had been walking beside her since the night her mother pressed her out a window and told her to run north and not stop running, and after a while you stopped flinching from every shadow on the road. You learned its gait. You learned it wasn't always coming for you.

But it was coming for Casper. Not for Wren. For the woman who had wrapped a freezing child in her only coat without hesitating, who fed her first from her own share, who taught her which berries lied and how to walk on scree without singing it loose, who never once asked Wren to explain the burning in her mark because—Wren understood this now, fully, finally—because Casper had lived ten years with the other half of it, alone, unasked, hunted, hiding a gift that must have felt like grief every single day of her life.

*One bearer's life to unbind the other.*

If it was Casper's life, then Wren would live free and wake a god and it would be built on the bones of the only person who had ever chosen her.

Unless.

There were hunters in the mountains. Vey's hunters. Wren had seen the fires of their camp two valleys over, small orange stars where no stars should be. And Captain Vey wanted the marked ones—that much even Wren knew, from the way Casper's whole body changed whenever the name was spoken, some door slamming shut behind her eyes.

But maybe the captain didn't want them dead. Maybe the crown wanted bearers alive. Prisoners were worth more than corpses; even Wren knew that from smuggler talk around waystation fires. And if Wren walked into their camp with her mark blazing and offered herself—offered both halves of the covenant, offered the thing the witch-hunters had been hunting across three provinces—

Then Casper wouldn't have to die. Then nobody would have to die, or at least only the person whose life had already been taken apart piece by piece since the window, since the run, since the beginning. A bargain. Grown-ups made bargains all the time. Sella had sold Casper for less.

Wren's heart was beating very fast and very hard, and her mark was hot enough now that she could see its glow through her sleeve, faint and gold-green like light through new leaves.

*I'm sorry,* she thought, not sure to whom—to Casper, to the god, to her mother somewhere south behind her, dead or imprisoned or simply gone. *I'm sorry. This is the only way I can love anybody without breaking them.*

She waited until the wind rose high against the thatch, masking sound. She took only what she could carry: the bread she'd hidden from supper, her boots, the little knife Casper had given her three days ago with the grudging instruction *don't cut yourself, don't cut anyone, we'll negotiate exceptions later.* She left everything Casper had given her that couldn't help her survive. She owed that much to honesty, at least: she was not stealing. She was paying.

At the longhouse door she paused. Casper lay nearest the exit—of course she did, she slept like a soldier even now, positioned between the child and the world—and in the banked firelight her face had gone young and undefended, the mercenary mask dissolved, and Wren memorized her like that. Stubborn. Scarred. Alive.

"Thank you," Wren whispered, knowing she'd never say it loud enough to be heard. "For finding me."

She slipped out into the dark.

* * *

The mountain at night was a different country.

Wren followed the ridgeline east, away from the hillfolk paths, following the orange star of the hunter-fires she'd memorized from the lookout. Snow came up past her boot-tops in places and she thought about the ravine, the ice closing over her like a second skin, and refused to think about it. Her mark stayed warm against the cold, a small stubborn hearth under her skin. *Keep burning,* she told it. *Burn so they can see me from very far away.*

Twice she heard things move in the trees and froze, hand on the little knife, and twice whatever it was moved on, uninterested in one small determined shape stumbling through moonshadow. Once she heard something else—a sound beneath the wind, deep and vast and cracked, like a bell rung underwater. Her mark flared and the sound resolved, just barely, into something almost like a word.

*...turn...*

"I can't," she whispered to it. "I'm sorry. This is the only way."

The sound came again, longer, sadder, and she covered her mark with her mittened hand as if she could muffle a god, and kept walking.

Dawn was a gray bruise in the east when she crested the last shoulder of the ridge and saw the camp below her in a bowl of frozen ground: six tents, picket lines, watchfires banked to embers, and figures moving among them in the dark gray uniforms of the crown's witch-hunters. Her stomach turned over. Her legs shook. Eleven years old, four days of hard travel in her body, facing down the machinery of a kingdom.

*Doesn't matter,* she told herself. *Casper's asleep right now. Casper wakes up safe. Everything else is fine.*

She took a breath that hurt going in, let her mark blaze bright through her torn sleeve—gold-green fire against the snow, impossible to miss—and stepped out onto open ground where any sentry could see her.

"Hey!" she shouted, her child's voice cracking across the frozen bowl, absurd and enormous. "HEY! I'm one of the marked! I'm what you're looking for!"

Tents rustled. Embers scattered upward. Figures rose all across the camp, and among them, near the largest fire, a tall woman turned—a commander's turn, economical, complete—and even across that distance Wren recognized the stillness in her. The name went ahead of her like cold air: *Vey.*

The captain started up the slope toward her alone, unhurried, one hand resting easy near her sword, and Wren stood her ground on the white ridge with her heart hammering and her mark burning and rehearsed her bargain under her breath—*take me, let the other go, take me, take me*—while behind her, miles back down the mountain, something began to scream.

Not a god. A person. A voice Wren knew better than her own heartbeat, raw with a fury and terror that needed no name, tearing down the slopes toward her through the snow.

Wren closed her eyes for one instant of pure, selfish despair.

*Casper.*
