## 7. Where Vey Stands

The child fought like something cornered, which was to say she fought like a Lockridge.

Captain Ilsa Vey had learned that particular ferocity years ago, on the training yards at Cael Marren, watching a sharp-elbowed recruit from the mountain provinces refuse to yield even after the bout was called—refuse to stop until her knuckles bled and her opponent walked away rather than face her again. She had loved that girl for it. She had promoted her for it. And then she had watched that same stubbornness carry Casper Lockridge out of the witch-hunters' ranks and into the wilds of Thornfell, carrying a secret that should have put her on a pyre.

Now the stubbornness was smaller, and younger, and half-frozen with terror in Vey's grip.

"Stop," Vey said quietly, and the word carried more weight than any shout. Two of her hunters had the girl pinned against the rockface of the ravine, torchlight guttering in the wind that funneled down from the high passes. The girl's hood had fallen back. The mark on her brow—the waking sigil, the one the crown's diviners described in whispers as if it might hear them—glowed faintly through the grime, like an ember refusing to die.

Wren. The name had come through the network like a spark down a fuse. Wren, maybe eleven or twelve, last seen fleeing the burning village of Hollowmere with an elder woman who hadn't survived the crossing.

Vey crouched so their eyes were level. Up close, the child smelled of woodsmoke and pine sap and fear-sweat. Her chin lifted anyway.

"You bit Dorn," Vey observed.

"He grabbed me."

"He did." Vey shot the hunter a look; he had the grace to look abashed. "You'll be treated fairly. Food, warmth, no chains unless you make me use them."

"I've heard what fair means," the girl said. "Fair means the pyre is lit quickly."

Something turned over in Vey's chest, slow and cold as river ice shifting in spring.

Behind her, the hunt waited: six riders, two hounds, the long shadows of Thornfell closing around them like fingers. Somewhere above, in the shattered passes where no sane rider followed, Casper Lockridge was watching. Vey knew it the way you know weather coming—by the ache in old scars, by the silence of the birds. Casper would not leave this child. That was the whole shape of her, always had been: a woman who hid herself in stone and shadow but could never quite hide her heart.

Which meant Vey had perhaps an hour before Casper did something reckless.

She intended to be ready for it.

* * *

They made camp in the mouth of an abandoned mine, three levels cut into the mountain's flank by miners who had dug too deep and fled whatever they found there. Vey posted sentries, fed the child stew she watched being salted—there were poisons subtler than herbs, and she trusted none of her own command completely—and then dismissed everyone but the girl.

Wren sat wrapped in a wool blanket, hands cupped around a tin mug, watching Vey with the flat, measuring stare of someone much older than her years.

"You're the captain," the girl said. "Casper talks about you."

"She does, does she?"

"When she drinks. When she thinks I'm asleep." Wren blew on her stew. "She says you were the best of them. That's why it hurt worse."

Vey kept her face still, though those words landed somewhere soft and unprotected. "And what else does she say?"

"That you burned a village once. That you stood there and watched."

The fire cracked. Outside, wind worried at the tarps over the mine entrance, a low moaning sound like something grieving.

"Hollowmere," Vey said. "That wasn't my order. I arrived after." She paused. "But I have burned villages. Before I commanded anything. When I was a lieutenant with more faith than sense." She looked into the fire rather than at the child. "You want me to tell you I'm sorry. I am. But sorry doesn't raise ash, and it won't save you, so instead I'm going to tell you the truth, because you deserve at least that much before whatever comes."

"What truth?"

"That I took this hunt so no one else would."

The girl went still. Even the mark on her brow seemed to quiet, its glow banking like a banked coal.

"There was a list," Vey said. "Forty-one names when the Gift Edict passed. Diviners' reports, neighbor's testimony, midwives' records. Forty-one people marked for questioning, and everyone in the capital knows what questioning means now." She turned the mug in her hands. "I requested command of the Thornfell operation specifically. My superiors believe it's because I know the mountains. Because I hunted smugglers here for six years. All true." A breath. "Also true: I lost a sister to the pyres, seven years ago. Merrin. She could coax plants to grow in dead soil. She never harmed anyone in her life. They said she communed with the old god. They made our father watch."

Wren's eyes had gone wide and wet. "You let them?"

"I tried not to. I filed petitions. I spent every favor I had. In the end I stood in the square with the rest, because if I hadn't, they'd have asked why, and then they'd have asked about me, and then—" Vey stopped. Her jaw ached; she realized she was clenching it. "Then there would have been two of us. It bought nothing. It saved no one. But I was twenty-four, and afraid, and I have been paying for that arithmetic ever since. So when they handed me a list of forty-one names, I took it. And I have been losing names off that list ever since—misfiled papers, wrong turns, hounds that lost scent in the rain. Eleven people are alive tonight because I am slow and unlucky. If the crown ever audits my campaign properly, I will hang. I've made peace with that."

The silence stretched. The child stared at her, and in that stare Vey saw something that frightened her more than any tribunal: hope. Bare, unguarded, the way only children and fools can manage it.

"Casper trusts you," Wren said finally. "Even now. Even angry. She says—" The girl's voice wavered. "She says you're the mirror she's most afraid of. Because you show her who she'd have become if she'd stayed."

"And what do you think I am?"

Wren set down her mug. "I think you're standing between me and the pyre right now. So I think you're trying."

* * *

Casper came an hour past midnight, as Vey had known she would.

No sentry saw her approach—which told Vey either that her sentries were compromised or that Lockridge remained the finest tracker the corps had ever failed to keep, and Vey suspected both. One moment the mine entrance was empty; the next, a figure stood in it, backlit by starlight, sword drawn but lowered, blood on her sleeve from some earlier encounter in the pass. Thinner than Vey remembered. A new scar along her jaw. Eyes that had once laughed across training yards now held the flat gray of mountain slate.

"Hello, Ilsa."

"Lockridge." Vey rose slowly, hands visible, away from her own blade. Around the camp, hunters stirred at their posts, and Vey lifted two fingers—the hold signal—and felt the tension bend without breaking. "You're bleeding."

"It's not mine." Casper's gaze flicked to Wren, who was sitting up in her blanket, eyes enormous. Something in Casper's face broke open for half a heartbeat—relief, fierce and naked—before the slate came back. "She's unharmed?"

"Fed and warm. You can verify yourself, if you come closer. I'm not going to take you, Casper. I want to talk."

"People who want to talk usually post fewer crossbows."

"People who wanted you dead wouldn't have posted sentries who can't see you coming." Vey stepped forward, out of the firelight's reach, lowering her voice. "Ten paces. Sword stays down on both sides. Agreed?"

A long moment. Then Casper crossed the gravel, ten paces exactly, stopping just beyond arm's reach—the distance of two women who knew precisely how fast the other could move. Up close, the years between them were a country Casper had crossed alone.

"You have her," Casper said. "Say what you came to say."

"The crown wants the child delivered to Cael Marren within the month. The escort will pass through Greywater Ford. There's a stretch of road there, two miles of gorge, where things go wrong. Wagons lose wheels. Prisoners escape. Captains miscount their charges." Vey held her gaze steadily. "I can arrange all of it. The girl walks free into the hillfolk territories, and no report of hers ever reaches the capital. In exchange—you surrender. Quietly. You go back to being a ghost. A different name, a different range; I'll see the paperwork buried myself. No pyres. No hunts with your name attached. You live hidden, the way you're good at, and the child lives free, the way she deserves."

Wren made a small sound—not protest, exactly. Fear. She understood the bargain's shape better than a child should.

"No," Casper said.

It came without hesitation, and Vey had braced for hesitation, had built the offer around the assumption that Lockridge's pragmatism would do what her heart couldn't. That was the error. She saw it land in Casper's expression—not surprise, but grief, as if Vey had confirmed something sad and long suspected.

"Casper—"

"You'd hand her to the hillfolk and call it freedom. Free to run forever. Free to spend her life flinching at torchlight." Casper's voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "You're offering her your life, Ilsa. Hidden, careful, slow and unlucky, saving eleven out of forty-one. I lived that life for ten years and I can tell you what it costs, because I paid it. You stand in a room like this one, with someone you loved, and you realize the hiding didn't keep you safe—it just kept you alone."

"The alternative is a pyre."

"The alternative is the Garden."

Vey stared at her. Of course. The old stories, the ones the Edict had been written to bury: the god beneath the mountain, the covenant split between bearers, the place where the first kings had chained their god and called it peace. "You actually believe it. You think you can unmake a prison older than the crown."

"I think the walls between realms are already failing, and when they go, they won't care whose orders anyone was following. I think Bram is waking whether the kings like it or not, and the only question left is whether he wakes caged or free." Casper's hand tightened on her sword hilt—not raising it, just holding on. "Step aside, Ilsa. Please. You of all people know what's waiting for her in Cael Marren. You've seen the pyres. You watched one eat your sister."

The words struck home with brutal accuracy, and Vey felt them go in, felt the old wound tear open and bleed fresh. For a moment she was twenty-four again in a burning square, doing arithmetic with a human life and calling it duty.

"Yes," Vey said quietly. "I did watch. And I swore I'd never watch another." She turned her head slightly, deliberately, toward the dark eastern wall of the camp—the direction of the latrine trench and the supply cache and the single blind spot in her perimeter that she had arranged that afternoon with meticulous care. "There's a gap in my line there. Sentries rotate in twenty minutes. The hounds are staked on the north side."

Casper went very still. "Ilsa."

"I'm not stepping aside. Understand that. If you fight through my line, my hunters defend themselves, and I can't call them off mid-battle without questions I can't answer." Her voice was steady; her heart was not. "But a blind spot is just weather, Casper. Weather happens. Mountains happen. And afterward, I'll write my report: prisoner escaped under cover of storm, pursuit impossible, trail lost in the high passes. Again." A ghost of a smile, bitter as ash. "Twelve names lost now. Maybe thirteen, counting you."

"Why?" It came out of Casper almost involuntarily, raw. "After everything—why?"

Vey looked past her, at the child wrapped in a blanket with an ember glowing on her brow, at the future burning in miniature.

"Because someone once chose the pyre over defiance, and it saved nothing," she said. "I won't do the arithmetic twice. Go."

For a heartbeat neither moved—two women on opposite sides of a line they had both already crossed. Then Casper nodded once, the smallest bow, soldier to soldier, and turned to Wren. Words passed between them too low to catch. The girl threw her arms around Casper, and Casper held her the way you hold the thing you'd burn the world for, and then they were moving—two shapes dissolving into the dark toward the blind spot Vey had built for them.

Vey stood alone by the fire and listened to the mountain swallow them.

Twenty minutes later, the alarm went up. Torches bloomed along the ridge. Dorn came running with his crossbow and his questions, and Captain Ilsa Vey gave him her coldest voice and her cleanest lies: storm coming down from the saddle, trail washed out, no pursuit possible before dawn.

By dawn it was done. By dawn, Ilsa Vey had committed treason in ink and intention both, and there was no path left but forward. She stood at the mine's mouth watching the sun rise over Thornfell, and she thought of Merrin in the green places of the world, wherever souls went when the gods were sleeping.

*Hold on,* she thought, to a sister seven years gone and a god seven centuries bound. *We're coming.*

Far below the mountain's roots, something ancient shifted in its sleep—and the first crack ran up through the stone, fine as a hair, singing under Vey's boots like a plucked string.
