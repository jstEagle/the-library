## 8. Descent into the Garden

The entrance was a crack in the mountain's throat, no wider than a coffin, and Bram's voice led them to it like a thread pulled through dark cloth.

*Down,* the voice said. It came from everywhere and nowhere, from the stone itself and the marrow of Casper's bones, and it was thinner than it had been among the hillfolk—thinner every hour, as if each step toward its source spent something that could not be recovered. *Down, bearer. Down where they planted me.*

"You could have said something like this ten years ago," Casper muttered, shouldering her pack. The sigil on her wrist ached with a cold, deep pain, like frostbite of the soul.

"He's dying," Wren whispered. The girl had not stopped shivering since the pass, though Casper suspected it had little to do with cold anymore. "Can't you feel it? He's like—" She searched for it, hands opening and closing around air. "Like a lamp running out of oil."

"I can feel what I've always felt." Casper tested the crack with one hand, found it breathing—a slow exhalation of earth-smell and old green rot—and began to widen it with her knife. "Nothing. Silence. That was my half of him, remember? The memory. You got the living part."

"That's not fair."

"No," Casper agreed grimly, prying loose a slab of granite that groaned like a waking animal. "Not much about this is." She wedged the slab aside and looked into the dark beyond. It was not empty dark. It was *occupied* dark, the kind that has weight and history, the kind that watches you back. "Stay behind me. Touch the left wall if we get separated—the roots will guide you. And Wren—"

"I know. If you say run, I run."

"If I say run, you run *toward daylight* and don't look back for me." Casper met the girl's eyes, pale in the gloom. "I mean it. I've been someone's reason to die before. Never again."

Wren said nothing, but her hand found the hem of Casper's coat and held it, and Casper let her.

They went down.

* * *

The roots of Thornfell were older than any tree above. They filled the passages like the ribs of some buried leviathan, thick as towers, pale and smooth where centuries of darkness had stripped their bark. Fungus glowed along them in veins of blue-white light, enough to walk by, not enough to see far. The air tasted of loam and rain that had fallen before kings.

And the walls were thin here. Casper felt it at once—a prickling along her arms, the old smuggler's instinct for danger translated into something vaster. The boundary between realms, the hedge-priest had called it. Bram's fading was unraveling it, and here beneath the mountain the weave had worn through entirely in places, and things bled through.

Visions.

The first came without warning: a woman's face swimming out of the root-wood, gray-skinned, mouth open in a scream that made no sound. Casper knew her. Marta, from the village of Hollowmere, three years ago—the witch-hunters had burned the granary with the accused inside, and Casper had stood in the crowd with her hood up and her gift locked behind her teeth, and done nothing.

"Marta," she breathed, and reached out, and her fingers passed through the vision like smoke.

"She's not real," Wren said softly. "Or—not anymore."

"She's real enough." Another face surfaced beside the first. A boy this time. Tam, who had guided Casper's first solo run at seventeen and frozen to death in a whiteout because she'd misread the weather signs. Tam, whose silence she had carried for eleven years like a stone sewn into her chest. "This is what I did to myself. This is what staying hidden cost."

"Everyone fails people," Wren said, with the terrible simplicity of a child. "My mama used to say the road doesn't punish you for what you carry, only for refusing to set it down."

"Your mother sounds wiser than me."

"She died anyway." Wren's voice cracked. "The hunters came and I ran and I didn't go back for her, and I tell myself she'd have wanted me to live, but the walls here—" She pressed her small hand against a root, and her eyes went wide and wet. "The walls are showing me her too."

Casper took the girl's shoulder and turned her away from it. "Then we keep moving. Grief is a country, Wren. You can visit. But you can't build a house there."

She said it as if she believed it. The visions walked beside them anyway, patient as pilgrims: every face Casper had failed to save, every warning swallowed, every night she had heard Bram's dead silence pressing against the inside of her skull and chosen, again and again, to pretend it was nothing. A decade of survival built on staying unseen—and now the unseen had all come down into the dark with her, crowding close, wanting acknowledgment.

Halfway down, her own face appeared in the roots.

Younger. Harder. Standing at a waystation window at dawn, watching a pyre burn on a distant hillside, doing the arithmetic of her own safety and finding the sum acceptable. The memory-Casper turned in the vision and looked directly out at her, and Casper stopped walking.

"There she is," the vision said, in her own voice. "The clever one. The survivor. Tell me, Casper—what did you survive *for*?"

"To live," Casper said aloud, and hated how thin it sounded in the dark.

"You hid. That's not living, that's postponement. Ten years of postponed life. And now you're walking down to give it back to him, is that it? Walk into the Garden and pour yourself out for the god who never once spoke up for you?"

"That's not the choice."

"Isn't it?" Her own face smiled, and it was cruel because it was true. "You've starved me, Casper. The memory-half, the feeling-half, whatever the priest called it. You cut me off so long I nearly died. And now you want to make a grand sacrifice and call it faith. But you don't even know how to *want* things anymore. Do you? When did you last want something just because you wanted it?"

Casper's hand closed over the sigil on her wrist. "Shut up."

"When did you last—"

"*Shut up.*"

Silence fell—but not Bram's silence, which had always been a presence, a held breath, an absence shaped exactly like a god. This was ordinary silence, mountain silence, and into it Wren slipped her cold hand into Casper's.

"I want things," Wren offered quietly. "Right now I want soup. And to not be hunted. And for you to be okay, even though you're obviously not."

A laugh broke out of Casper, rough and unexpected, echoing down the root-halls. "Soup."

"It's a good want."

"It is." Casper squeezed the small hand and started walking again. Behind them, her younger self faded back into the wood, unappeased but unanswered. Maybe that was the whole trick of it, she thought. Not defeating the failures. Just declining to let them do your talking.

* * *

Near the bottom, Bram's voice failed altogether.

It didn't fade—it *broke*, mid-word, like a bell struck too hard, and in the ringing aftermath Casper felt the mark on her wrist flare white-hot and then go utterly, terribly quiet. Quiet the way a room is quiet when someone in it has just stopped breathing.

"Bram?" she called. "Bram!"

Nothing. Wren clutched her arm, and through the girl's skin Casper felt the other half of the mark burning—Wren's living fire guttering, dimming, the flame of a candle cupped in a shaking hand.

"He's still there," Wren whispered. "I can feel him. But he's so small now. Like—he's holding himself together with both hands and he's almost out of hands."

Ahead, the passage ended.

They stepped out of the roots into a cavern so vast that the glow-fungus could not reach its ceiling, and the floor was not stone. Grass grew there—real grass, silver-green and luminous, soft underfoot after miles of root and rock. Fruit trees stood in rows gone wild and tangled, heavy with glowing fruit no one had picked in a thousand years. A stream murmured somewhere, singing in a language older than words. The air was warm. It smelled like summer, like childhood, like every garden Casper had ever been driven away from.

At the center, half-swallowed by a great white root that had grown around and through and over it, stood a door. Not a building—a single freestanding door, ancient oak bound in iron, standing upright in the grass with nothing attached to it at all.

The first kings' work. Bind a god behind a door, plant a garden over his grave, and call it peace.

"The covenant was sworn there," Casper said. Her voice came out hushed. "That's where they made the bargain. Two bearers. One mark split in two. One life given to keep the lock turning."

Wren had gone very still. In the strange light her face looked carved, older than her years. "I can hear him," she said. "Behind the door. Casper—I can hear him *breathing*."

Casper listened. And for the first time in ten years, the silence that had lived in her head since the day the priests marked her—Bram's silence, her curse and her hiding place—was gone. In its place, faint as a pulse through a wall, she heard it too.

Breathing. Slow. Labored. Fading.

And beneath it, so quiet she might have invented it, a voice—not words yet, barely a shape:

*Bearers. At last. Come quickly.*

*I cannot hold the door much longer from either side.*

The door shuddered on its hinge-less frame, and from the crack beneath it, thin as a blade, spilled a light that was not fungus-glow and not sunlight—something older, greener, hungrier. The grass around the door browned and regreened in waves, like a heartbeat.

Wren stepped forward. Casper caught her arm.

"Not yet," she said. "We don't know what he'll ask."

"We know what it costs," Wren whispered, and her eyes when they met Casper's were full of a fierce, terrible love. "One of us, Casper. That's why we're here. That's what it's been building to since the ravine."

The door shuddered again. And through the widening crack beneath it, Casper saw something move—something vast and antlered and alive, pressing against the threshold of its thousand-year prison, waiting to learn what its bearers had decided.
