## 10. A Name of Her Own

The mountains did not stop shaking for three days.

Casper felt it in her teeth, in the soles of her boots, in the deep stone hum that had once been Bram's silence and was now something else entirely—a vast, restless attention moving beneath Thornfell like a stag waking in a stall too small for it. The old paths had changed. Passes she had guided smugglers through for ten years now ended in walls of tumbled rock, and others had opened where no maps admitted a way, breathing warm air that smelled of loam and green growing things.

The crown's writ had changed with the stone.

Word came up the mountain the way word always did—on the backs of peddlers, in the mutters of waystation keepers, in the frightened letters that reached even the hillfolk's high pastures. In Corvane, the capital, the great bell of the king's chapel had cracked from crown to lip on the night Bram rose, and no smith could explain it. In the lowland towns, witch-hunters' pyres stood cold and unlit. The edicts that had governed the gift for three hundred years—*the old god is dead; those who hear him are corrupted; corruption is cleansed by fire*—had been read aloud in market squares and then, quietly, withdrawn. Because the god was not dead. Because the god had stood up out of the earth in front of a thousand witnesses, antlers against the moon, and the king's own priests had fallen on their faces in the mud.

Lies did not survive that kind of daylight.

* * *

Ilsa Vey came up the mountain in the fourth week, alone, without her colors.

Casper saw her from the ridge above the hillfolk's steading, a single figure on the switchback trail, walking with the deliberate evenness of someone who had decided not to run and not to hide. Casper's hand went to her knife out of ten years of habit, and then, slowly, dropped.

"You came without soldiers," Casper said when Vey reached her.

"I came without anything." Vey stopped an arm's length away. Up close she looked hollowed, the way stone looks after a fire—still standing, still itself, but changed in its grain. Her commander's braid was undone. Her sword was not on her hip. "I gave my testimony in Corvane. All of it. The forged orders, the quotas, the pyres that were lit for numbers rather than guilt. My sister's name is in the record now, with the truth beside it." Her jaw worked. "It won't bring her back. It won't bring any of them back. But the hunt is dissolved. Formally. And I am—" she exhaled, "—retired. Disgraced, probably. I find I don't mind."

Casper said nothing. There was a decade of things to say and none of them would fit through her throat.

"I owe you an apology," Vey said, "and more than an apology, and neither of them is enough, so I'll not insult you by pretending they are. I'll say only this: when they ordered me to take you, ten years ago, I told myself I was protecting you by refusing the assignment. Letting you vanish. I told myself that for ten years." She looked out at the mountains, at the new green haze on the lower slopes where nothing had grown green in living memory. "He was never silent, was he. You heard him the whole time."

"He was imprisoned," Casper said. "I heard the walls."

Vey nodded slowly, as if this confirmed something she had already decided to believe. "The child. Wren."

"She's well. She's with the hillfolk. She's—" Casper searched for the word and found it unexpectedly easy. "She's happy. She teaches the little ones the rites. She's terrible at the antler dance."

Something moved through Vey's face—grief and relief tangled together, the expression of a woman setting down a weight she had carried so long she had forgotten it was not part of her body. "Good," she said. "Good." She turned to go, then stopped. "Casper. The record in Corvane has your name in it now. Not your mark. Your *name*. Casper Lockridge, guide, witness, bearer. If you ever come down out of these mountains, no one will touch you." A pause. "You belong to the world again. If you want it."

Casper watched her go down the switchbacks, smaller and smaller, until the trail took her, and found that she felt—nothing sharp. No triumph. No forgiveness yet, either, but the ground where forgiveness might someday grow, turned and waiting.

* * *

Spring came to Thornfell like a rumor that would not stop spreading.

The hillfolk said the god walked the high places at night, and whether it was true or only the kind of truth that does not need to be literal, Casper saw the evidence of him everywhere: streams running clear where they had run brown, elk herds coming down to the steading unafraid, the old forbidden cairns on the ridgelines blooming with white flowers no one had planted. Bram did not speak to her the way he once had—there was no more silence to hear, no more imprisonment to translate. Instead there were impressions, vast and wordless, like weather. The feeling of being *noticed* by something enormous and benign. The sense of antlers at the edge of vision, always just turning away.

He did not ask for anything. That was the strangest part. He had been freed from a covenant that demanded worship, sacrifice, obedience, and he had chosen—apparently—to simply *be*, wild and unbound and grateful, a god with no church and no demands. Some nights Casper lay awake and felt him pass over the steading like a wind and thought: *he could have been this all along. They could have simply let him be this.*

The mark was gone from her wrist. It had faded in the days after the Breaking, the sigil dissolving like ink in water, and where it had been the skin was ordinary—scarred, weathered, a guide's hand, a smuggler's hand. *Her* hand. She caught herself looking at it sometimes, expecting to miss it, and finding instead that its absence felt like setting down a pack after a very long climb. The weight had been familiar. Familiar was not the same as good.

* * *

She planted the seed on a morning in late spring, on the south-facing slope above the steading, where the snow melted first and the ground stayed soft.

The seed had come out of the Garden with her, tucked without her knowledge into the lining of her coat—she had found it weeks later, a small dark thing like an acorn crossed with a star, still faintly warm. Wren had helped her choose the spot, pacing out the ground with the seriousness of a child founding a nation.

"Here," Wren had declared. "It gets the morning sun. And you can see the whole valley. And it's not too close to the byre because the byre *smells*."

"It's a shrine," said Marta of the hillfolk, who had come to watch, leaning on her antlered staff. "Ground where a god's seed is planted is holy ground. There will be rites. There will be offerings. There will be a wall, eventually, and a keeper, and—"

"It's a garden," Casper said.

She knelt and worked the soil with her hands, the way she had done nothing in ten years of guiding and smuggling, the way her mother had done before the crown's edicts reached even their valley. The earth was cold and alive. She made a small hole, and set the seed in it, and covered it, and pressed the ground down with her palms, and sat back on her heels.

Nothing dramatic happened. No light, no trembling. The seed did what seeds do, which is nothing, which is everything, which is wait.

"A shrine," Marta said again, but gently now, testing.

"Maybe," Casper allowed. "Someday. If it wants to be. But it doesn't get to be a wall first. It doesn't get to be a *rule* first." She looked up at the old woman. "That's the whole lesson, isn't it? The first kings loved him too, in their way. And they loved him into a cage, because love that needs to be *certain* always builds a cage. So." She brushed the dirt from her hands. "No walls. No keepers. No rites unless someone feels like them. If he wants a shrine, he can grow one. If he just wants a garden—" she shrugged, "—then it's a garden, and people can sit in it, and nothing is owed."

Marta was quiet a long moment. Then she laughed, a dry bark like a fox, and tapped her staff twice on the earth in the old blessing-gesture. "A god with no obligations," she said. "The first kings would have died of it."

"The first kings," Casper said, "did die of it. That's rather the point."

* * *

Wren found her there at dusk, when the valley had gone blue and the first stars were out over Thornfell's teeth. The girl was fourteen now by Casper's best guess, though she had stopped counting; she was taller, brown from the summer, and she walked like someone who had decided the ground would hold her. She dropped down onto the grass beside Casper without asking, the way family does.

"Vey's gone, then."

"Gone. Testified. Disgraced. Free." Casper turned a blade of grass between her fingers. "She said I could come down the mountain. Said my name's clean now. Said I *belong* to the world again."

Wren was quiet. Then, with the blunt precision that was entirely her: "You always belonged to the world. You just thought belonging was something they gave you. The mark, the guild, the roads—those were all things other people handed you and could take back." She picked up a pebble and tossed it down the slope. "This is yours, though. You planted it. Nobody gave it to you."

Casper looked at the child—no, the girl, nearly grown now, the girl she had pulled half-frozen out of a ravine because every rule of survival she owned had lost to something older and simpler—and felt the truth of it settle into her like the seed settling into soil. Belonging claimed, not granted. A name of her own, not a god's mark, not a smuggler's alias, not a fugitive's shadow. *Casper Lockridge.* It sounded, for the first time in ten years, like a name someone could say in daylight.

"Wren," she said.

"Mm?"

"Come here. I mean—stay here. I mean—" She laughed at herself, the guide who could find a path through any storm in the Thornfell and could not build a sentence. "I chose you in a ravine because I couldn't help it. I'd like to choose you again, on purpose. If you'd choose me back. Not because of the mark. Not because of the covenant. There's no covenant anymore. Just—" she gestured at the planted ground, the darkening valley, the vast benign attention of an unbound god moving somewhere in the high places, "—just this. Us. Freely."

Wren looked at her for a long moment with those eyes that had once burned with a living half of a broken god, and Casper saw the tears come and saw the girl let them, unashamed, the way the hillfolk taught their children to let things.

"Obviously," Wren said, and leaned her head against Casper's shoulder. "Obviously, yes."

They sat together as the dark came on. Somewhere above them, in the black spine of the mountains, something enormous and antlered and free moved through the night, asking nothing, owing nothing, simply *there*—and the seed in its warm bed of earth waited, as seeds do, for the morning.

And far to the south, past the passes Bram had opened, a road ran down out of the mountains toward the lowlands—toward towns that had never seen the old rites, toward people who had only ever heard Bram's name as a warning. Casper watched that road glimmer faintly under the stars and thought: *someone should tell them. Someone should walk down and tell them he was never dead, and never silent, and never theirs to cage.*

She was, after all, a guide.
