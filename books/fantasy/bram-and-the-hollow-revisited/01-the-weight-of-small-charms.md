## 1. The Weight of Small Charms

There is a particular art to being small, and I have mastered it the way other people master swordplay or statecraft. Seven years of practice. Seven years of selling luck-knots in Corvane's night market, three coppers apiece or two for five, and never once letting my voice do anything more dangerous than hum.

The knots themselves are honest work. Red thread, mostly, braided in patterns my grandmother taught me before I understood what her hands were really doing. A proper luck-knot holds a whisper of intention—a wish for safe roads, for full bellies, for the fever to break—and carries it against the wearer's skin until the thread frays. That much is legal. The Magistrate's Office sells its own version at twice the price, machine-braided and blessed by a priest who has never felt so much as a shiver of the gift. Mine work better. That is the whole of my crime, if you don't count the rest.

I set up my stall between a pie-seller whose crusts could sole a boot and a woman who claimed to tell fortunes but only knew how to say what lonely people wanted to hear. The lanterns came on one by one along Gull Street, and the market exhaled into its evening shape: dockhands spending wages they'd regret by morning, lovers pretending the cold was an excuse to stand close, children darting between stalls on errands that were mostly games.

"Knots for luck," I called, in the voice I'd built for it—light, a little rough, forgettable. "Safe roads, sound sleep, sweethearts true."

A girl stopped at my stall. She was maybe ten, all elbows and watchfulness, with a scarf wound up to her chin and eyes the color of river ice. She didn't look at the knots. She looked at my hands.

"You braid wrong," she said.

"Do I?"

"For the ones that actually work." She said it quietly, the way you'd mention a shared secret to a stranger you weren't sure of yet. "My gran did it too. Before they took her."

I kept my face pleasant and stupid, the face of a man who sold string. "These are just knots, little miss. Pretty ones."

She held my gaze a moment longer than was comfortable, then bought a knot with a coin she'd clearly earned the hard way, and left. I watched her go and told myself the familiar lie: *she doesn't know. Nobody knows. You are small, and small things survive.*

Her name, I would learn too late, was Wren.

* * *

The raid came at moonrise, which was early for them. Crown hunters worked Corvane's night market perhaps once a season, sweeping through like weather—lanterns doused, stalls overturned, everyone made to stand in rows while a hunter walked the line with a lodestone charm and waited for it to twitch. Most nights it found nothing worth finding. The gifted had learned to be small. Smallness was a civic virtue now; they practically taught it in schools.

I heard them before I saw them—the particular rhythm of boots that belong to men who enjoy their work. Six of them, grey-cloaked, moving up Gull Street with the unhurried confidence of people who answer to no one. The market went quiet the way water goes still before it freezes.

"Lines," said the tall one at the front. He had a voice like a door dragging over stone, and a badge I recognized from seven years ago and had hoped never to see again: the crowned eye of the Office of Hollow Affairs. "Everyone stands. Everyone stays quiet. We'll be quick."

We stood. We stayed quiet. The lodestone went down the row, twitching harmlessly past the fortune-teller, past the pie-man, past me—I had woven a dampening stitch into my own sleeve years ago, a cantrip so minor it barely qualified as magic, more habit than spell—

And then Wren screamed.

Not a scream of fear. I know those. This was something else—a child's voice cracking open with power it didn't understand, raw swordspeech, the old tongue that lives in the blood and not the throat, and every gifted thing within a hundred paces *felt* it. My dampening stitch burned off my wrist like spider silk in a candle flame. The lodestone in the hunter's hand leapt so hard it nearly left his grip.

He turned. Not toward Wren first.

Toward me.

Because here is what I have never told anyone who wasn't about to die: swordspeech answers swordspeech. When her cry rang out, mine rose to meet it—seven years of silence breaking in half a heartbeat, the old voice pouring out of me unbidden, a wordless answering note that said *here, little one, here, you're not alone.* Instinct. Protection. The one reflex I had never managed to kill.

The hunter's eyes went wide. Then narrow. "Well," he said, in that stone-door voice, and smiled like a crack in a wall. "Found a big one."

Wren stood frozen at the mouth of the alley, her small fists still smoking with spent magic, watching me with the terrible clarity children have. She hadn't meant to call out. She'd been caught lifting bread, probably, or cornered by one of the hunters' questions, and her gift had answered for her the way mine had answered for hers. Two voices calling across a crowded market, and both of us marked now, branded by sound itself.

"Take the girl too," the tall hunter said. "Witnesses say she cried out first. Witch-blood runs in litters."

Two hunters moved toward her. And I want to tell you I weighed it. I want to tell you there was a moment where I counted the cost, measured seven careful years against one reckless act, and chose with a clear mind.

There wasn't. There was just Wren's face, and my body moving before my cowardice could catch up.

I spoke.

Not words—not exactly. Swordspeech isn't language the way you're reading is language; it's command given weight, intention given edge. I spoke the oldest word I knew, the one my grandmother used to fell trees when we needed firewood, and the market's central lantern-pole cracked at its base and toppled across the street in a crash of sparks and splintered wood, scattering the hunters, plunging everything into lantern-oil darkness and screaming.

"Run," I said to Wren, in plain speech, in the small voice, because even now some part of me was trying to be quiet. "Alley. Now."

She ran. I ran. Behind us the hunters regrouped fast—too fast, these were professionals—and someone blew a whistle that cut through the chaos like a knife. Three notes, rising. I didn't know the signal, but I knew what signals were for.

Calling others.

* * *

We ran through Corvane's bones—the fish-market gutters, the undercrofts beneath the tannery row, places I knew because knowing escape routes is the other half of being small. Wren kept pace without complaint, which frightened me more than the whistles did. Children should complain. Children should be terrified. This one ran like someone who had already learned the lesson I'd taken seven years to teach myself: that nobody was coming to save her.

We surfaced near the salt marshes east of the city walls, where the drainage channels met the tidal flats and the mud swallowed sound. I pulled her behind a rotted pilings-hut and finally let myself breathe.

"They'll bring dogs," she said. Her voice was steady. Her hands were not. "They brought dogs for my gran."

"I know."

"You're the one from the stories." She looked at me with those river-ice eyes, and there was no awe in it, only assessment. "The last swordspeaker. They say the crown killed you years ago."

"The crown believes what's convenient." I wrapped her scraped knuckles with a strip of my sleeve, working quickly, not meeting her eyes. "Listen to me. You go back to the city, you find a hole, you stay small, and in a season this will—"

"It won't." Flat. Certain. "They marked me. You saw. Once they mark you, they keep coming. Gran said so." She swallowed. "Where are you going?"

I opened my mouth to say *away*, to say *alone*, to say any of the comfortable lies I'd built my life out of.

Across the flats, torchlight bloomed. Not two torches. Not six. A dozen, fanning out from the marsh gate in a practiced spread, and ahead of them a figure moving faster than the rest—grey-cloaked, crowned-eye badge catching the light, a huntress with a crossbow slung across her back who had, I realized with a cold drop in my stomach, been standing at the edge of the market all evening.

Watching me. Watching Wren. Watching everything.

"She's coming," Wren whispered.

The huntress raised one hand, and the torches behind her stopped. She came on alone, boots squelching in the mud, and stopped ten paces away with the easy balance of someone who had killed tired, frightened people before and expected to again.

"Ivo Underhill," she said. "You're going to come with me. Both of you. And you're going to tell me why the Office sealed my sister's file the week they sent her to the Hollow."

I looked at Wren. Wren looked at me.

Smallness, I thought, had just gotten very expensive.
