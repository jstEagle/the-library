## 2. What the Huntress Carried

*Ivo*

The huntress caught up with us at the edge of the salt marshes, which surprised me less than it should have. Seven years of selling luck-knots teaches you a thing or two about inevitability. You learn that luck is a currency, and someone always holds more of it than you.

What did surprise me was that she didn't bind my wrists.

She stood there in the grey pre-dawn with her irons in one hand and her writ of arrest in the other—both of them dripping marsh water, because apparently she'd waded through the fen rather than take the causeway road like a sensible person—and she looked at me the way you look at a ledger whose sums won't balance.

"You're Underhill," she said.

"Depends who's asking." It was a stupid answer. She had a crossbow. I had a satchel of knots that did nothing but look cheerful.

"Maren Voss," she said, as though the name should mean something to me. "Third Cohort of the Crown's Hunt. And you're going to come with me quietly, or I'm going to drag you."

I considered my options. Running: bad legs, worse ground. Fighting: I hadn't spoken a sword-word in seven years, and even if I had, I'd have sooner bitten off my own tongue. Begging: I'd watched enough people beg in Corvane's night market when the hunters came through. It never worked, and it always cost something you couldn't get back.

So I said, "The child. Wren. Where is she?"

Something moved behind the huntress's eyes—a shutter closing, or maybe opening. I still can't say which.

"That's the question, isn't it," she said quietly. "That's the whole rotten question."

* * *

*Maren*

Ivo Underhill looked smaller than the reports claimed. The reports said swordspeaker, said oath-bound, said *burned Harrow Fen to the ground*, and the man—I couldn't tell if it was a man or a woman, and after three days in their company I still couldn't, and eventually stopped caring—in front of me looked like someone who hadn't slept a full night since before the last king died. Which, to be fair, described half of Corvane.

I had orders. I always had orders; that was the architecture of my whole life, order stacked on order like stones in a wall. Bring in the swordspeaker. Bring in the child. Deliver both to the Hollow for sealing.

But here is what I carried into those salt marshes, and what I had been carrying for eleven years without once setting it down: my sister Liss's boots.

Not literally. They'd burned her effects, standard procedure, nothing left to carry. But I knew those boots. I'd bought them for her eighteenth birthday from a cobbler on Tanner's Row, and when they told me she'd been taken to the Hollow, I thought about those boots walking in and never walking out, and I could not stop thinking about it, the way you cannot stop probing a tooth that's gone bad.

Liss had been gifted. Small gift, harmless gift—she could make cut flowers open early. That was all. She made our mother's funeral roses bloom the morning we buried her, and I remember thinking it was the kindest magic I'd ever seen, and then four years later the Edict came down and kindness became contraband.

They told me she'd been sealed peacefully. They told everyone that. *The Hollow takes only the gift.* That was the official phrasing, stamped on every writ, preached from every pulpit: the Hollow takes only the gift, and the person comes home diminished but whole.

No one came home at all.

So when I cornered Underhill at the marsh edge with my writ and my irons, and they asked about the child first—not about themselves, not about mercy, just *where is the girl*—the wall of orders inside me developed its first crack. And when Underhill told me, in that flat careful voice, that sealed gifts don't vanish, they go somewhere, that somewhere has a name and an appetite—that crack became a breach.

Because I'd read the archives. Third Cohort gets archive access; it was one of the few perks. And two weeks prior, filing requisitions, I had found the tally sheets from the Hollow's founding. Eleven years of tallies. Names in, numbers out. And in the column where the sealed power was recorded—measured, weighed like grain—one entry repeated so often I'd stopped seeing it until that moment: *diverted to crown stores.*

Diverted. Not destroyed. Not returned to earth. Harvested.

The Hollow wasn't a wound we were guarding. It was a granary, and we were feeding it.

"I'm not taking you to Corvane," I heard myself say.

Underhill raised an eyebrow. "You're not?"

"No." My voice came out strange, like someone else was using it. "There's a shrine on the old border. Pre-Edict. The priests' maps show a foundation beneath it—something older. If what you're saying is true, if there's any way to unmake what the crown has built—" I stopped. Swallowed. "My sister went into the Hollow eleven years ago. I want to know what came out."

* * *

*Ivo*

We walked the marshes for three days.

Three days of black water and grey reeds, of mud that sucked at your boots like it had opinions about your travel plans, of the huntress Maren Voss setting a pace just fast enough to be punishing. She didn't trust me. Fair enough—I didn't trust her. Trust requires a foundation, and ours was built entirely out of mutual hatred for the same crown, which is a load-bearing material but not a comfortable one.

On the second night, huddled under a lean-to of reeds while the wind came off the flats cold enough to have been personally commissioned by some enemy of mine, she finally asked the question everyone asks eventually.

"Harrow Fen," she said. "Did you do it?"

I could have lied. I'd had seven years of practice lying. But there was something about the dark and the wind and the fact that she'd said *my sister* earlier without flinching that made lying feel like a debt I didn't want to take on.

"The lord who held my oath ordered it," I said. "That's what an oath is, Voss. It's a hand around your throat that speaks with your mouth. He wanted the granaries emptied and the village punished for the shortfall, and he gave the command in the formal words, and my voice obeyed him before I'd finished deciding I wanted it to." The reeds hissed. Somewhere out in the dark, a marsh bird cried once and went silent. "Forty-one people. I stood there and listened to my own sword-speech tear the roofs off their houses. I felt it *enjoying itself*. That's the part no one tells you about swordspeech. It isn't a tool. It's a hunger wearing your voice."

"And afterward?"

"Afterward I broke my oath, which nearly killed me, and ran, and swore a new one: never again. Seven years of luck-knots. Seven years of cantrips small enough to hide behind." I laughed, and it came out bitter as the marsh water. "Do you know what it's like to have a storm in your chest and spend every day pretending to be a breeze? I was so *tired* of being small. And then Wren looked at me across that market square with the hunters closing in, and she was terrified, and I forgot to be small."

Voss was quiet a long time. Then she said, "The child marked you to save herself."

"She marked me because she's eight years old and clever, and clever children survive however they can. I don't blame her." I pulled my cloak tighter. "I blame myself less than usual, actually. Which is new."

"The crown took her too," Voss said. "After you fled. She'll be in the Hollow within the week unless—"

"Unless." I finished the word for her, because neither of us seemed able to.

* * *

*Maren*

On the third day the marsh gave way to shingle, and the shingle rose toward a headland where the old border shrine stood—or knelt, rather; centuries of wind had bent it like a penitent. The sea crashed below. Gulls screamed their idiot liturgies.

Underhill stopped at the threshold and went very still.

"It's here," they said. "Underneath. I can feel it the way you feel weather coming." A pause. "It feels like Harrow Fen. It feels like the worst thing I ever did, waiting down there in the dark."

I put my hand on my sword, not because I meant to use it, but because my hands needed employment. "Can you do what needs doing?"

"I don't know." They looked at me, and for the first time the smallness fell away entirely, and I saw the size of what had been hiding inside them all along. "But I know the crown diverted eleven years of stolen voices into that stone's shadow, Voss. Whatever's down there has been fed. And fed things wake up hungry."

Below us, beneath the drowned shrine, something answered.
