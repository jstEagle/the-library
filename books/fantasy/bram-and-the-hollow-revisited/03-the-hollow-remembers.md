## 3. The Hollow Remembers

The shrine had been drowning for a hundred years, and it showed no sign of embarrassment about it.

Maren found the steps by moonlight—or what passed for moonlight these days, a thin white coin behind clouds that had been threatening snow since Corvane. The water came up to our knees on the lower stair, black and patient, and the pillars rose out of it like drowned men standing very straight so no one would notice they were dead.

"There," she said. "The stone."

I saw it. Of course I saw it. You don't spend seven years pretending to be a trinket-seller without learning to feel the real thing under the fakes, and even drowned, even broken-crowned with moss, the covenant-stone hummed like a struck bell that hadn't finished ringing. It sat in the flooded nave where the altar should have been, tall as two men, its surface crawling with old script that moved when I wasn't looking directly at it. Which is, incidentally, how all the best magic behaves.

"Wren's in there," Maren said quietly.

"I know."

I could hear her—had been hearing her since the marshes, faint as a whistle through a wall. The hunters hadn't killed the child. They'd carried her ahead of us, and whatever they'd done at the shrine's edge, whatever binding they'd wound around her gift, her voice leaked through the stone like light through a shutter. Not words anymore. Just cries. Small ones. The kind a person makes when they've learned that crying loudly only brings worse people running.

I had taught her that. Two years ago, in a Corvane alley, with a hunter's boot two streets away: *Be small, Wren. Be quiet. Be nothing.* Good advice, for surviving. Terrible advice, apparently, for staying whole.

Maren waded beside me, sword drawn out of habit rather than use. She'd stopped pointing it at me sometime during the second day of marsh, somewhere between my fever breaking and her telling me about her sister—Lira Voss, gifted, taken to the Hollow six winters back, returned as a list of names on a crown ledger marked *processed*. We were not friends. We were something more useful than friends: two people who had been lied to by the same institution, holding each other upright.

"The oath," she said. "Tell me again what it costs."

"It doesn't cost," I said. "It *keeps*. That's worse." I touched the water, and it was cold in the way that remembers being ice. "A swordspeaker's word binds the speaker first. Whatever I swear at this stone, I will be until I die. There's no taking it back. No unsaying. Most speakers starved rather than swear wrong. My master told me the second rule before the first: *never speak an oath you couldn't live inside.*"

"And the first rule?"

"*Never speak at all.*" I smiled, because if I didn't I'd do something undignified. "I was excellent at rule one. Seven years of excellence."

We climbed the last step into the nave. The water shallowed. And the stone woke.

* * *

I want to tell you the memory came gently. It did not.

The covenant-stone reached into me the way a reader opens a book they already own, and I was twenty-three years old again, standing on a hill above Harrowmere with Lord Aldric Vane's seal hot on my tongue and his command still ringing in my skull:

*Burn them out. They harbor a hollow-born. Burn them out.*

And I—I young, I proud, I *oath-bound*, which meant I had given away the deed to my own mouth—I spoke.

You should understand what swordspeech is. It isn't shouting. It isn't fire from the fingertips, none of that market-fair nonsense. A true word, spoken in the speaker's voice, becomes *true*. I said *burn*, and the world agreed with me. I said it once, in a voice like a drawn blade, and Harrowmere went up in a bloom of orange that I watched from the hill with my hands at my sides like a good soldier, and the screaming—

The screaming lasted longer than the burning. That's the part they don't carve on monuments. Fire is quick; grief is slow work, and I had spoken *burn*, but the village heard *grief* for a generation after.

Forty-one souls. One hollow-born among them, guilty of nothing but having too many fingers on one hand and a grandmother who remembered the old pacts. Aldric Vane got a tax exemption. I got a title and a voice I never used cleanly again.

That was when I learned the truth nobody tells oath-sworn children: your gift doesn't make you powerful. It makes you *aimable*. Someone else holds the bow. You are only the arrow, and arrows are famous for their opinions.

* * *

I came back to myself on my knees in the shallow water, and Maren's hand was on my shoulder, and Wren's cries were louder now, threading through the stone like smoke through a keyhole. The stone wanted a voice. It was showing me why I'd stopped using mine.

"This is what it does," I rasped. "It makes you look at yourself before it lets you speak."

"Then look," Maren said. "And then say it anyway."

"You don't understand what I am. What I *did*—"

"I know exactly what you did. I read the Harrowmere file. It was my induction reading, actually. They gave it to us as a *success story*." Her grip tightened. "I spent ten years hunting people like my sister because a file told me you were the hero of it. So no, Underhill, I don't need you to explain your guilt to me. I need you to spend it. Right here. On something worth the price."

Above us, through the broken roof, the clouds had gone the color of iron filings, and the cold was coming down in earnest now—the long winter, the one the hedge-witches whispered about, the one that would swallow both worlds if the pact stayed broken. Snow began to fall into the nave and die on the black water, one flake at a time, like the sky counting down.

Wren cried out. And in that cry I heard it—not fear this time, but my own name, or the shape of it, mangled by a binding but *hers*, aimed at me like a hand reaching through dark water.

Seven years I had been small. Seven years of luck-knots and cantrips and being nothing, and it had kept me alive and it had kept no one else anything at all. The child had exposed me with one word of recognition because I had broken my own rule for her—spoken true, just once, just to pull her out from under a falling beam—and gods forgive me, but I would break it again. That was the whole rotten, wonderful secret. Every smallness I'd built had a Wren-shaped crack in it.

I stood. The stone's script crawled faster, hungry, waiting.

"All right," I told it. "But hear me plainly, since plain speech is supposed to be my trade. I'm not giving you my power. Power is what ruined Harrowmere—mine, Aldric's, everyone's who ever loved the feel of a true word in the mouth. I'm giving you something better. Something you can't aim."

I put my palm flat against the wet stone, and it was warm, the first warm thing in a hundred miles, beating like a heart under moss.

"Ivo Underhill," I said, and my voice came out full for the first time in seven years, and it hurt like swallowing sunlight, "last of the oath-bound, speak thus: I renounce the sword-voice that commands. I renounce every word that bends another will to mine. I take instead the open voice—that which can be refused, that which asks and waits for answer. Let the pact between human and hollow-born be reforged not in obedience but in *consent*. Let every door between the realms stand open, and let me stand in it, diminished and unafraid, for as long as I draw breath. This I swear, and cannot unsay, and do not wish to."

The stone took it the way dry ground takes rain.

Light went up through the script, line by line, and the water around us turned silver and then clear, and the long winter cracked overhead like river ice in spring—a sound I felt in my teeth, in the scar tissue of my throat, everywhere I had ever been afraid. Wren's cry cut off. Then, blessedly, changed: not a cry anymore but a voice, calling down through the opening roof, getting closer, *getting free*—

And the Hollow, the wound itself, opened above the drowned altar. Not darkness. A door.

Through it, something old and many-eyed and gentle looked out at me, and looked at my empty hands, and made a sound I chose to interpret as approval.

Maren exhaled beside me. "Underhill," she said. "What did you *do*?"

I grinned at her, hoarse and half-deaf and lighter than I'd been since Harrowmere. "I made a promise I intend to keep. Now come on—"

The door widened. And through it, stepping daintily over the threshold of the world, came the first of the hollow-born, and behind them, far off across the marshes, the horns of Corvane began to sound.
