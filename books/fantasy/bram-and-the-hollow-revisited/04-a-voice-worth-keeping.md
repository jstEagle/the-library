## 4. A Voice Worth Keeping

I woke to the sound of birdsong, which was suspicious, because nothing had sung in Corvane for three years.

The Hollow lay open behind me. Not the wound I remembered from stories—not a tear in the world weeping cold into ours—but something more like a door left ajar on a warm kitchen, light spilling out of it onto the drowned shrine's stones. Through it I could see shapes moving: tall, slow, wrong-angled in a way that no longer frightened me. The hollow-born had waited seven hundred years for someone to knock properly.

Maren Voss sat beside me, wringing marsh water out of her braid with the grim efficiency of a woman who considered even relief to be a chore. "You're alive," she said.

"Barely. I feel like someone emptied me out and used the shell to store turnips."

"That's what giving everything away feels like." She said it flatly, but her hand found my shoulder anyway, brief and hard. Huntress affection. It came with bruises.

Because she was right. I reached for the swordspeech—the true voice, the one that had razed Aldenmere and unmade gates and once, in my arrogance, commanded a river to change its course—and found embers. Warmth without fire. A whisper where there had been a shout. The covenant-stone had taken nearly all of it, and what remained was small enough to cup in two hands.

Seven years ago that would have destroyed me. Seven years ago I'd have torn the door off its hinges to get the power back.

Now I just lay in the reeds and listened to the birds, and found, to my considerable surprise, that I wasn't grieving.

* * *

The walk back to Corvane took nine days. The winter broke around us as we traveled—snow retreating up the hillsides like a tide going out, mud appearing where ice had ruled since before Wren was born. Maren kept expecting ambushes at every crossroads. I kept expecting my own guilt to ambush me instead, and honestly, hers were better armed.

"You know they'll hang you," she said on the fifth day, as we shared a rabbit she'd snared with an ease that reminded me she'd been a huntress long before she was a defector. "Both of us. Desertion, consorting with hollow-born, failure to report a gifted fugitive. I've written the charges myself. They're very thorough."

"I spent seven years selling luck-knots," I said. "I'm told I have a face for acquittal."

"Your face has 'arrest me' carved into it. You flinch when guards sneeze."

She wasn't wrong. But when we crested the last ridge and saw Corvane spread below us—its walls grey, its smoke rising straight and unhurried—I felt something I hadn't felt approaching any city in years. Not dread.

Curiosity.

The gates were open. That alone would have been remarkable. What stopped us both dead on the road was the banner above them: not the crown's black stag on white, but a new device—a knotwork circle, open at the top, stitched in green thread by someone who clearly loved their work.

"A luck-knot," Maren said slowly.

"Someone's been paying attention."

* * *

Corvane had changed the way a house changes after a fever passes—the same rooms, but the air is different, and everyone moves a little lighter. There were still witch-hunters; old institutions die slower than their kings. But they walked in pairs now, and they walked carefully, because the edict nailed to the customs hall read, in the new queen's own hand:

*No person shall be seized for the nature of their gift. The covenant is reforged. Let the doors stand open.*

Wren had told her. Of course Wren had told her. The child who had exposed me with one shouted word in a night market had stood before the throne—so the story went, and Corvane loves a story—and described the Hollow, and the shrine, and the voice that spoke the oath while winter fell like a closing fist. Described how the winter had broken.

Described, I was fairly sure, with some embellishment, how the speaker had glowed.

"I did not glow," I muttered to Maren in the customs yard.

"You glowed a little."

"I was *frostbitten*. Frostbite shimmers."

We found Wren on the steps of the old border shrine—which, in our absence, had been scrubbed, roofed, and renamed the House of Both Voices, apparently by a committee of people with too much time and genuine timber. Wren was twelve now, taller, missing a front tooth, and wearing an official-looking sash that said APPRENTICE in letters that wobbled slightly, suggesting self-application.

They saw me and froze. The whole market square seemed to hold its breath with them.

Here is the thing about being recognized by a child: they remember everything. They remember that you sold them a charm against nightmares and charged half price. They remember that you hid. And if you're lucky, they also remember why.

Wren crossed the square at a dead run and hit me at waist height hard enough to relocate a rib.

"I thought you died," they said into my coat. "Everyone said the stone eats the ones who speak. Everyone said—"

"The stone and I came to an arrangement. I talk less now. It likes that."

Wren pulled back and looked at me with the ferocious, unsentimental honesty that only children and cats can manage. "Your magic's gone, isn't it. Mostly."

"Mostly."

"Good," said Wren, with devastating twelve-year-old certainty. "It was too big for you anyway. Now you'll have to teach properly."

And that—that was the moment I understood what the covenant had actually bought me. Not absolution. Not power. A purpose with edges sharp enough to hold.

* * *

The first lesson was a disaster.

I gathered six students in the shrine's back room: Wren, a baker's daughter whose bread rose unnaturally well, an old ferryman who could taste weather coming, twin brothers who finished each other's sentences whether they meant to or not, and Maren, who insisted she had no gift at all and attended anyway "to make sure you don't corrupt minors," though I noticed she always sat closest to the window where the light came through warmest.

I tried to teach them the old way, the way I'd been taught: discipline, silence, control. Command your gift or it commands you. By the second week, the ferryman had a headache, the twins weren't speaking to each other, and Wren announced, with the tact of a thrown brick, "This is stupid. You hated this. You told us you hid for seven years because the old way made magic into a weapon."

Out of the mouths of apprentices.

So we started over. We made it small. Luck-knots that actually worked. Bread charms that kept loaves from burning. Words spoken gently, offered rather than commanded, gifts given the way you give someone a seat by the fire—not because you must, but because you want to, and the wanting is the whole point.

That was the lesson the stone had tried to teach me at the shrine, and I'd needed a child and a disgraced huntress and nine days of mud to hear it plainly: belonging was never granted by oath. No vow, no crown, no ancient pact could hand it to you. You choose it. Daily. In small voices. With flour on your hands.

* * *

On the first evening of true spring, a year almost to the day since the raid on the night market, I climbed to the shrine roof with a mug of cider and watched Corvane below. Somewhere down there, the baker's daughter was charming her ovens. Somewhere down there, Maren Voss—now officially "liaison between realms," a title she claimed to despise and wore like armor polished daily—was arguing with a hollow-born envoy about trade tariffs. The door in the drowned shrine stood open, and things passed through it both ways, and nobody screamed.

My magic sat in me like a banked coal. Enough to warm. Never enough to burn. Some nights I missed the old power the way you miss a bad lover—sharply, briefly, and mostly at two in the morning—and then I'd hear Wren laughing at their own botched cantrip, and the missing would pass.

"You look insufferably content," Maren said, hauling herself onto the roof beside me. She'd brought her own cider. She always did.

"I'm allowed. I gave away a world-ending voice. Content is the pension plan."

"Mm." She drank, then nodded toward the horizon, where the evening sky over the eastern hills had gone strange—a shimmer, faint but unmistakable, like heat rising off summer grass in midwinter. "The envoys from the far side say there are others. Other shrines. Other stones. Sealed in other kingdoms, under winters that never broke."

I looked at the shimmer a long time. My ember of a gift stirred, gentle as a cat waking.

"Well," I said, and handed Wren—who had, inevitably, followed us up—my cider to hold. "I suppose we'd better start teaching people to knock."
