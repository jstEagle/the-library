## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Years

Grayhollow smelled of tar and secrets, and Mara Lockridge had learned to breathe both without flinching.

She stood at the window of the Saltcellar Inn, watching the harbor fog roll in off the Sere Sea, thick as spilled milk. Below, the evening lamps were being lit one by one along the crooked streets — a town that had never once been honest with the daylight, so it saved its true face for the dark. Fishing boats that ran more than fish. Warehouses with false bottoms. A customs house whose officers could be bought for the price of a decent dinner. Grayhollow was a smuggler's town, a liar's town, and it suited her fine, because in a place where everyone traded in things that weren't theirs, no one asked too many questions about a woman who dealt in memories.

The knock came at the appointed hour: three soft raps, then two, then one.

"Enter."

Aldous Wren — no relation to anyone worth mentioning, a shipping merchant with soft hands and softer eyes — stepped inside and removed his hat. He was nervous. They were always nervous.

"You're the memory-thief," he said. Not a question. He'd paid half up front precisely because he didn't want it to be a question.

"I'm a broker of recollections," Mara said, turning from the window. "Thief is such an ugly word. Thieves take without permission."

"And you?"

"I take *with* permission. It's the selling that's ugly." She gestured to the chair across from hers, set before the small fire she'd lit against the damp. "Sit. Tell me what you want to lose."

He sat. His hat trembled slightly in his hands, a fine felt thing with a feather gone gray at the edges. "Not lose," he said. "Sell. There's a difference."

"There is," Mara agreed. "Losing is an accident. Selling is a choice. You've made yours?"

He nodded, and his jaw tightened the way jaws did when men were about to hand over the best part of themselves. "Her name was Liss. Lissandra. We grew up on the same street, here in Grayhollow, back when I still thought this town was something other than what it is." He laughed, a dry sound with no humor in it. "I loved her from the time I was nine years old. Twenty years I carried it. And then she married a fisherman named Corren Hale, and he drowned two winters past, and now she sits in her house by the chapel with nothing but grief and an empty chair, and I—" He stopped. Started again. "I can't bear to love someone who will never look at me twice. So I'll sell it. The loving. All of it. Every year of it."

Mara had heard a hundred variations of this speech. Men who wanted to forget wars. Women who wanted to forget children they'd buried. Spies who wanted to forget the names they'd betrayed. She had stopped being moved by any of it somewhere around the tenth telling, or perhaps the hundredth; she genuinely couldn't remember which, and there was a certain irony in that she chose not to examine.

"The price stands," she said. "Forty crowns for extraction. The buyer pays separately."

"The widow Hale," Aldous said. "You found her?"

"She found me. Grief makes people resourceful." Mara rose and fetched her kit from the writing desk: a shallow copper bowl, a vial of poppy-and-mothwing tincture, a spool of silver thread that wasn't thread at all but something finer, spun from moonlight caught in still water. She'd bought it from a witch in the Thornfell foothills who had charged her a terrible price and smiled while taking it. "Drink this. It won't put you under. It just opens the doors."

Aldous drank. His pupils widened like wells filling with black water.

"Now," Mara said, seating herself across from him, close enough that their knees nearly touched. She took his hands in hers. His palms were sweating. "I'm going to walk down into you. You'll feel me moving. Don't fight it. Fighting leaves bruises on the soul, and I don't sell bruised goods."

"What does it feel like?" he whispered.

"Like being read aloud."

She closed her eyes and let herself fall.

* * *

Inside Aldous Wren's mind, the years lay strataed like river stone. Mara moved through them with a professional's grace, past the gray sediment of ledgers and cargo manifests, past the bright silt of his daughter's birth — he hadn't mentioned a daughter; they never mentioned the things they meant to keep — down and down to the vein of gold running through everything: *Liss, Liss, Liss.*

She saw her clearly. A girl of eleven standing on the harbor wall with her skirts whipping in the wind, laughing at something young Aldous had said, her hair the color of wet chestnut, her front teeth slightly crooked in a way that made her smile more honest than beautiful. Mara watched twenty years compress into moments: a stolen apple split between them behind the chandlery; a dance at the Midwinter fire where neither of them spoke because speaking would have ruined it; the morning Liss married Corren Hale and Aldous stood at the back of the chapel and smiled until his face cracked.

It was good material. Rich, warm, textured with detail. Mara had extracted thousands of memories and most were thin things, watered wine. But first loves, real ones, unrequited and enduring — those were vintages.

She gathered it carefully, winding the silver thread through each scene, and when she reached the outermost edge of it all — the moment, three days ago, when Aldous had decided to sell — she paused.

There was always a pause. A moment where she stood at the boundary of another person's treasure and considered, briefly, what it would be to keep something. To carry a memory out of a stranger's mind and hold it against the cold of her own. She owned nothing in there. Her own interior was a house with the furniture sold off room by room, and what remained — one item, one single item she had never sold, never shown, never even looked at if she could help it — stayed locked in the deepest cellar, behind a door she did not open.

*Never keep what you steal.* Her rule. Her religion. Because the ones who kept — she'd seen them, memory-brokers grown bloated and strange, carrying dozens of borrowed lives until they couldn't remember which griefs were theirs — those ones went mad, or worse, went *sentimental*, and sentiment in her trade was a slow poison.

Still. The pause. Every time.

Then she cut the thread, drew the whole golden length of it out, and surfaced.

* * *

Aldous blinked at her like a man waking from a long voyage. "Is it done?"

"It's done." Mara held up the thread, coiled now around a spool of black glass. In the firelight it glowed faintly, warm as skin. "Do you feel the hole where it was?"

He pressed a hand to his chest, frowning. "There's... a shape. An absence. Like a tooth missing." His eyes went distant. "I know I loved someone. I remember knowing. But the— the *her* of it is gone." He looked up, and there was relief in his face, and shame about the relief, and Mara knew both would fade within the week. "Will she suffer? The widow?"

"That depends on what she does with it." Mara wrapped the spool in oilcloth. "Some people are healed by a borrowed joy. Some are only reminded of what they've lost. I sell the goods, merchant. What the buyer builds with them is her own architecture."

She counted out his forty crowns' change, saw him to the door, and listened to his footsteps descend the stairs — lighter than they'd come up. They always walked lighter going out. That was the product, really. Not the memory. The lightness.

The widow Hale arrived within the hour: a small woman in mourning grays with red-rimmed eyes and hands that wouldn't stay still. Mara performed the second half of the ritual — the installation, gentler work, threading the memory into the hollow places grief had carved — and watched the woman's face change as twenty years of quiet devotion settled into her like sunlight into a cold room.

"Oh," the widow breathed, tears sliding down her cheeks. "Oh, he *loved* me. All that time. I never knew."

"No," Mara said quietly. "Now you do."

It was, she reflected later, alone in the rented room with the fire burned down to coals, a strange service she provided. She dealt in truths that changed nothing and everything. The widow would live out her days warmed by a dead man's living love, and Aldous would sail his ships with a tooth-shaped absence in his heart, and Mara Lockridge would take her percentage and keep nothing, hold nothing, *be* nothing, and call it professionalism.

Loneliness, she had found, made excellent armor. It never dented. It never needed mending. It just got heavier, year over year, until you forgot you were wearing it and mistook the weight for yourself.

* * *

The Vane family sent for her on a Tuesday, which she should have taken as an omen, because the Vanes did nothing on Tuesdays except make trouble.

Their messenger was a boy of maybe sixteen with a guild pin polished bright on his collar and fear polished brighter underneath. He found her at the fish market, of all places, haggling over smoked eel, and he stood at a respectful distance the way messengers did when the message might get them killed.

"Mistress Lockridge. The Honored Family requests your craft."

Mara handed the eel-seller her coins and turned slowly. "The Honored Family can request my craft the way anyone can. By walking through my door and paying my rates."

"Not this craft." The boy swallowed. "An extraction. From a living mind. Unwilling."

That gave her pause. Willing extractions were commerce. Unwilling ones were butchery — and worse, they were the kind of work that left marks on the thief. She'd known brokers who took forced memories and woke up months later unable to tell which thoughts were originally theirs.

"Whose mind?"

The boy glanced left, right. "A debtor. He owes the Family four hundred crowns and has nothing else of value. The Vanes wish to... liquidate."

"Liquidate his *memories*."

"His best ones. His mother's face. His wedding day. The name of the cove where he hid his real earnings." The boy recited it flatly, a list learned by rote, and Mara heard beneath it the flatness of a boy who had already stopped hearing these lists as horrors. "The Family pays well for specialists. Triple your rates."

Triple her rates. The number was designed to snag in her like a hook, and it nearly did. Forty crowns a job, two jobs a month if the tides were kind — she was not a rich woman, whatever Grayhollow assumed. Three hundred and sixty crowns a year versus what the Vanes offered for a single afternoon's butchery.

"No," she said.

The boy blinked. "Mistress—"

"No. I extract what people choose to give up. I install what people choose to receive. I don't strip a man to his bones because he owes money to people who wear nice coats." She stepped closer, and let him see what lived behind her eyes when she took the armor off, which was rare, and which was why the boy stepped back. "Tell Odric Vane that if he wants a butcher, he should hire one. There are plenty in this town. They're cheaper than me, and they deserve each other."

"He'll mark you," the boy whispered. It sounded almost like a warning. Almost like pity. "When someone refuses the Family, they get marked for collection."

"Collection of what?"

"Everything." The boy touched his collar, the bright pin, the way a man touches a talisman. "Debts, mistress. Everyone in Grayhollow owes the Vanes something. They'll find what you owe. They always find it."

He fled. Mara stood in the fish market with her smoked eel cooling in its paper and felt the town shift around her, subtle as a tide turning. The eel-seller suddenly very busy with his scales. Two dockhands finding urgent business elsewhere. Word traveled fast in Grayhollow, faster than ships, faster than fire.

*Marked for collection.* She'd seen it happen once before, to a fence named Tibbet who'd shorted the Vanes on a shipment of dreamsmoke. They hadn't killed him. Killing was quick, and the Vanes were patient people. They'd taken everything instead — his shop, his savings, his reputation, and finally, when nothing of value remained, they'd hired a butcher to take his memories of ever having been prosperous at all, so that Tibbet spent his last years a pauper who couldn't even remember why he felt robbed.

By Friday, the marks were on her door.

She came home to the Saltcellar to find a sigil painted across the planks in something that gleamed like tar and dried like blood: a circle bisected by a vertical line, the Vane collection-mark, the symbol every smuggler in the Sere ports knew the way sailors knew the color of storm clouds. Beneath it, in a clerk's neat hand:

*ALL DEBTS DUE. LOCKRIDGE, M. — ACCOUNT IN ARREARS.*

She owed them nothing. She'd checked, double-checked, kept her books cleaner than a chapel. But that wasn't how collection worked. Collection invented debts. Collection decided that a refusal was itself a borrowing — she had taken the Vanes' *time*, their *consideration*, their *offer*, and returned nothing, and therefore she owed.

Mara stood in the street looking at her own door for a long moment. Then she went upstairs, packed her kit, counted her coin, and began doing what she did best: planning thefts. Of herself, this time. Out of the town, across the border, a new name in a new port—

Which was when the knocking started.

Not at her door. At the inn's front entrance, heavy and rhythmic, and beneath it the murmur of voices, and beneath that — cutting through the floorboards like cold through a wall — a sound she knew from other people's extracted memories and had hoped never to hear in person: the particular silence of a street clearing for the Vanes.

They'd come tonight. No patience, no clerk's letters, no civilized arithmetic of debt. Something had changed. Something had made them hungry.

Mara blew out her lamp, slid the bolt on her window, and was reaching for her pack when she heard, from the alley below her window, a wet, dragging footstep, and then a voice — a man's voice, cracked and failing, whispering up through the dark:

"Lockridge. Memory-thief. I know what you are. Please — I'm dying, and I have something that needs stealing."
