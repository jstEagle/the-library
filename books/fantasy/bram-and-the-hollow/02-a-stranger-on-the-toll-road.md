## 2. A Stranger on the Toll Road

The knock came at the hour when even Grayhollow's honest thieves were asleep, and it was not a knock so much as a collapse — three dull thuds against her door like a body deciding, in stages, to lie down.

Mara woke with her knife already in her hand. That was the first lesson of the smuggling towns: sleep light, sleep armed, and never trust a sound you can't price. She crossed the cold boards of her rented room barefoot, pressed one eye to the gap in the shutters, and saw nothing at first — just fog rolling off the harbor in gray ribbons and the gutter-lamp across the lane burning its usual sullen orange.

Then she looked down.

There was a man folded against her door like wet laundry someone had hung carelessly. His cloak was dark all over, but some of that darkness moved, spreading slow across the threshold stones in a thin black tongue that found the cracks between the cobbles and drank into them.

She should have let him bleed. That was the sensible arithmetic. A dying man at your door in Grayhollow was not an opportunity; he was a debt, and debts in this town were collected by people with knives and no patience for explanations. The Vane family had marked her two nights ago — she'd felt their chalk sigil on her own doorframe like a bruise she couldn't see — and every instinct she owned said *open nothing, help no one, become a rumor instead of a name.*

But the man turned his head at the sound of her breathing through the shutter-gap, and his eyes found hers as if the wood between them were glass.

"Lockridge," he said. Not a question. A summons.

Mara swore under her breath, because there was only one kind of person who knew that name in that tone, and she had spent six years making sure they all died or forgot.

* * *

She opened the door because he was dying and dead men can't be interrogated, and because — though she'd have cut out her own tongue before admitting it — there was something in his face that snagged on her like a hook. He was younger than his ruined body suggested. Thirty, maybe. Gray at the temples already, the way men went gray who carried things too heavy for too long. And he was smiling, which was the worst part. Dying men didn't smile. Dying men bargained, wept, cursed the gods and the road. This one looked at her like she was the last warm room in a frozen house.

"You're heavier than they said," she muttered, hauling him inside by the collar while he tried to help and mostly failed. "Heavier and louder."

"Bleeding does that." His voice had a courier's flat calm, the tone of a man who had rehearsed his own last words on many roads and never expected to use them. "Bolts. Three. Crossbow. They shoot well."

"Who does?"

"Vanemen." He coughed and produced more red. "Your old friends."

"My old employers," she corrected, dragging him to the hearth and dumping him against the stones. "Friends get buried with speeches."

She worked while she talked, because hands busy kept the fear quiet. She got his cloak off — it came away from his back with a sound like tearing bread — and found the bolt wounds: shoulder, below the ribs, high in the thigh. Two of the three had gone deep. The third had gone true. She'd seen enough gutted men in Grayhollow's alleys to know what that meant, and from the way he watched her count, so did he.

"Name," she demanded, pressing wadding into the shoulder wound. "Give me a name before I waste linen on a stranger."

"Does it matter? You won't have time to use it." He gritted his teeth as she tied off. "Tam. Tam Vess. Courier, third rank, Hollow roads."

Hollow roads. The word landed in her chest like a dropped coin. There was no trade route called the Hollow roads; there was no trade at all down the south passes past Candlemere except the kind that never showed its face at toll gates. Everyone in Grayhollow knew the mountain stories — Thornfell, the court beneath it, the debts paid in years — and everyone wise treated them the way you treat deep water: real, and none of your business.

"I don't take contracts," she said. "Whatever this is—"

"You do now." He laughed, which cost him; the laugh broke apart into wet coughing. "That's the joke, thief. Nobody takes this contract. It takes you."

"Then it can find someone else."

"It did." His hand shot out and caught her wrist with a strength that startled her — the last strength, the kind the body spends all at once like a miser burning his whole purse. "Thirteen turns," he whispered. "It has to be you. It *chose* you. Look at me — look at my arm."

She looked, because refusing would have taken more energy than obeying, and what she saw made her forget how to breathe for a moment.

His forearm, bared where the sleeve had been cut away, was tattooed — but no artist alive had drawn it. The ink *moved.* Lines crawled beneath his skin like roots seeking water, forking and joining, sketching mountain ridges that rose and sank, tunnels that opened and sealed, a labyrinth drawn in living black that shifted each time she blinked. She had stolen ten thousand memories and never once seen ink behave like memory itself — restless, unfinished, waiting for something.

"That's a map," she said stupidly.

"That's the map. The only one. It doesn't sit still for anyone it hasn't chosen, and I've carried it eleven days without it showing me a single path." His grip tightened. "It's shown me yours."

"I'm not going anywhere near Thornfell."

"No." He smiled again, blood on his teeth now, and somehow the smile was worse than the wounds. "You're going *under* it."

* * *

Outside, somewhere up the crooked lane toward the toll road, a dog started barking and stopped abruptly, the way dogs stop when someone quiets them permanently.

Mara froze, head tilted, listening past the rain that had begun to tick against the shutters. Boot-heel on stone — two, then silence, then two more. The particular rhythm of men walking carefully. Hunters walked like that. Smugglers walked loud and drunk on purpose; hunters walked like apologies.

"They're close," Tam said. He had slid down until he was nearly lying against the hearthstone, and his color had gone from bad to worse, gray creeping up his neck like frost. "Listen to me. We have minutes, and I've spent mine badly talking. Take my arm."

"I've seen your arm."

"*Take it.*"

She took it, and the moving ink flared under her fingertips — warm, almost feverish, the lines rushing toward her touch like iron filings toward a lodestone. For half a heartbeat she saw the whole design settle: a mountain in cross-section, roads threading it like veins, and at the heart of it a small bright mark shaped like a keyhole.

Then it dissolved back into restless crawling lines, and Tam exhaled like a man setting down a weight he'd carried for years.

"It knows you," he breathed. "Gods help us both, it actually chose right."

"Chose wrong," Mara snapped, because panic in her always came out as anger. "I steal memories, Vess. First loves, dead mothers' voices, the smell of houses that burned down. I sell them and I walk away. I don't rescue children. I don't storm mountains. I am not the person for whatever this is."

"I know exactly who you are." His eyes were losing focus, drifting toward the fire, and he dragged them back by sheer will. "I've read your file. The Vanes keep files on everyone who's ever robbed them, and yours is the thickest. Mara Lockridge. Never keeps what she steals. Never sells a memory twice. Never—" he coughed, "—never takes a job that hurts a child. Six years of rules. You think I didn't choose you?"

"You said the map chose me."

"The map and I agree. Rare thing, agreement." He fumbled at his neck, fingers clumsy, and dragged a cord over his head. A token swung from it — a small disc of pale stone or bone, no bigger than a coin, carved with a spiral that seemed to turn very slowly when she wasn't looking directly at it. It was warm. Warmer than his skin. Warmer than it had any right to be.

And it *sang*, faintly, in a register below hearing — a note she felt in her back teeth, in the hollow behind her sternum. Mara had handled soul-tokens once, early in her career, before she'd learned the trade's uglier corners. She had sworn never to touch one again. They were the worst kind of merchandise: not a memory, not a life, but the *shape* of a life, the part that kept going when everything else was taken away.

"This is a child's," she said. Her voice came out flat.

"Wren." The name cracked him open; she watched it happen, watched the courier's calm split down the middle like river ice. "My daughter. Sold to the court below Thornfell to cover a debt I was fool enough to co-sign. Her soul-token held in escrow until the thirteenth moon-turn. If it isn't redeemed by then—" His jaw worked. "Servitude. Permanent. The court doesn't free collateral, Lockridge. It *files* it."

"So redeem it. Pay the court."

"With what?" He gestured at himself, at the blood pooling under him, at the whole ruined architecture of his last hour. "A courier's wages? Debts below are paid in years, in names, in *memories*. I tried to earn it back. Eleven days running the passes with the guild's hunters on my heels because the Vanes hold shares in her bond — your old masters profit every day that girl stays caged. I couldn't outrun them forever. Tonight I stopped trying."

Upstairs, or outside — closer now — a soft whistle sounded. Two notes. Answered by two more from the other direction. They were bracketing the house.

Mara's mind did what it always did under pressure: it priced the exits. Back window onto the fish-gutters' alley. Cellar hatch to the smuggler's run beneath the street. She could be gone in ninety seconds if she left him, and every rational bone in her body voted for it. He was dead anyway; the thigh wound alone would finish him before dawn. Staying meant the chalk sigil on her door became a sentence. Staying meant the Vanes, and the Vanes did not forgive, because forgiveness wasn't profitable.

"Take them," Tam said, pressing cord and token into her palm, folding her fingers closed over both. His skin was cold now. When had it gone cold? "Both. The token and the arm. Promise me."

"I don't promise strangers."

"You promised never to hurt a child." His smile came back, terrible and gentle at once. "This is that promise, wearing different clothes. Finish my contract. Steal her back. You're the best thief on this coast — steal my daughter."

The door shuddered. Once, testing. Then the whistle again, closer, and the low murmur of voices comparing positions.

Mara looked down at her closed fist. The token pulsed against her palm like a second heartbeat, off-rhythm from her own, patient, small, *waiting*. Thirteen turns, he'd said. However long a moon-turn was, thirteen of them was not much time, and every one of them belonged to a child sold to a court beneath a mountain by a world that priced souls like salted fish.

She had built her whole life on one rule: *never keep what you steal.* Take, sell, walk away, stay light, stay unloved, stay safe. It was a good rule. It had kept her breathing for six years in a town that ate the sentimental.

And here was a dying man handing her the one thing she could not sell and could not put down.

"I hate you," she told him sincerely.

"I know," Tam Vess said, and pressed her hand tighter around the token. "Thirteen turns. Count them well."

* * *

They came through the front door with a shoulder and a crowbar, three of them in oiled leather with crossbows leveled, guild chalk still fresh on their knuckles. What they found was a small rented room lit by a dying fire, a dead courier slumped peaceful against the hearthstone with a smile on his face —

— and no thief, no token, no map.

Mara went out through the cellar hatch into the smuggler's run with Tam's blood drying on her sleeves and his last words repeating in her skull like a bell that wouldn't stop ringing. In the dark under Grayhollow she ran with the token strung around her own neck now, tucked beneath her shirt, pulsing its slow alien warmth against her breastbone with every step.

*Thirteen turns.*

She didn't want it. She wanted that recorded somewhere, witnessed, carved into her eventual headstone: *she never agreed to this.* She had been minding her own crimes, trading honestly in other people's yesterdays, and the world had answered by dumping a dying man, a moving map, and a child's caged soul into her lap in a single night.

But the token sang its subterranean note against her skin, and the ink on the scrap of forearm-skin she'd cut free — the only piece of the map she could carry, rolled tight and hidden in her boot — stirred faintly whenever her stride turned south.

South. Toward Candlemere. Toward Thornfell. Toward the Hollow.

Toward the missing piece, though she didn't know it yet — because the map needed more than a carrier. It needed a key. And somewhere in the undercrofts of Grayhollow, sleeping in a nest of rags with a lullaby in his throat and nothing at all in his head, a boy without a yesterday had just dreamed, for the eleventh night running, of a door opening in a mountainside — and a woman's silhouette standing in it, holding out her hand.

Mara counted the turns as she ran.

One.
