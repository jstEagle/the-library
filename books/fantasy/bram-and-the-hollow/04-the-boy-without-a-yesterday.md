## 4. The Boy Without a Yesterday

The undercrofts of Grayhollow smelled like every place Bram had ever been, which was to say they smelled like nothing he could name.

He crouched in the gap between a wine merchant's collapsed cellar wall and the foundation stones of the old customs house, where the damp had eaten the mortar into something soft as bread, and he counted his possessions the way other men counted prayers. One knife, blade worn to a thumb's width. One coat, too big, stolen from a line behind the tannery three days ago or thirty — time down here was measured in patrols, not hours. And one song.

*Hush now, hush, the lantern's low,* he hummed against his own wrist, barely a breath of it, because sound carried and the guild men's boots did not carry kindly. *The river knows the road below.*

He didn't know where he'd learned it. That was the whole trouble with him, as far as he could tell: everything he knew had come without a door attached. He knew how to read the current of a crowd for the one face that was hunting. He knew which mushrooms in the flooded tunnels were food and which were lies. He knew how to sleep with one eye's worth of listening, how to make a blanket out of sacking, how to pick a lock with a fishbone. He knew these things the way his hands knew themselves — but if you asked him *who taught you*, or *where are you from*, or even *how old are you*, boy, the answer came back as a smooth gray blankness, like looking at fogged glass and finding no reflection on either side.

No yesterday. No tomorrow either, most days. Just this: the croft, the cold, the song.

*The river knows the road below.*

He stopped humming. Boots above — not the patrol rhythm, which dragged its heels, but the quick paired step of men who worked in twos and liked their work. Guild men. Bram folded himself flatter into the dark and went still in the way that had kept him alive, whatever else about him had been taken away.

They passed. The footsteps faded toward the water stairs. He let out a slow breath and pressed his forehead briefly against the cold stone, and hated — in the flat, practiced way he hated most things — that he didn't even know what he was hiding *from*, only that hiding was the shape his life had settled into, like water finding a crack.

Three nights ago, someone had tried to grab him by the fish market. A man with a Vane pin and a smile like a ledger line, saying, *There you are — we've been looking everywhere for you,* and the terrible thing, the thing Bram turned over in the dark like a stone with something underneath it, was that the man had sounded *relieved*. Like Bram was lost property. Like Bram belonged to somebody.

Bram had put his knife against the man's thigh and run, and hadn't stopped running since except when hunger made him stupid.

He didn't know why they wanted him. That was the worst part. A man could fight for what was his. But he couldn't even name what they thought was theirs.

* * *

On the fourth night, hunger made him stupid.

The bakeries along Candle Row vented their heat down through iron grates, and the smell of failed loaves — the burnt ones, the fallen ones, thrown out warm — rose up through the croft grates like an invitation written in a language older than caution. Bram told himself he'd wait until the third bell. He told himself that twice more. Then the grate above him scraped open under fingers that had done this before, and he was up in the dark of the bakehouse, mouth full, pockets fuller, alive in the specific way only stolen bread made him feel.

Which is exactly when the lamp came up.

"There he is," said the man with the Vane pin. Same smile. Two friends behind him, filling the doorway, filling the room, the lamplight making orange coins of their eyes. "Gods' teeth, boy, do you know what we've spent finding you? Do you know what your—"

"Don't." Bram's voice came out lower than he expected, rougher. "Don't say it like I'm yours."

"You *are*—" the man began, and then Bram moved, because whatever else had been stripped out of him, nobody had managed to remove the part that understood doors. There was a flour hatch behind the cooling racks; there was always a flour hatch. He went through it shoulder-first into a drift of chaff and out into the alley beyond, and the shouting started behind him like a nest of wasps opening all at once.

He ran. Grayhollow's undercrofts were his country and he ran them well — down the tanner's sluice, through the drowned arch where the ceiling kissed the water, up through the gap behind the cooper's yard that only a body his size could fold itself through. But they were herding him. He realized it too late, the way you realize a dream is a trap while still inside it: the boots ahead of him, the boots behind, lantern-light swinging in the wrong streets, and suddenly the open mouth of the old salt cellar yawning before him with nowhere else to go.

He went in anyway. Stupid. Hungry-stupid. Cornered-stupid.

It was a dead end of green-slick stone, and the guild men came in behind him with their lamps held high, and the tall one said, almost gently, which was worse than shouting, "Nowhere left, lad. It's just a debt. Everybody owes the Vanes something."

"I don't owe you anything," Bram said. "I don't even know *what* I'd owe."

"Course you don't remember. That's rather the point."

And Bram felt the words go into him like a fishhook, because *that's rather the point* meant they knew. They knew what was missing from him. They knew what had been taken, and they had names for it, and they wanted it back, and he stood there in the dripping dark holding a bread crust and a dull knife against three grown men and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that he had never once in his remembered life been free. Whatever he was, he'd been bought before he could buy himself back.

The tall one reached out. "Come on, then. Easy."

Bram bit him. He wasn't proud of it later — or he would have considered being ashamed, if shame required a self to be ashamed *with* — but he bit through the man's glove to the meat of his palm, and while the man screamed Bram went for the gap between two lamps, the dark seam between their bodies, the only door left—

—and a hand closed around his wrist.

Not a guild hand. Smaller. A woman's hand, strong and quick, and it caught him mid-lunge so perfectly that for one insane moment he thought she'd been waiting for the exact angle of his flight, had calculated it, had *known*.

"Hold still," she breathed, "or we both die in this hole," and then she did something to the air.

Later he'd try to describe it and fail. She made a sound — no, a *shape* — a word in a language that wasn't language, pitched somewhere below hearing and above pain, and the lamplight guttered. Not blew out. *Guttered*, bent sideways, as if the light itself had looked away, embarrassed. In that borrowed blindness she hauled him past the guild men — past, not around; she took him straight between them, close enough that he heard the tall one say "What in the—" and grope for a lamp that had decided to be somewhere else — and through a crack in the far wall that Bram had marked and dismissed as too narrow, and then they were falling together down a chute of old grain dust into the deep dark beneath the customs house, rolling, coughing, alive.

Above them, muffled, furious: *"Find her! The thief! She's got him!"*

"They know you," the woman whispered. Her voice was dry and sharp, like paper folding. "Interesting. They know you *by name*."

"They don't have my name," Bram said. "Nobody has my name."

* * *

She got a small flame going eventually — a stub of candle cupped in her palm, shielded by her body, as though light were money she couldn't afford to flash. It showed him a woman perhaps ten years older than him, though age sat strangely on her, like a coat she wore for work. Dark hair cut short and practical. Eyes the gray of coin metal, moving over him in quick inventory: hands, eyes, exits. She was doing to him exactly what he did to everyone, and he hated how familiar it felt, like meeting a stranger wearing his own walk.

"You're bleeding," she said.

"So are you. Your sleeve."

She glanced down as if surprised to find the tear in it. "Guild man's knife. It's nothing." She said it the way people say things they intend to be true by force of will. Then: "What's your name?"

"That's funny," Bram said. "I was about to ask you first."

A pause. Something flickered across her face that might have been respect, or might have been indigestion. "Mara."

"Mara." He tested it, filed it. "I'm Bram. I think. It's the only name anyone's ever called me, and they usually shouted it right before trying to catch me, so it may be an alias I gave myself and forgot."

"When?"

"When what?"

"When did you forget?" Her voice had changed — gone quieter, careful, the voice of someone approaching a wound. "Everything. When did you lose it all?"

Bram looked at the candle flame instead of her, because the flame didn't ask questions. "I don't know. That's the joke. I can't even remember losing it. There's no before. There's just — waking up on the toll road eight months back with a full set of survival skills and nothing to survive *for*. No home. No face in the mirror that means anything. No—" His jaw tightened. "One song. Somebody left me a lullaby, of all things. That's the entire inheritance. *Hush now, hush, the lantern's low.* I've been singing it to myself in cellars ever since like an idiot."

Mara had gone very still.

He noticed it the way animals notice weather. Her stillness wasn't calm; it was the stillness of a trap about to spring or a person about to bolt, and he couldn't tell which, and he slid his hand toward his knife without deciding to.

"What?" he said.

"Say it again. The song."

"Why?"

"*Say it.*"

Something in her voice made him obey — anger, mostly, at being ordered, and something else underneath the anger that he refused to examine. "*Hush now, hush, the lantern's low, the river knows the road below.* That's as much as I've got. Four lines missing, probably. Or maybe that's all it ever was. Maybe I invented it. Maybe—"

"Lift your sleeve," Mara said. "Left wrist."

"No."

"Bram." His own name in her mouth landed strange, heavy, a key turning in a lock he hadn't known was on him. "Three men just died getting a map to me. A map that hides itself from me, that goes dark whenever I look at it directly, that shows me *nothing* — and tonight, in a bakehouse, I watched the ink move. On the courier's skin it crawled like it was alive. I need to see your wrist, and I need you to trust me faster than feels safe, because those men upstairs will find this chute, and when they do, we are both dead, and I have plans that do not include dying in Grayhollow."

"And what plans are those?"

"None of your business yet."

"See, that's the thing about trust," Bram said. "It usually comes after the *all* of your business."

For a moment, in the guttering candlelight, they simply looked at each other — two creatures of the same species of damage, each recognizing the other's teeth. He saw a woman who had built herself out of other people's discarded memories the way he'd built himself out of other people's discarded bread. She saw, he supposed, a boy made entirely of survival instincts and one broken lullaby. Nobody in that cellar was offering a hand. Both of them were calculating whether the other's throat was closer than the exit.

Then Mara exhaled through her nose, and something in her face shifted — not softening, exactly. Recalculating.

"The guild marked me for collection four days ago," she said. "You've been running from them longer. Which means right now, you and I are the same kind of thing: property with legs." She held out her hand, palm up, empty. Not grabbing. Offering the choice. "Wrist. Please."

The *please* cost her something. He could see that. And it was the cost, more than the word, that undid him.

Slowly, watching her face the whole way, Bram pushed back his sleeve.

The mark had been there as long as he could remember — which was eight months, which was nothing, which was everything. A thin tracery of lines on the inside of his forearm like a bruise drawn in ink, some pattern he'd never bothered to decode because patterns required a past to compare them against. He'd assumed it was a birthmark. A scar. Dirt that wouldn't wash.

In the candlelight, it *glowed*.

Faintly, deeply, the way coals glow under ash — and it was moving. Lines shifting, rearranging, crawling along his skin like something waking up, like a map remembering itself, and Mara made a sound low in her throat that was half curse and half prayer and leaned in close enough that he could feel her breath on his arm.

"It's the same ink," she whispered. "Gods help us both, it's the same ink. The courier's map — it's been on *you* the whole time. Half of it, anyway." Her finger hovered over the glowing lines without touching, tracing paths, counting turns. "Thirteen marks. Thirteen moon-turns. Three of them are crossed through already, burned dark, and the rest are fading, Bram, they're *counting down*—"

"Counting down to what?"

She looked up at him then, and in the candlelight her coin-gray eyes held something he'd never seen aimed at himself before: not greed, not suspicion, not the appraising look people give lost property. Fear. For him, or near enough that he couldn't tell the difference, and somehow that was worse and better than anything the guild had ever offered.

"I don't know," Mara said. "But the courier died whispering a number, and your skin is keeping score, and three men with Vane pins want you badly enough to burn down half of Grayhollow to get you." She closed her fist around his wrist — not gentle, not cruel, just certain, the grip of someone who had decided. "So here are your options, boy-without-a-yesterday. Stay, and be collected. Or come with me, and be *useful*."

Above them, faint through the stone, boots began to fill the bakehouse. Many boots. Moving methodically. Checking floors.

Bram looked at her hand on his wrist, at the glowing map counting down beneath her fingers, at the only door in his life that had ever opened outward instead of closing shut.

"What's in it for me?" he asked.

Mara smiled without any warmth whatsoever, and pulled him up out of the dark.

"A yesterday," she said. "If we're fast enough to steal one."
