## 5. Terms of an Unwilling Contract

The undercroft smelled of wet stone and old smoke, and the boy smelled of both, and of something else beneath it — a scent Mara had only encountered once before, in a courier's dying blood. Ink. Not the kind sold in bottles. The kind that lived.

She had dragged him through three locked doors and one hidden one, and he had not once asked where they were going. That frightened her more than screaming would have. A child who didn't ask questions had either been trained out of it or had nothing left inside him to ask with.

"Sit," she said, and pushed him down onto the one chair in her safehouse that didn't wobble.

Bram — if the sketch in the courier's satchel was honest, and she had no reason to trust it — sat. He was perhaps twelve, maybe thirteen, all elbows and hollow cheeks, with the particular stillness of a stray that had learned stillness was safer than flinching. His eyes moved over the room the way a thief's eyes did, cataloguing exits, and Mara felt an unwelcome flicker of recognition. She knew that inventory. She had performed it in a hundred strange rooms across twenty years of other people's lives.

"Your name," she said.

He shrugged.

"You have one. Everyone has one."

"Then it's yours if you want it," he said. His voice was flat as river slate. "I don't use it."

Mara lit the lamp on the table between them and turned the wick low. She had done this before — the interrogation, the careful peeling — but always with marks who had something to lose and knew exactly what it was. This boy had nothing. Or rather, he had a hole where everything should be, and holes, she knew from professional experience, were the most dangerous things in the world. People fell into them. People built their whole lives around the edge of one, the way she had.

"I'm going to ask you some questions," she said. "You're going to answer them, because the alternative is that I put you back out on the street where the men with the grey sashes are cutting throats, and I don't think you want that."

"You could just leave me," he said. "You already tried. I followed you home."

Heat crawled up her neck. He had. She had lost him in the fish-market crowd, doubled back through the tannery, taken the long way over the sluice-gates, and still he had appeared behind her on the stairs like a debt she'd forgotten she owed. "How?"

"You glow," he said simply. "The way you walk. Like you know all the doors before you reach them. I know doors too." He held up his left wrist.

In the lamplight, the mark on his skin shifted. She had seen it in the alley — a smear of dark lines, like a tattoo someone had dragged halfway across his arm — but now, in stillness, it moved. Slowly. Deliberately. Lines crawling like roots under ice, like a map deciding where its rivers went. The same ink that covered the dead courier from collarbone to ankle. The same ink that refused, obstinately, to show her anything but a tangle of meaningless roads.

"Where did you get that?" she asked.

"I've always had it." He frowned, the first real expression she'd seen on him, and it looked like it hurt. "I think. I've always had —" His hand closed over the mark as if to hold it down. "I don't know what I've always had. That's the whole problem."

* * *

She made him tea she didn't drink herself, because a calm witness talked more, and she circled him with questions the way she'd been taught to circle a locked mind: not head-on, but glancing, oblique, patient.

Where had he slept last night? The undercrofts beneath the chandler's row — warm, because the tallow vats leaked heat through the floor. And the night before? The bell tower of the drowned chapel. And before that? A shrug. He didn't keep track. He kept track of food and weather and the movements of the guild's grey-sashed collectors, and nothing else.

"What's the earliest thing you remember?"

He thought about it honestly, she'd give him that. His brow furrowed, his fingers picked at the tea's string, and finally he said, "Rain. A roof I was under. Someone saying *don't look up*." He paused. "I don't know whose voice it was."

"When was that?"

"Two years ago. Maybe three. After that it's just — days. Days stacked on days." He looked up at her, and there it was, the thing under the flatness: a terror so old it had gone quiet, the way a wound goes quiet when it decides to stay forever. "You're the memory woman. Everyone in the undercroft says so. They say you can go into a person's head and take things out. They say you took a merchant's whole first love out of him last month and he cried for three days and then thanked you."

"They say a lot of things."

"Is it true?"

Mara set down her cup. She had a rule, and the rule had kept her alive and solvent and alone for a very long time: never keep what you steal, never give what isn't paid for, and never — *never* — go into a mind for free, because free work made a habit of it, and habits made a thief soft.

"Looking costs," she said. "Even a look. Even a scratch at the surface. If I go into your head, boy, I take something with me when I come out. That's how the trade works. I don't get to choose what falls loose."

"I don't have anything to take."

"Everyone has something."

"I have a lullaby," he said, and the flatness cracked, just a hairline. "I don't know the words. I don't know where I learned it. It's all I have that's mine. If you take that —" He stopped. Swallowed. "Take something else. Take the days. They're all the same anyway."

And there it was. The terrible arithmetic of the hollowed. He was offering her the only currency he had, and the exchange rate was ruin.

She should have said no. She told herself, later, that she had meant to say no.

"Give me your hand," she said.

* * *

Touching a mind was like entering a house in the dark. You learned its layout by what your hands found. Most minds were cluttered — furniture of grievance, corridors of habit, rooms locked with shame. Bram's mind was not cluttered. Bram's mind was *burned*.

She slipped under the surface of him the way she'd slip under a locked door, gentle, weight forward, and found — ash. Whole acres of it. Where a childhood should have been there was a scorched field, and the field had edges, and the edges were clean, surgical, deliberate. This wasn't decay. This was *excision*. Someone had gone into this boy with a blade finer than hers and cut a life out of him, and they had done it carefully, and they had done it on purpose.

And at the center of the burned ground, untouched, ringed by ash like a lamp ringed by smoke, something still burned.

She moved toward it and the dark house of him opened — not rooms now but a vast descending space, cold air, the smell of stone and tallow and old copper, and she was standing in a hall she had never seen and recognized instantly, because she had heard it described by a dying man's muttering for three days before the end took him.

Antlers first. That was what she noticed. Seven figures seated in a semicircle of black stone, and where their heads should have been rose branching crowns of antler, pale as drowned wood, hung with scraps of parchment that fluttered though there was no wind. Their bodies were robed in grey — the grey of the sashes, she thought, the grey of the collectors, the grey of everything that served this place — and their hands, their long many-jointed hands, were writing. Always writing. Quills that never stopped moving across ledgers that stretched away into the dark like roads.

Before them, a cage. Not iron — the bars were made of something like frozen lamplight, and inside the cage —

Lights.

Dozens of them. Small, soft, flickering, each one a different color, each one moving with the helpless drifting motion of a moth against glass. And as she watched, one of the antlered magistrates reached out one impossible hand, parted the bars like a man parting a curtain, and lifted a light out — a small gold one, trembling — and held it up to the light of no lamp at all, and *read* it, the way she read a stolen memory, and set it back, and wrote something in the ledger, and the light dimmed just slightly, the way a coin dims when it's been handled too long.

Collateral. The widow of a word rose in her uninvited: *souls as collateral*. She had heard the courier say it with his last wet breath — *they hold her until the turn, they hold her until the turn* — and she had thought it was delirium.

The magistrate's head turned.

Antlers are only bone; they have no eyes. But the thing wearing them looked at her anyway, across the impossible distance between his mind and hers, and one of its many-jointed hands lifted from its ledger, and a quill made of something white and wet pointed directly at the place where she stood —

Mara ripped herself out.

She came back to the room gasping, her chair skidding, her hand still locked around Bram's wrist hard enough to bruise, and the boy was staring at her with wide, frightened eyes, and on his skin, on both their skins, the ink was *moving*.

* * *

The courier's map had been a stubborn thing for three days — a tangle of lines that crawled and hid, that showed her a road and then unshowed it, that mocked her with the shapes of passes she half-knew and could never follow. She had held it to firelight, to moonlight, to the blood of a pigeon (a thief's superstition, and it hadn't worked), and the ink had ignored her the way a locked door ignores a knock.

Now it unrolled across the table between them — she had laid the courier's body's worth of skin-maps flat, all four of them, the shifting tattooed sheets — and the ink woke.

Lines ran. Rivers found their courses. Roads knitted themselves out of chaos, threading through the Thornfell passes, down, down, converging on a single point beneath the mountain's roots, where a small symbol bloomed in letters that assembled themselves out of crawling darkness:

*HALL OF ACCOUNTS.*

And above it, in the margin, in a hand she recognized as the courier's — the same hand that had scrawled *Bram* beneath the boy's sketch — a line of numbers appeared, and rearranged, and settled:

*Thirteen turns of the moon. Three already spent. Ten remain.*

"Ten remain until *what*?" she said aloud, and her voice came out wrong, scraped.

Bram had gone very still. He was looking at his own wrist, where the map's twin mark had gone quiet, settled, satisfied — a map that had finally found its reader. "It's never done that before," he whispered. "It's never — shown anything. It just crawls. It's crawled my whole —" He stopped, because he didn't have a whole.

Mara stood up and walked to the window. Below, Grayhollow was lighting its evening lamps, and somewhere out there the Vanes' collectors were still looking for her, and somewhere south a mountain sat on a courtroom of antlered things that kept children's souls in cages and wrote down everything in books that never stopped filling.

The smart play — her play, always her play, the play she'd run a hundred times — was to fold the map, take the token, and sell the whole contract to the highest bidder in a city far from here. Let some other fool descend. Let some other thief carry the debt. She had a gift for walking away; it was the one thing she'd never sold, the one skill she'd honed to a blade's edge. Walk away, and the grey sashes would follow the map instead of her. Walk away, and the boy —

She looked back at him.

He had pulled his knees up in the chair, making himself small the way children do when they've learned that smallness is safety, and he was holding his marked wrist in his other hand like something injured, and he was watching her with the particular dread of someone who has just watched a door begin to close and knows he doesn't have the strength to hold it open.

"You saw them," he said. "The things with the antlers. You saw what's in the cage."

"I saw."

"Is one of them mine?" His voice was very small. "Is one of the lights mine? Is that what's missing? Is that the hole?"

And Mara, who had lied to merchants and widows and magistrates and gods, who had built a whole life out of the clean architecture of other people's misdirection, found that she could not lie to him. Not because he'd catch her. Because he'd *believe* her, whatever she said, and there was something obscene about handing a lie to a person who had nothing left but belief.

"I don't know," she said. "But I know someone who might."

The courier's satchel was still under her floorboard, wrapped in oilcloth. She brought it up and emptied it onto the table between them: the token on its cord, a child's soul locked in a bead of amber light that pulsed slow as a sleeping heartbeat; a purse of coins minted in no city she knew; and the sketch — the boy's face, rendered in a sure and loving hand, with *Bram* written beneath it in the courier's cramped script.

Bram looked at the drawing for a long time. His fingers hovered over it without touching, the way you hover over a fire.

"Did you know him?" Mara asked. "The man who drew this?"

"I don't know anyone." He said it without self-pity, which was somehow worse. "I don't know *anyone*, that's what I keep trying to tell you. There's no one behind me. There's just the day I'm in." He looked up. "But he knew me. Whoever he was, he drew my face, and he put his map on my arm, and he made sure I'd be here when you —" He faltered. "When you found me. He *planned* me. Like a letter he mailed two years early."

The lamp guttered. Outside, a bell rang the hour, and Mara counted the chimes without meaning to — an old thief's habit, marking time, because time was the only debt collector you could never lose.

Thirteen turns. Three spent. Ten remaining.

She thought about the gold light in the cage, trembling. She thought about the magistrate's quill pointing at her, patient as winter. She thought about her sister, whose face she kept in the locked room at the bottom of herself, the one memory she had never sold, the one thing in the world she would not trade — and she thought, with a cold sliding dread, *they would take that too, if I let them; everything in that mountain is taken from someone.*

She had been planning her escape since the courier died in her doorway. She had routes mapped, coins counted, names of fences in three cities. She had been carrying the escape plan around like a second knife.

She found, examining herself the way she'd examine a mark, that she had already put the knife down.

"Ten turns of the moon," she said, "to get into a mountain that eats names, break into a court that owns the law, and steal back a soul that seven immortal things are holding as collateral." She laughed — one dry note, no humor in it. "I once stole a bishop's entire wedding day. Everyone said that couldn't be done either."

Bram blinked at her. "Is that a yes?"

"That's a yes." She swept the map, the token, and the sketch back into the satchel and buckled it shut. "Get some sleep. We leave for Thornfell at first light, and we take the smuggler's roads, because the guild will be watching the honest ones."

He stood, hesitated at the doorway to the back room — a boy made of exits, always checking them — and then turned around. "The memory woman. Mara. If the things in the mountain take memories —" His hand closed over his wrist, over the sleeping map. "Don't let them take the lullaby."

Something in her chest moved, heavy and unwelcome, like furniture being rearranged in a house she'd sworn was empty.

"Nobody's taking anything else from you," she said. "Not while I'm the thief in the room."

He nodded once and slipped into the dark of the back room, and Mara sat alone with the lamp and the satchel and the amber token pulsing its slow child's heartbeat against the table's wood, and she did not sleep, because she was too busy not planning her escape — and because somewhere far beneath the world, she could not stop imagining the scratch of a quill, writing her name into a ledger, adding her to the column of things the mountain already considered its own.

She just didn't yet know that the mountain already had a page open with her sister's name at the top of it.
