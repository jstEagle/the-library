## 6. The Smuggler's Road South

Mara had robbed enough minds to know that trust was a currency, and like all currency it was mostly counterfeit. So she did not trust the boy. She simply watched him, the way she watched every mark, every buyer, every shadow on a toll road — because watching was the only form of faith she'd ever practiced.

They left Grayhollow before dawn, when the fog off the harbor was thick enough to drown in. Bram walked half a step behind her right shoulder without being told to, which bothered her more than if he'd wandered. People who knew how to follow were people who'd followed before.

"The passes south of Thornfell are watched," she said. "The Vanes pay toll-keepers on both roads. The high road costs coin. The low road costs blood."

"What does the middle road cost?"

She glanced at him. "There is no middle road."

"There is," he said, in the flat tone he used for things he shouldn't have known. "It goes through the old quarry. There's a seam behind the third fall of scree where you can walk through the mountain's shoulder. It smells like wet copper."

"How do you know that?"

His jaw worked. "I don't know how I know anything." He said it without self-pity, which was somehow worse. "I just know it."

* * *

Bram counted his memories the way other men counted coins, except he never came up with a number higher than three: the lullaby, the smell of woodsmoke on snow, and the feeling — wordless, shapeless — of a small hand holding his. Everything else was survival, and survival didn't feel like memory so much as instinct wearing memory's clothes.

But the mountain knew things for him. That was the strange mercy of it. When they reached the quarry at midday, the scree field spread out below them like a gray sea, and his feet simply turned toward the third fall of stone as though someone had drawn the route on the inside of his skin. Maybe someone had. He touched the ink on his wrist — the faint ghost of the map, still warm even in cold air — and wondered if he was walking a path or being walked along one.

"Stop." Mara caught his arm. Ahead, the seam he'd described gaped between two slabs, dark and breathing cool air. But Mara was staring not at the seam but at the ground beside it: boot prints, fresh, pressed into the frost-furred gravel. At least four men. Heavy boots. Guild-issue iron toes.

"They found the quarry," she breathed. "How did they find the quarry?"

"Do you know these boots?"

"I know everything about these boots." Her voice went thin and hard. "Vane hunters wear them. They're weighted at the heel so a running man can't be knocked off balance. I trained some of the men who own them." She looked at him then, and Bram saw something in her face he hadn't seen before — not fear exactly, but the particular exhaustion of someone realizing the past has longer arms than expected. "We go around. There's a shepherd's track on the west face."

"It'll take two days."

"Then we take two days."

He hesitated. "Or we go through anyway. Four men in a corridor of stone is four bodies in a corridor of stone."

"You don't know what Vane hunters can do in corridors."

"No," he agreed quietly. "But they don't know what I can do either. Neither do I." And there was something in the way he said it — no bravado, just a door opening onto a dark room — that made her stop arguing.

* * *

Mara went first, because she always went first; letting others lead was how thieves died. She moved through the seam with her knife out and her breath shallow, counting her heartbeats instead of her steps, listening past them for the sound of men.

She heard them at forty paces. Voices, low. The scrape of a whetstone. The sour-sweet stink of guild-chewed kaf leaves.

She pressed back against the stone and signaled Bram to stop — but he wasn't there.

He had already gone around her. Silently, impossibly, he slipped down the passage ahead of her like water finding its level, and she had a heartbeat's mad impulse to grab his collar and drag him back, and then the light changed.

Four hunters sat around a shuttered lantern at the seam's midpoint, waiting for their quarry like spiders who'd been promised flies. And standing among them, plain as noon, was Bram.

"Evening," he said.

The nearest hunter laughed, reached for his crossbow—

—and stopped. They all stopped. Bram tilted his head, and the lantern flame leaned toward him as though listening, and the hunters' faces went slack, then confused, then frightened, four men suddenly unsure why they were sitting in a hole in a mountain, unsure what they'd been paid to hunt, unsure — Mara could see it dawning across their faces like frost — unsure of their own names.

*No,* Mara thought, ice flooding her chest. *Not again.*

Because she had seen this once before. Years ago, in a cellar in Grayhollow, a dying thief had grabbed her wrist and the world had gone white and when she woke the thief remembered nothing and she remembered everything — including a fragment of someone else's life that had lodged in her mind like a splinter: a courtroom, antlered shadows, a ledger the size of a door.

Bram turned and looked at her over the four empty-eyed men. His own eyes were wide and terrified.

"I didn't mean to," he said.

"I know." She took his hand. It was shaking. "Walk. Now."

* * *

They came out of the quarry's far side into falling dusk, and Bram collapsed against a boulder and put his head between his knees.

"What did I do?" His voice was muffled. "What did I do to them?"

"Took their names. Or their near-enough." Mara crouched an arm's length away — close, but not touching; she was learning his distances. "Guild hunters carry their contracts written into their heads. Oaths, faces, payment terms. You pulled them loose. They'll wander back to Grayhollow by morning, harmless as lambs, wondering why their boots feel heavy."

"And me? What am I pulling from?"

She should have lied. Lying was her craft, her armor, her oldest rule — never keep what you steal, never give what's yours. But under the last violet band of sunset, with the boy trembling like a plucked string, the lie wouldn't come.

"I think you're a room with the furniture missing," she said slowly. "And when you push against the walls, things fall out of other people. Because nature hates a vacuum. Even a made one."

He lifted his head. In the dying light his eyes were the color of the quarry stone. "The courier. The man who died at your door. Did he look like me?"

The question landed like a knife between ribs. She hadn't told him about the courier's face. Hadn't told anyone. But she'd studied the sketch in the courier's effects a hundred times — the boy labeled *Bram*, younger, smiling, a girl's arm flung over his shoulders — and yes. Yes, the jaw was the same. The set of the brow.

"Yes," she said. "A little."

"Then whatever I gave away," Bram said, standing, brushing stone dust from his knees, "maybe I gave it away on purpose. Maybe that was the price." He started walking south, toward the black teeth of Thornfell. "So let's not waste it."

Mara stayed crouched a moment longer, looking at his back, and felt something crack softly in the wall she'd built between herself and the world. She hated it. She got up and followed.

* * *

Two days on the west face nearly killed them both, and would have, if Bram's instincts hadn't kept finding water where maps said there was none, and Mara's knowledge hadn't kept them off the three ridgelines the Vanes watched by glass. They fell into a rhythm neither of them named: he scouted, she planned; he hummed the lullaby under his breath when the wind got bad, and she pretended not to recognize it, and failed, because on the second night she caught herself humming the harmony.

Bram stopped mid-note and stared at her.

"Don't," she said.

"Where do you know that song from?"

"Everywhere. It's a common song. Children sing it in every province from here to the coast."

"It's not common. I've asked. Nobody knows it." He stepped closer, firelight catching the sharp angles of his face. "You know it. Where from?"

Mara fed another stick to the flames and said nothing, which was its own answer, and they both knew it.

On the third day they came down out of the high country into the long pine shadow above Candlemere, and that was where the arrows came.

* * *

The first shaft buried itself in the trunk beside Mara's ear with a sound like a struck bell. She was moving before the second one sang — dropping, rolling, hauling Bram down behind a fallen pine fat as a house. More arrows thudded into the wood. Down-slope, figures fanned out through the trees: six, seven, moving in the Vane wedge formation she'd helped design, curse her clever younger self.

"Toll-keepers my ass," she snarled, drawing her knives. "That's a collection crew. They want us alive."

"Why alive?"

"Because dead men can't be interrogated." Another arrow split bark overhead. "They want to know where the map is. Where the token is. Who else knows."

Bram was pale but steady. "Can we run?"

"Not through seven of them. Not downhill — they've cut the trail." She peered through the branches, mind racing, cataloguing options and finding each one shorter than the last. The slope above was cliff. The slope below was death. The trees offered cover but not escape, and night was three hours off, and they didn't have three hours.

"Mara." Bram's voice had gone very quiet. "Take my hand."

She looked at him. "Absolutely not."

"The gate. The cave mouth. Whatever's coming — you said the map responds to memories it recognizes. Your hand on mine finished it once. Maybe—" An arrow screamed past close enough to part hair. "Maybe I'm not just a room with the furniture missing. Maybe I'm a road too."

"That kind of working strips people, Bram. It's not a trick, it's a toll—"

"So pay it!" For the first time since she'd known him, he shouted. "You want to protect your memories like coins in a vault, fine — but I don't have any! Whatever this takes from me, it takes from an empty shelf! Take my hand or we both die on this hillside and Wren serves thirteen hundred years under a mountain!"

Wren. He'd never spoken the name aloud until now. It seemed to surprise him as much as her.

Arrows. Shouting. Boots crashing upslope through the pines.

Mara swore — a long, fluent, heartfelt oath in three languages — and seized his hand.

* * *

The world folded.

Bram had no word for what happened next, and suspected no word existed. The forest peeled away like paper. He felt Mara's memories brush against his — not stolen, not taken, *shared*, the way heat moves between two hands held palm to palm — and in the space of a heartbeat he knew things he had never lived: a harbor at dusk, a widow's tear-bright kitchen, a sister whose face was a locked door even inside Mara's own mind. And Mara, gasping beside him, knew his three treasures whole: the lullaby, the smoke-and-snow, the small warm hand.

And beneath all of it, rising like a vein of ore through rock, the mountain answered. The ink on his wrist blazed. The slope beneath their feet *changed its mind* — and there was a path, sudden and impossible, a seam in the pines that had not been there a moment before, winding down through the rocks on the blind side of the ambush.

"Run," Mara said, and her voice came out doubled, as though the mountain spoke it with her.

They ran. Arrows chased them and found only afterimages. The Vanes' wedge broke apart trying to follow a trail that closed behind the runners like water closing over a dropped stone. Twice the path forked impossibly and Bram chose without choosing, his feet knowing, and Mara followed, her hand locked in his, her knives useless and forgotten in her belt.

They burst out of the treeline into the open bowl above Candlemere village as full dark fell, and the seam behind them sealed with a sound like a book snapping shut. Bram's legs gave out. Mara went down with him, still gripping his hand, both of them dragging air like landed fish.

For a long moment neither spoke. Below them, candlelight pricked the windows of the village — dozens of small flames, the town that had given itself its name.

"What did it cost?" Mara managed finally. "Tell me what it cost you."

"I don't know yet." He flexed his fingers. The ink on his wrist had faded further, worn thin as old paint. "Ask me tomorrow. If I remember tomorrow."

That was supposed to be a joke. Neither of them laughed.

Instead Mara did something she had not done in eleven years of trade and theft and armored loneliness. She did not let go of his hand. She sat on the cold grass with a boy who was mostly absence, looking down at the lights of Candlemere, and held on.

"We get the widow's help in the morning," she said at last. "Then we find the Hollow's mouth. And Bram — if it comes to it, if the mountain demands a price at the end of this — you don't pay it alone. That's not a promise. It's arithmetic. I can't finish the contract without you, and I don't leave debts unfinished."

"First honest thing you've said," Bram murmured.

"Don't get used to it."

But she was still holding his hand when he fell asleep against the boulder, and in his dreams the small hand in his had grown a twin, and somewhere far beneath the earth, in a hall of antlered shadows, something ancient turned a page and began, patiently, to write their names.

* * *

They reached Candlemere as the first bell rang, and the woman who opened the door of the crooked stone house took one look at Mara's face and went white as tallow.

"You," the widow whispered. "You sold me my daughter's memory. And now you come to my door with a soul-token round that boy's neck and the Vanes' arrows in your cloak." Her eyes flicked to the token, then to Bram's faded wrist, and something in her expression cracked and reformed into dread. "Child," she said, stepping aside to let them in, "you'd better sit down. Because what the Hollow does with souls isn't what the songs say — and I'm the only soul in this valley who knows the truth of it."

She closed the door behind them, and bolted it, and lit a candle that guttered as if afraid.
