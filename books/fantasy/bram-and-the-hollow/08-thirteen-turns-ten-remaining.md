## 8. Thirteen Turns, Ten Remaining

Bram dreamed in pieces, the way a dropped plate scatters.

The first piece was always the same: a room too tall for its walls, pillars of black stone rising into dark that never ended, and seated above him — or below him; the dream tilted — figures with antlers growing through their skulls like roots grown wrong. Their faces were smooth where faces should have been. Not blank. *Smoothed*, as if someone had taken thumbs to wet clay and worked away every feature that might have been accused of being human.

They did not speak. They wrote.

Quills scratched somewhere in the dark, a sound like insects in a wall, and each scratch made his teeth ache. He could not see what they wrote. He only knew, with the certainty dreams give you and waking takes away, that whatever it was, it was about him. That he was not a guest in this room. He was an entry.

*Line forty-one,* said a voice that was not a voice. *Paid in full.*

*Paid with what?* he tried to ask, but dream-mouths don't work that way, and the antlered ones turned their smoothed faces toward him all at once, and beneath the scratching of the quills he heard something else — small, far off, muffled as a cry through three closed doors.

A child crying. Or laughing. He could never tell which, and the not-knowing woke him every time with his heart slamming and his hands already reaching for something that wasn't there.

Because there was always a hand in his. Smaller than his own. A child's fingers laced through his, gripping hard enough to hurt, and in the dream he knew that grip the way you know your own heartbeat — knew it, trusted it, would have followed it anywhere — and he did not know whose hand it was.

He woke on the third night of the road south with that phantom grip still aching in his palm, and found Mara already awake, sitting up against her pack with her knife across her knees and the map spread out beside her, glowing faintly in the dark like something alive.

"You were talking," she said.

"I don't talk in my sleep."

"You do. You say *hold on*." She didn't look at him when she said it. She looked at the map, at the ink crawling along its veins of road, and her jaw was set in a way he had learned meant *do not ask me anything else.*

He lay back down and stared at the underside of the bridge they'd sheltered beneath, at the frost furring the old timbers, and pressed his palm flat against the cold ground until the ache went out of it.

Hold on. He'd been saying *hold on* to no one, in his sleep, for who knew how long.

The days blurred. Thornfell's southern passes narrowed to goat-tracks, then to ledges where the wind came off the drop like a living thing trying to pry them loose. Bram learned the shape of their traveling: Mara ahead when the way needed reading, himself ahead when it needed *feeling* — because that was the other thing, the thing that unsettled her more than his nightmares did. He knew hidden ways the way she knew stolen memories. Instinctively. Completely. Without knowing why.

Twice he stopped her before she'd seen the rockfall herself. Once he pulled them both off a trail that looked perfectly sound and watched, twenty minutes later, from the ledge above, as the whole shelf sloughed away into cloud. Mara hadn't thanked him. She'd just stared at him afterward with an expression he was beginning to recognize — the look of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like, adding up what he was against what he cost.

On the ninth day they came down out of the high passes into a valley where the pines grew thick and the light came green and underwater, and that evening, making camp in the lee of a fallen fir, the dream changed.

That was how he knew it mattered. Dreams that repeat are habit. Dreams that *change* are messages.

This time the courtroom was closer. The antlered magistrates leaned down toward him, and one of them — the tallest, whose antlers branched like winter oaks — extended a long gray finger and touched his forehead, and the touch opened him like a book.

He saw: a kitchen with copper pots. A woman's hands kneading bread, flour to the wrists. A girl — small, quick, dark-haired, missing a front tooth — spinning in the doorway with her arms out, demanding he watch, *watch me, watch me* —

And he was watching. He was holding her hand. Her grip was fierce and sticky with honey and he loved her so much the feeling had weight, had mass, sat in his chest like a stone he'd carried so long he'd mistaken it for part of his body —

Then the magistrate's finger withdrew and the vision snapped shut and the courtroom tilted, and the child's hand was torn out of his, and he heard — clearly now, unmistakably — the sound she made as she went.

Not a cry. Not laughter. His name.

She said his name, and it wasn't *Bram*, and the dream took the true name away even as he reached for it, the way the mountain's gate was said to take names, and he woke screaming into the green dark of the valley with birds going up from the pines in a clatter and Mara's knife already drawn, her eyes wild in the firelight.

"What," she said. "What is it. What did you see."

He told her. All of it — the kitchen, the flour, the girl with the missing tooth, the grip of her hand, the name that vanished. He told it badly, in pieces, his voice cracking on the parts that mattered most, and Mara listened without moving except for one thing: when he described the girl spinning in the doorway, her breath went in, very quietly, and did not come back out for a long time.

"The soul-token," he said. "The courier's daughter. Wren." He'd read the courier's effects enough times by now to know the name the way you know a word you can't pronounce. "It's her. It's her I'm dreaming about. Isn't it."

Mara fed a stick into the fire. Her face was composed again, all the doors shut, all the windows dark. "You're dreaming about a kitchen," she said. "Dreams are just the mind chewing its cud."

"She held my hand."

"Children hold hands. You've probably walked past a hundred children."

"In the dream I *knew* her." His voice came out harder than he intended. "I knew her the way you know — " he groped for it — "the way you must have known your sister."

The stick in Mara's hand stopped halfway to the fire.

For a moment the whole camp was very still. Somewhere upslope a night bird called once and thought better of it. Mara set the stick down with exaggerated care, the way a person sets down a blade they very much want to use.

"Don't," she said, "presume to know what I've known."

"I'm sorry. I — "

"And don't go building castles out of bad sleep." She met his eyes then, and hers were bright and furious and frightened underneath, and he understood suddenly that she wasn't angry at him. She was angry at the thing he'd said because it was *true*, and truth was the one cargo she'd spent her whole life refusing to carry. "You remember nothing. That's the whole of what you are to yourself. If you start stitching dreams together and calling it a past, you'll build yourself a lie comfortable enough to live in, and you will never come out. I've seen it happen. I've *sold* it happening."

"That's not what this is."

"Isn't it?" She laughed, short and sharp. "You want the girl in the kitchen to be Wren because wanting makes the road shorter. It doesn't. The road is exactly as long as it is. Thirteen turns, ten remaining, and every one of them bought with walking, not wishing." She lay back, turned her shoulder to him, and said, in a voice sanded flat: "Sleep. We move at first light."

But she didn't sleep. He heard her not sleeping — the careful stillness of it, the breathing kept deliberately slow — for hours after the fire burned down to coals.

Two nights later, in the ruins of an old toll-house with rain coming down like the sky meant it personally, Bram hummed.

He didn't decide to. It came up out of him the way the hidden ways came up out of him — unbidden, certain, older than his memory of himself. A small looping tune, four phrases, the last one falling away at the end like a step down into sleep. He hummed it while he watched the rain, and only realized he was doing it when the sound of Mara's whetstone stopped.

The silence went on too long.

He turned. She was sitting with the stone motionless in her hand, staring at nothing, and her face had gone the color of the wet ash in the hearth.

"What?" he said. "What is it?"

"Where did you hear that?"

"Hear what? I didn't hear it anywhere. It's mine. It's the only thing that's mine." He heard how that sounded — defensive, childish — and pushed on anyway. "It's all I kept. Whatever they took, whatever happened, the lullaby stayed. I don't know who taught it to me. I just know it."

"Sing it again."

"Why?"

"*Sing it again.*"

So he sang it, low, under the drumming rain — four phrases, the last one falling away — and halfway through the second time, Mara began to hum the harmony.

She didn't seem to know she was doing it. Her eyes had gone somewhere else entirely, somewhere years deep, and her voice came out thin and unwilling and true, a counter-melody winding under his like a second vine around the same trellis, and the two parts fit together the way two parts fit when they were learned in the same room, from the same voice, a long time ago.

The song ended. The rain filled the silence.

"That's a Candlemere tune," Bram said carefully. "The widow hummed it once, mending nets. I asked her about it."

"It's not from Candlemere." Mara's voice was barely there. "It's older than Candlemere. My mother sang it. She sang the low part and my sister sang — " She stopped. Her hand had gone to the locket at her throat, the one he'd seen her touch a hundred times and never open. "My sister sang the high part. Badly. She was always flat on the third phrase, and my mother said the third phrase was cursed, and we'd laugh — "

She shut her mouth so fast her teeth clicked.

Bram sat very still, the way you sit when a wild thing has come near you and any movement might send it away forever. Rain ran off the broken roof-beams between them. The map lay coiled in Mara's pack, and through the canvas he could see the faint pulse of its shifting ink, quicker now, like a heartbeat after running.

"Mara," he said softly. "Who taught you the harmony?"

"Don't."

"Because I think somebody taught us both. I think — "

"*Don't.*" She was on her feet, and the knife was somehow in her hand, though pointed nowhere, held the way you hold a thing when letting go feels dangerous. "You don't get to do this. You don't get to be a mystery that solves itself at me. I stole for a living. I know what memory looks like when it's real and when it's built, and yours is — " Her voice failed her, caught, came back rougher. "Yours is *real*. That's what frightens me. Real things have consequences. Real things mean the courier chose you, out of everyone, and I want to know why, and I can't know why, because knowing things is how they get taken from me — "

She stopped. Looked down at the knife. Looked at him.

And Bram saw it then, plain as the rain: she wasn't afraid of his past. She was afraid of her own. Every question he asked about himself was a question she'd spent years refusing to answer about herself, and the lullaby had just kicked the door in on both of them at once.

He stood, slowly, keeping his empty hands visible. "I'm not asking you to give me anything," he said. "I'm just telling you what I have. It isn't much. A kitchen. A hand. A song. But whoever I was, Mara — whoever I was loved somebody enough to hide her inside his own head, and let them wipe everything else out of him to keep her safe." His throat tightened. "That's not a mystery solving itself at you. That's just a man standing in the rain, holding the only thing he's got, and finding out it matches something of yours."

Mara looked at him for a long moment. Rain dripped from the rafters. The ink pulsed in her pack, steady now, patient.

"The third phrase," she said finally, and her voice was wrecked and quiet. "In our version, the third phrase goes up where it should go down. Everyone sings it wrong. Everyone in the world except — "

She couldn't finish. She didn't have to. He sang the third phrase, and it went up where it should go down, and Mara Lockridge — who had faced guild hunters and guild debts and the gate that eats names without blinking — put her hand over her mouth and turned away, shoulders shaking, and would not let him see her face.

He didn't try to see it. He sat back down by the dead fire and held his own aching palm, the one the small hand gripped in the dream, and grieved — before understanding, before proof, before anything — for a girl with a missing tooth whom he could not remember and could not stop loving.

Outside, the rain eased. And in the pack by the door, the map's ink began to move of its own accord, drawing a road none of them had chosen, south and down, toward the mouth of the Hollow — and beneath the roads, in letters that had not been there before, in ink that gleamed like tears:

*TEN TURNS REMAIN.*
