## 10. Market of the Unpaid Dead

**Mara**

The gate spat them out into a cavern so vast that the ceiling was only a rumor — a darkness overhead where pale lights drifted like slow snow falling upward. Mara stood on a ledge of black glass stone and stared down into the city of the Hollow: terraces of lantern-lit stalls carved into the mountain's throat, streets of packed bone-white earth, and everywhere the murmur of commerce conducted by people who had no breath left to spend.

"Gods below," Bram said beside her. His voice came out hushed and strange in the cave's acoustics, doubled by echoes he hadn't made.

"Not gods," Mara said. "Debtors." She pulled her hood lower and started down the switchback stair. "Keep your hands in your pockets, your memories behind your teeth, and don't sign anything."

"What could they possibly want to buy from me? I told you at the gate—"

"I know what you told me. That's exactly why you're valuable. Empty vessels are rare down here. Everyone else is leaking." She glanced back at him. The gate had passed him through untouched, and she still hadn't decided whether that made him safe or made him something worse than safe. "Stay close. If anyone offers you a bargain, the answer is no. If anyone offers *me* a bargain, the answer is also no, and then run."

The stair delivered them into the market itself, and Mara let it wash over her the way she'd once let cities wash over her — cataloguing exits, counting guards, pricing everything.

It smelled like a market should have been impossible to smell like: roasting chestnuts, tallow, wet wool. But the smells were wrong somehow, secondhand, the way a memory of a smell is wrong. A ghost woman turned skewers of meat that cast no grease and left no char, and the scent of them reached Mara's nose a half-beat late, like sound crossing a valley.

The vendors themselves were the true inventory. Some were whole ghosts, translucent and dignified, wearing the fashions of three or four different centuries. Others were less than that — a pair of hands wrapping purchases, a laughing mouth hawking lottery tickets, an old man's torso from the collarbones down, his upper half apparently spent or sold long ago. They sold regrets in stoppered vials. They sold years, decanted into clay jars that glowed faintly amber, each one labeled with the age it had been taken from. One stall displayed nothing but last words, chalked on slate boards in handwriting that changed as Mara watched.

*Thirteen turns*, the courier had whispered as he died. Nine remained now, by the widow's count. And somewhere under this mountain, a child's soul sat in a cage of light, accruing interest.

A ghost boy — maybe ten years dead, maybe sixty; it was hard to tell with the young ones — fell into step beside them with the practiced ease of a pickpocket who no longer needed to touch you.

"First time under?" he asked. His grin showed too many teeth for the size of his mouth. "You've got the look. Fresh names, full pockets. I can show you where the honest traders keep their stalls. For a small consideration."

"No," Mara said.

"For free, then. Call it an investment. You'll be back when you realize everyone down here lies except the dead, and even we lie mostly."

Bram made a small startled noise. Mara caught his sleeve and kept walking.

"The toll roads," she said to the boy. "We need to go deeper. Toward the Hall of Accounts. Who sells passage?"

The boy's grin flickered — not gone, but recalculated. "Passage is dear. Passage is *paid for*. You don't walk toward the Hall unless somebody wants you walking there."

"Somebody sent us," Mara lied smoothly. "The court sent us. Check your ledger if you doubt it."

That was a gamble. She saw it land wrong immediately — the boy's translucent face went carefully blank in the way of someone who has learned that curiosity is expensive.

"Then you won't need a guide," he said, and melted sideways into the crowd.

Bram exhaled. "That went well."

"It went fast. Fast is better." But her palms were damp, and she wiped them on her coat without letting him see. *Somebody wants you walking there.* Fine. She wanted to know who, before whoever it was wanted to know her.

They bartered anyway, because there was no other way forward. At a stall stacked with bottled lullabies — actual lullabies, humming faintly through their corked bottles like bees — Mara traded two of her stock memories: a fisherman's morning calm, a soldier's ability to sleep anywhere, both extracted years ago and never spent because they'd never found buyers foolish enough. The ghost vendor, a broad-shouldered woman missing everything above her jaw but somehow radiating skepticism anyway, took them, sniffed them, and grunted approval.

"Passage south galleries," she said, in a voice like wind through a keyhole. "Old freight tunnels. The clerk-ghosts run them. Find Ivo at the candle-stall, third terrace down, tell him Sefa remembers the winter he froze." She slid a brass token across the counter. "And thief — whatever debt brought you down here, pay it fast. Interest compounds crueler than grief."

Mara pocketed the token and did not ask how the woman knew she was a thief. Under this mountain, she suspected, everything knew.

* * *

**Bram**

Bram had died once. He was almost sure of it.

Not recently — there was no wound, no cold, none of the things dying was supposed to involve. But somewhere in the hollow place where his past should have been, there was a sensation like a held breath, like standing on the far side of a door he'd paid dearly to open. Walking through the market, listening to ghosts haggle over jars of their own childhoods, that feeling grew teeth.

He stopped at a stall where an ancient man was selling regrets individually, laid out on velvet like jewelry. *Regretted a kindness, 40 years old, lightly worn.* *Regretted a cruelty, 61 years old, heavy, price negotiable.* Bram leaned closer, and one of them — a small grey swirl in a locket — seemed to lean toward him too.

"You feel that?" the old man rasped. "That's recognition. Regrets know their own kind." His clouded eyes fixed on Bram with sudden terrible interest. "Yours is big, boy. Whatever it is you can't remember — it's not gone. It's *waiting*. There's a difference."

"Bram." Mara's hand closed on his shoulder, firm as a lock turning. "We're leaving this stall."

"He wasn't buying," the old man said pleasantly. "Just browsing. Browsing is free. Everything else down here costs, but looking is free, isn't it, boy? Go on. Look. See if anything looks *familiar*."

The grey regret swirled in its locket, and Bram felt the lullaby rise in his throat unbidden — four notes of it, the same four notes that always came, the fragment that was all he had. He hummed them before he could stop himself.

Everything within earshot went quiet.

Not dramatically. Not all at once. But the market's murmur thinned around them like water pulling back from a shore, and three separate vendors turned to look, and the old man's mouth opened slowly into something that was not a smile anymore.

"Now where," he said softly, "did you learn *that*?"

"We're going," Mara said, and this time it wasn't a suggestion, and her grip on Bram's arm hurt, and he let her pull him away into the crowd while his heart hammered against ribs that felt suddenly like a cage of their own.

"What was that?" he demanded when they'd put two terraces between themselves and the stall. "What did I do?"

"You didn't do anything. That's the problem." Mara had stopped in the shadow of a buttress, and in the lantern light her face looked carved out of something harder than flesh. "That tune. I know it. I've known it since—" She stopped herself, jaw tight, and he watched her decide not to finish the sentence. "It doesn't matter where I know it from. What matters is that nobody in this market should recognize it, and everybody here acts like they almost do."

"Maybe it's just a song people sing down here."

"Mara." She said her own name like she was tasting something spoiled. "I stole my first memory in a port town when I was nineteen. In eleven years I have heard every drinking song, every dirge, every nursery rhyme from Grayhollow to the southern coast. Four notes, minor key, descending. It's not from anywhere. It's from *here*." She pushed off the wall and started walking again, faster. "Come on. We need this Ivo before someone decides we're worth more than we cost."

Bram followed, but the old man's words walked with him. *It's not gone. It's waiting.*

He touched his own wrist, where the map ink sometimes glowed. Beneath his skin, faint as embers under ash, it pulsed — once, twice — in rhythm with the four notes still ringing in his skull.

* * *

**Mara**

Ivo's candle-stall was easy to find and hard to approach. Every candle burned a different color, and each color meant something, and the crowd around the stall moved with the wary deference people give to power they can't name. When Mara finally got close, she understood why: the candles weren't candles. Each flame was a memory, burning. Customers came, whispered what they wanted forgotten or remembered, and Ivo — a thin, stooped ghost with ink-stained fingers and the harried air of a man perpetually behind on his filing — would pluck a candle from the rack, hold it to the customer's lips like a sacrament, and let them breathe it out or breathe it in.

"Sefa sent me," Mara said. "She remembers the winter you froze."

Ivo's hands stopped moving. For a long moment he studied her with eyes that had seen ledgers balance and fail. Then he snuffed the nearest candle with two fingers and leaned across the counter.

"Sefa is a sentimental old ghost," he said quietly, "and a fool, and I love her. What do you want, surface-thief?"

"Passage. South galleries, toward the Hall of Accounts."

The reaction was immediate and instructive: the ghosts nearby drew away, conversations resumed at careful volume elsewhere, and Ivo's expression closed like a ledger snapping shut.

"No."

"I haven't named a price."

"There is no price. Do you know what happens to couriers who carry unauthorized business toward the Hall? They come back as *ink*. They write other people's letters until the mountain wears them out. I was a clerk up there for ninety years. I know the shape of the machine." He began rearranging candles that did not need rearranging. "Go home. Whatever you're owed, owe it instead. Debt is survivable. Curiosity under Thornfell is not."

"It's not my debt." The truth came out of her before she could dress it up as strategy, and she hated how raw it sounded. "It's a child's. A soul-token, thirteen turns, nine remaining. Her father carried the contract and died on my doorstep paying me to finish it."

Ivo went very still.

Slowly, he reached beneath his counter and produced something wrapped in oilcloth: a scrap of parchment, old, soft with handling. He unfolded it on the counter between them. It was a contract page — Mara recognized the format instantly, the dense columns of the Hollow's accounting script, terms and collateral and interest schedules. Half of it had been torn away.

"Torn contracts are void," Ivo said. "That's the law. So the clerks tear them. Whenever a debt gets too ugly — whenever even the magistrates' stomachs turn, and their stomachs turned rarely — the record is destroyed and the debt is declared settled." His finger traced a column of numbers. "Except debts aren't settled. They're *transferred*. Torn pages get rewritten into new bonds. Nothing under this mountain ever ends; it just changes hands."

Mara read the visible column. Collateral: one soul, juvenile, female. Term: thirteen lunar turns from date of bond. Conversion upon maturity: perpetual indenture, soul-bound, non-transferable.

Her blood went cold in the warm cave air.

"Juvenile," she repeated. "This is Wren's original bond. This is what her father was trying to tear."

"And failed," Ivo said. "Or half-failed. The courier got the token out — that much is true, the whole market heard the story — but the *bond* stayed in the books. A soul-token without its bond is just a pretty pendant, thief. The debt lives in the ledger, and the ledger lives in the Hall, and on the thirteenth turn, when the moon comes round, the conversion executes whether anyone holds the token or not." He looked at her with something that might, in a living man, have been pity. "Her father didn't steal his daughter's freedom. He bought her nine months of it. And now you're carrying the receipt."

Bram made a sound beside her — small, wounded — and Mara realized he'd understood first. She was still catching up. *Perpetual indenture, soul-bound.* The caged lights she'd glimpsed through Bram's broken memory, rows and rows of them, patient as lamps.

"Then we don't pay the debt," she said. "We erase it. Tear the bond properly. Burn the page."

"You cannot burn the master ledger. It predates the mountain. The magistrates wrote themselves into its foundation—" Ivo stopped. Something crossed his face, quick and involuntary, and Mara, who had built a career on reading faces, read it plainly: *he knew more, and telling her would cost him.*

"You were a clerk," she said softly. "Ninety years. You know the Hall's bones. You know which doors the wardens watch and which ones they forgot." She set the brass token on the counter between them, next to the torn contract. "Sefa froze one winter waiting for you to come back up the mountain. I'm asking you to make a different trip worth taking."

For a moment Ivo said nothing. Around them the market roared on, oblivious, selling pieces of itself.

"The freight tunnels," he said finally, in the tone of a man signing against his own judgment. "There's a maintenance stair behind the salt-vaults, disused since the Second Audit. It runs under the warden posts. I can take you as far as the outer records room." His eyes lifted to hers, and they were older than the mountain around them. "But hear me, thief. The Hall doesn't guard its books with soldiers. It guards them with *accounting*. Every step you take inside will be measured, priced, and added to what you already owe. By the time you reach the master ledger, the question won't be whether you can steal what you came for."

He began snuffing his candles, one by one, plunging his little stall into dusk.

"It'll be whether you can afford what you'll have become."

* * *

**Bram**

They followed Ivo through streets that got poorer and quieter the deeper they went, past stalls where the goods were nothing but apologies and unsent goodbyes, and Bram counted the lanterns to keep from thinking about bonds and conversions and the four notes of a song that lived somewhere under his missing years.

Nine turns. He turned the number over. Two hundred and seventy days, give or take, until a girl he'd never met became property forever. Unless—

Unless the map was right about him. Unless the ink glowing under his skin really did lead somewhere in his head, some locked room where the missing key waited. He thought about the old regret-seller's locket, swirling grey. *It's not gone. It's waiting.* He thought about the smaller hand in his, from the dream — antlered shadows all around, a courtroom, a voice saying *the brother will serve as surety* — and his stomach clenched around nothing, because there was nothing in him yet to grieve with, only the shape of grief, the negative space of it.

Wren. He tried the name silently. It fit strangely, like a shoe belonging to someone with similar feet.

Behind them, Mara walked with her shoulders squared and her eyes moving constantly — exits, hands, prices. But when he glanced back, he caught her looking not at the market but at him, with an expression he couldn't file. Not suspicion anymore. Something more uncomfortable than that. Something like responsibility, worn by a woman who had clearly never once let herself carry it.

Ahead, Ivo paused at a rusted grate half-hidden behind the salt-vaults, and the tunnel beyond breathed out air that tasted of dust and old paper and, very faintly, of candle smoke.

"Last chance to owe it instead," Ivo said.

Mara stepped past him into the dark. "Down here," she said, "everything's owed either way. I'd rather choose the direction."
