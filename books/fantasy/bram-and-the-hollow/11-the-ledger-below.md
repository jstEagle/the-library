## 11. The Ledger Below

Ivo found them before they found him.

They were three stalls deep into the Market of the Unpaid Dead, past the vendor who sold bottled sighs and the one who traded in last words, when the crowd simply opened — not parted, *opened*, the way water makes way for something colder than itself. Through the gap drifted a figure that Mara's eyes kept trying to slide off of. He was translucent the way old glass is translucent: still a shape, still a suggestion of a man, but you could see the market's lanterns burning through his chest.

He wore the rotted remains of a clerk's coat, its brass buttons gone green, and around his neck hung a slate on a cord. The slate was blank. That, Mara would learn, was the whole of his tragedy.

"You're the thief," he said. His voice had the texture of paper being turned. "The one from above. The one who takes memories out of heads like plums from a pie."

"And you're dead," Mara said. "Which makes you the only honest merchant here. Everyone else is selling something they don't have."

Something moved across the ghost's face — not quite a smile. The memory of one, worn smooth by years of disuse. "Ivo," he said. "Formerly Second Under-Clerk of the Hall of Accounts. Formerly a man with a name. Currently this." He lifted the blank slate and let it swing. "They took it when I misfiled a bond. One bond, out of four hundred thousand. I put a comma where a period belonged, and a child's debt came due nine years early, and the magistrates decided that a clerk careless enough to cost the court a decade of interest was a clerk who didn't need his name anymore."

Behind Mara, Bram shifted. He'd been quiet all day — quieter than usual — and she'd learned to read his silences the way sailors read weather. This one had a storm in it.

"You know the Hall," she said. It wasn't a question.

"I *was* the Hall, for sixty years. I know which stair groans, which door pretends to be locked, which magistrate takes his supper at which hour and which one never sleeps because sleep would mean dreaming about what he signed." Ivo drifted closer, and the lantern-light bent strangely through him. "You want into the Ledger. Everyone who comes down here wants into the Ledger eventually. The beggars want it to erase their debts. The merchants want it to erase their rivals. The dead want it to erase their deaths." His blank slate swung, pendulum-slow. "You, memory-thief — I think you want it to *read*."

Mara said nothing. In her experience, the less a ghost knew, the less it could be tortured out of him, and the Vanes had taught her exactly how thorough torture could be.

But Ivo only laughed, a dry rustle like a ledger's pages fanned by a cold wind. "You don't have to tell me. I can smell a bond-holder from here. You've got someone's soul-token in your pocket, and you've been carrying it the way people carry a coal — careful, and close, and hating that it's warm." His hollow eyes went to Bram. "And you've brought the boy. Interesting. The court has been asking after a boy."

The market's noise seemed to recede, as if the stalls themselves were leaning in to listen.

"Asking after him," Mara repeated. "How?"

"The criers. Every sixth bell, they read the outstanding bonds in the great square — it's how the court reminds everyone below that nothing is ever forgotten, nothing is ever forgiven. Three days ago, the criers added a new line." Ivo's voice dropped to that paper-soft register. "*'One bond, unclaimed, held in trust. The vessel is known. The vessel will present itself.'*" He tilted his head at Bram. "They're not hunting you, boy. They're *inviting* you. There's a difference, and the difference is traps."

* * *

They took Ivo to the rented room above the candle-seller's stall, because ghosts, Mara reasoned, could hardly be overheard by the living, and the dead below had their own etiquette about eavesdropping on each other. She bought the room with three years of a stranger's youth and considered it a fair rate.

Ivo passed through the door rather than opening it, which Mara decided she'd never get used to, and settled — *settled*, though nothing about him touched the floor — across from her at the table. Bram sat in the corner with Wren's soul-token in his cupped hands, watching it glow its slow, patient green, and Mara had stopped pretending she didn't notice that the boy guarded the token like a dog guards a grave.

"Here's what you need to understand about the Hall of Accounts," Ivo began. "Everything below Thornfell runs on debt. Not coin — coin is for the living and their little markets. Debt. Years of service. Years of memory. Years of *breath*. When a soul is bonded, it's written into the master ledger, and the master ledger is not a book. It's a *thing*. It's older than the magistrates. They found it when they found the mountain's heart, and they built their whole rotten court around it the way a pearl builds around a wound."

"And Wren's bond is in it," Mara said.

"Wren." Ivo said the name like he was testing whether it still worked. "The courier's daughter. Yes. And here is what your ghost-friend" — he tapped his own chest — "can tell you that no one else can, because I filed it. I filed her bond with my own hands, back when I still had hands that mattered." He leaned forward, and the lanterns burned brighter through his fading chest. "Her bond is *shared*, memory-thief. It's not held by the court alone. It's divided into shares, the way a cargo is divided among a shipping consortium. The court holds the majority. But the rest —" he let the blank slate swing between them — "the rest was *sold*. To interests above. To a guild that trades in everything the mountain needs and everything the mountain fears."

The room went very quiet. Bram looked up from the token.

Mara felt it before she understood it — a cold spreading through her chest, a recognition, the particular sick drop of a trap she'd walked into years ago finally closing. "Say the name," she said.

"Vane," Ivo said. "The Vane family guild holds eleven shares in the bond of the child Wren. Bought nine years ago, the same season the bond was written. They've been collecting interest ever since — a tithe of the girl's soul-stuff, siphoned up through the toll roads, paid out in luck and longevity to the family's inner circle. It's why the Vanes never lose at cards. It's why old Corvus Vane buried three wives and outlived them all with his hair still black." Ivo's voice curled with something that might have been contempt, if ghosts were permitted contempt. "Your guild doesn't just profit from the child's captivity, thief. They *feed* on it. Slowly. Yearly. Like a tithe to a god they don't even believe in."

Mara stood up so fast the chair went over backwards. She didn't remember doing it.

Nine years. Nine years she'd run contracts for the Vanes, carried their packages, stolen their memories, laundered their sins through her own two hands — and the whole time, they'd been holding shares in a child's soul the way merchants hold shares in a ship, waiting for the cargo to appreciate. And now the guild hunters were on the toll roads looking for her, and Bram, and the token — not because the courier had stolen from them. Because the courier had stolen from their *portfolio*.

"Mara." Bram's voice. Careful. He'd learned, in the weeks since the toll road, exactly how loud to be around her when her face did this.

"They knew," she said. The words came out flat and strange. "When the courier died on my doorstep, when they chased him into Grayhollow — they weren't trying to recover their property. They were trying to protect an *investment*. Eleven shares. Eleven shares of a little girl, and I spent nine years carrying their ledgers and licking their boots and telling myself the Vanes were just criminals, just smugglers, just the ordinary rot of the world —" Her hand had closed around the soul-token before she knew she'd reached for it, and it pulsed against her palm, warm as a sleeping bird. "They're not ordinary rot. They're *shareholders*."

"Sit down," Ivo said mildly.

"I will *not* —"

"Sit down, thief, because rage is a currency and you're about to spend all of yours at once, in a market that gives terrible exchange rates." The ghost's hollow eyes fixed on her, and there was something in them that was sixty years of watching people come apart in the Hall of Accounts. "I have watched fury walk into that Hall a hundred times. It walks in loud and it walks out *filed*. Do you understand? The magistrates have a drawer for rage. They have a whole *cabinet*. They will convert your anger into a debt — the debt of disturbance, the debt of trespass — and they will hold the paper over you until you die, and then they will hold it over your ghost, and I am *speaking from experience*."

Mara stood there, breathing. The token pulsed. Bram watched her with those eyes that remembered nothing and missed nothing, and slowly, joint by joint, she made herself sit down.

"Good," Ivo said. "Now. Rage is fuel, but it is not a *plan*. So let's build the plan, and we'll burn the rage later, when it will do the most damage."

* * *

He drew the Hall for them on the back of a slate, using a stub of candle-wax for chalk, and Mara watched his hands as he worked — hands that cast no shadow, that the wax passed through and somehow obeyed anyway, because even stripped of a name, Ivo had been a clerk so long that the work lived in what was left of him.

"The Hall sits at the mountain's root," he said. "Below the court, below the criers' square, below the market — everything above is built on it, the way a lie is built on a smaller lie. One entrance for the magistrates. One entrance for the clerks. One entrance for the debtors being dragged in to sign." The wax squeaked across the slate. "And one entrance that isn't in any of their records, because I found it myself, sixty years ago, and never reported it, because a clerk who finds a secret door and reports it is an honest fool, and a clerk who finds a secret door and *keeps* it is an insurance policy."

"You kept it," Mara said.

"I kept it for sixty years and never needed it, and then I misfiled a comma and lost my name, and now I'm spending it on you. That's the thing about insurance, thief. You never know what you've been paying for until the fire." He tapped the slate, at a small mark behind the Hall's eastern wall, where the mountain's root-veins ran close to the stone. "The clerks' cistern. It carries runoff from the hot springs up-slope, and it passes within an arm's reach of the master ledger's vault. The wall between is old. Old walls in this mountain are honest walls — they crack. And the crack is wide enough for a thin woman with flexible ethics."

"And the vault itself?" Bram asked. His voice had changed — steadier, and Mara had noticed over the past weeks that he was steadiest exactly when things were worst, as if fear were a memory he'd lost along with everything else. "The ledger. What does it look like?"

Ivo was quiet a moment. When he answered, the paper-rustle of his voice had gone soft and almost reverent, the way men speak of things that have outlived their gods.

"It's not a book," he said again. "I told you that. It's a slab of black stone as tall as the mountain is deep, and every debt ever written below is *on* it, and *in* it — cut into the surface and sunk into the stone both, so that no entry can be scratched out without cutting the mountain itself. Names. Sums. Terms. The whole architecture of everything the court owns, and the court owns everything, including, boy, a line that I filed with these hands, and have regretted with these hands, for sixty years."

He turned the slate around. At the bottom of the Hall's rough map, in the candle-wax, he'd written a single line, and then, with the stub, drawn a box around it.

*Wren, daughter of Aldous the courier. Term: until the thirteenth turn. Shares: Court, majority. Vane consortium, eleven.*

"The thirteenth turn," Mara said. "That's when the debt converts. That's what the courier was counting down."

"That's when the girl stops being collateral and becomes *property*. Outright, whole, and forever — and every share consolidates to the majority holder, and the Vanes collect their final dividend, and somewhere above us, Corvus Vane takes another decade off his face." Ivo set down the wax stub. "You have until the thirteenth turn. By my count — and counting is the one thing they left me — you have some turns fewer than that."

Mara looked at the wax line a long moment. Then she looked at Bram, who was watching her with the token's green light swimming in his eyes, and she made herself say the thing she'd been circling since Ivo had spoken the name *Vane*.

"If we steal the token and run," she said, "they'll collect. The bond doesn't care where the girl's soul is — it cares that it's *owed*. We could go over the passes, over the sea, and the debt would follow, because it's not written on paper. It's written in *stone*." Her jaw tightened. "The only way Wren goes free is if the entry itself goes. And you can't scratch out an entry cut into a mountain."

"No," Ivo agreed. "You can't."

"But," Mara said, and here was the thief's turn of mind, the thing that had kept her alive through nine years of the Vanes, "you said the ledger holds *everything*. Every debt. Every name. Every term." She leaned forward, and the candlelight caught the hard line of her smile, the one that had made grown smugglers ask for their memories back. "A ledger is only as strong as the clerk who keeps it. And a clerk who can *file* an entry—" her eyes went to Ivo, to the blank slate, to the ghost who had lost his name to a comma, "—can *amend* one."

Ivo went very still. Even ghosts could go still, it turned out.

"Amend," he repeated.

"The magistrates read the ledger as law because no one's ever dared to touch it. But it's still a *record*, and every record has an author, and every author has a blind spot." Mara turned the soul-token over in her fingers, watching the light. "I don't need to cut the mountain. I need to make the mountain believe the entry was always different. And I happen to be very, very good at making people believe things that were never true."

"That's forgery," Bram said.

"That's *justice*," Mara said. "Below this mountain, apparently, it's the same trade. The court steals souls and calls it accounting. The Vanes buy shares in a child and call it investment. Well. I've spent my whole life stealing from people who deserved it and calling it survival." She set the token down between them on the table, where its glow lit all three of their faces — two living, one something else. "I'm done calling it survival. This time it's a heist. And this time, I'm not keeping the take."

Ivo laughed — the dry page-rustle again, but warmer now, and underneath it, for the first time since they'd met him, something that sounded like *hope*, which is a dangerous thing to hear from the dead.

"Sixty years," he said, "I have wanted to amend one line in that ledger. One. And I told myself I'd need an army, or a miracle, or a magistrate's own hand turned against the court." His hollow eyes gleamed. "Instead I get a memory-thief and a boy the court is *asking for*. Thief — when do we start?"

Mara was about to answer when Bram made a sound.

It was small — a breath caught sideways — and when she turned, he was staring at the token on the table, at the green light, and his free hand had risen halfway to his own chest, fingers spread, as though pressing against something only he could feel. The lullaby, Mara thought. She could almost hear it, the one he hummed without knowing why, the one she'd never told him she recognized.

"Bram?"

"The criers," he said slowly. His eyes came up, and they were the eyes of a boy reading his own name in a stranger's handwriting. "The criers read the bonds every sixth bell. You said that." He swallowed. "Mara — the bell rang while we were in the square. I heard it. And I *moved* before I heard it. I was halfway across the square before the sound finished, toward the Hall, like I'd walked that road a thousand times." His hand closed over the token, and the light flared, once, bright as a struck match. "I don't think the court is inviting me because I'm a vessel. I think they're inviting me because I've *been there*. I think I've stood in front of that ledger before."

Ivo and Mara looked at each other across the green glow.

"Then we go tonight," Mara said, "before the court remembers why it knows your face."

She picked up the token. It was warm in her palm, warm as a held hand, and somewhere above them — beyond the market of the unpaid dead, beyond the gate that eats names, beyond a thousand feet of patient stone — the moon was turning, and the ledger was waiting, and eleven shares of a child's soul sat in a guild vault with her old enemies' names written beside them.

She intended to collect.
