## 12. Antlered Magistrates

The summons came as a sound.

Not a knock, not a voice — a sound like a bell rung underwater, felt in the teeth before the ears, in the bones before the blood. Bram was asleep in the clerk's alcove behind Ivo's old counting room, curled against the wall with Mara's knife under his hand and Wren's token on its cord around his neck, and the sound pulled him out of sleep like a hook through the sternum.

He sat up. The alcove was dark. Ivo's ghost-light had guttered to nothing, and Mara was gone — she'd gone down into the market hours ago, she'd said, to buy them a road out — and Bram was alone with the sound still ringing, and then alone with the silence after it, which was worse.

Because he knew that silence. He knew it the way you know a room you've dreamed of: not the details, but the *weight* of it. The held-breath weight of a place where something enormous is waiting to be said.

"No," he told the dark. His voice cracked on it. "No, no—"

The stone floor went soft as wax beneath his bare feet. Not melting — *inviting*. It tilted, gently, the way water tilts toward a drain, and Bram grabbed for the doorframe and the doorframe let him pass through it like smoke, and the last thing he saw of the counting room was Ivo's ledger lying open on the desk, its pages riffling as if in wind, as if applauding.

Then he was standing in a courtroom.

* * *

It was vast the way a cathedral is vast, but nothing like a cathedral otherwise. There were no pews, no altar, no windows. The walls were made of stacked ledgers — actual books, thousands upon thousands of them, bound in leather gone green-black with age, shelved from floor to a ceiling lost in shadow, and between the shelves ran channels of pale light like veins of ore, and the light moved. It flowed upward, always upward, slow as honey, and Bram understood without being told that what flowed there was payment. Years. Names. Everything the mountain had ever taken, rising to be counted.

The floor beneath him was a single page, enormous, blank except for one line of script that appeared as he looked at it:

**SUBJECT PRESENT.**

"Subject," Bram repeated aloud, and hated how small his voice sounded in all that stone.

They were already seated when he turned — or perhaps they had always been seated; perhaps they had been seated since before the mountain had a name. Seven figures on a dais of black wood, tall and thin as winter trees, and from their heads rose antlers. Not crowns. Antlers — branching, many-tined, grown rather than worn, and in the crooks of the antlers hung small lights, each one pulsing faintly, and Bram's stomach turned over because he knew those lights. He had seen their cousins in a dream: a cell full of caged brightness, souls on a shelf.

The magistrates did not breathe. He watched for it and found none. Their chests were still as furniture. When the centermost one spoke, its mouth moved but the voice came from everywhere at once, from the ledger-walls, from the floor under his feet, from inside his own skull.

"You are late."

"I didn't come," Bram said. "You *took* me."

"Late," the magistrate repeated, unbothered, "by thirteen years." A pause, in which the light in the walls seemed to lean closer. "Though by our accounting, subject, lateness is simply another form of debt."

"My name is Bram."

The word dropped into the room and lay there like a coin on stone. None of them moved. Then the leftmost magistrate — smaller than the others, its antlers stubbed and broken, as if something had snapped them off long ago — made a sound. It took Bram a moment to recognize it as laughter, because it was so dry, so papery, like pages turning.

"Bram," it said. "Yes. That would be the name you'd choose, wouldn't it. The name the courier gave you, when he wiped you clean and set you loose above ground like a message he hoped would never be delivered."

Something cold walked up Bram's spine. "*Courier.*"

"The man called Aldous Vane-adjacent — no, forgive us, we are getting ahead of the entry." The centermost magistrate lifted one long hand, and a ledger detached itself from the wall and floated down, down, settling open in the air before Bram at the height of his own chest. The page was written in ink that shifted as he watched, the same shifting ink as the map tattooed on a dying man's skin, and the handwriting was familiar in a way that made his throat close — because it was *his* handwriting. He knew the slope of it. He knew the way the letters leaned, tired, to the right.

He could not remember ever holding a pen.

"Read your entry, subject," the magistrate said.

"I'm not reading anything until someone tells me where I am and how I got here."

"You are in the Hall of Audience, below Thornfell, in the Hollow, which is the world's cellar and its countinghouse both. You are here because you were always going to be here. And you got here—" the magistrate's head tilted, antlers scraping a slow arc through the dark, "—the way everything arrives here. Owing."

"I don't owe you anything."

The broken-antlered one laughed again, softer this time, almost kind. Almost worse.

"Oh, but you do. Shall we read it to you? We do so enjoy the older entries. The ink was better then." It raised a hand, and the floating ledger turned its own pages, back and back and back, years peeling away, and stopped. "Entry four thousand one hundred and twelve. *The debtor Tammas Reeve presents himself for settlement of account. Debt incurred: nine years' interest upon a loan of mercy, drawn in the famine winter, when his daughter's fever was bought down with borrowed time. Principal unpaid. Interest compounded. Assets assessed: one brother, aged nineteen, sound of body, unencumbered.*"

Bram heard the words. They went past him like arrows and struck somewhere behind him, in the dark, and he stood very still and watched the magistrates with the flat attention of an animal deciding whether to fight or run.

"That's not my name," he said.

"It is the name you surrendered," said the centermost magistrate. "Along with everything else. Do you begin to see the elegance of it? Your brother came to us with a debt he could not pay. We offered terms. He refused to give us his daughter — she was collateral enough merely by existing; her soul was already pledged, you understand, the moment he signed. But the interest had come due, and interest, unlike principal, cannot be deferred. Something had to be paid." The antlered head inclined, courteous as a host. "So he paid you."

"*No.*"

"He paid you instead of her. Nineteen years of memory, healthily remembered, whole and undamaged — a fine vintage. We took the vintage. We left the bottle." A dry rustle passed through the seven of them, their version of shared amusement. "Your brother walked out of this hall carrying his daughter and a token, believing he had bought her free. He had not. He had only moved her debt into a different column. And you walked out carrying nothing at all, because there was nothing left in you to carry. You asked us, at the door, who you were. Do you remember asking?"

Bram's hands had closed into fists without his permission. His nails bit his palms. "I don't remember anything."

"We know," said the magistrate, gently. "We have the receipt."

* * *

The ledger drifted closer. He could not stop himself from looking. That was the cruelty of it, he understood dimly — they didn't need to force him; they only needed to leave the book open, the way you leave a wound uncovered. The page showed columns of entries in his own tired, rightward-leaning hand, and beside each entry, in the magistrates' colder script, annotations:

*Memory withdrawn: mother's face. Quality: excellent.*
*Memory withdrawn: the lullaby (source melody, Candlemere). Retained by debtor as partial payment. Balance outstanding.*
*Memory withdrawn: self.*

*Self,* Bram thought. *They wrote it down like a line item. Self, withdrawn.*

"The lullaby," he said. His voice came out sanded flat. "I still have the lullaby."

"A remainder," the broken-antlered magistrate agreed. "An error, if we are honest — and we are always honest; it is the one luxury of power. The withdrawal was incomplete. One thread slipped the shears. You have carried it about above ground for thirteen years, humming a song you cannot source, and every note of it has been accruing interest against your brother's account." Its ruined antlers dipped. "You are not a person, subject. You are an *outstanding balance*. And balances, in time, come home."

"I'm not a balance." The words came out of him low and shaking. "I'm — I survived. Thirteen years. I learned things. I kept myself alive with nothing, with *less* than nothing, and whatever you wrote in your book, whoever Tammas Reeve was, he's dead. I buried him. I'm what grew in the empty plot."

For the first time, the magistrates went quiet in a way that felt less like amusement and more like attention.

"Grew," the centermost one repeated, tasting the word. "Yes. We have observed this phenomenon. Emptiness, given enough seasons, does not remain empty. Things take root in cleared ground. Weeds, mostly." It gestured, and the great ledger turned forward again, past his childhood, past the wipe, and stopped at a fresh page whose ink was still wet and gleaming. "Which brings us to the present entry. Read it, subject. This one concerns your future."

He should not have read it. Some part of him, some deep animal part, knew that and said so, and he read it anyway, because it was in his handwriting and because thirteen years of not-knowing had carved a hunger in him that no caution could outweigh.

*Entry current. The debtor Tammas Reeve, remainder-designate "Bram," having re-entered the Hollow of his own accord, is hereby restored to active account. Outstanding obligations: one (1) soul-token, custody disputed; one (1) lullaby, accrued interest, thirteen years; one (1) theft-in-progress, unauthorized access to Hall of Accounts, accomplice identified —*

The name beside *accomplice* was **Mara Lockridge**, and beneath it, in smaller script: *memory-thief. Known associate of the Vane interest. Recommend seizure of subject as leverage against thief's continued intrusion. Subject's value: evidentiary.*

Evidentiary.

The word sat in him like a swallowed stone. Not hostage. Not prisoner. Evidence. A thing to be produced at trial, cited, entered into record, spent. He looked up at the seven antlered shapes on their black dais and understood, finally and completely, that to them he had never been a boy at all — not at nineteen, not now. He was a document. He was the receipt his brother had been too cowardly to keep, filed and shelved and forgotten until such time as the court required proof of payment.

"You're going to use me against her," he said.

"We are going to *file* you," the magistrate corrected. "What use others make of filed things is not our concern. We concern ourselves with accuracy." It folded its long hands. "Although. Since you raise the thief. She is resourceful, this Lockridge. She has gotten further into our mountain than any surface-dweller in a century, and she has done it with your help, which is a complication we had not priced in. So we offer you terms, subject. We offer everyone terms; it is our nature and our law."

The dais light shifted. Somewhere far above, muffled by a thousand feet of stone, a bell began to toll — slow, deliberate, counting.

"The twelfth turn approaches. In one turn of the moon, the child Wren's account converts, and her soul passes into permanent servitude, and your brother's debt closes forever, and you—" the magistrate's voice softened into something almost tender, almost human, and that was the most frightening thing yet, "—you become a closed entry. Nothing. A receipt for a transaction completed. Unless."

"Unless what."

"Unless the thief surrenders what she carries and withdraws from our mountain, in which case we will consider the matter — *consider*, mind, not settle — and you will be permitted to remain here, in the files, intact and unspent, for as long as the court finds you useful." The antlered head bent toward him, and the little caged lights among its tines flared, and in their glow Bram saw, for one horrible instant, that the magistrates' faces beneath the bone were not faces at all but pages. Written ones. Dense with entries, front and back, where features should have been. "Or unless you continue down this road, in which case we will take from you the only thing you have left."

"The lullaby," Bram whispered.

"The lullaby," the magistrate agreed. "And then, subject, you will be exactly what we made you. Empty. Accurate. Complete."

* * *

The floor tilted again — or the room did — and the ledgers on the walls began to turn their pages all at once, thousands of books rifffling in a wind that did not exist, and the sound was like rain, like applause, like laughter, and the last thing the centermost magistrate said, as the courtroom poured itself away around him like water finding a drain, was spoken directly into the inside of his skull, in his own handwriting, in his own voice:

*"Tell the thief: the ledger remembers what the thief forgets. Ask her what she never sold. Ask her what it costs."*

Then Bram was on his knees on the cold flags of Ivo's counting room, gasping, his palms scored with crescents of blood, and the alcove was dark, and Mara was still gone, and the token around his neck was burning cold as river ice.

He knelt there a long time, breathing.

*Tammas Reeve.* He tried the name on like a coat found in a dead man's closet. It fit his shoulders. That was the worst of it — it fit. Somewhere under the emptiness, something recognized the shape of it and stirred, and he thought: *my brother paid you instead of me, and you let him, and you've been sitting on a shelf for thirteen years waiting to be taken down and read aloud.*

His brother had traded him. His brother had walked out of this mountain with a daughter and a lie and left him hollow as a jar.

And yet —

And yet the lullaby. The one thread the shears had missed. He hummed it now, there on the floor, barely a breath of it, and felt it pull taut inside him like a fishing line running out into deep dark water, and at the far end of the line, faint but unmistakable, something *answered*. Not a memory. Smaller than a memory. A warmth. A small hand in his, the way a smaller hand fits into a larger one, and a voice saying — not words, just the shape of words, the shape of *don't be afraid, I've got you* —

Wren. It had always been Wren. The token wasn't the bond. The token had never been the bond.

The door scraped. Mara came in fast and quiet, hood back, eyes sharp with news he could see she didn't like, and she stopped dead when she saw him kneeling in the dark with blood on his hands.

"Bram?" Her voice changed. "What happened. What did they do to you?"

He looked up at her — at the woman the ledger had already written down as his accomplice, his co-conspirator, his fellow evidence — and he heard the magistrate's parting whisper curl out of his own mouth before he could decide whether to say it.

"They knew my name," he said. "Before yours. Before any of it. Mara — they said to ask you what you never sold."
