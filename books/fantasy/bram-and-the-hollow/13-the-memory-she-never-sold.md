## 13. The Memory She Never Sold

They gave her a room with a door.

In the Hollow, Mara would learn, that was not a courtesy. It was a statement. Doors below the mountain were for people whose debts were still being weighed — the undecided, the unclaimed, the collateral in transit. The cell blocks had bars. The servitors' quarters had none at all, just alcoves open to the corridor like stalls. But this room, three turns of the lantern-glass after they took Bram, had a door, and it closed behind her with a sound like a coin dropped into a very deep well.

She had been counting by the lanterns since she arrived. The Hollow kept no honest hours; the light in the veins of the rock swelled and dimmed on some tide of its own, and the court's clerks moved through the corridors to that rhythm, ink-stained ghosts trailing their ledgers like brides dragging train. Three turnings since Ivo's betrayal. Three turnings since she had run through the Hall of Accounts with Wren's soul-token burning cold against her sternum and Bram's name dying in her throat, and had not gone back for him because going back meant both of them caged, and a thief who loses everything has nothing left to trade.

That was what she told herself, anyway. It was a good lie. Well-constructed. She'd have sold it gladly, if anyone below the mountain had a memory worth buying.

Mara sat on the edge of a bed that was softer than anything she'd slept on in Grayhollow and understood, finally, what the softness was for. Comfort was a leash made of velvet. They wanted her rested. They wanted her *thinking*. A tired prisoner rages; a comfortable one bargains.

The magistrates had not spoken to her since the seizure. Their summons came instead as a slip of paper folded into a moth's wing shape, left on her pillow each morning: *The court remains disposed toward resolution.* Resolution. Below the mountain even menace wore accounting language.

On the third turning, the moth-wing said something different:

*Come to the weighing room. Bring your most guarded thing.*

* * *

The weighing room sat at the heart of the Hall of Accounts, and Mara had seen it once before, from the wrong side, during the infiltration — a chamber where the ceiling opened into stone carved like the inside of an antler, branching and branching until the branches became too fine to follow. Scales hung there, dozens of them, brass and bone, none of them balanced. Each held something weightless on one pan and something worse on the other.

Ivo had told her once, in his clerk's deadpan, what the scales weighed. *Years against years. Names against names. And sometimes, when the debt is old enough, love against love.* He'd said it the way he said everything, flat as ledger paper, and only later did she understand he'd been describing his own sentencing.

Two magistrates waited beneath the antlered dark. Not the full bench — two, which meant this was off the record, which meant they thought they were being generous, or clever, or both. The nearer one wore robes stitched with the marks of the founding families, and its antlers had grown down around its jaw like a cage it had built itself. The other stood very still, hands folded, in the way of someone who had practiced patience for centuries and resented every hour of it.

"Mara Lockridge." The first magistrate's voice was dry as a page turning. "Memory-thief. Debtor. And now—" it tilted its head, antlers scraping the air, "—holder of stolen collateral."

"She isn't stolen." Mara kept her voice level. "She's a child. Her father died on a toll road trying to buy her back."

"Her father *contracted*," the magistrate corrected gently, the way one corrects arithmetic. "He sold her soul's bond to cover a debt of passage, then repented of the sale. Repentance does not void a contract below the mountain. You know this. You have spent your life above it profiting from similar instruments."

"I profit from *willing* sellers."

"Do you?" The second magistrate unfolded its hands. "And the widow in Candlemere. Did she willfully sell her daughter's face, or did hunger sell it for her? Did you ask? Or did you weigh the coin and call the weighing mercy?"

The question went into her under the ribs, precise as a pick. That was the thing about the Hollow's courts — they didn't lie. That was what made them monstrous. Every cruelty here was written down, witnessed, *accurate*, and accuracy was its own kind of violence.

"Why am I here?"

"Because you have something we want," the first magistrate said, "and we have something you want, and the thirteenth turn approaches. Four nights remain, Lockridge. On the thirteenth turn, the child Wren's bond converts. Servitude in perpetuity. Her soul enters the mountain's accounts as labor, and no power above or below can redeem her afterward. Unless."

"Unless."

"The court is prepared to offer an exchange." The magistrate extended one long hand, and a scale descended from the antlered dark between them, empty-panned, waiting. "Your sister's face."

The words landed in the silent room and Mara felt them land, felt them go through her like a blade finding the one gap in armor she'd forgotten she wore.

"No one told you about my sister."

"You are a memory-thief," the second magistrate said. "We are the keepers of all accounts. Do you imagine we do not know what you carry? Every thief keeps one theft she cannot complete. Yours is the last true memory of Elinor Lockridge's face. You have guarded it for nineteen years. You have never sold it. You have never shown it. You hold it the way misers hold a coin they dare not spend — and the court finds such hoarding *inefficient*."

Her sister's name in its mouth. Nineteen years since anyone above ground had spoken it aloud, and the first voice to say it again belonged to a thing with antlers.

"What would you give for it?"

"Wren's freedom. Complete redemption of the child's bond. She walks out of the mountain on your arm, whole, hers." The magistrate let the silence stretch. "One face. One soul. The arithmetic is simple."

Arithmetic. Everything below the mountain was arithmetic, and the horror of it, the thing Mara had been circling since she first read the courier's shifting map, was that the arithmetic was *correct*. One memory against one soul. She had traded lesser things for greater a hundred times. She had taken a merchant's first love and called it commerce. She had watched a widow weep over a purchased past and called it mercy.

Now the market had turned its eye on her, and the price was hers.

"I need time," she heard herself say.

"Four nights," said the magistrate. "But not five. Nothing below the mountain waits past the turn."

* * *

Her room again. Door closed. Coin dropped down the well.

Mara sat on the velvet leash of a bed and did the thing she was best at in all the world: she took inventory.

The memory was easy to find, the way her own heartbeat was easy to find. She kept it deeper than her trade-stock, deeper than the stranger-memories she bought and sold like a fishmonger's iced catch. It lived in a place she never went except to check the locks, and she checked the locks nightly, had done so for nineteen years, a ritual as compulsive as prayer and far more honest.

Elinor. Eight years old and already taller than Mara had been at ten, all elbows and freckles and a gap in her smile where a tooth had lost an argument with a fence rail. Summer, the year the fever came. Mara could remember everything around the face — the smell of cut hay, the sound of their mother singing through the floorboards, the exact weight of her sister asleep against her shoulder on the last night. But the face itself, the actual living face — she had caught herself losing it within a year. Grief had eaten the center out first, as grief does, and so Mara, twelve years old and already learning that loss was a kind of poverty, had done the only thing she knew how to do.

She had memorized harder. She had built the face a vault. And in doing so she had frozen it — one expression, one angle, one afternoon's light, preserved like a moth in amber while the real girl would have gone on changing, laughing new laughs, growing lines of her own. Somewhere in nineteen years of guarding, Mara had begun to understand that the vault was not preservation. It was taxidermy. But it was *hers*, the last thing in the world that was entirely hers, unsold, unbought, unspent.

A thief's rule, her rule, the one she'd built her whole trade upon: *never keep what you steal.* Because keeping was how you got caught, keeping was how the taken thing rotted inside you, keeping was how a girl who stole memories became a woman with nothing of her own but one ambered face and an armor of loneliness worn so long she'd mistaken it for skin.

And now two children.

Bram, who had no yesterday, sitting in a cell somewhere beneath her feet with his wrist bare of glowing ink because the magistrates had bound it, humming a lullaby he couldn't place to a sister he couldn't remember. Wren, whose soul-token Mara carried against her heart like a coal wrapped in cloth, cold and patient and *waiting*, the way debts wait. The courier had pressed them into her hands with his blood going tacky between their fingers and she had said, aloud, over his body, *I never agreed to this.*

She had believed it, then. Standing in the weighing room, she found she no longer did. Somewhere between the toll road and the gate that eats names, somewhere between arrow-fire stitched trust and a bazaar of unpaid dead, the agreement had happened without her signature. That was the ugly secret of responsibility: it didn't wait for consent. It arrived the way weather arrived, and you were either shelter or you were rain.

If she paid, Bram stayed caged. If she paid, she walked out with Wren and the mountain kept the boy — the boy whose missing memories were the map's missing key, the boy who was evidence, the courier's brother, a man the court had already hollowed once and would hollow forever. And the lullaby. Gods, the lullaby. She'd known it before he ever hummed it for her. She'd known it from the courier's effects, from a scrap of notation in his pack, and she had said nothing, because saying something would make it true that these broken things were *hers now*, and Mara Lockridge had spent nineteen years refusing to own anything that could be taken.

The ambered face flickered behind her eyes. Elinor, eight years old, gap-toothed, mid-laugh, forever.

*Nineteen years,* she thought, *and you've never once looked at her without checking the locks afterward. Is that love? Or is it hoarding?*

The magistrates had asked the wrong question, or rather the right question with the wrong arithmetic. One face for one soul was simple — but souls didn't come singly anymore. They came in pairs now, in sets, a boy and a girl bound together across a wiped mind and a wax token, and Mara was the only hinge between them, and hinges don't get to choose which door stays closed.

She lay back on the bed and did the last thing she expected to do in the Hollow's velvet guest-room: she laughed. Quietly, bitterly, at the ceiling's carved dark. The greatest memory-thief in Grayhollow, cornered at last by the oldest trick in any market — the seller asked to become the goods.

There had to be another way. There was always another way; she'd built a career on the spaces between contracts. But every path she mapped required Bram free, and Bram was behind the court's deepest locks, and four nights was not enough time to steal a boy, forge a claim, and cheat a court that had been practicing cruelty since before her grandmother's grandmother learned to count.

Unless she stopped playing their game. Unless the exchange was real, and freely given, and therefore — her mind snagged on it, thief-sniffing-a-lock — binding in ways the magistrates hadn't anticipated. Old law honored willing payment. The widow had told her that in Candlemere, her hands steady around her tea: *the court honors forged or stolen claims, but it must honor a gift. Even the mountain bows to a gift.*

Even the mountain bows to a gift.

Mara sat up slowly in the dark, and for the first time since the courier died at her door, she felt the old familiar warmth kindle behind her ribs — not dread, not grief, but the low bright fire of a plan assembling itself out of parts nobody else could see fit together.

She would pay. She would stand in the weighing room and open the vault and let her sister's face go, freely given, and the law below would have to honor it — would have to release *both* children, because a gift freely given could not be partitioned, could not be taxed, could not be entered piecemeal into any ledger ever written. That was the flaw in their arithmetic. That was the seam in the lock.

And if she was wrong — if the magistrates took the face and kept the boy anyway — then she would do what she did best.

She would steal him back.

Mara rose, crossed to the narrow window slit where the mountain's vein-light pulsed like a slow blue heart, and pressed her palm against the stone. Two turnings left. Somewhere below, Bram was humming to himself in the dark, and somewhere deeper, a scale waited with one pan empty.

"Keep the change," she whispered to the mountain, and began to dress.
