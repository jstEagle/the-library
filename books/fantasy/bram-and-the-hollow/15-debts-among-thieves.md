## 15. Debts Among Thieves

Mara had spent her whole life learning the exact weight of a favor.

Not the weight of gold — gold was honest, it sat in your palm and told you what it was. Favors were stranger currency. They changed mass depending on who owed them and how badly. A merchant's gratitude for a memory of his mother might weigh nothing at all until the day he needed a door opened, and then suddenly it was heavy enough to break hinges.

She walked through the Market of the Unpaid Dead counting weights.

Dawn below the mountain was a rumor — the great cavern ceiling glowed faintly mineral-blue, and the vendors called it morning because they had no other word for it. The bazaar was already stirring. Ghost-lamps guttered along the stalls of regrets. A woman made of smoke laid out her years on a cloth like fish for sale, each one labeled with a small paper tag: *nineteen, wasted on a man named Corvin*. A child-ghost turned somersaults over a tray of borrowed names, tasting them like sweets.

They looked up when Mara passed. Word had traveled faster than she had. The thief who'd walked into the Hall of Accounts and walked back out. The one the clerk-ghost had betrayed and then, somehow, not betrayed at all.

Mara hated being known. Being known was a debt too.

She stopped at the smoke-woman's stall first, because she'd bought passage from her once with a stolen summer and because Sera-the-smoke had been dead for two hundred years without ever being paid what she was owed.

"They took Bram," Mara said. No preamble. Preamble was for people with time.

Sera's smoke-face rippled. "The antlered ones. Yes. It's all through the market."

"He hid that child's soul inside himself. Wren. The courier's daughter. He's been carrying her for years without knowing his own name, and now the court has him, and on the thirteenth turn they'll crack him open like a lockbox and take everything." Mara set both hands flat on the stall's counter, and let the truth sit there where anyone could read it. "I'm going to get him back. And I'm going to unmake every entry in that ledger while I'm standing in the building."

Silence spread around her like spilled water. Vendors had stopped pretending to arrange their wares. The somersaulting child-ghost hung upside down in the air, watching.

"You can't fight the court," said a voice from the crowd — the lamenter of Stall Row, a ghost whose entire trade was grief and who did it beautifully. "The magistrates are older than the mountain's name."

"I know." Mara reached into her coat and drew out the thing she'd taken from the Hall of Accounts — not the master ledger itself; that was too well-guarded still, and she wasn't fool enough to carry the whole mountain's sins on her back. But she'd taken a page. A single page, copied in her own hand before Ivo's betrayal scattered them, and it was the right page: the record of a village called Aldenmere, four hundred souls, purchased for three coppers each by the founding magistrates, entered as *voluntary settlement*.

She held it up.

"The law below honors forged or stolen claims. That's their own rule, written by their own hands. So here is my claim, stolen fair from their own archive: these magistrates are thieves. Every year they've lived past their natural span belongs to the people of Aldenmere. Every judgment they've handed down was rendered by dead men wearing stolen time." She let the page catch the ghost-light. "I don't need an army. Armies march in daylight and die loudly. What I need is everyone this mountain has ever cheated — and I am given to understand that is *everyone*."

* * *

It should have been harder.

Later, walking the market rows with the copied page held like a lantern, Mara kept waiting for the refusal — the shrug, the *not my fight*, the cold arithmetic of people who survived by staying small. She knew that arithmetic. She'd done sums in it her whole life.

But the unpaid dead came to her the way water comes to a crack.

The smoke-woman pledged first — two hundred years of unlived life, hers to spend however she chose, and she chose this. Then the lamenter, who could weep so terribly that guards dropped their pikes just to make it stop. The name-taster child, who could slip into any locked room because no door in the Hollow had ever been built to keep out something so small. A ferryman of the underground river whose tolls had been taxed into nothing. A bone-carver, three sisters who sold echoes, a man who had traded his shadow for his son's fever and lost both.

By midmorning-by-rumor, Mara stood on the Well Steps — the market's high place, where auctioneers cried their souls — and looked down at forty ghosts and half-ghosts and the living poor of the Hollow's outer galleries, and felt something she hadn't felt since her sister used to fall asleep against her shoulder.

Not alone. The absence of that weight nearly buckled her.

"I have favors called in from the surface too," she told them. Her voice carried strangely in the cavern, off stone that remembered everything. "And I'll tell you the truth about them, because you've been lied to by professionals and deserve better: most of them are thin. Grudges, mostly. Small mercies. I've spent years making sure nobody owed me anything real, because debts are how people get hold of you." She folded the copied page and put it away. "So if any of you are waiting for me to promise you victory, or payment, or even survival — I can't. I can promise you that when this is done, the ledger will say what actually happened. To Aldenmere. To Wren. To every soul down here that was ever entered as collateral by someone richer. That's all I have."

An old ghost near the front — the bone-carver, his hands yellow as tallow — raised one of those hands.

"That's more than the court ever offered," he said. "We're in."

* * *

The villagers of Candlemere came down the smuggler's road at dusk, and Mara knew them before she saw faces, because she knew the way mountain people walked — weight forward, always ready for the ground to betray them.

Hetta led them. The widow of Candlemere, seventy years old and straight as a pine, with her daughter's memory bought back from Mara six years ago wrapped somewhere safe beneath her shawl. Behind her came perhaps thirty villagers, and Mara's stomach turned when she saw what they carried: woodcutting axes, fish-gaffs, iron pry-bars from the mine that had already killed half of them.

"You shouldn't be here," Mara said, when Hetta reached the bottom of the Well Steps. "Candlemere paid its debts. This isn't your fight."

Hetta looked at her the way grandmothers look at liars, which is to say: fondly, and straight through.

"Six years ago a girl came to my door in the rain," Hetta said. "Sold me back the memory of my daughter's birth, which I'd pawned to feed us that winter. Charged me half what it was worth and told me it was because the baby in the memory was ugly." Around her, villagers rumbled something that was not quite laughter. "I've known since that night that you were a thief with a hole where her heart should be. And I've watched you walk into this mountain after a child you never met, carrying a dead man's map, and I'll tell you what I told my neighbors when I woke them at dawn—" she turned, raising her voice to the gallery, to the market, to the blue false dawn of the cavern roof— "*that hole has grown a floor.*"

"Hetta—"

"Thirty-one of us. Two of my sons. The miller, who owes you nothing but likes a fair fight. And the ferry-wife, who knows every crossing under Thornfell and can move your ghost-army on water without a single lantern lit." Hetta pulled off one glove finger by finger, deliberately, and held out her bare hand. "You called in favors, girl. This isn't one. This is family showing up, and family doesn't ask which side of the blanket it was born on."

Mara took the hand. Her eyes were doing something she refused to acknowledge in public.

"The Vanes will come for anyone who helps me," she managed. "The guild doesn't forgive."

"The guild," said a new voice, "is about to have a staffing problem."

* * *

He came down the steps with his hood thrown back and his guild badge already torn in half, hanging from the pieces of the pin. Mara knew him dimly — Rask, a mule-driver on the southern runs, one of the gray-faced hundreds who did the Vanes' actual work while the family counted coin in Grayhollow. He wasn't alone. Behind him, hesitant as men approaching a shrine they weren't sure was allowed, came maybe twenty more. Smugglers. Porters. A fence. Two guild couriers who wouldn't meet anyone's eyes.

"We heard the Hall was hit," Rask said. "We heard the Vanes hold shares in a bonded child. Shares." He spat the word like gristle. "I've carried crates for them eleven years. My brother carried loads till the mountain took his leg. Nobody ever told us the cargo was *souls*."

"It wasn't your knowing that was for sale," Mara said. "Only your silence."

"Then consider the silence ended." He unfolded a paper — a guild route-manifest, stamped with the Vane seal. "Every low-man on the south runs is here or coming. We're not soldiers. But we know every bolt-hole, every blind tunnel, every guard rotation on the approaches to the Hall of Accounts, because we've spent eleven years moving contraband around exactly those things." His jaw worked. "You showed the market the ledger page. Show us. All of it. Let us see our masters' handwriting."

Mara gave him the copied page of Aldenmere, and watched twenty faces change as they read — watched the last of their servitude die quietly somewhere behind their eyes.

When Rask looked up, he wasn't a guildsman anymore. None of them were.

"Tell us where you want us," he said.

* * *

They gathered after, in the hollow beneath the Well Steps where the market's forgotten kept their meetings — ghosts and villagers and defected smugglers pressed together in the close dark, breath and smoke mingling, and Mara stood on a crate and laid out the plan she'd been building since Ivo's face had gone gray and purposeful in the mineral light.

"The assault is noise," she said. "Rask's people and the ferry-wife's crossings move on the Hall's approaches at moonrise-twelfth — visible, loud, everywhere and nowhere. The guards are few and immortal, and immortals are cowards; they will defend the ledger, not the doors, which means the ledger's flank goes thin. That's when the name-taster child walks through whatever wall remains and opens the postern from inside. Hetta's villagers hold the corridors. The dead do what the dead do best—" she nodded to the lamenter, to the echo-sisters— "they haunt. Every magistrate's memory of this night should be terrible. That's a gift. Take it."

"And you?" asked Sera-the-smoke.

Mara reached into her coat and brought out the last things she owned that mattered: the soul-token, warm as a heartbeat, and beside it a scrap of paper where she'd written, in her own hand, the words to a lullaby she'd sworn she'd never sell and would now use as a key.

"I go in through the routes only a memory-thief could find," she said, "to the master ledger itself. And I finish what I started in that hall before Ivo sold us." She looked around the dark at the upturned faces — the dead, the cheated, the overlooked, her strange ragged family assembled out of every debt she'd ever thought was a burden. "The magistrates bought their forever with a village. Tomorrow night, I take the receipt."

A low sound moved through the gathered dark, ghost-light and lamplight and held breath — not a cheer. Something older. An oath, maybe, or just a hundred hard-used people agreeing at once.

Then, above them, through miles of stone, the great moon began its climb toward the twelfth turn, and Mara felt the token pulse against her palm like something answering.

And from the mouth of the meeting-hollow, a sentry called down, breathless:

"Riders on the upper road — guild colors — a full company, and they're flying parley white—" a pause, and then, disbelief cracking his voice— "it's the Vane heir herself. She's come alone under truce. And she's asking for the thief."

* * *

Mara climbed the steps slowly, token hidden, lullaby folded over her heart, and came out onto the Well Steps into the blue cavern light where Lady Vane sat a fine horse on the upper road with her hands visibly empty and her eyes fixed on Mara with an expression Mara had never once seen on a Vane face.

Fear.

"My family's shares," Lady Vane said, loud enough for the whole market to hear, "are the smallest part of what the ledger holds against us. And I've come to tell you what else is written there — before you burn it. Because there's an entry in that book, Mara Lockridge, with your sister's name on it, and you are going to want to read it before the moon turns."

The token went cold against Mara's heart.

"*Bring her up,*" Mara said.
