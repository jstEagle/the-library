## 16. The Twelfth Turn

The cell did not have walls so much as it had opinions.

Bram had learned that in the first hours. The stone around him was not inert; it leaned. It listened. When he pressed his palm against it, the rock grew warm and faintly pleased, like a dog that had caught a scent, and somewhere far above — or perhaps far below; direction meant nothing here — something made a note of his touch in a book he could not see.

He sat with his back to the wall anyway. Even a listening prison was better than standing.

They had taken everything from him again, and this time they had done it politely. Two clerks with gray gloves had come at the turn of the moon, bowed, and said, *The court requires your presence,* and when he refused, they had simply waited, patient as water stains, until the refusal rotted out of him. They had not beaten him. They had not needed to. They had asked him questions instead — soft, reasonable questions about things he could not remember — and every question had been a small hook, and by the end of it he had walked into the cell himself, because walking seemed better than being carried.

Now there was only the dark, the warm listening stone, and the lullaby.

He hummed it without deciding to. That was how it always came — uninvited, rising through him like groundwater. Four notes up, two notes down, a pause where a name should go. He had hummed it in Grayhollow's undercrofts while rats watched him with their coin-bright eyes. He had hummed it on the toll road while Mara pretended not to listen and failed, her hands going still on the reins. He had hummed it at the gate that ate names, and the gate had hesitated, he was almost sure, as if it had heard the song before and couldn't place from whom.

*Four notes up, two notes down.* Somewhere in the mountain, someone had sung it to him. Or he had sung it to someone.

He closed his eyes and tried, for the ten-thousandth time, to see her face.

Not Mara. The other one. The smaller hand in his, the one that appeared in his dreams holding a paper lantern in a courtroom full of antlers. He knew she was real the way you know a scar is real — not because you remember getting it, but because it aches when the weather turns. The magistrates had called him *brother* once, in that velvet courtroom voice, and the word had gone through him like a splinter working toward the heart. Brother. Evidence. Asset. They had used all three words in the same breath, and only one of them had felt true.

*Brother.*

The stone warmed under his shoulders. Listening. Recording.

"Go on, then," he muttered. "Write it down. Write down that I don't know anything."

But that was the lie, wasn't it. That was the lie he'd been telling since Grayhollow, since the undercroft, since the first morning he'd woken with no yesterday and decided — with the cold practicality of a boy who had nothing else — that not knowing was survivable. You can live without a past. He had proof. He was the proof. You build a today out of stolen bread and borrowed corners, and you tell yourself the hole behind you is just weather, just distance, just the road you happened not to take.

Except the hole had edges. And lately, in the dark of this cell, the edges had started to glow.

* * *

It began with the light.

There was no window. There was a seam in the ceiling where a pale, sourceless radiance leaked through — the Hollow's own light, the color of old paper, the light of a place that had never seen a sun and didn't miss it. It fell across the floor in a thin blade, and twice a day — or what he guessed were days — it moved, sliding an inch along the stone like the shadow of a sundial in a world without suns.

On the twelfth turn, the blade of light touched his wrist.

And the ink woke up.

Bram looked down and saw the map moving. Not flickering — *moving*, the way water moves, the way a sleeping animal's flanks move with breath. The courier's shifting ink, the tattooed roads that had hidden themselves from Mara, crawled over his skin in slow rivers, and where they flowed they left warmth behind, and where they left warmth they left — 

— *a door.*

Not a picture of a door. A memory of one. It arrived whole, without warning, the way ice cracks: a green door with a rusted latch, in a wall the color of honey, and above it a carved bird whose wings had been worn smooth by a child's fingers touching them every single morning for years. His fingers. He knew they were his fingers because he could feel them — small, sticky with jam, reaching up, reaching —

The vision snapped shut like a trap.

Bram sat very still. His heart was slamming. The ink on his wrist had gone still too, lying quiet as a cat that has decided you are not worth the trouble.

"No," he said aloud, to the cell, to the stone, to whatever clerk was writing all this down. "No, come back."

The stone warmed, savoring his distress. He hated it. He hated it the way you hate a witness.

But the door had done something the darkness hadn't. It had left a crack. All these months the past had been a sealed room, and now there was a crack in it, and through the crack came a smell — beeswax and woodsmoke and rain on a tin roof — and a sound, distant, half-remembered: four notes up, two notes down, sung by a woman's voice in a kitchen while two children chased each other between the table legs.

Two children.

He pressed both palms flat against the floor. "Again," he whispered. "Please. Again."

The stone said nothing. The light crept its inch. But inside his skull, quietly, treacherously, the crack widened on its own.

* * *

They came for him once more before the turn completed itself. He heard them coming — the Hollow announced its officials the way thunder announces lightning, a low pressure change, a hush in the stone — and he arranged himself on the cot, limbs loose, eyes dull, the picture of a broken asset. Let them write *docile* in their ledger. Docile got fed.

The gray-gloved clerk peered through the bars. Behind him stood one of the magistrates — Bram could tell by the antler-shadow thrown long and branching across the corridor wall, crowned points swaying with each step like a forest deciding to walk.

"The thirteenth turn approaches," the magistrate said. Its voice was beautiful the way a frozen lake is beautiful. "Your sister's debt converts at moonrise. Her soul passes into permanent servitude. Unless—" the antlers tilted, considering him, "—you have remembered something worth trading."

"I remember nothing," Bram said.

"Mm." The magistrate let the silence stretch until it creaked. "That is what you were purchased to say."

Then it was gone, and the hush lifted, and Bram lay on the cot shaking with a rage that had no address.

*What you were purchased to say.*

He turned the sentence over and over, and each time he turned it, it cut him differently. Purchased. Not wiped in punishment — *purchased*. Someone had paid for his emptiness. Which meant his emptiness was worth something. Which meant—

Which meant the thing they'd bought him to hide was still in here.

The thought landed like a dropped lantern in dry grass. He sat up so fast the cot scraped stone. Of course. Of course, of course — the courier's map that answered to memories, the ink that glowed on his wrist, the way the gate had passed him through untouched because it considered him *empty* — no. Not empty. Sealed. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole trick. An empty vessel rattles. A sealed one keeps its cargo safe.

*Wren.*

Mara had said the name like it was fragile, back in the market of the unpaid dead, showing him the soul-token: a small glass teardrop on a leather cord, and inside it a light that pulsed — and he had thought, absurdly, *it pulses like a heartbeat, like a sleeping child's heartbeat* — and Mara had said, *the courier's daughter, held until the thirteenth turn,* and Bram had felt grief arrive before understanding, grief with no story attached, weeping for a stranger.

But she wasn't a stranger. She was the smaller hand in his. She was the lantern in the courtroom. She was the second child between the table legs, and the lullaby was hers, or theirs, sung by a mother whose face lived behind the crack in the sealed room.

And the token — the token was a decoy.

He didn't know how he knew. He knew the way he knew which walls in Grayhollow were hollow, the way he'd known the smugglers' hidden paths on the road south before Mara found them — some knowledge lived below memory, in the body, in the marrow. The token was glass and light and a clever forgery, a thing carried openly so that no one would look for the real bond anywhere else. The real bond — Wren's actual soul, her actual self, the whole living weight of her — was hidden where no thief would think to steal and no ledger would think to look.

Inside him. Inside the amnesia. Inside the wound they'd paid good money to make.

His hands were trembling. He pressed them against his knees and breathed, and the cell listened, and for once he was glad of it. *Write this down,* he told the stone silently. *Write down that the boy remembers nothing. Keep writing it. Every hour. Until moonrise.*

Because if the magistrates ever suspected — if they ever dug past the seal they themselves had installed — they would find their collateral sitting in plain sight, guarded by the one lock no court could pick: his own missing mind.

* * *

Sleep took him like an undertow, and for the first time since Grayhollow, the dream did not scatter.

He stood in the kitchen with the honey-colored walls. Rain on the tin roof. Beeswax and woodsmoke. The woman at the stove had her back to him, but he knew her shoulders, knew the way she swayed when she sang — four notes up, two notes down — and at the table sat a girl of perhaps seven, tongue between her teeth, drawing on the back of a bill of sale with a stub of charcoal.

"You're doing it wrong," she said, not looking up.

"Doing what wrong?"

"Hiding." Now she looked at him, and her eyes were brown and fierce and utterly familiar, and his chest cracked open along the fault line the cell had spent weeks mining. "You always hide things in the worst places. Under the loose board. Everyone knows about the loose board."

"Where should I hide them, then?"

She tapped her own temple with the charcoal, leaving a small black smudge, and grinned. "Somewhere nobody would ever want to look."

The kitchen shivered. The rain went silent mid-fall. And the dream — gently, the way you close a book you mean to finish — began to fold itself away, but not before she said one more thing, quick and low, the way people speak when they know the candle is guttering:

"They'll come for me at the thirteenth turn. Don't trade yourself, Bram. Whatever they offer, whatever they threaten — *don't trade yourself.* Find the thief. Trust the thief. She steals better than they do."

"Wren—"

But the name woke him, and he said it alone in the dark, and the stone recorded it, and he found he did not care.

*Wren.* His sister. His little sister, sold to the court beneath Thornfell, her soul caged in a hall of accounts — except not caged, never caged, because he had already moved her. He tried to grasp how, and the memory slid aside like wet rope, but its shape remained: a bargain struck in desperation, a clerk's quill, his own voice saying *take the memories instead, take all of them, wipe me clean and put her inside the blank space, no one steals from a man who owns nothing* — and a price agreed, and a seal set, and then Grayhollow, and bread, and corners, and a lullaby with nowhere to go.

He had done this. He had chosen this. Some terrified, loving version of himself had walked into the mountain and handed over his entire life as a hiding place for his sister's soul, and that version of him had been braver than anyone Bram had met since — braver than Mara with her armor of loneliness, braver than the widow burning candles for a daughter's face.

Courage recovered from ruin. His ruin. His courage.

Bram stood up in the dark cell and put his hand flat against the listening stone, and this time he did not hate it.

"Listen carefully," he said. "I want you to carry a message."

The stone warmed, eager, servile, a clerk to its bones.

"Tell them the asset is docile. Tell them he remembers nothing. Tell them whatever you like." He smiled, and it felt strange on his face, like wearing someone else's coat — someone bigger, someone who had volunteered for things. "But know this. At moonrise tomorrow, my sister walks out of this mountain. And if the court stands in the doorway, then the court is going to learn what a man with nothing left to lose does with the one thing they forgot to count."

Far off, muffled by miles of stone, a bell began to ring — deep, slow, counting.

The twelfth turn, ending. One night left.

And somewhere above him — he was certain now, certain as the lullaby, certain as his own name — Mara Lockridge was already moving.
