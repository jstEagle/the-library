## 17. Storming the Hall of Accounts

The twelfth turn's eve came down through the Hollow like ash.

Mara felt it before she saw it — a pressure behind her eyes, the mountain's slow pulse quickening, every lantern in the bazaar burning a shade too blue. The unpaid dead had stopped selling. The ghost-vendors stood at their stalls with their hands folded, watching the stairwells that spiraled up toward the Hall of Accounts, and in their hollow faces was something Mara recognized from the world above: the look of people deciding whether tonight would be the night they finally said *no*.

She stood on the balcony of Ivo's old counting-house — reclaimed now, its ledgers burned or turned to their cause — and looked out over the market as it armed itself for war.

It did not look like an army. That was its beauty.

There was the widow of Candlemere, gray-haired and iron-spined, drilling a dozen villagers in the use of mining picks and fish-gutters because no one in Candlemere owned a sword and no one had ever needed to. There were three of the guild's low-level smugglers, men who had carried Vane cargo up and down the toll road for years without ever being paid more than crumbs, their faces still carrying the bruised astonishment of men shown the ledger page where their own years were listed as assets. There were ghosts — dozens of them — who could not hold a weapon but could pass through walls, smother lanterns, whisper a magistrate's crimes into his ear until he wept. There were cheated vendors and unpaid dead and a one-eyed ferryman who had once rowed souls across the black river below the Hall and never been compensated for so much as a single crossing.

And there was Bram's absence, sitting in the middle of everything like a stone in Mara's chest.

*Tomorrow,* she reminded herself. *Tomorrow night is the thirteenth turn. Tonight we take back everything they use to keep us afraid.*

Ivo drifted up beside her. Even after weeks among the living, he had not learned to stop moving like a clerk — quiet, sideways, apologetic about the space he occupied. But his eyes, when he spoke, were not apologetic at all.

"The Hall will be defended," he said. "Not by soldiers. By procedure."

Mara almost laughed. "Procedure."

"You've seen the antechambers. Doors that only open for correctly filed petitions. Corridors that loop unless you carry the right stamp. The magistrates never needed guards, Mara. They built the building itself into a lock." He paused. "Which is why you must not go through any door. Not one."

"I know." She turned to him. "That's why I'm going under it."

* * *

Every building keeps memories. Most people never learn this, because most people never bother to listen to anything but other people. But Mara Lockridge had spent fifteen years learning how memory lived in things — in a locket, in a wedding ring, in the worn step of a staircase where a thousand feet had climbed toward a thousand small hopes. Walls remembered too. Stone remembered pressure, heat, fear. And the Hall of Accounts, raised on the bones of a crime no one was permitted to remember, remembered more than the magistrates knew.

So while her ragged army made noise at the front — and oh, they made noise; the widow of Candlemere had insisted on a proper assault, horns and fire-pots and villagers singing a hymn loud enough to crack glass, because "if we are to die, we'll do it audibly" — Mara went in beneath.

There was a service way, Ivo had called it: a channel cut through the living rock when the Hall was built, meant for carrying away the ash of documents too dangerous to burn openly. It ran under the foundations like a vein, narrow enough that Mara walked it bent nearly double, her palms flat against stone that sweated cold. The dark down there was old. It pressed against her like a hand.

And the stone whispered.

She had prepared herself for echoes, for drips, for the ordinary complaints of a mountain. What she got instead was memory, leaking upward through the rock the way water leaks through a roof. Not words, exactly. Impressions. A village. Green fields above ground — real ones, sunlit, somewhere on Thornfell's southern shoulder. Smoke. Screaming. The particular quality of silence that follows screaming and does not leave.

Mara stopped walking. Pressed her forehead to the stone. Let it come, because a thief knows better than anyone that you cannot steal what you refuse to look at.

They had been farmers. She understood that much — a whole community, perhaps two hundred souls, gathered into some valley by promises or by force, and the thirteen who would become magistrates had stood among them wearing robes that were not yet robes, holding contracts written in ink that was not yet ink. And the village had signed. Of course they had signed. People always sign, when the paper is put in front of them by someone with a kind voice and a seal. They had signed away debts, and then years, and then names, and then — the stone shuddered around her, remembering — everything. All at once. Two hundred lives poured out of two hundred bodies like wine from a punctured cask, and thirteen vessels waiting to catch it, drinking deep, growing smooth-skinned and ageless and *justified*, because they had done it lawfully. Every signature witnessed. Every clause observed.

Thirteen magistrates. Thirteen turns of the moon. The number had never been a coincidence; it was a signature, a brand, the founding crime stamped across every ritual the court performed.

Mara knelt there in the dark under the Hall of Accounts, dirt on her knees and grief in her throat that was not entirely her own, and felt something in her settle into place like a lock accepting its key.

She had told herself, since Grayhollow, that she was doing this for Wren. For Bram. For the courier who had died pressing a map into her hands. Good reasons. Personal reasons. Reasons a thief could carry without embarrassment.

But kneeling in the ash-channel, wearing a murdered village's last moments on her skin, Mara understood the truth with a clarity that felt almost like violence: the Hollow was not a strange country with strange laws. It was her world, perfected. It was every guild ledger and every debt-bond and every contract signed by someone who couldn't read it, refined to its purest form and dressed in antlers and ceremony. The law below was theft. Theft wearing robes, theft holding scales, theft calling itself justice for thirteen centuries while it drank a village dry.

And she was very, very good at robbing thieves.

"Two hundred," she said aloud, to the stone, to the dead, to no one. "I'll make them forget all thirteen of themselves before dawn. You have my word, and I know what my word costs, because I've spent my whole life refusing to give it."

The stone said nothing. But the whispering eased, just slightly, the way a crowd goes quiet when it has decided to watch.

* * *

The ash-channel ended behind a wall of the Hall's lowest cellar, and here was the first test, because even forgotten doors remember being doors. Mara stood before the blank stone and felt it noticing her — a slow attention, like something waking. Somewhere far above, horns were sounding. The widow's diversion had begun. Boots thundered across floors overhead; dust sifted down through seams in the ceiling; and the Hall, the great procedural beast, was turning all its attention, all its locks, toward the front door.

Which left the back of its mind unguarded. Which left her.

Mara closed her eyes and did what she had done in a hundred merchant's houses and a dozen noble estates: she listened for what the room remembered, and then she became it. The cellar remembered ash — decades of document-ash carried out through a seam that had long since been mortared shut. So Mara let the cellar's memory lie over her own like a second skin. Ash, she thought at the door. Dust. Nothing. A servant's errand, finished and forgotten. She reached into her satchel — her stock, her hoard, the stolen pasts of strangers — and pulled free a scrap of memory she'd lifted from a hall-sweeper's mind years ago in another city: the simple, bone-deep tedium of sweeping, the way a broom's rhythm empties a person into a tool. She held it against the stone like a sigil.

The wall sighed and opened. Not dramatically. Just a seam, then a gap, then darkness breathing out at her, cold and ink-smelling.

Inside the Hall of Accounts proper, chaos reigned — beautiful, deliberate chaos. Through a high grate Mara could see the entry hall lit by firelight, the widow's people pouring in behind a ram made of a felled mine-timber, ghosts streaming through the walls ahead of them to unlatch doors from within. Somewhere a magistrate's voice was ringing off the marble, invoking clauses, citing precedents, and the widow of Candlemere was answering him in the voice of a woman who had buried a daughter and run out of patience for paperwork: *"Your law says this. My law says otherwise, and mine has more hands."*

Mara smiled, thin and fierce, and climbed.

The master ledger lay at the heart of the Hall, Ivo had told her, in the Chamber of Original Entries — the room where every debt in the mountain was first set down, from which all copies flowed. To reach it she went the way only a memory-thief could go: through the building's recollections rather than its corridors. When a hallway remembered torchlight, she moved in the torchlight's blind spot. When a stairwell remembered a clerk's nightly rounds, she borrowed the clerk's gait, his stoop, his cough, until the stairs believed she belonged to them. Twice she passed within arm's reach of court bailiffs — tall figures in gray, their antlered shadows wrong on the walls — and both times she was, to their senses, nothing but a draft, a settling of dust, a memory of someone who wasn't there.

The third time, she passed a cell block, and heard Bram's voice.

Just one word. Her name, spoken low, from behind iron. Her whole body tried to turn, tried to break, tried to spend everything at once on that single syllable — and she gripped the wall and forced herself still, because a rescue wasted now freed no one. *Tomorrow,* she told him silently through the stone. *Hold on one more turn. You taught me how to wait inside someone else's story. Let me return the favor.*

She climbed.

* * *

The Chamber of Original Entries had no door. One simply arrived at it, the way one arrives at the bottom of grief, and Mara understood that too was a lock — designed so that only those invited by the court could ever find themselves standing before it. But the chamber remembered being found once, long ago, by a petitioner who should not have been there, and Mara followed that memory the way a hunter follows a broken branch.

The room was round and vast and dim, and the master ledger filled it.

She had imagined a book. She should have known better. This was the Hollow, where nothing was merely what it appeared to be. The master ledger was the room — its walls shelved floor to vaulted ceiling with volumes bound in gray hide, tens of thousands of them, each spine lettered in shifting ink like the courier's map, each one humming faintly with the trapped warmth of the souls whose accounts they recorded. In the center, on a plinth of black stone, lay a single open volume, enormous, its pages the size of doors. The First Entry. The founding account.

Mara approached it the way one approaches a sleeping animal.

The ink on its pages was still wet. After thirteen centuries, still wet — because the entry was not finished, could never be finished, while the debt it recorded remained unpaid. And the hand was elegant, magisterial, confident:

*Received, in satisfaction of all obligations outstanding: two hundred and six souls, entire, from the freeholders of Miren Vale, this first night of the new reckoning. Disbursed: thirteen shares, to the undersigned, in perpetuity.*

Below it, thirteen signatures. And below the signatures, in smaller script, the clause that had kept the whole edifice standing for thirteen hundred years — the clause the magistrates had surely believed no one would ever be fool enough or thief enough to invoke:

*This entry shall stand while its recorders remember it. Should the recorders' memory fail, all shares revert, and the account reopens.*

Mara read it three times. Then she began to laugh — quietly, helplessly, the laughter shaking loose something that had been wound tight in her chest since a dying man bled out on her doorstep. They had written their own doom into the founding instrument. They had been so proud of their law, so certain that whoever held the ledger held justice, that they had bound their immortality to the one thing in all the world that could be stolen.

Their own names. Their own remembrance.

"Reckoning," she said softly to the empty chamber, and the ten thousand ledgers hummed as if the word pleased them. "You wanted a reckoning. I've brought one. It just doesn't wear robes."

Somewhere above, the fighting reached a new register — stone cracking, the widow's hymn rising, a magistrate's voice cracking mid-clause. The Hall was falling, or being fallen. Either way, the magistrates would come here soon, to the heart, to defend the one thing that mattered. She had minutes. Perhaps less.

Mara cracked her knuckles, rolled her shoulders, and settled before the First Entry like a pickpocket settling before a fat purse. Fifteen years of stealing love and grief and first snowfalls and dying breaths had trained her hands for exactly this. But she had never stolen from thirteen immortal minds at once, through a ledger that was also a room that was also a prison, and she would need everything she had ever learned and several things she hadn't.

She laid both palms flat on the wet page.

The ink climbed her wrists like the courier's map coming home.

And then the chamber's light changed — went hard and silver and cold — and a voice she knew, aged and vast and furious, filled the round room from every wall at once:

*"Thief."*

The First Magistrate stood in the entrance, antlers scraping the vault, and behind him, filing in with terrible ceremonial calm, came the other twelve.

Mara did not lift her hands from the ledger.

"Took you long enough," she said.
