## 19. The Price of the Thirteenth Turn

The Hall of Accounts was dying around us, and it screamed in ink.

Bram felt it through the floor first — a tremor like a heartbeat losing its rhythm, the great ledger cracking along its spine where Mara's forgery had taken root. Shelves of bound debts shuddered. Somewhere above, an antlered magistrate howled, and the sound was wrong, hollowed out, a voice that had forgotten what it was for.

Wren hung in the air at the center of the court, wrapped in chains of pale script, her soul flickering like a candle guttering in a draft. Thirteen years old. He knew that now. He knew her laugh before he knew his own name — that was the cruelty of it, the shape of everything the mountain had taken from him. His sister's soul had been sleeping inside him all this time, curled behind the lullaby he could never place, and now she hovered ten feet away and he could not reach her.

*One payment remains,* said the oldest of the magistrates. Its antlers had gone gray as ash. Its robes were unraveling into loose letters that drifted down like black snow. *The bond must be satisfied or severed. Satisfied, and the child goes free whole. Severed, and she scatters with the rest of our ruin.*

"Then satisfy it," Bram said. He stepped forward before he could think better of it, before Mara's hand could find his sleeve. "Take me. I'm the one who hid her. I'm the one who owed."

He meant it. That was the strange part — standing in the collapsing heart of the Hollow, he meant every word. He had spent so long being empty that the offer filled him up like water filling a cracked cup. Here, finally, was something to be *for*. A boy without a yesterday could at least buy someone else their tomorrow.

The magistrates turned toward him. All nine. Their eyes were ledger-lines, columns of figures scrolling behind them.

*A volunteer,* the eldest said. Something like hunger moved through its ruined voice. *A soul freely offered satisfies any bond. It is the oldest term we have.*

"Don't you dare," Mara said.

She was beside him — when had she gotten there? She always did that, arrived at his shoulder like a knife sliding home, quiet and certain. Her face was streaked with soot from the Hall's burning doors, and there was blood on her knuckles that might have been hers, and she looked at him the way no one had ever looked at him: as if he were not evidence, not collateral, not a debt. As if he were a person she had chosen.

"Bram." Her voice dropped low, just for him. "You don't get to do this. I found you. That makes you mine to look after, and I don't give back what I've stolen."

"You can't pay for her," he said. "You've seen what they take. Years. Names. You don't have—"

"I have one thing left."

And Bram understood, then, what she was going to say, and the horror of it landed in his chest like a stone dropped down a well. He had watched her guard it through every mile of the passes, through the gate that ate names, through the market of the unpaid dead. He had seen her touch the memory the way other people touched a wound — gently, constantly, without looking.

Her sister's face. The last thing she owned.

"No," he said. "Mara, *no.* There has to be another way. Take my memories — all of them, whatever's left, take the lullaby, take Wren's face if I ever even knew it —"

"The law is the law," said the eldest magistrate, and for once Bram heard something almost like fear beneath the formality, because the law was all they had left, and the law required what it required. *Freely given outweighs freely taken. It always has. It is the one clause we cannot forge.*

"That's why," Mara said softly. "Don't you see? They built a court where nothing counts unless it's stolen or owed. Where love only matters if it's been seized as collateral. But there's one loophole they wrote themselves and could never close." She smiled, and it was the saddest thing Bram had ever seen. "*Given.* Freely given outranks everything. Even them."

The Hall groaned. Somewhere in the dark, the freed souls of the drowned village were still rising, embers climbing toward a sky none of them would ever see again, and the magistrates were coming apart letter by letter because Mara had made them forget their own crimes and very nearly their own names.

"There's no other way," she told him. "I've looked. Believe me — I've spent my whole life looking for other ways, and this isn't one of them. It's just the way."

"Mara—"

"Hold my hand," she said. "If I'm going to do this, I want to do it holding onto something real."

* * *

He held her hand.

It was rougher than he expected, small, warm, trembling only slightly — and he realized she was afraid, truly afraid, in a way she had never let him see on the road south, not under arrow-fire, not at the gate, not in the Hall with its antlered horrors. This was the fear of someone about to lose the last room in the house of herself.

"I need you to remember it for me after," she whispered. "Not the face. I know you can't. But remember that I had it. Remember that I loved her. When I look at the space where she used to be and feel like screaming, remind me that the space exists because something precious lived there. Can you do that?"

"Yes," Bram said, and his voice broke on it. "Yes. I swear it."

She turned to the court. The eldest magistrate dragged itself forward, trailing ribbons of ruined script, and extended a claw like a quill-nib.

*Name the memory,* it rasped. *Speak it plainly. Freely given requires full knowledge of the gift.*

"My sister Liss," Mara said, and her voice did not shake, and Bram marveled at that even as it broke him. "Liss Lockridge. Seventeen years old when I last saw her. I remember her face. I have carried it for eleven years through every city and every crime and every cold bed in Grayhollow, and I never sold it, though I could have bought a house with it, though men offered me fortunes. I kept it because keeping it was the only honest thing I ever did." She drew a breath. "I give it away now. To save a child I met four days ago. Because that's what she would have done, and because someone has to show this rotten mountain what a gift looks like."

The quill-claw descended and pressed against her forehead.

Bram felt it happen. He didn't know how — some residue of the courier's magic still tangled in his veins, maybe, or maybe the Hollow simply let witnesses feel its transactions, the way a courtroom lets a gallery hear a verdict. He felt the memory lift out of her like a splinter drawn from flesh, whole and shining, and he felt the moment the space where it had been went dark.

Mara swayed. He caught her arm.

"It's done," she said. Her eyes were wide and wet and unfocused, staring at something that wasn't there anymore. "It's — I can't—" Her breath hitched, a small terrible sound. "There's a hole. Right behind my eyes. I know there was something beautiful there and I can't—"

The chains around Wren shattered.

They fell as light, as script unbinding itself, and Wren came down out of the air like a bird released, and Bram caught her — his arms knew how even if his mind did not — and she was solid, warm, alive, her heart hammering against his chest like a fist on a door.

"Bram?" she said, pulling back to look at him, and her eyes went wide. "Bram. You're — oh, you're so *tall*, you were little, you were little when they took me—" Her hands flew to her mouth. "I remember you. I remember pieces. The lullaby — Mama used to sing—"

"The one about the river," he said stupidly, tears running down his face. "The one about the river and the willow."

"*Both of us under the willow,*" they sang together, half-remembered, ragged, and the sound of it rang through the dying Hall like a bell through fog.

But Bram kept one eye on Mara over Wren's shoulder.

She stood apart, straight-backed, chin up, the thief to the end — but her hands were shaking now and would not stop, and she kept pressing two fingers to the space between her brows as if she could hold the door shut on a room that no longer existed.

*The bond is satisfied,* the eldest magistrate intoned. It was barely holding together now, its antlers crumbling to dust, its voice thinning to a whisper of arithmetic. *Freely given... outweighs... freely taken...* And then, in the last of its voice, something almost human, almost wondering: *Eleven years... for a child she never met. We never understood... that such payments existed outside our books.*

"We're leaving," Bram announced. He put Wren's arm over his shoulder and reached for Mara with his free hand. "All three of us. Now, while the doors are still doors."

Mara looked at his outstretched hand. Then at Wren. Then at the space behind her own eyes.

"I can't see her face," she said quietly. It wasn't a complaint. It was worse than a complaint — it was inventory, a thief counting what the job had cost. "I keep reaching for it and there's just... dark. I know I loved her. I know she laughed. I know she had a scar on her eyebrow from falling out of the pear tree, because I remember knowing it, but the face behind it is—" Her voice finally cracked. "It's gone. It's really gone. And I'd do it again. That's the worst part. I'd do it again and I hate that I would and I'd *do it again*."

"I know," Bram said.

"You don't. You can't remember anything either, but yours was *taken*, mine I *handed over*, and that's different, that's—" She stopped. Closed her eyes. When she opened them, they were clear again, hard again, and only someone who had watched her for thirteen days would have seen the grief underneath like a stone under shallow water. "Well. No use standing in the ruins feeling sorry for myself. Come on, children. Let's go see the sky."

* * *

They climbed.

Behind them the Hall of Accounts folded in on itself, shelf by shelf, debt by debt, ten thousand years of collateral burning cold and blue as the magistrates' power unraveled. Freed souls streamed past them in the corridors like fish swimming upstream, brushing against Bram's skin with touches like warm rain. One of them paused — a woman in the tattered shape of an apron — and pressed her palm briefly to Wren's hair in blessing before streaming upward toward the mountain's throat.

"She knows me," Wren whispered. "From the cells. She used to sing to the caged lights so they wouldn't be scared." Her grip tightened on Bram's arm. "Is this real? Tell me this is real. I dreamed about walking out of here so many times I don't trust it."

"It's real," Bram said. "I've got you. I don't remember much, but apparently remembering things was never my job. Keeping you was. So — I've got you."

Ahead of them, Mara walked without speaking, lighting the way with a ghost-lantern she'd bartered for in the market below. She moved like someone following a map printed on the inside of her eyelids, taking turns before the turns came, and Bram realized with a jolt that she was navigating by memory — the Hollow's memory, the routes only a thief who had crawled through a hundred minds would know.

At a junction where three tunnels split, she stopped. Stood very still.

"This one," she started to say, and then faltered. Her hand rose halfway to her brow again. "This one goes — there was a reason I knew this one. Someone showed me. Someone with—" She stared into the dark where a face should have been. "Damn it. Damn *it*."

"Mara." Bram came up beside her. "Hey. Look at me."

She did. Guarded already, armor going up, the loneliness settling back over her like a cloak she'd worn so long she'd forgotten it wasn't skin. He recognized it. He'd worn it his whole life — the whole length of it that he could remember, which was nothing, which was a lullaby and a reflex to survive.

"You gave it away," he said. "That means it's not gone. It means it's out there, in the world, doing what you wanted it to do. A girl gets to grow up because of it. That's not a hole, Mara. That's a door."

For a long moment she said nothing. The lantern flickered between them, and far above, faint as a promise, they could both hear wind moving through stone — open air, the mouth of the mountain, morning.

Then Mara snorted, wiped her eyes roughly with the heel of her hand, and said, "When did you get wise? Yesterday you couldn't find your own boots." She thrust the lantern at him and started down the tunnel. "Come on. Both of you. And Bram — when we're out, when we're somewhere safe, I'm going to ask you what she looked like. Not today. But someday. And you'll tell me everything, every detail, even the ones you invent, and I'll pretend to believe you."

"Deal," Bram said, and took Wren's hand, and followed the thief who had paid everything up the long road toward the light.

Behind them, deep in the mountain's roots, something vast shifted — the sound of a ledger closing forever, or perhaps of a new one opening, its first page blank, waiting to see what free people wrote on it.

Above them, dawn.
