## 20. Wren

**Bram**

The mountain was coming apart around them, and Bram could not stop looking at the girl in his arms.

She was small. That was the first thing. He had carried the weight of her for thirteen moon-turns without knowing it — a soul folded into the hollow of his mind like a coin sewn into a coat lining — and all that time he had imagined something larger, something that would explain why his chest ached when he hummed a lullaby he couldn't name. But she was small, and light as ash, and when her eyes opened they were gray-green like water over slate, and Bram knew them.

He did not know how he knew them. He knew them the way you know your own hands in the dark.

"Bram," she said.

His name. Not the name the magistrates had tried to hang on him down in the courtroom — not *courier's brother*, not *asset*, not *the vessel*. Just Bram, said the way someone says a word they've said ten thousand times, worn smooth at the edges from use.

"You know me," he managed.

"I know you." She frowned, and the frown was familiar too, a small crease between the brows that he had seen a hundred times in dreams he'd mistaken for nothing. "I know you, and I don't remember knowing you, and both of those things are true at once, and I hate it."

A laugh broke out of him, cracked and startled. Around them the Hall of Accounts groaned like a ship in a storm; shelves of ledgers were shedding their volumes, and every book that hit the floor burst into drifting motes of light, souls unbinding from their paper prisons. The antlered magistrates stood in a ring near the shattered dais, mouths moving, no sound coming out. They were trying to say their own names. They could not find them. Mara had taken them, entry by entry, and the law below had nothing left to stand on.

"Come on," Bram said. He got an arm under Wren and lifted — she weighed more than a soul should, less than a sister should, and both measurements hurt him somewhere he couldn't point to. "We have to get out before the ceiling decides it's owed something too."

"The ceiling isn't owed anything." Wren's voice was thin but steady. "Nothing here is owed anything anymore. That's what she did." Her eyes found Mara across the ruined hall — the memory-thief standing amid the storm of rising lights with ink-stained fingers and blood on her sleeve, watching the embers climb. "That's what the thief did."

"She has a name," Bram said.

"So do I." Wren touched the soul-token at her throat — the decoy, empty now, its silver gone dull as old rain. "I'm just going to have to steal mine back. I hear there's a professional nearby."

* * *

**Wren**

Wren remembered the dark first.

Not the cell — though the cell came later, the caged lights and the cold brass of the bars and the magistrate with the elk-antlers asking her, gently, terribly, *do you understand the terms of your father's contract?* No. Before that: the dark under Thornfell, the road down, her father's hand slick with sweat in hers, and his voice saying *whatever happens, don't tell them your whole name.*

She had been seven. She had told them anyway, because she was seven, and because they asked so kindly.

The shame of it rose in her now, whole and undimmed by thirteen years of being a light in a cage, and she gripped Bram's arm harder than she meant to.

"I told them my name," she said. "Bram, I — Papa told me not to, and I—"

"Hey." He stopped, right there in the collapsing corridor, dust sifting down through the glow of a thousand freed souls. He turned her to face him, and his face was a stranger's face and her brother's face at the same time, and that was its own kind of unbearable. "I sold my memories to hide you. Do you understand? Whatever you did at seven years old, I looked at it and decided the answer was to erase myself. We're even. We're more than even."

"That's not how debts work here."

"It's how they work with me." Something flickered across his face — a memory surfacing like a fish rising to break water, there and gone. He pressed his knuckles to his temple. "There was a kitchen. There was — flour on your nose. You were laughing at me because I'd gotten flour on *my* nose trying to wipe yours off. Mama was—"

He stopped. Wren felt the absence open between them like a door.

"Mama was singing," she finished quietly. "*Sleep, my hollow, sleep my own, the mountain keeps what's not yet known.*"

"*The river takes what's left to carry home,*" Bram said, and his voice broke on the last word, and then they were holding each other in the middle of a dying court while the mountain shed its stolen years around them like autumn letting go of leaves.

It was not enough. That was the thing no one warned you about — that reunion without wholeness was its own specific grief. She had her brother's arms around her and could not remember his laugh. She could feel that she loved him, the way you can feel a missing tooth with your tongue, the shape of the love present and the love itself gone. Somewhere in the unraveling of the Hall of Accounts, pages of their childhood were burning loose and rising, and they could not catch them.

But some things stayed. The flour on the nose stayed. The lullaby stayed. His hand, finding hers in the dark of the road down, stayed.

"We'll get the rest back," she said. "However we have to. However long."

"However we have to," he agreed, and did not ask her what it would cost, because they both already knew that everything below Thornfell cost, and they were done pretending otherwise.

* * *

**Mara**

Mara walked behind the siblings and let them have the corridor to themselves.

It was strange, being the third thing in a family's gravity. She had spent her whole life positioning herself at the center of rooms — the thief is only as good as her entrance — and now she trailed two children through a crumbling underworld, carrying nothing but a dead courier's map and the ache of a memory she'd given away hours ago. She reached, out of habit, for her sister's face and found the smooth blank wall where it used to live. She had known she would. Knowing didn't help.

The freed souls were everywhere now. They poured up through the stone in slow ribbons of ember-light, hundreds of them, thousands — the village the magistrates had bought with their founding crime, generations held in ledger-leather, all of them rising at once toward whatever waited above the rock. They passed close sometimes. One brushed her shoulder, and for half a heartbeat Mara smelled woodsmoke and bread baking, heard a woman laughing at something a man said, felt the ordinary warmth of a life that had been stolen and was now, finally, going home.

Her eyes stung. She blamed the dust.

"You're crying," said Ivo, drifting at her elbow. The clerk-ghost looked different since the ledger fell — brighter, less translucent, as if being witness to justice had thickened him somehow. His own name was still lost; he'd searched himself twice and come up empty. "For the record, I'm noting that you're crying."

"Noted and denied."

"They're going home," Ivo said softly, watching the embers climb. "Do you know how rare that is? In three hundred years of accounts, I never once recorded a debt forgiven. Only transferred. Only rolled forward. And you—" He shook his head. "You forged mercy into the master ledger. The mountain has no choice but to honor it. It's the most beautiful crime I've ever witnessed."

"Flatterer."

"Accountant," he corrected. "It's worse."

Ahead, Bram stumbled. Wren caught him — caught him the way siblings do, without ceremony, one hand flat on his chest — and Mara saw the boy go still, eyes wide, staring at nothing.

"What is it?" she called. "What did you see?"

"A door," Bram said slowly. "Green paint. Peeling at the bottom. There's a mark on it—" His hand moved unconsciously, tracing a shape in the air. "Three lines and a circle. Wren, does that mean—"

"Home," Wren whispered. "That's our door. That's—"

And then both of them gasped at once, the shared memory landing in two minds simultaneously, and Mara watched them look at each other with the particular shock of people discovering they are haunted by the same ghost. Pieces returning. Not in order, not complete, but *shared* — the way the map's ink had once shifted between the courier's skin and Bram's wrist, one memory living in two bodies.

"Well," Mara said, when she trusted her voice. "That's either very convenient or very cruel."

"Both," said Wren. "Probably both."

* * *

**Bram**

They surfaced from the Hollow at dawn, or what the mountain offered as dawn — a graying of the cave mouth's black, a thinning of the dark.

Behind them, the last of the embers rose. Bram turned once at the threshold and watched them go: the village souls streaming up through cracks in the rock, threading through stone like water running backward, and where they passed, the mountain itself seemed to exhale. Thirteen turns of dread, and this was how it ended — not with thunder, but with a slow bright migration, the unpaid dead going home.

One soul lingered at the edge of the stream. It hovered near Wren, and though it had no face, Bram felt it looking at her the way parents look.

"Papa?" Wren breathed.

The light pulsed once — yes, or goodbye, or *I'm proud of you*, all of it folded into a single flare — and then it rose, faster than the others, as if it had been waiting thirteen years and could not wait one moment more. It went up through the rock and was gone, and Wren made a small sound, and Bram put his arm around her shoulders, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing to say that the light hadn't already said.

Mara stood apart, arms crossed against the mountain cold, watching them with an expression Bram was beginning to be able to read. It was the look of a woman auditing a fortune she refused to count herself among.

"You could come with us," he said.

"With you where?"

"Anywhere. Candlemere. The toll road. Wherever you were going before a dying man bled on your doorstep." He hesitated. "You gave up your sister's face for us. That buys you a place, if you want it. That buys you anything."

Something moved behind Mara's eyes — the thief calculating, always calculating, and then, for once, choosing not to. "I didn't give it up for you," she said. "I gave it up because it was the only honest currency I had left. Don't make it prettier than it was."

"All right," Bram said. "Then come anyway. Make it ugly. Make it however you want. Just come."

From the ridge above, where the morning fog was lifting off the passes, came voices — the widow of Candlemere's rough alto, calling down that the villagers had fires lit and porridge on, and would the heroes of the Hollow *hurry up*, because heroes who arrived cold got no second helping. Ivo shot upward past them like a startled bird, chasing the smell of food he could no longer eat purely on principle.

Wren laughed — really laughed, and Bram's chest seized, because he still couldn't remember the sound from before, which meant this was the first one, which meant he got to keep it.

Mara looked at the mountain. Then at the children. Then, briefly, at the space beside her where a sister used to walk.

"Porridge," she said at last, as if conceding terms in a negotiation she'd lost from the start. "Fine. But I'm not staying past spring."

None of them believed her. She probably didn't believe herself. And high above, the last embers of the old village cleared the peak of Thornfell and scattered into the morning sky like sparks from a bonfire that had, at long last, been allowed to burn out.

Wren squeezed Bram's hand. "I remembered something else," she said. "Just now. A promise somebody made me, a long time ago." She smiled up at him, gray-green eyes wet and fierce. "Somebody said they'd never leave me in the dark again."

Bram's throat tightened. "Did they keep it?"

"They're keeping it right now," she said. "Come on. I want to see the sun I paid thirteen years for."

They climbed out of the Hollow together — the brother with no yesterday, the sister with too much of one, the thief learning to give, the ghost with no name, and behind them a mountain going quiet, its ledger closed, its debts, at last, forgiven.
