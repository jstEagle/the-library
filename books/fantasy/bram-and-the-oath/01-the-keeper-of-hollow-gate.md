## 1. The Keeper of Hollow Gate

There are three things you need to know about Hollow Gate, and I'll tell them to you in the order I learned them, which is also the order of importance.

First: the valley is beautiful, and it knows it. Second: nothing magical has ever survived a night inside its borders. Third: neither have I.

I learned the first thing as a child, catching trout in the Merrow with my sleeves rolled to the elbow and the water so cold it felt like an argument. I learned the second from old Keeper Aldous, who taught me the post before his lungs gave out—how he'd once watched a tinker's charm-lantern gutter and die at the boundary stones, only to flare back to life the moment the man carried it back out. And the third thing I learned gradually, over thirty years, the way you learn you're tone-deaf: not by one great disappointment, but by a thousand small ones.

My name is Bram Winterbourne. At least, that's what everyone here calls me, and I've never had reason to doubt it. I keep the gate at the valley's southern mouth—a stone arch older than any map I've seen, fitted with an iron door that hasn't opened in living memory, and a keeper's cottage that has opened for every traveler who's knocked. That's the whole of the job. The gate doesn't open; the kettle does. Between those two duties, a life fits quite comfortably.

* * *

Mornings begin with the ledger.

I was at my desk before the frost had thought better of itself, copying yesterday's entries into the fair book in my careful clerk's hand. *Tinker's cart, two mules, no goods of interest. Widow Marsh, visiting her sister, three days.* The valley sees perhaps forty travelers a season, and I record each one—the crown requires it, though I sometimes wonder what the crown imagines happens down here that needs recording. Smuggled turnips, probably. A dangerous quantity of jam.

The gift—that word still tastes strange, even now—shows itself in small ways when I write. Or it would, if the valley allowed. Outside these borders, I'm told, some folk can coax ink to move, or read the truth off a page like warmth off a stove. Mine is a modest talent: I can feel what's written. Run my thumb along a line and know whether the writer meant it, whether the words were set down in love or fear or boredom. A lie sits heavy under the fingers, like a stone sewn into cloth.

Or so I've been told. In thirty years inside the valley, I've felt nothing but paper. Damp paper, mostly. It's like being told you have a fine singing voice in a country where all the bells are broken.

"More tea than ink in this pot," Tam announced from the doorway, letting in a slab of morning cold. He had his axe over his shoulder and sawdust already in his hair, which meant he'd been working since before dawn and had come by chiefly to steal bread and complain about his wife's cousins, not necessarily in that order.

"You could knock," I said.

"I did. You didn't answer because you were talking to your book again." He dropped onto the bench across the desk, a big man gone soft around the middle, with a laugh that started in his belly and worked outward. We'd been friends since we were boys stealing apples from the vicar's tree, which meant he'd earned the right to enter my house uninvited, eat my bread, and call me a fool to my face. He exercised all three rights liberally.

"It's the fair book," I said. "Crown business."

"Crown business." He said it the way he said *tax collector* and *wet Tuesday*. "What does the crown want with a valley it can't even find twice?"

That was the joke we all made, and it was mostly true. The road down from the pass forgot itself; couriers took wrong turnings and ended up in Marrowdale by mistake. We paid our tithes through an agent in the lowlands who never visited. For all practical purposes, the crown was a story we told about ourselves, like the giant supposedly sleeping under the eastern fells.

Except this morning, apparently, the story had decided to visit.

* * *

It came up the road just past noon—a crown courier in royal livery, mud to the knee, leading a second horse stacked with satchels. I heard the hooves before I saw him, because in Hollow Gate you hear everything; sound behaves itself here, comes straight and honest, unlike everywhere else, if the travelers are to be believed.

He posted the decree on the gatehouse door himself, with wax and a stamp and the brisk efficiency of a man who wanted to be elsewhere before dark. Then he looked at me, looked at the gate behind me, and frowned slightly, the way people do when they're trying to remember something they've been told and can't.

"This the border post?" he asked.

"It's the gatehouse," I said. "Can I take your name for the ledger?"

He hesitated. I watched him glance back down the valley road as if checking it was still there. "Rellin," he said slowly. "Courier, fourth circle." Then, quieter, almost to himself: "Strange country, this."

"Country?" I said. "This is Hollow Gate."

"Yes," he said, and shook his head like a man clearing water from his ear. "Of course. Sorry. Long ride."

He drank one cup of tea standing up, refused bread, and left the way he'd come, walking his horse carefully, as though the road might shift beneath him. I copied his name into the ledger and noticed my hand had gone unsteady enough to smear it.

Then I read the decree.

* * *

It was written in the usual crown hand—all flourishes and certainty—and it took me three readings to believe it, not because the words were complicated but because they were monstrous in such a tidy way.

*By order of the Regent's Council: be it known throughout the realm that the hunting of the gifted is declared lawful and laudable. Let all persons possessing uncanny arts present themselves for registry by midwinter. Let all who conceal their nature be sought out, examined, and corrected according to statute. Lists of suspected persons have been compiled and forwarded to local authorities.*

Lists. I read the word four times before I understood it wasn't aimed at other people.

Because there it was, pinned beneath the decree in the courier's neat column, a page torn from some chancery ledger, and halfway down, in ink that had dried decades ago:

*Winterbourne, Quentin — keeper — Hollow Gate — born outside.*

Quentin.

I stood in my own doorway for a long while, long enough for the light to change, long enough for the kettle I'd forgotten to sing itself dry. The valley went on around me with perfect indifference—smoke rising from chimneys, the mill wheel turning, children shouting down by the ford—and I found I was looking at all of it the way you look at a room you're about to leave, cataloguing it without meaning to.

Born outside. Outside where? I had no memory of anywhere else. No memory of a mother's face or another sky or a door closing behind me. My history began at this gate, aged—I supposed—about ten, in Keeper Aldous's kitchen, eating bread and crying without knowing why. Before that, nothing. I'd always assumed the nothing was grief; grief does that, people said kindly, and I'd let them say it because it was easier than the alternative.

But *born outside*, written by a stranger's pen, in a stranger's ledger, years before I could have been on anyone's list—

And *Quentin*. Not Bram. Who was Quentin?

Here is the thing about my gift, the thing I have never said aloud in thirty years: I don't think it's broken. I think it's *waiting*. There's a difference between a bell that can't ring and a bell that isn't allowed to, and I have spent my whole life in a valley where every bell is muffled—including, evidently, whatever I am. The decree called my kind *the gifted*. The valley called me *Bram, the keeper, good man, quiet*. Both names fit like borrowed coats.

And the decree wanted me registered by midwinter. Examined. Corrected.

Tam found me there at dusk, still holding the page. He read it over my shoulder—he reads slow, and I heard his breath change when he reached my entry.

"That's not your name," he said.

"No," I said.

"Bram—" He stopped. I knew what he was stopping himself from saying, because I was stopping myself from saying it too: *they'll come for you.* He was thinking of his wife, his girls, the thin wall of respectability that keeps a family safe in a small place. I was thinking of a word I'd never used about myself until today.

*Gifted.*

"They've got the wrong man," Tam said finally, and his voice was doing something it almost never did, which was hoping out loud. "Some clerk's error. Quentin Winterbourne. Could be anyone."

"It says born outside," I said. "Tam, I don't remember being born anywhere."

He had no answer for that. Neither did I. The fire cracked, the valley darkened, and somewhere past the boundary stones, in a world I had never seen, someone named Quentin Winterbourne was waiting for me to become him.

I put the decree in the drawer with the fair book and locked it, and then I sat a long time listening to the gate.

It had never made a sound in all my years there. But that night, faint as breath against glass, I could have sworn the iron door whispered—one word, over and over, patient as winter coming down the pass.

A word that was not *Bram*.
