## 2. A Name in the Ledger

The courier's decree stayed nailed to the gatepost for three days before I took it down, and I'd like to say it was principle that kept me from tearing it off sooner. It wasn't. It was the nails. Crown-issue iron, driven deep by a man who clearly hated his hammer less than he hated the people he was posting about.

*By order of His Majesty: the hunt for the gifted shall commence at midwinter.*

Midwinter was six weeks away. Six weeks of frost-thick mornings and the same small rounds I'd kept for thirty years—oil the hinges, sweep the threshold, count the sheep that weren't mine through a gap in the wall as though they were. A whole life measured out in chores. I had loved it once, or something near enough to love that the difference never mattered.

Now every chore felt like evidence.

Because here is what the valley knew and I had never said aloud, not even to myself in the dark privacy of my own skull: the Hollow Gate kept magic *out*, and I had spent three decades assuming that was all it did. A wall against wonders. But walls work both ways, don't they? And there were moments—small ones, ignorable ones—that had begun, since the decree, to arrange themselves into an uncomfortable shape. The way frost never formed on my window while it silvered every other pane on the lane. The way Tam's daughter, when she was teething, would stop crying the instant I picked her up, like something in me had been turned down to a hum she could sleep to.

Small things. Ignorable things. Until a man with a list rode through your valley and made them a confession.

So on the third morning I did what any sensible coward does with a dread too large for his chest: I went looking for a smaller one.

* * *

The gatehouse archives lived under the east stair, behind a door that stuck in damp weather and hadn't been properly opened since old Keeper Aldous died, may his ledger rest easier than he did. The room smelled of tallow smoke and mouse ambition. Shelves ran floor to rafter, crammed with the accumulated paperwork of a border post nobody important remembered existing—which, given the crown's current enthusiasm for lists, struck me as either providence or irony, and I no longer trusted myself to tell those apart.

I brought a lamp and a stub of candle and told myself I was looking for precedents. Old decrees. Instructions for keepers who'd come before me, some guidance on what a man was supposed to do when the crown declared war on a quality rather than a country.

What I found instead, two hours in, at the bottom of a crate marked *PERSONNEL — DO NOT REMOVE* (a label that has never once stopped anyone), was the keeper's registry.

It was a fat book bound in green leather gone grey at the corners, and it recorded every keeper of the Hollow Gate back past the point where the ink faded into guesswork. I read names the way you read gravestones in a churchyard you're just passing through—half-attentive, faintly respectful. *Merrick. Ossler. Fenwick. Aldous.* And then, near the end, in the careful hand of whoever had entered the last record:

***Quentin Winterbourne. Keeper. Born outside.***

I sat down on the crate. My legs made the decision without consulting me.

*Born outside.*

Outside of what? Outside the valley, obviously—the valley, where nothing was born that didn't belong to it, where the sheep were born and the children were born and the fog came down the pass each autumn right on schedule. But I had no memory of anywhere else. Not a road, not a roof, not a mother's face. My earliest memory was this gatehouse: the smell of oil and cold iron, Aldous's cough through the floorboards, a wooden horse I still had somewhere in a drawer because I couldn't remember being given it and couldn't bear to lose it.

Thirty years in this valley and not one recollection of arriving. People arrive. There should be a cart, a road, a first sight of the pass. There should be *something*. Instead there was a hole where my history should have been, smooth-edged, like a stone worn round in a stream—and I had walked past that stone my whole life without once looking down.

You can live with a hole if nobody points at it. That's the trick of it. That's how we all get by.

The entry was dated the year I would have been nine. Nine years old, entered as keeper, which made no sense unless—unless the title meant something different then, unless *keeper* wasn't a job but a condition, like *orphan* or *ward*. Below the entry, someone had written a reference number, and the reference pointed to a folio, and the folio was—

Missing.

Not lost. Missing. I knew the difference within a minute, because I know this archive the way you know your own pockets. There was a gap on the shelf exactly one folio wide, dust everywhere except a clean rectangle where a document had lain undisturbed until recently, and then not. Recently. Within the season, I'd have sworn it. Someone had taken my history out of this room while I slept thirty feet above it, and they had done it carefully, and they had done it lately.

And here was the thing that turned my stomach from unease into genuine fear: the pages around it were wrong too. Folio forty-one jumped from a survey of the western fence line straight to a list of tolls from a year that hadn't happened yet when the survey was taken. Numbers out of order. A stitching job. Whoever removed my folio had taken others with it—not at random, but as camouflage, so the gap wouldn't look like a gap.

Someone had edited the record of my life with more care than anyone had ever shown my actual life.

* * *

Tam found me there at dusk.

Of course he did. Tam Halloway has an unerring instinct for wherever I am when I least want company; thirty years of friendship will do that, or perhaps it's simply that I'm predictable, which is the compliment I pay myself on bad days. He came down the stair with two bottles of cider and the particular cheerfulness of a man who has noticed something wrong and decided to fix it by pretending otherwise.

"There you are," he said. "Been looking since supper. Your lamp's lit down here like you're expecting company, and you've not had company since the tax man, and even he drank standing."

"Reading," I said, which was true, and held up the registry as proof, which was also true, and said nothing else, which was new.

Tam set the bottles on a shelf and looked at the crates. Then at the gap on the shelving. Tam is a farmer, and farmers see gaps the way hawks see movement—a ewe missing from a field, a fence rail down. He looked at that clean rectangle in the dust for a long moment, and I watched him decide whether to be the friend or the neighbor.

He chose wrong. Or I made him choose wrong. Thirty years on, I still haven't settled which.

"Something's been moved," he said. "Folio's gone. Wasn't gone at harvest festival—I'd swear it."

"You inventory my archive now?"

"I inventory everything, Bram, it's how I stay solvent." He crouched by the crate, turning pages of the registry himself, and I let him, because snatching it away would have been worse than any question. "Quentin," he read aloud. "That's you. Why's it say Quentin?"

"Because the crown spells names however it likes. You've seen the tax rolls. I'm 'Bramwell' half the time and 'Brum' the rest."

"But why two names?" Tam asked, gently, the way you ask a man if his wife is ill. "Why's it say *born outside*? You never said—" He stopped. Started again. "There's pages gone, Bram. Three, four folios, stitched over with nonsense. Who takes records out of a gatehouse archive?"

"A thief."

"What thief steals *your childhood*?" He laughed, but it came out thin, and neither of us pretended it was a real laugh. "The courier posted that decree and now pages go missing and you're sitting in the dark reading your own name in a dead man's book. Talk to me."

And I want to be honest here, in whatever corner of this account honesty survives, about what happened next. Because everyone believes they are truthful until the moment truth costs something, and I had believed it about myself for thirty years. I was the keeper. I kept things—hinges oiled, thresholds swept, the border between the valley and whatever waited beyond it. Truthfulness was just another thing I kept.

Tam looked at me with his open stupid loyal face, waiting, and I thought about the decree, and midwinter, and the clean rectangle in the dust, and I understood suddenly that questions are not harmless. Questions travel. A question asked tonight over cider becomes a fact repeated tomorrow at market becomes testimony by midwinter. And whatever I was—whatever that word *born outside* meant—I understood that the truth would not protect me. It would only give them a shape to hunt.

"Bram," Tam said. "Who took the pages?"

I lied.

It was astonishingly easy, which frightened me more than the missing folio did. The words came out smooth and warm as if they'd been waiting years for their turn. "I moved them," I said. "Weeks back. Reorganizing, after the courier came—didn't want crown eyes on old personnel papers if they came poking. They're at the mill, in storage. Nothing's stolen, Tam. You're chasing dust."

"The mill," he repeated.

"The mill. Come by Thursday and I'll show you, if your curiosity's got that kind of appetite."

And Tam—my oldest friend, who had taught me to fish and stood witness at my keeping-ceremony and named his second son after me—looked at me for a long, long moment. Long enough that I felt the lie sit between us like a third person. Long enough that I understood something else, something worse: he didn't believe me. He saw the seam where my life had been stitched, same as I'd seen the seam in the archive, and he was deciding, right there in the lamplight, whether to pull the thread.

"All right," he said finally, picking up his bottle, leaving the other untouched. "Thursday, then."

His boots went up the stairs. The door stuck, then gave, then shut.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat with the registry open on my knees while the candle burned down to a coin of wax, reading the entry until the letters stopped meaning anything. *Quentin Winterbourne. Keeper. Born outside.*

Here is the vertigo of an edited life, and I record it plainly so no one can accuse me of exaggeration: it is not like losing something. Losing has edges. Grief has edges—you feel the shape of the absence and you mourn against it. This was different. This was discovering the absence had always been there, that the room you've lived in all along has a wall where a window should be, painted over so smoothly you'd hung your coat on it. Every memory I owned suddenly seemed provisional. Had Aldous really coughed through the floorboards, or had someone *given* me that cough, planted it, the way a gardener sets a bulb? Was the wooden horse mine, or assigned?

And beneath the vertigo, quieter and colder, a practical thought, the sort that comes to a man at three in the morning wearing the face of common sense:

If my history had been taken out of this archive—if someone had gone to such trouble to stitch the record shut—then it hadn't been stolen. It had been *hidden*. And things are hidden by the people who put them there, or by the people who are afraid of them.

Which meant somewhere, possibly close, possibly already in the crown's hands, was a folio that explained why a nine-year-old boy with no past had been installed as keeper of a gate in a valley where magic could not follow.

The candle died. In the dark, the gate made its small settling sounds, iron cooling against stone, the sound I had fallen asleep to for thirty years and never once listened to.

That night, for the first time, I listened.

And I would almost swear—almost—that beneath the tick of cooling metal, something on the far side of the Hollow Gate breathed my name.
