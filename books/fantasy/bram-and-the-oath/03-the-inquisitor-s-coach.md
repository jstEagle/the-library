## 3. The Inquisitor's Coach

The coach came up the valley road on a Tuesday, which felt right. Nothing catastrophic ever happened on a Monday in Hollowmere; the valley seemed to have an arrangement about it.

I was mending the hinge on the gate's little postern door when the sound reached me—iron rims on packed dirt, and horses that weren't the shaggy, patient kind we bred here. These were tall, black, and unhappy, the way horses always look when they've been bought by the government.

I set down my mallet and watched it come. The coach was the color of a storm cloud that had been asked to be a coach, and on its door sat the crown's seal: a tower with no door painted on it. I'd always found that a strange bit of heraldry. A tower with no door. Now, standing there with grease to my elbows, I thought it less strange and more like a warning nobody had bothered to translate.

Tam appeared at my shoulder the way he always did, drawn to trouble the way other men were drawn to taverns.

"That's a truth-reader's coach," he said.

"It's a coach."

"It's *Serah Vane's* coach. I saw her name on the courier's papers when he stopped at the mill. Serah Vane. She's read the truth out of three mayors and a bishop." He swallowed. "The bishop cried, they say."

"Bishops usually do, when the truth comes out of them."

"This isn't funny, Quentin."

Quentin. That was the name the records used, the name on the decree, the name that had been sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone since the courier posted the hunt's proclamation on the gatehouse door. *The hunting of the gifted begins at midwinter.* And under it, a list of names to be verified, and there mine was, in a clerk's careful hand, spelled correctly, which somehow made it worse.

"I know it isn't," I said. "Go home, Tam. Kiss your wife. Count your children. Don't stand next to me when that door opens."

He didn't go home. He never did. That was the trouble with Tam, and I would think about that later, in the bad hours, how his loyalty and his fear were the same animal wearing two coats.

The coach door opened, and a woman stepped down.

She was smaller than I expected, in a grey traveling coat, with hair the color of wet ash pulled back tight enough to hurt. She stood in the road and looked at the valley—the terraced fields, the mill, the gatehouse with its leaning chimney—and I watched something cross her face that I recognized, because I'd seen it on every traveler who ever crossed our border.

Disappointment.

"Where is the keeper?" she called.

"That's me," I said, walking down. "Bram—Quentin Winterbourne. Gatekeeper."

"Serah Vane." She didn't offer a hand. Truth-readers, Tam would later tell me, don't shake hands; hands are for people whose word you have to take. "I'm here to verify the registry. You're listed as born outside the valley."

"According to the ledger, yes."

"According to your own memory, where were you born?"

And here is the thing I want set down plainly, because it matters: I told the truth. I said, "I don't know. I have no memory of anywhere but Hollowmere. The ledger says I was found at the gate as a child, and that's the whole of my history as far as I own it."

She looked at me for a long moment, and then her shoulders came down half an inch, and I understood—though I didn't understand it yet—that she'd expected to catch me. That she had ridden three days through country that made her ill just to catch a liar, and I had failed her by being honest.

"You understand," she said, "that I will ask again. Differently. People are clever about truths. They arrange them."

"Arrange away. I've only got the one."

* * *

From the coach window, Serah Vane watched the keeper walk back toward his gatehouse, and she was, for the first time in a week, not nauseated.

That was the valley's doing. Her gift—she had stopped calling it a blessing around her thirtieth year—worked like a tongue. Lies had a taste. Copper, mostly, sometimes tin, sometimes the sweet-rot flavor of a lie the liar believed himself, which was the worst kind and the hardest to spit out. Truth tasted like nothing. Truth was water.

And the moment her coach had crossed the ridgeline into this valley, her tongue had gone numb. Not numb exactly—*muted*, as though the whole world had been wrapped in wool. She had tested it, of course, professionally, asking the innkeep three questions she knew the answers to and one she didn't. All of it flat. All of it water. She might as well have been reading truth from a stone.

It should have been a relief. It was not. A truth-reader without her gift was just a woman with sharp questions, and sharp questions frightened people into lies she couldn't taste, which meant everyone in this valley was a locked door and she'd left her picks at home.

Except the keeper.

The keeper had answered her like a man reading from a page he'd never edited. *I have no memory of anywhere but here.* She had circled it four ways. *What did your mother call you?* — *I don't know my mother.* — *What is your earliest memory?* — *The gate, and a woman's hands, and then nothing for a year.* — *Do you resent the crown's decree?* — and here he had paused, honestly paused, and said, *I don't know yet. Ask me at midwinter.*

She had written in her ledger: *No deception detected. Subject either truthful or beyond testing.*

She hated that second clause. She had come all this way to be certain, and the valley would not let her be certain, and certainty was the only thing the crown paid her for.

There was a knock at her coach door. The miller's man—the friend, Tam Aldous, who had hovered at the keeper's elbow all morning like a dog watching a wolf near its master.

"Inquisitor Vane?" He was twisting his cap. "I—might I have a word. Not for the record. Off the record, as it were."

She opened the door. "There is no off the record. There is only what I write down and what I don't."

He flinched at that. She watched him decide something, watched the fear in him change shape—fear of her becoming fear for the keeper—and she thought, with the weariness of her trade: *here it comes. There is always a friend.*

"It's the ledger," Tam said, low. "The gatehouse ledger. Quentin—Bram—found his own page, and there were pages missing around it. Cut out. And he lied to me about it."

"Lied how?"

"He said the pages were just water-damaged. But I *know* him. Thirty years, I know him, and he looked me in the eye and lied, and he's never—" Tam's voice cracked. "He's never lied to me in his life. And now there's a decree, and his name's on a list, and he's got the gate key on him at all hours, even sleeping, and I thought—maybe if you knew, maybe you'd just *question* him. Properly. Clear it up. Because if he's gifted and hiding it, they'll send someone worse than you, and if he's not, then this all goes away and—"

"Stop," Serah said.

He stopped.

She looked at this man, this friend, and felt the old familiar disgust—not at him, exactly, but at the shape of the thing, the way love and betrayal kept using the same door. He thought he was saving Bram Winterbourne. He was, in the way a man saves a pig by pointing out which one is fattest.

"The ledger," she said. "Show me."

* * *

I was oiling the postern hinge again, because oiling hinges is what my hands do when my head is screaming, when Tam came up the path with Serah Vane behind him and a look on his face I had never seen on him before.

I had seen Tam frightened—when his daughter took the fever, when the mill dam cracked in the spring flood. This was different. This was a man who had done something and was arriving ahead of it, the way guilt travels faster than horses.

"Quentin," he said. "I—"

"He showed me the ledger," Serah Vane said, and her voice was not unkind, which was somehow the cruelest option available to her.

The mallet was in my hand. I don't remember picking it up. I set it down.

"Tam," I said, "go home."

"I was trying to *help*—"

"Go *home*, Tam."

He went. He went, and that was its own wound, that he went, that he obeyed me at the last when it no longer mattered, and I stood in the road and watched my oldest friend become, in the space of a hundred steps, the man who had done this.

"Inquisitor." I turned to her. "Ask me anything."

"I intend to." She opened her ledger against her forearm. "The missing pages. What was on them?"

"I don't know. I found the stubs. I told Tam they were water-damaged because the truth—that someone cut out my history with a knife—would have frightened him, and because I wanted to understand it myself before I said it out loud. That's the whole of it. That's the ugly little truth, and you're welcome to it."

"Where is the gate key?"

"On me. As regulation requires." I pulled it out on its cord. Iron, old, warm from my chest. "You can have it, if that's your next question. But I'd ask you to think about what a keeper is *for*, if you take it."

She didn't take it. She looked at it a long time, and her tongue—her numb, wool-wrapped tongue—could tell her nothing, and I saw her understand that. I saw her arrive at the place I'd been living for a week: that in this valley, honesty was worth exactly what anyone chose to believe, and belief was the one thing she'd been sent here to replace with proof.

"You could be the most gifted man in Aldermere," she said quietly, "or a foundling with a key, and I cannot tell the difference. Do you know what that does to an inquisitor?"

"Makes you like the rest of us. Having to decide on trust."

"Trust is not in my remit." She closed the ledger. "Here is what happens. The valley is sealed at dawn—no one in or out, crown's order, effective on my word. You will be taken to the provincial seat for formal questioning. Not the rack," she added, and I noted that she thought to add it, and noted what it meant that she thought to add it. "Questioning. A truth-reader of my rank reads memories as well as words, keeper. It's less painful than it sounds."

"Is it?"

She didn't answer, and her not-answering answered.

They took me at dawn, as promised. Tam was not there to see it, and I was grateful, and then I was not grateful, because I realized I would have liked to tell him it was all right, and then I realized I did not know if it was all right, and then the coach door shut and the valley I had guarded my whole life shrank to a square of grey light.

That night, for the first time, the Hollow Gate spoke to me.

Not in the coach. In my cell at the provincial seat, three days' ride from the border, which should have been impossible, and which I understood—lying in the dark with the iron key still on its cord around my neck, because they had searched my coat and my boots and my saddlebag and never thought to search a keeper's skin—was the beginning of everything I could no longer prevent.

The gate said: *Quentin is the cage-name. Bram is the true one. Come home and we will show you the door in the hillside.*

And I, who had never wanted anything, wanted.
