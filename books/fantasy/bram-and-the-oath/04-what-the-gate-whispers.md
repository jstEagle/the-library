## 4. What the Gate Whispers

I had kept the Hollow Gate for thirty years, and in all that time it had never once said a word to me. This was fine by me. I have always believed that the best relationships are the ones where both parties keep their mouths shut. The gate and I understood each other: it stood, I swept, neither of us made demands.

So you can imagine my feelings when it started talking.

It began on the third night after Serah Vane's coach rattled back down the valley road. I want to be clear that I did not invite this. I was not dabbling in anything. I was asleep, or nearly, in the loft above the gatehouse, with the fire banked and the wind doing its usual business in the eaves—and then a voice said, quite distinctly, *Quentin.*

I sat up so fast I cracked my head on the rafter, which is the kind of thing that happens when your house was built by short men with no regard for posterity.

"Very funny," I told the dark. Tam had a key to the gatehouse and a history of thinking himself hilarious. But the floorboards below were silent, and when I leaned out the window, the yard lay empty under a thin moon, frost silvering the woodpile, the Hollow Gate itself a black seam in the hillside at the valley's mouth. Nothing moved. Nothing spoke.

*Quentin,* it said again, anyway.

Here is the trouble with that name: it isn't mine. Or rather, it is mine the way a coat left behind in an inn is yours—somebody wrote it down, and now everyone agrees. Quentin Winterbourne, keeper, born outside. That's what the ledger says. That's what I've answered to for thirty years, ever since old Keeper Aldous found me at the border one autumn morning with no memory of how I'd got there and, apparently, no memory worth having besides.

But when the gate said *Quentin*, it said it the way you'd read a name off a shipping manifest. And beneath it, patient as groundwater, was another sound—a shape of a name, not quite formed, like a word remembered in a language you used to dream in.

I lay back down and stared at the rafters until dawn, which gave me plenty of time to consider the sensible explanations. Wind in a hollow stone. A dream. The onset of the same softness of the brain that took my mother's uncle. I liked all of these explanations very much and elected to believe none of them, because I knew—I knew the way you know the weight of your own hand—that the voice came from the gate, and that it had been waiting a long time to use it.

* * *

By day, the valley went about its business, and so did I. There is something obscene about routine in troubled times, but there is also something that keeps you from screaming, and I have always chosen the latter. Serah Vane had posted her decree on the meeting-house door: all gifted folk to be declared by midwinter, all gifts surrendered, all names entered into the crown's great ledger of the found. She'd interviewed half the village before leaving, me included, and I had told her the truth—the whole truth, as far as I knew it—which is that I had never once felt anything in this valley except cold hands and the occasional grievance about the porridge.

That was true. That has been true for thirty years. Magic cannot follow you past the valley line; whatever I am—or was, or might have been—died at the border every morning of my life before I woke.

Except.

On the fourth night I didn't go to bed. I took the lamp and walked down through the frost to the gate itself, feeling foolish, the way you feel talking to a horse about your problems. The Hollow Gate is not much to look at: two leaning stones with a lintel across them, older than the valley's oldest family, set into the pass where the hills pinch shut. No door hangs in it. There hasn't been a door since before Aldous's time, only the iron lock-plate bolted to the stone, and the keyhole, and the key—which I wore on a cord around my neck because that's what keepers do, and because a key with nothing to open is the most honest thing I own.

"Right," I said to the gate. "Say it again."

The gate, being a pair of rocks, said nothing. I stood there in the cold feeling like exactly the sort of man who talks to rocks, which is precisely the sort of man the crown was hunting, and I was about to go back inside and never speak of this again—

And then I stepped over the threshold line, the worn groove where the door would hang if there were one, and the world *opened*.

I don't know how else to put it. It was as if I'd lived my whole life in a room with the shutters painted black, and someone had finally scraped them. Something rushed into me—not through the skin, exactly, more through the idea of me—and my hand, without any instruction from the part of me that makes decisions, rose and turned palm-up, and a small gold light sat in it like a tame ember.

Thirty years. Thirty years of sweeping steps and oiling hinges and being a plain man in a plain valley, and here was proof that I was none of those things, sitting in my hand, warm as a fresh egg.

I did the reasonable thing. I dropped it, yelped, and fell over.

The light didn't go out. It hovered where it landed, bobbing gently, radiating an air of mild disappointment in me, and I sat in the frost with my heart going like a drum in a procession and laughed until my eyes watered, because I had spent three decades believing myself safe by virtue of emptiness, and emptiness, it turned out, had just been a locked room.

The gate hummed. Not the whisper of the night before—a hum, low and pleased, like a cat settling.

"All right," I said, wiping my face. "All right. What do you want?"

*Come home,* said the gate, in the voice that was not a voice, under the name that was not mine. And then it showed me.

* * *

I don't know how to describe what it showed me, because it wasn't seeing, exactly—it was remembering things that had never happened to me, which is either impossible or the definition of magic, depending on whom you ask.

A city built on terraces above a brown river, and everywhere bells—brass bells on the bridges, bells in the eaves, a bell the size of a grain barrel in a tower that rang noon in eleven notes, and the whole city answering like a struck chord. I could smell it. Woodsmoke and wet stone and frying onions, which is the smell of every good city, and beneath it something green and mineral that belongs to nowhere but one place.

A woman singing. Dark hair, grey coming in at the temple, standing at a window with her sleeves rolled, singing a song whose words I couldn't hold onto—the way a dream won't let you carry water out of it—but whose tune sat in my chest like something swallowed. I knew, with a certainty that made my throat hurt, that she was singing to me. That she had sung to me before. That she was—

A door in a hillside. Round-topped, banded in iron, set into a green slope under an old rowan. And carved over the lintel, worn shallow but legible, letters I could not read and yet understood entirely:

*ALDERMERE.*

Then it was gone, and I was sitting in the frost with a dead lamp and a live ember of light guttering out beside me, and I was crying, which was inconvenient, because I hadn't known I was going to do it and hate being surprised.

Here is the shameful part. Not the weeping—weeping's honest work. The shameful part is the wanting. I have wanted small things in my life: a dry boot, a good winter, Tam to stop stealing my tobacco. I have prided myself on needing nothing, on the comfort of smallness, on being a man whose desires fit in a coat pocket. And now, in the space of one night, I wanted a city I'd never seen, a song I couldn't remember, a woman whose face was already fading from me even as I reached for it—and I wanted them with a hunger that made every previous day of my life look like a long, polite starvation.

The valley was still the valley. The frost was still the frost. My hands were still my hands. But I had seen the shutters scrape back, and there was no unseeing it.

* * *

For six nights I went to the gate. I told no one—not Tam, though he watched me across the tavern table with the particular squint of a man doing sums he doesn't like. Not anyone. The decree was pinned to the meeting-house door and midwinter was coming down on us like a landslide, and every instinct I owned said: tell nobody, want nothing, be small.

But you cannot unlearn wanting. Ask anyone who's tried.

Each night the fragments came clearer. The bell tower's eleven notes—I began to hear the twelfth, the one that finishes the phrase. The woman's song—I got four words of it once, in a language I couldn't speak and yet somehow knew meant *the doors remember*. The hillside door appeared each night a little closer, until the last night I could see the grain of its oak, the rust weeping from the iron bands, and—this is the part I haven't said aloud even to myself—a keyhole.

Round. Old. Iron-collared.

The same shape, near enough, as the key that hangs against my chest.

I sat back on my heels in the frozen grass, the gate humming at my shoulder like a patient old dog, and did the arithmetic I'd been refusing to do. A gatekeeper with a key. A gate with no door here, whispering of a door elsewhere. A name on a ledger that wasn't mine. A country cut out of the maps like a bad tooth.

"You're not the valley's gate at all," I said quietly. "Are you. You're a way out."

The hum deepened, and the frost-light rose around the threshold stones, and the gate said the almost-name again—closer now, a breath away from shape, a word I felt in my teeth like incoming thunder—

And that was when I heard boots on the path behind me, and turned to find Tam standing in the starlight with his hat in his hands and his face gone the color of tallow.

"Bram," he said. "Bram, the inquisitor's coach is coming back up the valley. And Bram—" He swallowed. "She's asking for you by a different name."
