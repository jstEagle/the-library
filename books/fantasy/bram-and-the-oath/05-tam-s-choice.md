## 5. Tam's Choice

I have told lies before. I want that on the record, in whatever ledger the gods keep for men like me, because by tomorrow morning everyone in this valley will tell you that Tam Ashford is an honest man, a good man, a man who wouldn't hurt Bram Winterbourne if his life depended on it—and the terrible joke of it is that hurting Bram is exactly what I did, and I did it believing myself honest the whole while.

But let me start where it started, which was Tuesday, with my daughter asking me why the sky over the gatehouse looked wrong.

* * *

Maren is six and sees things she shouldn't, which in this valley means nothing at all, because nothing works here. Magic doesn't follow past the border stones. It never has. That's the whole point of Hollow Gate, though if you'd asked me what the point was two weeks ago I'd have said something dull about tolls and ledgers and keeping the road clear of wolves, because that's what we all said, because that's all any of us knew.

"Your eyes are playing tricks," I told her.

"They went *sideways*," she insisted. "Like the world hiccupped."

And I laughed and ruffled her hair and did not think about it again until midnight, when I woke up remembering that Bram had gone white as flour when the courier posted the decree. White as flour, and quiet in a way Bram is never quiet. Bram talks the way water runs—constant, cheerful, over everything. A silent Bram was a thing I had no shelf for.

* * *

Here is what you need to know about me: I am not brave. I have made peace with it, the way you make peace with bad knees or a receding hairline. Bravery is for men with nothing to lose, and I have a wife whose cough turns wet in winter, a daughter who sees sideways, and another one on the way—Edda says April, and Edda is never wrong about anything except how much ale I can hold. When the crown posts a decree saying the gifted are to be hunted beginning at midwinter, a man like me doesn't think *how do I fight this*. He thinks *how does my family stay off the list*.

The list. That's the part I keep circling back to. The courier's decree had a rider pinned to it, a supplement, names to be verified. And there, in the third column, in ink so ordinary it might have been a grocer's inventory:

*Winterbourne, Quentin — keeper — verify.*

Not Bram. Quentin. I'd known the man twenty years and never once heard him called Quentin. I asked him about it, casually, over cards, and he smiled his smile and said, "Crown records. You know how they are." And he lied to me. I know that now. I didn't know it then, and God forgive me, but not knowing it is half of what I did next.

Because here's the other thing about me: I love Bram Winterbourne like a brother. Closer than a brother. My brothers fought with me; Bram *listened*. Twenty years of listening, patient as stone, while I complained about crops and taxes and Edda's mother. And when Serah Vane came to the valley—black coach, gray coat, a face like a locked door—I watched her interview him and watched him answer every question straight and true, and I thought, *good, good, honesty will protect him*, because I still believed in honesty then, the way children believe in the moon following them home.

It was Tam who broke him, people will say. But no. It was Tam who believed the wrong thing completely, with his whole stupid heart.

* * *

Serah Vane stayed at the inn, and on her third night I brought her supper because Edda runs the kitchen and Edda ordered me to, and because some cowardly part of me wanted to look into those flat gray eyes and see whether she was going to burn us all.

She wasn't eating. She was staring at the window toward the gatehouse, and her expression was the expression of a woman drinking sour milk.

"You can't taste anything here," I said. It wasn't a question. The whole valley knew the truth-reader's gift failed at the border. It was almost funny, in the way our valley finds things funny—the great inquisitor, struck dumb at our door.

"No," she said. "Nothing. Like drinking from a puddle."

"And that frustrates you."

"It should relieve you." She turned those eyes on me and I understood why they paid her what they paid her. "Instead it frustrates me. Because I read your friend Bram for two hours, and every word rang true, and true answers to wrong questions are worse than useless. Someone edited something in that valley, keeper's-friend, and my gift can't tell me what."

"I don't know what you mean," I said.

Which was true, mostly. What I *didn't* say was: three days earlier, helping Bram carry archive boxes down from the gatehouse loft—he'd been up there nights, I now realize, hunting through old ledgers—I had seen pages cut out. Ragged stubs where a razor had taken a whole season of records. And I had seen Bram's face when he caught me looking, and Bram had lied to me again—"water damage"—and I had carried the box downstairs and said nothing, because saying nothing is my finest skill.

But Serah Vane sat there not-eating her supper, and she said, "Your decree lists him for verification. Verification means questions. Questions mean answers. If he has nothing to hide, he loses an afternoon." She shrugged. "If he does have something to hide, better we find it than midwinter finds him. Either way, he survives it. That's more than most of the listed get."

That's what she told me. *He survives it.* Remember that. Write it on my stone.

* * *

I want to be fair to myself, which is difficult, because fairness requires honesty and I've already established my credentials there are suspect.

I told myself: Bram is hiding something, and hidden things rot. I told myself: better a controlled question than a midwinter purge, when they stop verifying and start taking. I told myself: he'll thank me. He'll stand in that inn yard afterward, rubbing his wrists, laughing his water-run laugh, saying *Tam, you old fool, you worried over nothing*, and I'll buy him a drink and confess and he'll forgive me because he always forgives me, that's the shape of us.

So on her fifth night I came back to the inn after Edda slept, and I stood outside Serah Vane's door for the length of three prayers I didn't believe in, and I knocked.

"The ledger," I said, when she opened it. "The gatehouse archives. Whole seasons cut out. Pages missing around the years of Bram's birth. He's been searching them himself, nights. He knows something about himself that he doesn't even know he knows. I'm telling you because—" and here my voice did something shameful, went small and pleading— "because verification is just questions, right? He answers, he goes home. Right?"

She looked at me for a long moment, and I swear on my daughters' lives there was something almost gentle in her face, and she said, "Thank you, Tam. This helps him more than you know."

Then she closed the door, and I walked home through the frost feeling *lighter*, Gods help me. Lighter. I had handed a wolf a map to my brother's throat and come home whistling.

* * *

Dawn found me at the well, because sleep wasn't having any of me. And that's where I heard it—the sound every soul in this valley knows and dreads, the sound of the Hollow Gate being sealed.

Iron on iron, deep as a church bell rung underground. Then chains. Then the creak of the winch Bram maintained like a priest tending an altar. I ran. Forty years old and I ran up that road like a boy, breath sawing, and came out onto the rise in time to see it.

Crown riders at the border stones. A dozen of them, and more beyond, closing the roads. And Serah Vane standing at the gate itself, giving orders in a voice like falling slate.

"Seal the valley," she was saying. "No passage in or out. And bring me the keeper. We begin questioning at first light."

"He's being *questioned*?" I said. I hadn't meant to speak. "You said—you said questions, an afternoon—"

She turned. And whatever gentleness I'd seen in her face at the inn door was gone, packed away like festival decorations, and what remained was the thing the crown actually employed.

"Yes," she said. "Questions."

And one of the riders, young, bored, adjusting his gauntlet, snorted and said to another, "Truth-reader's rack again. Poor bastard. Rather take the whip myself—at least the whip leaves you knowing who you are."

Knowing who you are.

The world went very quiet and very loud at once, the way it does right before you faint. Because I remembered the ledger then. The cut pages. *Quentin Winterbourne, born outside.* Whatever was written in those missing pages, whatever Bram was—that rack wouldn't just ask him questions. It would *unpick him*. Thread by thread. It would go digging through everything he was to reach everything he might be, and leave him a hollowed coat standing in the yard, smiling at strangers, calling himself Quentin because someone else had decided that was his name.

And I had drawn the map. I had knocked on the door. I had walked home whistling.

"Bram," I said out loud, to nobody, to the frost, to God if He was watching this particular disaster. "Oh, Bram. Run."

* * *

They took him at dawn, as promised. I stood in the crowd with my hat in my hands like a mourner, because that's what I was, only the body wasn't cold yet. And Bram—he looked for me. In the inn yard, hands bound with plain cord, flanked by riders, he scanned the faces the way he always did, finding mine first the way a compass finds north. And he *smiled*. Small, tired, forgiving already, before a single question had been asked.

That smile is going to kill me. Not quickly. Slowly, every day, for the rest of my life.

I went home. I kissed Edda's hair while she slept. I sat by Maren's bed and watched her breathe and thought: *this is what I chose instead of him. This room. These small warm lives. And maybe it was worth it, maybe a man protects his own—but then don't call it honesty, Tam. Don't ever call it honesty again.*

Maren opened her eyes. Six years old, sees sideways, misses nothing.

"Papa," she whispered, "the man from the gatehouse. His name went *sideways* too. Last week. Like the sky did."

I sat very still. "What do you mean, sideways?"

"Like he had two names and one of them was trying to get out."

I kissed her forehead and told her to sleep, and I went downstairs, and I put on my boots and my winter coat, and I picked up the iron poker from beside the hearth, because I am not a brave man but I have discovered there is a kind of courage that isn't bravery at all—it's just guilt arriving early, ahead of the consequences, dragging you out the door by the collar.

They were holding Bram at the gatehouse overnight before transport. Two guards at the door. Dawn was hours off yet.

I had until dawn to undo the worst thing I've ever done.

I started walking.
