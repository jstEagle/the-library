## 6. Flight Across the Borderline

I had spent thirty years believing the iron key was ceremonial. That's the joke of it. I polished the thing every solstice, hung it on a nail above the ledger shelf, and told visitors it opened nothing but old arguments between the crown and the valley. A keeper's toy. A brass-weighted promise that the Hollow Gate mattered to someone, somewhere, even if that someone was only me.

It turns out the key was the only honest object in the valley.

I took it down the night after Serah Vane's coach rolled back toward the pass, the night after Tam looked at me across the tavern table with a face I couldn't read and hadn't been able to read since the inquisitor arrived. I told myself I'd bring it back. I told myself a great many things that night, and the trouble with being a man who has never lied is that every lie comes out of your mouth sounding exactly like the truth, even to you.

The gatehouse was cold. The decree was still nailed to the door where the courier had posted it, the wax seal fat and red as a wound: *By order of the Crown, all persons of gifted blood within the province of—* and then the smear where the valley's name should have been, a blank space in the ink, as if the scribe had fallen asleep mid-word. I'd read it a hundred times. I read it again. The blank space read me back.

I have never once in my life done anything dramatic. My great acts of rebellion, prior to that evening, included watering the crown's horse without being asked and once, memorably, telling a tax assessor that the road to the pass was washed out when it was merely muddy. So when I wrapped the key in my scarf and tucked it into my coat, I expected my heart to behave like it always did—steady, dull, keeper's heart.

It did not behave. It hammered like someone knocking at a door they very much wanted opened.

* * *

The Hollow Gate itself stands at the valley's western mouth: two leaning stones with a lintel across them, older than the road, older than the crown, older probably than stone has any business being. Beyond it the pass narrows into fog. I had walked through that gate four thousand mornings to check the boundary markers and four thousand evenings to walk back, and in all that time I had never once thought of it as a door. Doors open. This had only ever framed the view.

I stood before it at midnight with the key against my chest and the fog breathing at me like something patient, and I said, out loud, because there was no one left in the valley to hear me be foolish:

"All right. I'm listening."

The gate did not answer in words. I want to be clear about that, because what happened next was not words, and every attempt I've made since to describe it in words has felt like trying to carry water in a net. But the nearest I can come is this: the gate said my name. Not *Bram*. Not *Quentin*, the name in the ledger, the name the crown's records kept for me like a coat in a cloakroom. A third name. A name that fit like a key fits—not the shape of my mouth but the shape of *me*—and the moment I heard it, the iron key grew hot against my chest, and the fog beyond the stones parted like a curtain drawn by a careful hand.

I should have run. A sensible man runs. But I had spent thirty years being sensible in a valley where my gift had never once worked, where I was the least magical man in the least magical place in the world, and the gate was calling me by a name I'd never heard and it felt, God help me, like being *invited*.

I stepped through.

The world broke.

That's the only honest way to put it. One stride I was in the pass, fog on my face, and the next stride the fog was gone and the night was *full*—full of light that had no source, full of a wind that carried voices instead of air, full of a pressure against my skin like the moment before a storm, except the storm was inside me. My gift. The thing I'd been told I had and had never felt, the thing that had never once sparked in thirty years of valley silence—it came back all at once, the way blood comes back to a leg you've slept on, and it *hurt*, it hurt like glory, and I went to my knees on a road I had never seen in a country I had never walked and laughed until my ribs ached.

Because I could feel it. Everything. The road under my hands was humming. The hills were humming. Somewhere ahead of me a city that should not exist was ringing bells I could hear in my teeth.

Aldermere.

The name arrived in my head the way the third name had arrived—unasked, unearned, absolute. Aldermere, the country that wasn't on any map. Aldermere, the blank space in the decree. Aldermere, erased so thoroughly that even the ink forgot it, and here it was around me, shimmering like a reflection in water someone had dropped a stone into. Half-real. I could see the road and see through it. I could see hills wearing forests, and through the forests, the pale suggestion of rooftops. I could see, far off, a city of towers with brass bells catching a moonlight that had no moon in it, and the bells were ringing, and no one was ringing them, and the sound was the loneliest sound I have ever heard.

A country holding its breath. A country waiting to be remembered.

I got up. My legs shook. My gift roared in me like a river that had been dammed for thirty years and had just been given leave to flood, and I stood in the middle of a ghost nation and wept like a child, and if you'd asked me right then what I was running *from*, I honestly couldn't have told you. I could only have told you what I was running *toward*, and I couldn't have told you that either, because I didn't know yet. I just knew the gate had called me by my true name, and the true name had a country attached to it, and the country needed a key.

I had the key. I had never in my life been needed for anything bigger than a frozen pump.

* * *

I heard the hoofbeats an hour later, or what I think was an hour—time in Aldermere moves like water in a reed bed, slow and slantwise—and my first feeling, I'm ashamed to say, was not fear. It was a flash of the old valley irritation, the keeper's reflex: *someone's on the road after curfew.*

Then I remembered I was the one after curfew, in a country that didn't exist, pursued by a truth-reader who could taste lies like copper on the tongue, and the fear arrived all at once, punctual as taxes.

Serah Vane came up the ghost road on a gray horse that seemed, if anything, more real than the road beneath it. She had left her coach at the border—I learned later the horses wouldn't cross—and she sat in the saddle with her hands loose on the reins and her face doing what her face always did, which was nothing at all. In the valley she had been diminished like the rest of us, her gift smothered by whatever the valley did to magic. Out here, I could *see* it on her. It moved around her like heat off a forge. She was reading the country itself, and the country was lying to her by existing, and she did not look pleased about it.

"Keeper," she said. Her voice carried strangely in the half-light, flat, without echo, as if the country hadn't decided whether it wanted to repeat things.

"Inquisitor." I kept walking. There seemed no point stopping. If she wanted to take me, the country would have to help her, and the country didn't seem entirely sure she was there.

"You crossed the border with a stolen key."

"I crossed the border with a key I've kept for thirty years. Theft implies it belonged to someone else."

"It belongs to the Crown."

"Then the Crown is welcome to come and collect it." I gestured at the shimmering hills, the bell-haunted towers, the moonlight with no moon. "I believe they're holding its mail."

Something crossed her face then—quick, quickly buried. I have spent a lifetime reading small faces in a small valley; I know a crack in plaster when I see one.

"You're not afraid of me," she said. It wasn't a question. It had the tone of a woman checking her own arithmetic.

"I'm terrified of you," I said. "But you can't read me out here any better than I can lie to you in there, and we're both a long way from home, and the home you're from doesn't exist anymore. So." I spread my hands. The gift surged in me as I did it—ridiculous, glorious, the wind answering my gesture like a dog answering a whistle—and I saw her see it. Saw her *taste* it, almost, the way she'd taste a lie. "It seems we're the same out here, inquisitor. Two gifted people in a ghost nation. You could arrest me. Or you could stand still for one moment and feel what it feels like to be what we are."

She didn't move. But she didn't move the way a woman deciding not to draw a sword doesn't move, and her horse stood stock still, and the bells rang on in their city with no one to hear them but us.

For one moment—one long, slantwise Aldermere moment—we were exactly that. Two gifted people in a country that had been erased, and her gift and mine humming in the strange air like two struck tuning forks, and I swear the road brightened under her horse's hooves, as if the country were trying to be real for her, too. As if it remembered her. I didn't understand that then. I do now, and I'd give a great deal not to.

"Where is the door?" she said at last. Quietly. Not the voice of an inquisitor. The voice of a woman asking directions.

"I don't know," I said, and it was the truth, and out here, with her gift returned, she'd taste it as clean as spring water. "But I think it knows me."

I turned and walked on, toward the bells, toward the hills, toward whatever the gate's third name was calling me to. And behind me, after a moment long enough to hold a funeral in, I heard the gray horse follow.

The country shimmered around us both, half-real, holding its breath—waiting, I would soon learn, not just for a key.

But for a price.
