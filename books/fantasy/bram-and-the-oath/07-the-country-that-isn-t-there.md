## 7. The Country That Isn't There

The border, it turned out, was not a line. It was a bruise.

I crossed it the way you'd press a thumb into one—slowly, wincing, waiting for the color to spread. And it did spread. The moment my boot left valley stone and touched the ground beyond, the world came back to me like blood returning to a numb limb, all pins and needles and terrible sweetness. Thirty years of nothing, and then everything at once.

I felt the ley-threads first. They ran under the earth like veins of molten glass, and I could *see* them, which I hadn't known was a thing a body could do. I could taste the air the way Serah Vane tasted lies, I supposed—every current of magic in it, every old spell worn smooth by centuries of weather. The gift I'd mourned my whole life as stillborn woke up screaming, and I fell to my knees in the wet grass and laughed, or wept, or some disgraceful combination of the two.

Then I looked up, and the laughing stopped.

Aldermere was there. And it was not there.

The city of brass bells from the gate's whispering stood on the far hill—half of it, anyway. Towers rose confident and whole, and between them the air simply *stopped*, like a painting someone had abandoned. Streets began and ended in white mist. A bridge crossed a river that had no far bank. And everywhere, everywhere, that faint translucence, as if the whole country were a lantern lit from inside and the light was failing.

"Half-real," I said to the key in my pocket. It was warm. It had been warm since I stole it, and it pulsed now like a second heart, and I chose not to think about what that meant.

I walked toward the city because there was nowhere else to walk. Behind me, somewhere on the border, Serah Vane was picking herself up out of the grass with her gift flooding back into her and her certainties flooding out. I'd bought myself an hour, maybe two. In Aldermere, apparently, I had bought myself a country.

* * *

The people were in the market square, which was the only part of the city that seemed entirely solid, and I understood why the moment I saw them. They were *remembering* it into place.

There were perhaps two hundred of them. They sat in circles on the cobbles, old and young, holding hands in long chains, and they spoke—not to each other, exactly, but *at* the square, at the fountain, at the bell tower. A woman described the fountain in exhaustive detail, the chipped dolphin on its rim, the way the water tasted of copper in summer, and as she spoke, the fountain *was*. When she paused for breath, it flickered. An old man took up the slack without missing a beat, describing the dolphin's missing eye, and the fountain steadied.

They were keeping their city alive by talking about it. All day, every day, for thirty years. I stood at the edge of the square with my mouth open like a tourist, until a boy of maybe ten noticed me and tugged his grandmother's sleeve.

"Grandam," he said. "There's a *whole* man."

Every head turned. Two hundred faces, and I want you to understand what I saw in them: not fear. *Hunger.* The look of people who have been forgotten so long that the sight of anyone who remembers them is a kind of miracle.

"He's crossed over," someone whispered. "From inside. Look at his shadow—it's a valley shadow."

A tall woman with iron-grey braids pushed through the crowd. She was built like a door, and she looked at me the way a carpenter looks at a plank that might be load-bearing.

"Name," she said.

"Bram Winterbourne. Called Quentin by the crown's records, though I'm given to understand the crown's records are—" I patted my pocket, where the ledger page sat folded, "—creative."

A murmur went through the crowd. The woman's eyes went to my pocket, then to my face, and something in her expression shifted from appraisal to something almost like grief.

"Keeper," she said. "You're the keeper."

"I keep a gate," I said carefully. "In a valley. Where nothing works and nothing happens and the most exciting event of my year is usually a wasp in the honey jar."

The crowd laughed, and the laugh had an edge of hysteria to it, the sound of people who have been very serious for a very long time. The woman didn't laugh.

"Maren Odo," she said. "I was Warden of the Eastern Reach, before. Come with me, keeper. There are things you need to hear, and I'd rather you heard them sitting down, because you look like a man who's about to be told the floor is a lie."

"The floor's a lie?"

"Everything's a lie here, except us." She turned. "That's rather the problem."

* * *

She took me to a hall that existed because forty people had spent the morning insisting on it, and she sat me down before a hearth that burned because a child was reciting the exact color of its flames. Then she told me.

I will give it to you the way she gave it to me, plainly, because she had plainly told it so many times that the plainness was all that was left of her anger.

Thirty years ago, the crown had a problem. Aldermere was rich in magic and poor in obedience, and its people had a habit of looking at royal decrees and saying *no, we don't think so.* Wars are expensive. So the crown's artificers built a forgetting—a great working, anchored in a place called the Hollow Gate, that would erase the country from maps, from records, from *minds*. Not kill it. Kill the *remembering*. A country that no one remembers, she said, is a country that cannot be missed, cannot be avenged, cannot even be grieved.

"But it didn't work all the way," I said.

"It worked exactly the way it was designed," Maren said. "It erased us from *outside*. Everyone beyond the border forgot us—except the working needed an anchor, and an anchor needs a keeper, and a keeper needs to *stay*. So the valley inside the border was left whole. A pocket of the remembered, holding the door shut on the forgotten." Her mouth twisted. "They didn't count on the working leaking. Every year, a little of the forgetting seeps through. Every year, we lose a little more. Streets go. Names go. People go." She looked at her own hands. "My son went eleven years ago. I remember his face because I made forty people memorize it. But I no longer remember his voice. The forgetting took it, and no one had thought to memorize a voice."

The hearth flickered. The child reciting its flames faltered, and an older girl took over without opening her eyes.

"We are the Remnant," Maren said. "Two hundred and eleven of us, from a nation of two million. The ones who were *in* Aldermere when it happened—the sick, the stubborn, the ones who wouldn't leave. We hold each other in memory. That's all we are now. A country kept alive by two hundred people talking about it." She met my eyes. "And we are losing, keeper. Another ten years, maybe fifteen, and there won't be enough of us to hold the center of the city. It'll be the market square, then just the fountain, then just the dolphin's chipped eye, and then nothing."

I looked at my hands. They were shaking, which annoyed me, because I had a speech half-formed about how I was just a gatekeeper, just a small man, and shaking hands rather undercut it.

"The gate," I said. "The whispers. The city of bells, the woman singing, the door in the hillside. That's you. That's Aldermere, calling through the anchor."

"The gate is the working's heart," Maren said. "And its flaw. It was built to erase, but it was built *from* the old door-magic, the oath-magic, the magic that made borders in the first place. And the old magic has a rule older than the crown: a door can be unmade by the one who keeps it." She leaned forward. "You can open the Hollow Gate, Bram Winterbourne. All the way. Not a crack—a *flood*. The working will unravel, the forgetting will lift from every mind beyond the border, and Aldermere will come back. Maps will redraw themselves. Graves will be grieved. Two million people who never knew they'd lost anything will wake with a hole in their hearts and a name in it."

The hall was silent except for the recited fire. I felt something enormous and terrible rising in me, hope with teeth.

"And the price?" I said, because the key in my pocket had gone very still, the way things go still when they're listening, and I had learned in thirty years at that gate that stillness is never good news.

Maren didn't flinch. I'll grant her that.

"The working is held together by one memory," she said. "One whole, continuous, *lived* memory of the valley and the border and the gate—the keeper's own. That's the anchor. That's what the crown's artificers used. You, Quentin Winterbourne, born outside, keeper of Hollow Gate. Your life is the pin that holds the forgetting shut. To open the gate, the pin must come out." She said it gently, the way you'd tell someone the medicine is also the poison. "Every memory of the valley. Every morning of it. The gatehouse. The ledgers. The wasp in the honey jar." She paused, and her voice did something complicated. "And the people. Whoever you love there. Whoever loves you. Gone. You'll stand in a restored Aldermere and not know why your eyes keep going east, toward a valley you no longer remember existing."

The fire recited on. Somewhere outside, a bell that only existed because people believed in it rang the hour, thin and brave.

I thought about Tam. I thought about his terrible porridge and his worse singing and the way he'd stood in my doorway a hundred evenings with nothing to say and stayed anyway. I thought about thirty years of smallness, which I had called peace, and which was—God help me—*love*, wearing a plain coat.

"That's not a price," I said finally. "That's the whole purse."

"I know," Maren said. "I'm sorry. The crown built it that way. They wanted the door to be unopenable, and they nearly managed it. They didn't count on the oath-magic having one more rule." She stood, and offered me her hand, iron-grey and steady. "It always leaves a *choice*. Come. There's a door in a hillside you should see before you decide anything."

I didn't take her hand. I was busy pressing my palm flat against my chest, over the key, which had begun—softly, horribly—to *hum*.

And outside the hall, at the edge of the square, at the border of the half-real, a figure stood in the white mist with her coat dark from the river and her gift blazing around her like frost.

Serah Vane had found me.

And behind her, stumbling, half-carrying a lantern, a second figure that I knew the way you know your own hands.

Tam.
