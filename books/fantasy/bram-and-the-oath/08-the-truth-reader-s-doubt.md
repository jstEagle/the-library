## 8. The Truth-Reader's Doubt

I have tasted eleven thousand lies in my career, and I could tell you the flavor of every category. Fear lies taste of tin. Vanity lies taste of honey left too long in the sun. The lies people tell to be kind—the ones that ruin me most—taste like bread: warm, honest-seeming, and full of nothing.

The lie I told myself for twenty years tasted of nothing at all.

That should have been the first clue.

* * *

The door in the hillside was exactly where the fragments said it would be, which offended me professionally. Things that are erased from maps and memory do not get to also be punctual.

Bram Winterbourne stood before it with an iron key in his fist and half a country shimmering around him like heat off a summer road. Behind me, crown riders were still untangling themselves from the birch wood, cursing horses that kept stepping through puddles that weren't there. Ahead of him, a door that had no business existing in a country that had no business being forgotten.

"Quentin Winterbourne," I called, using the name on his warrant, because names are leashes and I have always been good with leashes. "Step away from the door."

He turned. And here is what I want recorded, somewhere, by someone who keeps better records than the crown does: he did not look like a fugitive. He looked like a man who had been asked to carry something very heavy up a very long hill and had finally, gratefully, reached the top.

"Inquisitor Vane," he said. "You're standing in Aldermere."

"I'm standing in a hole in the map," I corrected.

"That's what I said."

I liked him. That was inconvenient. I had liked him in the valley too, during the interview, when he'd answered every question with the flat, unadorned truth of a man who has never needed to practice lying because he's never had anything worth hiding. Truth-readers learn to distrust the truthful; they're the hardest to read, like tasting water after years of wine. But he'd been easy. He'd been *clear*. A keeper who swept his gatehouse, fed his cat, wound the clock, and had never once—not once in thirty years—felt his gift work inside those borders.

A valley where magic cannot follow. I had thought it a curiosity then. I know now what it actually was: a lock, and we were all of us inside it.

"The ledger," I said. "Your friend Tam says you found your own entry. Born outside. Pages missing." I walked toward him slowly, boots finding ground that only sometimes agreed to hold them. "You understand why I can't let you open anything until I've read you. If the gate is what you think it is—if Aldermere is real—then this is treason of the highest order, and I will need every truth you have."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the riders behind me take you apart looking for answers, and I stand here and watch, and write down afterward how much of it was necessary." I smiled, the way one does at funerals. "I'm offering you the gentler option. It happens rarely. Enjoy it."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he held out both hands, palms up, the key still gripped in his right fist, and said:

"Read me."

* * *

Reading a willing mind is like opening a door into a lit house. Reading an unwilling one is breaking a window. Bram opened the door himself, which is rarer than honesty and more disorienting, because I had braced for resistance and got instead—

Smallness. That's the word. A lifetime of small, clean truths laid out like linen.

Sweeping the gatehouse flags at dawn because dust gathers overnight and he cannot bear it. Feeding the cat, whose name changed four times over thirty years and who outlived three of her own names. Winding the clock. Counting travelers—forty-one years, two hundred and six souls through the Hollow Gate, and he remembered their faces the way other men remember scripture. Tam falling out of a pear tree when they were nine. Tam laughing. Tam, Tam, Tam—a friendship worn smooth as a river stone by decades of use. No ambition. No resentment. Not one lie, not one cruelty, not one secret except—

Except.

I went deeper, the way you descend a stairwell you've never noticed in your own house, and the smallness fell away, and there it was. The enormous thing. The thing so large he'd carried it so quietly I'd mistaken its weight for calm.

He wasn't hiding a crime.

He was hiding a country.

I saw it as he saw it: the decree, the old parchment in the gatehouse archive, the seal I knew better than my own mother's face. The crown's own hand. *Let the borders forget. Let the maps go blank. Let the name be struck from every ledger, every bell, every tongue.* Aldermere hadn't fallen. Aldermere hadn't betrayed anyone. Aldermere had been *edited*, the way a clerk edits a misentry—except the entry was a nation, and the erasure took everything in it, and the forgetting spread outward year by year like ink through wet cloth, eating the Remnant's memories, eating their children's names, eating—

Eating mine.

I stopped reading. Or rather, the reading stopped me, because I had reached the edge of something in him that touched the edge of something in me, and the two edges fit together with a click I felt in my teeth.

Serah Vane. Born in Marrowfield. Mother a laundress, father dead of fever. Left home at fourteen when the crown tested me and found I could taste falsehoods on the air.

That is what I remembered.

That is what I *had* remembered.

But standing inside Bram Winterbourne's memory of what erasure does, I suddenly saw the seams in my own history the way you see seams in cloth once someone points them out—Marrowfield had a laundry district but no record of my mother's washing-house. My childhood had a shape but no weather. I could recall learning to read lies, but I could not recall the first lie I ever read, and surely there was one, and surely it mattered, and surely a girl doesn't simply *begin* at fourteen like a character entering in the second act.

They had trimmed me. Clipped me like a hedge, neat along the top, so I would grow straight toward the crown and never lean toward anything else.

I have tasted eleven thousand lies. I never once thought to check whether I was standing in one.

* * *

"Vane." Bram's voice came from far away, then closer. "You've gone white. Whiter. You were already quite white."

"I'm thinking," I said.

"Don't hurt yourself."

I almost laughed. Twenty years in the Inquisition and the first honest laugh anyone's offered me comes from a man I'm supposed to arrest. I sat down on a rock that may or may not have existed and put my head between my knees like a green recruit on her first ride.

Here is the arithmetic I ran, there in the ghost grass of a ghost country, while the riders behind me waited and the man in front of me held a key:

If I stopped him, I preserved the crown's peace. The peace of a crown that unmakes countries and trims its servants' minds like hedges and calls the silence that follows *order*. Every truth I'd ever tasted in service of that order—I suddenly wanted to re-taste all of them. How many convictions had I delivered against people whose only crime was remembering? How many times had I stood in a courtroom, certain of my gift, certain of myself, while some poor soul swore they'd lived in a village that no longer appeared on any map, and I had tasted no lie in them and concluded—*concluded*—that the map must be right?

The map. I had spent my whole career believing the map.

"Bram," I said. "The door. When it opens. What does it cost?"

"Everything," he said. "Every memory of the valley. Of my life. Of Tam." He said the last name like setting down something breakable. "The gate takes payment from whoever turns the key. That's the oath. That's always been the oath."

"And you mean to pay it anyway."

"Aldermere has ten thousand people dying of forgetting a little more each year. I have one life made of sweeping floors." He shrugged, and it was the shrug of the gatehouse, the clock-winder's shrug, the smallest man in the world offering the biggest thing in it. "It's not a hard sum, Inquisitor."

It wasn't. That was the horror of it. I had come up that hill expecting a liar, a traitor, a man to crack open—and instead I'd found the only person in either country whose insides matched his outsides, and the sum he was doing was correct, and I hated it, and I believed it, and belief, I am professionally qualified to report, tastes like cold clean water after twenty years of drinking from the crown's well.

Behind me, Captain Ellery shouted something about positions. The riders were forming up. I had perhaps a minute before duty arrived in numbers, and duty in numbers does not sit down on rocks or reconsider its childhood.

I thought about my mother. Not the memory—there was no memory, just the hole where one should be, smooth-walled and tidy. Someone had made that hole. Someone with a seal I saluted under every morning of my adult life.

"Vane!" Ellery again, closer now.

Bram watched me. He didn't beg. That would have made it easier, and nothing about this was going to be easy.

I stood up. I drew my baton of office—the iron rod I'd carried since I was nineteen, the symbol of the crown's truth—and I turned, and I pointed it back down the hill at my own riders, and I heard myself say, in the voice I used for courtrooms and executions:

"The prisoner is under formal inquisitorial detention. He is to be questioned by me, alone, per crown statute twelve. Any interference with an active reading constitutes obstruction of the Inquisition." A beat. "Captain, if you come one step closer, I will read every man of you in front of the others, and we'll see whose careers survive what's in there."

Ellery hesitated. Good men hesitate; that's how you find them.

"You heard the statute," I snapped. "Fall back to the tree line. That is an order sealed with my office, and my office outranks your captaincy by a margin you should be grateful I'm not currently calculating."

They fell back. Horses stepped through puddles that weren't there. Somewhere in the birches, a bird sang in a dialect I suddenly, absurdly, half-recognized.

I turned back to Bram. He was staring at me with an expression I recognized from mirrors: the look of a person watching their whole architecture quietly collapse.

"Why?" he asked.

"Because you're telling the truth," I said, and my voice cracked down the middle like cheap crockery, "and I have just discovered I don't know what else in my life is." I wiped my face with the back of my glove. "Go. Open your door. Pay your price. And when whatever's on the other side comes through—" I looked up the hill, at the shimmer, at the brass bells I could almost hear ringing in a city that had no business ringing anywhere. "When it comes through, Quentin—or Bram, or whatever the gate decides you are—you make sure it remembers there were people in the crown who weren't trimmed all the way down to the quick."

He nodded once. Turned the key.

And the door in the hillside began, very softly, to sing.

* * *

What happened next belongs to Bram's telling, not mine—though I will say this much, because a truth-reader's testimony should count for something: when Tam came running up that hill shouting Bram's name, and when the two of them swore the old oath together, splitting the gate's price between them like brothers dividing bread, I stood witness with my hand on my baton and my back to the tree line, and I did not taste a single lie.

Not one.

For the first time in twenty years, everything I heard was true.
