## 9. The Oath at the Door

The door in the hillside was smaller than I'd imagined, which felt like a joke the world had been building toward for thirty years. All that whispering, all that longing, all those brass bells ringing through my dreams—and at the end of it, a door no grander than the one on Tam's larder.

It stood alone in the face of the hill, framed in iron gone green with age, and it hummed. Not a sound you heard so much as one you felt in your teeth, in the soft parts behind your eyes. The Hollow Gate, older than the crown that had buried its country, waiting for me like it had all the time in the world.

Which, I supposed, it did.

I put my hand flat against the iron, and the humming changed pitch—warmer, almost pleased—and then the price arrived.

Not gradually. Not politely. It came the way winter comes to the valley: all at once, from every direction, with total confidence that it belongs there.

*Every memory,* the gate said, in a voice like my own voice heard underwater. *Every hour of the valley. Every name. The boy who fished the cold stream. The keeper who swept the gatehouse steps ten thousand mornings. The friend.*

"The friend," I said aloud, because saying it made it less real, and I have always been a coward about real things.

I saw it laid out before me the way Serah must see lies—all at once, whole, undeniable. Thirty years of smallness. The taste of well water. The way fog came down the valley in October like something being poured. Tam's laugh, which started in his belly and took the scenic route to his mouth. The ledger with my false name in it. The decree nailed to the post. The first lie I ever told, and the second, and the look on Tam's face when he asked too many questions and I gave him too few answers.

All of it. Taken as payment. A door does not open for love; it opens for an even trade, and the trade was everything I knew of home.

And here is the terrible thing—the thing I'll never be able to explain to anyone, least of all to the stranger I was about to become: I would have paid it. Gladly. I stood there with my hand on the iron and I grieved, but I did not hesitate, because I had spent thirty years wanting nothing, and now I wanted this one thing so completely that everything else felt like kindling.

"Bram!"

I knew the voice before I knew I knew it. That's the trouble with a lifetime of knowing someone—they get into the marrow, past where any gate can reach.

Tam came up the hillside at a dead run, coat flapping, hat gone somewhere behind him, and behind him—God help us both—came Serah Vane on the crown's grey mare, and behind her, faint but gaining, the sound of riders.

"You absolute fool," Tam said, reaching me, grabbing my shoulders, shaking me like I was a rug he meant to beat the truth out of. "You were going to do it. You were going to pay it all."

"It has to be paid."

"No." He said it the way he said things when he'd already fought the argument with himself and won. "No, it doesn't. Listen to me. The Remnant's elder—the old woman, Maren—she told me before I left. There's another way. An old way. The oath."

"There's always an oath," I said. "Oaths are how we got here."

"Not this one." His hands were trembling. I'd known him thirty years and I could count the times I'd seen them tremble on one hand, and now they wouldn't stop. "The founding oath. The one the first keepers swore. It splits the cost. Two keepers, two halves of the memory. The gate takes from both or neither."

I stared at him. "There are no two keepers. There never were."

"There are now." He said it simply, the way he said everything that mattered. "I defected. Do you understand what that means? Serah will have my name by nightfall either way. My family's gone east to my sister's, beyond the crown's reach, because I'm not an idiot, Bram, whatever else I am. I burned my bridges with fire I stole myself." He laughed—a cracked, awful laugh. "I reported you. I did that. And I can't undo it. But I can stand here."

"Tam—"

"You don't get to refuse," he said. "That's the whole point of what I did. You don't get to carry it alone and call it nobility. I've watched you do that for thirty years—carry things alone, quietly, like a man stacking stones nobody asked him to stack. Well. Not this time. This time I stack some of the stones."

Behind him, Serah Vane dismounted. Her face was pale and set, and she looked like a woman arguing with herself in a language only she could hear.

"The riders," she said. "Crown riders, six of them, maybe eight minutes behind me. Whatever you're going to do, do it quickly, and know that I am about to commit treason, and that I've decided not to think about it until afterward, if there is an afterward."

"You're letting him open it?" Tam said.

"I read him yesterday," Serah said. "I read a man whose entire life is true. And then I read myself, and found gaps. Neat ones. Crown-made." Her jaw tightened. "I don't serve liars. I only ever thought I did."

The humming of the gate deepened. It had heard. Of course it had heard—it was a door; doors hear everything said in front of them.

*Two keepers may swear the old oath,* it said. *The cost divided. The bond permanent. What one forgets, the other remembers for him. What one loses, the other keeps.*

"What does that mean?" I asked. "Practically. I'd like the practical version."

*He will remember your valley,* the gate said. *You will remember his. Neither of you will remember sharing it.*

I looked at Tam. Tam looked at me.

"That's—" he started.

"Cruel," I finished. "That's cruel. We split the cost and then we can't even split the remembering. One of us gets the fishing, one of us gets the winters. One of us gets your mother's kitchen and the other gets mine, and neither of us knows why the other one looks sad sometimes."

"Yes," Tam said. "Probably. Yes."

"And you still want to?"

"I want you to exist tomorrow," Tam said, "and I want Aldermere to exist, and those are the same want. Everything else is details." He swallowed hard. "Besides. Think of it this way. In a year, you'll tell me a story about your valley, and it'll be my valley too, and neither of us will know why the other one cries at the good parts. That's not nothing, Bram. That might be everything."

The riders' sound had grown. Horses. The creak of leather. Somewhere below, a horn blew twice—the signal for a sealed perimeter, though sealing a country that doesn't exist seemed optimistic even for the crown.

"How do we swear it?" I asked the gate.

*Hands on the iron. Both keepers. Speak the words after me.*

We put our hands on the door. Tam's hand next to mine, big and work-roughed and shaking, and I thought: this is the last time I'll know why touching his hand feels like home. I thought: let me spend it well. Let me notice everything. The scar on his knuckle from the trap he set when we were nine. The smell of lamp oil on his sleeve. The fact that he bites the inside of his cheek when he's frightened and thinks no one sees.

I noticed. I spent it. I hoarded it like a miser at the end of the world.

*Speak.*

The words came, and they were old—not our tongue's ordinary words, but close cousins, words with dirt under their fingernails. The gate spoke a line; we spoke it back. It was about doors and thresholds. It was about debt and payment. It was about two people holding one rope over a long drop, each trusting the other's weight.

And then it came to the last line, and the last line was the price, itemized, and the gate recited my life to me in a single breath:

*The valley. The gatehouse. The stream. The fog of October. The mother's grave. The friend. The friend. The friend.*

"The friend twice," I whispered.

*Some costs weigh double,* the gate said, and began to take.

* * *

I remember it as light. That's the honest truth, strange as it sounds—I remember the losing as light, gold-white, pouring in through the place where my memories had been, filling the rooms as the furniture went out of them. I gripped Tam's hand. He gripped mine. Somewhere very far away Serah Vane shouted words I didn't catch, and horses screamed, and steel rang against steel, and none of it mattered as much as the light.

I felt the valley go first—the shape of it, the map of it drawn on my heart, erased like chalk from a slate. Then the gatehouse, room by room, the stairs I'd swept, the window that stuck in summer. Then the stream, cold on my ankles, gone. Then—

Then Tam. Thirty years of Tam, folding up like a letter being returned to its sender, and I held on as long as I could, I held the laugh and the knuckle-scar and the biting of the cheek, I held them right up to the last instant, and then the gate took them, gently, the way you'd take a book from a sleeping child.

And in the empty rooms the light settled, and I turned the key.

* * *

Sound. Enormous, rolling sound—the hillside opening like a chest unclenching, the door swinging wide onto a country pouring itself back into existence. Brass bells, hundreds of them, ringing across a landscape assembling itself out of half-memory and stubborn hope. Fields stitching themselves green. A river remembering which way was downhill. A city on the far horizon raising its towers like a man standing up after a long illness.

Aldermere. Whole. Real. Mine to look at, and no one's to take.

I stood in the doorway, swaying, and turned to say something to Tam—something about the bells, probably, something wry, because that's the sort of man I am, whoever exactly I turned out to be.

I saw a stranger looking back at me. A broad, weathered man with tears running freely down his face, gripping my hand like it was the only solid thing in the new world.

"Quentin," he said—no. No, that wasn't right. "Bram," he corrected himself, testing the word, and smiled through the tears. "Bram Winterbourne. Welcome home. Sort of."

"Do I know you?" I asked, and watched something break behind his eyes and mend itself by sheer force of will.

"You're going to," he said. "Come on. There's someone you should meet—an old woman named Maren, and a truth-reader who just committed treason for you, and a whole country that owes you everything. And—" he paused, wiped his face with his sleeve, and grinned the grin of a man carrying a weight for two, "—I've got stories. Apparently I've got all the stories. Thirty years of them. You're going to love them. They've got you in them."

Below us, Serah Vane sat on her horse amid three unconscious riders and one very confused lieutenant, watching the horizon where the crown's roads now led to a country that officially had never existed—and now, undeniably, did.

She caught my eye, and she didn't smile. She pointed east, toward the borderlands, toward the places the maps still showed as blank.

"There are more," she called up. "Other valleys. Other doors. And the crown is going to notice this one missing from its leash very soon."

The bells rang on. Tam squeezed my hand once—familiar, warm, a stranger's hand—and said, "Well, Bram. Shall we go be remembered?"
