## 10. A Stranger in Aldermere

The bells woke me on my first morning in a country I had supposedly saved.

They were brass bells, hung from the eaves of every house in the lower city, and when the wind came off the eastern hills they all rang at once—a sound like a thousand small arguments, bright and insistent and utterly indifferent to my sleep. In the valley—*in the valley that was*, I corrected myself, though the correction meant nothing to me now—the mornings had been silent. Frost on the window, the kettle's low complaint, Tam banging about in the gatehouse kitchen pretending he knew how to make porridge. No bells. I would know; everyone told me there had been no bells.

I did not remember it. That was the arrangement.

I lay in the guest room of the elder Maren's house—which was not really a guest room, since until three months ago Aldermere had not existed and therefore could not have guests—and watched the light come through shutters carved with interlocking rings, the old sigil of a kingdom the crown had spent forty years unwriting. The light was real. The shutter-cuts were real. The faint smell of coal smoke and river water and someone frying onions two streets over was so aggressively real that I nearly laughed.

A country restored does not do things by halves. It comes back *hungry*.

Downstairs, Tam was already at the table, and across from him sat my own history, bound in leather, wearing Tam's face.

That is unkind. Tam has been patient beyond all deserving. But you must understand what it is like to sit across breakfast from a man who keeps saying *remember when*, and to have nothing to offer him but an attentive expression. He tells me about the valley the way a man tells stories about his brother who died young—reverently, with too much care, checking my face after each detail to see if anything landed.

"The frost on the gatehouse window," he said this morning, pushing the honey toward me without being asked, because apparently I take honey, "used to make shapes. You swore one of them was your mother's handwriting once."

"Did I." I stirred my tea. "What did she write?"

"I don't know. You never said. You just stood there with your sleeve pressed to the glass for a long time." He smiled at his plate. "You were very dramatic about frost."

"I retain the capacity," I said. "I'm told I'm dramatic about bells."

"You haven't heard them long enough."

Three months of bells. But he was right in the way that mattered: I hadn't heard them *long enough*. There is a difference between hearing a thing and having heard it all your life, between knowing a sound and being made of it. My knowledge of the valley was exactly that—knowledge. Book knowledge. After the gate took its price, Tam and Serah had spent a week writing everything down for me, every remembered detail, and then Maren's archivists copied it fair into a volume titled, with bureaucratic cruelty, *Winterbourne, B.: Restored Context, Vol. 1.*

I have read it four times. It reads like someone else's beautifully documented life. A keeper named Bram Winterbourne tended a hollow gate for thirty years. He liked honey in his tea. His best friend was a cooper's son named Tam who lied badly and loved fiercely. He gave up everything he had to open a door, and what he felt about that—whether it had been joy or grief or some third thing with no name—is recorded nowhere, because the only man who ever felt it forgot it in the same hour.

Some mornings I want to shake that book and demand it tell me whether I regretted it.

Most mornings, I look out at the bells ringing in the wind and think: *this.* This is what it bought. Whatever the price column says, look at the purchase.

* * *

Aldermere in its fourth month of existence again is a country learning to walk.

The maps were redrawn first—that was Serah's doing, before her defection was even formal, walking the original charts into the Office of Survey with her inquisitor's seal still on her coat and daring anyone to question her. Cartographers wept, I am told, though whether from awe or the sheer workload of forty years of falsified geography, no one agrees.

Then came the borders, which simply *were* again one morning, invisible lines that farmers on either side could suddenly feel the way you feel weather coming. Then trade, envoys, the first ship into the harbor at Bellhollow flying Aldermere's interlocked rings beside the crown's own colors—an insult so profound that three kingdoms sent protests, and Maren framed the letters and hung them in the council hall like trophies.

The Remnant are Remnant no longer, though they keep the name out of habit and pride. They had been dying by increments, sustained only by telling each other stories of a place no map admitted to. Now their stories had weight again. Buildings that had stood half-faded for decades snapped into full color overnight. Old men who had begun to go translucent at the edges—you learn not to flinch at descriptions of that—woke solid, hungry, loud.

And me, in the middle of it. The stranger who saved them.

They are kind to me, which is somehow worse than if they weren't. Shopkeepers refuse my coin. Children follow me through the lower city chanting something about the Keeper of the Hollow Gate that Tam refuses to translate because it is, he says, "embarrassingly heroic and factually wrong." At the midwinter feast—held late, because a country returning from nonexistence keeps its own calendar—they gave me a bell cast in my honor, small enough to hold in one hand, and I rang it and felt nothing but the shape of the metal against my palm, and everyone applauded a feeling I could not find in myself.

I kept the bell anyway. I keep expecting the sentiment to arrive retroactively, like delayed post. It hasn't. But I hold the bell sometimes in the evenings, and I think: *somewhere in me, under whatever scar the gate left, this meant something. Be patient with yourself.*

Tam found me doing exactly that last night, sitting in Maren's garden with the bell in my hand and Volume 1 open on my knee.

"You're reading yourself again," he said. "You always get quiet after."

"It's a good book. Shame about the protagonist. He seems like he'd have been interesting to know."

"He'd have liked you," Tam said, and then winced, because we both heard the strangeness of it. He sat down next to me on the cold bench. For a while neither of us spoke, and the bells rang their thousand small arguments in the dark.

"I carry it for both of us," he said finally. "The valley. I know that's not fair to ask you to be grateful for. But I carry it, and when you want a piece of it—" He hesitated. "You could just ask, you know. Instead of interrogating a book."

"What would you tell me?"

"That the last thing you said to him before you turned the key was a joke. A bad one. About how he'd better visit." Tam's voice caught and steadied. "You laughed. Both of you. Right up until the gate started taking, and then he looked afraid for exactly four seconds, and then he looked peaceful, and then he was you."

The wind moved through the garden. Somewhere below, a drunk was singing an Aldemish drinking song with tremendous confidence and no accuracy.

"Thank you," I said, which was inadequate, and we both let it be.

* * *

Serah Vane arrived on the third of Thawmonth, riding hard, with mud to the saddle and a look on her face that I had learned to read as *bad news delivered efficiently.*

She had defected properly by then—resigned her commission in a letter the crown legal office was still arguing over, taken a small house near the archive, and developed, to her visible disgust, a reputation among the Remnant as a hero. She bore this the way she bore everything: like an insult she intended to survive.

She did not sit when she reached Maren's garden. She stood at the table, pulled a folded paper from her coat, and put it down in front of me.

"We need to talk about the gate," she said.

"The gate is open," I said. "It's been open for months. It's very satisfied with itself. The whole country is ringing bells about it."

"And the crown knows what it cost you." Her eyes went to Tam, then back. "Both of you. Half-price each. Which means the oath works, which means the gate can be opened *again*. Cheaply. By anyone who can find another keeper and split another price." She tapped the paper. "This came through channels I probably shouldn't still have. It's a survey order, sealed before Aldermere came back. They catalogued other places. Other valleys, other towns, whole provinces, filed away under forgetting writs the same as yours."

Tam had gone very still. "How many?"

Serah's face didn't move, which from Serah was its own kind of mercy.

"Eleven," she said. "That we know of. And Bram—" she paused, and for a moment the inquisitor's flatness slipped, and underneath it was something older and more frightened, the girl whose own childhood had been trimmed by the same crown, "—the writs list keepers. Every erased place kept a gate, and every gate kept a keeper, and the crown wrote down all their names. Yours is crossed off. The others aren't."

I picked up the paper. Eleven names in a clerk's careful hand, eleven doors in hillsides, eleven prices waiting in the dark to be bargained down. I did not remember wanting anything, once. Now wanting was nearly all I had.

"Well," I said, folding the list and putting it in my coat, where the iron key's absence still felt like a missing tooth. "Someone should tell them the gates open both ways."

Tam sighed the sigh of a man who already knew how this story went. Serah almost smiled. Above the garden wall, the brass bells took the evening wind and rang out over a country that existed again—loud, unfinished, alive—and began, all together, to call me toward the next door.
