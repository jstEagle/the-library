## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The bone had been waiting on his bench for three days, and Cyrus Thackeray had been avoiding it for two of them.

It was a good bone — a femur from a hill ox, thick as a man's wrist, cured in brine until it had gone the color of old ivory and taken on that faint sweet smell of salt and stone that all properly preserved bones carried. It belonged to Widow Corren's boy. Tam. Drowned in the millrace in the spring flood, eleven years old, and now the frost was coming down off the peaks and the ground would be too hard to dig by month's end, and so the widow had brought her son to Grayhollow wrapped in oilcloth like a bolt of linen, and paid Cyrus in silver and eggs and an apology for the smell.

There was no smell. There never was. But grieving people apologized for things they could not name, and Cyrus had learned long ago to take the apology along with the coin.

He sat at his bench with the lamp turned low — he always worked low; bright light made the grain of the bone look flat and dishonest — and held the femur against his palm the way another man might hold a sleeping bird. The whisper came up through his skin almost at once, thin and papery, the way it always did with the young dead.

*—cold, it was cold, I didn't mean to go in after the—*

"I know," Cyrus murmured. "I know, lad. You don't have to tell me."

That was the whole craft, if you wanted the truth of it. Anyone could carve a memorial bone. Any carver in any market town could put a name and a date into a femur and hang it over a door and call it done. What Cyrus did was different, though he'd stopped explaining the difference years ago, because the explanation always sounded like either madness or fraud. He listened while he carved. The dead left something in their bones — not a soul, nothing so grand, but an echo, a residue, the way a fire leaves warmth in a hearthstone hours after the flames are out. And when he cut into the bone with the right knives, at the right angle, following the grain instead of fighting it, the echo spoke. Not words, exactly. More like weather. Grief had a pressure to it, love had a temperature, fear moved through the marrow in gusts. He carved what he heard, and what he carved became a kind of vessel, and the families who hung those vessels above their doors swore the house felt less empty afterward.

Cyrus thought that was mostly grief doing its own work. But he carved anyway. It was honest labor, and it kept Maren's knife sharp.

That last thought arrived, as it always did, with its small familiar weight, and he set it aside the way you set aside a stone you've carried so long your hand has grown to its shape.

He chose his knife. The smallest one — a sliver of gray steel no longer than a finger, its handle worn to the print of his thumb. He set the blade against the femur just below the head, where the grain ran true, and drew the first line.

The whisper stuttered.

*—cold, it was cold, I didn't mean to—*

and then nothing. Not fading. Not trailing off the way voices did when the echo thinned toward silence, which was a slow thing, a tide going out over hours. This was a door closing. One moment Tam Corren's drowned terror filled the marrow of his own bone, and the next moment there was nothing there at all, nothing, a hollow Cyrus could feel all the way up his arm, and his knife skidded across unresisting surface because there was nothing underneath it anymore to push against.

He sat very still.

Bones went quiet sometimes. He knew that. Old bones, badly kept bones, bones whose echoes had finally bled away into the air — he'd carved enough of them to know the particular dumbness of spent bone. But this bone was fresh. Three days dead. The echo should have lasted years. And it hadn't faded. It had *stopped*, mid-word, mid-terror, like a man interrupted.

"Tam?" he said, and felt immediately foolish, and said it again anyway. "Lad?"

Nothing. The workshop was silent in a way workshops were never silent. There should have been the tick of the lamp's hot glass, the settle of the building, the wind working at the shutters, the far-off clatter of the inn below where Brannic was surely arguing with someone about the price of something. All of that was still there. But underneath it — beneath everything, the way the sea is beneath everything in a port town — there had always been the murmur. The low constant wash of the dead of Grayhollow, seeping up through the foundations, through the salt-packed walls, through the very mountain the town was cut into. Cyrus had heard it since he was six years old. He had heard it every day of his life, in every hour, waking and sleeping, so constantly that he had long ago stopped hearing it the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat — and now it was gone, and the absence roared.

He stood up so fast the stool went over behind him. The lamp flame bent and righted itself. His hands, he noticed distantly, were steady, which seemed wrong; hands ought to shake when the world broke.

He crossed to the window and threw open the shutter, and the cold poured in like water finding a breach, and he looked up.

No stars.

Not clouded — he knew clouded; Grayhollow lived under cloud half the year, and clouded skies had texture, movement, the gray-on-gray churn of weather passing over the peaks. This sky was clear. He could see the black shoulders of the Thornfell mountains against a darkness that was somehow darker than they were, a clean and perfect darkness, and there was nothing in it. No Salt Road constellation arcing over the pass. No Shepherd, no Lantern, no Wheel. The stars had been the one thing even the dullest smuggler navigated by, the one thing no war or winter or king had ever touched. They were older than the wards. They were older than the kingdom. They were simply *there*, the way stone was there, the way death was there.

And they were gone, and the sky looked flayed.

Cyrus gripped the windowsill and made himself breathe. Below him, the inn yard lay quiet, the stable lantern burning its small orange hole in the dark. Somewhere a dog began to bark — not the alarmed bark of a stranger, but the low, continuous, unhappy bark of an animal that could sense something wrong and had no word for it. Other dogs took it up, down the street, up the hill, until the whole town was muttering in its sleep.

Then the light changed.

It came from the east, from the direction of the border, and it was not dawn — it was hours yet before dawn, and dawn did not come green. But a pale luminescence was rising over the peaks of Thornfell like heat-shimmer given a color, a sickly glow the shade of foxfire, of rotting wood, of the light certain deep-sea fish wore to lure things close. Cyrus had seen the border wards once, years ago, from the high road: a lattice of pale lines laid over the passes like frost on a windowpane, humming faintly, older than any living memory, maintained by no one and belonging to everyone. They had looked permanent in the way only truly ancient things look permanent — not strong, exactly, but *assumed*. Part of the shape of the world.

Now they were coming apart, and they were coming apart like sugar.

That was the image that lodged in him and would not leave: sugar. He could see it clearly even at this distance, could see the great luminous threads of the ward-lattice losing their edges, softening, slumping, dissolving into glittering runnels that slid down the mountainsides and winked out. Not shattered. Not burned. *Dissolved*, the way a sugar figure dissolves in tea, gracefully, completely, without resistance — as though the wards themselves had decided to stop being, or as though something were drinking them.

The dogs were howling now. A shutter banged somewhere. In the inn below, boots on stairs, Brannic's voice, rough with sleep and alarm: "Thackeray! Thackeray, are you seeing this? Get down here!"

Cyrus did not move. He stood in the cold pouring through the window with his carving knife still in his hand — he had not put it down, he realized; he was gripping it the way a drowning man grips a spar — and he looked at the dying wards and the starless sky and he thought, with a clarity that frightened him more than anything else that night:

*This isn't weather. This isn't war. Wars don't reach the stars.*

Behind him, on the bench, the memorial bone lay in its bed of sawdust, blank and silent. And beside it, wrapped in waxed cloth at the back of the shelf where it had sat untouched for eleven years, the block of gray salt that had come to him with Maren's things — the block he had never once been able to bring himself to carve, and never once been able to throw away —

— the block was glowing. Faintly. Like a coal remembering fire.

"Cyrus!" Brannic again, closer now, on the stairs. "For the love of the deep, are you alive up there?"

"Coming," Cyrus heard himself say. His voice sounded like someone else's.

He looked back once at the shelf. The glow was pulsing now, slow and rhythmic, and though no sound reached him across the room — no sound could; salt held no voice, that was the whole cruelty of it, salt preserved flesh and nothing else — he found himself walking toward it anyway, knife in hand, the way you walk toward a door you've been afraid of your whole life.

Outside, over the broken border, the last thread of the oldest ward slid down the mountain and went out, and the first thing that had ever protected the world finished becoming nothing.

* * *

Brannic Odo stood in the inn's common room with his arms crossed and his jaw set, which was his posture for all emergencies, and the room behind him was full of everyone in Grayhollow who owned boots, which was nearly everyone, because nobody had gone back to sleep.

"The miller says it's an omen," Brannic said. "The ferryman's wife says it's the end of the world. Hesta Vane's boy swears he saw lights walking on the pass, figures, like men made of the same stuff as the wards." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Half of them want you to tell them it's nothing. The other half want you to tell them what it is. And I want you to tell me why you're looking at me like that."

"Like what?"

"Like you're listening to something behind me."

Cyrus closed his mouth. He had not realized he was doing it. But the habit of a lifetime dies hard, and twice tonight already he had caught himself turning his head toward sounds that weren't there — toward the place where the murmur of the dead should have been, the way a man missing a tooth keeps finding the gap with his tongue. The room was full of the living, warm and loud and smelling of tallow and spilled ale, and under all of it there was nothing. Flat nothing. He had never in his life been anywhere that flat.

"The wards didn't fall," he said.

"They looked pretty fallen to me."

"Wards fall when they're broken. Broken things break loudly — there's debris, there's backlash, the mountains ring with it for days. Ask any hedge-mage." He heard his own voice going flat and technical, the voice he used for pricing, and let it, because the alternative was the voice that shook. "Those wards dissolved. Something ate them. And whatever eats a ward that old has an appetite we don't have a word for."

The room had gone quiet around them. He hadn't meant to say it aloud. Grief does that, he thought distantly; it makes you honest at the worst possible moments.

"And the stars?" someone called from the back.

"I don't know about the stars." That was the truth, and the truth was worse than any theory, because the stars were not wards. The stars were not made or maintained or warded. Whatever had happened tonight had happened in two places at once — the border of the kingdom and the roof of the world — and no army he had ever heard of operated at both altitudes.

He climbed the stairs back to his workshop two at a time, and Brannic's voice followed him up — "Where are you going?" — and he did not answer, because the answer was insane.

The salt block was still glowing. Stronger now. And as he lifted it from the shelf, unwrapped the waxed cloth with fingers that had finally begun to tremble, and laid it on the bench beside the blank bone of Tam Corren, the pulse of it steadied into something almost like breathing. Almost like speech.

Eleven years ago they had shipped him his sister's remains in a crate of packing salt, because Maren had died on the Salt Road, far from any carver, and the caravan masters had done what caravan masters do. He had buried the crate unopened. He had kept the block. He had told himself it was sentiment. He had told himself a great many things.

Cyrus Thackeray picked up Maren's knife — her knife, the one she'd used, the one he'd inherited along with the salt and the silence — and set its edge against the block, and before he had even pressed, before the steel had touched the surface, a voice rose out of the salt like breath rising off cold water, and it was her voice, unchanged, unmistakable, eleven years dead and speaking as easily as if she'd just stepped out to the well:

*Cyrus. Oh, thank the deep — Cyrus, listen to me. The wards didn't fall.*

His knees hit the floor. He didn't remember them bending.

*They were eaten.*
