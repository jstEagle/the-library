## 3. The Fugitive in the Pass

Cyrus did not sleep. He sat at his bench with the lamp turned low and the salt block wrapped in three layers of wool beside him, and he listened to the only voice left in a world gone quiet.

*They didn't fall,* Maren said, from inside the grain of the salt, her words arriving the way water arrives through stone — slow, cold, and certain. *Cyrus. They were eaten.*

He had spent the hours since dawn doing what carvers do when the mind will not hold still: he worked. The memorial bone for old Tam Wickerlay — drowned in the millrace two winters past, finally paid for by a grandson with more guilt than coin — lay half-finished under his burin, the curling water-motifs taking shape around Tam's name. But every few strokes Cyrus stopped, lifted the bone near his ear out of habit, and heard nothing. Not silence exactly. Silence was a thing with texture; this was absence, a hole shaped like sound.

*Say something else,* he whispered to the salt. *Anything. Tell me about the winter you stole my boots. Tell me about—*

*I'm here,* she said, faint as breath on a window. *I'm here, I'm here. But Cyrus — if the wards were eaten, then whatever ate them is still hungry.*

That was when the dogs started.

Grayhollow kept six dogs between the inn and the tannery, and they were lazy, ill-tempered animals that barked at carts and children with equal indifference. What came down from the pass now was not barking. It was a long, rising wail, one dog and then all of them, the sound of animals smelling something that had no right to be smelled.

Cyrus blew out the lamp before he knew he'd decided anything.

* * *

The high pass above Grayhollow had no name on any map, which was the point of it. Smugglers called it the Needle's Eye — a throat of rock where the mountain pinched itself narrow and the old ward-stones stood shoulder to shoulder like a jury that had never reached a verdict. Cyrus had run contraband through it since he was fourteen: salt-spirits, unlicensed iron, letters that needed to travel faster than honesty. He knew its moods the way a husband knows a wife's silences.

He climbed it now with a lantern shuttered to a knife-blade of light and Maren's salt block strapped against his chest beneath his coat, because he could not bear to leave it behind and could not explain why. The wind came down off the peaks carrying a smell he had never met before and recognized anyway: scorched sugar. The same smell as the night the wards crumbled. Something had burned sweetly up here, and recently.

The first hunter he found was dead.

The man lay in the lee of the third ward-stone, and Cyrus knelt over him with his heart hammering before his eyes made sense of what they saw. No wound. That was the wrongness of it — the man wore leathers stitched with the boar-and-broken-crown sigil of the new regime at Thornfell, a sword still belted at his hip, a face gray and composed as a sleeping child's. And he was empty. Not dead the way men die, with blood and slackness. Dead the way a glove is empty. Cyrus touched the man's wrist — cold, but not grave-cold, cold like a thing that had never been warm — and snatched his hand back.

*Don't,* Maren breathed against his sternum. *Cyrus. Don't touch them. They're not men anymore. They're what's left after something eats the middle.*

"The middle," Cyrus whispered. "Gods' teeth, Mare, what talks like that?"

*I do. I'm dead. I'm allowed.*

He almost laughed, and the almost-laugh saved him, because it kept his hands steady while he searched the body. He found a folded paper in the breast pocket: a warrant, written in a hand too elegant for a soldier's kit. *Seeking: a man of middling height, traveling alone or with escort, who may claim any name. Do not trust his face. Do not trust his wounds. If found alive, deliver to the Regent's road-wardens at Thornfell. If found dead, burn the body and report the ashes.* Below, in smaller script, as if added later by a different and hungrier pen: *He believes he is already dead. Let him believe it. Belief makes the carrying easier.*

Cyrus read it twice. His mouth had gone dry as bone dust.

A fugitive who believed himself dead. Hunters who weren't hunters. A regime that wanted a corpse burned and reported rather than displayed — which meant the corpse mattered, which meant the corpse was dangerous, which meant—

Which meant it was none of his business. That was the arithmetic he'd lived by for thirty-one years. Carve your bones, keep your head, pay the Guild, don't ask what's in other men's packs. Grayhollow survived because everyone in it understood that the mountains ate the curious first.

He was folding the warrant back into the dead man's pocket when the second hunter screamed.

* * *

The sound came from higher up, past the pinch of the Needle's Eye, where the pass opened into a bowl of scree below the old shrine. It was not a scream of pain. It was a scream of command — a single wordless note that bent the air, and every loose stone on the slope shivered, and somewhere below him the empty man's sword rattled in its sheath as though trying to draw itself.

Cyrus did the cowardly thing first. He put out his light and pressed himself into the shadow of the ward-stone, and he told himself, clearly and reasonably, that whatever happened above the pinch was above his pay. He was a bone-carver. He carved names into the dead so the living could visit them. That was the whole of his ambition and the whole of his virtue, and he intended to die old and unremarkable in the workshop above the inn, with sawdust in his beard and Maren's salt block on the shelf—

*Maren's salt block.* The thought arrived like a hook behind the eye. Because if the wards had been eaten, and if the dead everywhere else had been silenced, then the voice in his arms was the last one. The only witness left speaking. And whoever had eaten the wards would want that voice the way a fire wants a draft.

They wouldn't have to find it. He was carrying it up the mountain toward them.

"Damn you," he said softly — to the salt, to his sister, to the dead man at his feet, to the whole collapsing architecture of his sensible life. "Damn you all the way down."

Then he went up.

* * *

He found the fugitive at the edge of the shrine's broken circle, backed against the altar-stone with his hands raised, palms out, in the posture of a man surrendering to something only he could see.

Later, Cyrus would try to describe him and fail. The descriptions that came were all wrong in small ways. Of middling height — yes, but standing he seemed taller than he was, the way ruined towers seem taller than ruins. Unmarked — yes, and that was the horror of it, because his leathers were slashed across the belly and shoulder, cut clean through, and beneath the cuts the skin was whole. Not scarred. Not healed. Whole, pink and unbothered, as though the blades had passed through a place he wasn't using anymore. Blood had soaked him dark from collar to knee, and it was drying in flakes, and there was no wound anywhere to account for it. None of it was his. Cyrus understood that with a carver's certainty about surfaces — this was a man wearing someone else's death like a coat that wouldn't stay buttoned.

And he was talking. That was the worst of it. He stood in the wind with two hunters closing along the rim of the bowl — Cyrus could see them now, moving with that wrong gliding gait, empty men in boar-and-broken-crown leathers — and the fugitive was talking to the altar-stone in a cracked, courteous, half-gone voice.

"—no, I understand, I understand, you've had your instructions. I only wish someone had told me sooner. It would have spared everyone such trouble." He laughed, a dry papery sound. "You see, I can't come with you. I'm already dead. I died — I'm nearly sure it was Tuesday."

One of the hunters raised its hand, and the hand was not entirely a hand anymore. The fingers had gone long and pale as candle-wax, and the shadows around it bent inward.

Every instinct Cyrus owned — thirty-one years of them, each one load-bearing — screamed at him to stay behind the stone. The sensible life. The sawdust and the shelf. He watched the wax-fingered hand begin to close, and he thought of a warrant that said *let him believe it*, and he thought of Maren saying *whatever ate them is still hungry*, and he thought — absurdly, unforgivably — of Tam Wickerlay's memorial bone sitting half-finished on his bench, a name waiting in the grain for a man who would never come to visit it.

"No," Cyrus said, and stepped out of the shadow, and heard his own voice crack like green wood. "No, he doesn't."

Both hunters turned. Their faces were the dead man's face from below — gray, composed, empty as gloves — and yet they turned with a kind of attention that made his skin crawl, the attention of things that had been given a task and nothing else.

"He's mine," Cyrus said, louder, and the lie came up out of some cellar of himself he hadn't known he possessed. "Claimed under Guild passage-right. Salt-road law. You want him, you file with the Ferryman's Guild at Vessamy, and you can wait a season like everyone else."

It was nonsense. Half of it was invented. But the words *Guild* and *passage-right* landed on the hunters like thrown water, and they hesitated — hesitated the way machines hesitate, recalculating — and in that hesitation Cyrus did the bravest and stupidest thing of his life. He ran into the open bowl, got his shoulder under the fugitive's arm, and hauled.

The man was lighter than he should have been. That was wrong too, another entry in the ledger of wrong, but there was no time for the ledger. The hunters shrieked — both together, one note, bending the air — and Cyrus dragged the king-shaped man (he did not know yet, would not let himself know) toward the crack in the shrine's north wall. The crack looked like nothing. It looked like the mountain shrugging. Only someone who had squeezed through it at fourteen with a pack of untaxed iron on his back would ever guess it opened onto the descent no map admitted to: the Understairs, the smugglers' spine, forty miles of road that existed nowhere except in the heads of maybe nine living people.

Eight, now. One of them was empty on the ward-stone below.

"Who—" the fugitive gasped, stumbling, bleeding someone else's blood. "Who are you?"

"Nobody," Cyrus said. "Keep moving."

"You shouldn't have—" The man's voice broke apart and reassembled somewhere else entirely, suddenly lucid, suddenly terrible in its clarity. "You should not have done that. They'll follow the debt now. Whatever you've claimed, they'll collect. Don't you understand? There's nothing here to save." He struck his own chest with the heel of his hand, hard, twice, the sound flat as knocking on a door no one was behind. "There is *nothing here*."

"I heard you the first time," Cyrus panted. "You're dead. Congratulations. Now move, because the dead are the only ones they're not eating."

Behind them the shriek rose again, closer, and the light in the bowl behind them dimmed — not clouded, *dimmed*, as if the day itself were being swallowed — and Cyrus shoved them both into the crack in the mountain and pulled the dark in after them.

* * *

The Understairs took them down in switchbacks through the mountain's own marrow — corridors of stone so old they had forgotten being quarried, where the walls sweated brine and the air tasted of iron and deep time. Cyrus moved by feel and memory, counting turns the way other men counted prayers. Behind them, muffled by a hundred fathoms of rock, the shrieking went on for a long while, hunting along the rim of the bowl. Then, gradually, it changed. It stopped sounding like pursuit.

It started sounding like conversation.

"They're talking to each other," the fugitive whispered. He had gone quiet in the bad way, walking when guided, stopping when stopped, his whole self drawn inward like a tide going out. "Or — no. Reporting. You don't understand what you've done, carver. It doesn't send men. It sends *reminders*. Everything it touches, it hears through. That man you robbed it of—" he touched his own slashed, unbroken leathers, wonderingly, "—that man was me, four days ago. It wears what it catches. And now it has your voice to wear next."

"Then it'll be disappointed," Cyrus said. "Nobody remembers my voice either."

The laugh that came out of the fugitive was awful — half sob, half delight, the laugh of a man who has fallen so far that falling further is funny. He slid down the wall to sit in the dripping dark, and Cyrus crouched opposite him, and for a moment neither spoke. Water ticked somewhere in the black. Against Cyrus's chest, faint as a pulse through a wall, he felt the salt block warm.

*Ask him his name,* Maren whispered, so quietly he might have imagined it.

"I won't," Cyrus said aloud. "Don't tell me. Names are how things get collected."

The fugitive looked up. In the dark his face was nothing but wet highlights and shadow, and for one moment the madness drained out of it entirely, and what looked back at Cyrus was tired beyond measure — a laborer's tiredness, a shepherd's, a man who had carried something heavy up a very long hill.

"That," the man said softly, "is the wisest thing anyone has said to me in a year." He drew a breath. "But you've claimed me under your Guild laws, master carver. Passage-right. So you should know what you're carrying, because debts attach to the cargo." His hands, Cyrus noticed, had begun to shake. "I am Aldric Thorne. I was crowned at the Salt Throne nineteen years ago. And three nights ago I walked out of Thornfell castle past my own guards, who looked at me and saw no one at all — because the thing wearing my crown has finished learning my face, and I am no longer useful enough to keep alive, and not quite finished enough to be allowed to stop."

The name went through Cyrus like cold water through cloth. He had carved the coronation year into a dozen civic bones. Every schoolchild in the marches could recite the dynasty. The king was dead — the heralds had said it gently, the king had died of a wasting fever in the spring, the Regent mourned publicly and ruled well—

"The Regent," Cyrus said. His mouth had gone stiff. "The drowned king's crown. That's—"

"A parasite," Aldric said simply. "Older than the throne. Older, I think, than the stars it ate. And it is very, very hungry, and you have just put yourself between its teeth." He smiled, and it was the most terrible smile Cyrus had ever seen, because it was grateful. "Run if you can. I mean that. I release you from your claim. But I warn you—" he leaned his head back against the weeping stone, and closed his eyes, and his voice dropped to the tone of a man reading his own epitaph, "—it already knows your sister's voice. It sang to me with it, the night the wards fell. It knows her name, Cyrus Thackeray. It knows everything the dead knew."

Above them, far off through the mountain's bones, the shrieking stopped altogether.

In the silence that followed, against his chest, Maren's salt block had gone cold as river ice — and for the first time since the stars went out, she said nothing at all.
