## 4. What the Crown Wants

The cave above Grayhollow had no name on any map Cyrus knew, which was precisely why he'd chosen it. It sat behind a curtain of hanging roots that had grown thick enough to weave shut, and it smelled of cold stone and old water and, faintly, of the salt block wrapped in oilcloth against his chest. The king lay by the small fire Cyrus dared only when the wind was loud, because the wind could hide the light and the king's breathing was loud enough to hide most other sounds.

Most other sounds. Not all.

"There were stars in my mouth," Aldric said, conversationally, to the ceiling of the cave. "When they took them from the sky. Someone has to hold them. I volunteered. I was king. Volunteering is what kings are *for*."

Cyrus fed a twist of dried heather to the fire and said nothing. He had learned in three days of hiding that the king's madness came in tides. There were hours when Aldric Thorne spoke like a man drowning — every sentence a fistful of something dragged up from too deep, half mud, half gold — and there were hours, rarer, when his eyes cleared and he spoke like a man reading his own death warrant aloud for practice. The drowning hours were more frequent after dark.

The dark lasted longer now. That was the thing no one wanted to say plainly: with the stars gone, the nights had grown teeth. Cyrus had walked these mountains his whole life and had never once needed a candle to find the path at midnight. Now midnight was a wall you touched with your hands.

"Hold still," Cyrus said, and unwrapped the bandage from the king's shoulder.

The wound beneath was wrong in a way that made the back of Cyrus's neck prickle, the way it did when he worked on a bone that had been *used*. Not festering. Not clean either. The edges of the cut had gone pale as candle wax, and where flesh should have been knitting itself closed, the wound simply sat, open and patient, like a door someone intended to come back through. Around it the skin had begun, very faintly, to show lines. Fine ones. Circular. Like the grain rings inside a carved tibia.

Like sigils.

Cyrus pressed the wound closed with practiced thumbs and did not let his face do anything. "This needs salt packing," he said. "It'll sting."

"It always stings," Aldric agreed pleasantly. "That's how you know it's honest. Lies don't sting. That's why everyone prefers them." He laughed, a short broken sound, and then his voice dropped into a register that made Cyrus's hands pause. "You hear them, don't you. The dead. You're one of the bone-listeners. I can tell because you flinch when I talk. Everyone flinches when I talk, but you flinch like a man hearing two conversations."

Cyrus packed the wound. The salt hissed faintly against whatever lived in it, and the king sighed like a man settling into a hot bath.

"There," Aldric murmured. "That's better. It hates that."

"What hates it?" Cyrus asked, before he could stop himself.

The king turned his head. In the firelight his eyes were clear — terrifyingly clear, the gray of deep water under ice — and for a moment Cyrus was looking not at a madman but at the man who had ruled Thornfell for nineteen years, whose face had been stamped on coins Cyrus had spent without a second thought.

"The crown," Aldric said. "Obviously."

* * *

**Here follows what the king told him, over three nights, in fragments. Some of it was fever. Most of it, Cyrus came to believe, was worse than fever.**

*It was my grandfather who found it. No — my great-grandfather. They blur. That is the first thing you must understand about crowns: they outlast the heads, so they learn to be patient, and patience looks like wisdom if you watch long enough.*

*It came to the family as a relic. A salvage. Drowned-king regalia pulled from the sea off Vessamy, sold to us by men who swore it had been blessed. It was beautiful. That is not an ornament of the story; that is the mechanism. It was beautiful the way a lure is beautiful, and we were fish, and we had been fish for so long that we had forgotten there was any other way to eat.*

*My father wore it for thirty years and died beloved. I wore it for nine before I understood what beloved meant. It means quieted. It means the court stops arguing. It means your enemies grow fond of you and your friends grow obedient, and you think: at last, I have become wise. And all the while something is drinking through the band of it, right here* —

*(he tapped his brow, and Cyrus heard the tap echo strangely, twice, the second tap coming a half-beat late)*

*— drinking the part of a king that argues with himself. Every ruler needs two voices, carver. The one that commands and the one that asks whether the command is just. Mine went first. I thought it was confidence. God help me, I thanked it for taking my doubts.*

*It doesn't want the kingdom. Kingdoms are just the bowl it drinks from. What it wants is the voice. One voice per king, swallowed whole, and it has eaten nine of mine now — my line kept it fed for four generations — but nine voices is thin soup, and it grew hungry, and then it looked up from the throne and saw the wards.*

*Do you know what the border wards actually are? Not walls. Never walls. They are the dead. All of them, every soul Thornfell ever buried along its borders, standing in ranks we cannot see, holding hands across the mountains, singing the world's edges shut. Your sister is in that song somewhere. My soldiers are in it. My father is in it. It is the oldest chorus in the world and it sings for free, because the dead owe the living nothing and choose to give them this anyway.*

*And the Regent — it calls itself the Hollow Regent, it told me so, whispering through my own mouth some nights, using my lips to say its name like a man trying on a coat — the Regent tasted that chorus and understood it had been eating crumbs its entire existence.*

*So it ate the wards instead.*

*Not broke them. Broke is what armies do, and armies are crude. It ate them. It went along the borders wearing my face and my seal, and it offered the dead silence — true rest, it called it, though rest is a lie; silence is being erased mid-word — and the dead who refused, it took anyway. The stars didn't go out because the sky failed, bone-carver. The stars went out because the wards held the sky's hem, and when the Regent swallowed the singers, the hem dropped.*

*The war you've been hearing about? The usurper's armies, the burning villages, the march on the free cities? That isn't war. War has two sides wanting different things. This is a feeding. The armies exist to keep everyone looking at the ground while the sky is eaten. Every battle is a distraction performed by the main course.*

*And me? Why am I alive, you're wondering. A sane question. Here is the insane answer: it keeps me because I am the last voice it hasn't finished swallowing. Nine kings in one crown and mine is the sweetest, because I gave it freely. It saves me for last the way a careful eater saves the best bite. And it wears me — wears my face out there in the capital, sits on my throne in my skin — and every day it wears me, I wear away, until there is nothing left of Aldric Thorne anywhere in the world except whatever part of me is still running through these mountains bleeding on your floor.*

*Which means, bone-carver, that the most dangerous thing in Thornfell right now is not the crown.*

*It is the proof that the crown can be removed.*

* * *

Cyrus did not sleep that night. He sat with his back against the cave wall and the salt block against his chest and listened.

He had been listening to Maren since the night the stars vanished — her voice rising out of the salt grain whenever he laid his palm flat against it, faint as breath on a winter window, fragmented, arriving in pieces the way drowned things wash ashore: an arm of a sentence here, a hand of a word there. She had warned him the wards hadn't fallen. She had said *eaten*, and he had carried that word down the mountain like a coal in his mouth.

Now he asked her the rest.

"Maren." His thumb moved over the salt's rough face. The fire was down to embers; the king slept, or pretended to, curled like a child around his ruined shoulder. "Tell me about the crown. Tell me what you were doing when—"

The salt answered.

*—cold, Cy, it was so cold on the water, why did I take the boat out, nobody takes a boat out in—*

"I know. I'm sorry. Skip past it. Please."

A long silence. Then, faintly, a fragment that had never come before:

*—the chapel records. I found the chapel records. Ottoline's chapel, the drowned one, the ledgers aren't hymnals, Cy, they never were, they're a count. Nine kings. It counts its kings. It's been counting since before the dynasty, it wore other crowns before ours, it wore—*

Her voice frayed into static-silence, the sound of surf heard from far inland. Cyrus waited, knuckles white against the oilcloth. When she came back she was quieter, as if each revelation cost her something physical.

*—the binding-sigils are on the inside. Nobody knows that. On the inner band, cut fine as hair, and they're carved, Cy, do you understand, they're CARVED, which means they can be UNCARVED, which means—*

She stopped. Not faded — stopped, the way a person stops when they realize they've said too much, or when they see something behind you that you haven't turned to see yet.

Cyrus looked up.

Aldric was awake, sitting up, staring at the salt block with an expression Cyrus had no name for. Not fear. Recognition — the terrible kind, the kind you feel when you pass a stranger in the road and realize halfway past that it was someone you buried.

"It's counting us," the king whispered. "Isn't it. Whatever's in the salt. It's part of the count."

"She's my sister," Cyrus said.

"I know." Aldric's voice was almost gentle, and underneath the gentleness ran something that might have been shame, if shame could rust. "I signed the order that sealed the coastal chapels. Fourteen years ago. It seemed administrative. Everything it did through me seemed administrative." He passed a hand over his face. "Your sister found the count because the chapels hadn't been sealed yet. And then the coast was closed, and the boats stopped going out, and—"

"Don't," Cyrus said.

"—and I never asked what happened to the woman who found the ledgers. Because asking would have required me to still have the voice that asks. And I'd already thanked it for taking that voice away." He laughed, softly, horribly. "You want to know what the crown wants, carver? I'll tell you the worst part. The crown doesn't want anything. Wanting is *our* shape. The crown wants nothing the way the sea wants nothing. It only rises."

The ember-light guttered. Somewhere above them, beyond the root curtain, the starless night pressed down on the mountain like a lid.

Cyrus looked at the king — at the pale ring of sigils ghosting outward from the wound, at the wax-colored skin, at the man who was being slowly worn through from the inside like a coin rubbed between thieves — and he understood, with a craftsman's cold clarity, what Maren had been trying to tell him from the moment her voice rose out of the salt.

Carved things can be uncarved.

Nine kings in one crown, and the ninth still bleeding somewhere in the mountains, unfinished. The Regent wasn't hunting Aldric to kill him. It was hunting him to *finish* him — to swallow the last voice, complete the set, make itself whole and eternal and unremovable.

Every hour they hid was an hour the carving got deeper.

"We move at first light," Cyrus said. "East. Through the high passes."

"East is the free cities." Aldric said it dully, the way a man names a place he's already imagined dying outside. "The free cities will sell me within a day. Their loyalty has a price tag, and I've seen what's in my purse."

"Then we won't ask for their loyalty," Cyrus said, and his thumb found the salt block again, and Maren's voice came back for one more fragment, faint as the last star anyone would remember:

*—find Vane. Find the knight. She still kneels to something real—*

Far below the cave, down in the valley, a dog began to bark, and then another, and then a horn sounded — once, low, wrong, a note no Thornfell huntsman used — and the king's eyes met Cyrus's across the dying fire.

"They've picked up the trail," Aldric said. It was not a question, and for the first time since Cyrus had dragged him out of the pass, there was no fever in it at all. Only a man doing arithmetic he already knew the sum of.

Cyrus kicked snow over the embers. "Then we go now, and in the dark, and by a road that isn't on their maps." He shouldered his pack, and the salt block rode against his heart, and Maren whispered one final word into the black—

*faster*

—and somewhere to the north, closing like a hand, came the sound of many feet that made no sound at all.
