## 6. Smuggler's Arithmetic

They came down out of the high country on the fourth morning, and Cyrus felt Vessamy before he saw it — a loosening in the air, the particular hush of a place where the dead had been negotiated with rather than feared. The road descended through terraces of pale grass into a bowl of hills, and there the city sat like a coin at the bottom of a purse: white walls, slate roofs, and above it all the great salt-crusted tower of the Ferryman's Guild, its spire hung with bells that never rang for the living.

"You'll want to know the rules," Cyrus said. He had been composing the speech for two days, in the gaps between Aldric's fevers and Ottoline's silence. "Vessamy doesn't kneel, doesn't swear, and doesn't do charity. Everything has a price. Everything is written down. If you can't pay, they'll still shelter you — but they'll own the debt, and the Guild collects debts the way rivers collect rain."

"I have a kingdom," Aldric said faintly, from Ottoline's shoulder.

"You have a face that's on a hundred wanted bills," Cyrus said, "and a crown that's currently wearing someone else. As far as the Guild is concerned, you're a liability with a pulse. I'm the one who'll be doing the talking."

Ottoline's hand rested on her sword the way other people rested a hand on a sleeping child — not to use it, but to know it was there. "And what will you be paying with, carver? You carry no coin I've seen."

Cyrus looked at the road ahead, at the white walls drinking the morning light, and did not answer. There was no honest answer yet. There was only the salt block in his pack, warm against his spine, and Maren's voice inside it, humming an old threshing song to keep herself company, the way she had hummed when they were children and the dark of the workshop pressed too close.

*You could ask me,* she said, when the walls of Vessamy rose close enough to count the watchmen on them. *Before you give anything away. You could ask me if I mind.*

*I mind,* Cyrus said, and did not say it aloud.

* *

The Ferryman's Guild kept its counting-house in the old drowned chapel district, where the streets ran with channels of brine and the gutters glittered like crushed jewelry. Everything in Vessamy was faintly salted — the bread, the doorframes, the air itself — because salt was the medium, salt was the ledger, salt was how the free cities had learned to hold what the kingdoms let slip away. The dead did not pass into silence here. They passed into contract.

The Guildmaster received them in a room with no chairs, which Cyrus recognized as the first term of the negotiation. She was a small woman, iron-haired, with the forearms of someone who had spent decades hauling on ropes, and her name — he learned it later, from a clerk who whispered it like a warning — was Vess Corvane, and she had been buying and selling passage for the dead since before Cyrus was born.

"Three travelers," she said. "One knight of a dead dynasty, one king of a dead kingdom, and one bone-carver of no consequence at all." Her eyes were the gray of old sea ice. "You want the coast road past the blockade, safe-conduct through the salt marshes, and a roof that isn't registered with the Regent's clerks. That's the request. Here is the price."

She named it in coin first, and the number was obscene, and they all three knew it was meant to be refused.

"I don't have it," Cyrus said. "And if I did, I wouldn't pay it, because coin leaves a trail. You taught the free cities that."

Something flickered at the corner of the Guildmaster's mouth — not quite approval, but the memory of it. "Then name what you do have."

"I have the roads. I have the passes the maps don't admit. I have—" and here his voice caught, and he hated it, hated the small tremor that gave the thing away before he had chosen to give it, "—I have a hearing."

The room went very quiet. Even the brine in the channels seemed to slow.

"Bone-carver," Corvane said, and her voice had changed, become the voice of a woman doing sums with souls. "The Guild pays well for a working ear. There are dead in this city who hold liens, who remember terms, who can settle disputes no living witness survived. A carver who can hear them is worth more than a ship." She folded her hands. "But you know that. You didn't come here to sell me a service. You came to sell me something I can't buy anywhere else."

Cyrus's mouth was dry. "A piece of it. Not the whole hearing. A piece — cut clean, the way I'd cut a joint of bone. Yours to keep. Mine to live without."

"And the piece would be—"

"The first voice I ever heard." He made himself say it steadily. "My sister's. She's in a salt block in my pack. If you take the hearing of her from me, you can put her voice in your walls, your ledgers, your bells. She'll settle your disputes. She'll testify in your contracts. And I'll walk out of here able to hear every other dead soul in the world except the one I crossed a mountain range to keep."

Aldric made a small sound — a king's protest, half-fevered — and Ottoline's hand closed on Cyrus's arm, hard enough to bruise. "Carver," she said, low, "no."

"It's arithmetic," Cyrus said, and his voice did not shake, and he was proud of that, and ashamed of how proud. "That's all it's ever been. You spend what you have to get where you're going. I've spent coin I didn't have and roads I can't walk twice and now—" he looked at the pack, at the salt block he could feel through the canvas like a heartbeat, "—now I spend this."

*Don't,* Maren said. Not *don't do it.* Just *don't* — the way she'd said it when he was nine and reaching for the carving knife, when she meant *I love you and I am afraid for you and I will not stop you.*

That was the worst of it. She would not stop him. The dead never stopped the living. That was the whole architecture of grief, the load-bearing wall of it: the dead could only watch.

Corvane studied him for a long moment. "You understand what you're selling. Not the voice — the voice is just wax in a seal. You're selling the *connection*. The thread. I'll have my cutters sever it at the root, and it will not grow back. You'll carry her salt block to your grave and it will weigh exactly what it weighs and nothing more."

"I understand."

"You understand that after, when you hold it, you'll hear nothing. That you'll spend the rest of your life holding a stone and wondering—"

"*I understand.*" The word came out of him like a splinter leaving flesh, and the channels of brine in the floor shivered, and somewhere above them a Guild bell rang once, unbidden, the dead leaning in to listen.

Corvane nodded slowly. "Then we have terms. But not today."

Cyrus blinked. "Not—"

"Not today." She unfolded her hands and, for the first time, looked at Ottoline and Aldric as though they were people rather than inventory. "A hearing like yours, cut by your own knife, in my counting-house — that's not a transaction, carver. That's an event. The dead will want witnesses. The Guild will want witnesses. And you—" her gray eyes settled on him with something that was almost, almost kindness, "—you will want a night to be certain. Sleep on my salt. Eat at my table. If in the morning you still want to pay, we cut. If not, you owe me the asking price, and you'll spend ten years hauling rope in my marshes to clear it, and I'll have your mark on that too."

She pushed a contract across the table — actual salt-pressed paper, the terms already written in a hand so fine it might have been etched by frost. Both roads. Both outcomes. The Guild left nothing to mercy, because mercy was the one currency it had never learned to mint.

Cyrus read every line. It was the first contract he had ever read that offered him a way to keep what he loved, and it was the cruelest document he had ever held, and his hand did not shake as he signed it.

* *

They gave him a room in the Guild tower, high up, where the bells hung silent and the salt crusted the window ledges in thick white fur like the city was growing fur against a winter that never came. Ottoline took the door — she would take the door every night, he was beginning to understand, the way a wall takes the weather — and Aldric slept at last without fever, his breathing slow and even, a man practicing at being a body again.

Cyrus sat on the floor with his back to the wall and the salt block in his lap.

It was the size of a loaf of bread, honey-colored, shot through with veins of darker mineral where the brine had pooled as it set. Their mother had made it the winter Maren died — had packed her daughter's voice into salt the way other families packed meat, because in Thornfell you did not let your dead go silent if you could help it. Cyrus had carried it for eleven years. He had slept beside it, argued with it, carved memorial bones to its murmuring, built his whole craft on the foundation of one dead girl's voice telling him he was holding the chisel wrong.

*You're holding the chisel wrong,* Maren said now, from inside the block, gentle as ever. *You're gripping it like it's a weapon. It's a tool, Cy. It's only ever been a tool.*

"I'm about to use it to cut you out of my head," Cyrus said. "That's a weapon."

*It's a toll,* she said. *There's a difference. A weapon is for making someone else lose. A toll is for paying your own way through.* A pause, the sound of her settling, if salt could settle. *Ask me the real question.*

He knew the real question. He had known it since the counting-house, since Corvane had said *you'll spend the rest of your life holding a stone.* The real question was not whether he could pay. The real question was what he was buying with the payment — and whether three travelers and a chance at a broken kingdom were worth the only voice that had ever made the world feel inhabited.

"If I do this," Cyrus said, "I do it for him. For the king. And I've known him nine days, and half of those he's been delirious."

*You're doing it for the roads,* Maren said. *You've always been doing it for the roads. The roads are the only thing you ever loved as much as me, and you never had to choose before.*

"That's not true."

*It's completely true. You drew maps in the dust when you were six. You memorized the passes the way other boys memorized ballads. I used to watch you from the door of the workshop and think, there goes my brother, in love with the space between places.* Her voice was warm and teasing and unbearably fond. *And now the space between places needs a king delivered, and you're pretending it's a sacrifice instead of a calling. Cy. Brother. You were always going to spend yourself on the roads. I'm just the coin you happened to be carrying.*

He pressed his forehead against the salt block. It was cool, and faintly rough, and it smelled of the workshop, of their mother's hands, of a winter eleven years dead.

"If I cut the thread," he said, "I won't hear you at the end. When I get to the Salt Throne. When it's finished, whatever finished means. I won't hear you say—"

*Say what? That I'm proud of you?* A silence, and then, softer: *You'll know. That's the secret nobody tells you about the dead, Cy. The hearing isn't how we talk to you. It's how you tolerate the quiet. We were always talking. You just won't be able to stop hearing the quiet after.*

Outside the window, Vessamy glittered under a starless sky. He had almost stopped flinching at it — that was the most frightening thing of all, how quickly a person could grow used to the absence of the oldest lights.

*One more thing,* Maren said, and her voice changed, and he heard — for the first time in eleven years — something in it he had never heard when she was alive. Fear. *The Guild's cutters. Ask to see their knives before they touch you. If the blades are bone, refuse the cut and run, because bone-cutters don't sever threads, they splice them, and you don't want to know what the Guild splices into the gap.*

Cyrus lifted his head. "Maren. How do you know that?"

But the salt block had gone quiet, the way it sometimes did, the way the sea goes quiet not because it has stopped but because it has turned its attention elsewhere, to some deep current a swimmer cannot see.

* *

In the morning, the counting-house was full.

Not with clerks — with the dead. They stood along the walls in the gray-gold light, dozens of them, salt-preserved presences that only Cyrus could see, and they had come to witness, because in Vessamy even a severing was a contract, and contracts required witnesses on both sides of the veil. Corvane stood at her table with the contract unrolled. Ottoline stood at Cyrus's shoulder like a drawn blade. Aldric sat — they had finally given him a chair — with his hands folded, watching Cyrus with an expression that was not pity and not gratitude but something older than either: recognition. One man who had lost a kingdom, watching another man price what he could not bear to lose.

The cutter was a woman with a leather roll of tools, and Cyrus, remembering, asked to see her knives.

Steel. Every one. He let out a breath he had been holding since midnight.

"Last chance, carver," Corvane said. "The terms stand as written. The piece severed is the first voice, the sister's voice, the thread to the salt block in your pack. In exchange: safe-conduct for three, shelter unregistered, and passage past the blockade when the coast opens. The Guild keeps its word or pays triple. That's the oldest law in Vessamy."

"I know," Cyrus said. "I grew up on stories of the Guild keeping its word. It's the only reason I came."

He took out his carving knife — his own knife, as the terms required, the one his mother had put in his hand at twelve, the one Maren had teased him about, the one he had used to carve a hundred memorial bones and would use tonight, or some night soon, to carve another. He set it on the table beside the salt block, and the dead along the walls leaned closer, and one of them — an old man in the rotted livery of a Thornfell border-guard — whispered something that might have been a prayer or an apology.

"Begin," Cyrus said.

The cutter's hands were gentle. That was the horror of it — the gentleness. She found the thread the way a midwife finds a cord, with two fingers and a patience that had outlived cruelty, and Cyrus felt her touch it, and the whole world went silent for one white-hot instant, every dead voice in Vessamy, in the world, holding its breath.

*Cy,* Maren said. *Cy, look at me. Look—*

He looked. There was nothing to look at. There was only a block of salt on a table, and a woman with a knife, and his own hand steady on the wood because a craftsman's hands did not shake, not in front of witnesses, not ever, that was the first law of the workshop and the last law he had left.

The knife came down.

It did not hurt the way a wound hurts. It hurt the way a room hurts when a door closes — the sudden draft, the sudden absence, the shape of something that had always been there now outlined by its vanishing. The thread parted. He felt it go, felt the last of Maren's voice pass out of him like a bird leaving an open window, and the salt block on the table did not change at all, did not dim or crack or weep, and that was the cruelest thing: it looked exactly the same. Eleven years of voice, and it looked like a loaf of bread.

"It is done," the cutter said quietly, and rolled up her tools.

Corvane stamped the contract with the Guild's seal — a ferryman poling a boatload of salt across a black river — and pushed it across the table. "Terms hold. Shelter, safe-conduct, and passage when the coast opens." Her gray eyes flicked to the salt block, and something moved in them — greed, or grief, or the place where the two had long since grown indistinguishable. "Your sister testifies well, carver. She settled a lien dispute in the marsh district this morning before you'd finished your breakfast. The Guild will treat her with honor."

Cyrus nodded. He could not speak. His throat was a closed door, and behind it a voice was knocking, and there was nothing on the other side of the door anymore, nothing at all, just the quiet, the enormous quiet, the quiet he had been running from since he was a boy in a workshop above a smuggler's inn.

Ottoline's hand came down on his shoulder. She did not say anything. Knights of the old dynasty, he was learning, knew when words were just noise.

* *

They left Vessamy's counting-house as the bells rang noon — rang, for the first time in Cyrus's memory, for the living, a bright bronze clamor that made Aldric flinch and Ottoline's hand go to her sword and Cyrus stand in the street with salt dust on his sleeves, listening to a city make noise, hearing every dead thing in it *except one.*

It was a strange arithmetic, he thought, as they turned toward the coast road and the blockade and the war. He had paid away his sister's voice and bought a king's survival. He had spent the one thing he could never earn back and received, in return, a contract, a road, and the company of a knight who watched doors and a king who was learning to be alive.

It was not a fair trade. No trade that mattered ever was.

They were a mile past the walls when the Guild's fastest rider caught them — a lean woman on a leaner horse, salt spray flying from the hooves, a sealed letter in her fist. She reined in hard, looked at the three of them with the flat appraisal of someone delivering news rather than judgment, and held out the letter.

"From the Guildmaster," she said. "For the bone-carver. She said you'd want it before the coast."

Cyrus broke the seal. The writing inside was Corvane's fine frost-etched hand, and it was short, and it was not about passage, or the blockade, or the terms of any contract the Guild had ever stamped.

*Carver,* it read. *The dead in my walls do not sleep, and last night they were restless. Your sister's voice is safe and honored — but she was not the only new voice to arrive. Sometime in the night, another thread came down the mountain roads and anchored itself in my tower, and it is asking for you by name. It says it carries a warning from Thornfell. It says the heralds of the Hollow Regent have found the road you took, and they are not following it.*

*They are waiting at the end of it.*

Cyrus read it twice. Then he folded the letter, and put it away, and looked east, where the coast glittered beyond the marshes like a lie told beautifully.

"Ride ahead," he told the Guild's rider, and his voice was steady, and he noticed, distantly, that he no longer had Maren's voice to tell him whether he was lying to himself. "Tell the Guildmaster we're coming. And tell her —" he hesitated, hearing in his memory a dead girl's last lesson — *a toll is for paying your own way through* — "tell her the bone-carver says the arithmetic isn't finished."
