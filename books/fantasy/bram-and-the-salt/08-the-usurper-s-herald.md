## 8. The Usurper's Herald

The bells woke them on the third morning.

Not the harbor bells, which Cyrus had learned in two days to sleep through — the low bronze tolling of the tide-bells that told the fisherfolk when the shallows shifted — but the great bell above the Council Spire, the one the Vessamyans rang only for war or for weddings. It had a cracked note at the top of its voice, and it fell through the fog like a stone through still water, and Cyrus came upright in his cot with Maren's salt block already clutched against his chest before he understood he'd moved.

*They're here,* she said from inside the salt, her voice thin as thread drawn through wax. *I can taste iron on the wind. They're here.*

"You said you couldn't feel anything past the harbor wards," he whispered. The room was gray; gulls argued somewhere over the fish-market; Ottoline's boots were already hammering up the stairs from the lower floor.

*I lied. Or I was wrong. I don't know which anymore.*

He dressed in the dark, hands steady because he made them be steady, the way he did when a piece of bone fought him. Downstairs, Ser Ottoline Vane stood in the doorway of the inn's common room with her sword already belted on, watching the street through the gap where the door didn't quite meet its frame. She had slept, if she'd slept at all, in her mail. She looked like something carved by a sculptor who had run out of patience halfway through.

"The Regent's ship anchored off the Teeth an hour ago," she said without turning. "A single galley. White sails. No arms visible." Her mouth did something bitter. "No arms needed."

"Amnesty," Cyrus said. "The Guildmaster sent word before dawn. The herald comes under parley-colors. He'll stand in the Meridian Square and make his offer to the whole city at once."

"And your council will listen." She turned then, and her eyes were the color of old ice, and they were afraid, and she was letting him see it, which from Ottoline was worth more than any oath. "Cyrus Thackeray, they will listen. Free cities do not kneel, but free cities also do not die for other men's crowns. If the price is right—"

"They've already taken my price twice," he said. "There isn't much left of me to sell."

She flinched. He hadn't meant it as cruelty. Lately he hadn't been able to tell the difference between honesty and cruelty, and he suspected that was what grief did when you fed it too long: it wore the grooves so deep that everything rolled into them, every word, every silence, and came out shaped the same.

*Come to the window,* Maren said. *Quickly. Before the square fills.*

He went to the shutter and put his eye to the slats, and below, in the pearl-gray light, the city was pouring itself toward the Meridian Square the way water pours toward a crack in a floor. Fishwives with their baskets still on their hips. Guildsmen in their chain-of-office colors, the Ferryman's silver oar blazing on half the coats he could see. Children hoisted onto shoulders. And everywhere the same hush, the held-breath quiet of a crowd that has come to look at something it does not want to see but cannot bear not to.

At the center of the square, on the steps of the weighing-hall where the Ferryman's Guild conducted its contracts, stood a figure in white.

* * *

The herald was alone. That was the first wrong thing. No escort, no honor guard, no soldiers lurking at the street-ends — just one slender figure on the top step, robed in undyed wool so pale it seemed to drink the fog and give nothing back, hands folded, head bowed, waiting with the patience of furniture. The crowd gathered around the square's edge like a tide that had decided not to come in.

Then the herald lifted its head, and Cyrus's stomach turned over.

It had no face of its own. Where a face should have been there was a smooth expanse of skin, unlined, unfeatured, like the surface of a bone before the first cut — and into that blankness, as the herald began to speak, faces surfaced. Rose up beneath the skin the way drowned things rise in still water. Settled. Became.

The first face was an old woman's, seamed and kind, and a man near the front of the crowd made a sound like a dog that has been struck, and someone caught his arm. His mother. His grandmother. Someone he had buried.

"Honored citizens of Vessamy," the herald said, and its voice was all of those voices at once and none of them, layered, harmonized, wrong — a choir singing through one throat. "The Regent Who Sits sends greetings, and grief, and terms."

Beside him, Ottoline had gone rigid. "Faces of the honored dead," she breathed. "That is — that is forbidden even among necromancers. That is the oldest law. You do not wear the dead. You *listen* to them. You—" Her hand found her sword-hilt and stopped there, knuckles white, because drawing steel on a parley-herald would doom them all before the man finished speaking.

"It's not them," Cyrus said quietly. "Look at the eyes. Dead eyes don't blink sideways."

But the herald was speaking, and the whole square leaned toward it the way wheat leans toward wind.

"The realm endures. This the Regent bids me swear, and I swear it wearing the faces of those who built the realm, whose bones are in its walls and whose love is in its law. A madness has gripped the throne. A king has fled his crown, has fled his people, has fled his own name. The Regent does not seek vengeance. The Regent seeks only *healing*."

The face changed as it spoke — an old soldier now, then a young priest, then a woman with a scholar's soft chin — each face surfacing, holding for a phrase or two, sinking again. Each one honored. Each one beloved. Cyrus watched the crowd and saw the poison working exactly as it was meant to: not fear, but *recognition*. How could a thing wearing your father's face be wholly a lie? How could you raise a blade against that?

"Terms," the herald said. "First: amnesty, total and sworn upon the salt of the deep roads, for every soul in this city who has sheltered, fed, guided, or merely failed to report the fugitive Aldric Thorne. What is done is done, and the Regent holds no ledger against the merciful." A ripple went through the crowd — relief, naked and shameful, and Cyrus hated how well he understood it. "Second: a bounty, weighed and paid in gold before sunset, for the fugitive himself. Ten thousand crowns. Enough to buy this harbor three times over. Enough that no family in Vessamy need ever bargain passage again."

And there it was. Not the threat. The *offer*. Cyrus felt the shape of it settle over the square like a net over a fish-pen. The free cities did not kneel — but ten thousand crowns did not ask anyone to kneel. It asked them only to count.

"Third," the herald said, and its voice dropped, and every face it wore softened into gentleness, and Cyrus's blood went cold a full breath before he understood why. "Third: the Regent extends mercy to the *bone-carver*. The man of Grayhollow who took the road with the broken king. The Regent knows his name. The Regent knows his trade. And the Regent knows—"

The faces sank. All of them. Every borrowed face slid beneath the smooth skin at once, and for a moment the herald was featureless again, a blank egg of flesh, and then a single face rose.

Maren's.

* * *

The world went narrow. Sound pulled away like a tide going out. There was the square, and there was the face, and there was nothing else.

Her face. Not approximately her face. Not a likeness carved by someone who had known her. *Her* — the small asymmetry where her left eyebrow never fully grew back after the ice, the chipped tooth she'd shown in every grin since she was nine, the way her mouth tucked at one corner when she was trying not to laugh at him. Twenty years dead. Salted away in a block on his shelf. And now standing on the steps of a weighing-hall in Vessamy, wearing white wool, looking up directly — *directly* — at the shuttered window where he stood.

*Cyrus.* Maren's voice, inside the salt against his chest, came out strangled. *Cyrus, that isn't me. Look at the eyes. Look at the—*

"I know," he said aloud. His lips had gone numb. "I know."

But the face smiled his sister's smile, and the herald spoke with his sister's mouth, and the voice that came out was not the choir anymore. It was one voice. Young. Warm. A little rough at the edges, the way hers had been rough from years of shouting across a boat-deck.

"Cyrus," the herald said, in Maren's voice, to the entire city, and every word carried as if the air itself had been paid to deliver it. "Little brother. Come down. Put down the knife. The Regent doesn't want your king. The Regent wants to stop the hurting. You've been carrying her so long, haven't you? Carrying the salt, carrying the voice, paying pieces of yourself at every toll. I know. I *know*, Cy. Give us the broken thing you're dragging east and rest. Rest, and hear me properly again — not scraps through a carving, not whispers in grain. All of me. Forever. Isn't that what you wanted? Wasn't it always?"

Below, the crowd murmured. Some wept openly. A Guild factor near the steps crossed himself with the wrong hand, dazed. And Cyrus stood at the window with his sister's murderer's puppet smiling up at him and understood, with a clarity that felt like a blade laid flat against the skin, that this was not an offer at all.

It was a demonstration. The Regent was showing Vessamy — showing *him* — what it could do. That it knew his name. That it knew his grief down to the grain of it. That it could reach into the one place he kept locked and turn the key from outside. That nowhere he ran would ever again be anywhere the dead couldn't be made to speak against him.

*It's lying,* Maren said, and her real voice was shaking. *Cy, look at me — look at the salt-me, not that thing. My brother. Listen to my actual voice.*

He tore his eye from the shutter and pressed his forehead against the salt block, cold against his skin, and listened, and there she was: thinner than she'd been a week ago, worn down by every toll he'd paid, but *hers*. Off-key with exhaustion. Real.

"Say something only you'd know," he whispered.

*You cried at Father's funeral and blamed the sea spray. You were twelve and furious about it for a year.* A wet, ragged almost-laugh. *The thing down there doesn't know why you were furious. But I do. Because you weren't crying for him. You were crying because you'd promised me you'd teach you to carve, and you thought dying was his way of getting out of holding you to it.*

Cyrus laughed. It came out broken in the middle, half sob, and Ottoline looked at him sharply, and he waved her off, and pressed his forehead harder against the cold salt.

"That's her," he said. "That's my sister."

*Then listen to me now, and don't you dare go down those stairs.* The warmth in her voice hardened into something older, something that had steered a boat through weather that killed grown men. *It wants you in the square. It can't touch you behind the harbor wards — that's why it's *talking*. Everything that thing says is a hook, Cy. Even the true parts. Especially the true parts.*

Down in the square, the herald — wearing Maren's face, wearing his sister like a stolen coat — spread its empty hands.

"The Regent waits until moonset tomorrow," it said, and the choir-voice returned, layered and vast, though the face stayed hers, cruelly, deliberately hers. "One night. Then the amnesty narrows, and the bounty stands, and the Regent's patience — the Regent's patience is not endless. No kingdom survives forever on the kindness of smugglers." The face tilted, and Maren's smile became something no living person had ever seen on it, and Cyrus felt it like a knife going in under the ribs. "Choose, Vessamy. Choose, bone-carver. The dead are patient. The Regent is less so."

The faces sank. The blankness closed over them like water. And the herald simply walked — unhurried, unarmed, untouchable — down the steps of the weighing-hall, through the parted crowd, out of the square, and was gone into the fog toward the white-sailed ship, leaving ten thousand crowns hanging in the air over the city like a storm about to break.

* * *

For a long time afterward, no one in the inn spoke.

Ottoline bolted the door, barred it, dragged the table against it, and then stood with her back to the wood as if expecting the hinges to be tested. The innkeeper had gone to the square and come back gray-faced, and now sat in the corner nursing a cup of something stronger than tea, muttering numbers — Cyrus caught fragments, *ten thousand, gods, ten thousand* — and did not look at either of them.

Cyrus sat at the workbench with Maren's salt block before him and his carving knives laid out beside it, and did not touch either.

"He used her face," he said finally. His voice sounded like someone else's. Flat. Planed smooth. The craftsman's calm coming down over the catastrophe, because it was the only tool he had left that still worked. "Out of everyone in that square, everyone in this city, it stood under my window and wore *her*."

"That was aimed at you," Ottoline said. "All of it. The amnesty was theater for the council. The bounty was bait for the mob. But the face—" She came closer, and her voice lost its parade-ground edge. "The face was a message with one reader."

"It knows about the salt." He turned the block slowly under the lamp, watching the light move through it. "It knows what she is to me. It knows what I've been paying at the tolls. Which means the Guild's ledgers aren't as sealed as they claim, or the guide they assigned us talked, or—" He stopped. Made himself say it. "Or the Regent hears through the silenced dead. Through whatever it uses to eat voices. And it heard *her*."

*Cy.* Maren's voice was very small. *What if it takes me? What if it learns enough of me, from you carrying me, that it can—*

"It won't." He put both palms flat on the block, careful, the way he handled green bone. "I won't let it."

*You can't promise that. You're spending me a coin at a time, and every coin tells it more of what I sound like—*

"Maren."

*—and someday you'll pay a toll and it'll keep the change—*

"*Maren.*"

Silence in the salt. Ottoline watched him with an expression he couldn't read — pity, maybe, or recognition, or the particular horror of a woman who had spent six years listening to nothing and was now listening to a dead girl argue with her brother through a block of salt.

"We leave tonight," Cyrus said. He heard his own voice go quiet and level, the voice he used over a difficult cut. "Before moonset. Before the council convenes, because half of them spent last night doing arithmetic with ten thousand crowns and I watched their faces in that square. Before the herald's patience runs out. We take the eastern roads."

"The eastern roads are closed," Ottoline said. "The pass at Vell's Teeth is watched since the blockade. The coastal route is Guild-water and Guild-law, and the Guild—" She hesitated, and Cyrus saw her choose honesty over comfort. "The Guild sold the Grayhollow ferry schedule to the Regent's agents two winters ago. Everyone knows. Nobody says. That is how the free cities survive, Cyrus. They are neutral the way a knife is neutral."

"Then we don't take the Guild roads." He was already pulling his kit together, hands moving, mind moving faster, falling back into the oldest rhythm he owned: read the material, find the grain, follow it. "There's a pass east of the Teeth that isn't on any Guild chart. My father ran salt through it during the last border war. The old families call it the Forgiving Road." He glanced up at Ottoline, and something in his chest tightened, because he was about to say the strange part, the part that would sound like madness in any city but this one. "It only opens for travelers the dead hold no grudge against. That's not smuggling folklore. That's ward-craft. Old ward-craft, older than Thornfell. And I'm beginning to understand," he touched the salt block, gently, "that I may know the only dead woman alive — dead woman *dead* — whose forgiveness actually counts on that mountain."

Ottoline stared at him. Outside, the fog thickened, and somewhere across the harbor the white-sailed galley rode at anchor like a held breath.

"And if the dead *don't* forgive us?" she asked.

Cyrus looked down at his sister's face ghosting faintly in the salt — tired, faded, spending herself whisper by whisper to keep him sane — and thought of a valley east of here where he'd heard, through three feet of granite, the sound the Regent's prisoners made when it finished with them. Which was no sound at all.

"Then we spend what's left of me convincing them," he said, and began to pack the knives.
