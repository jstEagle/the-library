## 9. Roads That Remember

The pass did not appear on any map Aldric Thorne had ever signed, and he had signed most of them. In his years upon the Salt Throne he had pored over the cartographers' great vellum rolls, the ones inked in gold leaf for the borders and silver for the rivers, and nowhere in all that gilded geography was there a gap in the mountains called the Forgiveness. Yet here it was, a seam of shadow between two shoulders of grey stone, and Cyrus Thackeray stood before it the way a man stands before a door he has knocked on all his life.

"It opens," the bone-carver said, "for those the dead forgive. That's what my grandmother called it. She never said what happens to those they don't."

Aldric laughed. It came out of him wrong — a dry, cracked sound, like wind through a burnt house — but it was laughter, and it startled all three of them. He could not remember the last time he had laughed. Perhaps before the crown. Perhaps before the drowning of his father, though the memories from before the crown had begun to return in fragments, arriving like letters forwarded through many hands, smudged and late.

"Then we are fortunate," he said, "that the dead have short memories."

"No," said Ottoline Vane, from the rear, where she had walked all day with her sword drawn and her eyes on the backtrail. "The dead have the longest memories of anyone. That is rather the whole of their condition."

* * *

They had been fleeing east for six days. Aldric kept count the way he had once kept count of petitions, of executions, of the small betrayals that accumulate in a reign like silt in a harbor — not because he wished to remember them, but because forgetting had been the crown's first gift to him, and he had sworn, in the lucid hours that came now more often, never to accept that gift again.

Six days since Vessamy. Six days since the herald had stood in the Guildhall square wearing the faces of the honored dead — and among them, cruelly, a woman's face that had made Cyrus Thackeray go grey as his own carved bone. They had fled that night, east into the mountains, away from the coast and the Guild's blockaded harbors, into country where the Regent's armies moved slow and the roads moved slower, except for the roads that did not exist.

Aldric had begun, in those six days, to understand what he was.

He had spent two years believing he was dead. That had been the crown's second gift: the sweet anesthesia of it. *You are already gone*, the thing on his brow had whispered in the voice of his drowned father. *Nothing you do matters. Nothing you suffer is real. Grieve yourself and be free.* And he had grieved himself, oh, thoroughly — had lain in the dark of his own skull while his hands signed the ward-decrees and his voice read the dissolution of the border orders, and had told himself it did not matter, because a dead king cannot be a murderer. A dead king is only a haunting.

But in the high cold air of the mountains, with the crown's whisper grown faint — it hated altitude, Cyrus had noticed; it hated the old wards that still clung in the stones like lichen — Aldric had begun to feel the terrible weight of being alive. His hands ached where the cold got into them. His stomach cramped with hunger. His feet bled inside boots he had not laced himself in two years, because a king's body was tended by others, and then, when the crown had eaten enough of him, not tended at all.

Alive. He was alive, and everything he had done while he pretended otherwise had been done by a living man.

* * *

The Forgiveness opened for them at dusk.

There was no gate. Aldric had expected a gate — he was a king, and kings thought in gates and walls and keys — but there was only a narrowness in the rock that had not been a narrowness a moment before, a passage perhaps eight feet wide between walls of black stone veined with white, and the white was not quartz. He knew that white. He had seen it in the crypts beneath the Salt Throne, in the old bones of the dynasty he had been the last of. Salt. The mountain was veined with salt the way a body is veined with the thing it cannot live without.

Cyrus went first, and the passage let him. Ottoline went second, sword still drawn, and the passage let her, though Aldric saw her flinch as she crossed the threshold, as if a hand had brushed her shoulder — and he saw her bow her head, briefly, to the empty air on her left, the way a knight bows to a superior officer. When Aldric's own turn came he stopped at the seam and stood there, and the cold air of the pass breathed out at him, and it smelled of brine and old stone and, faintly, unbearably, of his father's cloak, which had smelled of the same when they pulled it from the harbor.

"You don't have to ask their permission," Cyrus said quietly. "That's not how it works. It's not a door with a guard. It's more like—" the bone-carver searched for the word, turning his hat in his hands. "Like a wound. It closes around what's clean and won't close around what isn't."

"Then it will not open for me."

"It opened for the idea of you," Cyrus said. "We'll see about the rest."

Aldric walked through, and the mountain did not close, and he did not know whether to be grateful or grieved.

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow the carver seemed to know without knowing how he knew it — a shelf of stone above a frozen waterfall, sheltered from the wind that came down the pass in long white ropes. Cyrus built a fire small enough to hide and large enough to live by, and Ottoline took the first watch with her back to the rock and her face to the dark, and Aldric sat with his hands open on his knees and looked at them.

They were a king's hands. They had been soft once. Two years of flight had hardened them, and two years before that of signing had softened them again in a different way — a way he could feel in the tendons, a looseness, as though the hands had learned that what they touched they did not truly hold.

"You're going to ask me," he said.

Cyrus looked up from the fire. The bone-carver's face was all planes and patience, a face like his own work, and Aldric had learned in six days that the patience was not kindness. It was craft. A man who carved memorials for the dead learned to wait for what the living needed to say.

"Am I?" Cyrus said.

"You've wanted to since the chapel. Since Ottoline pulled her sword on me and you talked her down. You've wanted to know what a king was doing alone on the Thornfell road with no guard and no crown-guard and no—" he gestured at himself, at the rags, at the ruin, "—no anything. You've wanted to know if I'm worth the price you're paying." He touched, without quite touching, the place behind his own ear where Cyrus's hearing of the dead had been cut away in Vessamy, a payment made with a carving knife to a Guild that kept ledgers of it. "You've paid twice now. You'll pay again. A man wants to know what he's buying."

"I know what I'm buying," Cyrus said. "I'm buying the road east."

"You're buying a story," Aldric said. "And you've been too decent to demand it. So I'll give it. Not because you're owed—" his voice caught, and he was surprised by how much it hurt, how *alive* the hurt was, "—but because I'm owed to it. There's a difference. I think it's the only difference left that matters."

The fire ticked. Somewhere below, the frozen waterfall groaned as the temperature fell, the sound of a mountain clearing its throat.

"The crown was old when I was crowned," Aldric said. "Older than the dynasty. That's what no one says aloud. The Salt Throne is four hundred years, but the crown — the crown was found. Dredged from the harbor mud when the first Thorne raised the city, and every account agrees it was *already there*, already shaped, already waiting. We wore it because it was the crown. That was the whole of the reasoning. Four hundred years of kings, and the whole of the reasoning was *because it is the crown*."

He turned his hands over. The palms were cracked. He looked at them as if reading them.

"My father wore it for thirty years and died sane. His father before him, twenty years, died sane. There are stories of kings who went strange — my grandfather's brother, the one they don't put in the rolls — but we told ourselves the stories were politics. A king goes strange, the crown is blamed, a faction rises. It was easier to believe in factions than in the crown. It was easier to believe in anything than in the thing on your own head.

"And then my father drowned. And I was crowned. And the first night — the first *night*, Cyrus, before the feast was even cold — I heard it."

"Her voice?" Cyrus asked softly. "The Regent?"

"No." Aldric laughed again, that burnt-house sound. "That's the horror of it. It didn't speak at all. It *listened*. It listened the way a leech listens to a vein. I sat in the coronation bed with a thing on my brow and I could feel it — tasting. Not my thoughts. I want to be clear about that, because it matters for what I did after. It never took my thoughts. It took my *griefs*. My father's body in the water. My mother's empty chair. The son I lost the winter before, the little one, the fever — I had never said his name aloud since. And the crown knew it. The crown held it up to me like a lamp, and it said — not in words, in *weight* — it said: *I can carry this for you. Set it down. Set it all down. You were never meant to hold so much. No one is. That is what I am for.*"

Cyrus had gone very still. Ottoline, on watch, had not turned, but Aldric saw her shoulders had stopped moving, and he knew she was listening with the whole of her body, the way soldiers listen.

"And I set it down," he said. "That's the confession. That's all of it, and it's small, and it's everything. I was twenty-six years old and I had buried a father and a son in eighteen months, and something offered to hold it, and I *let it*. I told myself it was only grief. I told myself every king is haunted, that's what a crown is *for*, to be haunted in the king's place — the old formula, the crown grieves so the king can govern. I recited it like a prayer. And every grief I set down made room, and every room it filled it filled with itself, and within a season I could not have told you which sadnesses were mine. Within a year I could not have told you which *memories* were mine. It wore my griefs like clothes, and I walked around underneath, naked and light and so *relieved*, gods forgive me, so relieved, like a man set down from carrying a corpse — except the corpse was my life, and I had handed it over gladly.

"And then it began to govern."

* * *

He told it all, because he had decided in the pass, walking through the seam in the mountain with his father's cloak-smell in his throat, that he would tell all of it or none, and none was the crown's way, and he was done with the crown's ways.

He told how the Regent — the thing the crown wore when it wore him — had begun with the wards. Not with armies. The border wards of Thornfell had stood for four hundred years, and they were not walls; they were *agreements*, old bargains struck between the living and the dead of the mountains, renewed each generation by a king's oath spoken at the border stones. And the crown had said: *why renew? The dead are troublesome. The dead remember things about us. Let the agreements lapse, quietly, one clause at a time, and no one will notice, and you will not have to go to the border stones and stand in the cold and hear what the dead say about your reign.*

And he had let them lapse. Clause by clause. Year by year. He had signed the dissolution orders with a light heart, a *relieved* heart, because each signature was another grief set down, another duty the crown carried for him, another weight gone — and the whole time the thing was not carrying the weight. It was *eating* it. Eating the wards. Eating the oaths. Eating the dead's right to speak, because the dead had four hundred years of things to say about the crown, and the crown could not abide witnesses.

"The stars," Cyrus said. His voice had a strange flatness, the voice of a man fitting a last piece into a carving. "The wards were anchored to the stars. My grandmother said so. When the wards went, the stars—"

"Went with them," Aldric said. "Or were taken with them. I don't know which. I stood on the terrace the night they went out and I felt it *feed*, Cyrus. I felt the crown swell with it, like a tick, like a — and I remember thinking, with the last part of me that was still me, I remember thinking: *no one will ever know how much of this I agreed to.* Because that was the crown's masterpiece. It never forced anything. It only ever offered. And I — " his hands closed, cracked palms against cracked palms, "— I *said yes*. Not once. A thousand times. In signatures and silences and the things I chose not to look at. Maren Thackeray died carrying a warning about the crown, and I have read your face every time her name comes near us, carver, and I will tell you what I have never told anyone: when her warning reached the court — and it reached the court, I know it reached the court because I felt the crown flinch, the only time I ever felt it flinch — I was the one who ordered it filed and forgotten. Not the Regent. *Me.* A living man, with a living hand, put her warning in a drawer, because the drawer was easier than the truth, and the crown had spent two years teaching me that easier was the same as better."

The fire had burned low. Cyrus was looking at him with an expression Aldric could not read, and did not ask to.

"I am not telling you this so you will forgive me," Aldric said. "I am telling you because Ottoline is right that there must be an accounting, and I would rather the accounting begin here, on a mountain, with a bone-carver and a knight, than in a throne room with a crown on my head deciding which parts get told. The crown cannot cut itself out with steel. She told me that — Vane, at the chapel. It needs a confession, a true one, as the cutting word. So." He spread his hands, empty, the way a man shows he holds no weapon. "Consider this the first cut."

* * *

Ottoline came and sat when her watch ended, and Cyrus fed the fire, and the mountain held its breath around them, and Aldric learned something in the small hours that he would carry the rest of his life, however long the crown allowed it to be.

Shame, he had believed, was a kind of death. That had been the crown's deepest teaching, deeper than all the others: that to name what you had done was to be annihilated by it, and so the only mercy was silence, and the only silence was surrender. He had believed it the way he had believed the stars were permanent.

But shame spoken aloud, he found, sitting in the firelight with two strangers who owed him nothing — shame spoken aloud was not annihilation. It was *weight*, real weight, and weight could be carried. It was heavy the way a pack is heavy, and a man with a pack can still walk. He had spent two years weightless, and weightless, it turned out, had only ever meant carried — borne along in the grip of a thing that was eating him.

He was heavy now. He was heavy and alive and the pass had opened for him, and in the morning, when Cyrus rose and looked at him across the dead fire and said, "The road forks ahead. One way goes to the coast and the Guild's second price. The other goes where I said we'd never go — down the smugglers' road, into the capital itself, to the Salt Throne," — Aldric did not flinch.

"You're proposing we walk into its mouth," he said.

"I'm proposing we walk in through the door no one watches," Cyrus said. "There's a difference. I'm told you'd know about doors."

Aldric looked east, where the pass ran on toward the free cities and safety and a long life of running. Then he looked west, down the black seam of the mountain, toward the throne he had fled and the thing that wore it.

"The crown is listening even now," he said. "It's faint this high, but it's listening. So let it hear this." And he stood, this ragged, bleeding, heavy man, and said aloud to the cold air of the Forgiveness, to the salt in the stone and the dead in it: "I am coming back. And this time I am bringing everything I set down."

Far below, at the mouth of the pass, something in the dark turned toward them — and began, without hurry, to climb.
