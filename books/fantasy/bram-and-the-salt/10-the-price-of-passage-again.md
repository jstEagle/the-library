## 10. The Price of Passage, Again

The Guildhall of Vessamy smelled of brine and old money — a smell Cyrus had known all his life without ever being permitted to breathe it indoors. Salt-crusted pillars held up a ceiling painted with the nine tides of the Ferryman's calendar, and beneath them, behind a desk of black driftwood wide as a fishing smack, sat the Guildmaster of the Eastern Passage: a woman named Corvane Hale with rings on every finger and none of them gold.

Gold was for people who could be bought cheaply.

"You again," she said, when Cyrus was shown up to her gallery on the third morning after the council's ruling. Below them, through a window of green glass, the harbor lay under its blockade like a throat under a hand. The Regent's ships rode at anchor in a crescent, black-hulled, silent, their sails furled in a way that made them look less like ships than like folded wings. "I told you before, bone-carver. The first price bought you shelter. Shelter is not passage."

"The coast is closed," Cyrus said.

"The coast is *closed*," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "Every harbor from here to the Salt Steps has a Regent's man standing on it with a list in his hand. Your king's face is on that list. So is the knight's. Yours too, I'd imagine, though they spelled it wrong." She turned a paper toward him. *Sirus Thackery*, it read, in a clerk's careful hand. "There is one way past, and it runs through water the Guild controls and no one else does. The drowned channels. The old ferry-roads under the point."

"And your price?"

Corvane leaned back. Her rings clicked against the driftwood, one after another, like a slow clock. "You know what it is. You paid it once already. I watched you pay it." Her eyes went to his hands — to the faint white scarring along the knuckles where the knife had gone in, not into flesh exactly, but into whatever it was that let him hear. "Half of what remains. That's the fare."

* * *

He did not answer her right away. He asked instead for an hour, and she gave him two, because Guildmasters are generous with time when they know the outcome.

Cyrus carried the salt block out onto the gallery's seaward balcony, where the wind came off the blockaded harbor thick with tar and cold iron, and he set it down between his palms the way he had done ten thousand times in the workshop above the inn at Grayhollow. The block was smaller now. He noticed that the way you notice a candle burning low — not all at once, but in the way the light reached less far across the table.

"Maren," he said quietly.

The salt answered the way it always answered: not in sound, exactly, but in pressure, in a voice felt along the bones of the hands rather than heard by the ears. His sister's voice. Dead eleven years, drowned off the Thornfell coast, preserved by whatever stubborn alchemy lived in her — the same salt-craft he'd inherited, turned inward at the last, so that some part of her had refused the silence the rest of the dead had been forced into.

*"You're doing sums,"* she said. *"I can feel it. You get that look. You got it when you were nine and you broke Mother's carving chisel and spent a whole day deciding whether to confess."*

"I'm not going to confess anything," Cyrus said. "I'm going to pay a toll."

A pause in the salt. When she spoke again her voice was thinner, and he hated that he could hear it, hated more that he was the reason. *"How much did the last one take?"*

"Enough."

*"Cyrus."*

"Half," he said, because lying to her had never worked, not when she was alive either. "Half of what's left. After this there won't be much. A year, maybe two, if I'm careful. Less if I have to do it again."

The wind moved across the balcony. Down in the harbor, a gull screamed at the black ships and got no answer.

*"And what happens,"* Maren said, very carefully, the way she used to speak when she was angry enough to be gentle about it, *"to the person who can't hear the dead anymore? In a world where the dead are the only ones telling the truth?"*

"I'll still have you," Cyrus said. "That's the arithmetic. I spend everything else, and at the end of it I still have my sister's voice. There are men who'd call that a bargain."

*"And when I'm quiet?"*

He hadn't expected her to say it. He had thought — he had *hoped* — that the fading would come gradually enough, gently enough, that neither of them would ever have to name it. But Maren had never been afraid of naming things. It was why she'd died carrying a warning no one wanted to hear, and why he was here, smuggling a broken king through a world with no stars, because of a warning he himself had refused.

"When you're quiet," he said, "I'll have kept a king alive. And a knight. And however many people the free cities save because Aldric Thorne lives to give his confession at the Salt Throne. That's the trade. One voice against —"

*"Don't."* The word came sharp as a snapped line. *"Don't you dare do the sum out loud. Don't you dare put me on one side of a scale and weigh me against strangers. I am not cargo, Cy. I'm your sister. And you're spending me like coin."*

The salt block sat cold between his hands. Below, the black ships waited on their folded wings.

"It's my hearing to spend," he said.

*"It was my death you never asked about,"* she said, softer now, which was worse, *"and you're still not asking. You're just paying. Paying and paying. Do you even know what you're buying with it? Or does it just feel better than stopping?"*

He had no answer for that. He lifted the block, kissed its cold face the way he had kissed her forehead at the funeral they'd never properly held, and went back inside to tell Corvane Hale yes.

* * *

The cutting was done in a back room of the Guildhall that smelled of lamp oil and dried kelp, on a table scarred by other transactions of the same kind. Cyrus learned, lying down on it, that he was not the first. That there were others in Vessamy who had traded pieces of themselves to the Ferryman's Guild — a pilot who no longer dreamed, a widow who had sold her grief entire and now smiled at things that should have broken her. The Guild did not deal only in hearing. The Guild dealt in whatever a person had that couldn't be weighed.

The Guild's cutter was a small grey man named Pellwith, with hands cleaner than any surgeon's and eyes that never quite settled on what they looked at. He laid out the knives — not Cyrus's own; the Guild supplied its own instruments, thin-bladed, salt-dark — and asked him the questions the ritual required.

"What do you hear?"

"The dead," Cyrus said. "All of them. Every grave I've stood over. Every bone I've carved. They whisper. They've whispered since I was a child."

"And what will remain?"

"One voice," Cyrus said. "One voice only. The one in the salt."

Pellwith nodded as though this were a common request, which perhaps it was. "Then we cut around it. Hold what you're keeping. Think of nothing else. If your mind wanders while the blade is in, the cut wanders with it."

So Cyrus held his sister in his mind — not her death, not the warning she'd carried, but the small things: her laugh when she'd taught him to read tide-marks; the way she'd salted the workshop floor every solstice "for luck, though really for the smell"; the argument they'd had the last time, the one he'd walked out of, the one he'd never finished because three days later the sea had finished it for them. He held those things the way a drowning man holds a spar.

The knife went in.

It did not hurt the way flesh hurts. It hurt the way a room goes dark when a door closes — one moment full, the next moment less, and the loss arriving before the understanding of it. Cyrus felt voices go. Not named voices; he had never known most of them by name. Just *voices*, the ambient murmur of the dead that had underlaid every hour of his life since childhood, the sound of the world's basement, the constant company he had complained of and relied upon and never once imagined losing. They went like a tide going out. He felt the shape of the silence they left behind, vast and smooth and terrible, and in the middle of it, one small pressure remained, warm against the cold:

*Maren.*

Still there. Fainter. Like a voice calling from farther down the beach.

When it was over, Pellwith wrapped the excised thing — he could not see what it was; it looked like nothing, a curl of grey air, a sliver of winter — in oilcloth and carried it away to wherever the Guild kept its ledgers of intangibles. Cyrus sat up slowly. The room sounded different. The room sounded like a room. For the first time since he was six years old, he was alone in his own head except for one voice, and he found, to his horror, that part of him wanted to weep with relief at the quiet, and that relief frightened him more than the loss did.

Because if the quiet felt like mercy, then what else might he trade, given time? What else might start to feel like a gift?

* * *

Ottoline was waiting for him in the courtyard when he came out, in the grey light of late afternoon, standing beside the fountain with her sword across her back and her arms crossed in the way she crossed them when she had been rehearsing an argument. Behind her, Aldric sat on the fountain's rim wrapped in a fishmonger's cloak, watching the water with the fragile attention of a man who had recently remembered how to be present in a room.

"You're bleeding," Ottoline said.

"I'm not."

"Your hands are shaking and you've gone grey around the mouth. You're bleeding somewhere I can't see." She stepped closer. Up close, her eyes were the color of wet slate, and they missed nothing. "What did they take?"

"A hearing," Cyrus said. "Of the dead. Part of it. It's — it's how the Guild prices things the crown can't tax."

"How much part?"

"Enough."

"Cyrus Thackeray." She said his full name the way she drew a sword: completely, and meaning business. "I have served a dynasty that lied to itself for three generations, and I learned to hear the lie under the words. So I'll ask plainly, and you'll answer plainly, or I'll ask the king to order you, and he'll do it, because he owes you and he hates owing."

Cyrus looked at Aldric. The king looked back, and there was something in his face that wasn't madness at all — something tired and clear and ashamed, the face of a man who had begun, in the flooded chapel and the passes beyond it, to understand what his compromises had cost, and who recognized the same arithmetic starting again in someone else.

"He paid with his sister," Aldric said quietly. "Didn't you. The voice in the salt. Each payment takes more of it."

"She's not *paid*," Cyrus said, sharper than he meant. "She's not gone. She's — quieter. That's all. Quieter isn't gone."

"No," Ottoline said. "Quieter is the road to gone, and you know it, because you're a carver, and carvers know what things look like when they're being worn away. You've spent your life restoring what time erodes. And now you're standing in a Guildhall letting time erode the one voice you love, one toll at a time, and calling it arithmetic." She uncrossed her arms. "Why?"

"Because the alternative is losing *everything*," Cyrus said, and his voice cracked on it, and he hated that too. "Because the Regent's fleet is sitting in that harbor, and the roads east are watched, and the only way to get a king to a throne where someone can cut a parasite off his head is to keep paying a guild of legalized extortionists until we run out of sea. Because if Aldric dies, the silencing spreads, and every voice goes — hers included, salt or no salt, because the wards are *eaten*, Ser Ottoline, eaten from the inside, and when the Hollow Regent finishes its meal there won't be a dead voice left in the world to vouch for anyone. Including yours. Including the ones in your chapel walls that gave you back your faith." He was breathing hard. The courtyard seemed very quiet — truly quiet, newly quiet, and he realized with a lurch that he would never stop noticing that. "So yes. I'm spending her. Piece by piece. And I will keep spending her, because the sum at the end is worth it, and if that makes me a monster then the free cities can add me to the list of things they don't kneel to."

For a long moment Ottoline said nothing. The fountain murmured. Somewhere above, gulls argued over the masts.

"You love her," Ottoline said at last. It was not a question, and it was not an accusation. It was simply placed on the ground between them, like a drawn map.

"More than anything that's ever been true."

"Then hear me." She came closer still, and lowered her voice, and what was in her voice was not pity, which he could have borne easily, but recognition, which he could not. "I loved a dynasty. My whole life. I loved it the way you love her — without arithmetic, without price, without any thought of what it was costing me. And the dynasty ate itself, and when it fell I discovered I had nothing left, because I had spent everything on something that never once asked me to stop." Her hand came to rest on his shoulder, gauntleted, heavy, steady. "Your sister is asking you to stop. I heard her arguing with you on that balcony this morning — don't look surprised, the whole gallery heard you talking to a salt block, and the whole gallery understood every word. She is asking you to stop spending her. And a man who cannot hear the voice he loves when it begs him — that man is already half-deaf, whatever the Guild leaves him."

Cyrus opened his mouth. Closed it. Looked down at his hands, at the new scar laid over the old one, white lines crossing like tally marks.

"I'll pay the third one myself," Aldric said.

They both turned. The king had risen from the fountain's rim, and he stood straighter than Cyrus had yet seen him stand, wrapped in his fishmonger's rags with the ghost of a crown still visible in the set of his shoulders.

"There must be something in me the Guild would take," Aldric said. "There's a great deal in me that deserves taking. Let me carry some of the fare. I will not arrive at the Salt Throne having been purchased entirely with another man's sister."

"That's not how it works," Cyrus began.

"Then make it how it works," Aldric said. "You're a smuggler. Smugglers find ways. Find one."

* * *

That night, in the rented room above the chandler's shop where the three of them slept in shifts, Cyrus took out the salt block and held it a long time before speaking.

"I'm sorry," he said finally.

The salt was slow answering. Slower than yesterday. He timed it without wanting to — the way you time a pulse you're afraid for.

*"For which part?"* Maren asked.

"For weighing you. For putting you on the scale at all."

Another pause. Long enough that he pressed the block harder against his palms, hard enough to ache.

*"Cy,"* she said, and her voice was thin as thread now, a voice heard through a wall, through years, through the closing of a door, *"when this is over — when the crown is off him and the stars are back — promise me you'll carve my stone and walk away from it. Promise me you won't sit in workshops listening to a dead girl for the rest of your life. Promise me you'll go be among the living, who are loud and stupid and alive, and let me be quiet the way the dead are supposed to be quiet. Promise me the last payment buys you *out*, not just them in."*

"I can't promise that."

*"I know,"* she said, and he could hear the smile in it, faded but unmistakable, his sister's smile worn down to its last thread. *"Promise anyway. Lies run in the family."*

He laughed — a cracked, wet sound in the dark room — and promised, and knew, lying there with the harbor's black ships rocking beyond the shutters, that it was the first lie he'd ever told her that she saw straight through and accepted anyway.

In the morning, Corvane Hale sent word: the drowned channels would be open to them on the evening tide, weather and the Regent permitting. And with the message came a second slip of paper, in the same clerk's careful hand, that made Cyrus's blood go cold.

Not a bill. A report.

*A rider came asking after three travelers on the eastern road,* it read. *The Guild does not sell information. The Guild sells passage. But the rider paid in gold, and gold, unlike loyalty, is legal tender.*

Someone had described them. Someone had described the route east.

The drowned channels, or the long road over the Salt Steps — and one of them, now, had a price on it that wasn't measured in hearing at all.
