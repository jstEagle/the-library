## 11. The Bone Orchard

Ottoline Vane had seen fields of the dead before. She had knelt in the mud at Redford Crossing and pulled arrows from men she had trained as boys; she had burned plague-carts outside Vessamy's gates until her hands smelled of fat and ash for a season; she had stood vigil over a drowned chapel while the water rose and took the names off the tombs one by one. She had believed, in the stubborn way of old soldiers, that she had no more horrors left to be shown.

The valley corrected her.

They came down out of the pine shadow an hour past dawn, Cyrus leading with his hood up and his carving knives wrapped close against the cold, Aldric stumbling between them on a staff Ottoline had cut for him from blackthorn. The king was better than he had been — lucid in longer stretches now, his fever breaking like weather rather than drowning him whole — but he still flinched from open sky, still counted the stars that weren't there, moving his lips through constellations like a man reciting debts.

"Smoke," Cyrus said, and stopped so suddenly that Ottoline nearly walked into him. "Do you smell it?"

She did. Not woodsmoke. Something sweeter and fouler underneath, like resin burned in a chapel, like incense spoiled by rot. It rose out of the valley ahead of them in a thin grey veil, and beneath it there was another smell too, older, that took her a moment to name: salt. The same salt that lived in Cyrus's block of memory. The same salt that was eating the world.

"The road bends north around it," Cyrus said quietly. His voice had changed since they'd left the flooded chapel behind them. He spoke less to her now and more *past* her, listening always to some whisper she could not hear — his dead sister, she supposed, the voice he carried like contraband. "There's a shepherd's track along the ridge. We don't have to go down."

"We're going down," Aldric said.

Both of them turned. The king stood with his hand still resting on the blackthorn, his face pale as tallow but steady, and when he met Ottoline's eyes there was something in them she had not seen since Thornfell: not madness, not terror. Purpose. It sat on him awkwardly, the way a crown sits on a man who has learned what crowns cost.

"Aldric," she began, using his name because no one else would, because *your majesty* would have been a lie neither of them could afford, "we do not know what's down there."

"I know what's down there." His voice cracked in the middle, but he pushed through it. "I dreamed it for three nights running before I ever saw this valley. It is mine, Ser Ottoline. Whatever the Regent has planted, it grew from my failure. A king does not go around his own grave."

*His own grave.* The words settled over the three of them like frost. Cyrus looked at Ottoline — a quick, sideways look, the look of a smuggler measuring risk — and then shrugged, small and fatalistic, and turned down toward the smoke.

They went down.

* * *

The trees ended all at once, cut back in a clean arc as though a giant had drawn a boundary with a blade. Beyond the treeline the valley floor opened into a shallow bowl, terraced in long rows, and Ottoline understood, in the first heartbeat of seeing it, why the Regent's servants called it what the wind carried to them across the distance: an orchard.

Rows. Neat rows, spaced like apple trees, running the length of the bowl and climbing its far side. And from every row hung fruit.

They were bodies. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Suspended from gallows-poles of black iron that rose twenty feet out of the earth, each pole crowned with a crook like a shepherd's staff or a branch pruned to shape. From each crook hung chains, and from each chain a figure — upright, arms loose, heads bowed forward like blossoms too heavy for their stems. They were dressed as they had died: farmers in their smocks, soldiers in rotted livery, a woman in a wedding gown gone grey, children in garments too small even for the small bones inside them. Frost silvered them all equally. The morning light came slantwise through the smoke-veil and caught them, and for one obscene moment the whole valley glittered like a hoard, like treasure, like something harvested.

None of them moved. None of them needed to move. That was the horror of it — that they were tended, pruned, kept. The ground between the rows was swept. There were paths, raked gravel paths, for the gardeners who must come.

Ottoline's body knew what to do before her mind did. Her sword was half-drawn before she felt her own arm move, and Cyrus's hand closed on her wrist, cold and strong.

"Don't," he breathed. "Look at the poles. Look at the base of them."

She looked. At the foot of each iron tree, the earth was dark with moisture, glistening. And rising from that dark earth, faint as heat-shimmer, hung wisps of grey — thin ribbons of something drifting upward from the hanging figures, sinking downward into the soil, drawn off them slowly like sap from tapped maples. Like juice pressed from fruit.

"What are they doing to them?" Her voice came out flat, which was how she knew it was bad. When Ottoline Vane shouted, she was angry. When she went quiet, she was afraid.

"It's what we said in the chapel." Cyrus had not let go of her wrist. "The wards didn't fall. They were eaten. This is where it goes after — the voices, the—" He stopped. Swallowed. "You remember the walls at the chapel. How they spoke to you."

She remembered. She would remember it until she died: the dead of the drowned chapel murmuring through the stones, vouching for the bone-carver, the first voices she had heard in six years of silence. Six years since the stars went out. Six years since every ghost in Thornfell had gone quiet at once, mid-whisper, mid-prayer, mid-farewell — silenced like a door closing on a room full of singers.

"They're being milked," Aldric said.

He had come up beside them without either of them hearing, which should have been impossible for a man leaning on a blackthorn staff, and perhaps it was impossible, and perhaps kings simply arrived when they chose. His eyes were fixed on the nearest row, on a figure hanging thirty feet away — a broad-shouldered man in a soldier's coat, the coat's buttons stamped with the boar of House Thorne.

Aldric's own sigil.

"That's Harl Wicker," the king said. His voice had gone very soft. "Captain of the east gate at Carrowmere. He held the gate for two days against the Regent's first advance so the city could evacuate. I gave him a commendation. I pinned it on him myself." He stepped forward, off the path, toward the hanging dead, and this time it was Ottoline who reached for him and Cyrus who stopped *her*, a finger raised, his head tilted — listening.

"He wants to," Cyrus murmured. "Let him. Maren says— " He caught himself, the way he always did when the sister's name slipped out. "The dead here can't hurt anyone anymore. That's rather the point."

So they let the king walk alone down the raked path between the rows, and Ottoline followed at ten paces with her hand on her sword hilt and her heart going like a siege drum, because everything in her screamed that this place was a trap, that the gardeners would come, that mercy itself had been weaponized into bait. But no horns sounded. No dead things stirred in the smoke. There was only the drip of meltwater off iron chains and the vast, appalling silence of the harvest.

Aldric stopped beneath Captain Wicker and reached up.

Ottoline wanted to tell him not to touch it. Some instinct older than training told her that you did not touch the offerings of a god you did not worship. But the king stretched up on his toes, and his fingers brushed the hanging man's frozen hand — just brushed it, knuckles grazing frost — and then he stood there, waiting, the way you wait at a sickbed. Listening.

Nothing happened.

She saw it land on him. Saw the moment the hope went out of his posture, the slow collapse of the shoulders, the way his hand fell back to his side as though the captain's silence were a weight set into it. He touched the man again, harder, pressing palm to palm against the dead flesh, and still nothing — no whisper, no murmur, none of the wall-speech of the chapel, nothing at all.

"There's nothing in him," Aldric said. He did not turn around. His voice carried strangely well in the swept valley, flat and clear as a struck coin. "I held this man's commission. I heard him laugh. He had daughters. He used to sing to them on the walls and pretend he wasn't singing." His thumb traced the frozen fingers, gentle, unbearably gentle. "And there's *nothing*. Not sleeping. Not gone elsewhere. Empty. They've taken—" His voice failed, rebuilt itself. "Everything that made him Harl Wicker has been drunk."

He turned to face them, and Ottoline saw that the king was crying without any of the noise of crying, tears simply present on his face as though the cold had wrung them out of him.

"This is what I fed," he said. "This is what my compromises watered. Every grant I signed, every ward-mason I dismissed because the treasury complained, every night I told myself the crown was only a crown." He looked down the endless rows, at the thousands hanging in their orchards of iron. "It was never a crown. It was a mouth. And I set the table."

"You didn't know," Ottoline said. The words came out harsher than she intended, and she found she did not care.

"No," Aldric agreed. "Not knowing was my particular genius."

* * *

They walked the rows. It was Ottoline who insisted — over Cyrus's flat refusal, over the king's exhausted silence — because someone had to see it properly. Someone had to count. In six years of exile she had kept faith alive on scraps: a chapel wall speaking, a smuggler's decency, a broken king's confession. Faith starved on scraps becomes something harder. She walked the orchard the way she had once walked battlefields after the fighting, reading the dead, taking the tally that someone owed them.

She read them by their clothes and their wounds. A miller, flour still ghosting his sleeves. Three sisters buried — no, hung — together, their arms not chained but bound with ribbon, someone having loved them enough to tie them into the next world hand in hand. A whole rank of Thornfell border-wardens, forty men, their warden-green gone black with old blood, hung in perfect military order as though even in harvest the Regent appreciated discipline. Near the far terrace, smaller crooks. Smaller chains.

She stopped counting children at sixty.

Cyrus walked beside her partway and then drifted, his head bowed, listening to whatever remained to him to listen to. Once he stopped and put his palm flat against the swept earth and stayed there a long time, and when he straightened his face was the face of a man who has heard something he will spend years deciding whether to repeat.

"The soil's drinking," he said quietly. "All of it drains down to something under the valley. Maren thinks— " the name again, unguarded, grief making him careless — "Maren thinks there's a root. One root. Everything hangs from one root."

"Then everything can be cut from one root," Ottoline said.

Cyrus looked at her. Under the hood, his eyes were tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "That's the second time you've said something like that."

"And I'll say it again until it stops needing saying."

At the head of the central row stood a pole taller than the rest, older, its iron gone green-black with verdigris, and from it hung a figure that made Ottoline's breath stop in her throat. Not because of the body — though the body was wrong, preserved differently, dressed in robes of state gone stiff with frost — but because of the placard nailed above it, white wood, lettered in careful black script:

**THE LAST KING OF THORNFELL.**

Beneath the body's bowed head, the neck was bare. No crown.

"That's a lie," Aldric said behind her. He had followed despite everything, drawn down the rows like water finding its level. He stared up at the hanging figure, and his expression moved through recognition into confusion into something colder. "That's not a king. That's not any king. The robes are mine — the pattern is mine — but the face..." He climbed the low rail at the row's head, heedless of Ottoline's shout, and gripped the frozen jaw and turned it to the light. "Wax. It's wax over a frame. A *display*." He dropped back down, breathing hard. "They hung an effigy of me in my own robes. This isn't a graveyard, Ser Ottoline. It's a *garden*. And gardens are statements. This is what the Regent tells the world happened to the last king of Thornfell."

"And what does the Regent tell the world will happen to the current one?" Ottoline asked, before she could stop herself.

The question hung between them in the smoking air. Aldric smiled — a terrible, small smile, the first she had ever seen him wear — and it aged him ten years and freed him of twenty.

"That I'm already dead," he said. "That's what I kept telling you all, isn't it? Perhaps the Regent and I finally agree on something. Perhaps the only way out of that crown is through the truth of it." He looked up one more time at the wax king hanging in his stolen robes, and spat, delicately, into the raked gravel. "Let them keep the effigy. I intend to be considerably more trouble than that."

* * *

They made camp that night on the ridge above the valley, where the smoke couldn't reach and the pines stood thick, and Ottoline took first watch because she could not have slept if the gods themselves had ordered it.

Below, in the dark, the orchard glowed faintly. She had not noticed it by day. Now, in the blue hour, she could see it: a dim phosphorescence rising off the rows, off the hanging figures, off the dark drinking earth — pale as foxfire, pale as the last light of stars that no longer existed overhead. The harvested light of thousands of souls, seeping out of storage, illuminating nothing.

She heard footsteps and did not turn. She knew the weight of them. Twenty-two years carrying a knight's plate teaches a woman the sound of everyone she loves.

"He'll do it," Cyrus said, settling onto the rock beside her. "Cut it off. Or die trying. You know that."

"I've known it since the cave." Below them, the foxfire shimmered. "When I found him in the pass, I thought I was retrieving a king. I thought the task was to deliver a thing intact to people who would know what to do with it." She flexed her sword-hand, feeling the old ache in the knuckles. "But kings aren't things, Thackeray. I've served two dynasties and I am only now learning what I was actually guarding. Not the bloodline. The *accounting*. Somebody has to answer for all of this. Somebody has to stand in front of those rows and say *this was done in our name, and it ends, and here is the price*."

"The free cities will want a trial."

"The free cities can have their trial." She said it evenly, watching the glow. "Trials are for the living, and the living deserve them. But this—" she nodded at the valley, at the silent thousands, at the small ones she had refused to finish counting — "this doesn't wait for a court. There's no court with jurisdiction over a mouth that eats the dead. What's owed here gets paid outside any law ever written, or it doesn't get paid at all. I mean to collect."

Cyrus was quiet a moment. "That sounds like a vow."

"It is." She had never spoken one aloud before, not even at her knighting; oaths, in her experience, were things you kept, not things you announced. But the orchard below deserved announcement. The sixty children deserved announcement. "Before the dead of this valley and the one dead voice still kind enough to speak to us — hear me. I will not rest, I will not kneel, and I will not stop until the Hollow Regent is unmade root and branch and the silenced are given back their names. No court will hold me and no crown will command me. I answer to the tally." She drew her sword and laid it flat across her knees, blade catching the foxfire glow. "So witnessed."

Beside her, Cyrus said nothing for a long moment. Then, softly, in the tone of a man translating: "Maren says she heard. All of it."

"Good." Ottoline watched the orchard burn its cold light into the dark. "Then the dead have their first witness."

It was Cyrus who broke the silence again, later, when the fire had burned to coals and Aldric slept at last, truly slept, for the first time in days. The bone-carver sat up sharply in his bedroll, head cocked, listening to the voice only he could carry, and when he turned to her his face in the ember-light was grey.

"Ser Ottoline," he said. "We have a problem. Maren says the gardeners are coming back at dawn — and they're bringing wagons."
