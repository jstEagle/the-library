## 12. The Traitor's Road

The Guild guide's name was Fenwick Halse, and he smelled of tallow and honest sweat, and Cyrus trusted him the way he had trusted every Guild man since he was nine years old — which is to say, completely, because the Guild was the Guild, and the Guild did not take sides.

That was the first lie of his life he had ever needed to unlearn.

They were three days out of Vessamy when Halse began asking questions that were not a guide's questions. Not where do you want to go but how fast can you walk it. Not is the king resting but does the king sleep deeply. He asked them at the fire, mild as milk, turning a strip of dried eel over the coals, and Cyrus answered the way smugglers answer: with less than the truth and more than silence. But that night, lying under a sky still starless — the stars had not come back, would not come back until they reached the Salt Throne, if they reached it — Cyrus lay awake and turned Halse's questions over like stones in a stream bed, looking for what lived underneath.

Maren's voice came faint from the salt block wrapped in oilcloth against his chest. Fainter every day. Each cut of the carving knife in the Guild halls had taken its tithe, and what remained of her was a whisper through wool.

*The Salt Steps,* she said, or he dreamed she said. *He knows about the Salt Steps.*

Cyrus sat up so fast his spine cracked.

"Halse," he said into the dark. "How long to the Steps?"

The guide rolled over. His eyes caught the banked firelight, flat as coins. "Half a day, if we push."

"We don't push." Cyrus heard his own voice go flat and hard, the voice he used on buyers who tried to haggle over bones of the honored dead. "We turn east. There's a shelf road above the gorge. Rougher. Slower. We take it."

Halse sat up too, unhurried, and smiled, and there was nothing in the smile. "Master Thackeray. The Shelf Road hasn't been walked in twenty years. Rockfall. The Guild won't—"

"The Guild isn't here." Ottoline's voice came from the shadows where she kept her watch, always half-asleep, never asleep. Her sword whispered an inch out of its scabbard and stopped. "And neither are you anymore, I think."

Halse looked at her. Then at Aldric, who slept the broken sleep of a man whose blood was a beacon. Then at Cyrus, and something in his honest face finally let go of being honest, the way a mask lets go when the hand behind it tires.

"They have my daughters," he said simply. "Both of them. In the salt mines below Carrowgate. He showed me their names written in his ledger. He has a *ledger*, Master Thackeray. Every debt the free cities owe him, every favor, every fear. Your name was in it too. Under assets."

Cyrus felt the words land somewhere beneath his ribs. "What did it say?"

"'Hearing, partial. Two payments rendered. Value: high.'" Halse shrugged, and it was the shrug of a man who had already died a little and found it survivable. "You're not a person to him. You're an account. So am I. The only difference between us is that I settled mine tonight."

Ottoline moved. Cyrus shouted. Halse ran — not toward them, away, down the slope toward the black throat of the gorge, and by the time Ottoline reached the edge of the camp he was gone, swallowed by country he knew better than they did.

"Let him run," Aldric said. He was awake. He was always awake now; the crown's attention found him even in sleep, and sleep had become a country he visited briefly, like an ambassador to hostile court. His face in the firelight was gray. "It doesn't matter. It was never about whether he told them. It was about how long we'd take to notice."

*He's right,* Maren breathed, so faintly Cyrus had to close his eyes and cup the salt block to hear her. *Ride the Shelf Road. Trust the stone. And Cyrus — the Steps will be watched anyway. They'll want the choke point regardless. Don't trust the road. Trust the dead.*

*Which dead?*

But she was gone, spent, a candle burned down to the wick, and Cyrus knelt in the cold holding the block against his heart like a man trying to warm a stone.

* * *

They climbed through the grey hour before dawn, up switchbacks slick with rime, onto the Shelf Road — a ledge no wider than a cart, cut into the gorge wall by hands long forgotten, crumbling in places into nothing at all. Below them the river ran white and deafening. Above them, nothing. No stars. The sky was a lid.

Aldric walked badly. The wound from the ambush at the coast had never properly closed; it wept through its bandages, and the bandages had begun to smell sweet in a way wounds should not smell, and three times that morning Cyrus had seen him stop without knowing he had stopped, staring at the rock wall as though someone were writing on it.

"What does it say?" Cyrus asked him once, quietly.

"It says come home." Aldric's laugh had no floor to it. "It's very patient. That's the worst part. It doesn't rage. It *waits*. Kings are used to waiting things. I was raised by one." He looked at Cyrus then, and for a moment — one moment — the madness drained out and left something older and sadder underneath. "When we reach the throne, whatever happens, don't let me kneel. Even if I ask. Even if I beg. A kneeling king is halfway crowned."

Ottoline, ahead of them, said nothing, but her hand rested a moment on Aldric's shoulder as she passed, and that was its own kind of sentence.

They made the head of the gorge by late afternoon, and there the Shelf Road ended at a slide of shattered rock, and beyond the slide, visible across four miles of open white slope, rose the Salt Steps themselves — two hundred staircases of pale stone cut into the mountainside, ascending in long zigzags toward the pass that led east, toward the free territories, toward everything.

And on the Steps, small with distance, dark with intent: soldiers.

Hundreds. Ranked along the switchbacks like beads on an abacus, counting them. Banners hung limp in the windless cold — banners bearing no sigil at all, which was worse than any sigil, because the Regent's army did not march under a symbol; it marched under absence.

"There's another way," Cyrus said. He said it before he knew it was true, and then he knew it, because Maren had said *trust the dead*, and Cyrus Thackeray had been reading the roads' dead all his life. Every smuggler's path was a graveyard of the men who'd failed to walk it, and every failure left a mark on the stone, the way a scar leaves a mark on bone. "There's a chimney. A crack in the mountain above the slide. My father used it. His father used it. It goes up through the mountain's own marrow and comes out above the Steps, past the whole ambush."

"Can the king climb it?" Ottoline asked.

"No," Aldric said. "Yes. It doesn't matter. Take me up it anyway. If I die in a crack in a mountain, the crown gets a corpse and nothing else, and that's a victory of sorts."

They crossed the slide with the sun going down behind them — going down into a sky that gave back no stars, no moon, nothing but black — and found the chimney exactly where Cyrus's memory said it would be, a vertical fissure breathing cold air upward, wide enough for shoulders, not much more.

Cyrus went first. The rock took his weight grudgingly, the way old things do. Behind him came Ottoline, pushing Aldric ahead of her, her armor padded with rags so it wouldn't ring against the stone. They climbed in darkness and in the sound of each other's breath, and twice Aldric seized up entirely, rigid, whispering, and twice Ottoline held him against the rock with her body until the fit passed, and the mountain held all three of them in its cold throat and said nothing, promised nothing, asked nothing.

That was why Cyrus loved the mountains. Everything else wanted something from you.

They came out above the world an hour after full dark, onto a shoulder of bare granite below the summit crags, and Cyrus allowed himself one breath of triumph before the wind brought him the sound.

Boots. Many boots. On the Steps below, moving fast, moving *up* — not in ranks now but spread out, hunting formation, torches bobbing like will-o'-wisps. Halse had sold more than the route. Halse had sold the timing. They had known the chimney was the only escape, and they had sent a third of their force around to wait above.

Except they hadn't waited above. They were coming up the Steps. Which meant—

"They think we're still on the Shelf Road," Ottoline breathed beside him, flattened against the granite. She had seen it a heartbeat after he had. "They're sweeping the Steps because the Steps are the obvious road. They don't know about the chimney."

"They know about *everything*," Aldric whispered, and pressed both hands to his temples. "He's looking. He's looking right now, he's—" and then he screamed, short and bitten off, and clamped his own wrist between his teeth to keep quiet, and blood ran down his chin, and below them the torches paused, all together, like a flock startled by a hawk.

Cyrus made the decision the way his sister used to make decisions: instantly, and with total commitment, and with a prayer tucked inside it.

"Not down," he mouthed. "*Across.*"

There was a traverse. He'd heard of it from a dying smuggler named Corrag whose last confession Cyrus had carved into a memorial bone thirty years ago — a traverse along the north face of the summit massif, above the Steps, exposed to wind and void, that came out on the far side of the pass. Corrag had called it the Widow's Stitch. Corrag had also called it survivable, once, and he had been the only one.

They went. For an hour that lasted a season they went, toes on ledges the width of a knife's spine, fingers finding holds by faith, the drop beside them so absolute it stopped being frightening and became merely factual, the way death becomes factual when you carry it daily. Aldric climbed like a man being born again — clumsily, weeping, but climbing. Once his foot skated free and Ottoline caught his collar in her fist and held him against eight hundred feet of nothing with one arm while he found the rock again, and neither of them spoke of it after, because there was nothing to say that the grip hadn't already said.

They came down off the far side as the false dawn was greying east, into a hanging valley of snow and silence, and collapsed among the rocks like marionettes with their strings cut.

It was Ottoline who noticed the blood.

Not Aldric's. Cyrus's. Somewhere on the traverse a hold had opened his palm without his noticing, and now his left hand was sheeted red, frozen stiff, the carving knife-hand, the hearing-of-the-dead hand. He stared at it with strange detachment while Ottoline bound it with strips of her own cloak, and it was only when she finished, when the pain arrived all at once like a wave finding the shore, that he understood the shape of the trade he'd been making all this time without admitting it.

Every mile east cost him. His hearing. His hand. Maren, thread by thread. And the Guild — the neutral, honest, toll-taking Guild — had sold the map of his sacrifice to a thing that wore a drowned king's crown, for the price of two daughters and a line in a ledger.

"Cyrus." Ottoline's voice, low. "Look at the valley."

He looked. Along the far wall of the hanging valley, half-buried in snow, stood cairns. Dozens of them. Hundreds. Old ones, ancient ones, stones stacked by dead hands over dead travelers, marking a road so old no map admitted it existed.

And from the largest cairn, thin as breath, came voices.

Not Maren. These were strangers, centuries gone, the dead of the Widow's Stitch and the passes beyond it — and they were speaking. Murmuring. The silencing had not reached here. Whatever the Regent's power was, it followed armies and ledgers and roads men wrote down, and this road had never been written down by anyone living.

Cyrus pressed his bleeding hand against the nearest cairn, and the dead of the mountain murmured around him like a congregation settling, and one voice, dry as paper, said clearly:

*Welcome, keeper.*

He did not know yet what the word meant. He would learn. But standing there in the grey light with his hand ruined and his sister nearly silent and his king shivering in the snow, Cyrus Thackeray felt, for the first time since the stars went out, that the road itself had chosen sides.

And then the wind shifted, and from the pass beyond the valley came the smell he had hoped never to smell again: salt, and rot, and the particular ozone tang of the Hollow Regent's working. A rider crested the eastern ridge alone — a herald, black-clad, faceless — and looked down into the valley with eyes that were not eyes, and raised one hand, and every cairn in the valley fell silent at once, mid-word, like throats cut.

Except one. The smallest. The oldest. From deep within it, almost past hearing, Maren's voice — or the ghost of what was left of her:

*Cyrus. Run.*
