## 13. What Maren Never Said

The storm found them an hour after the pass narrowed, and the cave found them an hour after that — a seam in the mountain's flank that Cyrus knew the way other men knew their own doorways. He had slept in it twice as a boy, once as a young man running contraband salt-pork over the Shoulder, and never since. It was smaller than memory made it. A throat of grey stone, breathing cold.

Aldric went in first, half-carried between Cyrus's shoulder and Ottoline's braced arm, his fever burning so high that Cyrus could feel it through two layers of wool. The king had stopped raving somewhere on the scree slope below, which frightened Cyrus more than the raving had. Madmen shouted. This man only whispered, over and over, *it knows where I bleed, it knows where I bleed*, like a prayer said backward.

Ottoline got a fire going — small, screened by her shield, fed with the twisted dwarf-pine she'd hacked from the trail. She did everything without wasted motion, the way she did all things, but Cyrus noticed her hands were not quite steady when she struck the flint. The ambush at the Salt Steps was two days behind them and none of them had spoken of it. There was nothing to say that wouldn't have been an accusation, and the Guild guide who had sold them was beyond accusation now, somewhere at the bottom of a ravine with the Regent's arrows in him.

Cyrus sat against the wall with the salt block in his lap.

It was smaller than it had been. He had known this for weeks the way you know a debt is coming due — refusing to count it, refusing to look. But here in the firelight, with nothing to do but wait out the storm, he finally weighed it in his hands. When he'd pulled it from the floor of his workshop above the inn at Grayhollow, it had been the size of a loaf, dense as marble, humming faintly against his palms like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing. Now it fit inside two cupped hands. Now the hum came in fragments, like a voice heard through a closing door.

Maren.

He turned it over. Salt was honest material; it wore its history on its face. Every cut he had made to free her voice — the little channels, the resonating grooves his carving knives had opened — had cost the block substance. And every day of carrying it, every cold night, every time the Guild's brokers had laid their own knives to it in payment, the block had given up more of itself. Salt dissolved. That was its nature. He had always known this, in the way you know a thing and do not know it.

*Cy.*

The voice came thin as thread. Not weak exactly — Maren had never been weak — but *thinned*, stretched, like cloth washed too many times until the weave showed light.

"I'm here," he said.

*You're crying.*

"I'm not."

*Liar. You always were a liar about the important things and honest about the small ones.* A pause, and then, gentler: *What is it?*

And because there was no one left to perform for — Ottoline was watching Aldric, Aldric was watching the dark, the storm was watching all of them — Cyrus heard himself say:

"How did you die?"

The silence that followed was not empty. It was full, the way a held breath is full.

He had asked before. Of course he had. In the early days, when the block was still fat and her voice still rang clear, he had asked it a dozen ways — casually, over his carving, as though it hardly mattered; urgently, drunk on winter wine; angrily, when she deflected him. She had always given him pieces. *I was on the coast road. It was raining. I was tired.* Fragments, offered like coins from a purse that would not open fully. He had told himself she didn't remember. He had told himself the dead lost things, the way ice loses its shape.

But he had known, somewhere beneath the telling, that she remembered everything. That she was choosing. And that a sister who chose what to tell you was telling you something in the choice itself.

*Maren,* he said now. *Please. We're past it. Whatever it is, we're past it. I've paid the Guild twice. I've watched them hang the dead in trees like fruit. The herald wore your face, Mare. He stood in the square at Vessamy wearing your face and smiled at me with it. Whatever you've been protecting me from—"*

*I wasn't protecting you,* she said.

The correction landed like a dropped knife.

*That's the thing, Cy. Everyone thinks the dead protect the living. We don't. We can't. All we can do is testify. And I—* the voice frayed, gathered itself, frayed again — *I failed to testify. Once. Only once. And it cost everything.*

The fire popped. Ottoline glanced over; Cyrus shook his head minutely and she returned to her vigil over the king.

"Tell me," he said. His voice sounded strange to him, scraped down to the grain. "All of it. However it goes."

*It goes badly,* Maren said. *It goes badly for both of us.*

* *

*You remember the winter I left Grayhollow. You were twenty-three and furious about it — you thought I was running off with some tanner's son because I wouldn't say otherwise. You stood in the yard and told me if I walked out that gate I shouldn't come back expecting the workshop to be mine. You said the trade passed through blood or it didn't pass at all.*

"I remember," Cyrus said. His ears were hot. Twenty years dead and she could still make him feel nine years old and caught.

*I let you think that. It was easier than the truth, and I was young, and the truth required me to admit I was afraid. Here is the truth. I'd been to Thornfell. To the court — not the court itself, the under-court, the carvers' quarter, where they make the ward-bones. You know the great wards along the border are carved bone. Everyone forgets that. Everyone thinks of them as stones or spells, but they're bone. Old bone. Kings' bone, some of it, ground into the grain of them across centuries so the land remembers whose oath holds it up.*

*There was a master carver there, old Vessic, who had gone half blind reading the grain. He called me in because my work with salt had gotten noticed — salt remembers differently than bone does, Cy, it doesn't hold oaths, it holds* voices*, and he wanted to know why, wanted to know if the two could be made to talk. And while I worked in his shop he talked. Old men talk. He told me about the crown.*

"The crown," Cyrus repeated. On the far side of the fire, Aldric stirred at the word, a small animal sound.

*The Drowned Crown. The one they pulled from the bay when the old line nearly ended — you know the story they tell, how it rose with the tide and chose Aldric's grandfather. Vessic told me the true story. That crowns don't choose. They're chosen* for*. And the thing they pulled out of the water was older than any dynasty. It had been a regent once, long ago — a real man, or something shaped like a man — who had eaten himself hollow looking for a way to rule forever. What they fished out of the bay was his hunger, wearing the shape of gold. It doesn't sit on the head, Cy. It sits in it. It drinks the king slowly, year by year, and wears him, and when the king is empty it moves to the next, and each time it moves it grows stronger, because it carries every oath ever sworn to every crown it has worn. The border wards aren't just holding back raiders and wild weather. They're holding back the thing the crown serves. Or — no. That's wrong. They're holding back the thing the crown* feeds on*. The wards are the fence around the pasture.*

*Vessic had read it in the grain of a ward-bone that was failing decades before its time. He'd sent three warnings south. None were answered. So he asked me — me, a journeywoman salt-carver from nowhere, someone no one watched — to carry one more. Not written. Written things get intercepted. He taught me to press it into salt, into a block I carried against my heart for six hundred miles, and he told me: take it to your brother. He's a carver. He'll know what to do with it.*

The fire seemed very small suddenly. The cave very large. Cyrus became aware of his own heartbeat, slow and heavy, like something turning over in deep water.

"You brought it to me," he whispered. "You came home. You put it on my bench and told me you needed a favor, and I—"

*I know what you did,* Maren said, and there was no cruelty in it. That was the worst part. There was no cruelty in it at all.

*You laughed. You said the old man at Thornfell was senile, that everyone knew the crown was just crown, that I'd been taken in by a blind man's ghost stories. You said — I've had twenty years to memorize it, Cy, so forgive me — you said, "If the crown ate kings, someone would have noticed." And then you picked up the block and you almost threw it in the scrap bucket. I saw your arm go back. I saw you decide not to. You gave it back to me instead and you said, "Get rid of this nonsense before someone sees it and we both hang."*

"And then I went to bed," Cyrus said. The words came out flat, factual, terrible. "And you left the next morning. Because I wouldn't hear it."

*I rode west that morning because I thought — I still believed I could find someone else. Another carver. Another ear. Vessic was dying; he'd told me the Regent's people were already asking questions about his correspondence. I thought I'd get ahead of them. I took the coast road because it was fast, and fast was the only advantage I had left.*

*They caught me at the ford below Harrowmere. Four of them, and they weren't soldiers. Soldiers leave marks you can name. These left none. The last thing I heard was rain on the water. The last thing I felt was the salt block against my ribs — they never found it, Cy. I'd sewn it into my jacket lining months before and forgotten to tell even you. Some shepherd found my body three days later and stripped the coat for wool and threw the lining away, and the block lay in a ditch for two winters until a tinker sold it to a salt-merchant, and the merchant sold it to a chandler, and the chandler's widow sold her husband's stock to a dealer in Grayhollow, and the dealer—*

"Sold it to Father," Cyrus finished. His mouth was dry as the block itself. "Father bought it as a curiosity. He kept it on the mantel for eleven years. I inherited it with the tools. I spent years wondering why it hummed. I thought it was a flaw in the making."

*It's not a flaw,* Maren said. *It's the warning. It's been the warning all along, sitting in your workshop above your head every night, singing the same six hundred miles of road into the dark, waiting for you to listen. And when the wards finally fell — when the stars went out and the dead went silent — you finally listened. Not because I was your sister. Because you were frightened.*

"That's not fair," Cyrus said, and heard even as he said it that it was the same sentence, the same tone, the same stubborn younger brother's voice from the yard at Grayhollow, and that he had just proven every word of her testimony true. He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes.

"No," he said. "No, it's fair. It's fair, God, it's fair. I killed you. Not with a knife — with a laugh. With one smug sentence in a doorway. They put the blades in you at Harrowmere, but I handed them the reason to ride. If I'd listened—if I'd carried it myself, or taken it to the Guild, or done anything, anything but—"

*Stop.*

The word cracked across the cave like a whip. Even Ottoline's head came around this time; Aldric sat bolt upright, staring at Cyrus with fever-bright eyes, and for one strange moment Cyrus couldn't tell whether the king had heard Maren or simply recognized the shape of a soul being flayed.

*Cyrus Thackeray, you listen to me, because I am almost too thin to say this twice.* The voice shook, but it was her shake — the one she'd had before the hard words in an argument, the gathering, not the breaking. *Guilt is a luxury. Do you understand me? Guilt is what comfortable people feel instead of acting. You've been carrying me around the mountains for weeks like a relic, and half of that is love, and half of it is penance, and the penance part is useless. It feeds nothing. It saves no one. It just lets you keep the story about yourself — careless brother, doomed sister, tragedy, the end. But that story is a lie, and I am the witness, and I am telling you the truth: you did not kill me. A thing older than dynasties killed me, using four hired hollows and a ford in the rain. The only question that matters — the only one I crossed death to ask — is what you will do now that you know.*

Cyrus lowered his hands. His face was wet and he let it be wet.

"What do I do?" he asked. "Tell me what to do."

*I can't. Dead sisters don't give orders. But I'll tell you what I see, from in here, from the thin place.* A pause; the block flickered against his palms like a guttering lamp. *I see a king in that fire who has been drinking poison for thirty years and blaming himself for being thirsty. I see a knight keeping watch who has been alone so long she's forgotten she's a person and become only a function. And I see my brother, who spent his whole life trading in secrets because belonging to anything openly scared him worse than dying — I see him holding the one secret that ends all secrets. The crown can't be hidden from, Cy. It can't be outrun. We've proven that. There's only one road left, and it goes straight through the middle of the thing.*

Across the fire, Aldric had begun to speak — low, ragged, to no one, or perhaps at last to someone. Ottoline leaned toward him, and Cyrus caught the words in fragments between the storm-gusts at the cave mouth:

"...my father wore it and I never asked... I signed the ward-budgets myself, I *cut* them, I cut the carvers' quarter first because it was easiest..." A sound that might have been laughter or weeping. "...it doesn't want me dead. Death would end the meal..."

The block pulsed once against Cyrus's hands, warm, urgent, fading.

*He's going to ask you to leave him,* Maren said. *Tomorrow, maybe tonight. The fever will crest and he'll beg — I can feel it in the way the stone carries his voice. Don't let him. And don't let Ottoline carry him out of pity either. Pity breaks. Find the third thing. The thing I died trying to deliver. You already know what it is, Cy. You're a carver. You've always known: some things can't be argued loose or pried loose. They can only be* cut*.*

"Maren—" he said, because the hum was thinning even as he held it, the weave showing light, the door almost closed. "Mare, wait. Stay. Just — stay."

*Always,* she said. *That's the one promise salt keeps.*

And then she was quiet — not gone, never quite gone, but folded down into the grain like a voice at the bottom of a well, and the cave was silent except for the wind and the fire and the king's broken murmuring.

Cyrus looked across the flames at Aldric Thorne — at the hollows under his eyes, at the unmarked skin that should have been torn, at the man who insisted he was already dead — and understood, with the calm of a craftsman seeing the whole grain of a piece at last, exactly what had to happen and how impossible it was and that they were going to do it anyway.

Aldric's eyes found his across the fire. Fevered, lucid, terrified.

"She told you," the king whispered. "Didn't she. I heard her. She told you what I am."

"No," Cyrus said. He stood, joints aching, and crossed to the king's side, and knelt there in the dirt of a smuggler's cave with the salt block held against his chest like a shield. "She told me what you *were*. What you are is a cutting job. And I've brought the knives."

Outside, the storm broke apart all at once, the way mountain storms do, and through the clearing air came a sound none of them wanted to hear: horns, far down-valley, many voices strong, moving toward the pass.

They had run out of roads that hid.
