## 14. The King Who Would Be Unmade

The fever came in tides. Aldric learned to read them the way he had once learned to read the moods of a court — by the color of the air, by the small betrayals of his own body. When the tide rose, the cave walls wept salt and the dark grew crowded with faces. When it fell, he could hear the wind again, and the low murmur of the two people who had not yet abandoned him, and the terrible quiet where the crown's voice used to live.

He had begun to prefer the tides.

*You are dead,* the fever said, in the crown's old voice, patient as water wearing at stone. *You have been dead since the night you let it in. The man who walks is only the shape the crown has not finished eating.*

"Your Majesty." Ottoline's hand on his brow was cool and calloused, a gauntlet's ghost of pressure even bare. "Drink."

"I am not your Majesty." His voice came out cracked, a door off its hinge. "That is the whole of it, Ser Ottoline. That is the entire truth of my reign. I am not — there is no *there* there anymore. It hollowed me the way a worm hollows an apple, and it did it so gently I thanked it."

"Drink anyway."

He drank. The water tasted of the cave, of iron and old ice, and beneath it something sweeter that he did not want to name — the taste of the mountain remembering him, perhaps, or remembering the blood in him. That was the worst of it. The crown did not need to see him. It needed only his blood, and his blood was in him everywhere, faithful as a traitor, carrying word of him to it with every beat of the heart it had never quite owned.

"I know what you are doing," he said. "Carrying me deeper into the mountains. Away from the roads. You think distance will save us." He laughed, and hated the sound. "I wore that thing for eleven years, Ottoline. I know how it hunts. It does not ride. It does not march. It *remembers*, and everything that remembers me is a road to my door. The wolves on the ridge last night — did you see how they sat? Not hunting. *Waiting.* Listening for the beat of me."

Cyrus Thackeray sat by the fire at the cave mouth, carving. He had carved for hours without stopping — small things, tokens, the bone chips falling like snow — and Aldric had understood at last that it was not idleness. The carver was listening. Through his hands, through the salt and bone, he listened to the dead, and the dead were the only sentries they had left.

Now the carver looked up. "The wolves are gone," he said. "The dead say the ridge is empty. They also say the ridge won't stay empty. Something is walking up the valley from the south, and it isn't walking like a man."

"There," Aldric said, almost gently. "You see. It's found the road through my blood. It will always find the road." He pushed himself up against the stone, and the cave tilted, and he let it, because he had stopped fighting the tilting. "So we end the arithmetic here. You leave me. That is the sum of it. Leave me and go east, go to the free cities, go anywhere the map still admits exists. I will walk north and let it take me back, and while it wears me it will not be looking for you."

Ottoline Vane went very still. He had learned, in eleven years of her service, that her stillness was more dangerous than her motion.

"No," she said.

"You swore an oath to the dynasty, not to me. The dynasty is a boy's ghost and a drowned chapel. The oath is discharged."

"I did not swear to the dynasty." Her voice was low, and it did not shake, though he could hear the cost of that in it, the way one hears a bridge bearing weight. "I swore to the *crown*, and the crown is a parasite, and so I have spent three years learning what my oath actually was. It was to the kingdom. To the people under the wards. To the dead who trusted the crown to speak for them." She knelt, so that her eyes were level with his, and he saw that she had been crying, recently, and had not bothered to hide it. "You want me to leave you because it is the honorable arithmetic. I have done that arithmetic every night since Thornfell fell, Aldric Thorne, and every night it comes out the same: a dead king in the Regent's hands is a weapon. A living king in the mountains is a hope. I do not abandon hopes. I have nothing else."

"You have a sword and a kingdom's grief."

"I have a sword and a kingdom's grief," she agreed, "and you, and a carver, and a road. That is more than the dynasty had at the drowned chapel, and we held there for a hundred years."

He turned his face away. The fever was rising again; he could feel it coming up his spine like cold water filling a well. In the dark behind his eyes, the faces were already gathering, patient, polite, the court of the drowned. *Leave us,* they whispered. *Leave them. You were always good at leaving. You left the wards to the ministers. You left the border to the governors. You left your brother's funeral to a proxy. Leaving is the one thing you were ever excellent at, Aldric, and here is your last and finest departure—*

"No," he said aloud, and did not know if he meant it to them or to Ottoline.

* * *

He slept, or something near it, and woke to the sound of a knife on bone and the smell of woodsmoke, and for a moment — a merciful moment — he was a boy again in the summer palace, falling asleep to his father's stewards banking the fires, the whole kingdom a warm and distant hum beneath him.

Then he was forty-one years old in a cave, and the hum was the fever, and the kingdom was salt.

Cyrus was crouched by the fire, turning a finished piece in his fingers. It was small — a king, Aldric realized. A tiny king, no longer than a thumb, carved from the grey bone the carver carried in a roll of oilcloth. The figure was kneeling. Its face was smooth, unfinished, waiting.

"You've been listening to me," Aldric said. "Through the bone. Through her."

"Maren says fever-talk is the only honest talk left in the world." Cyrus did not look up. "She says kings do their confessing in their sleep, and liars do it awake."

"Your sister is wiser than my entire council."

"Your sister is a salt block," Cyrus said, without bitterness, and then, with a flicker of something that might once have been humor: "She'd like that, actually. She always said she'd outlast the family."

Aldric laughed — a real laugh, rusty, surprising them both — and then the laugh broke apart into coughing, and the coughing into the tide again, and when it receded Ottoline was sitting beside him with her sword across her knees, sharpening it, though it did not need sharpening. It was what she did instead of weeping. He had watched her do it through the whole of the retreat, through the pass, through the chapel, through the bone orchard where the Regent had hung the silenced dead like fruit and she had walked the rows and read their faces one by one, committing them, he understood now, to a court she intended to convene someday, outside any law but her own.

"Ser Ottoline." He waited until she looked at him. "I am going to say a thing, and I need you to let me finish it, because I have rehearsed it for two days and I will lose my courage if you interrupt."

She set down the whetstone.

"The crown can find me through my blood. That is true, and it is getting truer. Every day the fever climbs, the road it walks to reach me gets shorter. In a week, perhaps two, it will not need wolves or heralds or armies. It will simply *arrive*, the way a thought arrives, and I will stand up out of this fever wearing its face and its purpose, and I will kill you both with your own kindness." He held her gaze. "So. Leave me. Take the east road. I will walk north, and I will walk slowly, and I will buy you every hour my body is worth. That is the last command of Aldric Thorne, king of nothing, and I am asking you — I am *begging* you — to obey it, because it is the first command I have ever given that costs me nothing but everything, and I would like, just once, to be obeyed."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above, water dripped from the cave roof into a pool, keeping a slow time, like a courtier counting the seconds of an uncomfortable silence.

"No," Ottoline said again. But she said it differently this time. Slower. He heard the arithmetic turning over in it, the honorable sums refusing to balance, and he thought: *she is going to cry, and then she is going to agree with me, and it will destroy her, and I will have done the one kingly thing left to me, which is to be a burden well.*

It was the carver who spoke.

"There's a third road," Cyrus Thackeray said.

They both turned. He was still turning the little kneeling king in his fingers, and the firelight moved on the bone like something swimming under ice.

"You've been telling us to hide you," Cyrus said. "Ottoline's been telling us to carry you. Both of you are playing the same game — keep the king away from the crown. Distance, darkness, silence. And the crown wins that game every time, because it doesn't have to find you. It only has to wait. You'll get sicker. You'll get slower. Eventually the mountain itself won't be far enough, because the mountain remembers you too — you told us. Everything that remembers you is a road." He set the little king down on a flat stone, facing the fire. "So stop running on the roads it owns."

"Name the other road," Ottoline said quietly.

"Go *toward* it." Cyrus looked up, and Aldric saw that the man was afraid — properly, humanly afraid, the fear sitting in him like a swallowed stone — and was speaking anyway, and understood that this was simply what Cyrus Thackeray was: a man who did the frightening thing with his hands shaking. "The crown isn't the Regent. We keep saying it wrong. The Regent is just the thing the crown wears *now* — the way it wore you before. The crown is the root. And the root is on the Salt Throne, in the throne hall, in the capital, in the one place in the world where every binding-sigil it uses is carved into the stone it sits on. I'm a bone-carver. I've spent my life reading sigils in ivory and horn and the long bones of the old kings. The crown's bindings aren't magic, Your Majesty. They're *craft*. Someone cut them. Someone cut them into a band of metal and a man's skull, and anything that is cut can be — "

He stopped. His hand had gone, without his noticing, to the roll of oilcloth, to the two long knives he carried there, the carving knives, older than the rest of his kit, worn to the shape of his grip.

"— uncut," Aldric finished.

The word went through the cave like the drip of water through limestone: slow, and then all at once.

"That is heresy," Ottoline said. "That is worse than heresy. That is — " She groped for the word and could not find it, which Aldric had never once seen happen to her. "You are proposing that we walk the king into the capital. Into the Regent's hall. Past the armies we watched massing on the ridge. To perform surgery on a crown with a *carving knife*."

"I'm proposing we stop paying interest on a debt we can never afford," Cyrus said. "Every day we run, the crown gets stronger and he gets weaker. You said it yourself in the chapel, Ser Ottoline — the dead vouched for him because the dead know a stolen voice when they hear one. The crown stole his. The wards didn't fall; Maren says they were eaten. Everything wrong with the world right now is downstream of that one object on that one throne." He picked up the little kneeling king again, and his thumb moved over the smooth unfinished face. "I don't know if I can cut it. I'll be honest with you — I don't. The bindings on the inner band will be the finest work I've ever read, cut by hands that had centuries to practice. But I know this: no one has ever tried. And a thing that has never been tried has never been defended against."

Silence. The fire settled. Aldric became aware that his heart was beating — really beating, hard, the way it had not beaten since the night the stars went out — and that the fever had gone strangely quiet, as if the crown too had stopped to listen.

"It will kill him," Ottoline said. "If it fails, it will kill him, and us, and it will have cost the free cities their last hope."

"If we run, it kills him anyway," Cyrus said. "Slower. With interest."

"Then it should be his choice." Ottoline turned to Aldric, and her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely steady. "I will not refuse you twice, my king. I refused you once tonight and I will answer for it. But this one is yours."

Aldric looked at the little carved king kneeling by the fire. Its face was blank. Waiting for a confession to carve.

He thought of eleven years of mornings, waking with the crown already whispering, telling him which ministers to trust, which petitioners to dismiss, which border towns were worth the cost of defending. He thought of his brother's funeral, which he had sent a proxy to because the crown said the mourning would make him weak. He thought of the wards, and the ministers he had let thin them, and the night the stars went out, when he had felt the crown *drink* — a long, deep, satisfied swallow, like a man at a well after a drought — and he had lain in his bed and told himself it was only the wind.

He had spent eleven years being worn. He knew, now, the exact shape of the hollow in him, the way a glove knows the shape of the hand it has lost.

"I am so tired of being carried," he said.

Ottoline flinched.

"Not by you." He reached out and took her hand — the sword-calloused, oath-sworn, unyielding hand — and held it, and it was the first time in eleven years he had touched anyone without the crown's permission, and his own hand shook, and he let it shake. "You have carried me like a relic, Ser Ottoline, and I have let you, because relics don't have to answer for anything. But a relic is exactly what the crown made of me. A hollow thing, venerated and empty. And I am done." He looked at Cyrus. "Carver. You said the bindings are craft. Can you read them? Truly? Because if you cut wrong, you will not kill me — the crown will see to that. You will only *open* me. You will make of me a door, and it will come through me into the world with nothing left to slow it."

Cyrus was quiet for a long moment. The firelight moved on his face, and Aldric watched him not look away, and understood that the carver was paying something for this answer — some private coin, some piece of his own peace.

"I read the wards' death-marks in the sugar-glass at Grayhollow," Cyrus said at last. "Work that old. Work that hungry. And I read the binding on Maren's salt — the sigil that holds a voice past death. The crown's bindings are the same family. Same hand, or hands trained by it." He turned the little king over, and Aldric saw that the smooth face had changed — that while they had talked, without looking, without seeming to move, the carver had cut into it. Two lines. The beginning of a mouth.

"I can read them," Cyrus said. "It will take everything I know and everything I have left to learn. And I will need you lucid, Your Majesty. Not feverish. Not hollowed. *Lucid* — because Ottoline told me what the dead in the chapel walls said, and Maren says it too, and I believe it now: the crown cannot be cut by steel alone. It was bound with a king's consent, and it can only be unbound with a king's truth. Your confession is the cutting word. Every compromise. Every permission. Everything you let it in with, named aloud, in the hall where you let it happen." His eyes were very steady. "Can you do that? Not for me. For the dead in the orchard. For the stars. Can you stand in that hall and say all of it?"

Aldric looked into the fire, and the fire looked back, and for once there were no faces in it but his own.

"I have been rehearsing my confession for three years," he said. "I simply thought I would be delivering it to a headsman." He drew a breath, and the breath went all the way down, past the fever, past the hollow, into whatever small unspoiled thing still lived at the bottom of him. "Yes. I can do that. I will do it if it kills me, because it should kill me, and because it is the only thing left that I can give."

"Then it's decided," Ottoline said, and stood, and it was like watching a fortress decide to march. "The Salt Throne. The capital. The one road in that no army watches."

Cyrus was already rolling his knives back into the oilcloth, already moving, and it occurred to Aldric, watching him, that the man had known — had known since the wolves, since the ridge, since some night Aldric had slept through — that it would come to this. That the running had only ever been the long way round to the same door.

"Carver," Aldric said. "The little king. Will you finish his face?"

Cyrus paused. He looked at the figure, and then at Aldric, and something passed between them that had no oath in it at all — no crown, no dynasty, no kingdom. Only two men and a fire and an insane plan, held together with craft and confession and the fading voice of a dead woman in a block of salt.

"When it's done," Cyrus said. "When the crown is off you and the stars are back. Then I'll carve the face." He closed his hand around the little king. "I don't carve dead men, Your Majesty. I carve the dead *remembered*. So you'll have to live."

Outside the cave, far to the south, something in the dark stopped walking — and stood, and listened, and began, very slowly, to smile.

They did not hear it. But the dead did, and the dead, for the first time in three years, were afraid for someone other than themselves.
