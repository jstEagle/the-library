## 16. The Last Road In

There was a road beneath Grayhollow once that no cart had ever rolled down and no surveyor's chain had ever measured. It had no name because names were how armies found things. The old smugglers called it the Underway, or the Backward Path, or nothing at all — they only tapped their noses twice and said *down and east*, the way you'd speak of a dead parent you still loved. Cyrus's grandfather had walked it eleven times and come back from ten. The eleventh time he hadn't come back, and the map he'd kept of it — scratched in tallow and charcoal on a stretched hide of something Cyrus preferred not to identify — had passed to Maren, and then, when Maren passed, to him.

He unrolled it now on the flat top of a boulder above the tree line, weighted at the corners with four stones, and let Ottoline and Aldric look upon the only honest map in the world, which was to say a map of a road that did not exist.

"That's it?" Ottoline said. "That's the capital?" She pointed at the small blank space near the hide's eastern edge where the ink simply stopped, as if the cartographer had fallen asleep, or died, or looked at what waited beyond that line and decided against recording it.

"That's where the map gives up," Cyrus said. "The road doesn't. Maps give up. Roads don't. That's the difference between them." He traced the route with a cracked thumbnail: under the Shoulder of the World through a fissure called the Needle's Eye; along the drowned gallery of an old silver mine where the beams had been standing for two hundred years out of pure stubbornness; down through the weeping limestone where the mountain sweated in all seasons; and out at last, if the stories were true and his grandfather's ghost could be believed, into the root cellars of a tanner's yard half a mile inside the capital walls, behind a door so old and so ordinary that no soldier in the Regent's army had ever glanced at it twice.

No army watched this road. No army *could* watch it. You cannot garrison a place you have never admitted exists, and the kingdom's maps — the king's maps, the war-college maps, the beautiful illuminated things that hung in council halls — showed the mountain as solid to its core. The Underway survived precisely because everyone with the power to record it had agreed, over three hundred years, to lie.

"Two days' walk," Cyrus said. "Three, if the king—" He stopped. Aldric was sitting on his heels at the edge of the firelight, wrapped in Ottoline's spare cloak, staring at the blank eastern edge of the hide with an expression Cyrus had seen before on the faces of men looking into open graves.

"Three is fine," Aldric said quietly.

* * *

They went down into the Needle's Eye at dawn, because Cyrus wanted daylight behind them when they dropped below the sky. The fissure was exactly as narrow as its name promised. Ottoline went first with her sword slung across her back, turning sideways, exhaling to make herself thinner, her armor shrieking softly against the stone like something being skinned. Aldric followed. Cyrus came last, and just before the mountain swallowed him he turned and looked back one final time — at the white peaks, at the thin blue morning, at the whole enormous honest face of the world.

Then there was only the dark, and the sound of three people breathing, and the salt block knocking gently against his hip where it rode in its sling against his ribs.

"You're doing the thing again," Maren said.

Cyrus nearly dropped his lantern. Her voice came fainter every day — that was the arithmetic of it, the price of passage paid twice already, first at Vessamy and then at the blockaded coast, each transaction carved out of him with his own knife while the Guild's bone-surgeon held the ledger. What remained of her lived in the last finger-width of salt, and she sounded now like someone speaking to him from across a wide river in fog.

"I'm not doing anything," he whispered.

"You're listening for me instead of walking. You've done it since the second turn. Watch your left boot; the stone's rotten there."

He tested the stone. It was rotten. He stepped past it and felt something in his chest fold and refold, grief and gratitude laid over each other so tightly he could no longer have said which was which, or whether they had ever been two things at all.

* * *

The drowned gallery of the Vessel Mine took them the better part of the first day. It was a cathedral down there, though no god had ever been invited: pillars of black water standing perfectly still on either side of a causeway barely wider than a coffin, the mine's ancient timbering reflected upside-down in the flood so that they walked through a forest of inverted columns. Their lanterns made small trembling suns of themselves in the water. Somewhere far off, water dripped with the patience of geology, counting seconds no one would ever spend.

Ottoline kept her hand on her sword hilt the entire way, which Cyrus found both absurd and deeply comforting. There was nothing down there to fight but the dark, and the dark always won, but he understood her. Faith needs somewhere to put its hands.

"The men I buried," she said abruptly, halfway across. She didn't finish it. She didn't need to. In the chapel, months ago, the dead in the walls had spoken for Cyrus — the first proof Ottoline Vane had heard in years that her faith had not been spent on empty air. But here, over the black water, the walls were silent. The dead were silent everywhere now, everywhere but the salt against Cyrus's ribs, and every step deeper into the mountain was a step further into a world gone quiet.

"They're still there," Cyrus said. "Silenced isn't erased. When this ends—"

"*If.*"

"When," he said, with more certainty than he owned. "When this ends, everything silenced comes back. Every voice. All at once." He heard himself say it and realized, with a lurch, that he had been avoiding thinking the sentence whole. *Every voice.* Except the one he would have traded every other for.

Aldric stumbled, and Ottoline caught his arm, and the moment folded itself away unspoken.

They made camp in a dry side-gallery where the air moved, which meant somewhere ahead there was an opening, which meant the mountain was not a tomb but a throat. Cyrus laid out the hard bread and the smoked fish and the little iron kettle, and did the small domestic work of survival, and felt — absurdly, in the belly of a nameless road beneath a starless kingdom — almost peaceful. This was the oldest part of his craft, older than carving: keep people alive in ugly places. Feed them. Count the hours of a watch so each of them slept. He had been doing it his whole life for cargo. Now the cargo was a broken king and the last knight of a drowned dynasty, and the work was the same work.

Aldric ate maybe a third of his portion and gave the rest back to the mountain's silence. Then he lay looking at the ceiling of the world, and spoke without being asked, the way men do when the dark makes confession cheap.

"My father walked this road once," he said. "Did you know that? Before he was king. He told me about it when I was a boy — told me there was a way into any city in the kingdom that no crown could close, and he laughed when he said it, and I thought it was a story about roads." His voice caught on something. "It was a lesson. Everything the crown fears, it should buy or bury. I forgot the lesson. Or I thought being crowned was the same as being right."

Cyrus fed a stick to the small fire and said nothing, because there was nothing yet that needed saying.

"The crown didn't take me by force," Aldric said. "I want that understood, before whatever happens at the end of this road. It *asked*. It was very polite. It offered me my father's voice again — the dead were already going quiet, and it knew how afraid I was, and it offered." He turned his head and looked at Cyrus across the fire, and his eyes in the low light were terrible and young. "You've been paying pieces of yourself to carry me. I know. Ottoline told me. And I keep thinking: he pays with his sister, and I wouldn't even pay with my grief."

"You were afraid," Cyrus said.

"So are you."

"Yes," Cyrus said. "But nobody offered me my sister back. If they had—" He stopped. Looked at his hands, which had carved a hundred voices out of bone and knew exactly what they were worth. "I'd like to believe I'd have refused. I won't swear to it. That's the honest version, Majesty. Take it or leave it."

For the first time in all the miles since the pass, Aldric Thorne smiled. It was a small, wrecked, genuine thing.

"Aldric," he said. "On this road, no crowns. Just Aldric."

* * *

The weeping limestone began at midday of the second day, and it was there that the road earned its reputation.

The passage opened into a chamber where the mountain itself seemed alive with water — the walls slicked and shining, threads of moisture catching their lantern light like the rigging of a ghost ship, and everywhere, from every surface, the slow mineral tears of centuries sliding down toward roots they would never reach. Stalactites hung thick as the columns of a throne hall. Cyrus's grandfather's map marked this chamber with a single line of tallow-script: *the mountain dreams here; do not wake it.*

They woke nothing. They walked soft. And it was here, in that dripping hush, that Maren guided them through the turns that no map recorded — because the last third of the Underway had never been mapped at all, not even in tallow. It lived only in the memories of smugglers, and smugglers die, and dead smugglers are silent like everyone else now.

Except her.

"Left at the split stone," she said, and Cyrus pressed his palm to the salt block and repeated it aloud, and they went left. "Down the chimney, thirty feet, don't touch the white flowstone, it's lying about being solid." Down the chimney, thirty feet, past flowstone that looked like poured cream and crumbled like cake. "Now listen. Hear the water going different directions?"

Cyrus stopped. Listened. Beneath the general weeping of the chamber there was indeed a division — a thin thread of sound running left where all the rest ran right, water choosing a different future.

"Follow the wrong water," Maren said. "Always follow the wrong water. Right water goes where mountains send it. Wrong water goes where smugglers dug."

They followed the wrong water for an hour, through crawls that scraped the breath out of them and a final slot where Ottoline had to unbuckle her sword belt and pass it ahead, and then the slot spat them out into darkness that moved — real air, cold and enormous, smelling of woodsmoke and horse and the river-tang of a great city. They had come out under the roots of the capital itself. Through a seam in the rock, far below and to the east, Cyrus could see a smear of orange light against a wall: lamplight, cookfires, the ordinary glowing life of forty thousand people who did not know that a dead man and a carver and a knight were hanging above them in the dark like a held breath.

"We're here," he said, and his voice cracked on the size of it. "We're actually here."

"Not quite," Maren said.

And Cyrus heard it — heard what was underneath her words, the way you hear the hole beneath thin ice. Not hesitation. Depletion. Each word cost her now. He had known it intellectually, the way you know a wound is deep; he had not let himself *hear* it until this moment, crouched at the threshold of the end of everything, with his sister's remaining existence weighing less against his ribs than the bread in his pack.

"How much?" he whispered. "How much is left?"

"Cyrus—"

"Maren. How much?"

A pause. Water ticked somewhere in the dark, counting.

"Enough for the last turns," she said. "That's what I saved it for. You stubborn, self-sacrificing idiot — did you think I wouldn't notice what you were doing? Spending me by inches so you wouldn't have to spend yourself whole? I have enough to bring you to the door." Her voice frayed, and steadied. "Listen carefully, because I'm only saying the sequence once, and after this I'm going to be very quiet, and I need you not to panic when I do."

He listened. He wrote it into himself the way he wrote sigils into bone — stroke by stroke, exact, permanent. Left hand on the wall. Forty paces. The shelf where the rock steps down. The drop, six feet, onto sand. Then the old drain under the tannery, and the door with the iron ring, and beyond the door the city, and beyond the city the Salt Throne, and beyond the throne — he would not think past the throne.

"One more thing," Maren said, and now her voice was very small, a candle guttering in a doorway. "When it's done — when the stars come back — you'll want to hear me. Everyone will hear everyone. It'll be loud, Cy. The whole world talking at once. And I need you to promise me you'll listen to all of it. Don't stand in some quiet corner straining for one voice out of the millions. Listen to *everything*. Promise me."

"I can't promise that."

"You can. You're a terrible liar and an excellent brother, and those turned out to be the same skill. Say it."

The words stuck in his throat like the chalky crawlstone. He swallowed them twice before they went down.

"I'll listen to everything."

"Good." A breath that was not a breath, only the salt settling in its sling. "Last turns now. Hand on the wall. Count with me."

Forty paces. He counted them aloud, and she counted with him, her voice growing thinner with every number, and he matched his stride to hers as if slowing down could stretch her out, as if pacing were a form of mercy. Thirty. Twenty-five. At twenty she was a whisper. At fifteen she was less than a whisper, a shape the whisper left behind. At ten—

At ten she said, "I love you, Cy. I always did. Even when you wouldn't listen. *Especially* then."

And at the shelf where the rock stepped down, as his boot found the edge of the six-foot drop onto sand, she went quiet.

Not faded. Not trailing off. Quiet — complete, absolute, the quiet of a finished sentence. Cyrus stood at the threshold of the last turn with his hand still raised in the shape of her guidance and listened to the loudest silence of his life, and Ottoline, behind him in the dark, put a gauntleted hand briefly on his shoulder and said nothing, because there was nothing in the world to say.

Grief, he thought. Gratitude. He had assumed he would feel them separately, in sequence, and had braced accordingly. Instead they arrived together, indistinguishable, one weight, one warmth, the way salt and the sea are indistinguishable in the mouth of a drowning man — and he understood, finally, that they had been the same substance all along, and that this was what it meant to have loved someone completely: that losing her and having had her were, at the end, one act, one taste, one long falling.

He wiped his face with the back of his wrist. Took out the salt block. It was lighter than a sparrow now, translucent as sea-ice, and utterly still.

"Thank you," he said anyway, to the quiet, because some debts get paid in words regardless.

Then he dropped the six feet onto the sand, drew his knives, and led them down toward the door with the iron ring — and the tannery, and the sleeping city, and the Salt Throne where a drowned king's crown sat wearing the world.

Behind the iron ring, when Cyrus pulled it, something on the other side was already waiting. He heard it before the door opened: not footsteps, not steel — a voice, dry as sifted ash, speaking through the keyhole in perfect, patient courtesy.

"Master Thackeray," it said. "We've been expecting you. Come in. Bring the king. We have such a gift prepared."
