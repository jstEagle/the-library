## 17. The Salt Throne

The last road ended where all roads end, and Cyrus Thackeray walked into the throne hall of Thornfell with salt dust on his boots and his sister's silence in his ears.

The doors had not been guarded. That was the first wrongness, and it sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. Maren's voice had guided them through nine turnings of the smugglers' road — *left at the leaning stone, down where the roots drink the river, mind the third step, it lies* — and then, at the threshold beneath the citadel, she had gone quiet the way a candle goes quiet: not blown out, but finished. He had stood in the dark with his hand on her salt block for a long moment, feeling the faint warmth of her fading through the grain, and Ottoline had said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the road had not already said.

Now the doors stood open onto a hall that remembered a thousand coronations and had forgotten all of them.

Candles burned — hundreds, thousands, in tiers along the walls, in great iron crowns of flame suspended from the vaulting — and yet the light did not seem to land anywhere. It fell on the pillars and slid off. It fell on the banners of the old dynasty, the stag and the six stars, and the thread did not catch it. The whole hall had the quality of a thing seen through water. Cyrus had carved memorials from whalebone and from the femurs of saints, and he knew this light: it was the light inside a thing that was being consumed slowly, from within, and had not yet admitted it.

The Salt Throne stood at the far end on its dais of black stone, and upon it sat the Regent.

It wore Aldric's face.

That was the second wrongness, and it was worse than the first, because Cyrus knew Aldric's face now the way he knew the grain of his own carving wood — the broken-then-mended line of the nose, the gray in the beard that came in on the left side first, the deep furrow between the brows that was not anger but a lifetime of listening to bad news and trying to be worthy of it. All of that was there, seated on the throne, hands resting on the armrests with a king's easy possession. But the face was too smooth. It was Aldric's face the way a death-mask is a face: correct in every line, empty of every hour that had made the lines. The eyes were the color of the sea over a drowned chapel.

And the crown — the crown was *moving*.

It sat on the Regent's brow like a thing at rest that was not resting, and as Cyrus watched, the metal of it breathed. Not gleamed. Breathed. The inner band, the part no one was ever meant to see, pulsed with a faint gray light, and with each pulse the Regent's face shifted — a fraction, a hair's width, the mouth settling into a different shape, the brow rearranging its grief — and Cyrus understood, with the cold clarity of his craft, what he had been carrying across the mountains. The crown was not worn. The crown was *carving*. It had been carving Aldric Thorne for years, the way Cyrus carved bone, removing everything that was not the shape it wanted, and now it sat on a face it had nearly finished, wearing the king the way the king had once worn it.

*You are a craftsman*, Maren had said to him once, when he was young and she was already dead and he had not yet learned to hear her. *So act like one. When the work is terrible, the hands must be calm.*

His hands were calm. He was almost frightened by how calm they were.

* * *

Ottoline Vane moved first, because Ottoline Vane always moved first.

"The hall," she said, low, her sword already clearing its scabbard with a sound like a sentence being finished. "Cyrus. The hall."

He had seen it too. The candles were not the only things in the tiers along the walls. Between them, in the shadowed alcoves where the old kings kept their ancestors' effigies, the dead were standing.

They had come with the herald's voice still in them — the herald they had left behind in Vessamy, the herald that wore the faces of honored dead, Maren's face among them, smiling with her smile. They had walked. Cyrus could see it in the way they stood, in the salt crust on their clothing, in the particular stiffness of bodies that had been walking for a long time and would walk until they were told to stop. Dozens of them. A hundred. Courtiers and soldiers and a woman in a midwife's apron and a boy in a bell-ringer's coat, all of them with the sea-colored eyes, all of them silent, because the Regent had silenced them and silence was the leash.

"The king," the Regent said, and its voice was Aldric's voice played through a drowned throat. "You brought me my king. That was well done, carver. I had expected to chase you across half the map."

"You expected wrong," Ottoline said, and put herself between the throne and Cyrus, and the dead in the alcoves began to come down the steps.

What followed, Cyrus would remember always as work. Terrible work, but work — the kind he knew, the kind that had a rhythm and a grain and could be read.

Ottoline met the first of the dead at the foot of the dais, and her sword did not pass through them, because they were not ghosts; they were bodies kept walking by a will that was not theirs, and steel could stop a body. She fought the way she did everything — like a woman making up for lost years of faith with compound interest — falling back step by step, giving ground only in measured amounts, herding them, always herding them, into the narrow ground between the pillars where they could not come at him in numbers.

"The band," she called back, parrying a soldier whose face Cyrus half-recognized from a coin. "Whatever you're going to do to it — do it. I'll hold the hall. I held a worse hall than this at Cael Ford, and I was younger and stupider then."

"You're still stupid," Cyrus said, and heard, absurdly, her laugh over the ring of steel.

He went up the steps of the dais alone.

* * *

The Regent did not rise. That was the third wrongness, and by now Cyrus had stopped expecting the world to be right. It sat and watched him come with Aldric's borrowed face, and when he was ten steps away it said, in that drowned voice:

"Do you know what I am, carver?"

"I know what you're *made* of," Cyrus said. "I've been reading your work since Grayhollow." He set Maren's salt block down on the dais, gently, the way he set down every piece he was not currently cutting. "You eat. Kings, wards, voices, names. You ate the stars because they were the oldest witnesses and you couldn't abide being seen. And you're eating him right now —" he nodded at the face — "and when you finish him you'll wear him into the free cities and eat them too. I'm not here to know you. I'm here to unmake you."

"And how," the Regent said, and now the mouth curved, and it was Aldric's mouth making a smile Aldric had never once made, "does a bone-carver propose to unmake a thing older than his country?"

Cyrus knelt, and took out his knives.

He had seven. He had carried them since he was fourteen years old, since before Maren died, since the first memorial he ever cut — a sparrow, for a girl who cried over it, and the strange peace that had come into his hands when he understood that he could give the dead a shape that let the living let go. Six were working knives, worn to the particular curves that only his grip had taught them. The seventh was older than the rest, older than his trade, a blade of gray metal with a salt-white edge that Maren had pressed into his hands the last night of her life, saying words he had refused to hear, and which he had carried for twenty years without once using it for anything but the cutting of names.

He chose the seventh.

"The crown is a binding," he said, working as he talked, because his hands thought better when his mouth was busy. "I felt it on him in the pass, when I cut the fever-sigils out of his arm. It's not one spell. It's hundreds. Layered. Sigil over sigil over sigil, like lacquer, like scar tissue — each one a promise the crown made to something, or something made to the crown. That's how it holds him. That's how it holds *itself* together." He looked up. "You're not a thing, Regent. You're a *knot*. And knots can be cut."

The Regent rose.

It came off the throne the way water comes off a cliff — no haste, no effort, simply a change of level — and Cyrus felt the cold of it arrive before the body did, a cold that was not temperature but *subtraction*, the cold of a room where every voice has been taken away. The dead in the hall faltered. Even Ottoline, holding the stair against six of them at once, staggered as though the floor had tilted.

"You will not touch the crown," the Regent said, and it was no longer entirely Aldric's voice. Something older was using the shape of it, the way a hand uses a glove. "I have worn nine kings. I have eaten the wards that were raised before your people had names. I unmade the stars, carver. I reached up and I *drank them*. What is your knife to that?"

Cyrus did not answer. His hands were calm.

He caught the Regent's wrist — the flesh cold and slightly yielding, like meat kept too long in brine — and turned it, and drove the gray blade not into the body but up under the crown's edge, into the gap between metal and brow, where the first sigil lay against the king's skin like a leech.

The hall screamed.

Not the Regent. The *hall*. Every dead thing in it stopped at once and opened its mouth, and the sound that came out was the sound Cyrus had heard on the night the stars went out — the sound of a whisper cut off mid-word, multiplied by a hundred, by a thousand, by every silencing the Regent had ever done, all of it pouring out now through one opened sigil like water through a breached dam. The candles guttered. The banners snapped though there was no wind. Ottoline, on the stairs, roared something wordless and fought harder, and the dead she fought faltered — because the sound was *theirs*, was their stolen voices tearing loose, and some animal part of them remembered that they had been robbed.

Cyrus cut.

It was the finest work of his life and he knew it even as he did it, the way a man drowning knows the color of the water. The sigils of the inner band were laid in courses, like masonry, like the rings of a tree, and each one had a grain and a lie at its heart. The first was a promise of obedience, and he split it along the line where the promise met the fear that sealed it. The second was a theft — a name, a king's true name, taken and held — and he cut it free and felt it go like a bird off his knife. The third fought him. The third was old, older than the dynasty, and it had the taste of the thing that had first found the crown in whatever dark it had been made in, and it did not want to be cut, and for a moment — one long, salt-white moment — it pushed back up his blade and into his hand and showed him what it showed everyone it wore: the quiet. The perfect quiet of a world with no dead in it, no voices, no witnesses, no one behind your eyes saying *you could have been better* — and it was restful, it was so restful, and Cyrus understood in that moment how nine kings had sat still for it.

Then he felt Maren's salt block against his knee, faintly warm, and he thought: *she never once asked me to be quiet. Not even dead. Not even to spare herself.*

He cut the third sigil in half.

* * *

He lost count after that. The hall was a storm of stolen voices and ringing steel and the Regent's cold pouring off it in waves, and Ottoline was somewhere behind him holding a line that should not have held, and Cyrus knelt on the dais of the Salt Throne with his knife in the crown's inner band and did the only thing he had ever truly known how to do: he read the grain, and he cut with it.

Sigil after sigil. A binding of silence. A binding of hunger. A binding that had been Aldric's own — his oath at his coronation, taken up and turned inside out, so that the words that should have bound him to his people bound the crown to him instead. That one Cyrus did not cut. That one he left, because it was the king's, and because he was beginning to understand, with the terrible intuition of his craft, what this work would finally require.

The crown was cracking. He could feel it — hairline fissures running through the gray metal, weeping salt. But it did not break. With each sigil he severed, the Regent screamed and the fissures spread, and then the thing would gather itself, and the fissures would *close*, knitting like flesh, and Cyrus understood with a sinking craftsman's certainty that he was cutting a knot with half a blade. Steel could open it. Steel could not finish it.

There was a word missing. He had known it since the pass, in the place where he kept the knowledge he did not want. A crown like this was not a lock with one key. It was a lock with one *word*, and the word was not a word at all. It was a truth, spoken by the only mouth it belonged to, at the moment of cutting. A confession. A king's true confession, given freely, naming everything — and the crown could not survive being known, because it had spent nine reigns making sure it was never known.

The Regent knew it too. He could see it in the ruined, shifting, borrowed face — the sea-colored eyes fixed on him now not with contempt but with something faster, something that in a living face would have been fear.

"Stop," it said. "Carver. *Stop.*"

Cyrus kept cutting.

It seized him then — the whole cold weight of it, nine kings of stolen years — and hurled him across the dais, and his knife rang away across the black stone, and through the roaring of the hall he heard the thing speak with all of Aldric's drowned voice at once:

"I will wear him *empty* and I will wear *you* next—"

And then the hall went quiet — not silenced, but *held* — because Ottoline Vane had climbed the last of the stairs bleeding from a dozen cuts, and put her back against the dais, and raised her sword at everything the Regent had left, and said, in the voice of a woman who had waited twenty years for this exact moment and would wait twenty more:

"Come up and be judged."

Cyrus crawled for his knife. His hands were still calm. They had never once, in all of it, been anything but calm, and he understood at last that this was Maren's true inheritance to him — not the salt block, not the voice, but this: the craftsman's hands inside the catastrophe, steady, because the work was not finished.

The crown sat on the dais where the Regent's struggle had thrown it, cracked and knitting, cracked and knitting. It needed a word. It needed the one mouth in the world that could speak it.

And from the shadows at the hall's far end, where the open doors let in the first gray light of a starless dawn, came the sound of footsteps — uneven, wounded, unmistakable — and the voice of Aldric Thorne, king of Thornfell, saying:

"Then let it hear me."

Cyrus turned, knife in hand, and looked at the king who had followed them down the last road after all — and the crown began, very softly, to sing.
