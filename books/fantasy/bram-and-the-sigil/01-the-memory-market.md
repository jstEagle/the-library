## 1. The Memory Market

The lanterns of Corvane's night market burned the color of old honey, and Freya Kavanagh had learned to read them the way sailors read the sky.

A blue flame meant the lamplighter had watered his oil again to stretch it — a cheat, but a forgivable one, the sort of small dishonesty that kept the poor from going dark. A green flame meant a Warden patrol had passed within the hour and someone was burning warding-salts to blur the memory of their faces. And a flame that guttered sideways with no wind at all meant trouble, the kind that walked on two legs and carried a ledger.

Tonight, all the lanterns along Tallow Row burned steady and gold, which meant the market was safe, busy, and lying about its prices. Freya approved of all three.

"Charms!" she called, in the bright, easy voice that wasn't hers at all. "Charms for forgetting, charms for remembering! A first kiss, a mother's lullaby, the taste of a summer you never had! Two coppers, three if you want it to last past morning!"

An old woman with a face like a crumpled letter paused at her stall. "The lullaby," she said. "Is it a real one?"

"Every one's real, grandmother. Stolen fair and square."

"Whose mother?"

Freya smiled the smile that sold things. "Does it matter? It'll fit you like a coat."

The woman counted out three coppers, one of them bent, and Freya pressed the charm into her palm — a sliver of glass no bigger than a thumbnail, threaded with silver wire, warm as a held hand. The woman held it to her ear the way you'd hold a shell to the sea, and her face did something complicated and soft.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, I had a mother once."

"Everyone did. That's how the business works." Freya said it lightly, but the words sat wrong in her chest, the way they always did, and she covered it by turning to straighten her display tray. The tray held forty charms in neat rows, each one a stolen hour, a borrowed feeling, a life someone else had lived and paid her to take away.

That was the secret of her trade, the one the Wardens had never quite managed to catch: memory could be lifted. Not by force — memory fought back, all claws and screaming — but by *want*. If a person truly wanted to forget, truly, down to the marrow, then the memory came loose like a ripe fruit, and Freya could catch it, distill it, spin it into glass and silver. A first kiss for the widow who couldn't stop grieving it. A lullaby for the woman whose mother had died with the song still unsung. A bad year, bottled, for the man who'd drink anything to stop tasting it.

Small magic. Tiny magic. Magic so small that the Wardens, who policed the grand and the dangerous — the storm-callers, the gate-openers, the covenant-magics that had gotten everyone's grandparents burned — never thought to look at a girl selling trinkets in a night market.

Freya had built her whole life inside that blind spot. She intended to die there, comfortably, at a very old age, having never once been interesting.

* * *

The market thickened as the moon rose. Freya worked her clients in the rhythm she'd perfected over nine years: listen, price, deliver, forget them.

That last part was the trick nobody guessed. Every charm-maker in the old stories had gone mad from carrying other people's memories, and so Freya did the thing no honest text recommended and no dishonest one admitted to: she let the memories pass through her and out again, keeping only a sliver of each, a taste. Enough to know what she'd sold. Not enough to be haunted by it.

Mostly.

She kept a ledger in her head — not of names, never names, but of *shapes*. The baker on Candle Street who'd bought away the smell of smoke after the fire that took his shop, and had come back three times since, because grief, it turned out, had more rooms than one. The young clerk who sold her his memory of his father's funeral and then, a month later, quietly asked if she had anything that would give him back the feeling of being loved, and Freya had had to tell him no, she sold exits, not entrances, and watched him understand what he'd done.

The clerk was at the market tonight, she noticed. He stood at the far end of Tallow Row, not approaching, just watching her stall with the particular hunger of a man who'd sold something he couldn't afford to lose.

She gave him a small nod — *not tonight, but I see you* — and he nodded back and melted into the crowd, and Freya filed the moment away in the drawer of things she'd feel guilty about later, which was, admittedly, a very well-stocked drawer.

"Freya." A shadow detached itself from beside the pickled-eel cart. Maren, her fence, a broad woman with forearms like dock rope and the moral flexibility of a good rope as well. "Got a job for you. Good one."

"Every job you bring me is 'a good one,' Maren. The last 'good one' was a man who wanted me to steal the memory of his own wedding from *him*, and then his wife wanted me to steal it from *her*, and then they both came back separately wanting to know what the other one had paid."

"They reconciled, though."

"They reconciled because neither of them could remember what they'd been angry about. That's not a marriage, Maren, that's a hostage situation with flowers."

Maren grinned, unrepentant, and leaned in. "No, this one's clean. Rich one, up on Vellum Hill. Old money, the kind that's gone soft in the middle. Wants a forgetting. Big one. Whole decade, he says."

Freya's hands went still over her tray. "A whole decade isn't a charm. A whole decade is a *hole*. You can't take ten years out of a man and expect him to stand up straight."

"He knows. He says he doesn't care. Says the ten years aren't worth having." Maren shrugged. "His coin's good either way. He's paying half up front."

Half up front. On Vellum Hill, half up front was more than Freya made in a season. She looked at her tray of forty charms, at the bent copper the grandmother had paid, at the clerk-shaped absence at the end of the row, and did the arithmetic she always did: how long could she keep this up, how long before the Wardens' slow, patient ledgers caught up with her, how much was enough.

The answer, as always, was *a little more*.

"Tonight?" she asked.

"Tonight. He's expecting you after moonset. I'll walk you to the gate."

* * *

The house on Vellum Hill smelled of dust and money, which Freya had learned were the same smell. The rich man — Lord Aldery, though he'd asked her not to use the title, which meant he cared about it very much — received her in a study lined with books he clearly never read, and sat in a chair by a cold fireplace, and looked older than his years in the specific way of a man who has been rehearsing a decision for a long time.

"You're the charm-wright," he said. Not a question. His eyes had the flat shine of someone who'd already cried about this and was done with it.

"I'm a trinket-seller who happens to be very good at listening," Freya said. "What we do afterward is between you and your conscience."

"Ten years," he said. "From the winter my son was born to the spring he died. Take all of it. The birth, the christening, the fever, the — take the funeral most of all. I have sat in that pew so many times I can feel the wood through my trousers even now, in this chair, in summer." His hands were shaking. "I don't want the ten years. I want the *before* and the *after*, and I want to stop knowing there's a hole in the middle."

Freya had heard a hundred variations of this speech, and it never got easier, and she had made her peace with the fact that it shouldn't. "It will leave a scar," she said. "Not a wound — a scar. You'll know something happened. You'll feel the shape of it, like a missing tooth. Some people find that worse than the memory."

"Nothing," Lord Aldery said, "is worse than the memory."

She took his hands in hers, and she did the work.

The lifting was gentle, when it was wanted. She'd learned to make it gentle — a slow unwinding, like thread from a spool, like a knot untying itself because it finally could. Ten years is a lot of thread. She sat with the old man for the better part of an hour while his life with his son came away in long, luminous strands, and she caught them, and she coiled them, and she felt — as she always did — the sliver of it settle into her own keeping: a baby's fist closing around her thumb, the specific weight of a sleeping child on a shoulder, a small voice saying *again, again* about a story that had no ending.

*Again, again.*

Freya put the memory into the largest charm she'd ever woven — a disc of glass the size of a saucer, silver-wired at the rim — and sealed it, and handed it over.

Lord Aldery held it and wept, quietly, with relief, the way a man weeps when a tooth finally stops aching. Then he looked up with the smooth, unmarked face of a man who had no idea what he'd just paid for, and said, "Have we met?"

"No, my lord," Freya said. "I'm a trinket-seller. You bought a charm for your rheumatism. It's under the saucer there, if you'd like it."

"Kind of you." He paid her the rest, counted by a servant, and saw her to the door with the courtesy of a man greeting a stranger, and Freya walked down Vellum Hill with a purse heavy enough to hurt and a sliver of a dead boy's laugh lodged under her ribs where she kept all the things she'd never sell.

* * *

She got back to Tallow Row near moonset, and the market was winding down in its usual way — the eel-cart man hosing his cart with water he'd swear was fresh, two pickpockets conducting a professional disagreement behind the wool stall, the lamplighters going lamp to lamp with their long brass snuffers like men putting out a hundred small, patient eyes.

Freya was packing her tray when she noticed the last charm in the bottom row was wrong.

It was one she'd woven that afternoon, from a routine theft — a dockworker on Salt Quay who'd wanted to forget a bad night at cards, a two-copper job, nothing in it. She'd woven it without looking closely, the way you stop tasting your own cooking after nine years. But now, in the last of the lantern light, the glass sliver caught the flame and threw it back *wrong*. Not gold. Not the honey-color of Tallow Row.

White. A white with a depth to it, like the light you sometimes saw behind your eyelids, like the light in old paintings of the covenant days that the Wardens had scraped off every wall in the city.

Freya picked it up and turned it, and the light turned with it, and inside the glass — she leaned close, closer, until her nose nearly touched it — something *moved*. A flicker. A pulse. As if the memory were breathing.

That was impossible. Memories didn't breathe. Memories were thread and salt and the shape of a feeling; she'd handled ten thousand of them and none had ever so much as blinked. A dockworker's card game had no business glowing like a piece of the sky.

Unless it wasn't a card game. Unless the dockworker had seen something on Salt Quay that night, something he'd wanted so badly to forget that the wanting had stripped it clean, and what lay at the bottom of the memory, under the cards and the ale and the shouting, was —

The lantern beside her guttered. No wind. The flame bent sideways, all down Tallow Row, a hundred small patient eyes all looking at once at the sliver of glass in her hand.

And in the white light at the memory's center, Freya saw — just for a heartbeat, just long enough to doubt it — a shape. Tall. Standing very still. Watching her back through the glass, from the far side of a stolen night, with what she could have sworn, could have *sworn*, was recognition.

The flame righted itself. The market noise came back like a tide. The charm in her hand was just a charm again, a two-copper job, a dockworker's bad night at cards.

Except that it was warm. Except that it was still, faintly, impossibly, glowing.

Freya stood in the emptying market with her heart going like a drum, and did the thing she always did when the world offered her something interesting, which was to reach for the drawer where she kept her fear and find it — for the first time in nine years — locked.

She wrapped the charm in three layers of cloth, tucked it against her ribs where she kept all the things she'd never sell, and went home the long way, checking every shadow twice.

Behind her, all down Tallow Row, the lantern flames bent sideways again, one after another, like a hundred small eyes turning to watch her go.
