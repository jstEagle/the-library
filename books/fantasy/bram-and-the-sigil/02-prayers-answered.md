## 2. Prayers Answered

Freya Kavanagh woke to the smell of bread and the sound of a woman screaming.

Neither was unusual in the tenement on Cooper's Lane, though usually not at the same time, and usually the screaming came *after* the bread — old Marta Hallow had a temper that rose with her loaves like yeast. But this morning the screams were wrong. They weren't angry. They were joyful, which in Corvane was far more alarming.

Freya lay still under her thin blanket and listened.

"He spoke," Marta was sobbing, out in the stairwell now, her voice carrying through the whole building. "He *spoke*, do you hear me? Through the fire! Tam's voice, my Tam, my boy who's been dead six years — he said, 'Mum, the loaf's burning,' and I ran and it *was* burning, because I'd left it, I'd left it because—"

The rest dissolved into weeping. Doors opened up and down the stairwell. Freya heard the neighbors gather, heard the particular hush of people deciding together whether to believe something impossible.

She got up, dressed, and did what she always did when the world went strange: she made tea, counted her charms, and told herself firmly that whatever it was, it wasn't her problem.

It took until midmorning for the counting to fail her.

* * *

Her stall in the night market didn't open until dusk, but the day market on Tallow Street belonged to honest trades — fishmongers, cobblers, a man who sold songbirds he claimed could sing in harmony, though Freya suspected he simply owned three birds and one very good whistle hidden in his sleeve. She liked to walk it in the mornings, hands in her pockets, listening. Not for gossip exactly. For raw material. Grief, joy, longing — all of it floated over Tallow Street like smoke, and smoke could be caught, if you knew how.

Today the street was catching something else entirely.

"Did you hear about the beggar?" said the cobbler to the fishwife, leaning over his awning. "Old Fenwick. The one with the ruined leg, slept outside the granary since before the war."

"What of him?"

"Woke up whole. Whole, I say. Walked to the chapel steps on two good legs and asked for work." The cobbler shook his head slowly. "And that's not even the strangest part. Says he remembers dreaming of a door. A door of light. And a voice asking him — asking him, mind you — '*What do you ask?*' And Fenwick, being Fenwick, says, 'Well. A leg that works wouldn't go amiss.'"

"And?"

"And he got one."

The fishwife snorted. "Fenwick's been drinking shrine-water again."

"Shrine-water doesn't grow limbs, woman."

Freya kept walking, but her pace had changed. Slower. Her fingers found the little vial in her pocket without her permission — the stolen memory from three nights past, the one that had flickered with that impossible gold-white light. She hadn't sold it. She'd told herself it was because divine-touched goods attracted attention, and attention was death in her trade. That was true. It was also not the whole truth. There had been something in that memory, right at its edge, that had felt like a hand resting gently between her shoulder blades. She hadn't wanted to let it go.

Now she stopped at the corner where the Wardens posted their edicts, and read the new one, fresh ink, paste still wet at the corners.

BY ORDER OF THE WARDEN-COMMANDER:

Reports of unauthorized miracles are hereby classified as SEDITIOUS FABRICATION or UNLICENSED THEURGY, per the Ban of Thirteen Years. Citizens receiving communications from the dead, the divine, or any unlicensed source will report such events within one day to their district Wardenhouse. Failure to report constitutes complicity. Complicity is punished as practice.

The Ban stands. The gods are gone. Corvane endures.

— I. VARGA, WARDEN-COMMANDER

"The gods are gone," Freya murmured, reading it twice. "Then who's answering the post?"

A woman beside her, holding a basket of onions, laughed despite herself — then clapped a hand over her mouth and glanced nervously at the edict, then at the sky, then walked away very fast.

Freya understood the impulse. She looked at the sky too.

It was a perfectly ordinary gray Corvane sky, the color of unwashed wool. But she found herself remembering the flicker in the stolen memory: gold shot through with white, warm as an ember held in a cupped palm, moving with intention. Light wasn't supposed to move with intention. Candleflames leaned; sunbeams slanted. This had *looked* at things.

And now a baker's dead son was commenting on the state of the hearth, and a beggar had grown a leg overnight like a spring shoot, and Ilse Varga was calling it sedition.

Freya bought a meat pie she couldn't really afford and ate it standing in the square, thinking.

Here was the trouble with stealing memories for a living: you learned what people actually believed, underneath what they said. And underneath everything — under the Ban and the edicts and the twelve years of careful, enforced forgetting — the people of Corvane still prayed. Oh, they called it other things. Wishing. Hoping. Talking to themselves at gravesides. Muttering at the ceiling at three in the morning. But Freya had lifted enough memories to know a prayer when she heard one, and Corvane prayed the way rain fell: constantly, quietly, everywhere at once.

For twelve years those prayers had gone nowhere, which everyone pretended was fine.

Apparently they'd started going somewhere.

"*Idiot,*" she said to herself, crumpling the pie paper. "Don't even think it. Thinking it is how you end up in a Warden cellar."

But her hand was in her pocket again, around the vial, and the light inside it seemed faintly, smugly warmer than it had been yesterday.

* * *

By evening, the city had gone half-mad, and Freya had gone to work, because rent did not care about miracles.

The night market unfolded in the shadow of the old aqueduct as it always did — lanterns strung on fraying rope, stalls of dubious legality selling charms of every size and price. Freya had the best spot and knew it: tucked behind the fortune-teller's curtain, close enough to borrow her legitimacy and far enough to avoid her debts.

Her first client of the night was a young man with ink-stained fingers and the desperate look of someone who'd spent all day composing a resignation letter in his head and deleting it.

"I want to forget my master's face," he said. "Not — not forget him. Just the face. When I close my eyes to sleep, it's there. Disappointed."

"That's not his face," Freya said. "That's your father wearing his face. Different product. Costs more."

He blinked. "How much?"

"A silver for the forgetting. Two if you want the real fix." She reached under her counter and brought out a charm no bigger than a thumbnail, woven from a memory of a door opening onto sunlight — someone else's door, someone else's sunlight, harvested years ago from a girl leaving home happily, which was rarer than you'd think. "Sleep with this under your tongue. When the face comes, you'll remember the sunlight instead. Works nine nights out of ten."

"And the tenth night?"

"On the tenth night you'll dream of my aunt Berthe complaining about her knees. Frankly it's an improvement over disappointment."

He laughed — a surprised, cracked-open laugh, the kind she lived for, though she'd have denied living for anything under oath — and paid the two silver.

Business was steady after that. A widow wanting back the memory of her wedding day, which her son had pawned to pay a debt without telling her. (Freya didn't have it, but she knew who did, and took a finder's fee.) A dockhand who wanted to feel confident for one hour tomorrow, just one hour, long enough to ask a certain fish-packer to dance. Freya wove him a charm from a memory of a knight in a festival play, riding tall, absolutely certain the crowd loved him, which the crowd had not, but confidence had never required accuracy.

It was while she was tying off that weave, humming, that the screaming started again.

Different screaming this time. Not joyful.

The fortune-teller burst through her own curtain, cards scattering. "Fire!" he said. "Madam Osk's stall — she says her dead husband's in the brazier!"

Everyone in earshot did the same thing: froze, then moved. Half toward the fire and half away from it, which in a crowded market produces a great deal of elbowing.

Freya went toward it, because apparently she had lost her mind sometime that afternoon along with her self-control.

Madam Osk's stall was indeed smoking lightly. The brazier at its heart burned steady and low, and Madam Osk — ancient, toothless, feared by half the market — stood before it with her arms crossed, glaring into the flames with an expression Freya recognized intimately: the look of a professional confronting an amateur poaching on her territory.

"It keeps *talking*," Madam Osk announced to the gathering crowd. "In my husband's voice. He's been dead eleven years and he never talked this much alive."

"What's it saying?" someone asked.

"'Mind the coals.' 'You've let the salt run low.' 'That fortune-teller next door is a fraud.'" A ripple of nervous laughter. "Which is slander, because *I'm* the fraud on this street."

More laughter, thinner. Someone in the crowd whispered a name — a saint's name, an old god's name, one Freya hadn't heard spoken aloud since childhood, since before the Ban, since her grandmother used to leave a saucer of cream on the windowsill "for whoever's listening." The whisper spread like spilled water, finding every crack.

"Ban's business," said a hard voice at the edge of the crowd, and everyone scattered with the smooth practiced motion of people who had done this many times before. By the time the two patrol-Wardens pushed through, there was nothing to see but a fortune-teller, a smoking brazier, and an old woman insisting the wind must have carried some neighbor's words into her fire, sorry for the disturbance, won't happen again.

The Wardens lingered anyway. One of them wrote something in a ledger. Freya watched from the shadows behind her own curtain and noted, professionally, that they wrote a lot down these days. New ledgers. New lists. New questions asked of stall-keepers about their neighbors.

The Ban had teeth, but lately it seemed to be growing more of them.

* * *

Closing time came, and Freya packed her unsold charms into their lined box, doused her lantern, and sat alone in the dark behind her curtain doing the thing she did most nights: taking inventory of herself.

This was the secret cost of her trade, the one nobody paid for. Every memory she stole, she skimmed. A thimbleful here, a splash there — the fee the magic charged her, or perhaps the fee she secretly charged herself; she'd never been sure which. She carried fragments of hundreds of lives. She knew what a first kiss tasted like from forty different mouths. She knew the weight of sleeping children, the smell of a hundred kitchens, the specific quality of silence in a house the day after a funeral.

It made her excellent at her work. It also meant that on quiet nights, she was never quite alone, and never quite anyone.

Tonight the fragments were restless. She kept catching echoes — a hymn hummed by a grandmother, a blessing muttered over bread, a child's rhyme invoking a name that was now illegal to say. All the small devotions she'd siphoned over the years without a second thought, filed away as texture, flavor, background. Prayers. They'd all been prayers, and she'd been collecting them like pressed flowers for a decade, and now somewhere beyond the veil of the world, somebody appeared to be reading the collection.

"Nope," she said aloud to the dark. "No. Whatever this is, it is a problem for priests, prophets, and people with better boots than mine. You are going to sell that vial tomorrow to a discreet collector and forget you ever saw it."

The vial, in its box, radiated warmth like a cat pretending it wasn't sitting on your lap.

Freya sighed, took it out, and held it up to the last of the lantern-light. The stolen memory swam inside the glass — a fragment of some long-ago moment, a courtyard, a figure whose face blurred whenever she tried to focus on it, and threaded through it all, that light. Gold shot with white. Moving with intention.

And now — she frowned, tilted the vial — brighter. Definitely brighter than it had been three days ago. Memories faded; that was their nature. This one was doing the opposite. It was *ripening*.

As she watched, the light pulsed once, gently, like a heartbeat. Or like a knock.

Freya set the vial down on the counter with exaggerated care, the way one sets down a sleeping snake.

"Right," she said. "Questions."

Because here was what she knew, assembled from a lifetime of listening and a decade of skimming: the Ban had come thirteen years ago, sudden as a snapped bone. One day small magics were as common as cooking; the next, the Wardens were kicking in doors over a luck-charm. Everyone said the same thing — the gods abandoned us, the covenant broke, we're on our own now. Nobody said *why*. Nobody remembered why, which itself should have been suspicious, a whole city unable to recall the reason for the biggest event in its history. But grief makes bad historians, and fear burns libraries faster than fire does.

Freya's own memories of that year were soft-edged, waterlogged. Her mother's hands, quick and clever, weaving something that glowed. Her mother saying *hide it, Freya, hide it well, hide it even from yourself.* Then a Wardenhouse. Then a foundling school with cold porridge and colder teachers. Then nothing, until she'd discovered at sixteen that the emptiness inside her had a shape, and the shape fit stolen memories perfectly.

She'd always assumed her mother's warning was about the usual danger — getting caught practicing.

Standing in the dark with a ripening miracle in a bottle, Freya considered, for the first time in thirteen years, that maybe her mother had meant something else entirely. Maybe *hide it even from yourself* hadn't been advice about a charm.

Maybe it had been advice about *her*.

"Don't," she told herself again, but the word had gone soft, worn through like an old coin. Dread sat in her stomach, cold and heavy — dread of Wardens, of cellars, of edicts with her description on them. But beneath the dread, unwelcome as a weed through paving stones, something else was pushing up.

Curiosity.

Because she had spent her whole life handling other people's wonder secondhand — bottled, diluted, sold by the dram — and here, finally, glowing in a vial on her counter, was a piece of the genuine article. Alive. Growing. Possibly looking back at her.

"You are going to be the death of me," she informed the vial.

It pulsed warmly, and Freya could have sworn — though she would deny it later, under oath, before the Warden-Commander herself — that it pulsed in a way that felt distinctly like a laugh.

Outside, far off across the rooftops toward the river district, a bell began ringing. Not an alarm. The chapel bell of Saint Verene's, rung only for services, and services had been banned for thirteen years.

It rang three times. Paused. Then, unmistakably, deliberately, it rang thirteen times more.

Freya picked up the vial, put it in her pocket, pulled her hood up, and went to find out who was ringing.
