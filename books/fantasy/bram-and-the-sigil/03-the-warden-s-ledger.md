## 3. The Warden's Ledger

Ilse Varga kept her office colder than any other room in Warden headquarters, and she did it on purpose.

Comfort, in her experience, was the first symptom of decay. The commanders before her had padded their chairs and sweetened their tea and let the small magics creep back into Corvane like damp through plaster. She had inherited a cold office and an empty brazier and a ledger of failures twelve years thick, and she had promised herself, on her first day, that she would read every page of it.

Tonight she finished.

The ledger lived on the bottom shelf of her cabinet, bound in grey leather gone soft at the corners. Twelve years of unsolved charm-thefts, catalogued in her own cramped hand: dates, districts, victims, the particular flavor of what had been taken. It made dull reading, mostly. A wedding ring lifted from a drawer. A locket. Once, memorably, an entire stuffed heron from a collector's parlor, which even now Ilse maintained had been ordinary burglary and not magic at all, whatever old Hesk had insisted.

But charm-theft proper—memory-theft, the kind the ban existed for—that was different. That was a wound in the city you couldn't see, and Ilse had spent twelve years pressing her thumb against it.

She cleared her desk, set down the lamp, and began.

* * *

The first hour was arithmetic. Two hundred and eleven unsolved cases. She sorted them by district, because that was how you started anything: you put the world into piles until the piles confessed.

Lowbridge, where the poor lived crowded and generous with their griefs: sixty-three cases. The Lantern Rows, all taverns and tanners' yards: forty-one. The Merchant Stair, all brass knockers and locked doors: nineteen. And so on, up through the city's vertebrae to the Hill, where there were four cases total and every one of them involved someone's second-best memories being stolen by their own relatives, which was less magic than marriage as far as Ilse could tell.

She noted the distribution without surprise. Theft followed poverty; poverty followed the canals; the canals followed the shape of the old river, which the founders of Corvane had buried three hundred years ago for reasons nobody remembered. She wrote *concentration: water-adjacent districts* in the margin, and then she stopped, because her pen had written the word *water-adjacent* and something in the back of her mind had stirred like a dog hearing its name.

She knew that phrase. Not from Warden training. From before.

Ilse sat very still for a moment, the way you do when an old door in the house creaks and you're not sure whether to open it or nail it shut. Then she pulled a fresh sheet of paper and went back through the ledger properly—not as a Warden this time, but as a girl of nine who had once watched her mother draw circles in chalk dust on the kitchen floor and murmur about *where the water remembers.*

Her mother had been burned for it when Ilse was ten. Officially it had been "trafficking in prohibited charms." Unofficially, Ilse had never learned the unofficial version, because asking questions about your own mother's execution was a fine way to end a career before it began. So she had asked nothing, for thirty years, and built herself into the coldest, straightest instrument the Wardens possessed, and told herself the story that zeal was the same thing as certainty.

The circles in the chalk dust said otherwise. They always had.

* * *

By midnight she had a new map.

Not of districts—of methods. She went case by case and marked how each theft had been done, or rather what was missing afterward, because the method never left evidence. That was the whole art of memory-work: you took the memory and took the trace of taking it, both in one motion, so the victim was left with a smooth patch where the recollection should be, a hole they usually didn't even notice. *My husband must have sold that locket. I don't recall it clearly, but he must have.* The human mind patched over thefts the way skin patched over splinters, and only the object remained, orphaned, to testify.

Except.

Ilse laid out six cases, spread across eight years, and looked at them the way her mother had taught her to look at chalk circles: for the pattern underneath the pattern.

Case forty-one, Lowbridge: a widow robbed of the sound of her daughter's voice. Case fifty-eight, the Tanner's Walk: a man who could no longer remember his brother's face. Case ninety, by the old fish market: an old woman lost the memory of a lullaby. Case one-twelve. Case one-sixty. Case two hundred and three.

Six thefts. All small, by the standards of the trade. No fortunes in them, no leverage, no secrets worth selling. Just—the intimate debris of love. Voices. Faces. Songs. The kind of memories a person would pay almost anything to buy back and could not, because once distilled into a charm, a stolen memory kept its shape only if it stayed whole, and half of these had been *split*. The widow still had her daughter's laugh but not her voice. The man had his brother's face but not his name for it.

Ilse knew of exactly one tradition that split memories that way. Her mother had called it *covenant-craft*, and had explained it once, in the low voice she used for things the walls shouldn't hear: that the old order had forbidden the taking of whole memories, because a whole memory carried the self with it, and gods and mortals alike had agreed—back when gods and mortals still spoke—that the self was not for sale. So the practitioners of the covenant cut their thefts along the seams. Voice from laugh. Face from name. Half for the client, half for the charm, and the poor victim walking around forever with the other halves rattling loose inside them like coins in an empty purse.

That practice had died with the covenant, thirteen years ago. The Wardens had made sure of it. The ban had been absolute, and the craft, everyone said, was extinct.

Everyone except whoever was still doing it, quietly, in the water-adjacent districts of Corvane, with a skillfulness that made Ilse's twelve years of failure look less like incompetence and more like mercy—and she hated that thought the moment she had it, because mercy was not supposed to enter into it.

She lit a second lamp. Her tea had gone cold hours ago; she drank it anyway, grimacing, and pulled the six files close.

* * *

There was one more thing in the pattern, and it was the thing that made her hands go still on the paper.

The splits weren't random. She'd assumed craft-habit, the muscle memory of a dead tradition. But when she lined up the six cases in date order and looked at *what* had been taken—a voice, a face, a lullaby, a blessing spoken over a cradle, a name whispered at a door, a promise made at a graveside—she saw they fell into a sequence. An old sequence. One her mother had drawn once in chalk dust, six small marks arranged in an arc, and named with a word Ilse had spent thirty years pretending not to know.

*Sigil.*

Someone in Corvane was collecting, piece by piece, over years, the components of a working that had not been performed since before the ban. And not just any working. Her mother's word for it had been simple and terrible: *the gathering*. The first step a covenant-heir took, if there ever was one, to call the old stones out of hiding.

Which was impossible, because the Kavanagh line was extinct. Ilse had read the record herself, early in her career, hunting for it the way some orphans hunt for any scrap of their parents. The last of them had died—or vanished, the record was annoyingly vague—thirteen years ago, in the same season the covenant broke and the prayers of Corvane went suddenly, mercifully, unanswered. The Wardens' founder had built the whole order on that silence. *No gods, no griefs,* ran the motto on the headquarters lintel, and Ilse had walked under it every morning for twenty-two years and never once looked up.

She looked up now, in the empty office, at letters worn nearly smooth.

"No gods, no griefs," she said aloud, and heard, to her fury, that her voice cracked on the second word.

* * *

She allowed herself five minutes. She kept a sandglass on the shelf for precisely this purpose, though she had never before needed it at her desk. Grief, like comfort, was a symptom of decay, and decay was managed by measure.

In the five minutes she thought about her mother's hands. Chalk dust to the wrists. The smell of the kitchen floor after rain came in under the door. The way her mother had laughed—no. The way her mother had *sung*, low, while she worked, a tune Ilse had spent thirty years refusing to realize she had forgotten on purpose, scrubbing it out of herself the year after the fire, the way you'd scour a stain.

When the sand ran out, she turned the glass over, and instead of returning to work, she did something worse. She opened the bottom drawer of the cabinet, the one that stuck, and took out the thin file she consulted perhaps once a year, alone, at night, the way other people visited graves.

Her mother's case file. Case number one of the reorganized Wardens. The founding prosecution.

She had read it a hundred times. She read it differently tonight, because tonight she had sigil on the brain and grief in her throat, and the two together made her stupid enough to notice what she'd never noticed before: the file was incomplete. Pages nine through fourteen were gone—not redacted, which would have left a stamp, but *removed*, the binding re-sewn around the gap by someone in a hurry. And in the margin of page eight, beside the sentence describing the charges, someone had written a single word in pencil so faint she'd never seen it in a hundred readings:

*Kavanagh?*

Not her mother's name. Her mother had been Magda Sorel, of no line anyone claimed. But the question mark sat there like a hook in Ilse's chest. Someone, at the founding, had wondered whether the woman they burned had been working *for* the covenant rather than merely near it. Whether the last heir had not died thirteen years ago but passed something on—blood, craft, a charge—before disappearing into the crowd of the city.

And whoever had written that question mark had also, apparently, torn out pages nine through fourteen.

Ilse closed the file. She put it away. She pressed her palms flat against the cold desk until her breathing steadied, and then she permitted herself the smallest, most dangerous smile of her career.

Because here was what thirty years of discipline bought you, in the end: it bought you the ability to hold two truths in one head without dropping either. Truth the first: somewhere in the wet lower streets of Corvane, a covenant-heir was alive, and working, and gathering. Truth the second: the order she had given her life to had, at its founding, done something it needed to hide, and had hidden it in her mother's ashes.

She did not yet know what to do about truth the second. But truth the first was actionable tonight.

* * *

She worked until the fourth bell before dawn.

The warrant she drafted herself, in triplicate, because she trusted no clerks with this. She narrowed two hundred and eleven cases down to the ones with the cleanest splits, mapped the sellers' districts, traced the fence-network the way you'd trace veins under skin, and arrived, as she had always secretly known she would, at the night market: that sprawl of lanterns and licit stalls along the drowned steps of the old aqueduct, where the city went to buy back pieces of itself.

Somewhere in that crowd was a person who could split a memory along a seam that hadn't been taught in thirteen years. Someone young, probably—six of the thefts spanned twelve, and the earliest already showed mastery. Someone careful, invisible, beloved of no one and noticed by none, because that was how such workers survived. Someone who thought small magics kept them safe beneath the Wardens' notice.

Ilse almost pitied them. Almost. Pity was another symptom, and she had measures for that too.

What she felt instead, folding the warrant, was something hotter and older: the child's rage of watching her mother burn, transmuted at last into a target. If the heir was real, then the covenant was not history, and the ban was not justice but fear wearing justice's coat, and everything Ilse had built her cold bright life upon stood on a foundation somebody had hollowed out and hidden bodies in.

Fine. She had spent thirty years learning to see what was hidden. Let the heir spend one night learning what it was to be seen.

She stamped the warrant. Wax, seal, signature: *I. Varga, Warden-Commander.* Then, on impulse she would examine later and decline to regret, she added beneath the formal lines a single supplementary instruction to the raid captains, in her cramped hand:

*Take the fence alive. I want names—and I want the true name of whoever supplies him. Spell it exactly.*

Because she had done one last piece of arithmetic, in the smallest hour of the night, and it had produced one last pattern. Every one of the six clean-split thefts had passed through the same fence, a soft-bellied little vulture named Oskar Fen who moved goods through the Lantern Rows. And every one of those six clients, when Warden informants had asked weeks later who had sold them their charm, gave the same useless description of the seller: *small, hooded, quick hands, never the same face twice.*

Never the same face twice. Which meant the seller wore one, somewhere, when the lanterns were out. Everyone did. Even heirs. even gods, if the old stories held.

Ilse banked the brazier she never lit, took up the warrant, and went down through the sleeping headquarters to wake her raid captains. On the lintel above the great doors, the motto caught the lamplight as she passed.

"No gods, no griefs," she murmured, and for the first time in twenty-two years, the words sounded less like a creed than like an apology owed to a woman with chalk on her hands.

The raid would move at dusk, when the market filled. By midnight, she would have her thief.

She did not know—could not know—that three days earlier, in a stall on the drowned steps, a stolen memory had flickered with light the color of answered prayer, and that the thief had gone home holding it like a coal, and that the gathering had already begun.
