## 4. Bram at the Doorstep

The trouble with selling memories, Freya had learned over six years of trade, was that you had to know your customer before you knew your charm.

The widow Maren, for instance, came every third market night and bought nothing but forgetting — small forgettings, carefully rationed. A bad winter. A debt. The sound of her husband coughing in the next room during his last month. She paid in copper buttons she swore were silver, and Freya let her believe it, because the alternative was letting her count out real coin for a woman who clearly had none left to spare.

The dyer's apprentice, on the other hand, wanted remembering. He'd sold his mother's face years ago to pay a fine, back when he was stupid and seventeen, and now he saved up week by week hoping Freya might find another memory of her floating through the market like a lost glove. She hadn't yet. She kept looking anyway, though she'd never told him that. Hope was bad for business. It made people linger.

So when the stranger appeared at her stall that night — tall, unhurried, with the kind of stillness that made the crowd flow around him like water around a stone — Freya's first professional instinct was to sort him into a category.

He wasn't a forgetter. Forgetters hunched. They kept their eyes down and their hands in their pockets, ashamed of what they'd come to buy, as if the wanting itself were a wound they didn't want touched.

He wasn't a rememberer either. Rememberers leaned in. They gripped the edge of her stall like drowning men, and their eyes went wet at nothing, at everything, at the mere suggestion that somewhere in her inventory there might be one hour of their old lives left.

This man simply stood, and looked at her wares with an expression she could only describe as *fond*, and waited for her to finish wrapping the widow Maren's charm in waxed paper.

"Evening," he said, when she finally looked up. His voice was warm and low, the voice of someone who had never once needed to raise it. "You're the charm-seller."

"I'm a trinket-monger," Freya said automatically. "Lucky knots. Love tokens. Nothing the Wardens would care about."

"Mm." He glanced at the paper packet in her hand, which smelled faintly of rosemary, which was what forgetting smelled like when she distilled it properly. "And those are lucky knots."

"Extremely lucky. People weep with gratitude."

Something moved through his face then — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would live if it ever decided to move in. "I imagine they do."

Freya finished tying the packet and set it aside for Maren, who was haggling over candle prices two stalls down and would be back within the minute. Then she gave the stranger the look she gave all strangers: pleasant, flat, and priced by the word.

"If you're not buying," she said, "the queue starts behind the gentleman arguing with the eel-seller."

"I'm not buying." He set both hands on the counter of her stall, and she noticed them properly for the first time — broad, scarred across the knuckles, the hands of someone who had done work with them once, long ago, and hadn't since. "I'm returning something."

"We don't do refunds."

"It isn't a refund." He reached into his coat — plain wool, well-worn, wrong for him somehow, like a costume two sizes too honest — and drew out a small clay bead, unglazed, fired crooked. The kind of thing a child makes. He set it on her counter between the love tokens and the lucky knots, and Freya felt the hair rise along her arms before she understood why.

She knew that bead. Not its shape — its *weight*. There was a hum in it, faint as a struck bell heard through three walls, and she had felt that exact hum once before, eleven days ago, in a stolen memory that had flickered with light no memory should hold.

"Where did you get this?" she said. Her voice came out lower than she intended.

"You tell me," he said. "You're the one who took it."

* * *

Here is what Freya remembered of the theft, because she made a practice of remembering her thefts better than anyone:

Eleven days ago she had worked the Lantern District, which was rich enough to be careless and drunk enough to be generous. She'd worn her gray cloak and her gray face and drifted through a garden party like weather, brushing shoulders, lifting the bright loose things that spilled off people when they laughed too hard. Most of what she took that night was dross — a man's pride in his new boots, a woman's fondness for a cousin she secretly found tedious. Sellable, barely. Filler stock.

Then she'd brushed past a servant carrying wine, a gray-faced nobody among nobodies, and caught the spill of a memory so vivid it had nearly knocked her sideways: a hillside at dawn, dew burning off the grass, and a hand — *his* hand, she realized now, looking at the stranger's scarred knuckles — pressing a crooked clay bead into a child's palm. *Keep this,* the memory said, in a voice like low thunder being polite about it. *When you can't remember me, this will remember for you.* And the child had laughed, and the light — the impossible, gold-white, *wrong* light — had poured up out of the bead and through the memory like sunrise through a keyhole.

She'd cut the thread fast, the way you jerk your hand back from a stove. But she'd taken it. Of course she'd taken it. A memory that glowed? A memory with *light in it*? Six years of discipline, and she'd taken it anyway, because some hungers don't consult the head.

She'd told herself she'd sell it. She hadn't sold it. It sat in the locked box under her floorboard, wrapped in three layers of silk, and every night for eleven days she had told herself she was keeping it only until she understood what it was.

"And how," she said now, carefully, "would you know what I took? I don't recall serving you."

"You didn't serve me. You served a man named Odo, who was carrying my memory for me, because I asked him to keep it where I couldn't reach it." The stranger turned the clay bead slowly on the counter with one finger. "There are things I needed to forget, once. That was one of them. Odo held it for four hundred years, give or take, and then last month he woke up one morning without it, and I woke up one morning *with* it, and between those two mornings there is a girl in a gray cloak with quick hands."

Four hundred years. Freya looked at his face for the joke and found none. His eyes were dark and patient and very, very old, the way deep water is old, and standing this close to him she noticed something else: the lanterns above her stall burned steadier. The noise of the market seemed to lean away from them, leaving a pocket of quiet, as though the night itself had lowered its voice out of respect.

People were noticing. People always noticed, eventually, and then the Wardens noticed that people noticed, and then there were edicts and interrogations and a very bad month for everyone.

"Step back from the stall," she said quietly. "You're making a scene."

"I'm making a conversation."

"You're making *weather*. Look around you."

He looked. And whatever he saw in the nervous faces, the too-wide berth the crowd was giving them, made him sigh like a man who has forgotten, again, that he takes up more room than other people. The pressure eased. The lanterns flickered back to their ordinary sputter. The market noise closed over them like water over a dropped coin.

"Better?" he said.

"Worse," Freya said honestly. "Now you look like a man trying not to make a scene, which is worse than a scene. Scenes end. Trying-not-to goes on all night." She glanced down at the bead. Every instinct she owned was screaming at her to sweep it into the drawer with the rest of the stock, price it, sell it, be rid of it. Instead she heard herself ask: "What is it?"

"A promise." He said it simply, without ceremony, which somehow made it worse. "The first one I ever made to a mortal. Clay, because I figured clay was honest. It holds a piece of me. It's been holding it so long I'd half forgotten it was gone." He tilted his head. "You've opened it. You've seen inside."

Freya said nothing, which was answer enough.

"The light," he said. "That's mine. That's what I am, when I'm not pretending to be a man in a wool coat standing in a market buying nothing." He watched her with that terrible patience. "You took a god's memory, Freya Kavanagh, and you didn't burn, and you didn't break, and you didn't even drop the thread. Do you understand how unusual that is?"

Her whole body went cold and then hot, in that order. Not at *god*. Gods were stories; gods were what the Wardens had spent twelve years and a great deal of blood proving were stories. What froze her was the name.

"I never told you my name."

"No," he agreed. "But I've known it longer than you've been alive. It's an old name. It comes with obligations attached, most of them unpaid." He leaned forward slightly, and the lamplight did something strange to his face — softened it, or aged it, or showed her the shape of something underneath the shape of a man. "You carry sigil-blood, Freya. The covenant runs in your family like a river under a street. Everyone thought it had run dry. Everyone was wrong."

* * *

Later — much later, when she had time to take her own pulse on the matter — Freya would identify the moment everything changed. It wasn't *god*. It wasn't *covenant*, a word she knew only from banned pamphlets and Warden sermons. It wasn't even the name, spoken in that low warm voice, landing on her like a hand on her shoulder from behind.

It was this: he said *Kavanagh*, and some animal part of her, some part older than her training and her caution and her six careful years, *recognized* it. The way you recognize a song you haven't heard since childhood. The way the body knows home before the mind remembers the address.

Which meant, she understood with a lurch, that some part of her had been waiting for this man. Or for someone like him. Or — and this was the thought she shoved firmly away — for *him*, specifically, which was absurd, because she had never seen him before in her life.

Except that she had. In the memory. On the hillside. Pressing a crooked bead into a child's hand while the dawn came up gold behind him.

That child had been laughing. That child had known him. That child had —

"That memory," she said. Her mouth had gone dry. "The one I took. The child in it. Who was the child?"

His expression did something complicated and gentle. "Who do you think?"

"I think you're going to say something impossible."

"I'm a god. Impossibility is my native language." He picked up the clay bead and held it out to her across the counter, open-palmed, the way you offer food to a horse you don't want spooked. "It was you, Freya. Four hundred years ago, a version of you — your blood, your line, a girl with your same stubborn chin — stood on a hill at dawn and made me promise I'd come back. I have come back late. I am sorry for it. But I have come back, and I find the covenant broken, the sigil scattered, and the last heir of Kavanagh selling pieces of other people's lives in a night market because it's the only magic small enough that the Wardens don't see it."

The market roared on around them. Somewhere the eel-seller was winning his argument. The widow Maren was bearing down on the stall with her candle savings and her copper-button silver, and Freya stood rooted behind her counter with a god's first promise sitting in the air between her hands, untaken.

"This is a con," she said. It came out thin. "Some new kind of con. You mark the mark with talk of blood and destiny, and then — what? There's an investment? There's a relic I need to help you recover, for a modest fee?"

"There are relics," he admitted. "Stones. Three of them, scattered. And yes, recovering them will cost you. More than a fee." Something crossed his face then, quickly, like a bird over water — grief, maybe, or guilt — and was gone before she could name it. "But I'm not asking you tonight. Tonight I'm only returning what's yours to return." He nudged the bead an inch closer. "Odo carried it for me because I couldn't trust myself with it. Now I find I can't trust anyone else with it either. Isn't that always the way."

Freya looked at the bead. The hum of it was louder now, or she was simply closer, and underneath the hum she could almost hear — voices? One voice? A child laughing on a hillside four centuries dead?

"Why me?" she said. "If the blood matters — if I matter — where have you been? Thirteen years the Wardens have been burning anything that glows, and you show up now, at a *stall*, in a *wool coat*—"

"Because thirteen years ago the covenant broke, and when it broke, I—" He stopped. For the first time since he'd arrived, the patience in his face cracked, and underneath it was something raw and exhausted and very old indeed. "Because when it broke, I forgot which side of it I was standing on. It took me this long to walk here, Freya. That's the truth of it. Gods fell too. We just fell more quietly."

The widow Maren arrived at the stall then, breathless from her bargaining, holding out her coins, and the ordinary world crashed back in like a wave. Freya took the buttons, counted them, handed over the waxed packet, murmured the little blessing she always murmured — *may it weigh less than you expect* — and Maren shuffled off lighter by one bad winter.

When Freya looked up, the stranger was still there. Waiting. Holding out a crooked clay bead and four hundred years of apology, and looking at her — this was the worst part, this was the part she would turn over for nights afterward — looking at her the way you look at a door you once lived behind.

"Take it or don't," he said. "Either way, I'll find you again. I'm told I'm persistent. It's one of my fewer attractive qualities."

Freya Kavanagh, who had survived six years in Corvane by never touching anything that hummed, reached out and took the bead.

It was warm. It was warm the way a hand is warm, and for half a heartbeat she could have sworn it tightened around hers, gently, like a greeting between old friends.

"Bram," she said, testing the name he'd given her against the recognition still ringing in her bones. "Is that really your name?"

"It's the one you gave me," he said. "On the hillside. You were four, and you couldn't pronounce the old one." He smiled then, fully, and the whole market seemed to lean toward it like plants toward sun. "I've been fond of it ever since."

He stepped back into the crowd, and the crowd, obligingly, swallowed him — not gradually, the way people vanish, but all at once, the way dreams do.

Freya stood alone at her stall with a god's promise in her fist and her heart hammering out a rhythm she didn't recognize, and it was only when she went to lock the bead in the drawer that she saw the second thing he'd left behind, tucked under the love tokens where only she would find it.

A scrap of paper. On it, in handwriting far too elegant for a man in a wool coat, were three words:

*Aqueduct. Midnight. Come.*

And beneath them, smaller, almost shy, as if he'd added it after a long debate with himself:

*I'll bring the answers. You bring the questions. Between us we might manage a conversation.*

Freya read it twice. Then she looked up at the ruined aqueduct looming black against the stars at the city's edge, the one every child in Corvane knew to avoid after dark, and she did the single most foolish thing she had done in six careful years.

She began, already, to plan how she would get there.
