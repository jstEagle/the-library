## 5. What the Charms Cost

The morning after the stranger named Bram turned her whole understanding of the world sideways, Freya Kavanagh did what she always did when the world refused to make sense: she went to work.

There was a certain comfort in routine. Boil the water. Lay out the vials. Sort the day's charms into their little velvet rows — forgetting in blue, remembering in amber, the small dulling charms for grief in grey, because grief-charm customers always struck her as people who would find bright colors insulting. The kettle sang. The rain ticked against the shutters of her workroom above the tannery, and downstairs old Maretta was already yelling at her apprentice about the proper way to stretch a hide, which meant it was a perfectly ordinary morning in Corvane, gods answering prayers or no.

Freya told herself that was why her hands were steady. Ordinary morning. Nothing to do with the fact that a man with river-grey eyes had stood at her stall two nights ago and said *you carry the sigil-blood* as casually as if he were commenting on the weather.

She had not slept well. She had dreamed of light under her skin, and woken with her palm pressed flat against the floorboards as though checking whether the world was still there.

"Work," she said aloud, to the vials. "You're a charm-witch, not an oracle. Work."

The first appointment of the day was a widow who wanted to forget the sound of her husband's cough. Routine. Freya took the memory gently — she always took them gently, a touch like lifting a moth from a windowpane — and distilled it while the widow drank tea and talked about her late husband's terrible opinions about soup. By the time the charm cooled in its setting, the woman was laughing at something, and Freya thought, as she always did, *see? This is mercy. This is a kindness. The Wardens can keep their grand forbidden magics; I deal in small mercies.*

She would think about that phrase later — *small mercies* — and shudder. But that came after.

* * *

The trouble arrived at half past noon, in the person of Tam Voss.

Tam was a regular. A dockhand with forearms like mooring rope and a face that had stopped trying to express anything years ago. He came every few weeks for a forgetting-charm, always the same request, always the same flat voice: *take the night the ship went down.* Freya had taken pieces of it three times now. The sound of the hull cracking. The cold. His brother's hand slipping from his. Each time he came back, the memory had grown back around the edges like grass over a scar, and each time he paid double without haggling, and Freya had never once asked herself the question she should have asked, which was: *what is left of a man when you've cut the same wound out of him three times?*

Today Tam did not haggle either. Today Tam put his coins on the table and said, "All of it. Take all of it this time."

Freya looked up from her ledger. "All of what?"

"The night." He said it the way you'd order bread. "The whole night. Not just the sinking. Before it too. The card game where I bet him to go on that run. All of it. I want it gone."

"That's not—" Freya set down her pen. "Tam, that's not how it works. I take a thread, not the whole cloth. If I pull the whole night out of you, there's nothing to anchor the edges. Memories aren't stones you can just lift out. They're load-bearing."

"I don't care."

"You will." She said it more sharply than she meant to, and saw him flinch, and softened. "Listen. You lost your brother. That's a hole nothing fills. But it's *your* hole. It's yours. If I take it, you'll still have the hole, you just won't know its shape anymore. You'll hurt and won't know why. That's not mercy, Tam. That's just theft with better manners."

Something moved behind his eyes then — something desperate and young, buried under all that dockhand flatness. "I dream about it," he said quietly. "Every night. Every single night since the moon started doing — whatever it's doing. Since the hearths started talking. I dreamed my brother was standing in the doorway asking me why I sold his death for coin, and Freya—" his voice cracked, "—he was *right*. That's what I do, isn't it? I come here and I pay you to carry it so I don't have to."

Freya's hands had gone very still on the table.

Because that was the arrangement, wasn't it. That was the whole trade. They paid her to hold what they couldn't. And she held it. She kept fragments of everything she ever stole — a witch's habit, a hoarder's instinct, call it what you liked — little slivers of other people's worst nights stored in jars along her walls like preserves. She'd told herself it was necessary. Craft required reference material. A charm-witch who forgot what grief tasted like couldn't distill it properly.

She had never once asked what a shelf full of other people's griefs did to the woman who dusted it every day.

"Half the night," she said finally. "The moment of the sinking itself, and not a grain more. And Tam — you go see Mother Cilla at the lantern-shrine. Talk to someone living. That's the price. Charm plus confession, or no sale."

He almost smiled. It was terrible to see, like watching a building try to remember it had once been a house. "Since when do you charge in sermons?"

"Since the gods started answering prayers and made everything strange. Half the night. Deal?"

"Deal."

* * *

It should have ended there. She took the thread, she sealed the charm, she sent him off toward the shrine with instructions and a heel of bread she pretended wasn't charity. She even felt good about it, in the tired way of a job done slightly less badly than usual.

The trouble came back at dusk, wearing someone else's coat.

A girl — no, a young woman, though it took Freya a long moment to be sure — stood in the doorway of the workroom, swaying slightly, holding a blue vial in both hands like a candle flame she was afraid of losing. Freya knew her. Sefa. The cooper's daughter, nineteen, sweet-faced, forever saving up for a forgetting-charm one copper at a time. Her sweetheart had died in the flood-year. Freya had sold her four charms over two years. Four small threads. The funeral. The last argument. The way he'd smiled. The sound of his voice saying her name.

"Sefa." Freya rose slowly. "Where did you get that?"

"Bought it." Sefa's voice was pleasant and empty, a room after the furniture's been sold. "From your stall. Tuesday."

"I didn't sell you a full vial. I don't *make* full vials. I sell you a sip, a thimbleful, and I tell you—" Freya heard her own voice climbing and forced it down. "Sefa. Look at me. How many have you had?"

Sefa considered the question with the mild interest of someone asked about the weather in a country she'd never visited. "Had what?"

That was when the cold went through Freya like a blade of river-water.

She crossed the room and took the girl by the shoulders. "Sefa. Your name. Tell me your name."

"It's..." The girl frowned. Behind her eyes, something groped around in the dark for a handle and found nothing. "It's on the tip of my tongue."

"What street do you live on?"

Tip of her tongue.

"Who is your father?"

And here Sefa's face did the worst thing Freya had ever seen a face do: it relaxed. Because the question was hard, and she was tired, and somewhere inside her a voice said *you bought the charm so you wouldn't have to carry it anymore,* and so she let go. Freya watched it happen. Watched the last grip slacken. The girl swayed, sat down on the stool as if she'd merely decided to rest, and smiled up at Freya with the open, unburdened smile of a child.

"Hallo," said Sefa pleasantly. "Are you my mother?"

Freya did not remember crossing the room. She did not remember grabbing the girl's satchel and dumping it out — four empty blue vials, and a fifth, unopened, thank every god forgotten and otherwise, a fifth still sealed. She did remember sitting down across from Sefa afterward, holding the girl's hands, feeding her the remembering-charms one by one from the amber row — the strongest ones, the ones she never sold, the ones she kept for emergencies she'd never imagined would include *undoing myself.*

They didn't work.

Of course they didn't work. Remembering-charms could sharpen what remained. They could not rebuild what had been poured out. You cannot distill a person back into themselves from an empty bottle. Freya tried anyway, hands shaking, feeding the girl amber light until dawn, whispering *your name is Sefa, your father makes barrels on Tanner's Row, you hate the smell of the tannery but you love your father, your name is Sefa, your name is Sefa—*

By morning, Sefa could say her name if prompted. Could sometimes hold it for an hour. Everything else was smooth water over a drowned village.

Freya sat on the floor beside the stool where the girl dozed, her back against the shelving, surrounded by her neat rows of vials, and understood — slowly, completely, the way a drowning person understands water — what she had been doing all these years.

* * *

Maretta found her there at midmorning, having let herself up with the spare key and a great deal of clattering, because Maretta believed privacy was a kind of illness.

"Well," said Maretta, taking in the scene: Freya gray-faced on the floor, a stranger sleeping on her stool, forty spent amber vials. "Either you've taken up charity or you've finally murdered a customer and are keeping her as a warning to the others."

"Maretta."

"You look terrible. Have you eaten? Don't answer, obviously you haven't." The old woman peered at Sefa. "Cooper's girl. Heard about her yesterday — her da's been going door to door half-mad with worry. So she came here." Maretta's voice did something complicated. "And here she stayed."

"I did this." Freya's voice came out flat and far away. "Not the drinking. Well — yes, the drinking too, I sold her those sips, I never counted them, I never asked. But Maretta, it's bigger than one girl. I've been selling forgetting to this city for six years. Six years. Do you know how many Tams there are? People coming back every month, shaving a little more off themselves because the pain grows back and they've learned it can be cut?" She laughed, and it was an awful sound. "I thought I was running a mercy business. I'm a leech with good manners."

Maretta lowered herself onto the crate by the door with the groan of a woman whose knees had opinions. For a while she said nothing, which from Maretta was roughly equivalent to a eulogy.

"My sister," she said at last. "After her boy died, she drank herself smooth as river stone. Not your charms — the cheap kind, the gutter-kind, the stuff that leaves you smiling and useless. Family used her up and forgot her and she'd smile at strangers like they were long-lost friends." She nodded at Sefa. "So don't tell me what forgetting costs. I've seen the bill." She fixed Freya with one eye. "But I'll tell you something else, girl. My sister chose the gutter-charm because nobody offered her anything better. Nobody ever said to her: the pain is yours, and yours means it can't be stolen, and things that can't be stolen can't be hollowed. You're the only one in this city offering the cutting. Maybe it's time somebody offered the keeping."

"The keeping," Freya repeated.

"Aye. Harder to sell. Terrible margins." Maretta heaved herself up. "Feed the girl. Write down everything she remembers before it slides. And then—" she jabbed a finger at the shelves, at the hundreds of little jars, the slivers of a thousand strangers' nights, "—you might ask yourself what *you've* been drinking all these years, keeping bits of everyone. No wonder you look like a woman haunted. You've got a haunted house between your ears and you dust it daily."

She left. Freya sat a long time among her vials.

* * *

That evening she did something she had never done. She opened her own hoard.

Jar by jar, she went through the fragments she'd kept — not the stock-in-trade, but the private shelf, the pieces too interesting or too lovely or too painful to sell. A child's first snowfall, stolen from a merchant's daughter. An old man's memory of his wedding day, lifted because the payment was overdue and Freya had needed rent. A mother's lullaby in a language nobody spoke anymore. Her own earliest theft, back when she was fifteen and hungry and hadn't yet learned to feel the edges of what she took: a baker's joy at the smell of morning bread, which she had carried so long she sometimes thought it was hers.

She held the lullaby jar up to the lamplight and hummed the tune inside it, and realized she could not remember her own mother's voice. Only this stolen one. She had filled her hollow places with other people's memories the way Tam had tried to fill his with forgetting, and called the one craft and the other weakness, and they were the same thing wearing different clothes.

"Oh," she said softly, to the empty workroom. "Oh, you fool."

Every charm she'd ever woven had cost twice. Once for the one who forgot, and once for her — a grain of weight per theft, imperceptible, cumulative, six years of other lives settled into her bones like silt. She was not hollow yet. But she could feel, for the first time, the shape of the hollowing. The places where Freya should have been and instead there was a baker's morning, a dead man's wedding, a lullaby in a dead tongue.

She thought of Bram at her stall. *You carry the sigil-blood.* She had assumed he meant bloodline. Standing there among her jars, she wondered if he'd meant something heavier. Blood carries things. That was rather the point of blood.

Sefa stirred on the cot in the corner — Freya was not sending her home to a father who deserved better than a daughter-shaped absence, not tonight, maybe not for a while — and murmured her own name in her sleep, holding onto it like a rope in dark water. *Sefa. Sefa.*

Freya stood, and began, methodically, to clear a space on her workbench. Not to destroy the hoard — she wasn't there yet, and besides, some of these fragments belonged to people who might someday want them back, and she was done, *done*, taking what wasn't given. But she sorted them. Labeled them. Made a ledger page headed, in her careful thief's script: *To Return.*

Then she pulled out fresh paper and wrote at the top of a new page: *What I Owe.* Underneath, the list was short and terrible. *Sefa: everything. Tam: an apology. The city: a reckoning, eventually. Myself: to be found out.*

She was still writing when the knock came at the shutter — three soft raps, patient, unhurried, the knock of someone who had knocked on a great many doors across a very long time and expected, on the whole, to be answered.

Through the gap in the boards, lamplight fell on a familiar grey eye, and a voice she had spent two days telling herself she'd imagined said:

"I told you I'd come back. And I've brought you something better than an apology, Freya Kavanagh. I've brought you the first stone — and a way to pay what you owe."
