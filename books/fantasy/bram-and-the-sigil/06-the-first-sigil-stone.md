## 6. The First Sigil-Stone

### Freya

The aqueduct had been dead longer than Corvane had been walled. Its arches marched out of the city's eastern edge like the ribs of something enormous that had lain down to die and never quite finished. Moss owned everything now. Ivy strangled the old stone channels where water hadn't run since her grandmother's grandmother was young.

Bram walked ahead of her through the first arch, and Freya spent a suspicious amount of effort not watching him do it.

He moved like someone who had learned to walk in a world with better manners—no hurry, no wasted motion, as if the ground were obliged to be even for him. Which, she supposed, it might be. Gods probably got discounts on physics.

"You're staring," he said, without turning around.

"I'm assessing. There's a difference. Staring is free; assessing is professional."

"Of course." She could hear the smile in it, which was insufferable. "And what does your assessment conclude?"

"That you walk like a man who's never once stepped in horse droppings."

"I haven't." He paused at the second arch and looked back at her, and there it was—the smile, quiet and crooked, like it wasn't sure of its welcome. "Horses have always liked me. It's one of my more reliable qualities."

"Horses liking you is a quality?"

"It's the foundation of several religions."

Freya snorted and caught up with him, her satchel bumping against her hip. Inside it: three vials of memory-charm she'd brought against her better judgment, a coil of rope, a stub of candle, and the stolen memory that had started all of this—the one that flickered with light no charm should hold. Bram had asked for it back two nights ago at her stall. He still hadn't taken it. Every time she offered, he said *not yet*, in a voice that made her want to throw it at his head just to see what happened.

The tunnel mouth opened beneath the third arch, a black slot in the hillside breathing cold air. Old stonework framed it, and carved into the lintel—so worn she had to trace it with her fingers to read it—ran a line of sigils. Her fingers knew them before her eyes did. They prickled, faintly, the way a scar prickles before rain.

*You've touched these before,* she thought. *Or they've touched you.*

"The first stone is down there," Bram said. All the humor had gone out of his voice, which she was learning to distrust almost as much as the humor itself. "About four hundred paces. The guardian will know we're coming long before we arrive."

"Guardian." Freya looked at the dark. The dark looked back, in the way of all darks. "What kind of guardian?"

"A fair one."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I have." He crouched at the threshold and lit the candle from nothing—a small theft of flame from somewhere else, done so casually she nearly missed it—and handed it to her instead of keeping it. "It will test you, Freya. Not me. Whatever it shows you, it will show you truly. That's the cruelty of guardians. They don't lie."

"And if I fail?"

"Then the stone stays buried, the covenant stays broken, and in thirteen turns of the moon this city becomes a smear between two grinding worlds." He said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "So. No pressure."

"No pressure," she agreed. "Wonderful. Let's go wake up something ancient in a hole in the ground. This is exactly how I pictured my life going."

She went first. If she was going to die in the dark, she preferred not to watch anyone else's face while she did it.

* * *

### Bram

She counted her steps. He could see it in her shoulders—each pace measured, each breath rationed, the whole of her organized against fear the way a quartermaster organizes a siege. Thirteen years of thieving had taught her that control was survival, and she wore it like armor fitted by a very good, very lonely smith.

Bram had watched mortals walk into guardian-dark for three thousand years. Most of them prayed on the way in. Some sang. A few wept, honestly or strategically.

Freya Kavanagh counted steps.

*This one,* he thought, and the thought arrived with the weight of a bell. *Oh. This one.*

The tunnel widened into the old cistern chamber, and he felt the guardian stir before any human sense would have caught it—a pressure change, a hush, the particular silence of something very old opening one eye. The candle flame in Freya's hand bent sideways though there was no wind.

"Stop," he murmured.

She stopped. Of course she stopped; she'd been stopping on his word since the market, and he suspected she hated herself a little for it each time.

The chamber revealed itself by degrees as their eyes adjusted. A round pool occupied its center, black and perfectly still, ringed by twelve broken pillars. On the far side, set into the wall like a heart in a chest of stone, something glowed. Not brightly. Steadily. A light the color of dawn deciding whether to bother.

The sigil-stone. First-cut, oldest of the three. He'd last seen it when the world still kept its promises.

"Is that—" Freya began.

"Yes."

"It's smaller than I expected."

"They usually are," Bram said. "The important things."

The water in the pool shivered. Once. Deliberately. Like a finger tapped on a table.

Then the guardian rose out of it without disturbing the surface at all, which was the most alarming thing about it—it came upward the way a reflection comes upward, and stood upon the black water wearing a shape assembled from every mercy ever misused. A cupped hand offering bread. An umbrella over a stranger. A blanket laid on a drunkard. All of it translucent, all of it wrong, all of it watching them with a face that was simply a smooth oval, patient as weather.

Freya did not scream. Bram noted this with pride that surprised him. She went white as bone and gripped the candle hard enough to crack it, and she did not scream.

"What is it?" she whispered.

"A guardian of the covenant. Bound here when the stones were set." He kept his own voice level, because the guardian read voices the way she read markets. "It holds the first stone until an heir comes to claim it."

"And how does it decide who's an heir?"

The guardian answered for him. Its voice was not a sound so much as a memory of sound, arriving inside the skull rather than the ear:

***KAVANAGH.***

Freya flinched. "That's—that's my name. How does it know my name?"

***THE BLOOD KNOWS ITSELF. THE STONE KNOWS THE BLOOD. COME AND BE WEIGHED.***

"I don't suppose," Freya said, in the bright, brittle tone of a woman negotiating with a landslide, "we could skip the weighing? I'm very busy. Terrible week. Lots of appointments."

***COME AND BE WEIGHED.***

"She has to cross the water," Bram told her quietly. "Alone. I can't follow. Guardians test heirs, not gods—we're considered biased."

"Bias. Right. Because you'd obviously cheat on my behalf."

"I would," Bram said, "absolutely and without hesitation. Which is rather the point."

Something crossed her face then—not fear, or not only fear. He'd seen that look once before, thirteen years ago, on another Kavanagh standing at another threshold. The look of a person realizing that the door in front of them is also a door behind them, and both are open, and neither closes again.

Freya set the candle down on the pillar's stump. Straightened her satchel. Rolled her sleeves, because apparently that helped, and he loved her a little for it, and put the loving away in the place he kept such things, because now was not the hour.

"All right," she said. "Weigh away."

She stepped onto the water.

* * *

### Freya

The water held her. That was the first horror—not sinking, but standing, the surface firm as glass beneath her boots while ripples spread from her heels in rings that made no sense. The second horror was the guardian, drifting closer, its borrowed mercies shifting and reforming, and Freya understood with a lurch that it was showing her its inventory. Testing her reactions. Cataloguing her.

The third horror was hers alone, and it arrived from inside.

The chamber changed.

Not dramatically—no thunderclap, no smoke. Just a slow adjustment, the way a dream adjusts when you realize you're dreaming. The pillars became the plastered walls of a room she had spent thirteen years not thinking about. The black pool became a narrow bed. The candle-glow became lamplight, yellow and cheap, and the smell hit her next: tallow and fever-sweat and the specific sweetness of illness burning through a body.

"No," Freya said.

***WEIGHED IN TRUTH,*** the guardian said, gentle as a hand on the back of the neck. ***NOT IN COMFORT.***

Her mother's room. Her mother's bed. Her mother—

Her mother turned her head on the pillow. Thirteen years dead, and here she was, gray-lipped and bright-eyed, and she smiled the smile she'd saved for the bad days, the one that cost her everything, and she said:

"Frey-bird. Come sit with me, love. Tell me about the market."

Because that was the night. That was the worst one, the one Freya had stolen out of her own head twice and buried under forty other memories and still, still, it lived down there like a stone at the bottom of a well. The night her mother, dying by inches, had asked her daughter to take the pain away. Not cure it—there was no curing it—but *take it*, lift it out the way Freya lifted small memories from strangers, and Freya had knelt by the bed with her hands shaking and her gift humming in her blood, and she had said yes. She had reached in.

And she had taken the pain. All of it. Every ember of it, into herself, where it still lived.

But she had taken other things too, things her mother hadn't offered, because the taking was easy and the giving-back was impossible and she was nineteen and terrified and greedy in the way only the desperate are greedy. She'd skimmed the edges of everything her mother was. A childhood on a farm. A wedding dance in the rain. The name she'd wanted to give the baby, before Freya, before the city, before any of it.

And her mother had died lighter than she should have been, smiling, not knowing why the world felt thin, and Freya had walked out of that room carrying a dead woman's whole self in fragments, and she had sold none of them, and she had never once looked at them directly, and she had told herself for thirteen years that this was love.

It was not love. Standing in the guardian's truth, she finally saw it plainly. It was theft. The tenderest theft she'd ever committed, and a theft nonetheless.

"Take it back," she whispered to the guardian. Her voice cracked in the middle. "I don't—I didn't mean—take it back."

***IT IS YOUR MEMORY,*** the guardian said. ***IT CANNOT BE TAKEN FROM YOU. ONLY GIVEN. OR KEPT. THAT IS THE WHOLE OF THE TEST, HEIR. WHAT WILL YOU DO WITH WHAT YOU HAVE STOLEN?***

The room waited. Her mother waited, smiling her expensive smile, dying her second death.

Freya's hands were shaking. She looked at them—at the thief's hands, quick and clever, hands that had fed her for thirteen years off other people's surplus joy—and she understood, suddenly, completely, what the guardian was actually asking. Not *are you worthy*. Nothing so flattering. It was asking whether she could stand in the worst room of her life and choose differently than she had chosen at nineteen.

She couldn't undo it. That was the terror, and it was total, and she let it move through her like cold water because there was nowhere else for it to go.

But she could carry it differently.

Freya knelt by the bed—by the memory of the bed, by the truth of it, it hardly mattered which—and she took her mother's hand, the way she hadn't been brave enough to do slowly, properly, that night.

"Mam," she said. "I took from you. When you gave me the pain, I took more than the pain, and I kept it, and I told myself it was grief but it was greed, and I am sorry. I am sorry in front of whatever holy thing is listening." Her throat closed. She pushed through it. "I can't give it back. You're gone. But I'll stop hiding it. I'll carry it open, where I can see it, so it costs me what it should have cost. And everything I do after this—" her voice broke and reformed, "—everything I do after this, I'll do to pay it forward. To pay *you* forward. That's the only restitution left. So I'm making it. Here. Now. Witnessed."

The guardian's oval face tilted.

***AND IF THE PRICE IS MORE THAN YOU CAN PAY?***

"Then I'll pay it anyway," Freya said. "I'm a Kavanagh. Apparently we're bad at quitting."

For a moment, nothing. The lamplight hummed. Her mother smiled her terrible beautiful smile and, this time, Freya let herself memorize it freely—let it in without stealing, the way it should always have come.

Then the room folded up like paper, and she was kneeling on black water in a drowned cistern, and the guardian stood before her, and it was bowing.

***THE HEIR IS WEIGHED,*** it said. ***THE HEIR IS NOT FOUND WORTHY. WORTHINESS IS FOR FINISHED THINGS. THE HEIR IS FOUND *WILLING*. THIS SUFFICES. THIS HAS ALWAYS SUFFICED.***

The wall split along a seam no eye could find, and the sigil-stone drifted out of it, small and dawn-colored, and came to rest in Freya's cupped hands like a bird deciding to trust her.

It was warm. It pulsed once against her palms—once, like a heartbeat recognizing another—and light ran up her arms in fine golden threads and sank into her skin, into the blood beneath, into whatever the blood carried that had been waiting thirteen years for permission.

Freya gasped. It didn't hurt. It was worse than hurt: it was *purpose*, arriving all at once after a lifetime of mere survival, and it filled her the way floodwater fills a dry channel.

She looked up. Bram stood at the pool's edge, and his face—

Gods weren't supposed to look like that. Openly. Undone. Like a man watching a locked door swing wide after three thousand years.

"Well?" she managed. Her voice came out strange, resonant, as if the stone had gotten into it.

"You passed," he said.

"I *willinged*," she corrected, and heard the guardian's word settle into her like a keystone. "There's a difference. Apparently it matters."

"It's the only thing that's ever mattered." He waded out onto the water toward her—of course he could walk on it, of course, insufferable—and stopped an arm's length away, close enough that she could see the candlelight doubled in his eyes, once from the flame and once from the stone in her hands. "Two more, Freya. Two more stones, and then the hardest part, which I'll tell you about when I absolutely must and not one moment sooner."

"How comforting."

"I'm a god of my word."

"You're a god of *evasion* with excellent manners."

His laugh echoed off the pillars, and the guardian bowed again, and the first sigil-stone beat softly against her palms like a second heart learning the rhythm of hers.

They climbed out of the dark together, into air that smelled of moss and rain coming, and Freya was halfway up the tunnel before she noticed the light on the horizon above the eastern wall of Corvane.

It was red. Not sunset-red. Watchfire-red.

Somewhere behind them, in the city, something was burning—and from the direction of the flames, thin on the wind, came the unmistakable clangor of Warden bells.
