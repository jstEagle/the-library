## 7. Ilse Closes In

Ilse Varga had been awake for nineteen hours when the kettle in her office began to sing, and she regarded it the way a general regards a mutinous soldier.

"You'll wait," she told it.

The kettle did not wait. It never did. She poured the tea anyway, because Warden-Commander Ilse Varga had exactly two indulgences left in this world — black tea taken scalding, and the belief that her work meant something — and she guarded both with the ferocity of a woman who had once had more.

The reports from the Lower Terraces were spread across her desk like a hand of very bad cards. A baker's son speaking through a hearth. A beggar healed of a wasting sickness that three physicians had certified terminal. A woman on Tanner's Row who swore her dead mother's voice came out of a rain barrel.

Twelve years of silence from the old gods, and now this. Either the city was going mad in clusters, or something had opened a door.

Ilse drank her tea and believed neither, entirely. Madness clustered, yes, but it did not heal beggars. And doors — doors were the business of the covenant, and the covenant had been ash since before she'd sworn her first oath.

She pulled the thirteenth file from the bottom drawer. The one with the cracked leather spine and the coffee ring shaped, if you squinted, like a crescent moon.

Her mother's handwriting marched across the first page: *Charm-thefts, pattern unconfirmed. The thief takes only what can be spared. Who steals grief gently?*

*Who steals grief gently.* Ilse had read that line a thousand times as a girl, tracing it through the vellum while her mother worked late below, and had understood none of it. She understood it now, which was worse.

Someone in Corvane was stealing memories. Had been for twelve years. And whoever they were, they were good enough to make Ilse's entire division look like children hunting a ghost with butterfly nets.

"Sir?" Corporal Ansel poked his head in. He was twenty-three, earnest, and possessed the unfortunate habit of announcing himself to furniture before noticing people in it. "The market informants are in place. Merrow's stall opens at dusk."

"Doss Merrow." Ilse said the name the way other people said *boil lanced*. "Our fence."

"Our fence, sir. Allegedly."

"Ansel, the man has sold stolen memories out of a pickle cart for eleven years. There is nothing alleged about it except possibly the pickles."

"Right, sir." Ansel paused. "Is it true he once sold a memory of the Lord Protector falling into a fountain?"

"It is true that such a charm briefly circulated, and true that I confiscated every copy, and true that I watched them all burn personally and took no pleasure in it whatsoever." She had taken a small pleasure in it. She was not proud of the small pleasure. "Dusk, then. We move at dusk."

* * *

The memory market did not look like much, which was the point of it.

From the street it was a lane of tarpaulins and lantern-light behind the tanneries, where fishwives sold actual fish and nobody sold anything else, officially. But if you knew to ask for *the quiet stock*, if you hummed three notes of the old harvest hymn — banned, of course, but everyone over forty still knew it — a curtain would twitch and a price would be named.

Ilse went in wearing a borrowed coat and her mother's face, which was to say: tired, unremarkable, and faintly disappointed by everything. It was an excellent disguise. Her mother had used it for twenty years.

Merrow's stall smelled of brine and something sweeter underneath — the ozone tang of fresh-worked charms. He sat behind a pickle barrel like a toad behind a stone, a small bald man with spectacles thick enough to start fires and fingers stained blue from charm-glass.

"Evening," Ilse rasped. "I'm looking for something to forget."

"Aren't we all, mistress." Merrow beamed. "What'll it be? Bad marriage? Bad debt? Bad year generally? I've got a general-purpose forgetting, very popular, mild side effects."

"Side effects?"

"You might also lose your taste for cilantro. It's a risk we all take." He leaned forward, lowering his voice to a conspirator's whisper that carried perfectly to the four Wardens stationed around his stall. "Or perhaps — for a discerning customer — something rarer. Something remembered, rather than forgotten. I have a piece here of the last festival before the ban. Lanterns on the river. Real light in it, not the sad kind."

"That's pre-ban magic," Ilse said, letting her natural voice come back up through the disguise like a blade through water.

Merrow's smile stayed fixed. His eyes went to the exits. All four exits contained Wardens.

"Mistress," he said carefully, "if this is what it appears to be, I'd like the record to show that my pickles are entirely legitimate."

"The record will show whatever I write in it." Ilse stepped aside so the lantern caught her badge. Around the lane, cloaks dropped, and the quiet market discovered, all at once, how loud a crowd could become when frightened. "Doss Merrow, you are under arrest for trafficking in restricted enchantments, conspiracy under the Sanctity Edicts, and selling a memory of the Lord Protector falling into a fountain, which I am adding purely on principle."

"That one wasn't even mine! I was consignment!"

"Tell it to the magistrate."

They took him, the stall, three crates of charms, and — this was the part that mattered — his ledger. Every fence keeps a ledger; it is the vanity of the profession. Merrow wrote down sources, dates, prices, in a cipher so simple Ilse cracked it before they reached the headquarters steps. It was a child's substitution cipher. It had been a child's substitution cipher for eleven years.

She should have felt triumphant. Eleven years, and finally a thread to pull.

Instead, standing in the lamplight with the ledger heavy under her arm, she felt the first cold draft of something else entirely.

Because the cipher decoded to initials. Dozens of them, across hundreds of entries. And every single source was the same initial pair: **F.K.**

* * *

Back in her office, the kettle sang again and this time she let it, mostly because her hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking.

F.K. She turned the letters over like stones. The cipher gave her nothing else — no name, no district, no face. Just F.K., steady as a heartbeat, month after month, year after year. One supplier. One person, feeding half the underworld of Corvane its little mercies, for twelve years.

Twelve years.

The number snagged on something. Ilse set down the ledger and pulled the thirteenth file again, and this time she did not stop at her mother's neat summary pages. She went to the raw reports underneath, the ones her mother had flagged and her superiors had buried, and she read them properly for the first time since she'd inherited the drawer.

The thefts had started twelve years ago. Precisely twelve years ago — within weeks of the night the covenant broke, though no official record connected the two events, because the official record insisted the covenant had simply *faded*, quietly, like an old man in his sleep, rather than torn open like a wound.

And her mother's marginalia — tiny, furious script in the margins, the handwriting of a woman writing things she could not say aloud:

*The thief works small magics. Pre-ban forms. Only a covenant-trained hand could shape these.*

*Checked the registry. Kavanagh line recorded extinct after the Purification. Extinct lines do not sell charms in the Terraces.*

*Ilse, if you are reading this, look closer at what the Order tells us died. Some things are not dead. Some things are waiting.*

Ilse sat very still.

Outside her window, Corvane glittered and smoked and went about its evening, ten thousand hearths lit against the dark, and somewhere in it walked a person marked F.K. — a person whose family name her own order had crossed out in red ink a generation ago and called finished.

Kavanagh. She knew the name the way she knew her own oath, because the oath referenced it. *The covenant of Kavanagh is dissolved; the binding of gods is forbidden; the heirless line is ended.* They made every recruit memorize it. They had made her memorize it at fifteen, standing in the rain, and she had recited it proudly, and not once in nineteen years had she asked the obvious question:

Extinct according to whom?

She thought of her mother, then — really thought of her, not the polished memorial version but the actual woman, who had come home some nights smelling of lamp oil and secrets, who had died in a fire the Order had investigated for exactly six days before closing the file. Her mother had spent her last years pulling at the same thread Ilse now held, and her mother had burned.

That was coincidence. Fires happened. Corvane was full of lamps and carelessness.

Ilse wrote the word *coincidence* in her notebook, stared at it, and then drew a single line through it, which was not evidence of anything, but which made her feel marginally more honest.

* * *

By midnight she had done the thing that would have horrified her younger self: she drafted the warrant before confirming the arrest.

There was no one to arrest yet. There was barely a suspect — initials in a pickle-seller's cipher and a dead woman's marginalia. A proper commander would stake out the market, trace the supply chain, build the case brick by patient brick.

But the baker's son had spoken through a hearth three nights ago, and the holes between realms — she had felt it herself, walking past the ruined aqueduct, a wrongness in the air like a toothache you couldn't locate — were widening. Whatever was coming, it was coming fast, and it would come for a Kavanagh whether or not Ilse found her first.

So she wrote the warrant with the blank space where the given name should be, and filled in the surname in careful block letters:

**KAVANAGH.**

Then, because she was thorough and because her hands needed the work, she pulled the genealogical registers and hunted through the extinction records until she found it — a daughter of the line, born the year before the Purification, entered into the rolls as deceased in the plague winter.

Deceased in a plague that, she noted, checking the parish lists, had killed remarkably few children in that district. Deceased without a death certificate. Deceased the way paperwork makes things deceased: completely, and on paper only.

The girl's given name was Freya.

Ilse said it aloud once, testing the weight of it. It fit the cipher. It fit the timeline. A child smuggled out of an extinct house, raised anonymous in the Terraces, trained — by whom? — in covenant-craft small enough to hide from the Wardens, patient enough to take twelve years to matter.

She should have felt the old righteous heat. This was what she was *for*: the Sanctity Edicts existed because covenant magic had burned half the eastern provinces before the ban, because gods answering prayers unchecked was not mercy but weather — catastrophic, indifferent, worshipped by fools. Her whole life stood on that foundation. Her mother had built it beside her, stone by stone.

Her mother, whose marginalia said *some things are waiting*, and whose last case file ended mid-sentence.

Ilse looked at the unfinished report for a long time. Then she looked at the warrant, at the name she had written, and she understood — with the specific vertigo of a woman finding a crack directly beneath her own feet — that she was about to hunt the last living member of a family her order had helped exterminate, using laws written by men who had paid for their victory in ways the archives were suspiciously thin about describing.

"One thing at a time," she told the kettle, which had gone cold hours ago and offered no argument.

At dawn she posted the warrant in every precinct house in Corvane: *WANTED FOR QUESTIONING — FREYA KAVANAGH. Approach with caution. Report all sightings to the Commander directly.*

And in the privacy of her office, she unlocked the bottom drawer again and did not put her mother's file away. Instead she laid it open on the desk beneath the lamp, next to Merrow's ledger, the two documents facing each other across twelve years like strangers who were about to discover they were related.

Somewhere out there, Freya Kavanagh was reading the notices, or hearing them whispered, and learning that her name — the name she had presumably buried along with her family — was now written on every wall in the city.

Ilse almost pitied her.

Almost.

Because pity required believing the hunt was unjust, and Ilse Varga did not yet know what she believed. She only knew that her mother's last written words were *look closer*, and that for the first time in nineteen years, she intended to disobey the Order's version of looking — and follow her mother's instead.

She snuffed the lamp as the sun rose over Corvane, and in the grey morning light the warrant copies fluttered on their boards like white moths, carrying one extinct name back into the world.

It would take the thief less than a day to see it.

Ilse intended to be watching when she did.
