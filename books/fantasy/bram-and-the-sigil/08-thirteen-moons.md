## 8. Thirteen Moons

Bram had forgotten what it was like to be looked at.

Not worshipped—worship he remembered well, the hot weight of ten thousand upturned faces, the smoke of offerings, the terrible hunger of being needed. Being *looked at* was different. Freya Kavanagh looked at him the way a locksmith looks at a suspicious lock: professionally, skeptically, with one eyebrow already raised in anticipation of the trick.

"So," she said, turning her cup of chicory without drinking it. "You're a god."

"I'm a god," Bram agreed.

"Of what, exactly? You've been dodging that all evening."

"Small things. Thresholds. Doorways. The moment before a decision." He smiled. "I used to have a bigger portfolio. Once upon a covenant."

They sat in the back room of a tea house on Candle Lane, where the lamps burned low and the proprietor was deaf as a stone and twice as incurious. Freya had insisted on the location. "If a Warden walks in," she'd said, "I'll say you're a client who overpaid and I'm letting you down gently." There was a practicality to her that he found quietly marvelous. Thirteen years of watching her through cracks in the world, and she still surprised him.

"You said you wanted explanations," he said. "I should warn you they're worse than mysteries. Mysteries end. Explanations just keep going."

"Try me."

So he told her.

* * *

"The covenant was an agreement," he began, "but that word is too clean. Think of it instead as a seam. Two garments—the mortal realm and the divine—stitched together along every border there is: every threshold, every hearthstone, every prayer spoken honestly into the dark. The stitch-work was done by hand. Your family's hands. The Kavanaghs were the needle."

Freya's cup had gone still. "My family were seamstresses?"

"My family were thread." He let a little of the old ache show, because she trusted plainness more than poetry. "There were twelve of us then. Twelve gods, each bound to a house of stewards. My brothers and sisters held the storms and the harvests and the deep water. I held the small mercies—a kind word at the right time, a door opened for a stranger, the last coin shared instead of hoarded. Nobody builds monuments to those. Which is why I survived what came."

"What came?"

"A bargain made behind our backs. One of us—" He paused. Names had weight, and this name had teeth. "One of my siblings went to the founders of your Wardens and offered them a trade: sever the seam, ban the old magics, outlaw the prayers, and the gods would be locked out of mortal affairs forever. No more miracles to fear. No more whimsy from above. In exchange, the founder received... payment. The god of small mercies can be very persuasive when he wants to corrupt something gentle."

"You're talking about yourself," Freya said flatly.

"I'm talking about myself," Bram confirmed, "and I'd thank you not to look at me like that. I was younger. I thought I was being clever. The mortals wanted safety; I wanted rest; the deal seemed elegant. It took me a hundred years to understand that what I'd actually done was lock a door with everyone still inside both rooms."

She did look at him like that. Worse—she looked at him the way she must have looked at clients who came to sell memories of their own worst days, with a pity that refused to flatter.

"Thirteen years ago," she said. "That's when the answers started. That's when the seam started tearing."

"Thirteen years ago the last binding knot failed. Your grandmother's work, mostly—it was magnificent, and I'm not saying that to flatter a dead woman. But knots fail, Freya. Slowly, then all at once. And now the two realms are no longer stitched together but merely pressed against each other, like millstones set turning." He drew two coins from his pocket and spun them against each other on the table's scarred wood, grinding them slowly around in a circle. "Grinding. That's what answered prayers are. Pressure escaping through the gaps. The baker's dead son speaking through his mother's hearth isn't a miracle—it's friction. The realms are wearing each other thin, and everything loose falls between."

"And the thirteenth moon?"

"When the thirteenth moon completes its turn—thirteen turns from the first failure, which will be soon now—the seam gives out entirely. The realms won't simply drift apart. They'll grind until one devours the other. Either the divine swallows the mortal world and everything becomes symbol, or the mortal world swallows the divine and everything becomes..." He searched for the word and disliked all of them. "Gray. Everything becomes gray."

The coins stopped spinning. He left them lying together on the table, edges worn bright.

"And only a Kavanagh can reforge it," Freya said. Not a question.

"Only a Kavanagh. The stitching has to be done from both sides at once, by hands that carry the sigil-blood and the covenant-oath. Your line was supposed to be extinct. The Wardens hunted you like wolves after a last deer." He met her eyes. "I knew they'd missed one. A child smuggled out of the Kavanagh house the night it burned, raised by a woman named Maren Kavanagh who taught you card tricks and never once told you why your palms itched during thunderstorms."

Freya's face did something complicated. Grief and anger and, beneath them, a fierce relief she clearly hated herself for feeling—to finally know *why*, after a lifetime of being strange in ways no one would name.

"How do you know all this about me?" she asked quietly.

"Because thirteen years ago, I gave away my last true memory to a dying woman for safekeeping. She was the keeper of the covenant line, and I trusted her with the one thing I couldn't afford to lose: the memory of why any of this mattered. She hid it inside a charm and carried it until the Wardens came for her, and then she passed it to her granddaughter without telling her what it was." He spread his hands. "The first memory you ever stole, Freya—or thought you stole, that night at her deathbed when you took a flicker of light from a dying woman's mind because you were sixteen and terrified and it was the only thing you knew how to do—that was mine. You've been carrying my heart in your pocket for thirteen years."

The silence stretched long enough that the deaf proprietor shuffled past their door twice, checking on nothing.

"That memory," Freya said at last, and her voice was very careful, the voice of someone walking across ice while pretending to admire the view. "The one with the light in it. When I lifted it, I felt—I don't have words. It was like holding my hand over a candle and liking the burn. Is that normal? For stolen memories?"

"No."

"Good," she said. "Because if that's normal, my whole career has been even more of a disaster than I thought."

Despite everything—the millstones, the moons, the devouring—he laughed. It startled him. He hadn't laughed in a century, and it came out rusty, like a gate that hadn't been oiled.

* * *

She asked the questions he'd known she would, and he honored each one, because she deserved the truth plainly given.

"Why didn't you come to me sooner?" was the first. Because he had been afraid, he told her. Gods make poor cowards, but excellent ones. Because every year he delayed was another year she stayed safe in her small invisible life, and safety is the most seductive lie a coward can tell.

"Why me?" was the second. Why not some braver heir, some worthier bloodline. Because there were no other heirs. Because worthiness is a story people tell after the fact, and the truth is only ever: *someone must, and here you are.* He softened it as much as honesty allowed. "Your grandmother chose to teach you the craft without the burden. That wasn't cruelty. That was a mother hoping her child could be happy. I'm sorry it didn't hold."

"Why should I believe any of this?" was the third, and the most important, and he loved her for asking it. He showed her, instead of arguing: let her see the seam for herself, briefly, through his eyes—the shimmering wound in the air above the rooftops where Corvane's chimney smoke bent wrong, where the moonlight fell a half-second late. She went pale and gripped the table and did not scream, which told him more about her than any charm ever had.

And then, near the end, she asked the question he hadn't prepared for, because he was a god of thresholds and should have known better than to think himself past surprise.

"What happens to you?" she asked. "If the mortal realm wins. If the divine gets devoured. What happens to the twelve of you?"

"Eleven," he said. "We're eleven now. And what happens is nothing. That's rather the point of being devoured."

She studied him for a long moment. "You're going to help me fix this anyway. Even though fixing it might mean your end."

"It almost certainly means my end."

"Then why?"

Bram looked at his hands—hands that had opened ten thousand doors for strangers, and closed one very large one, and spent a hundred years regretting the closing. "Because I built the lock," he said, "and I would like, before the end, to build the key."

Something shifted in her expression. It was small—the softening of a lamp-flame, barely a thing—but Bram had spent eternity reading small things, and he knew tenderness when it finally turned toward him.

* * *

They left the tea house near midnight, because Freya insisted gods kept unreasonable hours and she needed to sleep like a mortal, and because, she said, "if you're going to recruit me into apocalypse prevention, you can at least walk me home." He walked her home. It was, he reflected, the best errand he had run in a hundred years.

Corvane at midnight wore its holes openly now. Above Tanner's Row a window hung in the empty air, glowing faintly, and through it came the smell of bread from a bakery that had burned down forty years ago. A cat sat beneath it, tail curled, waiting patiently for a hand that belonged to no one living. Freya watched it pass and said nothing, but her shoulder brushed his as they walked, and stayed there.

"You should know something," she said as they reached her street. "About the charms. What I've taken, over the years. What I keep." Her jaw worked. "If we're doing this—if I'm going to be your seamstress—I should probably confess that I'm not a good person, Bram. I'm a thief. I steal grief from people who need it less than I decided they did, and I keep the best pieces, and some nights I take them out and live in them because my own life doesn't have enough in it. That's who you've recruited."

"Yes," Bram said.

"Yes, you knew, or yes, I'm a bad person?"

"Yes, I know exactly who I've recruited. I watched you give half your week's earnings to a beggar on Wren Street three winters ago and then tell him you were overpaid anyway so he wouldn't feel indebted." He paused at her doorstep—which was, naturally, a threshold, and therefore his, and he felt it hum under his palm like a plucked string. "You steal memories, Freya, because you understand better than anyone alive what they're worth. That's not villainy. That's an education."

She stared at him. "You've been watching me."

"I've been watching you for thirteen years," he admitted. "It's less romantic than it sounds. Mostly it was grim. Some of it was you falling off a roof."

"I did *not* fall off a roof."

"The Tallowman's warehouse. Four years ago. You fell with tremendous dignity and swore at a pigeon."

The look she gave him could have curdled milk, and under it, unmistakably, the corner of her mouth was fighting a war it was losing. "Go away, Bram."

"Tomorrow," he said, stepping back from her door with genuine reluctance. "There's a third thing I haven't told you. About what reforging costs. I'll tell you tomorrow, when you've slept, because it's the kind of truth that keeps better after a night's rest."

Her hand was already on the latch. She stopped. "Bram. Whatever it is—don't soften it."

"I won't."

"Don't protect me from it because I'm the mortal and you're the ancient whatever-you-are. I've survived worse than information."

"I know," he said. "That's rather the problem."

She frowned at him, wanting more, and he gave her the only honest smile he had left—the tired one—and vanished the way gods do, which is to say he stepped backward through the threshold of her door and let the world forget to notice him.

But he did not leave. He stood in the space between her doorway and the night, an old god leaning against an old wall, and listened to her move around her small rooms, banking her fire, counting her charms, muttering—at length and with real creativity—about pigeons.

Thirteen years ago the seam had torn, and tonight, for the first time since, he had hope, and hope was a terrible thing for a god to carry. It had weight. It had edges. It pressed against everything else inside him and rearranged it.

He counted the moons in his head, as he did every night now. Eleven turns fallen. Two remained.

And below him, in the city's deepest dark, something that called itself the god of small mercies was also counting—and smiling.

Bram straightened, felt the threshold hum beneath his feet like a struck bell, and went to find Freya a roof she hadn't fallen off yet. Tomorrow, the drowned chapel. Tomorrow, the first freely given thing.

Tonight, he allowed himself one last minute at her door, guarding a threshold, as he was made to do.
