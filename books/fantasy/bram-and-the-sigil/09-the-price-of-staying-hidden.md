## 9. The Price of Staying Hidden

Freya had always believed that the trick to surviving Corvane was the same as the trick to stealing a memory well: you made yourself small. You made yourself nothing. You became a shadow at the edge of a lamplighter's vision, a smudge on a window, a face that no one could describe afterward even if you paid them.

For eleven years, being nobody had kept her alive.

She was halfway through a pot of barley tea in her room above the tannery when she smelled the smoke, and it took her an embarrassingly long moment to understand that this time the smoke was not coming from her own chimney.

She leaned out the window and saw the sky over Lantern Row glowing orange, and her first thought—her shameful, honest, unfiltered first thought—was: *the market*.

Her second thought was: *Maren.*

She was down the stairs and out the door before the third thought arrived, which was probably just as well, because the third thought would have been something careful and calculating about not getting involved, and there are moments when the body is wiser than the mind.

* * *

Lantern Row was burning.

Not all of it—the Wardens were precise people, and they had learned precision from twelve years of hunting things smaller than treason. They had set alight only the stalls, only the market itself, the rickety wooden guts of the place where Freya had spent four hundred-odd nights selling forgetfulness by the dram. The buildings around it stood untouched, their tenants pressed to their windows with shutters cracked open, watching the heart of their little economy go up like a festival bonfire nobody had asked for.

The heat hit her at the mouth of the row, a wall of it, thick with the smell of charred pine and scorched cloth and something sweeter underneath that she refused to name—the smell of charms cooking off, memories boiling out of their vials and evaporating into the night air. Somewhere in that smoke, she knew, were forty years of a cooper's grief for his dead wife, drifting up free. A child's birthday, unwinding into the dark. Someone's whole wedding day, rising like sparks.

Wardens lined both ends of the street in their grey lacquered coats, faces hidden behind visor-masks, holding back the gathering crowd with crossed staves. And between the staves and the fire, in neat rows, knelt the people of the market.

Freya counted them before she could stop herself—it was the habit of a woman who priced everything. Thirty-one kneeling. Old Tobbe the fence, who watered his beer and cheated strangers but never once raised his prices for a widow. Sela and her two daughters, who sold candied ginger two stalls down and had never asked what Freya kept under her counter. The pie-man, Doss. The knife-grinder whose name Freya realized, with a lurch, she had never actually learned, though he'd sharpened her paring knife every month for six years.

They were putting iron collars on them. Not the execution kind—not yet. The *questioning* kind, dull grey bands that hummed faintly, the sort that recorded every word spoken near them for later review. The sort you wore for weeks while men in quiet rooms read your life back to you looking for crimes.

Tobbe was arguing. Of course Tobbe was arguing; Tobbe would have argued with the flood. A Warden cuffed him across the ear without ceremony and Freya watched the old man's head snap sideways and felt something in her chest turn over like a key.

She should run. Every instinct she had honed since childhood said *run, be smoke, be nobody.* She was three streets from safety and one shadow from invisibility, and if she turned around now she would live.

She turned around.

She got exactly eleven steps toward the crowd before a hand closed on her arm—not a Warden's gauntlet but a workman's grip, calloused and urgent—and pulled her into the recessed doorway of a chandler's shop.

"Are you *mad?*" Maren hissed.

Maren. Alive. Smelling of woodsmoke and her own cheap rosewater, her gray hair half-down, her eyes furious and wet at the same time.

"I was looking for you," Freya said stupidly.

"Well, don't look for me in front of armed men, girl, I raised you better than—" Maren's voice cracked. She pulled Freya deeper into the doorway, wrapped both arms around her, and held on hard enough to hurt. "I thought they'd taken you. I saw the fire and I thought, they've finally found our Freya."

Our Freya. As though Freya belonged to anyone. As though belonging were a thing you could survive having.

"I'm fine," Freya said into her shoulder. "Maren, listen to me, you need to leave the city tonight. If they're collaring people, if Varga is working lists—"

"I'm not leaving."

"Maren—"

"Forty years," Maren said, pulling back to glare at her, which was a formidable thing to be on the receiving end of. "Forty years I have lived in this city, and I have buried two husbands and one daughter under its stones, and I am not leaving because some Warden has decided a woman who sells ginger candy is a threat to the realm." Her chin came up. "Besides. Somebody has to stay and say a kind word over all of this tomorrow."

And that—that small, stubborn, absurd decency of hers—was what finally did it. Not the fire, not the collars, not Tobbe's bloodied ear. It was Maren planning kindnesses for the morning after an atrocity, because that was simply what Maren did, and Freya looked at her and thought with sudden sick clarity: *she is only safe because I am small. Everyone in that line is only safe because I have spent eleven years making sure none of this ever touches me.*

The Wardens hadn't found her. That was the thing sitting in her stomach like a swallowed stone. Ilse Varga had raided the market, arrested her neighbors, burned her livelihood—and Freya Kavanagh, the actual thief, the actual heir of whatever cursed bloodline had started all of this, had slept through the entire event and arrived late, unharmed, unnoticed.

Invisibility had worked perfectly.

That was the horror of it. It had worked *perfectly*, and everyone around her was wearing iron.

* * *

She got Maren home—arguing the whole way, gently, the way you argue with someone you love—and then, against every survival instinct she owned, she went back.

Not into the square. Into the alleys behind it, where the market's back-of-house lived: the storage sheds, the sleeping lofts, the little bolt-holes where people like Freya kept the things too precious or too illegal to keep at home. If the Wardens were thorough, these would already be found. But Wardens were thorough the way engineers were thorough—they followed the plans. And Freya had built most of the plans.

She moved through the dark checking caches. Tobbe's ledger, gone—taken, along with Tobbe. Her own emergency kit under the loose stone behind the pie-shop: intact, untouched, *they didn't know about it*, and she felt no relief at all, only a cold spreading hollowness, because of course they didn't know about it. Nobody ever knew about her. That was the whole architecture of her life.

She was crouched at the last cache—a hollow in the aqueduct's footing where she kept the charms she'd never managed to sell—when the light caught her.

It came from inside the hollow. From the stolen memory she still couldn't sell: the flicker of gold-white radiance, the prayer answered, Bram's voice saying *given willingly, long ago.* She'd wrapped it in lead foil and three wards and it still glowed through all of it now, brighter than before, pulsing gently like something breathing.

Like something listening.

"You," Freya whispered to it. "This is your fault."

The charm pulsed again, almost apologetic.

Behind her, up on the aqueduct's spine, a figure in grey turned the corner of the parapet—a sentry she hadn't marked, doing rounds she hadn't known about. His lantern swept down. In three seconds it would find her, crouched over contraband, in the shadow of a raid, wearing a face that Ilse Varga had already named in ink on a warrant.

Freya did the sensible thing. She grabbed the charm, sealed the hollow, and ran.

The sentry shouted. Boots hammered the parapet above her as she ducked under the arch and cut through the tanner's yard, over the wall she'd climbed a hundred times, into the labyrinth of lanes behind Cooper's Row where she'd been dodging authority since she was seven years old. She knew every gap in every fence. She was three turns clear and slowing to a walk when she heard the second shout, and then the third, and understood with a drop of her stomach that they weren't chasing her anymore.

They were chasing *someone else*. Someone smaller. Someone slower.

She should have kept walking. She wants it noted, in whatever court eventually judges her, that she genuinely considered keeping walking. Every lesson of her life voted for it. But her feet had already stopped, and then they'd already turned, and then she was rounding the corner to find two Wardens backing a boy against a wall—a boy of maybe twelve, soot-faced, one of the market children who ran messages and swept floors and called her "the charm-lady" with the solemn respect of a professional addressing a colleague.

He had her emergency kit in his hands. He'd pulled it from her hollow. He'd been running *for her*, carrying her secrets, because someone had once been decent to him and he'd repaid it without doing the arithmetic.

"Please," the boy was saying. "Please, it's not mine, I was just—"

"We'll see whose it is," said the nearer Warden, and reached for his collar-band.

Later, Freya would have trouble reconstructing exactly what she did, because it happened in the peculiar bright silence of a person stepping off a cliff. She remembered stepping out of the alley. She remembered saying, in her steadiest market voice, "Put that down, it's mine." She remembered both Wardens turning, and the strange floating sensation of watching herself choose, deliberately, to be *seen*.

What she did not remember—what she could not explain—was the light. It came off the charm in her pocket like sunrise through a keyhole, one heartbeat of gold-white, and both Wardens flinched back with their hands to their visors, and in that heartbeat of blindness Freya took the kit from the boy, shoved him toward the alley, and said, "Run."

He ran. She ran the other way, on legs that shook, laughing in a high unhinged register she didn't recognize as laughter, and it wasn't until she'd put six streets and a locked door between herself and the night that she stopped in her own dark room, leaned against the wall, and let the shaking have her.

Because she had seen the Wardens' faces in that flash of light. Seen them through their open visors. And they hadn't looked afraid of a thief.

They'd looked afraid of a god.

* * *

She didn't sleep. Toward the coldest hour she sat at her window with the charm in her cupped hands, watching it breathe its slow gold light, and did sums.

Thirty-one collared. A market burned. A warrant somewhere bearing her true name, spelled correctly, which meant Varga knew *Kavanagh*, knew what it meant, knew more than Freya herself had known a month ago. And a boy of twelve had run into fire for her because she'd once given his mother a charm for free, and her whole grand strategy—the smallness, the shadow, the eleven careful years—had added up, in the end, to other people paying her bill.

"That was always the trade, wasn't it," she told the charm. "Other people pay. I hide. Very tidy."

The charm glowed. It did that a lot lately. She was beginning to suspect it was less an object than an opinion.

Somewhere out there, Bram was presumably lurking under some archway with his patient eyes and his unbearable calm, waiting for her to come to her senses about sigil-stones and covenants and moons. He'd want to comfort her. That was the worst part—he'd be *kind* about it, and she'd let him be kind, and kindness was how gods got their hooks into people.

But the fury had arrived now, and it was better than the shame, and it wanted things.

It wanted Tobbe out of a cell. It wanted Sela's daughters home. It wanted Ilse Varga—who had, Freya reminded herself, personally signed the order that burned four hundred nights of her work—to learn precisely what it cost to make an enemy of a woman with nothing left to lose except a name she was finished hiding behind.

And underneath the fury, quieter, in the place where she kept the things she didn't sell: it wanted the thirteenth moon to find her standing in the open.

"All right," she said to the charm, and to the city beyond the glass, and to whatever was listening. "You win. Find him."

The light flared once—bright, glad, unmistakably *answered*—and went still, like a held breath.

Far away across the rooftops, beneath the ruined span of the old aqueduct, something ancient opened its eyes.
