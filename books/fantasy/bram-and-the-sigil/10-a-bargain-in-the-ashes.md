## 10. A Bargain in the Ashes

The memory market smelled of cardamom and candle wax for eleven years. Now it smelled of nothing but char.

Freya picked her way through the ruin the morning after, when the ash had cooled enough to walk on but not enough to stop stinging her throat with every breath. The stalls that had sold candied ginger and secondhand prayers were blackened skeletons. Old Marta's charm-table lay split down the middle like a dropped plate. Somewhere under the ash, Freya knew, were hundreds of little glass vials, their woven memories boiled away into smoke — a first kiss from the spring of some year she'd never know, gone up like a moth.

She should not have come back. Every sensible instinct told her so. Ilse Varga would have eyes on this place; burning it was not an ending but an announcement, the way a hunter burns brush to see what runs out. But Freya had come anyway, because three of her clients lived in the tenements ringing the market square, and because Tam the fence had a wife who didn't know where he'd been taken, and because — though she'd never have said it aloud, not even to herself, not quite — because if you spent eleven years building something small and secret and yours, you owed it at least one visit to its corpse.

She knelt where her own stall had been and sifted ash through her fingers. A scrap of blue silk, half-burnt. The copper tray she'd used to lay charms out in rows, warped into a shallow bowl. Nothing else.

"You're sitting in my offering," said a voice behind her. "It's considered rude."

Freya came up fast, a knife already in her hand — old habits, good ones — and found herself facing a small man in gray robes sitting on Marta's broken table as though he'd been there since before the fire, which was impossible, because Marta's table had not existed since roughly midnight.

He was unremarkable in the way of people you can never describe afterward: medium height, medium years, a kind face slightly worn at the edges, like a coin rubbed smooth by too many hopeful hands. His eyes were brown. Or perhaps gray. They kept settling into whatever color you expected them to be.

"I don't make offerings," Freya said.

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "That's rather the whole trouble with you."

* * *

Bram had told her once — in one of those stolen hours she was trying very hard not to count, because counting them made them finite — that gods did not appear to mortals unless invited, and that invitations could be as small as a wish. Freya examined the man in gray and understood, with a lurch, that kneeling in the ashes of her life's work, she must have wished *something*. Even just: *let this not have been for nothing.*

Gods, apparently, took attendance.

"You're him," she said. "The small mercies one."

"At your service." He spread his hands. "I'm the god of finding your keys when you've looked everywhere twice. Of the last coin in your pocket being enough. Of rain holding off until you're under the awning." His smile was gentle. It was a very good smile. She distrusted it immediately and completely, and even so felt her shoulders drop half an inch, because it had been a long night and kindness, even counterfeit, is warm to stand near. "I'm also the god of mercy granted quickly, which is why I've come. You look exhausted, Miss Kavanagh."

"Don't use that name in the open."

"It's rather late for whispering, wouldn't you say? Varga knows it. Soon the whole city will know it. *Kavanagh*, the last of the covenant line, hiding in a market stall for eleven years, selling other people's childhoods at two coppers apiece." He clicked his tongue softly, not unkindly. "What a waste of a bloodline."

Freya's grip tightened on the knife. "If you're here to threaten me—"

"Threaten you? Child, I'm here to *rescue* you." He hopped down from the table, brushing ash from his robe, and the ash, she noticed, did not stick to him. Nothing about him quite touched. "Consider the situation honestly. Three stones scattered across Corvane, each guarded by things older and hungrier than me. A Warden-Commander who has your true name and twelve years of patience. A thirteenth moon coming, and beneath it, a covenant only a Kavanagh can reforge — by a ritual that will ask everything of you and give back less. And what do you get at the end?" He tilted his head. "Do you know? Has Bram told you the price?"

"He's told me enough."

"He's told you nothing, or you wouldn't still be following him." The god's voice went soft, and softness, Freya thought wildly, should be illegal in a voice that size. "Here is my offer. No quest. No guardians. No drowned chapels, no Warden dungeons, no moon grinding against the sky like a millstone. Give me your stolen memories — all of them, every fragment you've hoarded these eleven years — and I will use them to seal a covenant myself. A good one. A fair one. You'll wake tomorrow with clean hands and an empty pack, and no one hunting you, and no one expecting anything of you ever again. Isn't that what you've wanted? Since you were a girl?"

And there it was. Freya stood very still among the ashes and let the offer settle over her like snowfall, because she was honest with herself in at least one thing: it was beautiful.

Eleven years of fragments sat coiled behind her ribs. She had taken them the way a magpie takes shine — a mother's lullaby here, a soldier's last sight of home there, a baker's memory of his daughter's laugh, a widow's one afternoon by the sea. She kept pieces of everything she stole; that was how the craft worked, the thief's tithe. Nobody had warned her that a person could be built out of other people's leftovers until there wasn't much room left for the original architecture. Some mornings she woke and had to lie still a moment, working out which memories were hers. Some mornings the sea-afternoon felt more like home than any room she'd slept in.

To be empty of it. To put the pack down.

"You'd seal it yourself," she said carefully. "A god. Sealing a covenant meant to bind gods. Do you hear how that sounds?"

"I hear how it sounds to someone who's slept four hours in three days," the god said kindly. "It sounds like salvation. That's because it is." He took a step closer. Ash did not mark his feet. "Freya. You are tired. You have been tired since you were nine years old. Let someone else carry it."

* * *

"The trick with him," Bram had said, three nights ago, over burnt bread in the aqueduct's shadow, "isn't that he lies. Anyone can spot a liar. It's that he tells truths. Every word out of his mouth will be accurate. He'll simply arrange them like a merchant arranges fruit — the best side up, the rot hidden underneath."

"How do I fight that?"

Bram had turned the bread in his scarred hands, and his face had done something complicated. "You don't fight it. You just remember that a truth offered at the right moment can wound worse than any lie. When he comes — and he'll come, Freya, you're exactly the kind of tired he harvests — ask yourself what he isn't saying. Not whether his words are true. Whether they're *whole*."

Now, standing in the ashes, Freya heard the echo and looked for the missing fruit.

"What happens to the memories?" she asked.

The god blinked. "I beg your pardon?"

"The ones I give you. The lullabies and the sea-afternoons and all of it. What do you do with them?"

"They become part of the covenant. Woven into the sealing. It's a great honor—"

"For whom?"

The pause was very small. Someone not trained to read pauses — eleven years of reading faces across a market table, eleven years of knowing which client was about to haggle and which was about to weep — would have missed it entirely.

"For everyone," the god said smoothly. "For the city. For both realms."

"And the people those memories came from? If you weave their mothers' voices into a binding, do they feel it? Does the widow lose her afternoon by the sea twice — once when I stole it, once when you spend it?"

"Freya." Her name in his mouth was warm as a hand on her shoulder. She had to consciously stop herself from leaning into it. That was the horror of him, she realized; he felt like rest. He felt like the end of a long shift, like the moment the fever breaks. "You're asking questions to delay an answer you already know you want to give. I understand. But consider: every day you refuse, Varga tightens the net. Yesterday she burned a market. Tomorrow? Your neighbors are already in Warden cells answering questions about a woman with dark hair and quick hands. How many more, do you think? How much ash before you accept mercy?"

And there — there it was, the rot under the fruit. Because it was true. All of it was true. Tam was in a cell. Marta's stall was charcoal. The net *was* tightening, and she *was* tired, bone-tired in a way sleep didn't reach, and every word he said was a lamp lit in a dark room.

But he had said *your neighbors are answering questions about a woman with dark hair.* And Freya had watched Ilse Varga work for eleven years from the corners of rooms. The Wardens didn't need descriptions anymore. They had her name, her trade, her territory. Whatever happened next would happen because of what she was, not what her neighbors had seen — and the god knew that perfectly well, and had said it anyway, because frightened people make faster bargains than guilty ones.

"You're rushing me," she said quietly.

"I'm *helping* you."

"You're rushing me. That's how I know the deal is bad. Good deals can wait an hour." She sheathed the knife — slowly, deliberately, a thing done in front of witnesses — and looked up into his kind, shifting eyes. "No. I won't sell you my pack of scraps so you can build a cage out of them. I stole those memories, and that makes them mine to answer for, and I'll answer for them properly or not at all."

Something moved behind the god's face. For half a heartbeat the kindness fell away and she saw what was underneath: something vast and cold and practical, an accountant's eye measuring a loss. Then it was gone, folded up neat as a peddler's cloth, and the warmth returned, undimmed, which was somehow worse.

"Well," he said. "Worth the asking."

"Get off her table," Freya said, nodding at Marta's broken stall, "and get out of my market."

"It's rather everyone's market now. That's the nature of fire." He straightened his robe. "You'll want to know where the drowned chapel is. Second stone, sunken nave, singing congregation — Bram will pretend he doesn't know the way until you're desperate enough to seem grateful. Ask him instead about the third time he failed a Kavanagh. He counts differently than I do, but he counts." He began to walk backward into the ash-fall, and the ash swallowed him edge first, robe then hands then face, like a man sinking into gray water. "Change your mind, Miss Kavanagh, and wish for me. Everyone does eventually. It's why I keep the door open."

Then there was only the market, and the ash, and a silence with teeth in it.

* * *

Freya didn't realize she was shaking until Bram caught her elbow.

He'd come from the direction of the aqueduct with his hood up and his jaw set, and he must have arrived near the end, because his hand was steady and furious on her arm, and his eyes were fixed on the empty air where the god had been standing.

"How much did you hear?" Her voice came out scraped.

"All of it. Enough." He turned her gently, checking her over as if the god might have left fingerprints. "Did you touch him?"

"No."

"Good. Did you *want* to?"

She laughed, and it came out wrong, wet at the edges. "Bram, I wanted to so badly my knees hurt. Eleven years of other people's memories, gone in one bargain, no quest, no moon, no—" She stopped. Breathed. "Is that what it feels like around you? Around him? Like being forgiven in advance for anything?"

"Yes," Bram said simply. "That's his whole nature. Mercy without cost, mercy without judgment. It sounds lovely." His thumb moved once over her wrist, absent, unconscious, and Freya firmly declined to notice it. "It's mercy the way a flood is water."

"He said you'd lead me to the drowned chapel and pretend not to know the way."

"He's not wrong about much. That's rather his problem." Bram looked out over the ruined stalls, the split table, the warped copper tray Freya still held in one fist without remembering picking it up. His voice lowered. "He offered you the shortcut. Everything now, for your hoard. And you said no."

"I said no."

"Why?"

It was a real question, not a compliment dressed as one, and it deserved better than a shrug. Freya looked down at the copper tray, at her own smudged reflection bent in the warped metal — a tired woman with ash in her dark hair, holding a piece of her dead market like a shield.

"Because he asked for the memories like they were mine to give," she said slowly, working it out as she spoke. "But they're not, not really. They're the widow's sea and the baker's daughter and Marta's — whoever Marta's were. I took them. That debt is mine. Handing the whole sack to another thief doesn't settle it, it just changes whose ledger it sits in." She looked up at him. "Also because he paused when I asked who the weaving honored. Half a heartbeat. You said watch for what's missing from his truths. That pause was a hole big enough to drive a funeral through."

Bram was quiet a moment. When he spoke, his voice had gone rough. "Thirteen centuries I've watched Kavanaghs face that thing. Some took the bargain. I've always wondered if any of them ever worked out why they shouldn't before it was too late." He pulled his hood back. Ash drifted between them, slow as snow. "The chapel next, then. The second stone. It's under the old Harrow district, thirty feet down in water that remembers everyone who ever sang in it, and it will want a payment I should have warned you about sooner."

Freya closed her fingers around the warped tray, set it down gently atop the ashes of her stall — a grave-goods offering, the only kind she'd ever made — and turned to go.

"Bram," she said. "Who did you fail? The third time?"

He didn't break stride, but she saw his shoulders take the question like a blow taken squarely.

"Ask me after the water," he said. "If we come back up."

Behind them, far across the ruined square, one of the burnt-out stalls shifted — just a settling of char, surely, just heat leaving bones — and from somewhere beneath the ash, faint and patient, came the sound of a door being propped open.
