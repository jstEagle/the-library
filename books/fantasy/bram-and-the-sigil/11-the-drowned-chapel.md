## 11. The Drowned Chapel

*Freya*

The chapel had been drowning for a hundred years, and it had made no effort to be graceful about it.

"It's under there?" Freya peered over the edge of the flooded nave. The water was the color of old pewter, still as glass, and somewhere beneath its surface she could just make out the pale suggestion of an archway, like a hand reaching up through the dark.

"Under there," Bram confirmed. He was crouched at the water's edge, rolling his sleeves with the unhurried air of a man who had done this so many times the novelty had worn clean off. "The second stone sits on the altar. The first one was guarded by your worst memory. This one—" he paused, choosing his words with visible care, "—this one asks for something else."

"Of course it does." Freya sat down heavily on a fallen pillar and began unlacing her boots. "Why can't anything in this city simply *be* somewhere? Why does everything have to want something from me?"

"That does seem to be the theme of your year."

"Don't be funny at me before dawn. I'm not equipped for it yet."

He laughed — that low, startled laugh he seemed to produce whenever she caught him off guard, as though amusement were a language he'd half-forgotten and was delighted to find he still spoke. She was beginning to collect those laughs the way she used to collect memories: greedily, secretly, hoarding each one.

She pulled her boots off and set them side by side, then her coat, folded with the precision of habit. Old habits. A thief always knew where her things were, because a thief who didn't came home to empty pockets.

"The water's cold," Bram said.

"I assumed."

"Colder than you're assuming."

"Bram." She looked at him. "I grew up sleeping in a drain behind the tannery. I have been cold in ways you cannot imagine and would not enjoy hearing about."

Something flickered across his face at that — not pity, exactly. Something older and sadder. "Right," he said quietly. "Let's go, then."

They waded in together.

The cold hit her like a slap from someone she respected. It drove the breath out of her in a white gasp, and then the floor dropped away beneath her feet and she was swimming down through water gone suddenly black, past a drowned bell rope swaying like kelp, past a window of colored glass that glowed faintly even here, even now, casting red-and-gold light across the silt like embers scattered on a grave.

And she heard the singing.

It rose up around her from below — dozens of voices, soft and layered, a hymn she almost recognized. Not frightening, not at first. Just terribly, achingly *human*. Voices that had once belonged to people who sang this same hymn on dry land, in good coats, with coughs and colds and complaints about the sermon.

*Bram,* she thought, and felt him take her hand in the dark. His grip said: *I know. I hear them too.*

They descended toward the light.

* * *

*Bram*

He had watched cities drown before. That was the thing about being what he was — you accumulated drownings the way other men accumulated debts.

But he had never watched Freya Kavanagh see one.

She surfaced inside the chapel's flooded nave, hair streaming, eyes wide, and instead of fear he saw something worse: recognition. She looked at the pews furred with silt, the altar ahead glowing faintly where the sigil-stone sat embedded like a heart in a chest of stone, and then she looked at the singers.

They stood between the pews. Dozens of them. Waterlogged and gentle, their faces soft with the particular peace of the long-drowned, their mouths moving in hymn. They did not reach for the living. They did not threaten. They only sang, and the song moved through the water the way warmth moves through a wall.

"They're still here," Freya breathed. Bubbles escaped her lips; the chapel let her speak anyway, some courtesy of the place. "Bram — they've been singing down here for a hundred years and nobody ever heard them."

"No," he said. "Nobody ever listened."

Her jaw tightened. He knew that look. It was the look she got whenever the world revealed another piece of itself that had been abandoned by everyone whose job it was to care. He had learned, in these weeks of stone-hunting, to read her face like weather. Storm coming. Fury incoming. Someone was about to be held accountable, if only in the privacy of her own heart.

"The stone," she said. "Can we just—"

"Not yet." He touched her arm. "Look at the altar."

Between them and the sigil-stone, the water had begun to change. It thickened, silvered, and shaped itself into a figure — not one of the drowned, but something older, a presence without a face, wearing the chapel like a robe. When it spoke, its voice was the hymn itself, harmonized into words.

***A memory, freely given. Not stolen. Not sold. Given.***

Freya went very still beside him.

He watched her understand. He watched her understand, and then he watched her do the arithmetic she always did — quick, silent, defensive — tallying what she had, what it cost, what she could afford to lose.

"Freya," he said softly. "You don't have to give a big one. It doesn't weigh the memory. Only the truth of the giving."

"I know how giving works," she said, too fast, and then winced at herself. "Sorry. Sorry. I just — I've never given one back. Not once. Everything I ever took stayed took."

*Everything I ever took stayed took.* He wanted to tell her that wasn't true anymore, that she'd been giving things away since the night market burned, since she'd chosen Ilse Varga's prisoners over her own safety. But the guardian would not accept his testimony. Only hers.

The drowned had stopped singing. They stood among the pews, watching her with patient, waterlogged faces, waiting — not demanding, just *waiting*, the way a congregation waits for the person who is finally, after all these years, going to stand up and say the true thing out loud.

Freya looked at them for a long moment.

Then she turned to Bram.

"I'm going to give them one of yours," she said.

His breath stopped. In a hundred years of drownings, nothing had ever done that.

"One of mine?"

"You gave me one once. You told me. The night we met properly — you said the memory I stole from the prayer-light was yours, given willingly, long ago." Her voice wavered. "I kept it. I never sold it. I keep telling myself it's because it's evidence, or leverage, or — some thief reason. But it isn't. It's because it's the kindest thing anyone ever left in my head, and I couldn't bear to be without it." She swallowed. "So I'll give it back. To them. To the chapel. Freely. If that counts."

Bram found, to his considerable surprise, that he could not speak.

Because he knew which memory she meant. He had known since the aqueduct, since the first stone, since the moment she'd described the flicker of divine light in a stolen thought. It was a small memory. An afternoon, centuries ago, when he had still walked openly among mortals, and a woman had shared her bread with him on a church step for no reason except that he looked hungry, and he had thought: *this. Whatever breaks, let me remember this.* And later, when the breaking came, he had pressed that afternoon out of himself like a coin from a wound and let it fall into the world, because keeping it hurt more than losing it.

She had been carrying it. All this time. The god of small mercies' opposite number: a mortal keeping a small mercy safe.

"Freya," he managed. "That memory is mine. Giving it away means neither of us will have it."

"I know."

"You'll forget it. I'll forget it."

"I know." Her chin came up, stubborn as a drawn blade. "But they'll have sung for a hundred years without anyone giving them anything. And you spent centuries thinking nobody would ever hand you bread again." Her eyes were bright and furious and wet all at once. "I am *not* letting a chapel full of drowned parishioners out-give me, Bram. I have a reputation."

And that — that absurd, fierce, generous nonsense — was the moment he understood he was lost entirely, and had been for some time, and that no covenant in any realm could repair it.

"Give it, then," he whispered.

* * *

*Freya*

Giving a memory willingly, it turned out, felt nothing like stealing one.

Stealing was a pickpocket's motion: quick fingers, averted eyes, the little guilt-flinch afterward. Giving was different. Giving meant opening your hand and holding it open while the thing left you slowly, so slowly you felt every edge of it on the way out.

She held the memory up in her mind — the bread, the church step, the stranger with kind eyes and rain in his hair, the impossible warmth of being *seen* by something ancient and having it turn out to be gentle — and she offered it the way you offer your seat to someone older and tireder than you. Not because you must. Because it's right.

The guardian took it like a breath drawn in.

For one heartbeat she felt the shape of the absence where it had been — a warm hollow, a missing tooth of the soul — and grief went through her, sharp and clean, for something she could no longer name. She saw Bram's face and understood he felt the same hollow, and that they were grieving the same vanished afternoon together, and somehow that made it bearable. Grief shared, her mother might have said, if Freya had ever had a mother long enough to learn such sayings. Grief shared is grief halved. Or maybe doubled. With Bram it felt like both.

The guardian bowed — the whole chapel bowed, water and stone and glass bending together — and dissolved.

The singing resumed. But it had changed key. Brighter. The drowned turned toward the altar, and the water around the sigil-stone cleared, and the stone itself rose gently from its setting and drifted to Freya's open hands, warm as a loaf fresh from the oven.

She laughed. It came out watery and ridiculous, half sob. "Is that it? It just — gives itself to me?"

"It was always going to," Bram said. His voice was rough. "Stones don't test worthiness, Freya. People do. The stones just wait to see if anyone's willing to pay the toll honestly." He looked at her, and the look held more than she knew how to carry. "You paid honestly."

"Well." She tucked the stone into the oilskin pouch against her chest, next to the first one, where they clicked together like two coins deciding to be friends. "Don't tell anyone. I have a reputation as a hardened criminal to maintain."

"Your secret's safe with me."

They swam for the surface together, up through the hymn, past the bell rope, past the colored window still spilling its ember-light. And just before they broke the surface, Freya felt one of the drowned brush past her — a woman in a waterlogged shawl — and press something small into her palm.

They surfaced gasping in the grey dawn outside the chapel doors, hauling themselves onto the stones. Freya lay on her back, lungs burning, laughing at the sky.

"What did she give you?" Bram asked.

Freya opened her hand. Inside lay a small brass button, sea-worn, ordinary — and threaded through its shank, a single thread of gold that hummed faintly against her skin. Sigil-thread. The kind that only appeared where the veil wore thin.

"She heard us," Freya said slowly. "Down there. All of them, they hear everything near the thin places, don't they? The prayers. The edicts. The Wardens." She sat up, and the laughter drained out of her face. "Bram. This thread is fresh. The veil is tearing *here*, in Corvane, faster than you said it would."

Bram took the button. His expression, when he finished reading whatever gods read in a humming thread, made her stomach drop.

"We need to get back to the city," he said. "Now. There are holes opening that shouldn't be open for another moon's turn — and I think I know who's making them."

"The god of small mercies."

"He's tired of waiting for the thirteenth moon." Bram was already pulling his coat on, movements sharp. "And Freya — the last hole opened near the Warden headquarters. Near Ilse Varga."

Freya was lacing her boots before he finished the sentence, fingers numb, heart hammering, the warm hollow where the memory had been aching like a new scar — and underneath it, for the first time in her life, something that felt almost like hope, running alongside something that felt very much like dread, both of them racing for the city walls.
