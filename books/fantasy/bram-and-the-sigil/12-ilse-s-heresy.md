## 12. Ilse's Heresy

The cells beneath Warden headquarters smelled of wet stone and old penance, and Ilse Varga had always found the smell comforting. Tonight it smelled like a lie.

She sat across a scarred table from Tam Vetch, the memory-fence, who had been her guest for three days and had spent those days attempting to negotiate his way out of custody with the shameless inventiveness of a man who had sold other people's childhoods for coin.

"Commander," he said, for perhaps the fortieth time, "I want you to know I've been thinking about my future."

"You're going to have one," Ilse said. "It will be shorter than you'd like and considerably more supervised."

"That's fair." He folded his hands on the table with the air of a merchant closing a deal. "But consider: I know things. Things about the market, about the charm-trade, about—" he lowered his voice as though the walls might be embarrassed by it, "—about *her*. The Kavanagh girl. Names, faces, safehouses. I could give you all of it."

"You already gave me her name."

"I gave you a name. There's a difference between a name and a person, Commander. Ask any priest."

Ilse did not smile, though something in her wanted to. Vetch was a worm, but he was an honest worm, in the way that worms are honest about being worms. She respected that more than she respected most of her own officers.

"Here is what happens now," she said. "You tell me everything you know about the Kavanagh line. Not the girl—the line. Who kept the records? Who paid for their silence? And why does every file older than thirteen years have pages missing?"

Vetch's grin flickered. For the first time in three days, he looked genuinely uncertain.

"Commander," he said carefully, "you don't want to read what was in those pages."

"That," said Ilse, "is precisely why I'm going to read them."

* * *

The archive of the Wardens occupied the top floor of the headquarters tower, a long cold room where the order kept its history in ledgers bound in grey leather. Ilse had been coming here since she was nineteen years old, first as a junior clerk copying edicts by candlelight, later as an investigator, finally as the woman whose signature closed every case. She knew this room the way she knew her own hands.

Or she had believed so.

The missing pages were not missing. That was the first thing she learned, working backward through twelve years of unsolved charm-thefts to the founding of the ban. The pages had been *removed*—cut cleanly at the spine with a razor—and replaced with duplicates written in a hand she recognized, because she had spent her childhood practicing it under a tutor's switch: the formal script of the Founder himself, Aldous Varga.

Her great-grandfather.

She had known that, of course. Everyone knew it. It was the reason she'd been given her first grey coat at fourteen, the reason old Wardens looked at her the way farmers look at good soil. *Blood tells*, they said. The Varga line produced Wardens the way the Kavanagh line—allegedly extinct, allegedly heretical—had produced covenant-heirs.

She sat down among the ledgers with a lamp and a pot of tea going cold beside her, and she began to compare scripts.

The duplicates were very good. Aldous Varga had been meticulous; the forged entries matched his spacing, his flourishes, even his habit of blotting the final word of each paragraph. But ink fades unevenly, and paper remembers. Under the lamp's angle, she could see the ghost of the cut lines, and when she held a page up to the light, the watermark of the original stock showed through the forgery like a face behind a veil.

Someone had rewritten the founding of the ban.

It took her until well past midnight to find the one page the forger had missed—a loose sheet tucked inside the back board of the oldest ledger, apparently used as a bookmark by some clerk with no sense of history. It was not in Aldous Varga's hand. It was smaller, hurried, written by someone frightened:

*Aldous met with it again tonight. Third time. He says the god has offered him "a mercy beyond price"—the end of magic, forever, in exchange for the breaking of the covenant's last seal. He wept while he said it. He wept, and he took the payment anyway. God forgive us. We are not protectors. We are the knife.*

There was no signature. There didn't need to be. Ilse sat in the cold archive with the page trembling slightly in her fingers and felt the floor of her life tilt beneath her.

*We are not protectors. We are the knife.*

"No," she said aloud, to the empty room, in the tone she used on suspects who told her impossible things. "No, that's not—"

But it was. Because once she saw it, she could not unsee it. The ban had never been a law passed by a frightened city after the Sundering Riots. The riots had come *after* the ban, not before—she checked the dates three times, hands steadier than she felt. The city had been calm. The temples had been full. And then, in a single season, every practice of small magic had become a crime, every practitioner a criminal, and the Wardens—who had been, in the early years, little more than a constabulary with nice uniforms—had been handed an empire.

Aldous Varga had built that empire on a payment from a god.

And not just any god. The loose sheet named it only as "it," but Ilse had spent two weeks reading testimony from the market raid, and there was a phrase that recurred in the prisoners' accounts like a refrain: *the god of small mercies.* A thing that answered prayers too neatly. A thing that healed beggars and spoke through hearths and left people grateful and hollowed. A thing that, according to a half-mad charmer named Odile who would not stop singing, "buys your grief and sells it back to you smaller."

Aldous had sold the covenant to buy the death of magic. And the Wardens had enforced the purchase for thirteen years, calling it law.

Ilse put her head in her hands.

* * *

She was still there at dawn when a junior officer knocked—Corporal Bren, twenty-two years old, earnest as a spaniel, the kind of young man who saluted doorframes.

"Commander? You've been up all night. Should I—should I bring tea? Or a medic? You look like you've seen a ghost."

"Worse," Ilse said. "I've seen a ledger."

Bren blinked at her. He was holding a tray with two cups, because he had guessed, and Ilse took hers because refusing kindness was a habit she'd never managed to acquire. He lingered, shifting his weight, clearly working up to something.

"Out with it, Corporal."

"It's the prisoners, ma'am. From the market raid. The fence—Vetch—he's been asking for you since dawn. Says he'll only talk to you. And the woman, Odile, the one who sings?" Bren lowered his voice. "She stopped singing around midnight. Started talking instead. Very clear, very calm. She asked for you too. She said—" he consulted a notebook, though Ilse suspected he had memorized it, "—she said, 'Tell the Commander her mother asked the same question. Tell her where her mother stopped digging.'"

The tea went cold in Ilse's hand.

Her mother. Captain Sera Varga, dead eleven years, officially of a fever, officially mourned with full honors. Her mother, who had spent the last year of her life in the archives. Her mother, who had taught her to read scripts and watermarks and the difference between a name and a person.

Her mother, whose personal case files Ilse had sealed herself, at sixteen, because grief made a poor archivist and her father had wanted the room cleared.

"Which cell?" Ilse said.

* * *

Odile sat cross-legged on her bunk like a woman at a picnic, grey-haired, wrists bare where they'd taken her charms. Up close, without the market's noise around her, she looked less mad than tired—the particular tiredness of someone who has been carrying a message for a long time and is relieved to set it down.

"Captain Varga," Odile said, before Ilse had finished sitting. "She came to my stall eleven years ago. Bought a forgetting-charm and never used it. Carried it around in her pocket for months instead, like a stone. Do you know why a woman buys a forgetting-charm and doesn't use it?"

"She was considering what to forget."

"She was considering whether forgetting was *possible*." Odile smiled, and it was a gentle smile, which somehow made it worse. "Your mother had found the same page you found, Commander. Or its sister. She came to ask me—I was younger then, and closer to the old ways—whether the god of small mercies could truly pay for a covenant's breaking. Whether a bargain like that could hold."

"And what did you tell her?"

"I told her bargains always hold. That's the horror of them. The god doesn't cheat; it doesn't need to. It pays exactly what it promised and lets you discover what you actually sold." Odile tilted her head. "Your mother thanked me. Paid me double. Walked out into the rain. Three weeks later she had a fever, and six weeks after that she was dead, and everyone said how natural it was, how peaceful, how mercifully quick."

The word *mercifully* landed in the cell like a dropped blade.

"You think the god killed her."

"I think," Odile said, "that your mother was asking questions the god had already paid to keep unanswered. And I think you are asking them louder." She leaned forward, and her eyes were perfectly clear, perfectly sane, and perfectly terrified. "I sang in that market for thirty years, Commander. Mad songs, they called them. I sang them mad on purpose, because mad things get ignored, and ignored things survive. But you—you went and dug up the truth with your own hands, and now it knows your name. So listen to me, daughter of Varga, and I'll say this plainly for the first time in thirty years."

Ilse waited. The cell was very quiet.

"The god of small mercies does not want the covenant reforged, and it does not want the realms to devour each other either. Chaos feeds it, yes—but slowly. What feeds it *fast* is a mortal who knows the truth and chooses the lie anyway. Your great-grandfather fed it for a lifetime. Your mother starved it, and it took her. And now there's a Kavanagh girl gathering stones, and a god walking Corvane wearing a friendly face, and you standing in the middle with a warrant in one hand and your family's sin in the other." Odile settled back against the wall. "The question isn't whether you believe me, Commander. The question is what you do before the thirteenth moon."

* * *

Ilse walked out of the cells and did not go to her office. She went to the chapel.

Not the official one, with its grey altar and its sanctioned liturgy of Order and Vigilance, but the small one behind the infirmary where wounded officers convalesced and chaplains of the old rite—tolerated, barely—still kept a votive lamp. She had not prayed there in years. Prayer, in her experience, was for people who hadn't yet read enough files.

She knelt anyway, because her knees decided before her mind did, and she stared at the little flame and tried to assemble the pieces of her faith into something that would hold.

They did not hold.

Thirteen years of service. Twelve years of raids and warrants and the grey coats of her order sweeping through markets like weather. Every arrest she had ever made for charm-craft, every practitioner she had shipped to the re-education houses, every child she had watched weep as a confiscated charm—a lullaby, a first kiss, a dead father's voice—was catalogued and burned. She had called it law. She had called it protection. She had stood at her mother's grave and sworn that the Varga name would mean vigilance unto the last generation.

And underneath all of it, all along, was a god's payment and a traitor's signature, and her own blood in the traitor's line.

"Ilse Varga," she said to the flame, testing the name like a coin bitten for gold. It rang differently now. Hollow at the center.

The terrible thing—the thing that made her grip the prayer-rail until her knuckles whitened—was not the betrayal itself. Betrayal she understood; she had seen men betray for far less than a god's promise. The terrible thing was how *neatly* it explained everything. The speed of the ban. The ferocity of the enforcement. The way the Wardens' cruelty had always been slightly excessive for the threat, as though the order were not protecting the city but *collecting on a debt*. Even the missing pages—even her mother's fever—even the phrase *mercifully quick.*

Faith, she discovered, did not shatter like glass. It shattered like ice on a thawing river—one crack, then another, then the whole surface groaning and going soft beneath her, and nothing solid anywhere to stand on.

When she finally rose, her legs were stiff and her eyes were dry and her mind was terrifyingly clear.

She went back upstairs, past Bren, who was asleep at his desk with a half-written report about Odile's testimony, and she woke him with a hand on his shoulder.

"Corporal. Two orders. First: the Kavanagh warrant is suspended. Not withdrawn—suspended. If anyone asks, we're refining the search parameters."

"Yes, Commander." He rubbed his eyes. "And second?"

Ilse looked at the window, where the moon hung over Corvane—thinner than last month, counting down toward its thirteenth turn—and she heard Odile's voice: *what you do before the thirteenth moon.*

"Second," she said, "get me everything we have on the aqueduct district. Old maps, foundation surveys, anything from before the ban. And Bren—" she paused, hearing how the next words would sound, saying them anyway, "—do it quietly. As of tonight, I'm no longer certain the enemy is outside these walls."

Bren stared at her, and Ilse watched him decide—watched the moment a young man's honest face chose trust over fear—and he saluted, properly, and went.

Alone in her office, Ilse unlocked the bottom drawer where she kept the things that were not cases: her mother's sealed files, carried from house to house for eleven years without being opened. She set them on the desk. Somewhere in those pages, her mother had stopped digging—or been made to stop—and Ilse meant to find the exact spot, and stand on it, and dig.

Outside, the moon slid behind a cloud, and in the dark of the city below, faint as a hearth-whisper, someone was answering prayers.
