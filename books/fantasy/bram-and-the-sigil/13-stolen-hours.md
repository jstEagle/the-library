## 13. Stolen Hours

The morning after the drowned chapel, Bram did something no god had ever done in the history of Corvane, as far as I knew.

He slept in.

I woke on the pallet in my rented room above the shuttered charm-shop and found him still sprawled across the floor by the window, one arm flung over his eyes, breathing the slow, unguarded breath of someone who had forgotten, for a few hours, that he was four thousand years old. His boots were off. That alone nearly undid me. I had never seen him without his boots. It felt indecent, like catching a cathedral with its vestments hung out to dry.

I lay there longer than I meant to, just watching the light move across the floor toward him. The second sigil-stone sat wrapped in oilcloth in the corner, humming faintly to itself like a kettle deciding whether to sing. Two stones now. One more, somewhere, and then the thirteenth moon, and then—

No. Not yet. I made myself say it plainly, the way you'd set down a heavy load: *not yet*. Today there was light on the floorboards and a sleeping man who had held my hand under black water while dead parishioners sang, and I was allowed—perhaps for the last time in my life—to simply be in the morning without calculating what it would cost.

Bram opened one eye. "You're staring."

"You're snoring."

"I am a divine emanation of the old covenant. I do not snore."

"You snore like a cart going downhill."

He considered this with great seriousness. "That's the resonance of creation," he said, and rolled over, and I threw my spare blanket at him.

* * *

Here is the thing nobody tells you about gods: they are terrible at being people.

We went down to the Tallowmarket because Bram announced he wanted to see it—"the living city, the ordinary hours"—and within ten minutes he had offended a fishmonger, overpaid for a pear by an amount that could have fed me for a week, and stopped dead in front of a puppeteer's stall with the expression of a man witnessing a miracle.

"It's a puppet," I said.

"It's a *wolf*," he said, "that is also a sock."

"That's the whole idea."

"Who thought of this? Who looked at a sock and saw a wolf inside it?" He turned to me with such genuine awe that the fishmonger, still muttering, forgot to be angry. "Freya. Mortals are extraordinary. You take the most humble things—socks, pears, *mornings*—and you make them into wonders purely by deciding they are."

"They're socks, Bram."

"And you're pretending not to be delighted, which is the most mortal thing you've done all week." He bought the wolf-sock-puppet. Of course he did. He wore it on his hand for the rest of the market like it was a signet ring.

I should have been watching for Wardens. I should have been counting exits, memorizing faces, doing all the small survival arithmetic that had kept me alive for thirteen years of selling other people's forgetting. Instead I was walking through the Tallowmarket beside a barefoot-looking god with a sock-wolf on his hand, laughing—actually laughing, loud enough that heads turned—and feeling something dangerous unfurl in my chest like warmth from a hearth I'd been told all my life was forbidden.

"This is a terrible disguise," I said. "You're supposed to be inconspicuous."

"I am a god of thresholds and doorsteps. Inconspicuousness has never been among my domains." He wiggled the wolf at a passing child, who shrieked with delight. "Besides, look around you. Half of Corvane is praying again. The Wardens can't arrest the whole city for looking up. For one morning, we are only two people buying pears."

"For one morning," I repeated, and heard how carefully he'd said it. *For one morning.* Neither of us pretended we didn't both know what the thirteenth moon would ask. We just agreed, silently and completely, not to spend today paying for tomorrow.

* * *

At midday we climbed the old reservoir steps above the Tanner's Quarter—the ones with the cracked balustrade where courting couples used to carve their initials before the ban made even that feel like contraband. Bram spread his coat on the sun-warmed stone like he'd done it a thousand times, and maybe he had, in some earlier age when gods picnicked with mortals instead of hiding from them.

He'd brought bread. Actual bread, and hard cheese, and a jar of honey with a cloth over the top, stolen—I use the word with respect—from a kitchen somewhere, because when I raised my eyebrow he said, "Offerings. People leave them on doorsteps. It would be rude to refuse."

"So you live off doorstep honey."

"I live off *belief*, technically. But honey is more reliable."

I ate and watched the city below us smoke and glitter and shout. Somewhere in those streets a baker's son spoke through hearthfire; somewhere a beggar walked on legs that had been ruined for a decade. The holes between realms were widening—I could feel them now, a faint wrongness in the air like a tooth aching before it breaks—but from up here, with honey on my fingers, Corvane just looked like a city. My city. Ugly and stubborn and alive.

"My mother used to bring me here," I said, before I could stop myself.

Bram didn't move, didn't turn it into a moment. He just passed me the honey again and let the silence be a comfortable place.

"She wasn't Kavanagh by blood—or she was, distantly, but she never practiced. After my father died she wouldn't touch magic at all. Said the covenant's breaking had taken enough from us." I turned the honey jar in my hands. "But she'd bring me up here and tell me the old stories anyway. Carefully. Like they were recipes she wasn't sure still worked. She said her grandmother swore the gods used to walk the market streets in the harvest season, wearing borrowed coats, and nobody minded, because that was the arrangement—you let them be ordinary one day a year, and they kept the wells sweet and the winters short."

"The Festival of Borrowed Coats," Bram said quietly. "I remember it. I owned eleven coats, one year. I have never in four millennia been so warm."

I laughed, and then the laugh caught on something in my throat. Because I realized I was telling him things I had never sold. Every memory in my hoard was inventory—catalogued, priced, weighed against coin. But these hours with my mother, the reservoir steps, the story of the borrowed coats—I had kept those off the shelf entirely, not because they were precious but because no one had ever wanted to buy them, and so they had stayed mine, unpriced, soft as unspun wool.

And now I was handing them to a god for free, and it felt nothing like theft. It felt like setting down a basket I hadn't known was heavy.

"Bram." I looked at the honey, not at him. "What happens to me? After. When the covenant is reforged and the moons stop grinding. Do I go back to being nobody? A thief with a stall?"

"You were never nobody."

"You know what I mean."

He was quiet long enough that I finally looked up. The afternoon light did something unfair to his face—made him look young, or rather made him look the age he'd have been if he'd gotten to grow the way mortals grow.

"The covenant asks each keeper a price," he said. "It always has. The first Kavanagh gave her name to the stones. Her daughter gave her voice for a winter. It takes what the keeper holds dearest—that's how it seals true. Blood won't bind a covenant. Only cost does."

"What do you think mine will be?"

"I don't know." And I believed him, which frightened me more than any answer would have, because I could see he had already spent nights turning the question over and finding every door locked. Then he smiled, and it was a real smile, but I was a memory-thief and I read memories for a living, and underneath it I saw fear. "Ask me again after the third stone."

* * *

We came down off the reservoir as the light went gold, and Bram—who claimed gods don't get cold—walked closer to me than the air required.

There was a fiddler playing outside the Saltgate tavern, an old man with a dog asleep on his boot, and Bram stopped the way he'd stopped at the puppet stall. "Dance with me."

"No."

"There's music."

"There's always music. That's not a reason."

"It is the *only* reason. Everything else is justification." He held out his hand, wolf-puppet and all, and I want it recorded that I resisted for a full three seconds before taking it.

I am not a good dancer. Thirteen years of keeping your head down teaches your feet very particular habits, none of them suited to a jig. But Bram danced like weather—he pulled the rhythm out of me rather than imposing his own, and somewhere in the second tune I stopped caring that the fiddler was grinning, that the Saltgate crowd had come out to watch, that Ilse Varga might at that moment be reading my name off a warrant. I laughed until my ribs hurt. I spun until the golden light smeared into ribbons. The dog woke up and barked at us in approval.

Afterward, breathless on the tavern bench with two cups of small beer between us, Bram said, "There. Was that so terrible?"

"Yes. Do it again sometime."

His knee pressed against mine under the table, deliberate and warm. "Freya. There's something I should tell you, and I've been avoiding it because today has been—" he gestured at everything, the fiddler, the beer, the general state of us, "—and I didn't want to spend it."

"Don't, then."

"I'm going to, because you deserve better than a surprise." He turned his cup slowly. "When this ends—however it ends—I'll remember all of it. Every hour. That's what I am; I keep things. Four thousand years and I have never once been able to forget." He looked at me then, and the fear I'd glimpsed on the reservoir steps was plain now, and it was not fear for himself. "Whatever price the covenant names, I will carry the memory of this day for the rest of eternity. And if the price is what I think it might be—"

"Don't." This time I said it hard enough that he stopped. "Don't tell me what you think it might be. If it comes, I'll know it when I know it. Until then I'm having the day. You said it yourself—one morning, two people buying pears."

"One morning," he agreed softly. "Two people."

And that was when I understood—not with dread, not yet, just with a strange clear tenderness—that I had already paid something. That falling in love was itself a kind of spending, the one purchase I couldn't catalogue or price or sell, and that some part of me had signed for it days ago, under the aqueduct, when he'd said *you carry the sigil-blood* and my heart had answered *oh. Him. Of course him.*

I didn't say any of this. I drank my beer. I let our knees stay touching. Some truths you keep off the shelf precisely because they're the only stock worth anything.

* * *

We parted at dusk at the mouth of Cooper's Lane, because a god walking a thief home was one sentiment too many even for a day of borrowed coats, and besides, I had one piece of work left that I couldn't put off.

I checked the dead-drop behind the tannery—the chipped brick I'd used for years—and found the folded paper waiting exactly where Sera the fence always left it. Sera had gone quiet since the market raids; half her network was in Warden cells. The note was short, written in her cramped hand:

*Found where the third stone hides. You won't like it.*

Then an address. Not a street in the low quarters, not a ruin beneath an aqueduct.

An address inside the Warden citadel itself. The founder's monument, the heart of the order that had hunted my family's name to extinction for thirteen years. The stone I needed next sat embedded in marble above their ceremonial hall, behind their guards, under Ilse Varga's own roof.

I stood in the blue dark of Cooper's Lane with the paper shaking slightly in my hand, and behind me, faint as a hearth-voice, I heard the second sigil-stone begin to hum—as if it already knew where its brother was kept, and had started, patiently, to count the hours.

The borrowed coat was coming off. Tomorrow I would need my thief's hands again.

But tonight—just tonight—I let myself walk home slowly, past the lit windows, holding one whole unsold day against my chest like a charm I'd finally made for myself, knowing exactly what it was worth and refusing, for a few hours more, to name its price.
