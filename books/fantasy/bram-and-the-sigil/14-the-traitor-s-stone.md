## 14. The Traitor's Stone

The invitation arrived the way all my best ideas did: as someone else's terrible idea.

"It's a ceremony," Sela whispered across the counter of Bramble & Wick, the honest little charm-shop front I'd been running for three weeks now. She was seventeen, sharp-eyed, and had appointed herself my apprentice without asking. "For the founder. Founder's Day. They're unveiling a new plaque on his monument and everything. Half the city will be there."

"Half the city," I said, "does not include me."

"Because you're a criminal?"

"Because I'm *tired*, Sela."

She leaned in, lowering her voice to the register she probably thought was subtle. "Bram said—"

"Bram," I said, "says a great many things."

"He said the third stone is in the founder's monument. He said you'd figure out how to get it." She shrugged with the magnificent indifference of youth toward other people's survival strategies. "He seemed very confident."

Of course he did. Bram was confident the way old rivers were deep — quietly, endlessly, and mostly underneath where you couldn't see it. He'd been gone two days scouting something he wouldn't name, which left me alone with an apprentice who thought infiltration was a career path and a shop full of charms that could, at best, help a customer remember where she'd put her keys.

"Fine," I said, because she was going to keep looking at me like that until I said it anyway. "Tell me about this ceremony."

Her whole face lit up, and I had the distinct feeling I'd just been outmaneuvered by a teenager.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about Warden headquarters: it's beautiful.

I'd spent twelve years thinking of it as a fortress, a black shape on the hill where bad news came from. And from below, it was that — walls the color of storm clouds, towers built to look down on you. But inside the courtyard, where the Founder's Day crowd milled about in their good coats, it was all pale stone and old trees, fountains murmuring, banners of grey-and-gold snapping in a wind that smelled like autumn starting to think about winter.

The monument stood at the courtyard's center: a tall figure in Warden robes, one hand raised as though forever hushing a room. Aldous Crane, first Warden-Commander, the man whose name every child learned alongside the ban on magic. *Crane the Cleansing*, they called him in the histories they let us read. The man who made Corvane safe.

The man who, according to a god I was increasingly unwilling to trust, had sold the covenant out for payment from something that called itself merciful.

And somewhere in that monument — in the base, Bram's notes said, behind the dedication plaque — sat the third sigil-stone, hidden in plain sight for thirteen years while the Wardens polished its casing every morning.

"Stop smiling," Sela muttered beside me. She'd insisted on coming, over my objections, which had lasted approximately ninety seconds.

"I'm not smiling."

"You're doing your market face. The one where you're about to rob somebody."

"That is not — okay, yes, it's the market face. But I'm not robbing anybody. Yet. Look natural."

Sela looked at the fountain, the crowd, the forty armed Wardens lining the perimeter, and then back at me with an expression that said *define natural*. To be fair, we were both dressed as charm-sellers — which, technically, we now were — come to pay respects to the founder like any law-abiding tradeswoman. My stall banner was even folded under my arm as a prop. Nobody suspects the woman carrying her own advertisement.

A horn sounded. The crowd hushed and turned toward the headquarters' great doors, and out came a procession: councilors in grey silk, junior Wardens in formation, and then Ilse Varga herself, walking not ahead of the procession but beside it, as though she couldn't quite bring herself to lead a celebration.

Even at a distance she looked exactly as I remembered from the night she burned the market: tall, iron-grey hair pinned without mercy, hands clasped behind her back like a woman holding herself together at the seams. The crowd applauded. A few people actually cheered. She received it the way a wall receives weather.

"There she is," Sela breathed. "Warden-Commander Varga. They say she hasn't slept properly in six years."

"They say a lot of things."

"They say she reads case files at night. Old ones. From before the ban." Sela glanced sideways at me, sly as anything. "They say she's looking for something."

I kept my eyes forward. "People should mind their own business."

"You're literally here to steal a rock off their statue."

"*Shh.*"

The ceremony began. A councilor with a voice like wet velvet gave a speech about vigilance and sacrifice and the eternal debt we owed to Crane's memory. The crowd nodded along in the particular way crowds do when they've stopped listening three sentences ago. Behind the speaker, workmen waited with a cloth draped over something — the new plaque, presumably, though from our angle near the monument's base, all I could see was stone.

Stone, and, if I let my senses open the way Bram had taught me, something else.

I felt it before I saw any sign of it. A hum, low and patient, like a note held under water. The same frequency I'd felt in the aqueduct dark and the drowned chapel, but older somehow, wearier. It came from directly behind the dedication plaque at the monument's base, three feet from my hip.

Thirteen years, I thought. Thirteen years of ceremonies, of speeches about vigilance, and they'd been standing guard over the thing they least wanted found, and never once knew it.

The speech droned. The crowd's attention pooled toward the podium like water finding a drain. Sela nudged me. I drifted — unhurried, bored-looking, a tradeswoman sidling out of the sun — until I stood at the monument's base with my back to the crowd, examining the stonework like a woman with nothing better to do than admire architecture.

The dedication plaque was bronze, taller than I'd expected, bolted at each corner. Old lettering:

**ALDOUS CRANE. FIRST WARDEN-COMMANDER. HE GAVE ALL THAT THE CITY MIGHT BE SAFE.**

I almost laughed. *Gave all.* If the god of small mercies told true truths, Crane had indeed given all — given everyone else's covenant, everyone else's gods, everyone else's small magics, in exchange for whatever price had been paid into his hand. He gave all right. Just not his own.

The bolts were bronze too, greened with age, but the bottom-left one had been replaced more recently — newer metal, fresher cut. Someone had opened this before. Within the last few years, by the wear. I filed that away next to the growing pile of things about Ilse Varga that refused to stay simple.

I palmed my smallest charm — a loosening-wisp, the kind I used to sell to locksmiths' widows — and pressed it to the bolt. It softened like warm butter. The plaque swung outward on a hidden hinge with a sound the crowd's applause generously covered.

Inside, set into the stone like a heart in a chest, was the third sigil-stone.

It was smaller than the others — no bigger than a plum — and duller, as though thirteen years behind bronze had starved it. But when my fingers closed around it, it woke. Light moved under its surface like blood returning to a numb limb, and the hum rose through my palm, up my arm, into my teeth, and sang one clear, unmistakable wordless syllable of *recognition*.

*Found you,* it said, or something like it. *Found you, heir.*

My eyes stung. I didn't have time for that. I pocketed the stone, eased the plaque shut, and turned around directly into Warden-Commander Ilse Varga.

* * *

Later, I would try to reconstruct those first seconds and fail completely. There was no plan. There was no clever market trick, no charm up my sleeve, no graceful lie waiting on my tongue. There was just the flat animal fact of standing three feet from the woman who had burned down the only world I'd built, with her stolen stone warm in my pocket, and nothing between us but air.

She wasn't looking at me.

That was the strange thing. The ceremony was still going — the councilor had moved on to a passage about Crane's humility that would have made a statue laugh — and Varga stood at the edge of the crowd, apart from it, her gaze fixed not on the speaker but on the monument itself. On the plaque. Her jaw was tight in a way I recognized from mirrors.

She was wearing civilian clothes.

Not quite — a Warden's coat, unbadged, collar plain. But no insignia, no command sash. She had come to her own founder's ceremony dressed as nobody, standing at the back like a mourner who hadn't decided whom to mourn.

"You're not watching the speech," she said, without turning her head.

It took me a moment to realize she was talking to me.

"Neither are you," I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect.

"No." A pause. "I know it by heart. I wrote part of it. Councilor Hesse cut the good half." Her mouth did something that in another woman might have been a smile. "You're the charm-seller. Kestrel & Co., the new stall on Tanner's Row. You've been open a month."

My stomach performed a slow, elegant dive. "We get around."

"Mm." She finally turned and looked at me, and I braced for the moment of recognition — the market, the warrant, the name Kavanagh spoken aloud in a courtyard full of Wardens. Instead what crossed her face was something worse. Something tired.

"The founder's monument," she said, "was designed by Crane himself. Did you know that? Every measurement, every inscription. He supervised the setting of the dedication plaque personally. Refused to let anyone else touch it." Her eyes went to the bronze, to the newest-cut bolt, and stayed there. "For a man so devoted to openness, he was very fond of hiding things."

The stone pulsed against my ribs like a second heartbeat. *Run*, it didn't say, but I heard it anyway.

"You seem very interested in the plaque," I managed.

"I'm interested in a great many things lately." She looked back at me, and here it came — I felt the shift in her attention the way you feel a door lock behind you. "Your stall. Tanner's Row. You sell remembering-charms. That's unusual. Most charm-sellers deal in forgetting; it's simpler, safer, less likely to attract attention. A remembering-charm requires actual craft. Actual knowledge." Her voice dropped, quiet enough that only I could hear it over the applause. "Where did you train?"

"Nowhere," I said. "Self-taught. Books."

"Which books?"

"The dangerous ones."

Something flickered across her face — not anger. Closer to respect, which frightened me considerably more. "Twelve years," she said, "I chased a thief through this city. Small thefts. Careful ones. Never greedy, never cruel. I built half a ledger on that thief, and do you know what I concluded, at the end of it?" She shook her head slowly. "That whoever it was, she wasn't doing it for gain. She was doing it because she had no choice. Because the alternative was starving, and the law offered her nothing."

The crowd burst into applause — the speech had ended; the cloth was being pulled off the new plaque — and in the noise and movement, Varga stepped closer, close enough that I could see the fine lines at her eyes, the shadowed skin of a woman who truly did not sleep.

"I have spent six years reading files that predate my commission," she said. "Files my own order sealed. And three weeks ago I found a confession in my mother's handwriting that has undone everything I believed I was." Her hand brushed her coat, where a folded paper sat heavy in the inner pocket — I saw the outline of it, saw her fingers press it like a wound. "So I am going to ask you one question, charm-seller, and I want you to consider very carefully whether lying to me serves you."

The stone burned against my side. Somewhere across the courtyard, Bram emerged from the crowd's edge, and I watched him see us — watch him start to move — and give the smallest shake of his head when Varga's backup, two plainclothes Wardens, shifted position. Trapped, all of us, in a ceremony.

"What question?" I asked.

Varga held my gaze, and her voice, when it came, was almost gentle, which was the most terrifying thing yet.

"When you opened that plaque just now — don't insult us both — did you find what you were looking for?" A breath. "Or did it find *you*?"

Every instinct I owned screamed at once, a dozen lies queuing up like customers at closing time. *What plaque. What are you talking about. I'm just a tradeswoman.* And beneath them, absurdly, rising through the fear like light through water, was the memory of this woman's face the night she arrested my fence — the crack in her certainty, the grief she thought she'd buried — and the terrible, inconvenient understanding that Ilse Varga was not my opposite. She was what I might have become, if the city had ever given me a badge instead of a hunger.

If I lied now, I lost her. If I told the truth, I might lose everything else.

The new plaque was unveiled to polite applause. The stone in my pocket pulsed once, warm as a held hand.

"Yes," I said.

Ilse Varga closed her eyes, just for a moment — a woman receiving a verdict she'd been dreading and hoping for in equal measure. When she opened them again, she said four words that turned my whole world on its edge:

"Then we need to talk."
