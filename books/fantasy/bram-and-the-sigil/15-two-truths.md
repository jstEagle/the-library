## 15. Two Truths

*Ilse*

The founder's confession had been hidden in plain sight for twelve years, which Ilse found either very funny or very damning, depending on how much tea she'd had.

It sat in the third drawer of her own desk — a desk she had inherited from Warden-Commander Oskar Bren, who had inherited it from the founder herself, Saint Aurelia the Unyielding, whose marble likeness stood in the atrium holding a sword and a set of scales and an expression of serene certainty that Ilse had spent thirty years trying to earn.

The confession was written on the back of a child's primer. *Practical Letters for Young Wardens.* Someone had peeled back the pasted endpaper of the drawer and pressed the pages flat against the wood, where they had waited through four commanders' worth of paperwork, three wars of succession in the Council, and one memorable incident involving a runaway goat during the Harvest Review.

Ilse had found it because she'd been looking for a pen.

She had read it eleven times now. Each time, the words refused to become less true. *I sold the threshold,* Aurelia had written, in a hand far shakier than the monuments suggested. *The god of small mercies came to me as a beggar with a burned arm, and I gave him the covenant's anchor-name, and he gave me power over every charm and prayer in Corvane, and I told myself it was mercy, that mortals were safer small, that the gods could not hurt us if we cut them off at the knee. I have never slept a whole night since.*

Below that, in fresher ink, someone — Bren, almost certainly — had added a single line: *If you are reading this, you know now. Do what she couldn't.*

Ilse had done nothing for two days except read it and drink tea and fail to sleep whole nights, which felt appropriately traditional.

Then the Kavanagh girl had walked into Warden headquarters wearing a stolen ceremonial sash and an expression of profound regret, and Ilse had decided the universe had a sense of humor after all.

"Sit," Ilse said now, and did not draw her weapon, which by the girl's flinch she understood to be the strangest thing that had happened all week.

Freya Kavanagh sat. Up close, without the market-lantern shadows, she looked younger than her file claimed and more exhausted than her posture admitted. Her eyes went everywhere — the door, the window, the confession on the desk, the teapot — cataloguing exits. Old habit. Ilse recognized it because it was hers.

"You're not arresting me," Freya said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I've had you in a cell since yesterday. You'll notice I haven't processed the paperwork."

"That's not reassuring. That's just bureaucratic."

Despite herself — genuinely despite herself — Ilse felt the corner of her mouth move. "Yes. Well. Welcome to my entire career." She slid the primer across the desk. "Read it."

* * *

*Freya*

Freya had planned for many contingencies upon infiltrating the ceremony: pursuit, capture, interrogation, a dramatic escape involving the drainage channels she'd scouted twice. She had not planned for being given tea.

Warden-Commander Varga drank her tea like a woman conducting an execution — black, scalding, no ceremony. She watched Freya read without blinking, which made concentration difficult. The primer's pages were soft as cloth from handling, and the handwriting started proud and ended ragged, like a road washing out.

*...I gave him the covenant's anchor-name...*

Freya read it twice. Three times. Somewhere behind her ribs, the first sigil-stone — sewn into her coat lining these weeks now, humming faintly like a cat that wanted feeding — grew warmer, as if it too were reading along.

"The founder," Freya said slowly. "Aurelia. She broke the covenant."

"Aurelia sold it." Ilse's voice was flat, but there was something underneath the flatness, something with a hairline crack running through it. "Thirteen years ago. To the god of small mercies. In exchange for the ban — the authority to outlaw small magic, to lock down prayers, to build everything you've spent your life hiding from."

"Why are you showing me this? You could burn it. You could bury it and arrest me properly and everything would stay tidy."

Something flickered across the Commander's face — pain, quickly governed. "Because tidy is what got us here." She turned the primer to its last page, to the newer ink. "*Do what she couldn't.* My predecessor left that for whoever came next. He died before he could do anything himself." A pause. "I have spent twelve years hunting charm-thieves, Kavanagh. Small magics. Small people. I told myself it was law." Her jaw tightened. "It was maintenance. We weren't guarding justice. We were guarding a lie so large nobody thought to check under it."

Freya looked down at the confession again. The sigil-stone pulsed once against her ribs, patient as a heartbeat.

"And the betrayed?" she asked quietly. "You said justice *for* them. Which ones?"

Ilse was silent long enough that the fire popped in the grate.

"My mother," she said at last, "was a hedge-witch. Village charmwork, blessing-candles, weather-songs. Nothing worth a Warden's boot, before the ban. After it—" The crack in her voice showed, and she sealed it over with visible effort. "After it, she was a criminal. I reported her myself when I was seventeen. It was the proudest day of my life." She met Freya's eyes. "She died in a holding cell in this building while I was being decorated downstairs. And I have spent every year since telling myself the law was worth it." She pushed the primer an inch closer. "So. Now you know what I want. Tell me what you want, thief, and let us find out whether it's the same shape."

* * *

*Ilse*

The girl didn't answer immediately, which Ilse respected. Liars answered fast.

Instead Freya Kavanagh reached into her coat — slowly, telegraphing the motion like a soldier surrendering a blade — and drew out a stone. It was smaller than a fist, gray as river slate, but light moved inside it the way light moves inside deep water, slow and green and old. When she set it on the desk between them, the fire guttered, and every candle in the room leaned toward it like flowers toward sun.

Ilse had trained her whole life to feel nothing in the presence of forbidden magic. She felt something anyway. Homesickness, maybe. For a home she'd never had.

"There are three of these," Freya said. "Sigil-stones. Anchor-stones. Whatever your founder would have called them before she sold their names." Her thumb rested on the stone's edge, not possessively — protectively, Ilse thought, the way one rests a hand on a sleeping dog. "With all three, a covenant-heir can reforge the bond between the realms before the thirteenth moon turns. Without it, the realms grind together until one eats the other. Probably both. Bram isn't certain."

"Bram." Ilse kept her voice level. "The god who's been leaving love-letters in stolen memories. He's real. He's *here.*"

"He's helping." Freya's chin came up, and there it was at last — the thief's defiance, bright and stubborn as a struck match. "And before you ask, yes, I know how that sounds. Gods lie. This one lies less than most, and when he does, it's usually about how handsome he is."

Despite everything — the traitor's monument forty feet below her office, her mother's case file still open in the archive, thirteen years of edicts bearing her signature — Ilse heard herself say, dryly: "Is he?"

"What?"

"Handsome."

Freya blinked. Then, caught off guard, she laughed — a short, startled sound, like a bird flushed from cover, gone as fast as it came. But it had happened. Ilse noted it the way she noted everything: precisely, and against her will.

"That's classified," Freya said.

"Mm." Ilse rose and walked to the window. Below, the atrium: Saint Aurelia in her marble serenity, scales in one hand, sword in the other. *Unyielding.* They should chisel the name off. Or better — leave it, and carve the truth underneath, so every young Warden who marched past learned that unyielding could mean simply *unwilling to admit wrong.* "Here is my problem, Kavanagh. Everything you've said matches the confession. The stones, the heir, the thirteenth moon. I have independent confirmation from a dead saint, which is more corroboration than most cases get. And yet I am expected to believe that a career criminal, a banned magician, and a *god* intend to repair the world out of the goodness of their hearts."

"Not goodness." Freya picked up the stone and held it in her palm, and the light in it steadied, as if it knew her. "Necessity. And—" She hesitated.

"Say it."

"Love, if you want the honest answer. Not the way you think. The covenant isn't a leash, Commander. It's a *promise.* Gods answer only what mortals freely ask, and mortals keep the doors open freely. Nobody owns anybody. Aurelia couldn't imagine that, so she made it a war instead." Freya looked up. "I'm not asking you to trust me. I'm asking you to check my math."

Ilse turned from the window. Outside, dusk was coming down over Corvane, and already — she saw it now, had been seeing it for weeks and calling it lamplight — holes in the air, faint and widening, where the other realm pressed through. A city full of cracks. A city built on a lie she had enforced.

"If I ally with you," she said, "I am committing treason against my own order. If I don't, I am complicit in whatever the thirteenth moon does to this city, and to the hundred thousand people in it, including several hundred Wardens who are fools but mine. So understand what I require." She crossed back to the desk and put both hands flat on the wood. "No secrets between us. None. Every contact, every plan, every word that god says to you. And if I conclude you've been played — if I conclude this Bram is using you as a key instead of a partner — I will kill him myself, covenant or no covenant, and I will expect you to hold the door."

Silence. The sigil-stone glowed softly between them, indifferent to politics.

Then Freya Kavanagh — thief, liar, last heir of a broken promise — stuck out her hand.

"No secrets," she said. "But fair warning: Bram talks *constantly*, and half of it is about bread. Thirteen years of watching mortals, and his great revelation is sourdough."

Ilse took the hand. It was calloused in strange places, a worker's hand, a pickpocket's hand. Warm.

"I once interrogated a smuggler for six hours about a cheese ring," she heard herself reply. "I can survive bread."

They shook once, formally, two enemies agreeing to a truce neither believed would hold — and yet when they let go, the room felt different. Less like a trap. More like a garrison with only two soldiers in it, watching the same wall.

* * *

*Freya*

Later — much later, past midnight, in a safehouse that smelled of lamp oil and Ilse's aggressively practical cooking — Freya sat by the window with the sigil-stone in her lap and tried to feel what she was supposed to feel.

She had shaken hands with the woman who burned the memory market. Whose warrant bore her true name. Who had taken Marla, taken old Tam the fence, taken half the faces she'd known in the dark. The fury hadn't gone anywhere; it sat in her chest like a swallowed coal. But beside it now, uncomfortably, sat other things: the crack in the Commander's voice when she said *my mother*. The six hours over cheese. The way Ilse had listened — actually listened, checking the math, as promised — and then pulled her own order's files and spent four hours cross-referencing Aurelia's dates against the moon-record without once looking for credit.

"She's not doing this for us," Freya said aloud, to the room, to the dark window, to whoever might be listening.

The shadows by the hearth shifted, and Bram unfolded out of them, tall and rumpled and smelling faintly of the bakery on Cordwainer Lane, because apparently gods could be sentimental about flour.

"No," he agreed. "She's doing it for the betrayed. It's the best reason there is." He settled onto the bench across from her, elbows on knees. "You distrust each other beautifully. It's practically a covenant already."

"Don't be charming at me. I'm having a crisis of ethics."

"You're always having a crisis of ethics. It's one of your finest features." But his eyes were gentle, and tired, and older than the walls outside. "For what it's worth — she checked your math. No one has checked anything in thirteen years, little thief. That woman just performed a miracle and doesn't know it."

Freya turned the stone over in her hands. Its glow spilled green across her knuckles. Two stones remained: one beneath drowned water, singing; one somewhere the god of small mercies would guard with everything he had, because it was the piece of the lie he'd been paid in.

"We go for the chapel stone tomorrow," she said. "At dawn. Both of us. And—" she hesitated, then made herself finish, because no secrets meant no secrets— "the Commander comes too. She dives like a brick, by the way. I asked. Subtly."

Bram smiled, slow and warm, and for one unguarded moment Freya forgot to be wary at all.

"Drowned parishioners, a hostile Commander, and a test that demands a memory given freely," he said. "Dawn, then. Wear something you can grieve in."

Outside, high above Corvane, the twelfth moon cleared the rooftops, fat and white and nearly done turning — and somewhere beneath the city, in the dark where small mercies curdled, something ancient felt the alliance form, and began, patiently, to make its counter-offer.
