## 16. The God Who Lies Truly

The message came by moth.

Not a messenger carrying a moth, mind you—a moth itself, wings patterned like tiny inked script, landing on the rim of my teacup at the safehouse Bram had found us in the Tanners' Quarter. I stared at it. It stared back with the particular patience of something that knows it has your full attention.

"That's not natural," I said.

"No," said Bram, from the doorway, where he was doing that thing he did—leaning, arms crossed, radiating the calm of someone who had watched empires rise and fall and mostly found them tedious. "Read it anyway."

I tipped the cup toward the window light. The wing-script rearranged itself as I watched, letters crawling like ants on honey:

*THE WARDEN-COMMANDER IS OUR GUEST. THE OLD PRISON BENEATH THE AQUEDUCT, WHERE YOU FOUND WHAT YOU FOUND. COME ALONE OR SHE DIES TIRED. WE ARE NOT CRUEL. MERELY THOROUGH.*

"Alone," I repeated. "Of course alone. It's always alone. No one ever says *come with friends*, come with snacks, come with an army."

"You have me."

"The moth said alone, Bram. Even you can't argue with a moth."

"I've argued with worse." He came closer, and his shadow fell across the teacup, and for a moment the little insect trembled—as if even a divine courier knew what it was to stand in the shade of something old and vast and fond. "It wants the stones. All three. This is how it negotiates: not with threats against you, but against everyone you might care about. It learned that trick from watching mortals. We taught it everything it knows about cruelty, dressed up as kindness."

I looked at him. "And if it's a trap?"

"It's a trap."

"And if I go?"

"It's still a trap. But Ilse Varga is in it, and you'll go regardless, because you're you." He smiled, and there was grief folded inside the smile, thin as paper. "I would go too. That's what makes this so inconvenient."

I finished my tea. The moth waited politely until I set down the cup, then dissolved into a puff of grey dust that smelled, absurdly, of lavender and bandages. The god of small mercies, announcing itself even in its messengers. Everything about it smelled like someone trying to make suffering comfortable.

"All right," I said. "Let's go be thorough."

* * *

The old prison beneath the aqueduct was a place Corvane preferred to forget, which meant it was damp, dark, and full of the particular silence that comes from people deciding collectively not to listen. We had pulled the first sigil-stone from its guardian-chamber two levels up; the prison proper sprawled below, cells carved from living rock back when the city was young and crueler in more formal ways.

Bram stopped at the mouth of the stair. "I can't follow you past this point. Not because of the moth's instructions." His jaw tightened. "Because it has woven the threshold out of prayers. Old ones. Prayers I answered before the covenant broke. If I cross, I feed it."

"So it built a fence out of your own kindness."

"It's very good at that."

I touched his hand—briefly, the way we'd both learned to touch each other, like people handling something they couldn't yet afford to keep. Then I went down alone, with three sigil-stones wrapped in oilcloth against my ribs and a knife I had no intention of using, because gods don't bleed and I'd only feel silly.

The prison opened into its central hall, and there was the god.

It wore no shape I could name, which is to say it wore all of them at once, softly: a nurse's apron, a beggar's blanket, a mother's shawl, the white coat of a plague-doctor two centuries dead. Its face was every face that had ever leaned over a sickbed and whispered *there, there*. Its eyes were the worst part. They were kind. Genuinely, deeply kind, the way a pillow is kind when it's being held over your face.

Between its hands—or what served as hands, folds of blanket-light—hung Ilse Varga.

She was suspended like a marionette whose strings had been replaced by threads of pale glow. Awake. Furious. Her Warden coat was gone; she hung in her shirt and trousers, boots missing, which struck me as somehow the most humiliating detail, the way captors always know exactly where dignity lives.

"Kavanagh," she rasped. Her voice was roughened but her spine was still iron. "You took your time."

"Traffic," I said.

"She jokes," the god said, and its voice was every comforting voice I'd ever heard layered together—the apothecary who numbed a wound, the priest at a funeral, me, telling clients *it won't hurt, just a little pinch behind the eyes.* That last one made my stomach turn. It had been listening to me work. Perhaps it had always been listening to everyone work. "Good. You'll need humor. What comes next is heavy."

"Put her down."

"I will. Gladly." The glow around Ilse pulsed, gentle as a heartbeat. "That is the entire purpose of this meeting. I am not your enemy, heir. I am the god of small mercies. Do you know what a small mercy is? It is the difference between a death that breaks you and a death you can carry. It is the lie that lets a dying man sleep. I am the reason the world is survivable. And you—" the blanket-folds spread wide, encompassing the stones against my ribs, the whole dripping dark of the prison, perhaps the whole world— "you are going to break the world, and call it justice."

"The covenant kept the realms apart for a thousand years."

"And strangled prayer for a thousand years!" For one instant the kindness flickered, and beneath it was something enormous and tired and *hurt*, and I understood suddenly that this god had spent centuries catching every unanswered prayer like a servant catching dishes thrown by a master. "Do you know what I do, little thief? When a mother prays over a fevered child and the child dies anyway—I am the one who makes her able to get up in the morning. I take the edge off. I soften the blow. That is mercy. Small, yes. But everything human is small, or have you forgotten? You dealt in small things yourself. Memories. Charms. Little mercies of forgetting." The eyes crinkled, warmly. "We are colleagues, you and I."

"We are nothing alike."

"Aren't we? How many people did you unmake, a pinch at a time, so their lives would be survivable?"

Ilse made a sound low in her throat—not agreement, exactly, but not disagreement either, and I hated that I heard it.

I put my hand flat against the oilcloth bundle at my ribs. "What's your offer."

"Simple. Beautiful. The stones, freely given. The girl goes free. The Wardens go back to their ledgers. The realms keep grinding, slowly, over decades instead of months—but decades! Long lives. Weddings. Harvests. Whole generations born and buried before anything truly ends." The god drifted closer, and the air filled with the smell of lavender and clean bandages. "Or: refuse, and I return her to you in pieces small enough to carry. I will make sure each piece is comfortable."

There it was. The trap inside the trap, the thing Bram had told me and I hadn't wanted to hear: it never threatened you directly. It threatened through the people you'd let matter.

I thought about the market burning. About neighbors in chains. About Marek the fence, whose only crime was keeping accounts. About Ilse herself, hanging there, who had hunted me for years and who had, two nights ago, stood shoulder to shoulder with me against the god's twisted manifestations and fought like someone paying off a debt.

Horror arrived first. Clarity came right behind it, the way thunder follows lightning, close enough to seem like the same event.

"You're lying," I said.

"I never lie." The god sounded almost wounded.

"You lie by telling truths." Bram's words, in my own mouth now. "Everything you say is true and shaped like a knife. Yes—you soften blows. And every softened blow teaches the world it doesn't have to change. Twelve years of unanswered prayers didn't happen because the covenant was cruel. They happened because your master—" I saw the flinch, small but real, in the blanket-folds— "paid the Wardens' founder to betray the covenant so that prayers would curdle, so that people would learn praying hurt, so that they'd turn to *comforts* instead. To you. To merchants like me. You didn't catch the falling dishes. You cut the ropes."

The hall went quiet. Even the water dripping somewhere in the dark seemed to hold its breath.

Then the god laughed, and the laugh was gentle, and that was the most frightening sound I had ever heard.

"Oh," it said softly, "you're going to be magnificent when you lose."

"I'm refusing," I said. My voice shook. I let it. Some refusals deserve to shake. "Keep her another day, another year—it won't change the answer. The stones don't go to you. Not for her, not for the city, not for anyone. She'd tell me the same. Wouldn't you, Commander?"

"Obviously," Ilse croaked. "Though I'd phrase it better."

"There," I said to the god, and my heart was hammering and my eyes were stinging and I hated every second of it. "Your offer is refused. Now tell me why you really brought me here. Because a god of small mercies doesn't waste a hostage on a negotiation it already knows it can't win. You wanted me here for something else."

For a long moment the god simply looked at me, all those kind eyes at once, and I felt it reassessing me the way a merchant reassesses goods discovered to be worth more than marked.

"Yes," it admitted at last. "Yes, I did." The glow holding Ilse dimmed—not releasing her, but slackening, the way a hand loosens when it's about to gesture. "Here is my final truth, heir, freely given, because I deal in truths the way you once dealt in memories. You cannot reforge the covenant. Not fully. Not without paying."

"The quest has a price. I assumed. Everything does."

"Not a price like gold. Not a price like blood—blood is cheap, I should know, I've mopped up enough of it. The covenant is a bond between realms, and bonds must be anchored. The anchor is the heir. The reforging requires the heir to give up—" and here the god's voice dropped into something almost tender, almost sorry, the voice of a doctor delivering a diagnosis— "*the thing she values most.*"

The words went into me coldly, precisely, like a needle finding the one vein that matters.

"That's vague," I managed.

"It must remain vague. The covenant chooses. It looks into the heir's heart and takes what it finds there gleaming brightest." The god tilted its many-faced head. "I confess I don't know what it will find in you. I find that fascinating. Most heirs, it would have been obvious. Land. Legacy. Power. But you—" the warmth deepened, insidious as laudanum— "you are a woman who has spent her life owning nothing, hoarding only what others discarded, letting go of everything. What could such a woman possibly value most? Surely not her life; you gave that up years ago, when you started selling pieces of it. Surely not the stones; duty is cheap to the dutiful." The eyes glittered. "Unless, of course, she's recently acquired something new. Something she kept rather than sold. Something she's begun, quite recklessly, to treasure."

My hand moved before I could stop it. Not toward the stones.

Toward the memory. The hidden one, the stolen hour I'd never distilled, never sold, never even named—Bram at my stall in the rain, laughing at my terrible tea, the first hour of him that lived behind my eyes like a coal that wouldn't cool.

The god watched the motion. Smiled with every face it owned.

"There it is," it whispered.

"You're lying," I said again, but my voice cracked on the word *lying*, and clarity came again, merciless, arriving right beside the horror like a twin.

"I told you. I never lie. I merely arrange truths until they cut." The glow around Ilse brightened again, firm now, the bargain withdrawn but the prisoner kept. "Take your time, heir. Deliberate. Despair. I'm patient—I have been patient for thirteen years. But know this: the thirteenth moon rises in six days. And when it completes its turn, the choice will be made for you, one way or another." The blanket-folds began to close around Ilse like a curtain. "Do give my regards to Bram. Tell him his brother sends love."

The hall emptied of god the way a room empties of candlelight—not gone, but everywhere else now, in the walls, in the water, in the smell of lavender that would never quite wash out.

Ilse's glow faded with it. She dropped—caught herself badly, hit the stone floor on her knees, and stayed there a moment, breathing hard, a soldier relearning gravity.

Then she raised her head, and her eyes were wet with fury, and she said:

"Six days. Then we finish this. Both of us. Whatever it costs."

I helped her up. Her arm was ice and iron under my hands.

Above us, through the broken crown of the aqueduct, the sky was already bruising toward evening—and somewhere beyond the hills, unseen, patient as arithmetic, the thirteenth moon was climbing toward its final turn.
