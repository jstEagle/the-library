## 17. Moonrise Over Corvane

The twelfth moon did not rise so much as *arrive*, the way an unwanted guest arrives—already in the room before you've finished deciding you'd rather it weren't.

Freya felt it first in her charms.

She had been sorting her remaining stock on the workbench behind the shop—Ilse Varga's rewritten law said she could sell openly now, and the strangeness of that hadn't worn off yet; she still flinched when customers knocked instead of whispering through shutters. The charms were humming. All of them at once, a low chord like a wet finger run around the rim of a glass: the lullaby charm, the first-kiss charm, the little vial of somebody's grandmother's kitchen, bread and cardamom and rain on a tin roof.

"Stop that," Freya told them.

They hummed louder.

Through the window, over the crooked teeth of Corvane's rooftops, the twelfth moon came up fat and bruise-colored, one turn shy of full completion of its impossible thirteenth-year cycle. Thirteen moons in a single year—the astronomers had given up explaining it months ago and taken up drinking. It hung there like an open mouth, and beneath it, all across the city, the holes between realms began to yawn wider.

Freya knew because the dead started strolling past her window.

Not ghostly, not wispy, not the pale shivering things from cheap melodramas. These were *solid* dead, wearing their old coats, complaining about the cold. An old man in a fishmonger's apron paused outside her shop to read the new sign—FREYA KAVANAGH, CHARMWRIGHT, LICENSED—and nodded approvingly, as though he'd known her grandmother, which he might have, being several decades deceased and recently ambulatory.

"You want to watch the mortar on your east wall," he advised through the glass. "It's crumbling."

"Thank you," said Freya, who had learned in the last two days that arguing with the returned dead was like arguing with weather.

He wandered off toward the harbor, presumably to inspect his old stall, or possibly just because walking was pleasant after thirteen years of wherever the dead went. That was the thing nobody had predicted about the holes widening—not horrors first, but *pedestrians*. The forgotten dead of Corvane were coming home, and they were mostly bewildered, mildly annoyed, and deeply interested in how much rent cost now.

Then the other things came too.

* * *

Bram found her on the roof, because Bram always found her, because he was a god and gods were terrible about boundaries.

"The baker's street is gone," he said without preamble, dropping onto the tiles beside her with the casual grace of someone who had once been worshipped for exactly this sort of entrance. "Swallowed. Not destroyed—*translated*. There's a field of white poppies there now, growing where the ovens were. And the poppies are singing."

"Singing what?"

"Hymns. Badly." He tipped his head back toward the moon. His face, which usually held a kind of patient amusement at the mortal world, was grave tonight, and older than the city. "The realms are grinding, Freya. Where they rub against each other, things leak through in both directions. My realm leaks wonder. Their realm—" he gestured, broadly, at the singing poppies, at the dead fishmonger, at a distant street where the shadows had begun flowing uphill—"leaks everything else."

"How long?"

"Till the thirteenth turn? Hours. Perhaps less. The moon is nearly full of its year."

Freya pulled her coat tighter. Below them, Corvane was doing what Corvane always did in crisis: adapting with furious pragmatism. Neighbors were hauling their furniture away from streets that had become meadows. A woman was selling roasted chestnuts to the dead, who found the novelty delightful. Somewhere a Warden bell rang—not the alarm pattern, something newer, faster.

"Ilse," Freya said.

"Ilse," Bram agreed. "The council has voted to seal the gates and let the districts fend for themselves. She's decided otherwise."

"Can she decide otherwise? She's one woman."

Bram smiled, and there was something almost fond in it, which alarmed Freya more than the singing flowers did. "You've spent twelve years underestimating what one stubborn woman can do. So did I, once. Come on. If we're going to assault a god's altar tonight, I'd rather we did it with an army."

"We don't have an army."

"We might," said Bram, "in about ten minutes."

* * *

The Wardens' assembly hall had not hosted this many people since the founder's last great ceremony, and Ilse Varga stood on the dais where the founder's monument used to be—it was a scorched socket of stone now, the third sigil-stone pried free days ago by a thief she had been hunting for twelve years and now, absurdly, called an ally.

The hall was packed. Wardens lined the walls in full kit. Behind them, pressing at the doors, were citizens—bakers, dockworkers, the healed beggar from the Tallow District holding his healing like a candle he didn't know where to set down. The dead were there too, standing politely at the back because even in crisis, Corvane's dead retained their manners.

On the council bench above, the nine councilors sat in a row like nine sour plums, and Councilor Edmun Bray was on his feet.

"—outrageous. Unprecedented. The districts beyond the walls are lost, Commander, and the correct response is triage, not—what is it you're proposing? *Marching out?* Toward the phenomenon?"

"The phenomenon," Ilse said, "has a name. It has an altar. It has a god underneath our aqueduct who has been eating this city's mercy for thirteen years and spending it on cruelty." She let the silence stretch. She had learned silence from her mother, who had been a Warden before the purge, whose case files Ilse had read until the candles died. "And every one of you signed the order that fed it."

Bray's jowls went gray. "That order predates all of us—"

"The order is thirteen years old. The *policy* is thirteen years old. The ban on small magics, the burning of the memory markets, the arrests of charmwrights and hedge-healers and grandmothers who sang over broken bones—" Her voice did not rise. That was the trick of it; the angrier she got, the quieter and more precise she became, until the whole hall leaned in to hear. "Every prayer those people whispered while we confiscated their hope went somewhere. It went to him. We built his altar out of confiscated prayers and called it law."

"You have proof of these accusations?"

Ilse lifted a leather folder. Inside it: the founder's confession, extracted from the sealed vault three nights ago. Pages written in a hand every Warden recognized from their training plaques.

"I have his handwriting," she said, "admitting he sold the covenant to a god for power, and I have eleven years of my own case files showing exactly who profited. But no, Councilor, I don't intend to put it to a vote."

"You intend to stage a coup."

"No." Ilse looked out at the ranks of Wardens—young ones, old ones, faces she had drilled and disciplined and, yes, loved in the fierce unlovely way commanders love soldiers. "I intend to ask them."

The hall went very still. Councils gave orders. Commanders gave orders. Nobody *asked*.

"Wardens of Corvane," she said. "For twelve years we enforced a lie. Some of us suspected. Most of us didn't. That ends tonight. The covenant can be reforged before moonset—but only if we hold the streets while three people go into the dark under the aqueduct to do it. The council orders us to stand down. I'm asking you to stand up. Those who follow the council's order, leave now, with my respect and no reprisal. Those who follow me—" she drew her sword and laid it flat on the dais, hilt outward, the oldest gesture in the order, older than the lie itself, "—do the same."

A heartbeat. Two.

Then the ringing clang of steel on stone, one blade after another, rolling through the hall like hail on a roof, and the plum-faced councilors watched their entire military lay down its loyalty in front of them and pick it back up pointed elsewhere.

Councilor Bray sat down slowly, like a man discovering the chair had moved.

Ilse allowed herself exactly one breath of vertigo—*my mother tried to say this, once, and they burned her for it*—and then turned to the doors.

"Open the gates," she said. "All of them. And somebody find the fishmonger's ghost—I'm told he knows the aqueduct tunnels better than any living surveyor."

* * *

They gathered at the ruined aqueduct as the moon climbed toward its zenith: three sigil-stones wrapped in cloth on Freya's back, Bram glowing faintly at her side like a lantern trying to be subtle, and behind them, spilling down the embankment with torches and improvised weapons and a truly alarming number of soup pots, the people of Corvane.

Not the army Ilse had mustered—that force was holding the streets against the things leaking uphill from the shadow-side of the veil, buying time with discipline and pike-walls and occasional creative blasphemy. This crowd was the other kind. The small-magic folk. Charmwrights and hedge-healers, the ones Ilse had spent a week un-arresting, the neighbors whose names Freya had spent twelve years carefully not knowing because names were dangerous and invisibility was survival.

They knew her name now. They were chanting it, badly, in a rhythm that suggested none of them had ever chanted anything before.

"This is mortifying," Freya muttered.

"It's wonderful," said Bram.

"They're chanting my name to the tune of a *drinking song*."

"That's traditional. All the best heroes got drunk-songs eventually. You're just getting yours early."

An old woman pressed through the crowd—Maren, the hedge-healer whose arrest Ilse had reversed personally, whose hands smelled forever of comfrey. She took Freya's face in her rough palms and looked at her the way you look at a granddaughter before she does something either magnificent or fatal.

"You come back up," Maren said. "Whatever's down there. Whatever it costs. You come back up, girl, and you let us carry the rest."

Freya wanted to say something brave. What came out was: "There's a hole in the east wall of my shop, the mortar's bad, could someone—"

"We'll fix the wall," said forty people at once.

She laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her—and turned to the dark mouth of the tunnel where the god of small mercies waited with everything he had ever stolen arranged between her and the altar. The moon stood directly overhead. The air tasted of ozone and lilies and something older underneath, like a coin left too long in a dead man's fist.

Beside her, Bram took her hand. His was warm. Gods ran warm, she'd learned; it was one of her favorite discoveries about divinity.

"If he offers you yourself again," Bram said quietly, "remember—he lies by telling truths. He'll show you the exact price and make it sound unbearable, and it will be unbearable, and we'll pay it anyway, because that's what covenants are."

"And what are you?" she asked, because the moon was high and she might never get another chance to ask. "At the end of it. When the covenant binds you again—when you can only answer freely offered prayers—will you still come to my door?"

Bram looked at her for a long moment, moonlight and tunnel-dark fighting across his face.

"Freya Kavanagh," he said, "I answered a prayer the night I met you, and no one offered it, and no covenant compelled me. Whatever we reforge tonight will be weaker than that."

From the tunnel below, sweet and hollow as a music box, the god of small mercies began to sing her name.

Freya squeezed his hand once, shouldered the stones, and walked down into the dark.
