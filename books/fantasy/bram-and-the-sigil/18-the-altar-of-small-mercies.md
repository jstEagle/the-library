## 18. The Altar of Small Mercies

The aqueduct had been ruined for a hundred years, but its bones still stood—arches like broken ribs marching out of Corvane's oldest quarter and into the hills, carrying nothing now but ivy and pigeons. Beneath it, where the old engineers had cut their maintenance galleries into the living rock, the god of small mercies had made his home.

Freya felt him before she saw anything: a warmth in her chest like a coal that wouldn't die, the same feeling she'd had at every answered prayer, every hole torn between realms. It was stronger here. It was everywhere here.

"Cheerful place," Ilse Varga said. She carried a Warden lantern in one hand and a Warden blade in the other, and she wore both like accusations. "Damp, dark, smells of old water and worse intentions. Very on-brand for a god."

"He was never like this," Bram said quietly. He walked ahead of them, and the dark seemed to make way for him reluctantly, the way a crowd parts for someone they'd rather not look at. "Before the covenant broke, he was the smallest of us. The kindest. He answered the prayers nobody else bothered with—a lost child finding her way home, a coin found when rent was due. Small mercies." His jaw tightened. "Thirteen years alone in the dark, feeding on the gaps we left behind. That will rot anyone."

"Then let's not keep him waiting," Freya said. "Rot spreads."

They descended through the maintenance gallery, down stairs worn smooth by feet that had been dead for centuries, until the passage opened into a chamber so vast that Ilse's lantern couldn't find its ceiling. And there, at the center, on an altar built from the shattered pieces of a hundred shrines—the little household altars, the roadside cairns, the chapel stones of the drowned church—they saw him.

He looked like everyone. That was the horror of it. He looked like the baker who'd sold Freya buns as a girl, like Ilse's mother in her Warden grays, like a kind-eyed stranger at a market stall, his face shifting gently from shape to shape, always almost someone you loved. He smiled at them with all those borrowed mouths at once.

"The heir," he said, and his voice was every voice that had ever said *there, there* over a wound. "And the traitor's sword-arm. And—" his gaze settled on Bram, and for one moment his face stopped shifting, and it was old, and tired, and terribly sad, "—brother."

"I'm not your brother tonight," Bram said.

"No," the god agreed. "Tonight you're the doorstop."

The altar flared. And the mercies came.

* * *

They came out of the walls like smoke given purpose, and each one wore the shape of a kindness that had gone wrong somewhere along the way.

The first was a mother's mercy: a woman of gray light who wrapped her arms around Freya and whispered, *let me carry it for you*, and began to drink her memories straight out of her skull. Freya felt her seventh winter going soft at the edges and drove her elbow backward into nothing that shrieked anyway.

"Don't let them finish their sentences!" Bram shouted from the threshold behind them. He had planted himself at the gallery mouth, arms spread, holding open the way they'd come—and holding closed everything else trying to squeeze through. Light poured off him like heat off a forge. "A twisted mercy is only half a lie! Make it say the whole thing!"

"How?" Ilse demanded, hacking at a mercy shaped like a surgeon's gentle hands, hands that had once held a dying man and now simply held, endlessly, crushing slowly, murmuring *rest now, rest now.*

"It promised rest! Demand to know whose!"

Ilse grabbed the thing by its glowing wrists. "*Whose rest?*" she snarled.

The hands shuddered. The light flickered—and beneath it Freya glimpsed the truth of it, the way you glimpse the frame under a painting: a Warden, decades dead, ordered by his own commander to give a prisoner "rest" that meant a quiet grave. The mercy screamed, *HIS, HIS, I ONLY MEANT HIS,* and came apart like sugar in rain.

"Oh," Ilse said. Something moved across her face that wasn't battle-fury at all. "Oh, you *coward.*"

She stopped fighting them like monsters after that. She started fighting them like case files.

A mercy of bread offered food that would have starved a family while feeding a lord's hounds—*whose hunger?* A mercy of shelter that had locked doors against the wrong refugees—*whose safety?* Each one, forced to name the person it had actually served, broke apart in shame. It turned out, Freya thought wildly, parrying a tendril of light that wanted to tuck her into bed forever, that cruelty kept alive by calling itself kindness could not survive being called by its own name.

She should know. She'd spent twelve years doing gentler versions of the same thing.

The thought hit her mid-swing, and she nearly went down under a mercy shaped like a lullaby.

"You're stalling," the god observed. He hadn't moved from the altar. He didn't need to; the chamber was full of him. "You're thinking about your charms, little thief. About the forgetting you sold and the remembering you kept. Do you want to confess? Confession is a mercy. I'm very good at those."

"Later," Freya gasped. "I'm busy."

Behind them, Bram cried out.

* * *

She heard it before she understood it—the sound of something giving way, the sound Bram made when the threshold he was holding took more than even a god could shrug off.

She turned, and the sight of it carved itself into her deeper than any memory she'd ever stolen.

Bram stood in the gallery mouth, and the dark beyond him was pressing in—not shadow, but the raw between-place, the grinding seam of two realms, full of shapes that had no business having edges. He held it with his whole body, arms braced against nothing visible, and where the seam touched him his skin was going gray and grainy, like stone left out in weather. Like a statue of himself being sculpted by something that hated him.

"Bram!"

"I'm fine," he said, in the tone people use when they are demonstrably not fine and know it. "Go. The altar. I've got the door." He grinned at her, blood-bright at the corner of his mouth, and added, with terrible gentleness, "Freya. Whatever happens—go."

Something in her chest tore neatly down the middle, and she discovered that the space behind the tear was fury.

She crossed the chamber faster than she'd ever moved in her life. Ilse covered her, blade singing, shouting the names of mercies like a magistrate reading charges—*the orphan tithe! the plague houses! the pardon bought with silence!*—and each named cruelty burst around her like rotten fruit. Freya reached the altar steps and climbed them three at a time, and the god of small mercies finally stopped smiling.

"You can't reforge it here," he said. "You need all three stones. You have two."

Freya set her hand flat on the altar, on the jumble of stolen shrine-stones, and felt the third one answer her blood like a struck bell. Sigil-blood. Kavanagh blood. Her mother's mother's mother's covenant, humming under her palm.

"They're not three stones," she said. "They were never three stones. They're one stone the Wardens broke into three and scattered so no heir could ever do this." She lifted it free—all of it, the shards leaping together into her hand like iron filings to a lodestone, knitting as they came. "You just assumed the lie would hold. You've been assuming that for thirteen years."

For the first time, the god's face stopped changing. It settled into one shape—one small, plain, tired face, a face like a country priest's, like somebody's overworked uncle—and what filled it was fear.

"Little thief," he said softly, "you don't know what it costs."

"I'm about to find out."

Behind her, Bram screamed.

It was not a scream she had ever imagined him capable of making. She spun, and the seam had him—it had gotten past his guard somehow, or he had *let* it, she would wonder about that later, she would wonder about it for the rest of her life—and the gray was climbing past his shoulders now, up his throat, and he was still holding the door, still holding it, because if he let go the between-place would pour into the chamber and take all three of them before Freya finished a single word of the rite.

"Hold the threshold!" Ilse roared, throwing herself between Freya and the last of the mercies, taking a blow across the shoulder that would have felled an ox, staying up anyway. "That's an order, god! You hold that door or I swear by every law I ever enforced—"

"Ilse," Freya said. Her voice came out strange and calm. "Watch the door with him."

"What?"

"Whatever comes through—I can't fight and forge at once. Neither can you. So we do this the stupid way." She planted her feet on the altar step, sigil-stone blazing in her fist, and looked back at Bram one more time. He was watching her. Of course he was. Even now, half-turned to stone, he was watching her with those kind eyes, and he shook his head—*don't, don't pay it, find another way*—and she smiled at him because it was the only weapon she had left that worked.

"Ilse," she said. "If I start to forget him—remind me I chose it."

"Freya—"

"*Remind me.*"

She turned to the altar, raised the mended sigil-stone high, and spoke the first words of a covenant thirteen years broken.

The chamber went white. The god of small mercies began, very quietly, to laugh—or perhaps to weep; from him it had always been the same sound. And the price, which had been waiting patiently all this time like a bill unpaid, stepped forward out of the light to name itself.

Freya felt it reach for her, and knew exactly what it would take, and did not stop speaking.
