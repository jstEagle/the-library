## 19. The Thirteenth Turn

The altar beneath the aqueduct had stopped being a place and started being a wound.

Freya felt it in her teeth first—a low grinding hum, like the city itself was clenching its jaw. Then in the stones under her knees, which trembled in a rhythm too slow to be an earthquake and too steady to be anything kind. Above, through the broken ribs of the aqueduct, the thirteenth moon climbed into the space the twelfth moon had barely vacated, fat and bruise-colored, and the sky around it shimmered like the surface of a pond someone had dropped a stone into.

The three sigil-stones lay in a triangle before her, humming in answer to the moon. The traitor's stone still smelled faintly of Warden marble and Ilse's fury. The drowned chapel's stone was cold as a held breath. And the first stone—the guardian's stone, the one that had made her relive the night she'd stolen her own mother's forgetting—pulsed like a small, stubborn heart.

Bram knelt across from her, and he was smiling, which was ridiculous, because his side was soaked through and his hands were shaking, and because the world was ending, and he was smiling at her the way he'd smiled at her on the ordinary day, the stolen day, when they'd bought bad apples from a vendor who'd cheated them both and argued about whether the stars were holes or lamps.

"Stop that," she said.

"Stop what?"

"Smiling like this is going to be fine."

"It is going to be fine," he said. "Eventually. In the way that most things are fine eventually, which is to say, not fine first."

"You are the worst god I have ever loved," she said, and then heard herself say it, and felt the words land somewhere in her chest and settle there, permanent as a brand.

Bram's smile went soft and crooked. "I'm the only god you've ever loved."

"That doesn't make you less the worst."

Ilse Varga stood at the threshold behind them, sword drawn, watching the dark of the tunnel where the god of small mercies had poured himself out and been refused. Her knuckles were white on the hilt. She did not look at them. She was looking at the hole in the world, and her face had the expression of a woman who had spent her whole life guarding a door and had just learned the door had never once been locked.

"Kavanagh," she said, without turning. "Whatever you're going to do. Do it soon. The dark is getting ideas."

* * *

The reforging was not complicated. That was the first cruel joke of it. Thirteen years of dread, a city of stolen prayers, a god's whole campaign of lies—and the covenant came down to this: three stones, one heir, and the asking.

Freya laid her palms over the first stone. It was warm. It had always been warm, from the very first moment in the dark under the aqueduct, when the guardian had held her worst memory up to her face like a mirror and asked, *Is this yours to carry?* and she had said, through tears and fury, *Yes. It's mine. I stole it fair.*

"I, Freya Kavanagh," she began, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect, "last of the covenant line, heir of the binding, do ask the old accord to wake."

The stones flared. Light ran between them like water finding its old channels, and the grinding hum in the world changed pitch—deepened, quickened, became almost a voice. The moon overhead slid a fraction further into its final place, and the sky rippled, and somewhere far above them Freya heard the sound of Corvane holding its breath.

*What is offered,* said the covenant, in a voice that was not a voice, in the space behind her sternum where she kept the things she never sold.

"Everything that is needed," Freya said.

*What is valued,* said the covenant.

And Freya—clever Freya, thief Freya, who had spent thirteen years reading what people wanted before they knew they wanted it, who could price a memory by the set of a stranger's shoulders—felt the question go through her like a fishhook through water, and she understood.

Not her life. She had offered her life, had squared her shoulders for it, had already written the small bitter speech she'd give about how a thief's death was a fair price for a city. The covenant wasn't interested. Lives were cheap; the realms had spent thousands of them already, and the grinding had not slowed.

*What is valued.*

Her hands went cold.

"No," Bram said.

She hadn't moved. She hadn't spoken. But he was looking at her across the triangle of stones with his face gone white under the godlight, and she realized her expression had told him everything, the way his had always told her everything, from the first day at her stall when he'd asked to return a memory instead of buy one.

"Freya." His voice cracked in the middle of her name. "No. There's another way. There's always—"

"Is there?" she said gently. "You've had thirteen years to find one. Longer. You've had *centuries*."

"There has to be—"

"Bram." She reached across the stones and took his hand. It was shaking. Hers was not, which surprised her; she'd expected terror, and what she found instead was something quieter and much worse—a terrible clarity, like the moment before a theft when you know exactly where the memory sits and exactly what it will cost to lift it. "You told me once that the god of small mercies lies by telling truths. Well. The covenant doesn't lie at all. It just asks. And I know what it's asking."

*What is valued,* said the covenant again, patient as stone, and the moon slid another fraction, and the sky above the aqueduct tore a little wider, and through the tear Freya could see—not fire, not darkness, but something worse: the *other* realm, pressing close, curious, hungry, its edges made of every unanswered prayer ever spoken.

* * *

She had kept them. That was the thing. In thirteen years of stealing, of selling, of pouring other people's lives into charms and watching them walk away lighter, she had stolen exactly one life for herself and never sold a drop of it.

Him.

The first day at the stall, when he'd stood there with his too-clever smile and asked for something back that she hadn't known she had. The taste of that memory—divine light in an ordinary evening, a god walking through a night market like a man, the strange ache of looking at a stranger and feeling *recognized*. She'd kept it because she couldn't sell it; no charm could hold it; the light burned through every vessel she tried.

The aqueduct, and the guardian's test. The flooded chapel, where drowned voices sang and she had given her first willing memory and he had held her in the cold water after, and she had thought: *oh. So this is what people mean.* The bad apples. The argument about the stars. The morning he'd fallen asleep sitting up in her shop chair with a charm catalog open on his chest and she'd watched him for an hour instead of working, and had thought, with the particular despair of a woman who knows her own trade too well: *I am hoarding. I am hoarding him, hour by hour, and I will never sell a single one, and someday this hoard will be the death of me.*

She'd thought it was a figure of speech.

*What is valued.*

"It's not fair," she said, and laughed, and the laugh came out wet. "You know what's funny? I've spent my whole life taking memories from people who wanted to forget. And the one thing I ever wanted to keep—"

"Freya." Bram had both her hands now, across the stones, the light pouring up between their fingers. His eyes were doing the thing they did, the lamp-not-hole thing, the thing she would never see again after tonight and knew it and was already grieving it and was already, even now, memorizing it, the way she memorized everything, because old habits—

"Listen to me," he said. "The covenant takes what's valued *most*. Not what's loved most. There's a difference. There has to be a difference. You value your mother's face. You value Ilse's alliance, the city, the—"

"I value those," she said. "I love you. The covenant isn't asking what I'd defend. It's asking what I'd *keep*." She swallowed. "And you know it too. I saw your face when it asked. You've known since the chapel, haven't you. Since I gave that memory freely and it *cost*, really cost, and you looked at me like—"

"Like I was watching someone practice dying," Bram said, and his voice was very quiet, and very old, and it was the voice of a god who had watched thirteen years of prayers go unanswered and had finally, finally been answered himself, in the worst possible grammar. "I hoped I was wrong. I have been alive for a very long time, Freya Kavanagh, and I have hoped I was wrong about a great many things, and I have *always* been wrong about hoping."

Behind them, Ilse shouted something—the dark was moving, the tear was widening, the moon was *almost there*—and Freya heard it the way you hear rain on a roof when you're saying goodbye to someone at a door.

* * *

The moon reached its place.

The world went silent. Not quiet—silent, the way a held breath is silent, the whole realm suspended between the last turn and the first, and in that silence the covenant spoke one final time, and this time it did not ask.

*The price is known. The heir will speak the terms.*

Freya Kavanagh knelt between three stones under a broken aqueduct, holding the hands of a god, and spoke.

"I give," she said, "every memory I have of Bram. Kept, not stolen. Given, not sold. Freely."

"Freya—"

"Every hour," she said, louder, over him, because if she stopped she would never start. "The stall. The apples. The stars. The chapel. The day of pretending to be ordinary people, which was the best day of my entire life, which I am now spending my last ordinary moments cataloguing like the greedy little thief I have always been." Her voice broke and she let it. "I give the way he says my name. I give the way he looks at lamplight. I give—"

"Stop." He was crying. She had never seen a god cry, and it was the worst thing she had ever seen, worse than the tear in the sky, worse than the drowned singing, worse than the god of small mercies with his gentle terrible offers. "Freya, please, there is always another way, there is *always*—"

"There isn't," she said. "And here's the thing, Bram. Here's the thing I figured out in the ashes, when the god of small mercies offered me the shortcut." She leaned across the stones, across the light, and pressed her forehead to his. "He offered to take my memories and I almost said yes. Because I was tired. Because forgetting seemed like rest. But you told me he lies by telling truths, and I finally understood what you meant: everything he offers is true, and everything he takes is *everything*. The covenant isn't like that. The covenant asks for one thing and means one thing, and it asked me what I valued most, and the honest answer—the answer I'm not ashamed of, even now, even *especially* now—is you."

The light was rising around them. The stones were singing. The thirteenth moon stood at its final turn, and the tear in the sky began, slowly, to knit.

"So take them," Freya whispered. "Take all of it. And when it's done, when I look at you and don't know you—be kind to me. That's my only term. Be kind to me, and I'll be kind to you, and we'll both pretend it doesn't hurt, because I'm very good at pretending and you taught me everything I know about—"

She stopped.

Because Bram was no longer crying. He was looking at her with an expression she had never seen on him before—fierce, and sudden, and lit from underneath like the first stone—and he was gripping her hands hard enough to bruise, and he said:

"No."

"Bram, the moon—"

"*No.* I am a god of the old accord, heir, and I am *party to this covenant*, and I have not spoken my terms." His eyes were blazing. "You want to give me every memory you have of me? Fine. *Fine.* But the covenant takes what is valued most—and I am valued by two people in this room, Freya Kavanagh, and I have had thirteen years and more to decide what *I* would keep."

Freya's heart stopped. "Bram, no. You can't. If you give *your* memories of me, then neither of us—there'll be nothing left, no one to carry it, no one who even knows what was—"

"Then we'll be two strangers," Bram said, "who feel, for no reason they can name, that the world was just saved by someone worth knowing. And I will spend the rest of forever being kind to you, and neither of us will know why, and it will *ache*, and it will be worth it, because that is what love weighs, and I have finally, finally learned to pay my own debts instead of watching mortals pay them for me."

The moon turned.

The light came down like a bell being struck, and Freya felt the covenant take hold of her hoard—all of it, the whole secret treasury, every stolen hour—and she clung to his hands and to the last seconds of knowing him, cataloguing, always cataloguing, the blaze of his eyes, the grip of his hands, the terrible stubborn shape of his jaw, and she heard herself say, one final time, in the space between the last turn and the first:

"You are the worst god I have ever—"

And the light took the rest.

* * *

The covenant sealed with a sound like the whole world exhaling.

The tear in the sky closed. The grinding stopped. The moon, whole and ordinary now, just a moon, began its long slow slide back down the far side of the sky, and beneath the aqueduct the three stones went dark and quiet and cool, finished, at last, with their work.

Freya Kavanagh knelt between them, whole, and hollow, and weeping without knowing why.

Across the stones, a stranger with kind eyes and a bleeding side was also weeping without knowing why, and when he saw her looking, he smiled—a crooked, aching smile, the smile of a man who has lost something enormous and cannot remember what it was and knows, with everything in him, that it was worth it.

"Hello," he said.

"Hello," said Freya, and wiped her face, and frowned at her own tears, and at his, and at the strange full feeling in her chest, like a room from which all the furniture had been carried out but which still, somehow, knew it had been loved in. "I'm sorry. Do I—have we met?"

"Everyone's met everyone, somewhere," the stranger said, and his voice caught on something, and he laughed at himself, wet and helpless. "I'm Bram."

"Freya," she said. "Freya Kavanagh."

And somewhere in the dark of the tunnel behind them, Ilse Varga lowered her sword, and said, very quietly, to no one at all:

"Oh, you *idiot* gods. Both of you."

Above the broken aqueduct, dawn was coming. And in the stranger's hand, held so tightly his knuckles had gone white, was a small charm-vial that had not been there before—glass, and stoppered, and glowing faintly with a light like lamplight through a hole in the night sky—labeled, in handwriting neither of them recognized, though it made them both ache:

*For the thief, if she ever wants it back.*
