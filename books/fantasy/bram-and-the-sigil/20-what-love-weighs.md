## 20. What Love Weighs

*Freya*

The altar of small mercies was quiet now.

That seemed wrong to her. Everything that had happened in this chamber—the twisted mercies with their smiling faces, the blood on the threshold, Ilse's ragged breathing somewhere behind her—should have left a noise in the stone forever. Instead the cavern beneath the aqueduct held only stillness, and the three sigil-stones hovering above the broken altar like patient moons, waiting for the thirteenth to rise.

Freya stood before them with Bram's hand in hers and tried not to think of it as an ending. She failed.

"Tell me again," she said. "Tell me there's another way."

"There's another way." He said it immediately, without hesitation, and that was the cruelest thing he could have done, because she could hear the lie in it the way she'd once heard lies in every client who swore they only wanted to forget *a little*, just the edges, just the worst of it. Bram had been telling truths for two thousand years. She knew the texture of them now. This wasn't one.

"Don't," she whispered. "Don't make me be the sensible one. I've never been the sensible one. That's always been you."

"I'm not being sensible." His voice cracked, and for a moment he was not a god grown weary of centuries but simply a man with kind eyes watching something terrible approach at a walking pace. "Sensible would have been staying away from your stall that night. Sensible would have been letting the realms grind together and taking you somewhere the grinding couldn't reach. I am being *selfish*, Freya Kavanagh, and I know it, and I can't seem to stop."

She turned to look at him. The light of the sigil-stones caught his face the way candlelight catches glass—warmth passing through, nothing kept. Thirteen years since the covenant broke. Two thousand years before that of prayers and patience and loving mortals from across a widening gap. And in all that time, she thought, no one had ever asked him to hold anything. Gods carried. Mortals were carried.

"Well," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved, "then we're both selfish, because I don't want to do it either."

* * *

*Bram*

There were perhaps four hundred hours of her that he kept.

He had counted once, in the early sleepless days after her stall, after the sigil-blood, after everything began—not out of sentiment but out of the old habit of inventory. A god who has watched empires forget him learns to keep ledgers. Four hundred hours: the way she haggled (viciously, dishonestly, charmingly), the way she hummed when she wove charms and lied about humming afterward. The morning she'd eaten stolen plums in a graveyard and pronounced them better for the theft. The night at the drowned chapel, when grief became a thing they carried between them instead of alone. The ordinary day—his favorite hour of all four hundred, though he'd never told her so—when they had pretended to be nobody, buying bread, arguing about whether pigeons had opinions, and she had laughed with her whole self, unguarded, and he had thought: *this. If I could keep one mortal hour for the rest of eternity, this is the one.*

Now she meant to pour all four hundred into the sigil. Not just him. Him most of all.

"You could keep some," he said. bargaining was beneath gods and he did it anyway. "The covenant says what she values most. It doesn't say *all* of it. Keep the plum morning. Keep the pigeons."

"Bram."

"Keep one hour. Pick any hour. I'll pick for you if picking hurts too much—"

"*Bram.*" Her hand tightened around his, and he felt it—the tremor she was trying to hide, running through her fingers like water under ice. "You know it doesn't work like that. You've known longer than I have. The covenant doesn't take a tithe. It takes the whole heart of the thing, or it takes nothing, and if it takes nothing then the thirteenth moon rises and Corvane goes with it." She smiled, and it was a terrible smile, bright and wet. "You taught me that. Lesson eight. Or nine. You had a lot of lessons."

"Lesson eleven," he said hoarsely. "Gods lie by telling truths. But mortals—" He stopped. He could not finish it.

"But mortals love by giving things away," she finished for him. "You taught me that too."

* * *

*Freya*

She had sold thousands of memories. She knew exactly how it went.

A memory, lifted properly, didn't vanish like smoke. It went like water from a cup poured into another cup—you could feel the weight leave you, feel the shape of it remain as an absence, a socket where a tooth had been. Clients described it as a gap in a conversation, a name on the tip of the tongue forever. Most of them thanked her.

This would not be like that. This would be one cup pouring itself out until it was empty, and the sigil drinking, and Freya Kavanagh standing at the end of it holding a vessel that had once held a god and now held nothing at all.

"I want to say it aloud first," she said. "While I still know why it matters. Will you listen?"

"I will always listen."

"The plum morning," she began, and her voice shook, and she let it. "The graveyard on Vell Street, east corner, under the crooked angel. You stole the plums, which is hilarious, because what does a god need with stolen fruit, and you looked so *pleased* with yourself, like a cat presenting a dead bird. And I ate them and the juice ran down my wrist and I thought—I remember thinking—that I had spent my whole life taking memories from people because I wanted to know what it felt like to have moments worth keeping. And there I was, standing in the dirt with purple stains on my hand, keeping one."

He made a sound low in his chest. She pressed on.

"The pigeons. You swore the fat one had opinions about me. You did the voice. You did a *pigeon voice*, Bram, an ancient god doing pigeon ventriloquism, and I laughed so hard the baker's wife came out to see what was wrong with me." A breath. "My mother's lullaby—I gave that away once, sold it, before I knew what I was. But you found it. You went and found it and brought it back to me in a vial, and you said, 'Some things shouldn't be for sale,' and that was the first time I thought maybe I could be a person who kept things instead of a person who took them."

"The chapel," she went on, softer now. "The singing. Your hand on the back of my neck when I gave my memory freely for the first time and thought my legs would give out, and they didn't, because you were there. Every lesson you ever taught me, numbered wrong on purpose so I'd argue with you. The way you say my full name like it's a spell. The way you looked at me last night when you thought I was asleep."

Her voice finally broke.

"That one," she said. "That look. That's the one I wish I could keep."

* * *

*Bram*

He had held dying kings. He had held the last priestess of a faith older than Corvane's walls. He had stood at thresholds for millennia while realms tore at each other's seams, and he had never once wept, because gods weep inwardly or not at all.

He wept now, quietly, the way rain begins.

"Then keep it," he said. "Not in your head. Here." He pressed her palm flat against his chest, over the place where even gods kept their stubborn mortal-seeming hearts. "I will carry all of it. Every hour. Four hundred hours and every one I find afterward in the empty places you leave behind. I will carry them the way the sea carries rivers—not losing them, *becoming* them. And someday, when the covenant holds and the worlds stop grinding and there is time—there is always time, Freya, I am made of time—someday I will sit across from you in whatever shop you build and tell you stories about a woman with clever hands and purple-stained fingers, and you will think, *what a strange, lovely stranger,* and you will not know. But I will know for both of us. That is my half of the new covenant. That is what I offer."

"And what do I offer?" she whispered.

"You already offered it. You offered it the day you chose justice over the shortcut, the day you refused the god of lies, the day you walked into Warden headquarters with nothing but nerve and a bad disguise—"

"It was an excellent disguise."

"It was a hat, Freya." A wet, broken laugh escaped him, and she laughed too, and for a moment they were only two people laughing in the dark above a broken altar, which is, he thought, how all the truest covenants begin.

Above them, the twelfth moon reached its height. Light fell through the crack in the aqueduct, silver and final, and the three sigil-stones began to sing—a low harmonic, patient, vast, the sound the world makes when it asks a question it already knows the answer to.

*It is time,* said the stones, without words.

Bram framed her face in his hands. He wanted to say a thousand things and had two thousand years of practice knowing that at the end, only a few words fit.

"I loved you before I met you," he said. "I prayed for you. Do you understand? Every century, I prayed to whatever hears gods, *send me someone with hands like hers.* And something did. Whatever else breaks tonight, know that you were answered prayer."

"Kiss me, then," said Freya Kavanagh, thief of memories, last heir of the covenant. "So I can steal one more."

He kissed her. He put the plum morning in it, and the pigeons, and the lullaby, and the chapel, and the look she wished she could keep—and she kissed him back and stole it, greedy and thorough, one last theft, the best thing she ever took.

Then she stepped to the altar, placed her palms against the stones, and began to pour.

* * *

*Freya*

She had thought it would feel like drowning. It felt like *giving*—like opening her fist after years of clenching, like the moment a charm leaves your hands and becomes someone else's treasure. The hours rose up out of her like sparks off a struck flint: the plums going, going, the graveyard dissolving to gray; the pigeons wheeling away into light; her mother's lullaby unwinding note by note into the sigil's song, and oh, the song was *better* for having it, she could hear her mother inside the harmony now, woven permanent into the bones of the world—

Bram's hand never left hers. Through every emptied hour he held on, and his voice ran underneath the singing like a bass note:

"I'll carry enough for both of us. The plum morning. The pigeons. Lesson eleven, which you'll argue about and lose. Your name. My name in your mouth. The look. *Especially* the look—"

The last hour rose in her like a tide.

She had saved it deliberately, without admitting she was saving it: not the kiss at the drowned chapel, not the confession on the rooftops, but this—the plain miracle of his hand in hers in the present moment, warm, alive, *now*. She poured it out last, slowly, savoring the strange arithmetic of loss: that a thing could pass entirely out of her and yet, in the instant of its leaving, weigh more than everything she had ever stolen combined.

The stones blazed white. The thirteenth moon began its turn.

And Freya Kavanagh, hollow where a god used to live, heard him say—one last time, in a voice she was already forgetting how to place—*"Look at me. I'll remember. Look at me, and I'll remember enough for both of us—"*

She looked.

The light took everything.

* * *

Somewhere beyond the white, the thirteenth moon settled into its place in the sky, and far below, in a city waking to its last dawn—or its first—Warden-Commander Ilse Varga drew her sword, turned to face her own council, and spoke a word that had not been spoken aloud in thirteen years.

The word was a name. It was not hers.

It was the founder's—and it was the key to everything the council had buried.
