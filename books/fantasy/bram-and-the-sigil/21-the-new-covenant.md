## 21. The New Covenant

Freya woke to sunlight.

Not the thin, grudging light that leaked through her shutters over the memory market — she had no shutters now, and no market either — but a great slab of morning pouring through the tall window of the Warden infirmary, lying across her blankets like something warm and alive. She lay still for a long moment, cataloguing herself the way she'd once catalogued stolen goods. Hands: present, unburned. Heart: beating, slow and steady. Mind: whole.

Whole was the strange part.

She reached for the night before — the altar of small mercies, Ilse's bloodied hand gripping hers, Bram at the threshold holding back a tide of twisted kindnesses with nothing but his own body and his stubborn, ancient patience. It was all there. The reforging itself was there too: the sigil-stones singing as they fused, the thirteenth moon sliding into place like the last tooth in a lock, the words rising out of her not in Kavanagh blood-script but in plain Corvani, the language of markets and mothers and ordinary mornings.

*Gods may answer only what is freely offered. Mortals may keep what is freely given.*

And then—

And then there was a shape in her memory where something had been cut away. Not pain. Pain would have been easier, because pain you could hold. This was a clean absence, the way a wall is absent where a door has been bricked up. You could press your palm flat against it and feel nothing but plaster.

She knew his name. That was the cruelty of it. *Bram.* The name sat in her mouth like a stone from the riverbed, smooth and meaningless. She knew he had held her hand while she wept. She knew she had loved him — knew it the way you know a fact in a ledger, entry dated, sum total entered. But the hours themselves, the stolen day pretending to be ordinary people, the drowned chapel where grief became intimacy, the first time he'd stood at her stall and asked not to buy but to give back —

Gone. Given. Poured into the sigil like wine into a thirsty earth.

"Ah," Freya said aloud, to the empty infirmary. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "So that's what I bought."

The door opened. Ilse Varga stood in it, out of uniform for perhaps the first time in her adult life, wearing a grey coat that made her look less like a blade and more like a tired woman who hadn't slept. Which, Freya supposed, was accurate on both counts.

"You're awake," Ilse said. "Good. The city's been asking for you."

"The city can wait."

"The city disagrees." Something shifted in Ilse's face — not softness, exactly; Freya doubted anything in Ilse ever fully softened. More like frost thinning enough to show the water underneath. "There are about four hundred people camped outside this building. They've been there since dawn. A baker brought bread. A woman whose son the hearth spoke to has been telling everyone you're a saint."

"I'm a thief."

"Yes." Ilse came further into the room, arms crossed. "Well. The law's changing. We'll need to decide what thieves are, going forward." She paused, and the frost thinned a little more. "How much do you remember?"

"All of it. Except—" Freya touched her temple, the way you touch a tooth that isn't there anymore. "Except him."

Ilse nodded slowly. She didn't offer comfort; Freya suspected Ilse had never learned the grammar of it. What she did instead was worse and better: she told the truth.

"He stayed by your side for two days while you slept. He said—" Ilse's jaw worked, as if the sentence were physically difficult. "He said he'd carry enough for both of you, and that when you were ready, if you ever wanted to know what you'd paid, he'd be easy to find. Gods usually are. That's rather the point of them, he said."

Freya laughed. It came out cracked and startled, half a sob wearing a laugh's clothing.

"That sounds like someone I would have liked."

"You would have," Ilse said quietly. "I only met him twice, and I wanted to arrest him both times. That's how I know."

* * *

Bram walked the city at dawn, as he had done for thirteen years, and for the first time since the covenant broke, nobody flinched from him.

That wasn't quite true. A fishmonger dropped an entire crate of mackerel when Bram smiled at her. A child pointed and shouted "MAMA, THE WALLS ARE DREAMING AGAIN." But no one ran. No one prayed at him in terror, which was its own peculiar torment — prayers aimed at Bram had always slid off him like rain off oiled canvas, but the *fear* had soaked through every year of his exile.

Now an old woman grabbed his sleeve outside the tanneries and said, "You're him, aren't you? The one from the stories my grandmother told. You used to leave coppers in shoes."

"I did," Bram admitted. "It seemed polite. Gods get such a bad reputation for grandeur."

"Well, don't stop on my account," she said, and shuffled off, and Bram stood in the middle of Tanner's Row feeling something he had almost forgotten the name for.

Welcome, he decided. The word was welcome.

He found Freya's absence everywhere, which was the nature of absences. In the ruined aqueduct, where the guardian spirit of the first sigil-stone had dissolved into motes of gold and drifted upward like dandelion seeds, finally released from thirteen years of duty. In the flooded chapel, where the drowned parishioners had sung their last verse as the covenant sealed and gone quiet — not silenced, Bram understood, but *answered*, which for the dead was the same thing. In the ash of the memory market, where new stalls were already going up, because Corvane's poor were weeds and weeds did not mourn long.

He had promised himself he wouldn't hover. Gods who hovered became the kind of gods people built altars to out of fear, and Bram had spent a millennium cultivating a reputation as the god who minded his own business. But promises, he reflected, were made to be kept by better men than gods.

So instead of hovering, he went to work.

There was so much work. The holes between realms were knitting closed, but slowly, reluctantly, like wounds that had been picked at for years. Through them, things still leaked: echoes of answered prayers that had never been properly finished, small mercies curdled mid-delivery, the ghost of a lullaby that had been promised to a dying child and never arrived. Someone had to shepherd those loose ends home. It was fiddly, undignified labor, exactly the kind of divine task no one wrote hymns about, and Bram found he loved it desperately.

By the third hour he had reunited forty-one unfinished mercies with their intended recipients, including a rain-shower for a farmer's field that had been sitting in transit for six years, and a single perfect plum for a man who had asked for it as a joke and then, upon receiving it, sat down on a curb and wept.

"Is it always like this?" asked a voice behind him.

The guardian spirit of the first stone — or what remained of it, a shimmer shaped vaguely like a cat — sat washing one translucent paw.

"Like what?" Bram said.

"This. All these little threads. I guarded a rock for thirteen years and never once wondered what the rock was *for*."

"No one does," Bram said. "That's the secret of small magic. Everyone looks up at the big miracles and misses the plumbing."

The spirit considered this. "You're very sad," it observed, in the blunt way of cats and spirits, "for someone doing work you clearly adore."

Bram fed it a scrap of golden light, which it ate with every appearance of pleasure. "Yes," he agreed. "Both things are true. Most true things come in pairs. Ask any mortal."

* * *

Ilse Varga had not slept in four days, and had discovered that righteous purpose was a more effective stimulant than any apothecary's tincture.

The council chamber of the Wardens smelled of ink, panic, and the particular staleness of men who had been arguing since before sunrise. Twelve of them sat at the long table. Ilse stood at the head, which was not where she belonged hierarchically, and none of them had yet found the courage to mention it.

"The edict stands," said Warden-Captain Ossory, for the fourth time. "Magic is banned. Magic has always been banned. The founder—"

"The founder took payment from the god of small mercies to make the ban possible," Ilse said, "in exchange for betraying the covenant that kept this city standing for nine hundred years. I have read his confession. I have read my mother's case files, which he buried. I have it in his own handwriting, gentlemen, and I will happily read it aloud until one of you faints, though I warn you I have a strong voice and all morning."

Ossory's face went through several interesting colors.

"The ban was built on a lie," Ilse continued, laying the founder's confession on the table with a click that echoed like a gavel. "And a lie cannot be enforced forever. It certainly cannot be enforced now, when the old gods walk openly in our streets answering honest prayers, and the alternative to regulated small magic is what we saw on the twelfth moon. Does anyone here wish to argue that the walking dead were preferable to licensing charm-sellers?"

Silence. Somewhere, a clerk dropped a quill.

"Then hear the new law." Ilse unrolled a fresh document, written in her own severe hand, amended three times at Freya Kavanagh's suggestion — the third amendment being, in Freya's words, "so the rich can't buy their way out of ethics, because they will absolutely try." Ilse had kept it nearly verbatim.

"Small magic is legal. Memory-craft, hearth-whispering, hedge-healing, blessing-work — licensed, taught, inspected, and taxed like any other trade. No charm may be woven from a memory taken without consent. No prayer may be answered that was not freely offered. And the Wardens—" she looked down the length of the table, at twelve faces that ranged from mutinous to hollow-eyed with revelation, "—the Wardens exist to protect the covenant, not to police it. If any man or woman among you cannot serve under that mandate, there is the door, and there is honest work in the city, and I will write you a reference that omits everything embarrassing."

Warden-Captain Ossory rose, purple with fury. "This is heresy."

"It is," Ilse agreed. "I checked the definition carefully. Heresy means belief against the doctrine of an order. Our order's doctrine was purchased from a liar-god by a traitor. So yes. Guilty as charged." She rolled up the new law. "All in favor?"

Nine hands rose. Two men kept their hands down and left by the door she'd offered, and Ilse noted their names without malice — she would need honest clerks somewhere, and even men who could not bend might prove useful if they could not break either.

Afterward, alone in the chamber, she permitted herself ninety seconds of grief.

Her mother had been a charm-worker. Ilse had known it the way children know things they must never say — dimly, wholly, unspeakably. Her mother's arrest had been the first file Ilse had ever pulled from the archive, at sixteen, telling herself she sought understanding. What she had found instead was a system designed to bury understanding, and she had spent twenty years mistaking her grief for zealotry because zealotry at least gave you orders to follow.

She blew out the lamp. Ninety seconds ended. There was a city outside that needed a new kind of Warden, and Ilse Varga intended to build it, and if her hands shook slightly as she gathered her papers, well. Hands were allowed to shake. That was rather the point of the new law, when you came down to it.

* * *

They gave Freya a ceremony, which she endured the way one endures a dental extraction: with dignity and considerable internal complaint.

The square before the old cathedral — the drowned chapel had been raised, drained, and consecrated anew, its parishioners' names carved in white stone — was packed shoulder to shoulder. Ilse read the new covenant-law aloud from the steps. The crowd cheered at intervals, apparently at random, since half of them couldn't hear. A choir sang something that was probably ancient and was definitely off-key. Four separate religious processions had shown up uninvited, each convinced the covenant was primarily about their god, and were currently negotiating territory with the diplomacy of wedding parties sharing a venue.

"They're saying your name," Bram observed. He stood beside her at the edge of the square, in the shadow of a buttress, because a god at a civic ceremony drew attention the way a bonfire draws moths.

"I noticed," Freya said. "It's dreadful. I spent twelve years making sure nobody knew my name."

"Twelve years ago you'd have sold a stranger's first kiss for bread money. People change."

"People change," she agreed. "Cities too." She watched a girl of maybe ten weave a tiny charm of light between her fingers — legally, openly, her mother watching with tears running down her face — and felt something twist gently in her chest. Not grief, exactly. Grief's cousin, who visits sometimes and brings bread.

The mayor pinned a medal on her. It was heavy, ugly, and shaped like the sigil, which struck Freya as a bit like giving a locksmith a medal shaped like a keyhole. The crowd roared. Somewhere in it, the baker whose hearth had spoken with his dead son wept openly into his apron. The beggar who had woken healed lifted Freya's hand like a prizefighter's.

When it was over, when the square emptied and the processions went home to argue theology over supper, Freya stood on the cathedral steps in the last of the light.

"He's here, isn't he," she said. Not a question. Some part of her, the part below the brickwork, had known all day.

"He's been here since dawn," Ilse said from beside her. She had attended the ceremony in full uniform after all. "He helped set up the chairs. Nobody recognized him until he fixed the choir's broken harp string, and then the entire soprano section had a religious experience."

"I don't remember him." Freya's voice was flat and careful, a floor tested for weight. "I know everything around him. I know the shape of the hole. Ilse — tell me something true about him. Anything. I want to hear it from someone who isn't trying to give him back to me."

Ilse was silent a long moment.

"He waited," she said finally. "Two days by your bed. And when I told him you'd given up every memory of him willingly, that it was the price and you'd known and paid anyway — do you know what he said?" She shook her head at the memory. "He said, 'Of course she knew. She's a Kavanagh. We count the cost first and love anyway. That's the whole family curse.' Then he thanked me. For guarding you." Her jaw tightened. "Nobody has thanked me for anything in twenty years. It was deeply irritating. I nearly arrested him."

Freya laughed, wet-eyed. "That sounds like someone I would have—"

"Liked," Ilse said. "Yes. You mentioned."

Below them, in the emptied square, a figure stood looking up. Tall, unhurried, with the patient air of a man who had waited thirteen years and could comfortably wait thirteen more, though the slight tension in his shoulders suggested he hoped he wouldn't have to. Evening light caught him, and for just a moment Freya saw what the city had begun to see everywhere now: not grandeur, not terror. Just a man-shaped warmth, the god of copper coins in shoes and plums delivered late, of small mercies honestly finished.

She knew his name. She knew the ledger-entry: *loved him, chose justice, paid in full.* She knew the shape of the hole where he had been.

She descended the steps toward him, medal swinging, heart hammering at a stranger — and stopped three paces away, close enough to see that his eyes were kind, and that they were looking at her the way people look at things they have grieved and gotten back.

"Hello," he said. "You don't know me. That's all right. I know enough for both of us, and I've always been generous."

"Everyone says you're a god of small mercies."

"I prefer 'god of unfinished business.' Mercies are just business I take personally." He smiled, and something below the brickwork in her chest pressed its palm flat against the plaster, feeling for a door.

"My name is Freya Kavanagh," she said. "I'm told I used to know you."

"You did," Bram said. "Would you like to start again? I'm told the second time is slower, and mortals do everything important slowly. It's their best quality."

Above them, the first star of evening appeared over the rebuilt chapel, and the thirteenth moon — past its turn now, merely a moon again, ordinary and silver and safe — rose thin behind the rooftops of a city learning, at last, to say its own name.

Freya put out her hand.

"Start again," she said.

* * *

Three months later, a customer left a small vial on the counter of the charm-shop on Tanner's Row — labeled, in careful handwriting, *for the thief, if she ever wants it back* — and Freya turned it in the lamplight, reading the label twice, and did not yet open it.

But she kept it by the register. Just in case.
