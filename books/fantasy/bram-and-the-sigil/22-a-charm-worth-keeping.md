## 22. A Charm Worth Keeping

The bell above the door of Kavanagh & Apprentices rang in the particular off-key way it always did when the wind came off the aqueduct, and Freya looked up from her ledger with the resigned expression of a woman who had learned, over eleven months of honest trade, that the bell meant either a customer or a catastrophe, and lately it was difficult to tell the difference.

"CLOSED," she called, out of habit.

"We know," said Tamsin from the workbench, not looking up. She was seventeen, freckled as a thrush's egg, and currently attempting to weave a grief-easing charm without crying, which was part of the curriculum. "You say closed every time someone comes in. It's like a prayer."

"It is *not* a prayer," said Oskar solemnly, from the corner where he was grinding moonpetal. "Prayers have to be freely offered. That's the whole point. I wrote an essay on it."

"You wrote half an essay on it and then fell asleep on the second page."

"The second page was *very* long."

Freya hid her smile behind the ledger. This was, she had come to understand, what peace sounded like: bickering children, the smell of beeswax and dried vervain, the afternoon light coming through the shop window at the angle that meant she'd survived another market day without stealing anything from anyone, including herself.

Eleven months since the thirteenth turn. Eleven months since the covenant sealed itself around her sacrifice like scar tissue around a wound, and the city woke up knowing her name — not the name she'd sold charms under, not the name whispered in the memory market before Ilse Varga burned it to ash and salt, but *Kavanagh*, spoken in the open air, printed on the new Warden edicts that Ilse rewrote by hand because, as she put it, "the council's calligraphers have the moral backbone of wet paper."

The door opened anyway, because the sign said OPEN and customers were constitutionally incapable of reading.

It was Mistress Aldery, the baker, who came every fortnight for the same charm: a small amber bead woven with three seconds of her son's laugh, kept from before the hearth spoke with his voice, from before the holes between realms let him visit on the first night of each moon. She didn't need the charm anymore, strictly speaking. But she liked holding something in her hand while she listened.

"Same as last time?" Freya asked.

"Same as always." Aldery counted out honest coins — real coins now, not bartered secrets — and then lingered, turning her hat in her hands. "There was a fellow asking after you. Down by the fountain. Young man. Said he'd heard the heir of the covenant ran a charm-shop and taught ethics to children."

"And?"

"And I told him you did." Aldery shrugged. "Was I wrong?"

"No," Freya said slowly. "That's right. That's all true." She watched the baker go, the little bell ringing its broken note behind her, and felt the old cold finger of attention trace down her spine. People asking after her was nothing new. The whole city knew her face; there was even a mural on Tanner's Row that made her look taller and considerably more serene than life. But *asking after* was different from *knowing of*.

"Tamsin," she said. "When we close, take the long way home."

Tamsin rolled her eyes with the fluency only teenagers possess. "Ilse already taught us the long way home. And the short way. And how to break someone's grip if they grab your wrist."

"Ilse," said Freya, "has an excessive enthusiasm for pedagogy."

"She says the same about you."

They were both right, which was the trouble with living among people who liked you.

* * *

The lesson that evening was on consent, which was the first lesson and would be the last lesson, if Freya had her way, for as long as the shop stood.

"Say I want to make a charm of courage," she told the four students arranged on the floor cushions — Tamsin, Oskar, little Wren who never spoke but wove better than anyone, and a nervous widower named Colm who had enrolled late in life and took notes like the exams might matter in heaven. "Where does the memory come from?"

"The client," said Oskar promptly. "Their own courage. Their own worst day they got through anyway. You shape it, you don't take it."

"And if they offer me something extra? Something beautiful? A first kiss, say. A mother's voice singing." Freya kept her tone light, which cost her something every time. "Just a scrap. Just the edge of it. They won't miss it."

"Then it's theft," said Tamsin.

"Even a scrap?"

"*Especially* a scrap." Tamsin lifted her chin. "You told us. Scraps are how it starts. You take a scrap and you keep a scrap and one day you've got a jar full of other people's best moments and no idea who you are without them."

The room went quiet in the way rooms do when someone repeats your own wound back to you with a teenager's merciless accuracy. Freya looked at the shelf behind her workbench where, these days, exactly two things sat under glass: the three sigil-stones, dull as river pebbles now their work was done, resting in a cradle of velvet like retired soldiers. Everything else she had ever distilled or hoarded had gone into the covenant. Every stolen hour. Every borrowed joy. Every fragment of every stranger's brightest day.

And one thing that had never been stolen at all.

"That's right," she said. "Especially a scrap. Class dismissed. Tamsin, lock up. Oskar, stop eating the vervain, it's for tinctures."

"It was *one leaf*—"

"*One* leaf becomes a habit. Ask anyone who used to buy forgetting-charms in the old market." She said it gently, and Oskar went pink and put the leaf down, and outside the window the first stars came up over Corvane, thin and clean, in a sky that had held together for eleven months without tearing.

She stayed late, as she often did, doing the ledger by lamplight. Honest arithmetic was a kind of meditation: so much wax, so much vervain, so much owed to the widow Pellar whose roof charm had leaked, minus the discount, plus her forgiveness, which was free and therefore priceless. Freya had discovered she loved bookkeeping with an intensity she found faintly embarrassing. Numbers didn't pretend to be anything but what they were.

She was balancing the third column when she noticed the vial.

It sat on the counter beside the till, small as a sparrow's egg, glass gone cloudy with age — or with use, or with waiting. It had not been there when Aldery left. It had not been there when the class arrived. It had simply *arrived*, the way certain objects in Corvane now sometimes did, in the soft places where the veil still wore thin: a lost ring returned to a mantelpiece, a dead woman's recipe appearing in her daughter's cookbook in handwriting no one alive could imitate.

The gods were careful these days. They answered only what was freely offered, and mostly they answered small: a candle relit, a fever broken, a letter delivered forty years late. Bram had told her once that this was what divinity looked like when you stripped away the hunger — not thunder, but errands. A god of small mercies' opposite number, running mercy like a courier service.

Freya picked up the vial.

Inside, light moved. Not divine light, not the tearing gold-white of the old answered prayers — but memory-light, warm and ordinary, the color of lamplight through wine. She knew it instantly. Eleven months of emptiness where a year of him used to be had not dulled her craft. You could strip a thief of everything she'd taken, but not the hands that knew how to hold it.

A label was tied to the neck with green thread, in handwriting she did not recognize — round and careful, the script of someone who had learned letters late or lovingly:

*For the thief, if she ever wants it back.*

Her heart did something complicated, a stumble and a sprint in the same beat.

*A man with kind eyes,* the memory inside seemed to whisper, though vials didn't speak, though memories didn't whisper, though she of all people knew the difference between a charm and a voice. *Laughing at his own joke before he finishes telling it. Holding a stranger's hand on a bridge. Looking at a woman across a night market stall as if she were the answer to a question he'd been asking for thirteen hundred years.*

Freya set the vial down very carefully, as though it might bruise.

She knew who had sent it. Of course she did. There was only one person alive who would carry a memory of Bram willingly, tenderly, for months, and then give it away — because giving it away was the only gift he had left that wouldn't break the covenant. He couldn't come to her. The reforging had taken him back to the threshold he'd held bleeding, back to whatever duties gods keep when their war is over. He couldn't cross into Corvane more than glimpses, more than answered prayers, more than the smell of rain that wasn't falling.

But he could remember. He had promised he would carry enough memory for them both.

And now he was trying to give her half the weight.

"Oh, you *idiot*," she said softly, to no one, to him, to the lamplight. "You wonderful, patient, thirteen-hundred-year-old idiot."

Because here was the cruelty folded inside the kindness, and she turned it over and examined it the way she'd once examined every charm for cracks: if she drank it, she would remember him. One memory. His laughter, his eyes, the bridge, the stall. Enough to know what she'd lost. Enough to grieve it properly instead of grieving a hole shaped like a person. And enough — this was the part that made her breath catch — enough to fall in love again, in a single swallow, with someone she was not permitted to keep.

Or she could leave it on the shelf. Let it gather dust beside the sigil-stones. Stay whole, and hollow, and safe.

Freya Kavanagh had spent twelve years taking scraps from other people's lives because her own had been too empty to bear. She had spent one terrible perfect year learning that love costs exactly what it weighs. And she had spent eleven months discovering that honesty is not a single grand sacrifice at an altar beneath the aqueduct. Honesty is a ledger. It's a column you balance every night, and some nights the numbers don't come out fair, and you write them down anyway.

She thought about what she would tell her students.

*Tamsin, especially a scrap.* But this wasn't a scrap taken. This was a whole memory, freely given, by the only person in either realm with the right to give it. Consent, freely offered, wrapped in green thread. By the law she'd helped write — by the covenant sealed in her own blood and emptied memory — this was the most lawful magic in the city.

By the law of her own heart, it was a door she'd been standing in front of for eleven months, afraid to knock.

Freya blew out the lamp.

Then she lit it again, because she refused to make this decision in the dark like some girl in a bad ballad, and because Bram had always teased her about her horror of melodrama, and somewhere in the hollow where those teasing evenings used to live, she wanted to honor that.

She untied the green thread. She pulled the stopper.

Warmth went through her like dawn going through a window — not the tearing gold of answered prayers, just lamplight, just wine-color, just an evening market stall and a man leaning on it as if he had all the time between the world's first morning and its last, saying something absurd about the price of moons, laughing before he finished the joke, and looking at her — looking at *her*, tired sharp-edged Freya with her thief's hands — as if she were the answer to a question older than the walls of Corvane.

Kind eyes. She remembered the kind eyes. She remembered everything, one everything, whole and entire, hers now not because she stole it but because he pressed it into her palms like bread.

Freya laughed. Then she cried, briefly, efficiently, the way she did everything — and then she sat down at her ledger, opened to a fresh page, and wrote at the top, in her neat bookkeeper's hand:

*Bram. Carried forward.*

Underneath, after a moment, she added: *Balance owed: one memory, to be repaid in person, with interest, whenever he can get through the door.*

Outside, the bell above the shop rang its broken note, though no wind came off the aqueduct, and no footsteps crossed the threshold — only the smell of rain that wasn't falling, and a warmth in the doorway shaped exactly like someone leaning there, waiting to see whether she'd open the till, or the door, or her arms.

Freya Kavanagh, heir of the covenant, terror of the old market, teacher of ethics, balanced her books, stood up, and went to unlock the door.
