## 1. The Weight of Being Forgotten

The blizzard came down off the Thornfell like a debt collector, and August Iversen met it the way she met all her debts: early, quietly, and with a plan.

"Keep the rope taut," she called back over her shoulder, though the wind ate most of it. "If it goes slack, you've wandered off the road, and the road doesn't take kindly to wandering."

Behind her, eleven mules and nine merchants groaned in chorus. Somewhere in the middle of the line, a man named Ostler Hetch—third son of a grain factor, soft hands, softer opinions—shouted, "How can there *be* a road? I can't see my own boots!"

"You can't," August agreed. "That's rather the point of me."

She said it without vanity, which was a trick, because it was entirely vanity. Anyone could find a road in fair weather. A child could do it. A mule could do it, and frequently did, better than the merchants who paid for the privilege of following one. But a road in a whiteout, on the Kettleback Traverse, where the mountain had spent four hundred years trying to shrug the path off its shoulder like an unwanted coat—that required a different kind of knowing. The kind that lived in the soles of your feet and the back of your throat and didn't have words for itself.

August had been born without words for a great many things. It was one of her finer qualities.

She counted the rope-tugs behind her—one, two, three, all the way to nine—and turned into the wind. The snow wasn't falling so much as arriving, sideways, with intent. Above them the pass narrowed to a slot of grey nothing, and somewhere in that nothing was the cairn she'd built herself, seven years ago, out of spite, after a guild master told her the Kettleback couldn't be crossed after the Feast of Lanterns.

It could. It just couldn't be crossed by anyone who needed to *see* where they were going.

"Left!" she barked, and hauled the lead rope two hands' width starboard, because the mules, bless them, had learned that when she said left she usually meant the opposite of whatever they'd been considering. "There's a drop on your right with opinions about merchants. Stay left."

Hetch again: "How do you *know* where the drop is?"

"Because I fell into it when I was twelve," she said. "Thoroughly. It took the guild two days to find me, and when they did, Master Ellery said, and I quote, 'Ah. There you are. Try not to be found so slowly next time.'"

Someone laughed. It carried strangely in the storm, a small warm thing in all that cold, and August felt it settle somewhere under her ribs where she kept the things she'd never admit to wanting. Laughter aimed *at* her was common enough. Laughter *with* her—well. That was a weather even rarer than clear skies on the Kettleback.

She put her head down and walked.

The trick to being forgotten, she'd learned early, was that it worked best when you agreed to it. The world forgot you if you gave it nothing to hold: no loud colors, no loud voice, no debts unpaid or favors owed. A face like hers—brown, unremarkable, the kind of face that appeared in a witness's memory as "someone, probably"—was a door that closed behind her wherever she went. She'd spent nineteen years oiling that hinge.

And the truth, the one she kept in the locked room at the back of her chest where she kept all her best truths, was that she was *good* at it. Not just passable. Not just useful. She was the best wayfinder the Wayfarers' Guild had produced in a generation, and the reason no one knew it was that being known was the one thing she couldn't afford. A famous guide got followed. A followed guide got watched. A watched guide got *asked questions*, and August Iversen had built her whole self out of not being asked questions.

The cairn appeared out of the murk like a rumor confirmed—a squat fist of stones with a crooked marker pole, its top wrapped in the red rag she'd tied there seven years ago, faded now to the color of an apology. She touched it as she passed. Two fingers, quick, the way you'd touch a friend's shoulder.

"Still there," she told it. "Good."

The pass opened beyond it, and the wind, finding nothing left to push against, sulked and dropped. Within a hundred paces the world reassembled itself: the long white shoulder of the mountain, the black teeth of pines below, and far off, past the frozen shallows of the Mirren, the smoke of Waymeet rising in a thin grey thread. Home. Or the place she kept her bed, which was the closest thing to it she'd ever bothered to own.

Hetch came up beside her, blowing like one of his own mules. His eyebrows had frozen into two white caterpillars of astonishment. "You did it. By the Lantern, you actually did it. The other guides said it couldn't—"

"They were right," August said. "For them."

She let him buy her a cup of mulled wine at the waystation at the foot of the pass, mostly because refusing would have required conversation, and conversation was a door she kept locked from the inside. He talked enough for both of them—about the grain tariffs, his brother's disappointing marriage, the price of mules in the low country—and she made the small sounds that kept him going, and by the time the wine was gone he knew three things about her: her name, her price, and nothing else whatsoever.

"Come south with us in the spring," he said, gripping her hand with both of his soft ones. "A guide like you, in the capital— you'd make a *name*—"

"That's the idea," she said, "to not," and he laughed like she'd made a joke, and she let him.

* * *

The guild hall at Waymeet had been built to hold two hundred voices, and it had once done so. August remembered—or rather, she had been told, by people who remembered—feast nights when the rafters rang, when maps were unrolled down the length of the great table like banquet courses, when every chair was full and every chair had a name and a reputation attached to it.

Now her boots echoed.

She stood in the doorway a moment longer than she meant to, letting the echo find her. Snowmelt dripped from her cloak onto the flagstones, each drop a small announcement: *here, here, here.* The great map table stood bare except for a scatter of uncollected weights and one cup, ringed with old wine, that no one had claimed. The map drawers along the east wall were locked, and half the locks were rusted, and no one had complained, because no one had come to open them.

"Master Ellery?" she called.

The silence answered first. Then, from the back rooms, a cough—the deep, wet, structural kind, the kind that sounded less like an illness than a demolition in progress.

She found him in the small room behind the scriptorium, propped in the chair he refused to replace even though its left arm had given up years ago and he'd wedged a stack of atlases under it. Master Ellery had been old when she was a child; now he was old in the way that mountains are old, all his sharpness weathered down to something patient and inevitable. His eyes found her the moment she ducked through the door.

"Kettleback," he said. Not a question.

"Closed," she said. "For everyone else."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. It might once have been a smile. "Sit down, girl. You're dripping on my floor."

"It's the guild's floor."

"It's *my* floor. I've been dripping on it for fifty years; I've earned squatter's rights." He waved her into the other chair—the good one, the one with both arms—and she sat, and for a while neither of them said anything, which was the most comfortable conversation either of them knew how to have.

The fire in the little stove ticked and settled. Outside, the storm was still worrying at the shutters, but in here it was only a sound, the way grief was only a sound if you'd had practice.

"Nine merchants through the pass in a whiteout," Ellery said at last. "That's the third impossible crossing this season. You know what the southern guilds are saying?"

"That I'm lucky."

"They're saying you're a ghost." He said it mildly, the way he said most cutting things. "That the Wayfarers have stopped training guides and started haunting passes. They're saying it as a *complaint*, August. We've eleven members left. Eleven. I can count them on the fingers of one hand if I borrow yours."

"I can count."

"I know you can. You count everything. It's the only time your face moves." He coughed, and it went on too long, and she watched the tendons of his neck stand out like bridge cables until it released him. When he could speak again, his voice had dropped to the gravel at the bottom of itself. "I'm dying, girl. You know that. The physicker in Waymeet knows it. The stove knows it. The only one pretending otherwise is you, and you're pretending so well I almost admire it."

"I'm not pretending anything."

"You're not *here* anything, is what you are." He fixed her with the look he'd used on her since she was eight years old and had just walked the guild's practice maze blindfolded, in the dark, in winter, because she'd been told not to. "You come in, you hand over your fees, you take your assignments, you vanish up some mountain for a month, and you come back thinner and quieter and you think I don't notice that the quiet gets worse every time. You wear that loneliness like armor, August, and I'll tell you what I told every apprentice I ever trained about armor."

"That it's heavy?"

"That it's *for fighting*. You're not fighting anything. You're just wearing it to dinner."

August looked at the stove. The stove, having no opinions, declined to help.

"There's no one left to fight," she said. "The roads are dying. The guild's dying. You're—" She stopped. Restarted, from a safer angle. "The world doesn't need wayfinders the way it did. Everyone's maps are good enough now. Everyone's roads are safe enough. I'm a luxury, and luxuries are the first thing people stop buying."

"The world doesn't need you *forgotten*, either."

"It's cheaper that way."

Ellery was quiet for a long moment. When she finally made herself look at him, his eyes were wet, and he didn't trouble to hide it, because Ellery had never in his life hidden a thing that cost him nothing to show.

"Your mother left you on our step in the dead of winter," he said. "You know the story."

"I know the story."

"No, you know the *facts*. Left on the step, wrapped in a wayfarer's cloak, with a scrap of map sewn into the hem. But you don't know the story, because the story has a part at the end where the child grows up and lets people love her, and you've been skipping that part for nineteen years." He leaned forward; the atlases under the chair arm groaned in sympathy. "I'm not asking you to weep at my deathbed. I'm asking you to be *findable*. For once in your life. When I go—and it will be soon, the stove and I have an understanding—there will be things to settle. Papers. The guild's remaining accounts. And I'd rather settle them with the one apprentice I have left than with a solicitor who'll sell the map drawers for scrap."

"You have other apprentices."

"Had. They went where the work went. South, to the low roads, to the caravans that don't need guides so much as company." He smiled, and this time it was unmistakable, tired and fond and a little bit wicked. "You stayed because you're the best I ever trained and you can't bear to be second-best at anything, including leaving."

"That's not—" August began, and found she had no second sentence. It was annoying, how often that happened around him.

The storm pressed at the shutters. The stove ticked. Somewhere in the empty hall, the wind found a loose rafter and made it sing, one long low note, like a hall remembering it used to be full.

"Stay the night," Ellery said. "There's stew. It's terrible. You'll have some."

She had some. It was terrible. They ate it in silence, and the silence was, for once, the good kind—the kind that didn't need armor, because nothing was attacking.

* * *

She woke before dawn, as she always did, to the particular silence of snow-heavy air, and lay in her narrow bed in the loft listening to the guild hall breathe. It breathed strangely now. Creaks she'd known all her life had gone quiet; the building was settling into itself like an old man into a chair, folding its unused rooms away.

She was halfway down the ladder, thinking about the day's small chores—oil for the locks, a walk to Waymeet for Ellery's physicker, the endless arithmetic of a guild running out of guild—when she saw the light.

Not lamplight. Lamplight was yellow and steady. This was red, and it moved, low along the eastern road, weaving the way a drunk man walks or a dying horse.

She stood in the frozen yard with her breath pluming and her heart doing something she didn't authorize, and she watched the light come on. It came slowly. It stumbled. Twice it dropped into the ditch beside the road and twice it rose again, and August Iversen, who had spent her whole life perfecting the art of not being where trouble was, heard her own boots crunching toward it before she'd decided to move.

The figure at the road's edge was burned. Not sun-burned, not hearth-burned—burned the way paper is burned, in patches, in patterns, as though the fire had been reading him. His courier's coat was charred to lace. In his fist he held a scroll case, and the light she'd seen was the case itself, glowing through the man's scorched fingers like a coal that refused to die.

He saw her. His eyes were the only whole things left on his face, and they were wide, and they were *afraid*, and they fixed on her with the terrible accuracy of a man spending his last coin.

"You," he rasped. "The one they can't— you're the one they can't *find*."

August stopped. Every instinct she owned was screaming at her to be elsewhere, to be no one, to be fog. She stood exactly where she was.

"I don't know what you—"

He was already stumbling toward her, and there was no time, and the scroll case came up in both his ruined hands, and the last thing August Iversen saw clearly for a very long while was the courier's mouth shaping three words as the case came apart in a bloom of red light and the map inside it—inked in something that was not ink—pressed itself, searing, into her open palm:

"The Spire remembers."
