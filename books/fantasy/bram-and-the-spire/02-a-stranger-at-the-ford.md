## 2. A Stranger at the Ford

The river called the Sill had a voice like an old woman arguing with a cat, and August Iversen knew every note of it.

She'd been guiding the Merrow caravan for eleven days—wool merchant, four wagons, two mules with opinions, and a driver named Petch who narrated his own sneezes. They were three miles north of the ford at Hollow Bridge, and the light was going the color of weak cider, which meant August had perhaps an hour before the Sill's evening fog came up out of the water like the river exhaling after a long day.

"Another hour to the crossing," she called back along the line of wagons. "Then we camp. Nobody argues with the ford after dark."

"Nobody argues with *you* after dark," Petch said, sneezing, and narrating it. "That was a big one. Did you hear that? Like a door."

August didn't answer. Answering was how you became a person people remembered, and being remembered was a thing she had spent twenty-two years learning to avoid the way other people avoided debt collectors.

It was not a small skill. Most people thought being invisible was something that happened to you, like rain. August knew better. It was a trade, practiced daily, like kneading bread or mending nets. You learned to stand at the edge of a fire's light rather than in it. You learned to speak just loudly enough to be heard and no louder. You learned that the fastest way to be forgotten was to be useful in a forgettable way—*the guide knew the trail*, people would say later, *I don't remember much else about her*—and to never, ever be useful in a memorable one. She had once watched a man become a legend for wrestling a river-pike with his bare hands, and she had watched that same legend get a knife in his ribs in a tavern in Duncarrow two years later, because legends have enemies and forgettable people have none.

She was proud of it. That was the strange part, the part she didn't examine too closely in the dark hours. Most people would find it a sad thing, to be proud of being nothing. August found it a comfortable thing, like an old coat with the elbows worn soft.

The caravan came down through the alder scrub to the ford as the last light failed. The Sill ran shallow and quick here over pale stones, and on the far bank stood the ruin of Hollow Bridge itself—a single broken arch, mossy as an old tooth, all that remained of the king's road that had once run north into the Thornfells. Nobody used the king's road anymore. Nobody used anything that led north, if they could help it.

August waded in first, as always, testing the stones with her boots. Cold, but not the killing cold of early spring. She waved the wagons across one at a time, standing hip-deep in the middle with a lantern held high, calling the depth to Petch like a litany. The mules complained. Petch narrated. The wool merchant, a round and anxious man named Odo Merrow, counted his wagons four times, arriving at four each time, and looked surprised about it.

And then, while the last wagon was still splashing and the lantern light was swinging shadows across the water, August saw the man come out of the alders on the north bank.

She saw him the way you see a wrong note in a familiar song. The ford was empty. The ford was *always* empty at dusk—shepherds crossed at dawn, smugglers crossed at midnight, and sensible people didn't cross at all. But here was a man walking out of the trees with a gait that was less walking than falling, corrected, falling again, and even before the lantern reached him August knew two things: he was burned, and he was dying.

"*Down!* He's coming down—give him room—" She was moving before she'd decided to, lantern thrust at Petch, wading back through the shallows with water soaking her to the ribs. The man made it to the water's edge and then his legs simply resigned their post, and he went down onto the stones with a sound like a dropped sack of turnips.

Up close, the burns were worse. His cloak was charred to lace across his back and shoulders. The skin of his hands was tight and shining, blistered in the pattern of something that had burned fast and hot and close. But it was his face that made August's stomach drop—not the burns, which had missed it, but the expression on the unburned parts. He wasn't grimacing. He was *relieved*. He looked at August with the naked, staggering relief of a man who has been running for days and has just heard, at last, a door close behind him.

"You're—" His voice was a ruin. He tried again. "Wayfinder."

"Not anymore," August said, which was a lie so automatic it barely counted as one. "Guild's mostly empty. Who did this to you?"

He laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing brought up something dark. "Who," he managed. "The Crown did this. The Crown does everything now." His hand came up—burned, shaking, terrible—and caught the front of her coat with a grip that had nothing left in it but will. "Listen. Listen to me. There's no time. They track it. They track the *heat* of it, and I have led them a merry chase down half the Thornfells, but I'm done leading anything."

"I don't know what you're—"

"You will." He fumbled at his cloak, at the ruined breast of it, and drew out a thing wrapped in oilcloth and bound with wire. Even before he unwound the wire his fingers were shaking too badly, and August, hating herself a little for the curiosity that had always lived in her like a mouse in a wall, took the bundle and unwound it herself.

Inside was a map.

She had seen a hundred maps. She had drawn a dozen. This was not like them. It was drawn on skin—she understood that with a lurch, on *skin*, tanned and cured and fine as vellum—and the ink was not ink. It was dark red at the edges and gold at the center, and it moved. Not obviously. Not the way water moves. But when she tilted it toward the lantern, the mountain ranges seemed to breathe, the rivers to crawl, and the whole of it gave off a heat she could feel against her cheek, like holding a loaf of bread fresh from the oven, like standing near a sleeping animal.

"That's the Thornfells," she said. Her voice came out strange. "That's the whole range. Nobody's mapped the whole range. Nobody *can*—"

"It maps itself," the courier said. "It's been mapping itself since before there were kingdoms. It's dragon's blood, girl. The last of it. And it has decided—" he stopped, and something crossed his burned face that was almost apologetic, "—well. It has decided you."

"That's not how maps work."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

The caravan had gone quiet behind her. She was distantly aware of Petch holding the lantern, of Odo Merrow saying something anxious about bandits, of the mules screaming their opinions. It all seemed to be happening across a river, on another evening, to another woman. August knelt in the shallows with a dying man's hand around her collar and a burning map in her fingers, and she felt her invisibility—the coat she had worn so long, the soft-elbowed, comfortable nothing she had made of herself—tear straight down the middle.

"Give it to someone else," she said. "Give it to Odo. Give it to the king, give it to a priest, give it to—"

"The king *sent* the thing that burned me." He said it flatly, without drama, which was somehow worse. "And the priests are gone, girl, or worse than gone. You're a Wayfinder. The last one, near enough. The map knows its own." His grip tightened. "I carried it from the Spire. Do you understand? From the Spire itself. And I am the last man who will ever carry it, because I am the last man who *can*, and I am done. So it chose you, and I am not fool enough to argue with it, and you should not be either."

"I'm not arguing with it. I'm arguing with *you*. I didn't ask for—"

"Nobody asks," he said. "That's rather the whole of it."

And then he did the thing she would think about, afterward, for the rest of her life, in whatever life she had left. He took the map out of her hands—gently, the way you take a knife from a child—and pressed it against her left palm, and held it there.

It burned.

Not like fire. Like *recognition*. The heat went through the skin of her palm and into the meat of her hand and up the bones of her arm, and August heard herself make a sound she had never made before, high and involuntary, and she tried to pull away and found that her hand would not close and would not open, that it simply *held*, as if her own fingers had been waiting all her life for something to hold.

The map sank.

There was no other word for it. The edges went first, then the ranges, then the rivers, the gold and the dark red drinking into her skin like rain into summer dust, and where it went it left lines—fine, branching, beautiful lines, a map written in the palm of her hand, faint as an old scar, warm as a held coin.

"The Spire remembers," the courier whispered.

He was looking at her with that terrible relief, and his eyes had gone somewhere past her, past the lantern and the river and the ruined bridge, to something at the top of the world that only he could see.

"The Spire remembers," he said again, softer. "Tell them—tell whoever comes after—*the Spire remembers*."

Then the light went out of him the way a lamp goes out: completely, and all at once, and with a small sound like a sigh.

* * *

They buried him on the south bank, above the flood line, because August insisted and because even Petch, who narrated sneezes and weather and the quality of his own boots, understood that some things are not for narrating. Odo Merrow said words over the grave that were mostly about wool prices and trailed off into silence. The mules watched with an air of having seen all this before.

August stood apart, as she always stood apart, and looked at her hand.

In the lantern light the lines of the map lay across her palm like a fortune told by a cruel fortune-teller. She traced one with a fingertip—the Sill, she was almost certain, the river she had just crossed, running silver and fine from the wrist toward the heart of her hand. North of it, the lines climbed and tangled into the shape of mountains she knew the way she knew her own teeth. And at the top, where the Thornfells went up into the high white country that no map in any guild hall had ever gotten right, a single mark: a small, burning point of gold, like a star fallen into her skin.

The Spire. She knew it without being told, the way you know a word you've never read. Everyone in the north knew the shape of the story: the king's mountain fortress, the king who had not been seen in nine years, the king whose edicts still came down from the heights in a hand that had changed—flatter, colder, written by something that had learned to imitate a man the way a magpie learns to imitate a bell.

Nobody went to the Spire. That was not a rule. It was simply a thing that was true, like the fact that rivers run down.

"Miss Iversen." Odo Merrow had come to stand beside her, round and sweating and kind. "That man. He said—the Crown, he said. And now you've got his—" he gestured helplessly at her hand, at the faint warm lines, "—his *map*, on your—well. On you." He wiped his face. "You'll come back to Duncarrow with us. Warm bed, hot meal, and we'll—someone will know what to do. The guild. Someone at the guild will know."

August looked south, the way the caravan would go in the morning. Duncarrow was four days. The guild hall was in Duncarrow. Her master was in the guild hall, coughing his life out into a kerchief by a stove that never quite drew properly.

She looked north. The fog was coming up off the Sill now, soft and white, and beyond it the Thornfells stood black against the last of the light, and somewhere in the high white silence at the top of them, a mark on her hand said *here*, in a voice like an oven door opening.

Her palm ached. Not the burn—the burn was already fading, which should have been impossible, which was its own kind of terrifying. It ached the way a tooth aches before it breaks. It ached like something *waiting*.

"Miss Iversen?"

"Tomorrow," August said. "I'll—tomorrow. Let me think tonight."

Odo nodded and went back to the wagons, relieved to have someone else's problem to stop thinking about. Petch banked the fire. The mules sighed. The river went on arguing with its stones, and the fog came up the bank and took the caravan one wagon at a time, softening the lantern light into a smudge, and August sat down alone on a stone above the grave of a man whose name she had never learned, and held her burning hand against her knee, and tried to think.

The trouble was that every thought came back to the same place, and the place was this: for twenty-two years she had made herself nothing, and nothing had kept her alive, and she had been *proud* of it. And now, in one evening at a ford, a dying stranger had looked at her across the water and seen—what? Not nothing. Something. Something the map had wanted, something the Crown would burn men to retrieve, something that had pressed itself into her skin like a signet into wax.

*Chosen.* The word sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

She had spent her whole life learning how not to be chosen. How to be the fourth person in a room of four, the second choice for every job, the face that witnesses couldn't describe. And it had worked. Gods, it had worked so well. And now the whole vast machinery of the north—king and Crown and dragon-blood and burning couriers—had ground to a halt at a river crossing and pointed at *her*, and every trick she knew was suddenly, uselessly, beside the point.

You cannot be forgettable, she thought, when you are the only map in the world.

The fire crackled. The fog thickened. And on the north bank, far up in the alder dark where the ruined king's road began its climb, August saw—or thought she saw, and would spend the rest of the night deciding which—a light.

Not a lantern light. Not campfire orange. Something colder and steadier, high and small, like a star that had come down to walk the road. It stood there for the space of three heartbeats. Four. Watching, she was quite sure, the way a hawk watches: not with curiosity, but with patience.

Then it went out, and the dark came back, and August Iversen sat very still in the fog with her hand burning against her knee, and understood with a cold, clear, terrible certainty that the running had already started, and that it had started without her.

She looked south, toward Duncarrow, toward the guild hall and her dying master and the warm, forgettable life she had built out of being no one.

She looked north, toward the mountains, toward the small gold star written in her skin.

Then she got up, quietly, so as not to wake the caravan, and went to tell Petch they would be breaking camp before dawn—and which way they would be going.
