## 3. What the Ink Wants

The first thing August tried was fire.

She built the fire herself, properly, the way Master Odell had taught her when she was nine and still tall enough to be useful for carrying kindling: dry birchbark beneath, split pine over that, the whole arrangement angled so the draft would pull. She was good at fires. She was good at everything small and practical, which was what happened to people nobody noticed—they got very good at doing things alone.

She fed the map to the flames at dawn, when the guild hall's common room was empty except for a sleeping carter and the smell of yesterday's porridge.

The map did not burn.

It lay in the coals like a leaf in a stream, untouched, unbothered, its dark lines gleaming wetly as though it were enjoying itself. The edges of the parchment should have caught. Anything catches, eventually. August had once watched Brenna's people burn a barge down to the waterline with a cask of lamp oil and spite. But the map sat in the fire with the serene patience of a cat being bathed, and after a full minute she reached in—hissing, snatching her fingers back twice—and pulled it out by one corner.

Cool. It was cool. The paper was cooler than the room around it, as if the fire had been warming itself on the map rather than the other way round.

"Right," August said to the hearth, because talking to hearths was better than talking to herself. "We'll try something else, then."

* * *

The second thing she tried was giving it away.

This was harder than it sounded, because the obvious recipients kept declining. The carter took one look at the dark lines crawling across the parchment and developed an urgent appointment elsewhere. Old Marta in the kitchen accepted it graciously, set it down beside her kneading trough, turned back to her dough, and then straightened up with a frown, saying, "Now how did that get there, and why am I holding it?"—because she *was* holding it again, without any memory of picking it up.

August watched Marta stare at her own hands with dawning, superstitious alarm, and felt something cold walk down her spine.

"I'll take that back," August said quickly. "Sorry. Guild business."

"Guild business," Marta muttered, surrendering it, "oughtn't to crawl."

"It doesn't crawl," August said. "Probably."

* * *

By midmorning she had also tried burying it (she dug it up twenty minutes later from a spot she had not buried it in), dropping it down the well (it was in her pocket when she came back up the ladder, damp, and then, disturbingly, not damp), and throwing it off the bridge into the Thornwash, where it vanished beneath the grey-green water with what she could have sworn was relief.

She made it all the way back to the guild hall steps before the itching started in her right palm.

She looked down. The brand—the mark the courier had pressed there with his last living strength, the shape of roads she didn't know leading to a tower she'd only heard of in drunk songs—had faded to almost nothing overnight. But now the lines were rising again, faint as old scars in cold weather, and beneath them she could feel the river water drying, could feel the parchment's texture ghosting against skin that held nothing at all.

The map wasn't returning to her hand. August understood, standing on the guild hall steps with her heart going thin and fast, that the map had never left her hand. The paper was just where it kept its notes.

"Oh, that's foul," she said quietly. "That is genuinely foul."

"You're talking to your hand again," said Tam, the youngest apprentice, passing with an armful of kindling. He was fourteen and had the gift of noticing nothing, which August normally envied. "Master says you're wanted."

"How is he?"

Tam's face did the thing faces did lately—flattening, careful. "Awake," he said. "Mostly."

* * *

Master Odell's room smelled of camphor and candle smoke and the particular staleness of a man who had stopped believing in fresh air. He was propped in bed against three pillows, and he was thinner than the man who had taught August to read contour lines in snow, but his eyes were still sharp enough to make apprentices feel briefly transparent.

He looked at her hand before she'd even closed the door.

"Show me."

"I was going to explain—"

"Show me, girl. I'm dying, not patient."

August sat on the edge of the bed and peeled back the wrap she'd bound around her palm. In the candlelight the brand showed plainly now, dark lines fine as spiderwork, branching across her palm in a pattern that hurt to look at directly—not with the eyes but somewhere behind them.

Odell went very still.

She had seen him frightened exactly twice in seven years: once when the avalanche took the Kestrel Pass road with two Wayfinders on it, and once the night Brenna's men came to collect a debt he'd sworn he didn't owe. This was the third time, and it was worse than either, because this time he didn't move at all. He just stared at her hand as if it had become a stranger sitting on his blanket.

"Where," he said, in a voice sanded down to gravel, "did you get dragon-blood ink on your skin?"

"That's what it is? The courier—it's what he used to draw the map? But the map's paper, I've got the—"

"The paper is nothing." His hand shot out and gripped her wrist, and for a dying man his grip was astonishing. "Paper burns, girl. Paper rots. Paper is just the *draft*. What he marked you with—" He stopped. She watched him gather himself the way she'd watched him gather himself to deliver hard lessons: the loss of the pass road, the death of friends, the arithmetic of risk every guide carried. "You know the old stories. Before the Covenant, before kings learned to cage fire in crowns. How do you think the dragons' blood was spent? On wars?"

"I don't—"

"On *maps*." He released her wrist and fell back against the pillows, breathing hard. "A dragon's blood remembers everything it has ever flown over. Every ridge, every valley, every road that ever crossed its shadow. Ink made of it doesn't show you a way, August. It makes you *become* a way. A living road, walking where it's told." His eyes closed. Opened again. "And it collects tolls along the route."

"Tolls," August repeated. Her mouth had gone dry. "What kind of tolls?"

"What did you lose already? Think. Something small. A name, a date, the words to a song you always sang wrong anyway."

She thought. And thought. And felt the horrible sliding sensation of reaching for something ordinary—a memory of the village where she'd been born, the one she never visited, the one whose name sat right there at the front of her mind next to her mother's face—

The name was gone. Not hidden. *Gone*, the way a tooth is gone from the jaw: a smooth place where something had been.

"There was a village," she whispered. "I grew up—I was born in a village, before Brenna, before the guild, and I can't—" She looked up at him, and to her fury her eyes were burning. "I can't remember what it was called. Master, I can't remember my own—"

"Hush." He said it gently, and it was worse than shouting. "Hush now. Listen to me, because I haven't the wind for saying it twice. That courier chose you or the map chose you or both, and it doesn't matter which, because the Hollow Men will come for the ink whether you want their king saved or not. You cannot fight them. You cannot hide from them—they don't track scent or sound, they track the heat of the ink itself, and you are carrying a lit lantern under your skin."

"So I give it back. I'll find whoever sent him—"

"You can't give back what's written on you." He almost smiled; it was a terrible, tired smile. "You've tried, haven't you? Fire? River? I can see it in your face. Seven years guiding caravans and you still believe every problem is a stubborn mule that wants firmer handling." He coughed, long and wet, and when it passed he reached for the cup at his bedside and she helped him drink, and the ordinariness of it nearly undid her.

When he'd settled, he said, "South."

"South?"

"Run south. Out of the mountains, past the Saltmarshes, down to the coast. There are ships. Ships go places maps can't follow, and the sea swallows heat like it swallows everything else. Take the southern passes—you know them better than anyone alive, better than the map does, maybe, because you learned them with your feet instead of some dead lizard's blood. Go tonight. Now, if you can manage it."

"And do what? Live in a port town jumping at shadows while the Hollow Men burn their way through everyone who ever knew me?" She heard her own voice climb and forced it flat. "They burned the courier, Master. They'll burn whoever they think helped him."

"They will burn whoever they find near the ink," Odell agreed mildly. "Yes."

"So the answer isn't running. The answer is—" She gestured helplessly at her own chest. "Whatever they're trying to prevent. Going north. To the Spire. To the king."

The silence that followed lasted long enough that she counted four of his slow, labored breaths.

"No," he said at last. "Absolutely no. Do you know what's at the Spire? Do you know what's been wearing Aldric's crown these thirty years? Nobody knows. That's the point of nobody knowing. The guilds signed treaties, girl. The Wayfarers gave our word generations ago: we don't ask what sits on the throne, and in exchange the Crown lets us keep our roads. Your whole guild, your whole *guildcraft*, exists inside that bargain, and you want to walk into the middle of it carrying the one thing the Crown has killed to protect?"

"Yes," August said. "I think so."

"*Why?*"

Because a dying stranger had chosen her out of a river of strangers. Because the map had eaten the name of her village and she could feel it getting comfortable, settling in, planning to eat more. Because she had spent her whole life being forgettable on purpose and something in her had finally, furiously refused.

But what she said was: "You told me once that a Wayfinder's job is to go first. Down the bad path, across the rotten bridge, so everyone behind learns whether it holds."

"That was about fords, August."

"It was about fords," she agreed, "but I don't think it was only about fords."

He looked at her for a long moment, and she saw the argument die in him—not won, just abandoned, the way a general abandons a position he can no longer hold. He was quiet so long she thought he'd fallen asleep.

"When I found you," he said finally, "you were eleven years old, half-starved on the Kestrel road, and you had walked forty miles through open country without being seen by bandits, wolves, or tax collectors. I asked myself then: what manner of child survives like that? And I told myself: a lucky one." His eyes were wet. "I should have asked harder questions. We all should have. The guild doesn't take in strays, girl. It never has. Someone brought you to us. Someone *placed* you."

The cold walked down her spine again, and this time it stayed.

"If I run south," she said slowly, "they follow me south. Through the towns. Past everyone who ever shared a fire with me." She stood, and wrapped her palm again, and found her voice had gone very steady, the way it did on bad crossings. "If I go north, they follow me north, through empty country, and the only person they find is me."

"That is not the comfort you seem to think it is."

"No," August admitted. "But it's mine." She leaned down and kissed his forehead, papery and warm. "Rest, Master. I'll wake Tam. We should get the ledgers out of the east room before the roof leak gets worse."

"August."

She paused at the door.

"Whatever you meet on that road," Odell said, "remember that the map eats memories. So before it takes them—say the good ones aloud. To someone. Anyone. Even to yourself. A thing spoken is a thing copied. It lives in the air afterward, outside your skin." He managed the ghost of his old teaching smile. "Consider it homework."

* * *

She spent the evening doing her homework badly.

She sat in the kitchen with Marta and Tam and the carter, who'd decided the map business was above his pay and was therefore aggressively interested in his supper, and she said things out loud. The name of her village—gone already, so she described its crooked church spire instead, and the smell of the tannery, and the way the river froze in shapes like fists. Her first caravan, age twelve, when she'd vomited over a merchant's boots in a switchback fog and he'd tipped her anyway, saying anyone that honest about her fear would do. The song Marta sang when the bread came out right. Tam's terrible laugh, which sounded like a door that needed oiling.

Marta listened with her floury arms folded and said, at the end, "You talk like someone packing a bag."

"I talk like someone with a lot of bags," August said.

Nobody believed her. That was the strange mercy of being forgettable: even when she told the truth, people filed it away as one of August's moods. She let them. She banked the kitchen fire low, checked the shutter latches out of seven years of habit, and climbed to her cot in the loft intending to lie awake until midnight and slip away before dawn.

She was asleep within minutes. She was seventeen years old and exhausted and grief makes heavy blankets of us all.

* * *

She woke to orange light.

For one merciful second she thought it was morning, that Marta was baking, that the world was ordinary. Then she heard the screaming, and felt the heat pressing through the floorboards, and understood that the light was coming from *below*.

The common room windows were full of flame. Not spreading flame—*arriving* flame, standing in the glass like visitors, and behind the fire, in the yard beyond, figures moved. Tall. Wrong. They moved the way smoke moves when there's no wind, purposeful and drifting at once, and where their faces should have been there was nothing but smooth pale blankness, like eggs, like frost on stone.

Hollow Men. Here. Already.

August was moving before her mind finished waking—down the loft ladder, through the smoke that tasted of pitch and burnt wool, toward the kitchen, because the kitchen had a cellar and the cellar had a coal chute and the coal chute opened behind the woodshed, and she knew that because she knew every inch of this building the way she'd once known a village whose name she could no longer say.

The hall was roaring now. Beams cracked overhead like ice going out. In the doorway between the common room and the stair she saw one of them—tall, blank-faced, unhurried—and it turned its head toward her with no eyes to turn, and she felt the brand on her palm flare hot as a poker, and she knew it couldn't see her at all. It was looking at the lantern under her skin. It was looking at the ink.

Then Tam's scream cut through the roar—from the east wing, the apprentice rooms—and August's feet made her decision for her.

Later, she would remember it in pieces. Dragging Tam out through the coal chute with his nightshirt smoldering. Marta appearing out of the smoke with two kitchen boys and the entire stock of pickled herring, because Marta's priorities were Marta's priorities. Master Odell's window, high on the second floor, framed in fire.

She stood in the snow behind the woodshed with Tam sobbing against her shoulder and looked up at that window, and part of her started calculating—rope, angle, the drainpipe's load rating—and the larger part of her knew, with a clarity that felt like the map's cold patience, that the stairs were gone, that the east stair had gone first, that no trick she owned could carry her up through that.

Odell appeared at the window anyway. Silhouetted against his own burning room. He didn't call out—he wouldn't waste breath—but she saw him raise one hand, the old Wayfinder's signal, the one that meant *path holds, keep walking.*

Then the roof beam came down, and the window was only fire, and the guild hall that had taken her in when she was a stray with no name and no future folded inward like a hand closing.

They stood in the snow until the cold drove them back. Tam shook. Marta prayed to gods she'd always claimed were superstitions. The carter wept openly and without shame.

August did none of those things. She stood apart, looking north, where the peaks stood black against the burning sky, and she opened her right hand inside her glove.

The brand was glowing. Faintly, steadily, like an ember that intended to be a fire. And beneath the glow, in the deep quiet of her, she could feel the map considering her grief the way a merchant considers coin.

*I know,* she told it silently. *I know you're counting. Take your tolls. But you'll pay interest, in the end. Everything does.*

Behind her, Marta touched her arm and said, "There's a farmstead over the ridge. We can shelter there till morning."

August nodded. But she kept looking north, and the snow kept falling, and far up in the black peaks something ancient waited at the top of a road only she could walk—because she was, now, the only road there was.
