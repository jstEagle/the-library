## 4. The Hollow Men

August had a theory about being followed, and the theory was this: you never find out by looking back. Looking back was how they caught you—because the moment you turned, your body told the whole world *I am worth turning around for*. So she didn't look back. She listened with her spine instead, the way her master had taught her, and for two days her spine said nothing at all.

On the third day it began to whisper.

She was three hours past the burned ruin of the guild hall, deep in the goat tracks south of Thornfell where the pines grew crooked and the trails had no names because nobody important had ever bothered to walk them. Her spine whispered first as a warmth between her shoulder blades, then as an itch, then as the unmistakable feeling of being *considered*.

She stopped at a stream to drink, cupping water in her good hand—the left one, because the right still wore the map like a second skin—and watched the surface. Not for her reflection. For movement upstream.

Nothing. Just water, and the grey sky doing its usual grey business above the trees.

Then she saw it: in the shallows downstream, a fish floating belly-up. Then another. Then, drifting past her boot, a third, and August felt her stomach drop through the mountain itself, because fish did not die of nothing, and things that killed fish without touching them were not things you outran on foot.

*The map's heat,* she thought. *They're not tracking me. They're tracking it.*

She looked down at her palm. The blood-ink lines glowed faintly under her skin, a soft amber shimmer, like embers seen through a closed fist. It had been quiet since the fire. Quiet the way a banked stove is quiet.

"Fine," she told it, through her teeth. "But if you get me eaten, I'm giving you a very poor review."

The map, having no sense of humor, pulsed once, gently, like a heartbeat. Or like something laughing in a language she didn't speak yet.

She ran.

* * *

By evening she'd put a ridge and half a valley between herself and the dead fish, and she allowed herself the luxury of a hollow under a granite overhang, a small fire built low and screened with wet stone, and half a heel of bread that had gone hard enough to qualify as a weapon. She ate it slowly, listening.

That was when the singing started.

It came up the valley from the north, thin and wrong—a sound like wind through a keyhole, except there were no keyholes out here, and the wind had stopped an hour ago. It rose and fell without rhythm. It had no words. And underneath it, so faint she might have imagined it, was something worse: it sounded almost like a lullaby she half-remembered, hummed by someone who had forgotten what lullabies were *for*.

August put out the fire with her boot, wrapped her cloak tight, and pressed herself into the deepest shadow of the overhang.

They came up the trail below her an hour after full dark.

There were five of them. They walked in single file, unhurried, and the moonlight did a terrible thing when it touched them: it went straight through. Not through them like ghosts—she could see boots, cloaks, the shape of shoulders—but their faces gave the light nothing back. No shine on cheekbones. No shadow in eye sockets. Faces like unlit windows in a house everyone had moved out of long ago.

And they were all facing slightly upward. All five. Their chins tilted toward her hand, toward the brand sleeping under her glove, the way sunflowers tilt toward the sun—or the way starving men tilt toward the smell of bread.

August held her breath until colors bloomed behind her eyes.

One of them stopped directly beneath the overhang. Close enough that she could hear its breathing, which was the strangest part, because it *did* breathe—in, out, patient, human-shaped—and yet nothing else about it moved. Not the chest. Not the blink. Just breath, going in and out of something that had apparently forgotten why.

Then it spoke, and its voice was gravel poured into a bag made of someone's old words.

"*Reads,*" it said. "*Still reads.*"

Another one answered, further down the line, and its voice was different—a woman's voice once, maybe, worn down to the grain. "*Warm. Getting warmer.*"

The first one stood there a moment longer. And then—it tilted its head, slow as a door creaking, and August would have sworn on every smuggler's grave she knew that for one instant, the blank face *looked sad*. The way a dog looks when it's forgotten whose whistle it's obeying but remembers that whistles used to mean dinner.

"*Long ago I read,*" it said, to no one, to the trees, to whatever was left inside. "*I read all night. It was so beautiful. There was a road on it that went to my mother's house.*"

Then it turned, and the five of them walked on northward, singing their wordless lullaby, leaving August alone in the dark with her heart hammering and tears standing in her eyes that she had absolutely no intention of acknowledging.

Because she understood now. That was the horror of it—not the faces, not the light passing through them. The horror was that they hadn't been made. They'd been *readers*. People who had looked too long at a map inked in dragon's blood, people who had wanted it the way she already caught herself wanting it some nights—wanted the roads, the knowing, the beautiful terrible certainty of it—until the wanting wore them down to hinges and handles, until the Crown reached through the pages and took the rest.

*I read all night. There was a road on it that went to my mother's house.*

August sat in the cold with her knees pulled up and thought about the courier at the ford, burning alive from the inside, spending his last strength to press the thing into *her* hands. He must have known. He must have known exactly what he was handing her and what it did to people, and he'd done it anyway, because the alternative was letting it go back to them.

"You could have just thrown it in the river," she whispered. "River was right there."

But maps like this didn't drown. She'd tried burning it; it had come home. Maybe rivers were the same.

She pulled her glove tighter over the brand and made herself a promise in the smallest, hardest voice she owned: *You will not read all night. You will not follow the pretty road to your mother's house. You will use this thing and be done with it, and then you will bury it so deep even the mountains forget.*

Somewhere in the dark, the map warmed against her palm, very slightly, like something pleased to be spoken to.

"That wasn't a compliment," she muttered.

* * *

For six days she traveled the way water travels—downhill, patiently, by routes no honest person would take. She crossed the Greywater on a fallen alder with the bark slicked black. She slept in a charcoal-burner's hut that smelled of forty years of other people's fires. She stole eggs from a ptarmigan nest and apologized to the ptarmigan, who did not accept.

And everywhere she went, she learned the rules of the Hollow Men, cataloguing them the way she catalogued everything, because rules were armor and armor was how a forgettable girl stayed alive:

Rule one: they tracked heat, not scent. Dogs lost her at streams. Hollow Men lost her whenever the map cooled—and the map cooled when she stopped *thinking about where she was going*. This was the maddening discovery of day four: she'd spent an hour lost in genuine, aimless confusion, and the itch between her shoulder blades had faded to nothing. The moment she got her bearings—*ah, the pass is southeast, the river bends there*—the warmth flared like a struck match.

So she learned to travel stupid. To refuse to know where she was going. To let her feet choose forks at random while the thinking part of her mind recited smugglers' songs and inventory lists and the exact texture of Brenna's betrayal, over and over, a fire blanket thrown over the map's glow.

It was like walking blindfolded through a country she loved. Every mile cost her twice: once in blisters, once in deliberately not-knowing.

Rule two: they sang when they were close, and the song got into your head and rearranged the furniture. Twice she caught herself humming along before she'd heard anything. Both times the humming had been leading her somewhere—up-slope, toward open ground, toward places where a lone figure on a skyline is visible for miles. She broke the humming by biting her cheek until she tasted copper, and after the second time she traveled with a strip of leather between her teeth during the high exposed stretches, which made her look, she reflected, entirely mad, and which she decided was a fair price for remaining un-murdered.

Rule three—and this one she learned on the sixth night, huddled in a shepherd's bothy with rain hammering the turf roof—they remembered fragments, and the fragments were worse than the blanks.

She heard them before she saw the firelight, two of them sheltering in the next fold of the hill, close enough that the words carried through the rain in those ruined, sanded-down voices:

"*...had a daughter. Did I? Someone had a daughter. The road went to her house.*"

"*Everyone's house is on the road. Everyone's house was always on the road.*"

"*I was a surveyor. Wasn't I. I drew the bridges.*"

"*You were warm. You are warm. We are all so warm.*"

And then the lullaby again, and August lay with her back against cold stone and her hand curled protectively around the brand, and she thought: *they're not hunting me. They're homesick. The map ate their lives and left them hungry for the shape of them, and the only place they'll ever find that shape again is in the ink.*

Which meant that to the Hollow Men, she wasn't prey.

She was a library.

* * *

On the ninth day, the map did something new, and August finally stopped pretending she was in charge of this journey.

She had been traveling blindfold-in-the-head for two days straight, wandering on purpose, and the brand had gone cool and sulky under her glove. Then, at a nameless fork above a ravine—one path descending toward the lowlands and safety and the south her master had begged her to run for—the map woke up.

Not warm. *Hot.* A wire of heat drawn across her palm, precise as a compass needle, pointing not south, not toward any road at all, but west and up, into the high country where the passes went white and the air went thin and nothing lived but goats, eagles, and people with excellent reasons to avoid other people.

"No," August said aloud. "Absolutely not. South is the plan. South is where my master told me to run, and my master is dead because of this thing, and I do not take directions from the murder weapon."

The heat sharpened. Somewhere far behind her—two ridges, maybe three—a thread of song lifted on the wind.

August stood at the fork for a long time, chewing the inside of her lip, doing sums she didn't like. South meant the lowlands, the guild towns, the king's roads, the bounty notices with her description—which would say *forgettable*, which would be useless, which was the one advantage she had left. But the Hollow Men didn't need descriptions. They needed her to know where she was going, and eventually, exhausted and grieving and human, she would know.

West and up meant cold, hunger, and whatever the map wanted. But it also meant the one place in all the histories where dragon-fire still breathed under stone.

Corvane of the Ash Choir. Her master had said the name like spitting out a seed: *defrocked, exiled, dangerous, and the only man alive who ever read the old ink and kept his face.* If anyone knew what was written on her hand—knew what it wanted, knew what it paid, knew how to make it stop paying—

The song drifted closer. Fainter than memory. Closer than comfort.

"All right," August said to the map, to the mountains, to the nine days of dead fish and sleepless nights. "West. But I want it noted for the record that I hate this plan, I hate this map, and when this is over I am going to write such a complaint."

The brand pulsed—warm, brief, unmistakably smug—and pointed her up the trail like a finger.

She climbed.

Behind her, down in the grey morning, the singing changed key. It didn't fade. It turned, the way a flock turns, all at once, following something warm.

Following, she understood with a clarity that felt almost like calm, *her*.

So she stopped being careful about being followed and started being careful about being *fast*—and she ran west into the high passes, toward hot springs and exile and the one man who might teach her how to survive her own hand.

The mountains rose ahead of her, white and silent, hiding their fire.

She just hoped the priest hiding among them had gotten kinder since his defrocking. Because the way her week was going, he'd be the last friendly face she'd see for a hundred miles—if he turned out to have a face at all.
