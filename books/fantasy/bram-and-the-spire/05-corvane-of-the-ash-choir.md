## 5. Corvane of the Ash Choir

The springs had been quiet for eleven days, which Corvane had learned to regard with suspicion. Quiet meant the mountain was thinking. Thinking mountains, in his long and mostly regrettable experience, were mountains about to do something.

He was elbow-deep in the third spring, harvesting the sulfur-crust that passed for salt in his exile, when the water changed its mind about being water. It shivered. It went warm in a way that had nothing to do with the vein beneath it—warm the way a hand goes warm when someone remembers you fondly from across a room.

"Ah," Corvane said to the steam. "So it's to be that kind of season."

He was sixty-one years old, mostly bald, and had spent the last nine years in a valley the maps called *uninhabited*—a word he had come to find flattering rather than insulting. He lived in the ruin of a sanctuary that had once housed forty priests of the Ash Choir and now housed one defrocked heretic, three goats of dubious loyalty, and a library of burned books he had spent a decade reassembling from memory and stubbornness.

The mountain was speaking. And when a mountain speaks, something is usually walking toward you with bad intentions or worse wounds.

He dried his arms on a robe that had once been white and was now the color of honest work, and climbed the terraces to the old watchstone. From there he could see the whole approach: the scree field, the frozen falls, the narrow thread of trail that any sensible person would avoid and any desperate person would take.

He saw her within the hour. A small figure, moving wrong—not the stagger of exhaustion, which he knew intimately, but the careful, rationed stagger of someone who had been conserving strength for days and was now spending the last of it. She moved like a smuggler. He'd known smugglers. Some of his best confessions had come from smugglers, back when confessions were still his business.

And she moved *toward* the springs. Not around them. Toward.

"Well," he said. "Either the mountain invited you, or you're the worst-lost traveler in Thornfell."

* * *

He let her come to the gate, because the gate was the gate for a reason. The sanctuary's outer wall was old Ash Choir work—stone laid in the pattern of a dragon's rib, and the pattern still held, the way old prayers hold, out of habit if nothing else. When she reached it, he was already standing in the archway with a bowl of barley soup, because in nine years he had learned that the fastest way to disarm a dangerous stranger was to make them hold something warm.

She stopped ten paces out. Young, he saw now. Early twenties. Gray-eyed, road-worn, with the particular hollow look of someone who had recently lost something and was refusing to say what. Her right hand was wrapped in strips of linen that had been white once and were now spotted with something that had soaked through and dried brown-black.

"You're not a bandit," she said. It wasn't a question. It was an accusation of disappointment, almost.

"No. I'm worse. I'm a priest."

"I don't want a priest."

"Excellent. I'm not one anymore. They took the collar back, but they couldn't take the soup." He held it out. "Barley. The goats object to my cooking, but the goats object to everything. It's their whole character."

She didn't move. Her eyes did the arithmetic travelers do—the distance to him, the distance past him, the weight of the soup versus the weight of whatever she was carrying under that cloak. He watched her decide she could take him in a fight, and watched her decide it anyway with the grim courtesy of someone who would prefer not to.

"I'm not staying," she said.

"No one ever is. That's rather the point of a sanctuary. It's a place you pass through on your way to somewhere you'd rather be." He wiggled the bowl. "It's getting cold. Cold barley soup is a sin in eleven traditions and I've been defrocked by most of them."

That got him something that was almost a smile, killed quickly, like a candle pinched between fingers. She came forward and took the bowl. And when her sleeve shifted—when her wrapped hand rose to take it—he saw it.

He did not gasp. He was proud of that. Nine years of solitude had at least taught him not to gasp. But every hair on his arms stood up in a way that had nothing to do with the mountain wind, and somewhere below them, all three springs went still at once, like dogs pointing.

"You'd better come inside," he said quietly.

"I said I'm not staying."

"You're not staying. You're sitting down indoors for the length of one bowl of soup, after which you'll leave, and I will wave, and we will both feel we behaved correctly." He turned and walked in, because the second-fastest way to disarm a dangerous stranger was to stop watching them. "The bench by the fire is the one without the goat on it. Probably."

* * *

She sat. The goat, whose name was Turnip and whose loyalty was to warmth alone, was indeed on the other bench, and regarded the newcomer with the flat contempt of the comfortably positioned.

Corvane built the fire up and did not look at her hand. This took effort. It took the effort of a man standing in a river trying not to notice the current.

"You knew the old roads," he said instead. "Coming up the north face. That trail's been dead for sixty years. The Ash Choir closed it when the vein under the falls went cold."

"Did they." Flat. Guarded.

"They did. I was there, more or less. I was nine, and mostly I remember being told to stop asking questions, which I have since made my life's great project of ignoring." He poked the fire. "You walked a dead road to a dead sanctuary in the middle of a dying season. People only do that if something is leading them."

"Nothing's leading me."

"Mm." He nodded at her wrapped hand, casually, as though remarking on the weather. "How's the burn?"

Her whole body changed. He'd seen it before—in the confessionals of his old life, the moment a penitent realized the priest already knew. The stillness. The recalculation. The brief, furious consideration of lying, and the tired surrender to the fact that lying to a priest was like lying to a stone wall that occasionally asked follow-up questions.

"It's fine," she said.

"It's not. It's dragon-ink, and it's not a burn, it's a *brand*, and it will be fine for exactly as long as you keep it wrapped and never, ever look at it directly after dark." He said it mildly, the way you'd tell someone their bootlace was undone. "Also, your soup's getting cold again. I did warn you about that."

She stared at him. Then she set down the bowl, unwound the linen with the brisk anger of someone tearing off a bandage to spite the wound, and held her hand to the firelight.

The map sat in her palm like a thing alive. Ink in lines fine as hair and dark as a vein of ore, branching and forking across the skin of her hand—roads, he realized. Roads and rivers and the small precise marks of passes and fords, drawn in a script that was not ink at all, that sat *in* the skin rather than on it, that moved, when the firelight shifted, the way water moves under ice.

Corvane looked at it for a long time. The fire cracked. Turnip chewed something with tremendous indifference.

"Nine years," Corvane said at last, "I have lived in this valley telling myself the Choir was finished. That the last of it died with my ordination and my disgrace and the fire at the monastery, and that whatever the old men raved about on their last nights was senility and sulfur fumes." He turned his own hands over, palms up, an old habit—showing them empty. "And then you walk up a road that hasn't existed in sixty years, wearing the map of the Spire on your hand, drawn in the blood of the last dragon, and you tell me nothing is leading you."

"How do you know what it is?" Her voice cracked on it. Not fear, exactly. The thing under fear, the thing fear grows from. "Everyone who's seen it—the ones who've seen it—they go strange. They want it. Or they want me dead because of it. You're just *sitting there*."

"I'm sitting here because I am sixty-one years old and I have already been burned by everything worth being burned by." He smiled, and knew it was a tired smile, and let it be. "Also, I want something from you, which makes me predictable, and predictable is safe. Ask me what I want."

"What do you want?"

"To see it," he said. "The dragon. Before it dies."

* * *

She didn't answer for a while. She ate her soup instead, which he respected enormously; fear that doesn't interfere with appetite is fear that's been properly managed, and he'd known kings who never learned that.

"The thing at the Spire," she said finally. "You know what it is."

"I know what the Choir said it was. Whether the Choir was right is another question. The Choir was wrong about a great many things." He fed a stick to the fire. "We taught that the dragons gave themselves to the crowns. Willing sacrifices, binding their fire to the kingdoms so the land would never hunger. It's a beautiful doctrine. It's on the first page of the first book they give you, and you weep when you read it, and you spend the rest of your life not looking too closely at the second page."

"Which says?"

"Which says *willing*." He said the word the way you'd spit out a bad tooth. "I spent twenty years as a priest of a church built on one word I never believed, and when I finally said so out loud, they took my collar and gave me this valley. So. You see my credentials. Defrocked for honesty, exiled for persistence, and kept alive since by three goats and a hot spring with opinions."

That earned the almost-smile again, and this time it stayed a moment longer. "What did you say? Exactly? To get defrocked?"

"I said that a gift with a chain on it is not a gift, it's a hostage." He shrugged. "The Prior said I was young. I said he was comfortable. Neither of us was lying."

She looked at her hand. The map-lines caught the firelight, and Corvane watched her watch them, and saw the moment her jaw set—the moment she decided, the way travelers decide, with the whole body and not the head.

"If I say no," she said, "you'll try to take it anyway. Everyone does."

"I won't." He held up his empty palms again. "Look at me, girl. I couldn't take bread from a determined grandmother. What I *will* do is this: I know the roads the map will show you, because my order made those roads. I know which passes the Hollow Men can't climb and which ones they can, and the difference will kill you if you guess wrong. I know the old rites, and you will need them, because that thing on your hand is not a map, it's a *toll road*, and you are paying for it with—" he paused, choosing, "—with pieces of yourself you haven't noticed missing yet. Have you?"

The silence was answer enough. He watched her reach inward, quickly, the way you pat your pockets after leaving a market. He watched her not find whatever she was looking for, and watched her decide not to say so.

"Your name," he said gently. "Do you still have it?"

"August," she said. Too fast. Then, slower, testing it: "August."

"August." He nodded. "Good. Hold on to that one. Names go first, in my experience, and everything else goes in the order you loved it."

* * *

They sat until the soup was gone and the fire had settled into its evening voice. Outside, the springs murmured their long sulfur psalm, and the mountain beneath them did its slow thinking, and somewhere far to the north—Corvane did not say this, and did not need to—something faceless was following the heat of a brand through the dark.

"I have conditions," August said at last.

"Of course."

"You walk behind me. You don't touch the map. You don't pray over me, or near me, or in my general direction. And when we get there—when you see it—" she stopped, and started again, quieter. "Whatever it is. Whatever it looks like, whatever it's done. You don't get to decide anything. You just get to *see*. That's the price. You see it, and then it's mine to deal with."

Corvane considered this. It was, he thought, the most priestly thing anyone had said to him in nine years, and she wasn't even a priest. She was a smuggler with a dragon's map in her hand and holes in her memory where a life used to be, and she had just negotiated terms of witness like an abbess.

"Agreed," he said. "I have one condition of my own."

She braced for it. He saw her brace, and hated that she'd had to learn to.

"Teach me the goat's name," he said. "Because she'll follow us to the valley mouth, she always does, and she'll pretend it's an accident, and you'll need to know what to shout when she blocks the trail."

August blinked. Then she laughed—one short, rusty, astonished laugh, like a door opening in a house that had been shut for years—and Corvane thought: *well. There's the second page. There's what they don't put in the doctrine.*

"Turnip," she said.

"Turnip," he agreed, and began packing.

They would leave at first light, up the dead roads, toward the Spire. And three valleys away, in the black under the treeline, something that had once been a man turned its faceless head toward the smell of sulfur and old fire, and began to climb.
