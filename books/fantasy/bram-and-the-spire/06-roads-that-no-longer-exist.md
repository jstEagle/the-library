## 6. Roads That No Longer Exist

The map woke her before dawn.

Not with sound—maps don't speak—but with heat, a slow pulse against August's ribs where it lay wrapped in oilcloth inside her coat. She had learned its rhythms in three days on the road: it warmed when she strayed from its intention and cooled when she found the path again. It was, she had decided, the most ill-tempered piece of parchment ever inked, and she had said as much aloud more than once.

"Stop complaining," she muttered now, sitting up in the lee of a fallen birch. The fire Corvane had built was down to orange eyes in the ash. He was already awake—of course he was—sitting cross-legged with his scarred hands resting on his knees, watching the treeline with the patient expression of a man who had spent eleven years in exile learning that patience was the only virtue nobody could take away from you.

"The map?" he asked.

"The map."

"You spoke to it again."

"It started it."

Corvane made a sound that was almost a laugh, which for him was practically a festival. "In my order we were taught never to address the ink. The old priests believed dragon-blood carried intent, and that intent could hear you." He rose, joints cracking like green kindling, and began kicking dirt over the coals. "I always thought that was superstition."

"And now?"

"Now I think it's worse than superstition. Superstition doesn't burn guild halls." He said it without looking at her, which was his particular kindness. Three days ago, in his sanctuary among the hot springs, he had told her about the smoke rising over Thornfell's southern passes—the guild hall gone, her master gone with it, though whether by flight or by flame no one yet knew. August had chosen to believe in flight. Believing anything else would have stopped her feet.

They walked north as the sun came up, and the world did something wrong.

August had known every road in the Thornfell since she was small enough to fit in a grain sack being carried over one. That had been her gift, her one undeniable talent: roads settled into her mind like water finding a channel. Ask her the way between any two villages in the range and she could tell you not just the path but the feel of it—which stretches were muddy after rain, which hollows held fog until mid-morning, where a certain crooked pine leaned so far over the trail you had to duck. She'd guided caravans through blizzards by this knowledge. She'd been proud of it in a quiet, private way, the way you're proud of a thing nobody else can see well enough to envy.

So when the trail forked above Miller's Run—and there was no Miller's Run below it—she stopped walking entirely.

"That's wrong," she said.

"What is?"

She pointed. "That valley. There should be a village down there. Thirty roofs, maybe forty. A mill on the stream—you can see the stream right there, it's *right there*, the mill would sit exactly—" She gestured at an empty green slope where a mill wheel absolutely should have been turning. "There should be smoke. There should be dogs. There should be an enormous dog named Turnip who once ate half a wheel of cheese off my pack while I slept."

Corvane looked down at the empty valley for a long moment. "I've crossed this range twice," he said carefully. "There has never been a village there."

"There has *always* been a village there. I've bought bread there. I've hidden in their hay loft. An old woman gave me honey cakes every solstice for six years running because she thought I was somebody's granddaughter and I never corrected her." Her voice was climbing. She could hear it climbing and couldn't stop it. "Turnip is real. Turnip *happened*."

"All right," Corvane said, calm as deep water. "Show me the fork."

She showed him the fork. The fork was correct—it matched the map's branching lines precisely, two trails splitting around a boulder the size of a chapel. But when she tried to hold the rest of the picture in her head—the road down to Miller's Run, the mill, the honey-cake woman, Turnip—it slid. Not all at once. In pieces. Like trying to carry water uphill in your hands.

And the map, against her ribs, went cool. Content. Almost smug.

*It took something,* she thought, and the thought arrived already knowing what, the way you know you've lost your keys only by reaching for them. She stood very still and went looking through herself.

Her name was August Iversen. She was twenty-three years old, or close enough. She had been raised partly by the Wayfarers' Guild and partly by Brenna's smugglers, and both parties would deny it. She could read weather like scripture and lies like large print.

But when she reached for the name of her home village—the first one, the true one, where she'd been born before anyone decided what she was for—

Nothing.

Not an ache where the memory used to be. Not a scar. Just nothing, smooth and complete, like a page torn out so cleanly you'd never know the book had been longer. She remembered that she had been born somewhere. She remembered knowing its name once. She even remembered the shape of the fact, the way you remember the shape of a room in a dream. But the name itself was gone, and worse—when she tried to reconstruct it from everything around it, the roads leading out of it, the faces in it—those slid too, softening at the edges like wet ink.

"August." Corvane's hand was on her shoulder. When had he come back down the trail? "Breathe. You've gone white as a birch."

"It took it." She heard her own voice from very far away. "The map took the name of my village. I can't—I reach for it and there's just—" She laughed, high and horrible. "Do you understand? I'm a *wayfinder*. My whole self is roads and places, and this thing is eating them one bite at a time, and it started with home."

* * *

They argued about it over cold noon bread, which was to say Corvane argued and August paced and the map pulsed gently whenever she paced toward south.

"Burn it," she said for the fourth time. "We've tried. It comes back."

"We've tried," Corvane agreed. "Fire returns it. Water returns it. I buried it under a cairn yesterday while you slept and it was back in your coat before you woke." He turned the bread loaf in his hands without eating any. "You cannot throw away a thing that refuses to be thrown. You can only decide how you'll carry it."

"Or someone else carries it." She stopped pacing. "That's the answer, isn't it? It wants me because the courier gave it to me. So we give it to someone else. A stranger. Any traveler on the south road—"

"No."

"You didn't even think about it."

"I thought about it for three days, before you ever asked." He set the bread down. His scarred hands were very still. "August, do you remember what I told you about the Hollow Men? What they were before?"

"People who read the map too long." Everyone in the high country had some version of the story now. Faceless riders in the king's livery, hunting the brand's heat through the passes.

"People who *held* it too long. Couriers, priests, one unlucky tax collector whose descendants still tell the story badly. The ink doesn't just take payment from the one who bears it. It takes from everyone who touches it willingly." He looked at her then, directly, and his eyes held the particular sorrow of a man who had watched his entire order burn and had learned to stop flinching from the word. "If we hand this thing to some farmer's son on the south road, the Hollow Men will find him in a week, and whatever he was will be scraped out and worn like a coat. And you'll have traded your horror onto someone smaller."

"So I just let it eat me instead."

"I didn't say that either."

"Then what *do* you say, Corvane? Because you wanted to come. You said you wanted to see the dragon before it died. Well, congratulations—we're going. But don't stand there telling me what I can't do while the thing in my coat is chewing on my childhood."

He was quiet for a moment. Then, unexpectedly, gently: "What did the village smell like?"

She blinked. "What?"

"Your home. The name is gone—I believe you, I'm sorry, may every ash in the Choir grieve it. But the name isn't the whole of a place. What did it *smell* like? Close your eyes if it helps. I'll watch the road."

She wanted to refuse out of spite. Instead she closed her eyes.

Woodsmoke, immediately. That was strange—the memory was supposed to be gone—but woodsmoke rose up whole and vivid, pine and birch smoke and underneath it bread baking, and wet wool drying by a fire, and a specific smell she had no word for except *the cellar smell*, stone and earth and apples keeping through winter. And a sound: someone singing low and tunelessly, some working song, the words gone but the melody still there, patient as dripping water.

Her eyes flew open. "It's still there. Parts of it. The name's gone but the—the rest is still there, under it."

Corvane nodded slowly. "Then here is what I say. The map takes what it takes, and we cannot stop that today. But it takes names first because names are easiest to lift—clean cuts, little resistance. What it cannot seem to take—" he tapped his own chest, where old scars crossed like a ladder, "—is what a place *made* of you. The smoke stays. The song stays. The dog that stole your cheese, gods help us all, stays." The almost-laugh again. "So we walk. And when it takes a thing, you grieve it honestly, and then you look for what it couldn't reach. That's not comfort. I know the difference. It's strategy."

August sat down heavily on a rock and took a bite of the bread she didn't want. It was, annoyingly, good. Corvane baked like a man making amends to the world.

"Turnip stayed," she said at last, grudgingly. "I checked. Turnip is intact. Cheese theft and all."

"Then the map has limits," Corvane said. "Everything does. Even dragons." He paused, and she caught him glancing north, up the trail, with an expression she'd begun to recognize—a hunger he tried to keep polite. "Even kings."

* * *

They made eight miles that afternoon, following the map's warmth along trails that behaved increasingly like strangers.

It wasn't only that roads were missing. Some of them had *moved*. A switchback she'd climbed a hundred times now bent the other way around a shoulder of rock. A ford she knew—knee-deep, gravel-bottomed, safe—had become a chattering brown torrent twice that depth, as though the river had reconsidered its course sometime in the last three days out of sheer spite. Once she led them confidently down what she swore was the Shepherd's Cut, a smuggler's path Brenna's people had used for decades, and came out at a shepherd's hut that was wrong in a way she couldn't name until Corvane said quietly, "The door's on the wrong side."

He was right. Every mountain hut in the Thornfell faced its door east, away from the prevailing storm. This one faced west. Otherwise it was perfect—the same turf roof, the same peat stack, the same carved horse-head above the lintel that shepherds put up for luck. Just mirrored, like a reflection that had gotten impatient and stepped out of the water.

"The land remembers differently than I do," August said, staring at it. "Or it's remembering on purpose wrong."

"Or," Corvane said, "the map isn't showing you where things are. It's showing you where things were, a very long time ago, and the world is having to scramble to keep up." He touched one of the hut's walls and drew his fingers back quickly. "Warm. Do you feel it? Stone shouldn't be warm here. Something under us is awake."

August pressed her palm flat against the turf-and-stone wall. He was right. A faint, deep heat, rhythmic, almost like breathing—or like a pulse. The same pulse as the map against her ribs. She snatched her hand back.

They camped that night in the ruins of a waystation, one of the old Guild shelters, and August dreamed of the honey-cake woman. In the dream the woman's face kept sliding sideways, becoming other faces, becoming no face at all, and each time it slid August grabbed for the woman's name and came up with handfuls of nothing, and the woman only smiled and handed her another cake and said, in a voice like wind through an empty house, *"Eat, child. You'll need the weight of yourself for what's coming."*

She woke before dawn with tears frozen on her lashes and the map burning hot enough that she yanked it out of her coat and threw it on the ground.

It landed face-up in the frost. And in the grey light she saw, for the first time, that the ink had changed.

Three days ago the map had shown her route north in fine branching lines, precise as frost-ferns. Now those lines had thickened, darkened, and around the line marking her own position—not marked with an X, never with an X, but with a tiny bead of red that moved when she moved—new script had surfaced. Tiny characters, dense as insect wings, crowding the margins. Not any alphabet she knew. But she knew handwriting when she saw it. Someone had written this. Or something. And the newest line, still glistening wet, ran along the top margin in letters that seemed to shift when she looked directly at them:

*PAID: ONE NAME. REMAINING: MORE.*

"Oh no you don't," August whispered.

The map pulsed. Warm. Patient. Pleased.

Behind her, Corvane sat up in the ruined shelter, and his voice came out flat and careful, the voice of a man reading bad news in a fire's behavior. "August. The springs at my sanctuary—they've been cooling for years, that's why the order left the high country. Last night, for the first time in a decade, they ran hot again."

She turned. On the northern horizon, past the black shoulders of the peaks, the sky above the Spire was the faintest bruised orange, like an ember breathed upon.

Something far away had felt them coming. And something much closer, folded in oilcloth in the frost at her feet, was very glad about it.

"Pick up your map," Corvane said quietly. "And walk fast. I don't think we're the only ones who noticed."

Far behind them, down the southern trails, something with no face had also begun to move.
