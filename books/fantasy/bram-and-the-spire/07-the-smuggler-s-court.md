## 7. The Smuggler's Court

The Salt Lantern hung crooked over the harbor road in Dredgemouth, the way it had hung for as long as August could remember, its painted lantern faded to a suggestion of itself. Below it, the door breathed out warmth and the smell of fried onions and wet wool, and above all that—faint as a secret—the smell of Brenna's pipe.

August stood across the street for a long moment, hand pressed against her coat to keep the map flat against her ribs, and let herself want something for the first time in weeks.

She wanted to walk through that door and be nobody again. She wanted Brenna to look up from her ledgers, squint, and say, "Oh, it's you," in the tone that meant *I have missed you and will die before admitting it.* She wanted soup. She wanted the corner bench by the hearth where she'd done her sums as a girl while smugglers argued over her head about whether eel-pots or false-bottomed crates were the more profitable vice.

The map pulsed once under her palm, patient and hot, like a cat deciding not to scratch her yet.

"I know," she muttered. "I'm going."

She knew what she was risking. She had rehearsed the argument the whole way down the coast path: *Brenna raised me. Brenna does not sell people. Brenna sold* cargo*, which is different, because cargo doesn't sit at her table.* But she also knew there was a bounty on the map now—she'd seen the notice nailed to a tollhouse door, three hundred crowns, no questions, and she'd watched a tollkeeper's wife read it twice and then look at August's traveling cloak the way you look at a purse someone dropped.

Three hundred crowns bought a lot of loyalty. It bought whole fleets of it.

Still. This was Brenna.

She went in.

The common room was half full of the usual Tuesday crowd—fishwives, a fence named Piotr who owed everyone money, two off-duty harbor guards drinking carefully watered beer and pretending not to notice the contraband stacked in plain sight because the contraband was technically theirs. Conversation dipped when August entered, the way it always did for a stranger, and rose again when Brenna's second husband, Odo, waved her through to the back with his chin.

"She's in the counting room," he said. "Complaining about you, as it happens."

"About me?"

"About your absence. Apparently grief has made her insufferable." He paused, and his eyes went briefly, professionally to her hand, wrapped now in a linen bandage she'd told no one about. Something in his face closed a fraction of an inch. "Go on in, girl."

The counting room smelled of tallow candles and Brenna herself, who was a small round woman built like a barrel with opinions, silver-streaked hair pinned up with what appeared to be a fishhook. She looked up from her ledger, and her face did three things very quickly: relief, fury, and then nothing at all, which was how August knew things were already going wrong.

"You look like something the tide refused," Brenna said. "Sit."

"I'm not staying long."

"No one ever is." Brenna set down her pen. Her eyes flicked to the bandaged hand, then away, deliberately, the way you look away from a loaded crossbow on a dinner table. "Soup first. Business after. That's the rule of this house and I'll thank you to remember it even if you've forgotten your manners."

The soup was barley and leek, and it was so good that August's eyes stung, which she blamed on the steam. Brenna watched her eat without eating anything herself, and asked questions in her sideways way—not *where have you been* but *how were the passes*; not *what happened to your hand* but *you always did burn yourself on other people's fires.*

It was almost enough. August got as far as the third bowl before she made her mistake, which was to relax.

"The guild hall burned," she said quietly. "Master Ellery is dead. The Hollow Men came for me, Brenna. They came through the Thornfell passes in winter, which they shouldn't be able to do, tracking something I'm carrying." She hesitated. "I need to get north. Quietly. Off every ledger you keep."

Brenna was quiet for a long moment. Outside, a gull screamed at another gull about something neither would ever back down from.

"And there it is," Brenna said at last. She took out her pipe, packed it, and did not light it, which was worse. "You come into my house with king's hunters behind you and ask me to make you invisible. You know what you're asking? Three hundred crowns, girl. That's the number on your head, or near enough—it's the paper, really, they want, but you come with the paper stapled to your hand." She leaned forward. "Show me."

"Brenna—"

"*Show me.*"

And here was the thing August would turn over and over in the weeks after, in the cold places between sleep: she showed her. Because it was Brenna. Because some starved, stupid part of her still wanted to put the terrible thing down on the table of the one person who might help carry it.

She unwound the bandage. The map lay inked across her palm in lines of dark red-brown, fine as spiderwork, and it seemed to shift when Brenna looked at it—or maybe that was the candlelight. Maybe it was wanting to be seen.

Brenna looked at it for four seconds. August counted them.

Then Brenna sighed, stood, and walked to the window, and said, in a voice August had never heard her use, flat as slate: "Odo. Now."

The door opened. Odo came in with Piotr and the two harbor guards, and none of them were pretending anymore.

"I'm sorry, love," Brenna said, not turning around. "Truly. But you've been carrying three hundred crowns into my house in a bad season, and I've got forty mouths that eat whether the ships come in or not. The Hollow Men don't rough their merchandise. They gave their word. You'll be read, and it'll hurt, and then—" she shrugged, a small terrible motion "—then whatever's left isn't my problem."

August's whole body went cold and clear, the way it did on rooftops at night. Later she would grieve. Right now there was arithmetic.

One door, blocked by Odo, who was big but slow and had a bad knee she'd helped him wrap last spring. Window, second floor, onto the alley—she'd gone out that window a hundred times as a girl sneaking to the docks, and Brenna knew it, which meant Piotr was probably in the alley, and Piotr was a coward, which meant he'd flinch. The guards were the real problem; they'd want the bounty themselves and they had steel.

"You taught me better than this," August said. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "Never show anyone the cargo."

Brenna's shoulders twitched. "Aye," she said. "Well. Some lessons take."

August threw the soup bowl at the candle.

It was a good bowl, heavy glazed clay, and it took the flame out and scattered burning tallow across the ledger desk, and in the half-second of shouting that followed she was already moving—over the desk, shoulder into Odo's bad knee just hard enough to fold him, through the doorway into the common room where she snatched a lantern off its hook and hurled it into the corner where forty years of Brenna's account papers sat in dry baskets, because if the court was going to burn anyway it might as well burn the evidence.

Then she was out the front door into the street, and behind her the Salt Lantern began, with the enthusiasm of old timber and old grievances, to properly catch.

* * *

Dredgemouth knew fire the way an old dog knows thunder—with immediate, universal panic. Bells started up somewhere. People poured out of houses in nightclothes, forming bucket chains with the efficiency of people who had done this many times and resented it every time. In the confusion, August ran.

She didn't run for the gates. She ran for the warehouse.

It was instinct, and she hated the instinct even as she obeyed it, because the instinct said: *when hunted, go to ground you know,* and the only ground she knew in Dredgemouth was Brenna's ground—Warehouse Nine on the salt quay, with its false wall behind the tar stores and its crawl-space under the eel-smoking room that came out through a drainage culvert below the tide line. She had hidden in that crawl-space at eleven years old while revenue men tramped overhead, giggling into her own sleeve until Brenna found her afterward and cuffed her ear and said, *never hide twice in the same hole, girl, and never hide where they can see you love it.*

But the Hollow Men tracked heat, not scent, and the map was a bonfire in her hand, and every rational thought said run for the marshes, run for the hills, run anywhere—

She went to Warehouse Nine anyway. Because it was hers. Because some part of her, even now, even tonight, thought of it as a place that belonged to her and she to it, and she wanted to say goodbye to it, and she was willing to die of that wanting.

Later, she would understand that this was exactly the kind of thinking the map fed on.

The warehouse loomed against the orange-lit sky, tar-black, smelling of salt and pitch and smoked eel. The side door's latch was broken in the same way it had been broken for nine years. Inside, darkness, dust, the shapes of crates. August moved through it by feel, past the tar stores, toward the false wall, and her hand found the familiar seam, and she stood there with her fingers on it and let herself feel one clean moment of grief—

Which was when the trapdoor above the eel-room opened, and light spilled down, and Piotr's voice said, conversationally, "In here! She's in here!"—and from the doorway behind him, filling it, came the Hollow Men.

There were three. They wore the king's gray, or what had been the king's gray once, though the cloth hung on them wrong now, like clothing remembering a body rather than covering one. Where faces should have been there was smooth skin, featureless except for a faint suggestion—a pressure where eyes had been, a seam where a mouth had sealed itself shut. They had read the map too long, Corvane had told her, back when telling her things was still news. They had looked at the roads until the roads looked back, and what looked out of them now wasn't them.

The worst part was that they moved gently. Careful, courteous, unhurried, like ushers. One extended a hand toward her, palm up, in a gesture that had clearly once meant *come along, please.*

August ripped open the false wall and went through.

Behind her, wood splintered—no, she realized, plunging down into the crawl-space's black throat, behind her, wood *burned*. The fire had crossed the quay. Of course it had. A warehouse full of tar, pitch, lamp oil, and smuggled spirits does not wait politely for narrative convenience; it goes up like a celebration. Heat breathed down the passage after her, and smoke followed the heat, and ahead the drainage culvert grate was rusted in a way it hadn't been rusted nine years ago.

She hit the grate with her branded hand, and the map flared, and the iron gave like toffee, which frightened her more than the fire did, and then she was out, sliding down weed-slick stone to the tideline, the warehouse roaring above her, embers falling around her like furious orange snow.

She looked back once.

Through the burning doorway, silhouetted, one of the Hollow Men stood in the heart of the flames, unhurried, its smooth face tilted down toward her little figure on the shore, and it did not follow. It simply stood there among the fire, patient as winter, and August understood that it didn't need to chase her tonight. It had seen where she was going. The roads she traveled were written on her hand, and hands could be read later.

She turned north and walked into the sea-smell and the dark, and the fire lit her way for a mile.

* * *

She stopped at dawn, in the ruins of an old signal tower on the headland, and allowed herself, finally, to feel it.

It arrived all at once, the way grief does when you've held the door against it too long. Not the guild hall—she'd mourned Ellery already, in the walking. This was older and smaller and worse: Brenna, packing her pipe without lighting it. Brenna saying *love* in a voice like a closing ledger. Brenna weighing forty mouths against one ungrateful orphan and finding the math easy, and—here was the part that made August press her fist against her mouth—the math *was* easy, wasn't it? That was the lesson, wasn't it, that Brenna had been teaching her since she was six years old, over and over, with kindness and cruelty in equal measure because she'd never distinguished between them:

Everything is cargo. Everything has a price. Love the price, never the goods.

August had loved the goods anyway. That was her error, and tonight it had cost her a home, a history, a childhood's worth of hiding-places and shortcuts and secret doors—all the little private geography of being Brenna's girl—burned or bartered, either way gone.

And yet.

And yet, sitting in the cold tower with the sun coming up over a sea the color of hammered tin, August found that beneath the grief lay something unexpected: clarity. Cold, bright, and strangely comfortable, like the air at altitude. Because she had spent her whole life believing that invisibility was safety—that if she was small enough, forgettable enough, useful enough, the world would leave her alone—and tonight the world had said, plainly: no. Tonight had burned down to the studs. And standing in the ashes of it she could see, for the first time, the actual shape of her situation, which was this:

There was no version of this where she was left alone. There never had been. She had been bred for this road, marked for it, hunted across a kingdom for it, and the only choice she had ever truly owned was what to do next. Brenna hadn't taken that from her tonight. If anything, Brenna had finally given her something honest: a bill of sale, and proof that the price of everything was real.

Fine, August thought, flexing her branded hand, watching the red lines move under the skin like something waking. Then I choose the road. Not because the map wants it. Because I do.

She got up, brushed the ash from her coat—it was everywhere now, in her hair, her collar, a fine gray film she suspected she'd be wearing for the rest of her life—and looked north along the cliff track toward the high country, where the hot springs steamed in the valleys and a disgraced priest was waiting, whether he knew it yet or not.

Behind her, far down the coast, Dredgemouth smoked quietly. Ahead, the mountains waited with the particular patience of mountains.

"Right," August said aloud, to nobody, in the tone Brenna had used for settling accounts. "North, then."

The map pulsed warmly against her palm, pleased as a cat that had gotten the door opened, and she set off along the clifftop—walking, for the first time in her life, not away from something, but toward it.

She did not look back again. Which was why she didn't see, far behind on the headland road, a rider in travel-stained green following her track at a careful distance—and why the sight of the old shrine three days north, with Brenna's unmistakable brass lantern-charm hung on its post, would catch her entirely by surprise.
