## 8. Fire Under the Ice

Corvane had spent eleven years learning to be unremarkable, and he was rather good at it now, which he considered the great joke of his life. A defrocked priest of the Ash Choir, hiding in a valley of hot springs where no one came except goats and the occasional lost pilgrim, and here he was with a companion: a wiry smuggler-girl with a map burned into her hand and an expression like a cat that has been carried somewhere against its will.

"You're enjoying this," August said.

"I am walking," Corvane said. "Enjoyment is a separate matter."

"You're smiling."

"That's wind damage."

The pass above his sanctuary climbed in long gray switchbacks, and by midmorning they were above the treeline, where the world simplified itself to stone and sky and the enormous white shoulder of the Vhal glacier ahead. The air had that thin, ringing quality it got at altitude, as though the mountains were humming a note too high for human ears. Corvane felt it in his teeth. He always did, up here.

August felt something too. He could tell because she kept flexing her left hand, opening and closing it, the way a person does when their palm is itching from the inside.

"It's louder up here," she said, not quite asking.

"The map knows where it comes from." Corvane adjusted his pack strap. "Ink remembers its well."

"That's not comforting."

"I wasn't aiming for comfort. I'm out of practice."

They walked on. The girl was quieter than she'd been in the valley—quieter even than the night she'd arrived half-frozen at his spring, all suspicion and sharp questions, demanding to know what a dragon-priest was doing living alone among the steam vents like a hermit crab in a stolen shell. He'd liked her immediately, which had surprised him. Eleven years of solitude had taught him to distrust anyone who didn't bore him.

Now she walked beside him through the high passes because she had nowhere else to go and because, though she'd never say it aloud, the map pulled her this way and she wanted someone along who knew why.

He understood that. He'd spent eleven years wanting someone who knew why.

* * *

The glacier appeared around a bend all at once, the way glaciers do—a wall of blue-white ice filling the head of the valley, cracked and groaning, ancient beyond any honest reckoning. Meltwater ran from its toe in channels of startling clarity, and where the water pooled in the hollows it lay so still it looked like glass laid over darkness.

Corvane stopped at the edge of the ice field and let his pack slide off his shoulder.

"Here?" August said. "There's nothing here."

"There is never nothing here." He crouched and pressed his bare palm flat to a patch of exposed stone between the ice sheets. "That is rather the entire point of my order, and the reason there is no longer an order."

She crouched beside him, skeptical, arms crossed against the cold. Then her eyes widened.

He watched her feel it—the heat coming up through the stone, faint but unmistakable, a warmth with a pulse to it. Slow. Enormous. Not the quick flicker of a fire but the deep patient beat of something asleep.

"What is that?"

"A vein." He stood, knees cracking—he was fifty-three and the mountain had opinions about his joints—and gestured along the ice field with one arm. "Follow the blue line in the glacier. Do you see it? Where the ice is darkest, down near the base."

August leaned forward. Deep beneath the surface, running the length of the glacier like a vein under skin, was a ribbon of color—not blue exactly, not green, something older than either, a shade that seemed to move when you weren't looking directly at it. As they watched, the light within it swelled and dimmed, swelled and dimmed, keeping time with the heat in the stone.

"It's alive," she said quietly.

"The mountain is. Or parts of it are." Corvane found a flat rock and sat, because this was going to take a while and his legs had already filed a complaint. "Sit down, Wayfinder. This is a history lesson, and history lessons go better seated."

"I'm not a Wayfinder. The Guild's gone."

"The Guild's gone. You aren't." He patted the rock beside him. She sat, after the small obligatory delay that pride required.

* * *

"When I was a novice," Corvane began, "my teacher told me that before there were kings, there were fires. Great fires, sleeping in the bones of the world. Dragons, we called them, though that word means less than you'd think—it's a human word, a small word, the way 'ocean' is a small word if you've only ever seen a pond."

August snorted. "You're saying dragons aren't dragons."

"I'm saying the thing in your stories that hoards gold and eats knights—that's a folk memory. A child's drawing of something vast. What actually slept under these mountains was—" he searched for it, and gave up on elegance, as he often did— "*fire that thinks*. Fire that dreams. It moved slow, slower than seasons, and it warmed the deep places, and where its blood rose near the surface, things grew. That's what a dragon-vein is. A place where the fire comes close enough to touch the world."

"And people touched it."

"People touched everything. We're like that." He smiled ruefully. "The first priests—the true Ash Choir, centuries before me—we didn't worship the fires. We tended them. Kept the passes open where the veins ran warm, kept villages fed in the hard years, sang the old rhythms to help the fire sleep easy. We were shepherds, more or less. Very cold, very solemn shepherds."

"Gentle humor," August said. "From a priest."

"Ex-priest. Defrocked. I'm allowed to be funny now; it's in the paperwork."

But she was watching the light in the ice, and her humor faded as she looked. "And then kings came."

"And then kings came." The warmth went out of his voice, and he felt it go, and let it. "A king is a man with a problem every other man doesn't have: everyone can see when he's weak. And in the bad years—the famine years, the long winters—every man could see it. So some clever advisor, some ancestor of every advisor since, looked at the sleeping fires and thought: *there is power that doesn't fail.*"

"The Covenant," August said. "You mentioned it at the springs. You stopped talking and made tea instead."

"Tea is how I avoid finishing sentences. Ask anyone. No one's left to ask, but they'd confirm it." He drew a breath. "The Covenant was simple, the way cruel things usually are. A dragon-vein could be bound. Channeled. Its fire drawn up through crafted vessels—crowns, scepters, rings—and a king wearing such a crown would never have a weak year. His fields would ripen while his neighbors' rotted. His armies marched warm through the worst winters. Famine became a weapon he held and others didn't."

"How do you bind a mountain?"

"You don't bind the mountain. You bind the *fire*—a piece of it, drawn thin, given a shape and a hunger. And it needs feeding." He looked at her, and made himself say it plainly, because she deserved plainness and had gotten very little of it. "Fire that thinks needs a mind to think with. Every bound crown ate a man. Slowly. The king poured himself in year by year—his judgments, his memories, eventually his name—until the crown ruled and the man sat quiet underneath. The kingdom stayed green. The king became furniture."

August was silent a long moment. Her scarred hand had closed into a fist without her seeming to notice.

"How many crowns?"

"Once? Nine. The Nine Thrones, the old texts call them, back when the maps had more kingdoms on them. Eight burned out or broke over the centuries. Their veins went dormant or wild." He nodded at the glacier. "This one sleeps because its fire was never fully taken—only sipped. But there's one crown left. One throne still fed."

"The Spire," August said. "King Aldric."

"King Aldric," Corvane agreed, "and whatever wears him."

* * *

They sat with that for a while. The glacier ticked and settled. Somewhere under the ice, the vein pulsed its slow light, indifferent to the two small warm creatures sitting above it, and Corvane found, as he always did up here, that awe and anger were sitting together in his chest like old married couples do—close, familiar, not speaking.

It was August who broke the silence, in the sideways manner she used for anything that mattered.

"The ink. The map on my hand. It's from here."

"Dragon's blood," he said. "Drawn from a vein like this one—or from the last fire itself, if the stories hold. It's the only ink that remembers roads. Ordinary ink sits on paper. Dragon-blood ink sits in *the world*, and so it moves when the world moves." He turned to look at her branded palm, wrapped now in linen she'd wound around it herself, stubbornly, as if a bandage could argue with a covenant. "Whoever made your map knew the veins. Knew them intimately. Which raises questions I don't like, about who sent you and what they expect to find at the Spire."

"You think I was sent."

"I think maps like that aren't accidents. Nothing about the old fires is accidental anymore. The age of accidents ended when the first king picked up a crown." He sighed. "But that's tomorrow's problem. Today I promised to show you the source, and I keep my promises. It's the only habit the Choir left me that I'm fond of."

He led her out onto the ice.

* * *

The glacier's surface was sculpted by wind into long gray waves, and they followed a melt channel down to where the dark vein ran nearest the surface—a window in the ice, a pocket of clear frozen depth perhaps four feet down. Corvane knelt and brushed snow from it, and the light below brightened as if noticing them.

August knelt too. Up close, the vein was not a ribbon but a *depth*—color folding into color, and within it, shapes. Slow shapes. Vast slow curves turning like whales seen from very far above.

"Oh," she said. Small sound. Reverent despite herself.

"Yes," Corvane said. "That's the correct response. My teacher said novices were only permitted one 'oh' per vein, and then they had to get back to sweeping."

"What am I looking at?"

"The dream. The fire dreaming itself." His voice had gone soft, the liturgy-soft he couldn't entirely shed. "Look long enough and you'll see forms. The priests said the fire dreams in the shapes of what it remembers—mountains, storms, the first creatures that ever warmed themselves at it. Some of us believed that when a vein dies, the dream ends. Others believed the dream just goes on with no one left to see it." He paused. "I was always in the second group. Sentimental. Another item for the defrocking paperwork."

August's marked hand hovered over the ice, and the vein answered—brightening, gathering toward her, the light leaning up through four feet of ancient glacier like a plant toward sun. She snatched her hand back.

"It knows me."

"Ink calls to ink. Blood calls to blood." Corvane studied her face in the strange shifting light. "You asked me once why I offered to come with you. You assumed it was the bounty, or boredom, or that I wanted the Spire's secrets for myself."

"Didn't you?"

"I want to see the dragon before it dies. I told you that, and it's true. But it isn't the whole truth, and you've been paying for other people's partial truths lately, so here's mine." He turned back to the vein, because it was easier to say to the light than to her face. "I was defrocked for teaching the old rites to a village whose vein was failing. Warming rites. Survival rites. The Synod called it interference—the fires must fade as the Covenant fades, they said, it is the natural order. I called it letting people freeze so that a doctrine stays tidy. They took my vestments and my name from the rolls and put me on a road south." He smiled, without much humor in it. "Eleven years later, a girl walks into my sanctuary with the last dragon's blood written on her hand, headed for the last bound crown, and asks me—no, doesn't even ask, just *assumes*—that I'll help her break it."

"Is that a problem?"

"It's the opposite of a problem. It's the first time in eleven years the world has asked me to be what I trained my whole life to be." He laughed, short and startled by its own honesty. "Do you understand what that's worth to a man? I would follow you for that alone. The rest—the danger, the Hollow Men, the map eating your memories piece by piece—that part I'd happily skip."

August looked at him for a long moment. In the vein-light her face was young and old at once, the way faces get on long roads.

"They'll come after us," she said. "The Hollow Men. They track the map's heat. You know that."

"I know it."

"Up here there's nowhere to run."

"There never is. That's what makes it a story worth telling." He stood and offered her his hand, and after a moment she took it, and he hauled her up onto the wind-scoured ice. "Come. There's one more thing to show you, and then we should be moving, because you're right—they'll come, and I'd rather meet them tired of walking than tired of waiting."

* * *

The last thing was a quarter mile along the glacier's flank: a shelf of black stone thrust up through the ice like the rib of something buried. On it, carved deep and worn shallow by centuries of weather, ran lines of script.

August traced one with her finger. "Old Choir marks?"

"Covenant law." Corvane's jaw tightened. "The binding formula. They carved it at every vein they took—like a brand on a steer. *By this sign the fire is held; by this crown the fire serves; by the king's own mind the price is paid.*" He read it flatly, without ceremony, the way you'd read a bill of sale. "Voluntary, technically. That was always the elegant cruelty of it. The kings *chose*. Famine at the gates, kingdom starving, and a crown offered: give us your mind, grain by grain, and your people live. What kind of man says no? What kind of man says yes?" He shook his head. "Both answers are terrible. That's how you know a thing was designed by clever, frightened people."

August had gone very still. She was staring at the carving, but Corvane had learned enough of her in these weeks to know she wasn't seeing it. She was seeing the Spire. Seeing a king she'd never met, sitting quiet underneath a crown, grain by grain.

"Aldric chose," she said slowly. "Whatever's up there—whatever wears him—he let it happen. For his kingdom."

"So the histories claim. Kings get generous histories; it's one of the perks." Corvane looked north, where beyond the glacier's shoulder the peaks climbed toward the high country, and past them, hidden in cloud and distance, the Spire waited like a held breath. "Though I'll tell you a priest's observation, free of charge. In all the old accounts, in every rite and record the Choir kept—there's one question nobody ever wrote down an answer to."

"What's that?"

"What happens to the fire when the last crown breaks." He met her eyes. "The Synod assumed it dies. The rebels hoped it dies. Everyone assumes the binding goes both ways—that the crown holds the fire, and so breaking the crown kills it." He tapped the black stone, once, on the worn carving. "But I've lived beside a sleeping vein for eleven years, girl. And I'll tell you what I've learned about fire that thinks."

August waited.

"It doesn't like being owned," Corvane said. "And it has been planning something for a very long time."

Far under the ice, unhurried and immense, the vein pulsed—and this time, both of them felt it answer, a beat of warmth rolling up through their boots like a word spoken just below hearing.

August looked down at her wrapped hand. When she peeled back the linen, the map's lines had shifted again—roads redrawn, miles gained overnight, the route bending north toward the Spire with new urgency, as if the ink had heard the mountain speak and was eager to reply.

"We should go," she said.

"Yes," Corvane said, and reached for his pack. "Before it gets impatient. Fire that thinks is bad enough." He settled the straps on his shoulders and started north along the glacier's flank, into the wind. "Fire that *hopes*—that's how mountains change hands."
