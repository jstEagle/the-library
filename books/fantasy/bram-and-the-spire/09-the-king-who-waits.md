## 9. The King Who Waits

The king dreamed of bread.

Not the grey barley loaves of his reign, dense as river stones and twice as bitter, but real bread—his mother's bread, torn steaming from the oven in the little kitchen behind the fallow fields of Vaelmoor, butter melting into it in golden pools. He was nine years old and he had done nothing all day but be hungry, which was the proper work of a farmer's son in autumn, and the whole world smelled of crust and woodsmoke and the rain coming on.

*You are not nine*, said the Crown.

"I know," Aldric told it, because he had learned long ago that arguing with the Crown was like arguing with winter. You could do it. It was even bracing. But the snow would fall regardless.

*Then stop eating phantom bread. There is work.*

"There is always work." He looked down at his hands—young hands, always young in this place, unspotted and unbent—and found them full of petitions. They were always full of petitions. "The eastern levees need rebuilding. The granary at Dunmore has rats. The third daughter of some minor house wants a title, and her father wants a tax exemption, and neither will speak to me again unless both get their way."

*I will handle it.*

"You will handle it," Aldric agreed, and felt the familiar sensation of his own mouth moving through the words while something else chose them.

That was how it went, these days. These years. He had stopped counting them precisely, because the counting made the walls of the dream feel closer.

* * *

He had not always resented the Crown. That was the thing no one understood—the histories would never say it, because the histories were written by people who had never been hungry enough to bargain with heaven.

He had *wanted* it.

He remembered the year of the Black Famine with the terrible clarity that only guilt provides. He was twenty-six, newly crowned, still wearing his father's crown which sat on him the way his father's boots had: heavy, borrowed, smelling faintly of another man's ambition. The rains had failed three years running. The rivers of the lowlands had shrunk to brown threads. In the capital they buried the poor in common graves by lamplight so no one would have to watch, and in the villages they did not bury them at all, because there was no one left standing to hold the shovel.

Aldric had gone out among them—he remembered insisting on it, over the objections of ministers who spoke of fever and daggers—and he had knelt in a field at Vaelmoor, not four miles from his mother's kitchen, and an old woman had gripped his wrist with fingers like dry reeds and asked him, quite reasonably, *what is a king for?*

He had no answer. He had speeches. Speeches are what you give instead of answers.

It was Corvane's order that came to him then—or what would become Corvane's order, the dragon-priests of the Ash Choir, keepers of the old Covenant, who came down from the high passes in their smoke-grey robes and said, quietly, in the way of men offering either salvation or poison: there is fire under the mountains, majesty. There always has been. And there is one dragon left to hold it.

"Bind it to the crown," the eldest priest had said, "and its fire will warm your winters and wake your springs. The land will bloom. The rivers will run."

"And the cost?" Aldric had asked, because he was young but not stupid.

The priest had looked at him for a long moment. "The cost is that you will no longer be entirely the man who pays it."

He should have walked away. He had rehearsed walking away a thousand times since, in the amber corridors of the dream, turning the moment over like a coin worn smooth. He could have been a small king of a sad country. He could have buried the poor properly, with names. He could have grown old telling the truth about the limits of crowns.

But the old woman's question was still wrapped around his wrist like a shackle, and the famine carts were still creaking through the gates at dusk, and he was twenty-six, and the whole weight of a starving kingdom fit so neatly into one offered solution.

"Yes," he said.

* * *

The binding took three days. Nobody tells you that. In the songs it is a lightning-strike moment, crown meets brow, thunder, destiny. In truth it was three days of sitting on a cold throne in a cold hall while priests chanted and a creature older than the language they were chanting in considered him.

Vhalorath. He had not seen it clearly even then—it kept itself folded in shadow at the back of the throne room like a mountain range deciding to sit indoors—but he had felt it looking at him. Not with hunger. That was the surprise. With something worse. With *recognition*.

*You are tired*, it had said, in a voice like a hearth speaking.

"I am a king."

*Those are not opposites. I remember when kings were shepherds. Then they were generals. Now they are tired men in chairs.* A sound that might have been laughter, if landslides laughed. *Very well. You wish to save them. So did I, once.*

"What happened to you?"

*Other people saved things with me,* said the dragon, *until there was very little of me left over.*

And then the fire had come up through the floor, and the crown had come alive on his brow, and Aldric had screamed for a while, and then stopped screaming, and then—

and then the rains came.

* * *

They came that very spring. The rivers fattened. The black fields turned green with an indecency that made grown farmers weep into their sleeves. The famine ended so thoroughly and so quickly that within five years people had begun to insist it had never been quite so bad, and within ten it was a story grandmothers told to make children finish their soup.

And Aldric was beloved. That was the joke at the bottom of everything, the trapdoor under the trap. He was *beloved*. Aldric the Green, they called him. Aldric the Restorer. Statues went up. He cut ribbons. He settled border disputes with a word and watched the disputants leave grateful, because the Crown lent his voice a gravity it hadn't earned, a warmth like standing near a lit forge.

The first thing the Crown answered for him was small.

A petition—a widow, a burned mill, a greedy neighbor lord. Aldric had read it and begun composing a careful judgment, the kind that satisfied everyone by disappointing everyone equally, and then he heard himself deliver a ruling instead: crisp, severe, perfect. The widow got her mill rebuilt and her neighbor fined to the last copper. The court murmured approval. Aldric stood smiling on the dais and thought, with a distant, cold curiosity: *I didn't say any of that.*

*I said it better than you would have*, observed the Crown.

"You stole my sentence out of my mouth."

*I finished it. You were going to waste forty minutes being fair. Fairness is what weak men call their fear of choosing.* The Crown's voice was his own voice—that was the part no ballad captured. It sounded like Aldric. It had always sounded like Aldric. How do you argue with a room where every voice is yours?

He tried anyway. He was stubborn about it, in the beginning. He would stay up nights drafting his own edicts in secret and delivering them before the Crown could intervene, and the Crown would let him, patient as a parent letting a child tie their own shoes badly. And then, gradually, the gaps between interventions shortened. Or perhaps his ability to notice the gaps shortened. Memory is a soft ledger; someone else was keeping the books now, and the books balanced beautifully.

The kingdom flourished. The levees held. The rats starved somewhere far from the granaries. Every morning Aldric woke rested—which was strange, because he could not remember sleeping—and every morning the hardest decisions of a realm were already made, neatly, in a hand almost exactly like his own.

"The people love you," his chancellor told him once, glowing with pride, and Aldric had looked into the man's honest face and realized he could not remember loving any of them back. Not specifically. He loved them the way a stove loves a room. It was not nothing. But it was not a king, either.

That night, alone in the great bed, he had tried to recall his mother's face.

He could see the kitchen. The bread. The butter. The rain on the window. But where her face should have been there was a smooth warm blankness, like a thumbprint pressed into wax, and after a moment the blankness resolved into the faces of a hundred portrait painters' guesses, none of them right.

*I had to take something*, said the Crown, gently, from inside his own skull. *Fire does not burn for free.*

"It took my mother."

*It took the memory. She lived and died well; those facts remain. Only the door to them is closed. You have ten thousand other doors.*

"But that one was mine."

There was a silence then, and in the silence Aldric felt—for the only time in all the long amber years—something from the deep end of the bond. Something vast shifting its weight in the dark, weary beyond the reach of language.

*Yes*, said the Crown, and for once it did not sound like him at all. *That one was yours.*

* * *

Now the dream again, the bread again, the ninth year again. He had taken to living here, in the recursion of good days, because the present had grown too quiet. The petitions had thinned. The decisions came pre-made. Some mornings he stood at the high window of the Spire and looked down at the green and golden kingdom—the kingdom he bought with his mother's face—and could not feel the wind on his skin unless he concentrated very hard.

"How long?" he asked, sometimes, of the darkness at the back of the throne room.

*Longer for me*, Vhalorath would answer, and that was all.

He understood it now, finally, in the way one understands things only when it no longer matters: the dragon had not conquered him and the Crown had not conquered the dragon. They were three prisoners sharing one cell, each holding the keys for the others, each too weakened to turn the lock. The fire kept the land alive and the land's faith kept the fire fed and the feeding hollowed the king, and around and around, a wheel of mercy grinding everything fine.

He had wanted to save them all. He had. Whatever else the histories said or didn't say, let one line stand: *he wanted to save them all.*

Lately—this week, this decade, time moved like syrup down here—the dreams had changed. New fragments drifted through the amber, and he couldn't tell whether they were memories fraying loose or something arriving. A girl. He saw a girl. Unremarkable in every particular except that the world declined to notice her, a smudge of a person walking roads that bent politely out of her way, and on her open palm a mark that glowed like a coal breathed upon.

The map. The blood-inked map, climbing.

She was coming toward the Spire, and with her came a feeling Aldric had nearly forgotten the name for, so long since he'd felt anything that wasn't administered to him.

Hope. Terrible, selfish hope.

"No," he whispered, into the dreaming kitchen, over the phantom bread. His hands were nine years old and shaking. "Don't come here. Whatever it promised you—"

But even as he said it, he felt the Crown attend to her, felt the vast attention swivel southward through stone and root and vein, and he understood that his hope and his horror were the same thing now, fused at the base like king and beast and crown.

*Someone is coming*, said the Crown, and there was hunger in it, and fear.

Down in the world, on a road that no longer existed, a girl pulled her coat tighter against the wind and climbed.
