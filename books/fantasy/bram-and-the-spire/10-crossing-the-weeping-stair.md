## 10. Crossing the Weeping Stair

The Weeping Stair did not weep so much as sob, endlessly, in a hundred small waterfalls that spilled from the cliff face like the mountain had been crying for a thousand years and had no intention of stopping.

"It's called the Weeping Stair," Corvane said, "because of the water."

"I'd guessed," August said. "The water gave it away."

"Most people assume it's named for grief."

"The water *is* grief, Corvane. Look at it. It's having a very long, very public cry all over this cliff face, and we're about to climb through it."

He considered this with the grave attention he gave everything, which was one of the things she was—grudgingly—starting to like about him. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with a beard he clearly trimmed by feel and a voice like a landslide that had learned manners. His hands were scarred to the wrist, old burns layered over older burns, and when he prayed—which was often, quietly, under his breath—he pressed his thumb into the worst of them like a man touching an old wound to make sure it still hurt.

It occurred to her, not for the first time, that she didn't actually know what his scars were from. She hadn't asked. Asking questions meant answers, and answers meant someone knew things about you, and knowing things about you was how people sold you. Brenna had taught her that lesson so thoroughly it lived in her spine.

Well. Brenna had also sold her. So perhaps the lessons needed revising.

They stood at the base of the Stair as the morning went grey and then went worse than grey. Above them the cliff rose sheer for what the map insisted was four hundred feet, seamed with ledges barely wide enough for a boot, all of them streaming. The map on her palm pulsed faintly whenever she looked at the route, warm as a sleeping animal, and she had learned to read its moods: contentment when she faced the right direction, a low ache when she strayed, and now a kind of eager insistence, like a dog at a door.

"You want me to go up there," she told it.

The map pulsed.

"You're a terrible map. You know that? A good map would say, here's a lovely bridge, here's a nice flat road. You say, climb the crying cliff in November."

"The Stair is the only way past the Sorrowmere," Corvane said. "Below it, the passes close for winter. Above it—" he squinted up, "—above it is the high shelf road, and beyond that, the town of Vell."

"Vell." She turned the name over. It sat strangely in her mouth, like a coin she couldn't remember receiving. "Do I know Vell?"

"You'll know it when you see it." Something in his tone made her look at him sharply, but he was already checking knots, and Corvane checked knots the way other men said prayers: thoroughly, and with visible love.

The storm came in while they were still arguing about rope order, because of course it did. The Thornfell weather had a sense of comic timing that August deeply resented. One moment the sky was merely oppressive; the next, the wind arrived sideways with a cargo of ice in it, and the Weeping Stair began to freeze even as it fell.

"We go now or not at all," Corvane shouted over it. "If the ledges ice fully, nothing climbs them. Not us, not goats, not the map's opinion."

"And if we wait?"

"There's no shelter worth the name within two days' walk, and the Hollow Men are two days behind us. I counted their fires last night." He held up fingers. "Seven."

August looked up at the cliff. Four hundred feet of wet stone, in a storm, carrying a wounded man's pack and a map that ate memories.

"Right," she said. "But if I die, I'm haunting you specifically."

"That seems fair," Corvane said gravely, and handed her the rope.

* * *

The first hundred feet were almost pleasant, in the way that being terrified in a controlled manner can be almost pleasant. The ledges were narrow but honest. The water was cold but predictable. August climbed the way she did everything—small movements, no wasted effort, thinking three holds ahead—and behind and below her she could hear Corvane breathing in the slow rhythm of the Ash Choir's climbing prayers, each exhale a word she half-recognized from the rites at the hot springs.

*Ash to ash. Stone to stone. What the fire gave, the fire keeps.*

At two hundred feet the storm decided they were having too easy a time of it.

The wind came around the shoulder of the mountain in a single enormous shove, and August flattened herself against the rock and felt the whole cliff shudder—or felt herself shudder against the cliff; there was no telling the difference anymore. Water sheeted across the ledge. Her fingers went from cold to pain to that dangerous distant numbness where hands stop reporting in.

"How are you doing?" she called down.

There was a pause. Then Corvane's voice, serene as a man discussing bread: "I have been better. But I have also been considerably worse, so on balance—"

The ledge under him let go.

She heard it before she understood it—the crack, deep and structural, the sound of a mountain deciding something. Then the sound of Corvane's boots finding nothing at all.

August spun on her anchors in time to see him drop. Not slip—drop, clean and total, the rope snapping taut between them with a crack like a whip, and then the rope *parted*, frayed through on some hidden edge, and the last coil whipped away over her shoulder into the void, and Corvane of the Ash Choir fell backward off the Weeping Stair into four hundred feet of screaming air.

She screamed his name. She would deny this later, but she screamed it, raw and stupid, and threw herself flat along the ledge reaching down with one hand into nothing, into rain and dark and empty space where a man had been.

Nothing. Just the storm, and the drop, and the absurd indifferent weeping of the mountain.

"No," she said. "No, no—you great useless wonderful—"

And then, from somewhere below her, out of the white roar:

"*Mind the loose rock.*"

August stopped breathing entirely.

She leaned out over the edge, heart slamming, and peered down through the sheets of rain. Far below—impossibly far, impossibly *low*, at the base of the cliff where the talus field began—a shape was standing up. Slowly. Shaking itself, the way a man shakes off rain.

The way a *dog* shakes off rain.

"Corvane?" Her voice cracked on the second syllable.

"Go on ahead!" he bellowed cheerfully. "I'll come round by the base! There's a scree slope! Take your time!"

"You *fell*!"

"I did!"

"Four hundred feet!"

"A bit more, I think. The last part was quite fast." He cupped his hands around his mouth. "Don't stop! The ledge above you is the bad one! Left hand inside, always!"

She stared down at him for a long moment, rain running into her open mouth, every smuggler's instinct she owned telling her that dead men did not give climbing advice.

Then she climbed.

* * *

She found him at the top of the scree slope an hour later, sitting on a boulder, wringing out his beard, looking mildly embarrassed, like a priest caught dancing.

August stood over him, dripping, shaking—not from cold, though the cold was considerable, but from the specific fury of a person who has grieved unnecessarily.

"Explain," she said.

"I'd rather not."

"Corvane."

He sighed. It was the sigh of a man who had been dreading this conversation for weeks and had hoped, foolishly, that physics would spare him. He held out his hands, palms up, and pushed back his sleeves.

August had seen his scars before—at the springs, in firelight, glimpsed and politely ignored. Now she looked properly. They weren't burn scars. Or—they were, but they were *arranged*. They ran in patterns across his forearms and up past his elbows, fine lines like frost ferns, like branching lightning frozen mid-strike, and in the grey storm light they had a faint luster to them, a shimmer just beneath the skin, the way the map's ink shimmered on her palm.

"They're old," he said. "Older than me. The Ash Choir used to say the blessing skips generations, lands where it likes. My grandmother carried it. I was born marked." He flexed his fingers, and the lines brightened briefly, ember-orange, then dimmed. "Fire remembers its own. When I fell, it—it caught me. Slowed me. Broke my fall the way a current breaks a swimmer's."

"Four hundred feet."

"Fire doesn't measure in feet."

"Corvane." She crouched in front of him, and her voice came out smaller than she wanted it to. "You fell off a cliff. I watched you fall off a cliff. I *screamed*." She looked away, jaw tight. "You don't get to do that to a person."

"I know," he said quietly. "I'm sorry. Truly. If I'd known the ledge would fail—if I'd known what would happen—I'd have warned you. As it was, I was rather busy being surprised myself." He paused. "You screamed my name."

"Don't."

"I'm not teasing. I'm—" He searched for the word with the care he gave everything. "*Touched.* People don't usually mind when I fall. In the Choir, they mostly minded the noise."

"Well." August scrubbed rain out of her eyes and stood, because standing was easier than whatever her face was doing. "People are idiots. Come on. The shelf road's above us and I want walls around me before dark."

He gathered his pack, wincing—dragon-fire or no, something in him had been bruised—and followed her up onto the high road, and neither of them spoke for a while, which was its own kind of company.

* * *

The map took its payment at dusk.

They had made camp in the lee of a fallen watchtower, a roofless ring of stones that some forgotten garrison had once thought important enough to defend. August got a fire going—small, disciplined, the kind of fire that apologized for existing—and Corvane doctored his bruises with a paste that smelled like pine tar and regret. The storm had moved off eastward, taking its tantrum with it, and the clouds tore open just enough to show a few hard stars.

She was eating hardtack and thinking, distantly, comfortably, about her mother.

That was the strange thing. She hadn't thought about her mother in years—not deliberately, never deliberately—but tonight the memory came unbidden and warm: a kitchen, or something like a kitchen, a wagon's cramped interior maybe, and a voice humming. Low. Off-key in a particular way, always sliding under the note and climbing back up, and August had loved that wrongness, had thought it was the most beautiful—

The map woke.

She felt it the way you feel a hand close on your wrist in the dark. The warmth in her palm sharpened, focused, became intent. And the memory—that kitchen-voice, that beautiful off-key humming—began, gently, patiently, to be taken.

"No," August said aloud.

Corvane looked up. "What?"

"It's—" She gripped her own wrist, as if that would help, as if you could hold a memory in place by holding flesh. "No. Not that one. Not—not her voice, take anything else, take the hardtack, take my knees, take—"

The map did not argue. That was the horror of it. It didn't wrestle or threaten. It simply continued, calm as erosion, and the humming thinned like a thread pulled through cloth, and August scrambled for it, grasping at details—*the words, what were the words of the song, what was the song, what was her name, Mama, what was your name*—and found her hands closing on smoke.

Then it was done. The warmth in her palm settled back into its usual low pulse, satisfied, and the evening was exactly as it had been: fire, stars, pine-tar smell, Corvane watching her with wide dark eyes.

Except there was a hole where the humming had been. A hole shaped like a voice. She knew a mother had existed. She knew there had been a song. She knew she had loved it. The knowledge remained, precise and cruel, like the outline left by a thing removed.

"She sang," August said. Her voice sounded far away. "Off-key. Under the note, always. I remember that I remember that. But I can't—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eye. "I can't hear it anymore. I can't hear *her*."

Corvane set down the poultice. He crossed the small circle of firelight and sat beside her, not touching, just near, a wall of warm scarred presence.

"How many?" he asked softly.

"The village name. On the first road. And now—" She laughed, and it came out wrong. "That's two. Two payments. Do you know what frightens me, Corvane? It's not that it hurts. It hardly hurts at all. That's the frightening part. First time, I cried all night. This time I ate hardtack through it." She stared into the fire. "I'm getting *good* at it. Losing things. Like it's a skill. Like it's smuggling—first you move one small thing and feel sick, and a year later you can move a body and sleep fine after."

Corvane was quiet a long moment. The firelight moved in the branching scars on his arms, and for once they seemed less like blessing and more like a warning written in a language only he could read.

"In the Choir," he said at last, "we had a saying. *The fire asks a price of everyone it warms.* We thought it was about devotion. Sacrifice. Giving things up for glory." He shook his head slowly. "It isn't. It's arithmetic. Everything costs. The question is only whether you choose the price or the price chooses you." He looked at her, and his eyes were very steady. "You chose. Remember that, when you count what's gone. You chose, and you chose to save a king you've never met and a dragon that would eat you without chewing. That's not becoming hollow, August. Hollow is when it's taken and you never cared."

"But I *do* care. That's the problem. I cared and I paid anyway, and tomorrow I'll pay again, and the day after, and one day I'll pay something and not even notice the hole." She hugged her knees. "How will I know when I've lost too much? How does anyone know?"

"You won't," Corvane said honestly. "So we keep count for each other. I'll remember what you lose. You remember what I've given. Between us—" the ghost of a smile moved through his beard, "—we might assemble one entire person."

August snorted despite herself. "Between us we'd have half a person and a lot of opinions about rope."

"Half a person with excellent opinions about rope."

She laughed—wetly, but she laughed—and after a while she slept, and the map on her palm pulsed gently all night like something digesting.

* * *

Dawn came thin and gold. They broke camp and walked the shelf road east, and by midmorning August saw the smoke first—thin threads of it rising from a valley fold ahead—and then the roofs, grey and crooked and clustered like sheep.

"Vell," Corvane said.

"Yes," August said. And frowned, because the name still sat wrong in her mouth, a coin she couldn't place, and because something else was wrong too—something about the smoke. She had grown up reading smoke the way other children read letters, and this smoke was wrong. Too thin. Too scattered. Chimney-smoke from houses with no fires lit, or fires lit and abandoned.

And the fields, as they drew closer, were full of people. Standing in the barley stubble. Standing in the lanes. Standing at their doors.

Not working. Not talking. Just standing, faces tilted toward the pale sun, smiling.

August stopped at the head of the lane. Beside her, Corvane's breath went out slow, and his thumb found the oldest scar on his wrist and pressed hard.

"August," he said quietly. "I think we should find whoever's still in there who remembers their own name."

"Assuming anyone does," she said, and walked down into the town that smiled.
