## 11. The Town That Forgot Itself

The map had stopped being a map three days ago.

It had become something more like a conversation—one-sided, mostly, with August doing the listening and the ink doing the talking in that slow, patient way it had, like a grandmother telling a story she already knew you'd misremember. *Left at the split birch,* it said. There was no split birch. There was a split oak, or there had been, the last time August had walked this valley, which was six years ago, which might as well have been never, because the map kept eating her past one bite at a time.

She'd lost her mother's voice on the Weeping Stair. She'd lost her home village's name before that. She'd started keeping a list of what remained, scratched into the back of her glove in smuggler's shorthand, because if the ink was going to rob her she intended to at least keep an inventory of the theft.

*What I still have:* Corvane, snoring by the fire like a man twice his size and half his dignity. The smell of pine resin. The word for the feeling when you're warm and someone you trust is nearby—she didn't have a word for that one, actually, but she had the feeling, and she was counting it.

*What I don't:* everything before age nine, mostly. Her mother's voice. Her village's name. Whether she'd ever had a favorite color, and if so what it had been.

"You're making the face again," Corvane said without opening his eyes.

"What face?"

"The one where you look like a woman doing arithmetic she doesn't want to finish."

"I'm always doing arithmetic I don't want to finish."

"Yes," he said, "but usually you do it quietly." He sat up, joints crackling like green wood in a fire, and nodded down the slope ahead of them. "Smoke. Thin. Cooking smoke, not burning smoke. A town?"

August shaded her eyes. Below them, tucked into the crook of the river where the Thornfell foothills gentled into farmland, lay a cluster of rooftops. Chimney smoke rose from perhaps a third of them. Laundry hung on lines. A dog barked somewhere, unhurried, the bark of a dog that had never been afraid of anything in its life.

"Millbrook," she said automatically, and then frowned. "No. Millbrook is two valleys east. This is—" She reached for the name and found her fingers closing on nothing, the way they closed on her mother's voice now, on the village name, on the shape of her own front door. "I knew this town," she said slowly. "I know I knew it. I've slept in its haylofts. Brenna used to fence goods through here through a cooper who watered his barrels and thought nobody noticed."

"Then what's it called?"

"That's just it. I can't—" She pressed her palm flat against her thigh, against the ghost-heat of the brand. "It's not gone the way the others were gone. The others felt *taken*. This feels like it was never there."

Corvane was quiet for a moment. When he spoke, his voice had the careful flatness he used when he was about to say something he'd rather not say. "How many chimneys are smoking?"

"Maybe twenty. Of sixty, seventy roofs."

"And how many people does a town that size have?"

"Two hundred. Three hundred." August looked again. Looked harder. The laundry lines were full. The fields beyond the town were—weeds. Not fallow, not resting, but weeds, waist-high and seeding, the kind of neglect that took years. The mill wheel by the river turned, but slowly, and nothing ground inside it. "Corvane."

"I see it."

"Nobody's harvesting. Nobody's *doing* anything. But there's cooking smoke."

"There is," Corvane said. He began packing up their camp with the methodical movements of a man who had learned to always be ready to leave. "And there is a brand on your hand that Hollow Men can feel across three valleys. And we need food, and rope, and a night under a roof before the high passes. So we are going to walk into that town, and you are going to let me do most of the talking, and if anything feels wrong—"

"Everything already feels wrong."

"—then *more* wrong," he said patiently. "We leave. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

They went down together into the town that had no name—or rather, into the town whose name lived only in the hole where August's memory used to be, which was somehow worse.

* * *

The first thing August noticed was the smiling.

A woman stood in her doorway watching them come down the road, and she was smiling—not the wary smile strangers give strangers on mountain roads, not the calculating smile of a townsfolk assessing travelers for coin or trouble, but a soft, settled, vacant smile, like a cat in a sunbeam. She smiled at August. She smiled at Corvane. She smiled at the mule cart trundling past behind her, driven by a man who was also smiling, at nothing, at the road, at the middle distance.

"Afternoon," August tried.

"Afternoon," the woman agreed warmly, and said nothing else, and kept smiling, and went back inside.

"Well," said Corvane.

The street was busy in the way an anthill is busy after you've kicked it—movement everywhere, purpose nowhere. People drifted. A man stood in his garden holding a watering can over flowers that had been dead since spring, smiling, pouring. Two children played a game with no rules, tossing a ball back and forth and laughing at intervals that had nothing to do with the ball. An old man sat on a bench outside the tavern, and August would have sworn on any god she could remember that he had been sitting on that bench in exactly that position when she'd passed through six years ago, except that then he'd been scowling over a ledger and complaining about tariffs, and now he was smiling at his own hands.

"Hello," August said to him, crouching to his eye level. "Old man. What's this town called?"

He looked at her. For a heartbeat—just a heartbeat—something moved behind his eyes, something that scrabbled at the walls of itself like a mouse in a jar. His smile flickered.

"It's called..." he said. And then the smile came back, smooth as water closing over a stone. "It's a lovely day, isn't it?"

"It's called what?" August pressed, gentler. "You know it. You've known it your whole life. You've cursed it every winter and praised it every harvest. What is it called?"

The old man's hands trembled slightly in his lap. "Lovely day," he repeated, with the serene certainty of a man reciting scripture, and August understood, with a cold that started in her brand and spread outward, that to him it genuinely was. That there was no other sentence left in him worth the trouble of thinking.

She stood up. Her hands were shaking. "They read it," she whispered to Corvane. "The map—the king's map, whatever the Crown uses—they read it too long. These are what the Hollow Men are *before*, aren't they? Before they hollow all the way through."

"Worse than before," Corvane murmured. His eyes swept the street, cataloguing, and August saw him arrive at the same arithmetic she had: no guards, no dogs on chains, no shutters barred, no fear anywhere—and yet every instinct she owned was screaming. "Before, they forget themselves. After, they serve. These people are between. Waiting to be collected." He touched her elbow, light as a moth. "August. We take what we need and we go. There is nothing here to save."

Which was, of course, the exact moment August heard the crying.

* * *

It came from behind the tavern—a thin, strangled, deliberately quiet crying, the crying of someone who has learned that being heard is dangerous. August had made that exact sound herself, once, in a smuggling crate in Brenna's hold, aged seven and certain the customs men had come. You never forget how to cry quietly. It stays in the body like a scar.

She followed it around the corner and found a gap between the tavern wall and a stack of barrels, and in the gap, folded up small as a comma, a girl.

Eight years old, maybe nine. Dark hair chopped short with a knife. A dress two sizes too big, belted with rope. She wasn't smiling. In the whole smiling town, her face was wet and furious and *awake*, and when she saw August she scrambled backward until the barrels stopped her, and picked up a broken stave, and pointed it with both hands.

"Don't," she said. "Don't touch me. Don't smile at me. Everyone smiles. If you smile I'll hit you."

"I promise not to smile," August said. She crouched, slow, the way you'd approach a wounded animal or a tripwire. "Look. No smile. See? My face doesn't even know how."

The girl's eyes narrowed. They were sharp eyes, red-rimmed, and they went from August's face to her hand—to the brand—and widened.

"You've got the mark," the girl breathed. "The hot mark. Mam said—I told her, I said the mark would come, I dreamed it, I dreamed a woman with a burned hand walking out of the mountains, and she laughed, and then—" The fury crumbled all at once, like a bank into a flood. "Then the tall men came. With no faces. Just smooth, like eggs. And they talked to everyone, and everyone listened, and afterward everyone was *happy*, and nobody remembered my name. Nobody remembers *anything*. My mam looked at me and smiled and asked me if I was lost." Her voice cracked clean in half. "My own mam asked me if I was *lost*."

August's chest did something complicated and painful. "What's your name?" she asked quietly. "Your real one. Tell me fast, tell me all of it, so it's said out loud today."

"Bramble," the girl said. "Bramble Fenwick. Like the brambles, Mam said, because I scratch. I'm nine. I was born under the eave during the lambing storm and Da said I came into the world biting. I live—lived—at the croft past the mill, the blue door, the door's blue because I spilled paint on it when I was four and Da said well, now it's decided." She said it all in one breath, the way you'd pour water on a fire, hoarding every drop of herself. Then, smaller: "Is that enough? Is saying it enough to keep it?"

"No," August said honestly, because the girl deserved honesty more than comfort. "But it's a start. Saying things out loud matters. It's why they take voices first." She held out her hand, palm down, hiding the brand. "I'm August. I'm going to get you out of here."

"They'll see us."

"They won't. Watch." August pulled a strip of smoked pork from her pack, and a scrap of mirror-polished tin, and did the oldest trick in the smuggler's book: she made the distraction boring. A dropped bundle here, a muttered argument with Corvane about tolls there, a flash of tin catching sun across the square so that every drifting gaze slid toward the sparkle and away from the small shadow moving along the barrel line. It wasn't invisibility magic. It was just the craft of being unremarkable, which August had practiced longer than she'd practiced anything, and which the map had not yet taken from her because the map, apparently, needed it.

They reached the edge of town without incident. Behind them, the mill wheel turned its empty turn. Somewhere a woman laughed at nothing.

At the last house before the fields, Bramble stopped dead. Through the open window, a woman was kneading bread—kneading and kneading and kneading, long past any dough becoming anything, smiling at the wall. Bramble made a sound like something tearing.

"Mam," she whispered.

August knelt. "Listen to me. That isn't your mam right now. Your mam is the one who named you for brambles and let you ruin a door. That woman in there is a coat your mam got put into. And coats can be taken off—I've seen it. I've seen worse than this undone." She hadn't, quite, but she was going to, and hope was a tool like any other, and you could use it true. "So we keep your name safe, and mine, and Corvane's, and we come back. Swear it with me. Say 'we come back.'"

"We come back," Bramble whispered.

"Louder. Names get shy."

"*We come back.*"

* * *

There was a Wayfarers' waystation two miles north, in the pines—an old guild shelter, stone-walled, iron-doored, built back when the Guild had mattered and maintained by nobody since. August knew it the way she'd once known every bolt-hole in the Thornfell: by heart, by habit, by the particular ache of a place that had fed you when you were cold. Or she had known it. Now she knew it the way you know a story someone else told you—but her feet knew it perfectly, and that was enough. Whatever else the map had eaten, it had left her legs intact.

She got the iron door open with a trick involving a hairpin and blasphemy, and lit no fire, and showed Bramble the cellar hatch, and the girl went down it without a word of complaint, which told August more about the girl's week than any amount of talking could.

Corvane stood watch at the window, and for a while neither of them spoke. Then:

"You lied to her," he said. Not unkindly.

"About coming back?"

"About the coat coming off. You don't know that it comes off."

"No," August admitted. "But I've decided it does. There's a difference between lying and deciding, Corvane. Lying is when you don't intend to pay for the words."

He huffed—almost a laugh. Down in the cellar, faintly, Bramble was whispering. Reciting. *Bramble Fenwick, nine, born under the eave, the blue door.* Over and over, a rosary of one.

"She'll keep," August said. "Kids who learn to hide that young, they keep. I kept."

"You did." Corvane glanced at her, and there was something in his face she couldn't read—pity, maybe, or its cousin. "August. Those people in the town. The tall ones will come back for them. Soon. A town that size, mid-harvest, ripe for collecting—you felt it too. And when they come, they'll feel your brand from the road. Every hour we stay, we risk her."

"I know."

"And yet you're building a fire."

August looked down. She was, indeed, building a fire—small, screened, in the old hearth, because the girl was nine and shivering and had spent a week hiding from her own mother's smile, and some things outweighed caution. "I know that too," she said.

She fed the flame a stick. Outside, the pines moved, and somewhere far off—too far to be sure, too near to ignore—a horn sounded, low and wrong, the sound of something with no throat calling to something with no ears.

Bramble's whispering stopped.

"That's them, isn't it," the girl said from the hatch, very calmly, the way children are calm when they've already spent all their fear. "The egg-faced men."

August looked at Corvane. Corvane looked at the window, where the evening was going purple, and at the road, where the horn had sounded, and then at August with an expression that said, clearly, *this is the part where you decide who we are.*

August rolled her sleeves.

"Yeah," she said. "That's them. Bramble—come up here. I need someone who knows the back of that town better than I do, and I need her tonight."
