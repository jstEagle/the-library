## 12. Brenna's Bargain

There were three things Brenna Oakes had learned in forty years of smuggling: never drink with a man who apologizes, never trust a door that locks from the outside, and never, ever climb a mountain for free.

She was breaking the third one now, and hating every step of it.

The shrine sat in a fold of the mountain like an old coin dropped in a purse—small, worn smooth, and worth more than it looked. Her knees had opinions about the climb. Her knees had been voicing their opinions since the second switchback, loudly, in the language of aching joints, and Brenna had told them the same thing she told all her crew when they complained: *nobody made you come.*

Well. Nobody had made her come except herself, which was the worst kind of somebody.

She settled onto the shrine's low stone wall, unwrapped the bundle in her lap—bread, hard cheese, a flask of plum brandy that could strip varnish—and waited. The morning fog moved through the pines below in slow grey rivers. Somewhere up the trail, a marmot whistled the alarm that meant a traveler was coming, and Brenna allowed herself a small, grim smile.

*About time, girl. I've been sitting on this cold stone long enough to write my will.*

* * *

She had known August Iversen since the child was nine years old and had picked Brenna's lock with a hairpin, mostly to prove she could, and had then stood in the doorway of Brenna's cabin holding the broken pin like a confession. Brenna had laughed until she cried, fed her stew, and never once asked where she'd come from. That was the arrangement. The guild took in strays the way a riverbank takes in driftwood—without questions, because questions sank you.

But she'd known, in the way you know a knife is sharp even when it's sheathed, that August was different. The girl could walk into a room and be forgotten before she finished crossing it. She could find roads that weren't on any map, trails that unmade themselves behind her, passes through the Thornfell that had swallowed better guides than her whole crew put together. Brenna had told herself it was talent. She had told herself that story for twenty years, the way you keep a coin you know is clipped, because it spends anyway.

Then the courier had died at the ford with a map in dragon's blood, and the king's Hollow Men had started burning everything August had ever touched, and Brenna's own people—the family she'd built out of orphans and thieves—had looked at the bounty and done the arithmetic.

Brenna had done the arithmetic too. That was the shame of it. She'd stood in her counting room with the ledger open and the bounty notice pinned above it, and she had thought, *enough coin to buy the whole river trade for a generation.* She'd thought it for the length of one long breath. Then she'd torn the notice down and burned it in the candle, and gone to find the girl herself, before anyone else could.

Not to capture her. To warn her. Though Brenna suspected, with the flat honesty she applied to herself the way she applied it to ledgers, that the warning was also a kind of payment—a settling of a debt she'd been carrying since the day she'd let a nine-year-old pick her lock and never asked why the girl already knew how.

* * *

August came up the trail like fog coming uphill—quiet, grey-cloaked, half-gone before your eye settled on her. Twenty years and it still made the hairs on Brenna's neck stand up, that talent of hers. The girl could have walked into a wedding and left with the bride's purse and the groom's regret, and nobody would have been able to swear she'd been there.

"Sit down before you fall down," Brenna said. "And before you say anything clever, I brought breakfast, and I'll feed it to the marmots if you're rude."

August stopped. Even at twenty paces, Brenna could see the change in her—the girl had left as a sharp-eyed waif and had come back as something with a soldier's stillness and a brand wrapped in bandages on her right hand. The Hollow Men's fires had burned a lot of things out of her. Brenna hated them for it with a clean, simple hatred, the kind that didn't need feeding.

"You're supposed to be selling me," August said. Her voice was flat as a frozen pond. "Half the river trade is looking for me. You climbed a mountain to do it in person?"

"I climbed a mountain because my crew can't be trusted and my knees are stupid, and one of those is the reason I'm here." Brenna patted the wall beside her. "Sit. Eat. Then I'm going to ruin your life, and you'll want a full stomach for it."

August hesitated the way she always had—like a cat deciding whether the offered hand held food or a trap. Then she sat, because she was smart enough to know Brenna didn't climb mountains for traps, and hungry enough that the decision made itself.

They ate in silence for a while. The fog lifted off the pines. Somewhere below, a woodpecker hammered away at a dead tree with the persistence of a tax collector.

"You look terrible," Brenna said, with affection. "Like something a cat dragged up a glacier."

"You should see the other side of me."

"Oh, I believe it. I've seen the reports." Brenna poured two cups of brandy, and August's eyebrows went up, because Brenna did not share the varnish-stripper with just anyone. "You burned the warehouse at Grayharbor. You cut Marta's line while she was holding it."

"Marta sold me."

"Marta sold you for less than I'd have sold you for, which is its own insult." Brenna said it lightly, and watched the girl flinch anyway. Good. Better it hurt now, up here, where the only thing to fall off of was a shrine wall. "I'm not here to apologize for Marta. I'm here to apologize for me. There's a difference, and the difference is I'm going to tell you something true, and Marta would've just taken the coin."

August set down her cup. "Say it, then."

Brenna looked out at the fog for a long moment, gathering the truth like a net full of eels—slippery, and bound to bite her.

* * *

"The guilds made a pact with the Crown," she said. "Before you were born. Before I was born. Before my mother was born, and she was old enough to remember when the Thornfell tolls were paid in eggs."

August said nothing. She had gone very still, the stillness of a person standing on ice and listening for the crack.

"Every guild. The Wayfarers, the River Cartels, the Salt Roads, us. The Crown doesn't rule the mountains, girl—it *leases* them. And in exchange for our licenses, our safe roads, our little kingdoms of gray trade, we owe the Crown one thing." Brenna turned and looked at her straight on, because this part deserved eyes. "It owes the Crown a Wayfinder. Every generation, the Wayfarers' Guild breeds one. Finds one. Makes one. A child who can walk roads that don't exist. And when the Crown calls that child, the guild delivers. That's the toll. That's always been the toll."

"That's not—" August started, and stopped. Brenna watched her reach for the argument and find nothing there to hold. "I was *found*. I was a stray. You took me in off the—"

"Off the north road, in the autumn of the year the Wayfarers lost their last finder to a fever." Brenna said it gently. "You were nine. You were already picking locks. You already knew the Thornfell trails better than men who'd walked them thirty years. You'd never been south of the mountains in your life, August, and you knew where the fords froze first." She let that sit. The woodpecker hammered. "You were never a stray, love. You were a *delivery*. And I signed for the parcel."

The silence after that was the kind of silence that has weight. August's hands were flat on the stone wall, and Brenna saw the bandaged one trembling, just slightly, like a plucked string.

"I remember the roads," August said at last. Her voice had gone quiet in the wrong way, the way of a person reading their own eulogy. "I remember them because I was *made* to. Every trail I ever loved, every shortcut I was proud of—it was all—" She laughed, one short, ugly note. "It was all *homework*."

"I don't know if it was all of it," Brenna said. "I honestly don't. That's the cruelty of the thing. Maybe the guild found a girl with a gift and sharpened it. Maybe they built her from scratch. I've read the old contracts and they don't say, because contracts that say things get burned." She took a drink of the brandy to keep her own hands honest. "What I know is what I did. I fed you, I taught you, I loved you the way you love a thing you're going to have to hand over someday. And I told myself that made it different. Twenty years I told myself that."

"And now?"

"Now the Crown's men burned the guild hall, and the pact is eating its own, and the delivery is running instead of arriving, and every guildmaster from here to the coast is soiling himself." Brenna shrugged. "The bargain's broken, girl. Which means the only question left is whether you were ever anything more than the bargain. And I climbed this mountain because I wanted you to hear it from me, from someone who—" her voice caught, just once, and she bulldozed through it, "—from someone who loved you *and* used you, and can't always tell you which was which."

August was crying. She didn't sob; she'd never been a sobber. The tears just came down her face the way meltwater comes down a cliff—silent, inevitable, cutting channels. Brenna did not reach for her. Some things you had to let a person carry down their own face.

"I lost my village's name," August said finally, wiping her cheek with the back of her wrist, businesslike even in grief. "The map took it. I lost my mother's voice. And now you're telling me I might have lost things that were never mine to begin with. That the roads I gave up might have been *installed*." She laughed again, and this time it wobbled worse. "What am I, Brenna? If the memory's gone and the memory was a lie, what's left?"

Brenna considered the question with the seriousness it deserved, and with the plum brandy's assistance.

"Well," she said, "when you were eleven, you gave your winter coat to a boy whose family had been turned out, and then you walked three days cold and never told a soul, and I only found out because he did. Nobody installed that. When you were fourteen you came back from a run two days early because you had a *feeling* old Tammo's boat would founder, and it did, and you were on the jetty when it came in. Nobody installed that either, unless they installed stubbornness, in which case they did it to all of us." She nudged August's shoulder with her own, an old gesture, worn smooth as the shrine stones. "The guild built you a road, love. They didn't build you. You've been walking off their map since the day you picked my lock. That's the truth I climbed up here to give you, and it cost me a fortune in knees, so I hope you're grateful."

* * *

August was quiet a long time. The fog burned off the pines below, and the sun came through and made the whole mountainside gold, and Brenna thought—not for the first time—that the world wasted its best light on moments when nobody was in a state to enjoy it.

"The Hollow Men," August said. "They were people who read the map too long. Corvane told me. They read until there wasn't anything left but the reading." She flexed her bandaged hand. "The map's been eating me since the ford. And now I find out the eating might have started before the ford. That I might have been *raised* as a meal."

"That's one way to see it."

"What's the other way?"

"The other way," Brenna said, "is that a meal doesn't get to choose the table. And you're still choosing. You're up here with a brand on your hand and a dragon-priest at your back and half the kingdom's coin on your head, and you could still turn south. Nobody would blame you. I'd even help you pack." She held up a finger before August could speak. "But you won't. And I know you won't not because of the guild, and not because of the map, but because you gave a boy your coat when you were eleven and I've watched you do versions of it ever since. Whatever they built in you, girl, they built it on ground that was already *yours*."

August looked at her for a long moment. Then she did something that undid Brenna more than any tears could have: she leaned over, stiff and awkward, and put her head briefly against Brenna's shoulder, the way she hadn't since she was small enough that it didn't cost her anything.

"Thank you," she said into the wool of Brenna's coat. "For not selling me. For climbing."

"Don't thank me yet," Brenna said, because her voice was doing something embarrassing and she needed a moment. "There's more, and it's worse, and it's the actual reason I'm here."

August pulled back, wary again. "Worse than *I was bred for this*?"

"Marginally." Brenna reached into her coat and drew out a folded paper, soft with handling, and held it out. "The guildmasters didn't just make a pact. They made a *schedule*. The Crown's been waiting for a finder for sixty years—the last one died before the Crown could call her. Your masters weren't raising you for the road, August. They were raising you for a *date*. And according to this—" she tapped the paper, "—the date was this winter. The Crown expected you at the Spire by the first hard frost. You're ahead of schedule. And the thing in the Spire has been *waiting* for you, girl. It's known you were coming since before you knew yourself."

August took the paper. Brenna watched her read it, watched the color leave her face in stages, like a tide going out.

"That's not possible," August said. "The courier at the ford—he was dying, he chose me, he—"

"Or he was the last honest link in a chain sixty years long, and he chose the only person the chain would hold." Brenna stood, knees cracking like green firewood, and looked down at the girl who had been her stray, her parcel, her pride, and her sin. "I'm sorry. I'd have carried this lie to my grave if the grave weren't so crowded with everyone else's."

Below them, far down the mountain, a sound rolled up through the pines. Not thunder. Thunder didn't come in a line. This came in a line—a long, low, wrong hum, like a struck bell the size of a valley, and the marmots went silent all at once, and the birds went up off the trees in one dark sheet.

August was on her feet, hand pressed flat to her bandage, and Brenna saw the brand's heat glowing faintly through the cloth like a coal under ash.

"They're not following you anymore," Brenna said quietly, as the hum rolled closer. "Are they. They don't need to follow you. They're just—coming to the same place. Because it's time."

August stared down the trail, at the grey line of it vanishing into the trees, and Brenna watched her make the choice—the real one, the one that had nothing to do with guilds or pacts or maps.

"No," August said. "It's not time. Not on their schedule." She turned, and her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely steady. "Brenna. Which way is the Weeping Stair?"
