## 13. What August Was For

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen watchtower, one of the old border keeps that dotted the Thornfell like broken teeth. August built the fire while Corvane saw to his bandages—she had learned not to offer help with those anymore, and he had learned to stop pretending it didn't hurt.

The shrine at Brenna's mountain was a day behind them now. Brenna herself was a lifetime behind her, or would be, once August finished sorting through what she'd said.

*The guilds signed their pact before your mother was born. Wayfinder stock. Bred careful, raised careful, kept invisible until the road was ready for you.*

August fed sticks to the fire and tried to make them mean nothing.

"You're doing the thing," Corvane observed from across the flames.

"What thing."

"The thing where you go very quiet and arrange small objects with great precision, as though the fate of kingdoms depends on the optimal placement of twigs."

"I'm making a fire."

"You're making an argument," he said. "With yourself. I've watched priests do it before difficult sermons. You always lose, incidentally."

She threw a twig at him. He caught it out of the air without looking up, which was insufferable, and put it on his side of the fire, which was somehow worse—it felt like a truce she hadn't agreed to.

The map pulsed faintly against her palm where it had lived since the ford. She'd stopped thinking of it as inked skin and started thinking of it as skin, full stop, which was its own kind of horror she didn't have room for tonight.

"Ask me," she said finally.

"Ask you what?"

"Whatever you're not asking. You've been circling it since the shrine. You talk more when something's bothering you than when something isn't, which is backwards, and I've been waiting three days for you to get to the point."

Corvane leaned back against the fallen stone and considered her across the firelight. He had a way of looking at people as though reading a difficult passage of scripture—patiently, expecting the meaning to be worth the trouble.

"All right," he said. "Brenna told you that you were bred for this road. What did you hear?"

"I heard that everything I am is a lie someone else wrote."

"Hm." He turned a flat stone over in his fingers. "That's what I expected you to hear. It's the wrong thing, but it's what most people hear."

"There's a *right* thing?"

"There's a true thing." He set the stone down. "But you won't want it yet. So instead I'll ask you this: when you guided caravans through whiteouts that killed men who'd walked those passes thirty years—when you found trails that weren't on any chart, cut by no one alive—did it feel like lying?"

August opened her mouth to say yes, obviously, and discovered the word wouldn't come.

Because it hadn't. That was the terrible thing. The knowing had never felt like a trick or a talent or even a skill. It had felt like *recognition*. Walking a ridge she'd never crossed and feeling her feet settle into old ruts. Knowing that the third switchback below Graywater Pass had washed out two winters back—not guessing, *knowing*, the way you know your own hand is at the end of your arm.

"It felt like remembering," she said quietly.

"Yes."

"But I'd never been there. Any of it. I was born—" She stopped.

Where had she been born? She'd lost the village name miles ago, paid into the map's hungry dark along with her mother's voice. She reached for the memory the way you reach for a stair in darkness and find only floor.

"You were born somewhere the map has already eaten," Corvane said gently. "I know. But think about what you just said. *Remembering.* A talent remembers nothing. A talent is learned, drilled, sharpened—and it tires, and it fails in the cold, and it dies with you. What you have doesn't fail. It doesn't tire. It behaves like a road that was laid down in you before you ever took a step." He looked at her steadily. "That's not training, August. That's design. Someone wanted a person who could carry this map to the Spire and never lose the way, and they spent generations building her."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the pass with a sound like slow breathing.

"They used me," she said.

"They used everyone. They used kings and smugglers and a whole choir of priests, and they used the land itself, and they did it so long ago that the using wore smooth as river stone. You're angry at the right crime but the wrong century."

"Don't tell me what to be angry at." Her voice came out low and dangerous, and she let it. "You didn't spend twenty years being proud of something that turns out to be a leash. You don't know what that's like."

"No," Corvane admitted. "I spent twenty years being proud of something that turned out to be ash. Different leash, same collar-maker." He gestured vaguely upward, toward the peaks, toward whatever old powers had bound dragons to crowns and written covenants in blood-ink. "So we are, at least, fellow prisoners arguing about whose cell is smaller."

* * *

She didn't sleep. Around the coldest hour she got up and stood at the edge of the camp, looking south—the way she'd come, back toward the lowlands, toward roads that still existed whether or not she remembered making them.

She could leave. That was the thought that had been sitting on her chest all night like a cat that knew it wasn't wanted. She could walk south tomorrow and keep walking, and the Hollow Men tracked the map's heat, not her scent—if she could figure out how to put the burning thing down. Except it wouldn't burn, wouldn't be buried, wouldn't be given away; it came back to her hand like a dog that loved her. So: she could leave, and the map would leave with her, and the king would stay crowned with his parasite, and Vhalorath would die slowly on a throne feeding a kingdom that had never once fed it back.

Or she could keep climbing, and arrive at the Spire as exactly what she'd been manufactured to be—a delivery mechanism with legs. Every step forward would be a step someone else had planned before her grandmother's grandmother drew her first breath.

*What August was for.*

The phrase arrived in her head uninvited and settled there, ugly and precise. Not *who*. Not *who August was*. *What August was for.* Like a tool in a kit. Like the little brass instrument her master used to measure star angles—you didn't ask the astrolabe its opinion.

Except—

Her master. Old Wenna, dying in the emptying guild hall, who had begged her to run south. Who had recognized the dragon-blood ink on sight and gone gray around the mouth. Who must have known—mustn't she have? If the guilds signed the pact, if Wayfinders were bred for the errand, then every master of the Guild had known what their apprentices' uncanny gift was *for*. And Wenna had looked at a branded girl in her doorway and said *run.*

Wenna had tried to break the design. In the last hour she had left, she'd spent her breath telling August to be nobody, go nowhere, be free.

And August had stayed because the hall burned down that same night.

The grief hit sideways, the way it always did now—no warning, no build, just sudden weight. She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until the sparks behind her lids stopped arranging themselves into maps.

"You should sleep," Corvane said, from much closer than she'd expected. He'd come to stand beside her without a sound, which for a man his size remained a theological puzzle she'd given up on.

"So should you."

"Oh, I intend to complain about my wounds extensively tomorrow. Tonight I'm conserving material." He followed her gaze south. After a moment he said, "You're measuring the distance."

"To what?"

"Whatever you're measuring. The lowlands. An ordinary life. A road with no map on it." He didn't look at her. "I'm not going to stop you. I want that understood plainly, because I've noticed you brace for lectures the way other people brace for blows. If you turn south, I'll walk you to the tree line myself, and I'll wish you well, and then I'll climb on alone and probably die on the Weeping Stair like an idiot, and none of that will be your debt."

"That's a terrible speech. You're supposed to say something inspiring."

"I'm a defrocked priest, not a bard. My speeches are honest, which is different, and worse." He turned to face her fully then. "But since you've accused me of withholding my point, here it is. Take it or throw it in the fire.

"You believe that being designed for a purpose makes the purpose false. That if someone planned your road, your walking it is obedience, not choice." He held up a scarred finger before she could speak. "Consider: I was ordained at nine years old. Chosen, trained, shaped—every prayer I know was poured into me by men who decided what I was for before I could read. And when I finally broke from them, do you know what I discovered? That the prayers were still mine. The shaping gave them to me, yes, but what I *did* with them—that part was never theirs. They built the vessel. They could not choose what it carried.

"Someone bred your gift, August. Fine. Terrible. Grieve it, curse them, carve their names on a post and shoot arrows at it, I'll hold the post. But the gift itself—your feet on those impossible trails, your eye for the hidden road—that's yours now. Whoever planted it is centuries dead. Every mile you've walked with it, you chose. Every traveler you brought home safe, you chose. They may have built the door. You're the one who decides what walks through it."

The wind gusted, flattening the fire, and for a moment the world was all shadow and snow-glare. When the flame recovered, August found she had her fists clenched and didn't remember clenching them.

"And if I decide to walk away?" she asked. "Is that also mine?"

"Entirely. It might even be the bravest thing available." He smiled, thin and tired. "It's just not the thing you'll do. I've watched you for weeks. You talk like a smuggler and glower like a thief, but underneath you're the worst kind of person to argue with: you actually mean all that nonsense about bringing travelers home safe."

"I hate you."

"Noted. It's in the ledger, under Tuesday."

Despite everything—despite the rage sitting in her chest like swallowed coals—something in her chest loosened, half a degree. It was infuriating. He never comforted her; he simply handed her the truth like a heavy package and waited for her to decide whether to carry it. Somehow that worked better than kindness. Kindness would have felt like another hand shaping her.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of pewter. They struck camp in silence, and August took the first watch of walking, because the trail ahead climbed through country she knew—or had known, before the map ate it—and some stubborn animal part of her wanted to test what remained.

The path bent along a streambed and then, at a place where the water split around a boulder, her body simply went left. No hesitation. Left, up a shelf of rock, through a gap in a deadfall that she could not have seen from ten paces back.

She stopped. Stood there with her boot on the first stone of a route she'd never learned, heart hammering.

Corvane caught up. "Problem?"

"This isn't the main trail."

"No."

"There is no trail. There's barely a goat-path."

"Mm. Does it go where we need?"

She consulted the map in her palm—the heat of it, the pull of it, the sense of direction that lived in her bones rather than her skin. Up. Over the shoulder of the mountain. Toward the Spire.

"Yes," she said. "It goes exactly where we need. Better than the main trail. It avoids the Hollow Men's sightlines entirely. It knows—" She laughed, and it came out cracked. "It knows a hollow under the fourth ridge where three people can shelter from a storm. How does it know that? Who counted?"

"A Wayfinder," Corvane said quietly. "Long ago. One of yours. Someone walked these mountains until the walking sank into the family bone, so that a girl generations later could carry a dying god's errand through the dark." He touched the gap in the deadfall almost reverently. "Whoever she was, she didn't know what she was building either. But she built it anyway. And it's still carrying people home."

August looked at the goat-path. At the mountains stacked blue and white against the sky, vast and indifferent and—she realized with a jolt—familiar. Not mapped-familiar. *Loved*-familiar. However the knowledge had gotten into her, however cynically it had been planted and pruned, it was love that had sunk into the bone. Somebody's long patience. Somebody's care.

Maybe that was the whole ugly truth of it: everything she was had been made by hands other than hers. And everything she *did* was still hers, every single morning, starting with the next step.

She stepped through the gap.

They climbed all morning. By midday the Spire showed itself between two peaks—distant, black-needled, threaded with the faint red glow of the waking veins—and August felt the map pulse hard against her palm, eager as a hound at the end of its lead.

Let it be eager, she thought. Let it drag. She'd been bred for this road, yes—but the road didn't know what she'd do when she reached the end of it. Neither did the guilds, or the Crown, or the covenant, or anyone else who thought they'd finished writing her.

There was room at the end of every plan for one thing the planners never accounted for.

A person who'd read the plan.

"We're close," she said aloud, and heard her own voice come back level and certain off the rocks. Ahead, past the next ridge, the mountain began to hum—a deep, subterranean note, like a struck bell still ringing somewhere under the world. Corvane went very still, and his face, for the first time since she'd known him, showed naked fear.

"That's not the veins," he said. "That's the Choir. Something's singing back."

Far below them, at the tree line, dark shapes were beginning to move.
