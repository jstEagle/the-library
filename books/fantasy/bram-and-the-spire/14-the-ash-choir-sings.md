## 14. The Ash Choir Sings

Corvane had dreamed of the monastery every night for eleven years, and in all that time his dreaming mind had been kinder to it than the truth.

The Ash Choir had not crumbled so much as *melted*. Its bell tower leaned like a drunkard against its neighbor. The cloister walls wore a shaggy coat of lichen the color of old pewter, and snow had drifted through the broken roof of the chapel and lay on the altar stones like a congregation that had finally, after centuries, settled down to listen. Wind moved through the colonnade and made a low, mournful sound across the mouths of the flues — the old ventilation channels the priests had cut to carry heat from the veins below up into every room. Even dead, even cold, the building still tried to sing.

"Charming," said August, stamping snow off her boots in the gateway. She was gray-faced with exhaustion and had been for three days, but she managed to look around at the ruin with the particular expression of a woman being shown a house she'd been promised was a palace. "This is it? This is the great sanctuary of your order?"

"It was," Corvane said, "the greatest library of fire-lore south of the Weeping Stair, the training ground of four hundred priests, and the only place in the Thornfell where you could get a decent cup of tea."

"And now?"

"Now it is a very large pile of stones with excellent acoustics." He smiled, because if he did not smile he would have to grieve, and there would be time for grief later or there would not be time for anything at all. "Come. The rite requires the heart-oven, and the heart-oven is this way, and I would very much like to sit down before my knees file a formal complaint."

The brand on her hand had been getting louder. He could see it in the way she carried her arm slightly away from her body, as though the map were something she'd caught rather than something she wore. Three nights ago she had woken him by sitting bolt upright and saying, in a voice not entirely her own, *"Left at the split birch, past the stone that weeps."* Then she'd looked at her own hand as if it belonged to a stranger and hadn't spoken until dawn.

The ink was hungry. It had eaten her village's name, her mother's voice, and who knew what else she hadn't told him yet. And it was pulling them north and east along routes no living person should have known — routes that ran, Corvane realized with a priest's old dread, straight through the places where the dragon-veins ran closest to the skin of the world.

The map wasn't just guiding her to the Spire. It was walking her along the body of the thing that had made it.

* * *

The heart-oven was exactly where he remembered, which surprised him more than anything else that day. Eleven years of exile, and his feet still knew the way: down the cloister stair, under the arch where the novices used to hang their wet socks (a practice strictly forbidden and universally observed), and into the round chamber at the mountain's root where the floor was warm even in the deepest winter.

It was not warm now. That was the wound of the place. Corvane knelt and pressed both palms flat against the oven-stone, and it gave back nothing but the dumb cold of ordinary rock. When he had been a young brother here — before the disrobing, before the trial, before the word *heretic* got attached to his name like a burr — this floor had been blood-heat. Novices had slept here in rows. Old Brother Tammasen had sworn the stone cured rheumatism, and he had died at ninety-one with the posture of a much younger liar.

"Oh," August said quietly behind him. She had understood. He heard it in her voice. "It's gone out."

"The whole vein went dormant when the last dragon was bound." Corvane kept his hands on the stone. "We told ourselves it was grief. The mountain mourning its kin. The elders performed the Rite of Vigil every day for sixty years, waiting for the fire to come back." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "I was defrocked for suggesting we stop waiting and go do something about it. Apparently asking *why* the fire left is heresy. Asking questions generally is."

"So what happens now?"

"Now," Corvane said, rising, joints crackling like green wood, "we find out whether they were right to throw me out."

He made her help him clear the altar. It took an hour, hauling aside fallen beams and a truly impressive quantity of bird droppings, during which August discovered that the chapel swallows had claimed one of the rafters and objected loudly to human presence, and Corvane learned that a woman who had lost her mother's voice could still swear with remarkable fluency. By the time the altar stood bare — a slab of black basalt veined with threads of dull red, the old offering-stone — the light through the broken roof had gone amber, and the wind in the flues had risen to a proper moan.

He laid out the rite with hands that shook more than he liked. Ash from the pouch at his belt, saved from his last fire eleven years ago. Salt. A candle stub he had carried across half a kingdom without ever quite admitting why. And last, unwrapped from oilcloth with the care owed to a relic or a wound: the censer of the Ash Choir, bronze, dented, its chains green with age, shaped like a dragon curled nose-to-tail.

"You'll want to stand back," he told August. "And whatever you hear, don't answer it."

"What will I hear?"

"I genuinely don't know. That's rather the point of a rite. If you know how it ends, it's just a recipe."

She stepped back to the doorway and folded her branded hand into her other fist, and watched him with those road-gray eyes that missed nothing, and Corvane thought, absurdly, *I hope I'm worth watching*, and struck the flint.

The candle caught. He lit the ash in the censer, and the smoke that rose was not the smoke of any ordinary fuel — it was thin and silver and it fell as well as rose, pooling along the floor like fog with somewhere to be. He began the chant.

Eleven years since he had sung it, and it lived in him the way roads live in a Wayfinder. The First Versicle, which named the mountain. The Litany of Veins, which walked the fire down through the deep places, naming each junction the way a shepherd names sheep. His voice cracked on the third verse and he kept going, because the Ash Choir had never required beauty, only endurance. *Sing until the stone remembers you.* That was the whole of their theology, really. Everything else was commentary.

The wind in the flues changed.

It had been moaning; now it hummed, low and resonant, finding the pitch beneath his chant and holding it, and the pooled silver smoke began to move — streaming toward the altar, sinking into the red-veined basalt like water into cracked earth. The veins brightened. Dull rust-red became ember-orange became a white-gold thread, and the light spread outward through the slab, branching, reaching, and then —

The floor warmed.

Not quickly. Not dramatically. Just a slow, rising warmth, like a bed long empty finally occupied again, and Corvane felt it come up through his knees and into his chest and he stopped singing because his throat had closed entirely.

August made a small sound. He turned. She was staring at her branded palm, held out flat, and the map-ink was glowing — not searing, not burning, but *glowing*, the lines of it lit from within, and where the light touched the air it steamed faintly, and her face above it was terrified and awed in equal measure.

"It likes it here," she whispered. "Corvane. The map *likes it here*."

"Of course it does." His voice came out rough. "It's home. Dragon's blood knows dragon-fire. The ink isn't a map, August — it never was. It's a *memory*. The last memory of a dying creature, written in the only ink that lasts. And we're standing inside the place where its people prayed."

Above them, through the broken roof, the evening sky had begun to fill with snow. But the snowflakes near the roof-holes melted before they landed, and the meltwater ran down the ancient channels in the walls, and somewhere far below, deep in the dark of the mountain, something vast and slow turned over in its sleep and, very faintly, exhaled.

The heart-oven sang.

* * *

They made camp in the refectory, which retained most of its roof and all of its echoes. Corvane cooked barley and dried mutton over a fire built in one of the old hearths — connected, he explained with the enthusiasm of a man showing off a childhood home, to the flue system, so that in the old days the whole kitchen had been heated by the mountain itself, free of charge, "which is why our cooks were insufferable and our food was still terrible."

August laughed. It was a rusty sound, rarely used, and he treasured it the way he treasured the candle stub: proof that some things survived long journeys.

"How many priests lived here?" she asked. "At the height."

"Four hundred and twelve, in my grandfather's time. Sixty when I took my vows. Nine when I left." He stirred the pot. "Fire-lore doesn't feed anyone, the guild-masters said. The kings need roads and soldiers, not men who sing at rocks. And they were right, in the way that a man who sells his roof for drink is right that he needed the money."

"And you stayed anyway. Until they threw you out."

"I stayed because someone has to remember the words." He shrugged. "That's all a priest is, in the end. A memory with a bad singing voice."

She was quiet a while, turning her branded hand over, watching the faint glow pulse in time — he noticed, and decided not to mention — with the heartbeat of the warming stone beneath them.

"If the rite worked," she said slowly, "if the vein's waking up... won't that draw attention? The Hollow Men track the map's heat. You said so yourself. And we've just lit a bonfire."

Corvane looked into his pot and lied with the serene confidence of a man who had spent eleven years lying mostly to himself. "The mountains are full of hot springs and natural vents. One warm room among hundreds—"

The dogs started first.

There were no dogs. That was the point of the sound — a baying with no throats behind it, coming down the night wind from the western pass, and August was on her feet before the second note, snuffing the fire with one kick, and Corvane felt the temperature of his own blood drop ten degrees.

"They followed the waking," he said. "Of course they did. Of course. I am an idiot."

"We have maybe an hour." August was already moving, checking the refectory's doors with a smuggler's economy, her fear packed away somewhere efficient. "Maybe less. How many ways out of this building?"

"One good one, two poor ones, and a chimney I wouldn't ask you to attempt." He was gathering the censer, the ash-pouch, the candle — eleven years of carrying, and now his hands wanted to leave it all and run. "August. Listen to me. There's a postern gate at the north end of the cloister, behind the collapsed granary. It opens onto the goat tracks. You can be over the ridge before moonrise."

She looked at him the way one looks at a beloved idiot. "And the gate needs holding while I go through it."

"The door is iron and the hinges are sound—"

"Corvane."

"—and I have held worse doors against worse things, although admittedly I was younger and had both eyebrows—"

"*Corvane.*"

He stopped. In the dark, with the map-glow pulsing on her palm between them like a small captive star, she looked suddenly very young and very old at once, and he understood that she had already done the arithmetic he was avoiding: one priest, one gate, one girl the world had spent twenty years teaching to survive alone.

"You don't get to die for me," she said. "I haven't decided to keep you yet."

"Then hold the decision in reserve," Corvane said, "and go through the gate."

* * *

The Hollow Men came up the processional road, because of course they did — the dead keep to the old paths even better than the living. Corvane counted nineteen shapes in the moonlight, moving with that terrible smoothness, faces smooth as eggs, and where their footprints pressed the new-fallen snow the snow did not crunch but *hissed*, faintly, as though remembering fire.

He stood in the gateway of the Ash Choir with an iron-shod staff and a censer full of sacred ash, wearing the vestments he had sworn never to wear again, and he found — this was the strange part, the part he would carry with him — that he was not afraid.

"Brothers," he called out, because the Hollow Men had been people once, people who had read the map too long, and the Ash Choir had always believed that a name remembered was a soul half-saved. "Brothers, you are standing on holy ground. Whatever wears your faces now, the stone beneath you remembers who you were. Let it remind you."

The nearest one tilted its blank head. And for one impossible moment, its mouth — a mouth with no expression, carved smooth — opened, and from somewhere inside it came a sound like a hymn played backward, three notes, almost familiar.

Then it charged, and the moment for preaching was over.

What follows, Corvane would reconstruct afterward in fragments, from bruises and burns and August's furious account. He remembered the first blow, staff meeting hollow chest with a sound like striking a frozen log. He remembered the censer swinging on its chains, scattering silver ash that burned the creatures like brands, making them recoil and shriek in voices that were almost human. He remembered chanting — the Litany of Veins, the only weapon he had ever truly owned — and remembered that the ground helped him, that the warming stone sent up little breaths of heat where he stood, that the mountain itself seemed to lean into the fight like an old dog waking to defend its yard.

He remembered the gate at his back staying shut.

He remembered the moment he went down — a blow to the knee, another to the temple, the world going sideways and white — and the largest of the Hollow Men standing over him, raising something that might once have been a sword.

And he remembered, more clearly than anything, the sound of the postern gate banging open, and a voice he had known for mere weeks but would have known in any life:

*"GET AWAY FROM MY PRIEST."*

August came through the smoke like something out of the old stories — the ones about the fire-bringers, the ones the Ash Choir had sung and the guilds had laughed at. She had found the vestry stores, and what she carried was a censer chain whirling a lump of live coal, and what she *was* was a woman with nothing left to lose except a future she'd only just decided to want. The silver ash flew wide. Two Hollow Men went down wreathed in pale flame. The big one turned, and she hit it with everything, and it staggered, and Corvane — from the ground, bleeding, laughing — drove his staff up into the smooth dark of its face and spoke the one word every Ash Choir rite ended with, the word that meant *remember*.

The Hollow Man screamed. And in the scream, for half a heartbeat, there was a man's voice, a real one, saying something that might have been a name.

Then the gate, the courtyard, the world — all of it went soft and dark, and the last thing Corvane of the Ash Choir knew was the weight of small strong hands keeping pressure on his side, and a voice saying, over and over, with none of its usual dryness:

"Don't you dare. Don't you *dare*. I decided to keep you, you stubborn old man, so you are not allowed—"

He woke once, briefly, to firelight and pain and August's face, smudged and tear-tracked and furious with relief. Beyond her, through the gateway, the snow lay littered with empty robes and, here and there, with sleeping figures — men and women breathing, dreaming, their faces returning to them like tide returning to a shore.

"The mountain," he managed. "Is the mountain—"

"Warm," she said. "The whole east wall is warm. Now shut up and bleed quietly."

He shut up. It was, he reflected as the dark took him again, the first order from a superior he had obeyed without argument in thirty years.

Above the ruined chapel, through the broken roof, the snow was melting into the old channels, running down into the deep places, and the mountain sang — softly, hoarsely, like a choir long silent finding its first note. And far to the north, at the top of the Spire, something ancient felt the heat climbing the veins toward it, and opened one eye.
