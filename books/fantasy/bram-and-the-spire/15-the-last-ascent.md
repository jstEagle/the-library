## 15. The Last Ascent

The Spire did not look like a building from below. It looked like a decision the mountain had made and then regretted.

It rose out of the glacier's shoulder in a single column of black stone, taller than any tower August had ever seen, wider at its crown than at its root, so that it seemed to lean over her like a curious giant. Wind screamed around it in long, slow spirals. Somewhere near the top, she could see a faint orange pulse behind the stone—slow as a sleeping heartbeat.

"That's him," Corvane said. He was slung across her back, arms hooked over her shoulders, and he had stopped apologizing for his weight somewhere around noon, which August appreciated. "That's the fire you can see. Vhalorath's breath behind the rock."

"You're heavy," August said, by way of acknowledging this information.

"I'm dying," Corvane corrected mildly. "There's a difference, though I grant it's subtle from your position."

His wounds from the monastery gate had gone bad in the way deep burns go bad—not bleeding anymore but weeping, the skin around them gray as old ash. He'd walked for two days on legs that shouldn't have held him, singing under his breath the whole way, one of the low droning hymns of the Ash Choir that made the snow shiver off the branches. Then, that morning, at the foot of the Spire, his legs had finally filed their formal resignation.

So now it was August, and the wall, and whatever tricks she had left.

She set him down against a boulder and looked up. The outer face of the Spire wasn't sheer—that would have been almost merciful. It was broken, cracked, studded with ledges and frozen waterfalls of meltwater refrozen into what amounted to glass fangs. A smuggler's wall, if she was honest with herself. Ugly, irregular, full of holds if you knew how to look for them.

She knew how to look for them. That had never once been the problem.

"Talk to me," she said, checking the rope at her hip. She had maybe sixty feet of good cord left, plus a coil of the thin waxed line Brenna's people used for hoisting contraband over city walls, plus three iron pitons, two wooden ones, and a climbing pick whose handle was held together with hope and tarred string.

"About what?"

"Anything. Your choir. Your hot springs. Whether you've ever actually been up this thing before, or whether you've been letting me believe you have."

Corvane managed something that was mostly a smile. "I climbed it once. Forty years ago. From the inside, down the old stair, when the priests still had leave to visit." His eyes went distant. "Vhalorath spoke to us then. Asked us our names. Do you know what it's like to be asked your name by something that has watched kings be born and die like mayflies?"

"No," August said. "But I'm about to be asked my name by a wall, and I intend to lie to it shamelessly."

* * *

The first hundred feet were almost pleasant, in the way that things are pleasant when they are merely trying to kill you rather than actively succeeding.

August worked the cracks the way Brenna had taught her, back when she was small enough to fit through warehouse windows: test every hold twice, trust nothing that sounds hollow, keep three points of contact and let the fourth be a rumor. She drove a piton into a seam, slung the rope, hauled Corvane up in the sling she'd rigged across her shoulders, anchored him to the piton, and climbed on ahead.

It was slow. It was brutally, maddeningly slow. But it worked, and by late afternoon she had developed a rhythm she could almost lose herself in: climb, place, haul, rest, climb. The wind came at her in gusts that felt personal. Below, the glacier caught the last of the light and turned the color of a bruise healing.

"August," Corvane said, from thirty feet below, tied to an anchor. "Your hand."

She glanced down at her right palm without meaning to—the brand had become a habit, the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth. The map was showing again. It did that more often now, the closer they came, ink surfacing through her skin like something rising through dark water. This time it showed the Spire itself, drawn in strokes of dried blood, and a route spiraling up the outside of the tower, and at the top—

At the top there was a mark she hadn't seen before. A circle, open like a mouth.

And beneath it, in the map's cramped blood-ink script, words she couldn't read yet because they kept sliding apart when she looked directly at them.

"Noted," she said, and climbed on.

* * *

Night fell while she was crossing the worst section—a bulge in the stone where a century of freeze-thaw had peeled the surface away like bark, leaving nothing to hold but a horizontal crack barely wide enough for her fingers.

She got her body across it. That was the easy part.

Getting Corvane across it required her to untie him from his anchor, pass the rope through a loop she'd hammered into the seam above, and lower-and-raise him hand over hand along the underside of the ledge while the wind tried its level best to turn him into a pendulum. He weighed too much. He weighed far, far too much, and halfway through the traverse her grip began to fail, and she heard herself make a sound she didn't recognize—something between a grunt and a laugh.

"What's funny?" Corvane called up, swinging gently, terribly, in the dark.

"I'm thinking about Brenna's warehouse rule," August said through her teeth. "Rule six. *Never lift anything you aren't willing to drop.*"

"A wise woman."

"She's a horror. She taught me to pick locks with my teeth before I could write my own name." Hand over hand. The rope burned. Her shoulders had passed through pain sometime in the last hour and come out the other side into a strange white silence. "I used to think that was funny too."

She got him onto the ledge. They lay there side by side on four feet of frozen stone with a mile of air beneath them, and Corvane laughed—actually laughed, a dry rusted sound, wincing as it pulled at his burns.

"You're going to get us both killed," he said fondly.

"One of us, anyway," August said. "I haven't decided which."

They ate half a brick of pressed travel-bread and drank melted snow, and August realized, distantly, that she was humming. Not one of Corvane's hymns—she didn't know those, not really—but something older, tuneless, a road-song the caravans used to count miles by.

She couldn't remember where she'd learned it.

That frightened her more than the wind did.

* * *

Dawn came gray and hostile. They were perhaps two hundred feet from the top, and the map had begun to *pull*.

August felt it in her palm like a fish on a line. Every time she reached for the next hold, the brand tugged her slightly rightward, or upward, toward a route that was not always the sensible one. Twice it steered her away from holds that looked perfect and toward cracks that seemed impossible—and both times the perfect hold crumbled at a touch, shedding frost and rotten stone, while the impossible crack took her weight like it had been waiting for her.

"The map knows the wall," Corvane murmured from the sling. "Of course it does. It was drawn by someone who climbed here."

"Then why doesn't it just carry me up?"

"Because it isn't a rope, child. It's a ledger." He coughed, wet and long, and August pretended not to hear what was in the cough. "Every road it shows has a price. You've been paying all the way here."

"I know what I've paid," August said, sharper than she meant to.

She did know. In fragments, the way you know a room by its empty spaces. She no longer knew the name of the village where she was born. She no longer knew her mother's voice—she remembered that her mother had sung, remembered that the songs existed, but the sound itself was gone, a window painted over. And other small things, small losses she only noticed at night: the taste of a particular stew. A dog's name. The color of a door.

The map fed on memory, and it preferred, she had learned, the memories that mattered least to anyone else and most to her.

"Two hundred feet," she said aloud, to the wall, to herself, to the wind. "Two hundred feet and then it can have whatever it likes."

The wall, naturally, did not care.

* * *

The last pitch nearly killed them both.

It began well. There was a chimney—a genuine, generous chimney, wide enough to wedge her whole body, running forty feet straight up the final face—and August almost wept with relief. She wedged, and pressed, and wormed upward, hauling the rope behind her, and for fifty glorious feet the Spire seemed to be done playing games.

Then the chimney narrowed. Then it narrowed again. Then, ten feet from the top, it simply ended, capped by an overhang of smooth black stone, and August found herself pressed into a slot of rock with nowhere to go, her arms shaking, Corvane's weight dragging at the rope around her waist like an anchor with opinions.

"Back down?" Corvane called from below.

"If I go down, I don't come back up," she said. Her voice sounded very calm and very far away. "My arms are finished. Whatever I do next, I do it once."

She hung there in the dark slot and let herself, for exactly ten seconds, feel everything. The burning in her forearms. The cold working into her joints. The grief of all those vanished memories rattling around inside her like coins in a stolen purse. Ten seconds. Brenna's rule nine: *Panic is allowed, but only on schedule.*

Then she looked up at the overhang, and the smuggler in her—the girl raised in warehouses and river towns, who had crossed a hundred rooftops with stolen goods strapped to her back—took inventory one last time.

Sixty feet of rope. Three iron pitons. One hammer. One pick with a dying handle.

And the overhang was not smooth, not quite. There was a seam. Hairline, frozen shut, invisible unless you were hanging directly beneath it looking up with nothing left to lose.

"Corvane," she said. "How much do you trust my hammering?"

"Infinitely," he said. "From a safe distance."

* * *

It took four attempts.

On the first, the piton skated off the seam and nearly took her knuckles with it. On the second, it bit and held—for the three seconds it took her to hang her full weight on it, at which point it tore free with a crack like a snapped tooth and she dropped six feet before the rope caught her. On the third attempt she widened the seam with the pick until her fingers bled, and the piton went in true and deep and held her weight, and held Corvane's weight, and held the weight of everything she intended to be angry about later.

She went over the lip of the overhang at midmorning, hauled Corvane up beside her, and collapsed onto a broad ledge of black stone with the sky suddenly enormous above her and the whole world reduced to a glittering plain far, far below.

"We're on the Spire," Corvane said wonderingly.

"We're on a ledge of the Spire."

"August. We are *sitting on the Spire.*" He spread his burned hands against the stone, palms down, the way a man touches the flank of a horse he thought he'd never ride again. "Forty years. Forty years I told myself I'd never see it again, and here I am, delivered by a smuggler girl with a rope and a grudge."

"It was more of a debt than a grudge," August said. "You carried me across the Weeping Stair. Fair's fair."

The ledge ran along the tower's flank to a place where the black stone changed—where it became, impossibly, worked stone, carved with columns and friezes worn featureless by wind, marking the edge of some ancient gallery built by hands that had loved this place once. Beyond the gallery, an archway stood open onto darkness. No door. No bars. Nothing guarding the heart of the Spire at all, as if whatever lived inside had long ago concluded that guards were unnecessary.

The orange pulse was brighter here. August could feel it against her skin like standing too close to a hearth.

"Well," she said, and stood up, and swayed, and caught herself on a column, and stood up again properly because some things mattered. "One archway. Can't even be lost now."

She took one step toward it.

The map woke.

* * *

It didn't burn this time. That was the terrible part. All the other payments had hurt—white-hot, searing, a theft she could feel. This one was gentle. This one was almost kind.

Her palm warmed. The ink rose, complete at last, the whole spiral route finished, the open-mouthed circle at its crown glowing softly. And the sliding, unreadable words beneath it settled into place, and she read them, and understood them, and felt them begin to lift out of her like steam off a morning road:

*The last toll is taken at the threshold. Payment: the reason.*

"No," August said.

She knew, immediately, what it meant, the way you know a thing you will spend years failing to explain. Not a fact. Not a name. Not a face or a song or a road. The *reason*. The thing underneath all the other things—the answer to the question nobody had yet thought to ask her, which was *why*, August, why did a girl who prized invisibility above all else agree to carry a cursed map up a mountain to unbind a king she'd never met?

Why had she picked up the courier's head at the ford? Why hadn't she run south, as her master begged? Why, when Brenna sold her, when the Hollow Men hunted her, when the map ate her childhood bite by bite—why had she kept climbing?

The answer was there. It was warm and simple and hers, and she reached for it the way you reach for a coin rolling toward a drain.

And the map took it.

* * *

There was no pain. There was a moment of perfect quiet, like the pause between lightning and thunder, and then August stood on the ledge of the Spire and could not remember why she had come.

She could remember everything else. She remembered the ford and the burned courier and his blood on her hands; she remembered the guild hall burning; she remembered Corvane's sanctuary and the glacier vein and the Weeping Stair and the empty village and the child she'd hidden; she remembered Brenna's face at the shrine and every word of the pact between the guilds and the Crown. All of it intact, vivid, present.

Only the center was gone. Only the *why*. She turned inward, searching for it, and found a shaped absence, a hole exactly the size of a purpose, and around the edges of the hole the strangest sensation: she knew she had had a reason. She knew it had been good. She knew it had been worth everything she'd paid. And it was simply—gone, the way a dream is gone, leaving behind only the certainty that dreaming had occurred.

"August?" Corvane had pushed himself upright against the column, watching her face. He'd seen the ink rise; he'd read the words over her shoulder. His voice was careful, the way voices get around wounded animals. "What did it take?"

"I don't know," August said. And then, because honesty had become the only currency she trusted: "That's what it took."

He was silent a moment. Wind moved his gray hair. Down below, the world was ice and distance, and above them the archway breathed its slow orange light.

"Do you want to stop?" he asked quietly.

And here was the strange thing—the thing she would turn over for years afterward, in waystations and on roads she could suddenly see without maps. She examined herself, honestly, thoroughly, the way she checked a knot. Fear: yes, plenty. Grief: yes, a whole silent country of it. Arms: ruined. Reason: absent.

And yet the wanting was still there. Stripped of its story, stripped of its justification, some bare stubborn engine in her chest still pointed at that archway the way a compass points north, asking nothing, explaining nothing, simply *pointing*.

Whatever the reason had been, it had already done its work. It had built the person who would climb anyway.

"No," August said. She flexed her right hand, watching the completed map glow in her palm like a coal. "I don't seem to need a reason anymore. Turns out I run fine without one."

Corvane laughed—wet, cracked, real. "Then let's not keep a dragon waiting."

August squared her shoulders, stepped past the wind-worn columns of the ancient gallery, and walked through the archway into the dark and the heat, toward the slow orange heartbeat of the thing wearing the king's crown.
