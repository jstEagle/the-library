## 16. The Thing Wearing His Crown

The door at the top of the Spire was not a door.

That was the first thing August understood, standing before it with Corvane's weight sagging against her shoulder and her legs shaking like a foal's. It was a door-shaped absence in the wall—a place where the black stone simply declined to be a wall anymore, worn through not by tools but by centuries of someone passing. Someone patient. Someone who had walked the same path so many times that even granite had given up arguing.

She should have found that frightening. She was too tired to be frightened. Somewhere on the climb—she could not remember exactly where, and the *not remembering* sat in her chest like a swallowed stone—the map had taken its largest payment, and whatever reason she'd once carried for making this ascent had gone with it. She climbed now because she was climbing. Because Corvane breathed shallowly against her shoulder and she had promised him, or he had promised her, one of those things.

"You're bleeding on my only clean sleeve," Corvane said. His voice was a ruin, but there was a smile buried in it somewhere, the way there was heat buried under a glacier.

"You're bleeding on both of mine."

"Yes, but mine were already ruined." He straightened, wincing, and pressed his scarred palm flat against the door-absence. "It will let us in. It has been letting us in since the Ash Choir sang. The mountain has been expecting you, Wayfinder."

"Don't call me that."

"Why not? It's true."

"Because three days ago it wasn't, and I haven't finished being angry about it."

He nodded, slow and serious, as though this were the most reasonable position in the world—which, she was learning, was his way of being kind. Then he stepped through the absence, and August followed him into the heart of the Spire.

* * *

The throne room was a wound of light.

No torches burned. No windows admitted the storm still raging outside. Instead the whole vast chamber glowed from within, the black stone shot through with veins of dull ember-red, pulsing slowly, like the inside of some enormous sleeping animal. And perhaps that was exactly what it was, because the heat hit her like a hand—wet, heavy, smelling of sulfur and old iron and something sweeter underneath, something like the air above a hearth in a house where someone loves you.

At the center of the chamber stood the throne.

August had imagined it a hundred ways on the long road: gold and cruel, obsidian and cruel, plain wood made cruel by who sat in it. The reality was none of these. The throne was stone, and the stone was *alive*, grown over and around in ridged plates the size of barn doors, and rising from it—fused to it, rooted in it, wings pinned open and webbed through with the same dim ember-light—was the dragon.

It was dying. That was the second thing she understood, and it arrived with an ache she hadn't braced for. She had expected terror. She had rehearsed terror all the way up the Weeping Stair. But Vhalorath was *vast* and *ruined*, scales gone gray as ash, the great ribs showing through where flesh had wasted, one wing folded wrong, and the eyes—

The eyes opened, and they were old. Old the way mountains are old, old the way grief is old. They looked at her the way she imagined her own eyes would look back from a mirror after everything this year had taken.

"Oh," August said. It came out very small. "Oh, you're *sick*."

A sound moved through the chamber—not quite a laugh, not quite a rockslide. When the dragon spoke, she didn't hear it so much as feel it form behind her teeth, arriving fully formed in her own head like a memory she'd never made.

*Nobody has said that word to me in four hundred years,* said Vhalorath. *They say monster. They say god. They say the thing wearing his crown. You say sick.*

"I'm sorry," said August, who could not remember ever apologizing to anything larger than a mule. "Is that worse?"

*It is honest.* The great head shifted; stone groaned; dust sifted down like gray snow. *Honesty is rarer than flattery here. Sit, if your legs will permit it. Your priest is already sitting.*

Corvane had indeed folded onto a fallen slab near the wall, gray-faced, one hand pressed to the wound in his side. "I'm fine," he said, before either of them asked. "Keep talking to it. I want to hear everything."

"You want to *record* everything," August muttered, but she lowered herself onto the warm floor beside him, because her legs had been overruled.

*You carry the map,* Vhalorath said. *I can taste it. My own blood, spent like ink, come home at last.* A pause, long as a held breath. *And yet you are not what I expected either. I expected a weapon. I find instead a girl apologizing to a dying animal.*

"That makes two of us finding things we didn't expect," August said. "I came to free King Aldric. Nobody mentioned you."

*Everybody mentions me eventually,* said the dragon. *They simply do it wrong.*

* * *

Vhalorath told her the story while the ember-veins pulsed around them, and August listened the way she had learned to listen on the roads—with her whole body, cataloguing, weighing—but this time the cataloguing kept failing, because pity kept getting in the way.

*I was young when they caught me,* the dragon said. *Younger than you. There were many of us then, and we were proud, and pride is a slow poison. The kings of men learned to bind us—not with chains, which we would have shattered, but with bargains, which we did not. Fire for harvests. Fire for long winters survived. Fire for a kingdom that would never starve again. All it costs is everything.*

"And Aldric," August said quietly. "He bound himself too."

*Willingly.* Something moved across the ancient face—too slow and heavy to be called a flinch, but its cousin. *There was famine. His people ate bark and boiled leather. He came to the Spire alone, a boy with a crown he hadn't earned yet, and he offered himself in exchange for my fire. I thought—I believed—that a king's mind would be a feast worth sharing. Two keepers of one flame. A partnership.* The ember-veins pulsed, dimmed. *I was starving, little wayfinder. Starving things agree to bad bargains. I have been feeding on him for sixty years, and he has been feeding the land, and neither of us can stop, and the fire is going out regardless. Everything is spent. Even mercy runs on fuel.*

"Then unbind him," August said. "Let go. Let it end."

*If I release him, I die, and the fire dies with me, and the veins beneath every field from here to the sea go cold. The kingdom starves inside a generation. If he dies, I die. If I die, he follows within days—he has no mind left of his own to live on. There is no version of this where everyone walks away.* The dragon's voice, arriving behind her teeth, had gone very quiet. *So I offer you the only deal left, girl, and I offer it because you called me sick, and honesty deserves wages: turn around. Climb down. Live your life. Tell them the Spire cannot be reached. Let the world have whatever years the fire has left. Leave, and live. No one will blame you. No one will even know.*

August sat with it.

That was the strange thing, looking back—she didn't argue, didn't flare up, didn't do any of the heroic things songs insisted on. She just sat on the warm stone beside a wounded priest and thought about the offer with the cold practical part of her brain that had kept her alive through blizzards and betrayals.

And underneath the practical part, quieter, was the other thing. The thing that had made her apologize.

"You're lonely," she said at last. "That's the real deal, isn't it. Not 'leave and live.' '*Please leave.*' Because if I stay, you'll have to watch another person decide what happens to you, and you've been doing this for four hundred years, and you're tired, and—" Her voice cracked, annoyingly. "And nobody's called you sick in four hundred years because nobody ever *looked*. They looked at the crown. They looked at the king. You're just the thing underneath."

The chamber was silent except for the slow pulse of the dying veins.

*Your priest said you were blunt,* Vhalorath said at last.

"He said I was rude," said Corvane, without opening his eyes.

*Both may be true.* The great head lowered, until one ancient eye hung before her, large as a wagon wheel, close enough that she could see herself reflected in it—small, smudged, unremarkable, forgettable August Iversen, last apprentice of a dead guild. *Do you know what he dreams about? Your king. When I take from him—and I am always taking—he dreams of the famine years. Over and over. The children he saved. The ones he didn't. Sixty years of the same arithmetic. I am not a cruel feeder, girl. I take his memories of ruling, his memories of joy, the names of his friends. I leave him the guilt, because guilt is the richest meat. That is what wears the crown down there in the dark of him. Not a monster. A man doing sums forever.*

August wrapped her arms around her knees.

She thought about Brenna's face in the burning warehouse. She thought about the child in the waystation, hiding her name like treasure. She thought about the village that had forgotten itself, smiling their empty smiles, and the Hollow Men collapsing into remembered selves—though that hadn't happened yet, and wouldn't, unless—

Unless.

"Here's my problem," she said slowly. "Everyone keeps giving me two choices. Brenna gave me two. The map gives me two—you pay or you don't. Now you're giving me two. Leave and live, or stay and fail. But every single time, when I refuse to pick either one, there turns out to be a third option. It's usually terrible. It usually costs more than anybody wants to pay. But it's always *there*." She looked up at the dying dragon. "You're offering me a deal because you think deals are all there are. You made one yourself, once, when you were starving. Look how that went."

*Careful,* said Vhalorath, and there was heat in it now, real heat, the first flicker of true fire she had felt in the chamber, and Corvane's hand found her wrist in warning.

But August had been invisible her whole life, and invisible people learn things that thrones don't teach. She leaned forward, absurdly small, toward the wagon-wheel eye.

"You're not asking me to save myself," she said. "You're asking me to stop you from having to hope. Because hoping is worse than starving, isn't it? Hope costs more."

The fire-flicker died. The eye regarded her for a long, long moment.

*What is your name, girl?*

"August."

*August.* The name settled into the stones, into the veins, into the marrow of the mountain. *When I was young, there was a woman of the Ash Choir—a priestess—who used to bring me stolen honeycakes and tell me I was too clever for my own good. She said cleverness was just courage that hadn't decided yet. I ate her, in the end. In the worst years. You should know what I am before you hope at me.*

"I know what you are," August said. "You're sick. And you're lonely. And you're the last of your kind, holding up a kingdom that forgot you were ever a *someone*." She took a breath. "I'm not leaving. So you may as well show me the rest of it. Show me the king."

Something vast shifted in the dark behind the throne—a depth to the chamber she hadn't guessed, a stair descending into red gloom, breathing warmth like a throat.

*He is below,* Vhalorath said softly. *Where I keep the best of him. He will tell you not to save him. He believes it with his whole broken heart.* A pause, and then, so quietly she almost lost it: *So did I.*

Corvane struggled upright beside her, white-knuckled, jaw set. "Together, then," he rasped.

"Together," August agreed—and started toward the stairs, into the dark where the man beneath the Crown waited, still doing his sums.
