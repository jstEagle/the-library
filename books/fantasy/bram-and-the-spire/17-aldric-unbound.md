## 17. Aldric, Unbound

**August**

The throne room at the top of the Spire was smaller than she'd imagined, which felt like a joke the mountains had been saving for the whole journey. After the Weeping Stair, after the Ash Choir's gate, after carrying a wounded priest up a wall with her fingernails and her spite—after all of that, the great seat of the kingdom was a round stone chamber with one window, and in the window, snow.

The dragon filled the rest.

Vhalorath lay coiled around the throne like a serpent around a staff, scales gone the gray of old ash, wings folded to a size that suggested they had once blotted out the sun and now would struggle to blot out a barn. Its ribs rose and fell with a slowness that made her own breathing feel frantic. Its eyes—both of them, one gold and one the milky white of a blind fish—tracked her as she crossed the floor.

*You should have taken the bargain,* it said, and its voice was not sound so much as heat, arriving inside her skull the way warmth arrives through a door left open. *I offered you your life. Humans so rarely take the sensible offer.*

"I've never been good at sensible," August said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt, which was the one useful trick the smugglers had ever taught her. "I'm not here for you. I'm here for him."

*Then you are here for nothing. I have worn this king for sixty years. There is no *him* left. There is what I have kept alive, the way a farmer keeps a field alive—out of necessity, and with no illusions about who is serving whom.*

"Show him to me anyway."

The dragon's good eye narrowed, which in a creature that size was less an expression and more a geological event. But the great head lifted from the throne's armrest, and August saw what lay beneath it.

King Aldric sat in his own throne like a coat hung on a hook. He was thinner than the coins suggested, thinner than the statues in the border towns she could no longer name. His beard had gone white in patches, as though time had gotten bored and only finished half the job. His eyes were open. They were the eyes of a man who had been awake for a very long time, and August knew that look—she'd worn it herself, once, on the worst night of the Weeping Stair, when the map had taken her mother's voice and she'd kept climbing anyway because stopping meant feeling it.

He looked at her the way a drowning man looks at a rope and a cliff at the same time.

"You're not one of them," he said. His voice was rust and dust. "The Hollow. They come up the stair sometimes. They stand at the door and *wait*, and I pretend to be the crown's thing, and they go away. But you're—"

"August," she said. "Iversen. Last of the Wayfarers' Guild, which sounds more impressive than it is, since the guild hall was one building and it burned down." She held up her palm, where the map's lines had gone bright as forge-light. "I've come to unbind you."

Something moved across his face that was almost a smile and mostly grief. "Oh," he said softly. "Oh, you *poor* thing. They found someone."

*She cannot unbind you,* Vhalorath said, but there was something in the heat of it now, something August's ear for danger caught and filed away. *The map takes payment. It has taken hers for a thousand miles. What remains to pay with, child?*

"That's my business," August said.

"Listen to me." Aldric leaned forward, and the chains of the Crown—thin filaments of gold running from the band at his brow into the dragon's skull, into the throne, into the stone itself—pulled taut with a sound like harp strings about to give. "Listen, because I have had sixty years to get this speech right and no one to say it to. If you break the binding, the fire goes out of me and into nowhere. The dragon dies. And when the dragon dies, the veins under the mountains go cold, and the valleys stop growing, and within three years this kingdom eats itself down to the stones. I did the arithmetic when I was twenty-two years old and hungry for heroism, and the arithmetic has not changed. There is no version of this where you save me and the wheat still grows."

"There's a version where I save you and the dragon both."

"There isn't."

"You're right," August said. "There isn't. There's a third one. But I can't find it while you keep arguing for your own death, so I'm going to need you to stop that."

For a moment—just a moment—the King of the Nine Valleys looked at her exactly the way Brenna used to look at August when she'd announced some plan involving rooftops and fire, and August felt a stab of something that was not homesickness, because she could no longer remember home, but was its ghost.

*She is stubborn,* Vhalorath observed, with what August could have sworn was approval. *They breed them stubborn in the lowlands.*

"I'm not from the lowlands," August said automatically. Then stopped. Because she didn't know where she was from. The map had eaten it. She stood in the throne room of the kingdom she was trying to save, and could not remember the name of her own village, and the grief of it arrived on schedule and she set it aside, the way she'd learned to, the way you get good at carrying a pack.

"Corvane's below," she said. "In the monastery. Holding the gate with his ribs cracked and both hands burned, singing the old rites at the top of his lungs, and I want you to know that when I tell him you argued with me, he's going to say *of course he did, kings always argue*, and he's going to be right."

"Corvane." Aldric's breath caught. "Of the Ash Choir. I thought they were all—"

"Dead? Mostly. Exiled, the rest. He wants to see the dragon before it dies. That was his price for guiding me, and I intend to make sure he collects."

*Then let him climb,* Vhalorath said, and the heat of its voice had gone strange and soft. *Let the last priest see the last dragon. It is a tidy symmetry, and I have always been a fool for symmetry.*

August stepped closer to the throne. Up close, the Crown was worse than she'd imagined—not a circlet but a root system, gold threading into the king's skin at the temples, at the jaw, down the throat, and she understood suddenly that the binding had never been a crown at all. It had been a graft. The king and the dragon and the land, three things grown into one body, and every story she'd been told on the way up—the story the map told, the story Corvane told, the story the dying courier had bled into her hand—had been a story about cutting.

"I'm going to touch the binding," she said. "I need to feel how it's made before I decide how to unmake it."

*Child—* the dragon began.

"You can stop me," August said, "or you can watch. I'd rather you watched. I think you're tired of stopping people."

The great eye closed. Opened again. *Watch, then,* it said, and it sounded, for the first time in sixty years, like something that had once been young.

August reached out and laid her branded palm against the gold.

**Aldric**

The girl's hand touched the Crown, and Aldric felt her the way he felt weather—a pressure, a change, something moving through the long sealed room of his mind.

She was a road. That was the first thing he understood about her, before he understood anything else: the map had made her into a road, a living way through the Thornfell, and roads do not think about where they go, they only *go*, and she had been going so long that the going had eaten everything behind her. He felt the absence where her childhood should have been and it was shaped exactly like the absence where his youth should have been, and he thought, with the strange clarity that sixty years of captivity grants: *they sent me a mirror.*

*Don't,* said Vhalorath, inside the binding, inside his skull, inside the place where they had been one creature so long that Aldric could no longer find the seam. *Don't you dare pity her. Pity is how it starts. Pity is how it started with me.*

*I'm not going to pity her,* Aldric answered. *I'm going to argue with her. It's the only kindness I have left.*

She pulled her hand back. Her face had gone white, and her breath came in the short controlled pulls of someone who has learned that gasping is a luxury. "The binding isn't a lock," she said. "It's a *knot*. You tied it from the inside."

"I tied it from everywhere," Aldric said. "That was the problem."

"Tell me."

So he told her. It was easier than he expected, telling her; the story had been cooped up in him for sixty years with only a dragon for company, and dragons, it turns out, interrupt.

"I was twenty-two. The famine had been three years. My father was dead of it—not of hunger, kings don't hunger, but of the *shame* of it, which kills faster. The priests of the Ash Choir came to me with the old rite. Bind a dragon to the Crown, let its fire feed the veins, let the veins feed the valleys. One life for ten thousand. And I—" He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, the laugh of a man who has rehearsed his own foolishness. "I *volunteered*. Do you understand? They didn't trick me. There was no sorcery, no deception. A boy stood up in a room full of desperate men and said *I will do it*, and every one of them let him, because every one of them was hungrier than he was honest."

*And I let him,* Vhalorath said, and its voice had gone quiet, the way mountains are quiet. *I was old and I was failing and I thought: one more century of fire, spent well, is worth more than three centuries of hoarding it. I did not know what the binding would ask of me. Neither did he. That is the trick of it, girl. The Covenant was honest about the cost. It simply could not be understood from outside.*

"The first year was good," Aldric said. "You have to believe that. The wheat came back. The valleys greened. I rode through villages where children had stopped dying, and I thought, *this was the right thing, this was the only thing.*" His hands, on the arms of the throne, had gone white at the knuckles. "The second year, the Crown started answering questions before I asked them. Small things. A tax ruling. A border dispute. It was always right. That was the horror of it—it was always *right*, and so I stopped checking, and then I stopped *being asked*, and by the fifth year I was a passenger in my own reign, watching my hands sign things, watching my mouth say things, and the dragon's fire was in the ground and the kingdom was fed and no one—*no one*—came to ask the king if he was still in there."

"I came to ask," August said.

He looked at her. She stood in the snow-light with her branded hand curled at her side like a wounded bird, and she was so young—younger than he had been, at the ford, at the altar, at the moment of the tying—and she had walked out of the mountains carrying a map made of a dead man's blood and a priest's stubbornness and, he could feel it through the binding, through the stone, up the long stair: a fire in the monastery below that was not the dragon's fire, not the Crown's fire, but something older and more stubborn than either.

"You should leave," he said, and heard his own voice break on it. "Girl—August. You should turn around and climb down and live a long life and forget my name, because I am telling you the truth that took me sixty years to earn: there is no saving me that doesn't drown the valleys. Cut me loose, the dragon dies. Kill the dragon, I die and the land dies. Leave the binding, I rot here another century and the Hollow Men harvest everyone who ever looked at your map. Every road ends in the dark. I've walked them all in my head. *Every one.*"

"Then you've been walking the wrong map," August said.

And she smiled at him.

It was not a brave smile, exactly. It was the smile of someone standing at the edge of a thing with no floor under it, choosing the air. He knew that smile. He had worn it at twenty-two, in a room full of desperate men, and he had spent six decades telling himself it had been courage, and looking at it now on her face he finally understood what it had actually been: not courage. *Refusal.* The refusal to accept the terms of the story as given.

"The map wants payment," she said. "That's what Corvane figured out, in the monastery, with his ribs cracked. It doesn't want blood, it doesn't want the dragon dead, it doesn't want the king broken. Those are just the prices it's been *offered*. Blood, death, despair—the cheap currencies. But it's inked in dragon's blood, and dragon's blood is memory, and what a memory-map wants, more than anything, more than anything in the world—"

"—is to be *remembered*," Aldric breathed.

"To be *given*," she said. "Freely. Willingly. A payment that isn't taken but *offered*. Corvane says no one's ever tried it, because no one ever got this far with their mind intact enough to offer anything." She held up her palm, and the map-lines had begun to move, crawling like living rivers, and her voice did not shake, or rather it shook and she spoke through the shaking, which he supposed was the same thing. "I've paid it villages. I've paid it my mother's voice. It's eaten my whole childhood a mile at a time and I let it, because I thought that was the toll. But it was never the toll. It was the *appetite*. And appetites, your Majesty, can be fed on purpose."

*She is going to offer it everything,* Vhalorath said, and the heat of its voice had gone strange—awed, and afraid, and under the fear something so old and so tired that Aldric felt it in his own chest like a second heartbeat. *She is going to offer it the whole road. Every mile. Every memory. Herself.*

"Then I forbid it," Aldric said, and was horrified to hear how much of the Crown's old authority was still in his voice, how much of the king remained in the ruin of the king. "As your king—"

"You're not my king," August said, pleasantly. "I'm a smuggler. We don't recognize the monarchy. It's in the charter."

*She's right,* said Vhalorath, and Aldric could have sworn the ancient creature was enjoying itself. *You have no authority here. It's rather wonderful.*

"August." He leaned forward as far as the gold threads allowed, and the filaments sang, and sixty years of arithmetic stood up inside him and shouted its sums. *Three years of famine. Ten thousand lives. The valleys. The wheat. The children.* "If you are wrong, the land dies. Do you understand? Not me. Not the dragon. The *land*. The villages where children stopped dying. If your third road is a mistake, I will have spent sixty years on this throne for nothing, and so will you, and so will the dragon, and the wheat—"

"The wheat," August said quietly, "grows out of the ground. And the ground, your Majesty, is full of dragon-veins. And the veins—" she touched her branded palm, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal, "—don't need a king on a throne to carry fire. Corvane showed me. Under the glacier. The veins were *alive* before there was ever a Covenant. The Covenant didn't make them. It just *fenced them*."

*She's right,* Vhalorath said again, softer now. *Aldric. My king. My rider. My long captivity and my strange companion. She is right, and I have known it for a century, and I could not say it, because saying it meant admitting what we did to each other was never necessary—only possible, and then done, and then permanent. Nothing is permanent. I had simply forgotten.*

Aldric sat in his throne, in his chains, in the snow-light, and felt the arithmetic collapse.

It did not collapse all at once. It came down the way a bad building comes down—piece by piece, each certainty finding no purchase on the next—until what was left was just a man, twenty-two years old and sixty years old at once, sitting in a round stone room, being offered something no one had offered him since the tying.

A choice.

"If you're wrong," he said, and his voice was small now, only a man's voice, "the famine comes back. And it will be *mine*. Again. Twice."

"If I'm wrong," August said, "then we'll be wrong together, and the valleys will have two ghosts to blame instead of one, and I'm told that's how new kingdoms get started." She was already pulling something from her pack—a strip of cloth, a vial of ash, the small practical tools of a smuggler's kit, and beneath them the map, blazing now, its lines crawling, its hunger rising up through her arm like a tide. "Corvane's coming up the stair. He'll want to do the rites properly, and he'll lecture us both, and it'll take an hour, and I need you to do one thing for me in that hour."

"Anything," Aldric said, and meant it, and knew he would regret the word and meant it anyway.

"Remember something," she said. "One thing. Anything. A memory the Crown never got to. Hold onto it like a rope. Because when the binding breaks, everything the Crown took from you is going to come back all at once, and it's going to be a flood, and I can't carry you through a flood, your Majesty. I'm a road, not a boat. But a man with one rope—"

"—can hold," Aldric finished.

He closed his eyes. And in the sealed room of his mind, behind the sixty years, behind the Crown, behind the long gray winter of being answered-for, he went looking—and found it, small and warm and absurd: bread. Not the famine bread of bark and ash. Real bread, the loaf his mother's cook had pulled from the oven on the morning of his twelfth birthday, and the smell of it, and the way the crust had cracked under his thumbs, and how he had eaten half of it standing up in the kitchen and no one had scolded him, because it was his birthday, and he was, for one morning, just a boy with bread.

He held it. He held it like a rope.

* * *

**August**

The stair below the throne room rang like a bell, and August knew that sound—Corvane, singing, climbing, coming up with his burned hands and his broken ribs and his terrible voice, and behind him, faint but rising, the heat of the mountain itself, waking.

The map blazed in her palm. It had gone quiet in a new way—not sated, not waiting, but *listening*, the way a creditor listens when a debtor finally says the word *everything*.

"Well," she said to the dragon, to the king, to the snow at the window, to herself. "Here goes nothing."

*Here goes everything,* Vhalorath corrected gently.

"That's what I said."

And the stair door opened, and Corvane's voice came up it, cracked and burning and absolutely certain, singing the first rite of the unbinding—and the mountain answered.
