## 18. The Third Choice

The Spire's throne room smelled of hot iron and old grief.

August stood at the edge of the dais with her sword—Brenna's sword, the one she'd carried since she was twelve and had never once used for anything but cutting rope—held loosely at her side, and understood with a strange, floating clarity that she had run out of clever options somewhere on the stairs behind her.

Leaving hadn't worked. She'd tried that first, because she was a smuggler's daughter and leaving was the first thing you learned. She had walked backward down three flights of the Spire's interior stair, out into the wind-scoured ledge, and the map in her palm had simply *closed*—the roads inked there folding up like a fan, every path home erased, until the only route left open glowed faintly gold and pointed back inside. She had stood on that ledge for a full quarter hour, telling herself she was considering it, knowing perfectly well she wasn't going to jump.

Killing hadn't worked either. She'd raised the sword to Vhalorath—the dragon fused to the throne like a candle burned down to its socket—and the dragon had looked at her with one vast, rheumy eye and said, without malice, *Do it, then.* And when she couldn't, when her arm shook and dropped, the dragon had said, even more gently, *Yes. That is how it always goes. They come to kill me and they leave weeping, and I burn a little less brightly for their pity.*

So: not leaving, not killing. Which left the thing she had been circling for two days now, the way you circle a cold pool before committing your whole body to it.

"Corvane," she said. "Tell me about willing payment."

* * *

He was sitting against the far wall where she'd dragged him, his wounded side bound in strips torn from his own robe, his ash-gray vestments stiff with old blood and new. He looked terrible. He also looked, for the first time since she'd met him at the hot springs, entirely awake.

"You already know," he said.

"I want to hear it said properly. Rites like saying things properly."

That earned her the ghost of a smile. Corvane smiled the way other men bled—slowly, and at cost. "The map is not a map," he said. "You've known that since the village. It is a ledger. Every road it shows you, it has bought from someone—a wayfinder who walked it first, a child who named it, a mother whose voice knew the turns by heart. Dragon's blood remembers what it has purchased. The Hollow Men are what happens when the ledger takes everything and gives nothing back." He shifted, wincing. "But blood is a bargainer, August. It respects an honest trade. A payment *offered* weighs more than a payment *taken*. A whole life offered—" he stopped.

"Says who?"

"The Covenant says it. The rites say it. The mountain says it, if you're fool enough to listen."

"And if I offer my whole childhood—the roads, all of it—what happens? To Aldric. To the dragon. To me."

"To them?" Corvane looked past her, to the throne, where the dying dragon lay coiled around a man who had almost stopped being a man. "The Crown holds because it holds *something*. A king's mind, wound tight as thread around a spindle. Take away the need for the holding—give the fire somewhere else to go, give the debt another coin—and the Crown has nothing left to grip. It cracks. It has been waiting to crack for forty years. Everything waits to stop hurting, August. Even crowns."

"And me."

He was quiet for a moment. Outside, the storm that had chased them up the Weeping Stair threw itself against the high windows, and somewhere below them the mountain grumbled in its sleep, warm-blooded under all that stone.

"You," he said, "will be a woman standing in a room, alive, with no idea where she came from. You will remember this. You will remember the last two months, or most of them—you'll lose whatever the ledger decides the childhood contains, and childhoods are greedy things. Your name might survive. Your name might go with the rest." He met her eyes. "I will not lie to you to make it easier. That is the one honesty I have left, and I intend to spend all of it here."

August laughed. It came out wrong—too high, too thin—but it came out. "You know what the funny thing is? Everyone keeps telling me I was *made* for this. Bred for the road, groomed for the errand, designed like a lockpick. Fine. So here's the design finally getting used." She looked at her palm, where the map pulsed its slow gold light, patient as a spider. "It just turns out the thing I was made for was being spent."

"There is no shame—"

"I know. There's no shame in it. That's not the same as wanting it." She flexed her fingers. The map-light rippled. "When I was small—when I think I was small; it's getting hard to be sure—the other kids in Brenna's court used to play a game called *what would you never sell*. Everyone had an answer. Boots. A knife. One boy said his sister, which got him hit." Her voice steadied. "My answer was always the roads. Because the roads were mine. Nobody gave them to me, nobody could take them, they lived in my head like a second set of bones. I used to think: sell anything else, sell the boots, sell the coat off my back, but the roads are *mine*."

She looked up at Corvane.

"So I suppose the ledger knows exactly what it's asking for. It wants the one thing I said I'd never sell. Of course it does. Debts always come for the favorite first."

* * *

They prepared the rite in the throne room itself, because there was nowhere else and because—Corvane insisted—the mountain needed to witness it. He built the circle out of what the Spire offered: a ring of old crown-coins pried from the masonry, each one stamped with a king's face worn smooth as a river stone. He laid them while kneeling, which cost him visibly, and August knelt too, unasked, and set the last coins in place herself.

"You don't have to do that," he murmured.

"You can't kneel twice. Someone has to finish the circle."

"It's bad luck for the priest to be helped."

"We're smugglers' luck, both of us. Bad is our natural climate."

He huffed something that was almost a laugh, and then, because he was Corvane, he turned it immediately into instruction. "The anchor is the hardest part. While the ledger takes, you will want to hold on to *everything*, and you cannot. Holding makes the taking crueler—it drags. You must let it flow through you and stay yourself at the bottom of it, like a stone in a river. I'll sing the Ash Choir's binding. When you feel yourself start to scatter, you look at me. Not at the map. Not at the fire. At me. Can you do that?"

"I've kept my eyes on worse men in worse weather."

"Flatterer." But his voice cracked slightly on the word, and August realized, with a small shock, that he was frightened—not for himself, he'd proven how little he cared about that at the monastery gate, but for her. The defrocked priest who had traded his whole life for one glimpse of a living dragon was afraid of what this would cost her.

"Corvane. After it's done. If I don't remember you—"

"You will remember me. This is not childhood."

"But if the edges take more than they should. If I look at you and see a stranger." She swallowed. "Be patient with me. That's all. Don't be hurt if I have to learn you again."

For a long moment he said nothing. Then: "I have waited eleven years in exile to perform one true rite. I can wait a few weeks longer to be recognized by a friend." He cleared his throat roughly. "Besides. You'll like me again. I'm very likable. Ask anyone. Ask literally anyone except the entire Ash Choir."

The laugh that escaped her surprised them both, bright and startled in that ancient room, and Vhalorath opened one eye at the sound.

*You are certain,* the dragon said. It was not quite a question. Its voice filled the throne room like heat filling a forge, and beneath the vastness of it August heard something she hadn't noticed before: exhaustion. Bone-deep, centuries-deep tiredness. *There is a version of this where you walk away and live long and forgettable, girl. I have offered it. I offer it still. It is not nothing.*

"I know it's not nothing," August said. "I thought about nothing very carefully on the ledge." She took a breath. "But here's the thing nobody told me about being invisible your whole life. You spend so long making sure no one sees you that you forget to ask whether *you* see yourself. I don't know anymore which parts of me are real and which parts were bred into me like tricks in a dog. The roads, the quiet feet, the face people slide right past—all of it might be somebody's design." She spread her hands. "So let it go. All of it. Whatever's left after the ledger takes its due—that's mine. Actually mine. Chosen, not installed. I'd like to meet her, honestly. I hear she's got good taste in priests."

Vhalorath regarded her for a long time. Then the great head dipped, once, the closest thing to a bow a creature fused to a throne could manage.

*Aldric,* the dragon said softly, *did you hear that? She bargains like a queen.*

And from somewhere deep in the tangle of throne and scale and failing fire, a voice answered—thin, rusted, human. *"Don't. Don't let her pay for me. Please. I'm not worth—"*

"Yes, you are," August said, before she could think better of it, before wisdom or caution could catch her. "You're worth it precisely because you don't think so. That's how I know this is a fair trade and not a swindle. Now hush. Let the adults commit ritual crimes."

*"She sounds like my sister,"* Aldric whispered, wonderingly, and then the voice withdrew, and August found her eyes were burning, and decided that was acceptable, and knelt in the center of the coin-circle.

* * *

Corvane began to sing.

She had heard him sing before—at the hot springs, fragments; at the monastery, the full weight of the Ash Choir's rites while Hollow Men hammered at the gates. But this was different. This was not a rite for the mountain. This was a rite for one person, sung low and close, the melody threading through the coin-circle like smoke through a keyhole, and August felt it settle over her the way a blanket settles—weight without pressure.

"Whenever you're ready," he sang, between verses. It was absurd, conducting mortal business mid-liturgy, and she loved him a little for it.

She looked at her palm. The map looked back, gold and patient, and August spoke to it the way she'd spoken to stubborn locks and stubborn harbormasters and Brenna herself, on the one occasion she'd talked her way out of a hanging.

"All right," she said. "Here's the offer. Full payment. My childhood—every road, every roof, every winter. The village I can't already name. The ford where I learned to swim. Brenna's kitchen. The loft above the warehouse. The game about what you'd never sell. The boots. The knife. All of it. Offered freely, no taking, no dragging." Her voice held. She was almost proud of it. "In exchange: the Crown lets him go. The fire goes home to the mountain. Nobody owns it after today—not kings, not guilds, not queens of the smuggling roads. That's the deal. Take it or find another fool, because I am the last one coming up this mountain."

The map went still.

Then, slowly, the gold light gathered itself up out of her palm and stood before her—not a shape, exactly, more a *presence*, the way a road is present in the landscape even where there's no road. It did not speak. It didn't need to. She felt its attention move over her like a customs officer's hands, weighing, assessing, finding the offer—

—*good*.

"August." Corvane's voice, steady, close. "Look at me. Stone in the river. Ready?"

Her heart was slamming. Her hands were shaking. Every instinct bred into her screamed *run, be forgotten, survive*—and she looked at that fear, and examined it, and thought: *you were installed too.*

"No," she said, and grinned at Corvane with all her teeth. "But go anyway."

He sang the binding note, and the ledger took its payment.

It did not hurt, which was somehow the worst part. It was like snow melting off a roof in spring—one moment the eaves were heavy and white, the next they were bare, and you couldn't say when it happened. The roads went first, all of them at once, a lifetime of ways and turnings dissolving like sugar in rain. She grabbed for the stone-in-the-river feeling, for Corvane's voice, and held—held—as the village went, as the kitchen went, as a woman's laugh she suddenly couldn't place went, as Brenna's face softened and blurred into *someone important, someone who mattered, someone whose name is—*

*gone.*

She held. The river roared. Somewhere impossibly far away a crown shrieked—she felt it more than heard it, a sound like ice giving way on a spring lake—and the fire beneath the world turned over in its sleep and began, at last, to flow *outward*, into the veins of the mountain, and the throne room lit up gold from below, light pouring up through the floor seams, and Vhalorath made a sound that was not a roar but a sigh, the longest sigh in the history of the world—

—and August Thackeray—Iversen—*someone*—knelt in a ring of dead kings' coins, in a pool of golden light, and could not remember where she had slept as a child, or whether she had ever been a child, or—

"—with me," Corvane was saying, gripping her shoulders, his forehead nearly touching hers, his voice wrecked and singing both at once. "Stay with me, stay with me, you held, you *held*, you magnificent, impossible—"

"Did it work?" Her own voice sounded foreign. Young. New-minted. "Is he free? Is the dragon—"

Above her, wings unfolded for the first time in forty years.

August looked up, memoryless and grinning through tears she had no history to explain, as the last dragon rose from the broken throne into the light of its own returned fire—and the doors at the far end of the throne room, sealed since before her grandmother's grandmother, swung open on their own, as if the Spire itself had remembered how to welcome guests.

Down the corridor beyond, footsteps were approaching. Many of them. Human ones, stumbling, confused, calling out names—*their own names*, she somehow knew, though she didn't know how she knew—names returning to men who had walked faceless for years.

Corvane pulled her upright, laughing, crying, leaning hard on her shoulder. "Well," he managed. "Whoever you are now—"

"Ask anyone," August said. "I'm very likable."
