## 19. Unbinding

The throne room of the Spire was not what the songs said.

The songs said gold. The songs said banners and trumpets and a hundred candles burning for a hundred years. What there actually was: a vaulted hall gone gray with ash-fall, windows like blind eyes, and at the far end, coiled around a throne that had long since stopped being furniture, the dragon.

Vhalorath did not look up when we entered. I had imagined—God help me, in the rare moments I let myself imagine anything anymore—that meeting the last dragon would feel like meeting a storm. It felt instead like kneeling beside a grandfather who has fallen asleep in his chair by the fire and will not wake again, no matter how you call him. His scales were the color of old iron left out in rain. His ribs rose once every ten breaths, and each rise took so long I found myself counting, willing the next one to come. Firelight moved under his skin the way light moves under ice on a river in late winter: still there, but going somewhere else.

And on his head, fused to him as roots are fused to stone, sat the Crown.

It was smaller than I expected. That was the horror of it, really. All this—the Hollow Men, the burned guild halls, Corvane's ruined hands, my mother's voice traded away somewhere on the Weeping Stair—all of it for a circlet of black metal no wider than a bread plate, sunk halfway into the dragon's skull like a ring into a swollen finger.

"August." Corvane's voice came hoarse from where he leaned against the doorframe, one arm pressed to the wound in his side that he insisted was nothing and which bled with great conviction anyway. "The rites. Before it wakes fully. We do them now, or we do not do them at all."

I knelt at the foot of the throne and spread my palm flat against the cold floor.

The map flared.

It always flared now. Ever since the Ash Choir, ever since the mountain answered Corvane's song, the ink beneath my skin had been less like a map and more like a second heartbeat, one that lived slightly ahead of mine and kept pulling. I could feel the whole shape of it without looking—the roads that no longer existed, the rivers that had moved, the village whose name I had paid away somewhere past the glacier, the waystation where a small girl named Wren (I had learned her name; I had made myself learn it, as if names were bread you could carry) waited with hot soup and no idea what she was guarding.

The map wanted its final payment. It had wanted it since the ford, since the courier's blood dried into my palm and whispered *the Spire remembers.* And here, at the root of everything, it would collect.

"Say it back to me," Corvane said, lowering himself down beside me with a grunt that turned into a laugh that turned into a wince. "All of it. The words matter."

"The words matter," I repeated. "You're very fond of saying that."

"I am very fond of being right, and the words happen to be how."

I said them back. The opening litany of the Ash Choir, the one he had taught me in the hot springs while steam curled around us and he pretended not to be testing whether I'd run. *Fire that was given, return to the giver. Fire that was taken, return to the taken-from. Fire that was hoarded—* and here my voice always caught, because the next line was the one that mattered— *fire that was hoarded, forgive us, and go home.*

Beneath my hand, the stone began to warm.

* * *

Here is the thing nobody tells you about paying with your own memories: it doesn't hurt. That's what makes it monstrous. When the map took my home village's name, it felt like reaching into a pocket for a coin and finding the pocket sewn shut—not pain, just an absence you kept worrying at with your tongue. My mother's voice had gone the same way. I remembered that she had sung. I remembered that I had loved it. The sound itself was a hole shaped exactly like itself.

So when the Crown began to crack, and the map rose up in me like a tide asking for its due, I braced for the usual quiet theft.

What came instead was a negotiation.

*Not all,* the ink seemed to say, pressing up against the inside of my skin like a moth against a window. *Not pieces this time. A whole. Give me the childhood entire—the roads, the hiding, the years of being no one—and I will unmake what binds him.*

"That's robbery," I said aloud. Corvane looked over sharply. "That's—" I laughed, and the laugh came out cracked. "That's everything. That's the whole account."

*I know,* said the ink, gently, which was somehow worse. *I know what it is. I have carried it since the ford.*

"You've been carrying *me* since the ford," I snapped. "There's a difference."

Corvane put his good hand over mine. His fingers were scarred from thirty years of tending fires that weren't supposed to exist, and they were shaking, and he didn't pretend otherwise. "August," he said. "Listen to me. I'm going to say something honest, because I promised I'd never comfort you, and honesty is the only thing I have left that isn't broken."

"Go on, then."

"When I first saw your brand, I thought: there is a girl who has been spent like a coin by everyone who ever held her. The Guild bred her for a road. Brenna sold her. The courier chose her dying. Everyone decided what August was *for*, and none of them asked." His thumb pressed down, warm and steady. "But I have watched you pay this map five times now, and every single time, you chose what to give. Not what hurt least. What mattered most. Do you understand? They used you. But they never got to decide what you were worth. Only you get to do that. So decide. Either way, I will anchor the rite, and either way, I will drag your stubborn corpse home if it comes to that, and either way—" his mouth twitched, "—you still owe me a proper argument about whether the Third Choir was written in E major or E minor."

"E minor," I said. "It's obviously E minor."

"There. See. You have things to live for."

Above us, the dragon breathed. One breath. Two. On the third, firelight flared beneath his scales like sunrise coming early, and the Crown screamed.

Not the dragon. The Crown. A sound like a bell dropped down a well, like a kingdom's worth of locked doors opening at once, and I understood suddenly why the Hollow Men had faces like smoothed-over clay: the Crown had been eating *names* for two hundred years, and it was full, and it was afraid.

I closed my eyes and gave it my childhood.

* * *

I gave it the road out of Thornfell that only I knew, the one that threaded between the two dead pines and the cairn that wasn't a cairn. I gave it the smell of the smuggler's warehouse, tar and pepper and Brenna's pipe smoke. I gave it learning to pick locks at six and lie at seven and disappear at eight, and the pride I wore like armor over the loneliness underneath, and the loneliness too, all of it, every year of being forgettable, every door that slid shut behind me without noticing me, every time invisibility saved my life and starved something else in me instead.

I gave it the name of the place I was born. Whatever it was. I couldn't check anymore. That was the trick of it—you paid, and then you couldn't even audit the payment.

The ink drank like a river drinking snowmelt, and it sang while it drank, and its song was the opening litany, *fire that was given, return to the giver,* and Corvane's scarred hands gripped mine and his voice wove through mine and the mountain beneath the Spire woke up.

I felt it before I heard it: a vast slow warmth rising through the rock, the dragon-veins that ran under the whole range like a second system of rivers, veins that kings had dammed and capped and bound for centuries. Vhalorath's fire, which should have been in the mountains all along, which the Covenant had rerouted into a circlet of black metal because a crown that held fire could hold a kingdom.

*Fire that was taken,* we sang, *return to the taken-from.*

The Crown split down the middle with a sound like the world's oldest ice giving way in spring.

And the Hollow Men—

Oh, the Hollow Men. There must have been forty of them filling the great doors behind us, come up the Stair after us through the storm, faceless and patient as weather. When the Crown cracked, every one of them stopped mid-stride. And then, like wax held near a flame, their faces *came back*. Features surfacing. Eyes remembering their color. A woman with grey braids gasped like a diver breaking water. A young man with a farmer's shoulders fell to his knees and began to sob a name—over and over, a woman's name, someone waiting somewhere, someone who had probably been told he was dead for twenty years. They collapsed into themselves, into their remembered selves, and the sound of forty people remembering who they were at once was the loudest and gentlest thing I have ever heard.

One of them—an old man, a courier's build, and something in the way he stood made my chest ache though I couldn't have said why—looked straight at me across the hall. He touched two fingers to his heart. The old Wayfarers' salute. Then he turned and walked out into the snow, free, toward whatever was left of his life.

*Fire that was hoarded,* Corvane and I sang, voices ragged now, *forgive us, and go home.*

Vhalorath opened his eyes.

They were the color of the forge at dawn. They found me kneeling at his feet, and I felt the full weight of an ancient attention settle on me like snowfall, and inside it, unbelievably, I felt something like gratitude.

*Little thief of roads,* the dragon said, and his voice was not sound so much as heat given meaning. *Two hundred years I have fed this kingdom's fields and been fed upon in turn. He bargained fairly, your king. He simply did not know that crowns do not bargain back.* The great head lowered until one ancient eye filled half my vision. *You could leave. I offered you that. Live, and walk away, and let the fire gutter out with me, and let the land learn hunger again.*

"I know what you offered."

*And yet here you are. Broke. Paid down to the studs. You have nothing left to trade, child. Why stay?*

"Because the story we were handed is a bad one," I said. My voice shook and I let it. "Leave, or kill him, or watch the land die—that's not a choice, that's a trap with three doors painted on it. So I'm choosing a fourth door. Your fire doesn't belong in a crown and it doesn't belong in a corpse. It belongs in the mountain. Let it *go home.*"

For a moment, silence. Even Corvane had stopped breathing.

Then Vhalorath laughed. It rolled through the floor and up through my boots and out through the blind windows, and somewhere far below I swear the glacier shifted to make room.

*A fourth door,* he rumbled. *In two hundred years, four hundred priests, a dozen kings, and one smuggler's brat finds the fourth door.* The great coils loosened, scale by scale, from the throne—from Aldric, who slumped forward like a man setting down a load carried since boyhood, alive, breathing, *unbound*. *Very well. Hold the rite, little thief. Sing it to the end. This will not be quiet.*

"No," I agreed. "I don't imagine it will be."

Vhalorath drew the last breath of his long captivity—a breath that went on and on, drawing fire up from the deepest part of himself, every spark he had hoarded and banked and starved for—and then, instead of breathing it out in flame, he *let go.*

The fire poured out of him like water finding its old bed after the dam breaks. I felt it rush past me, through me, singing the litany faster than any throat could, pouring down through the throne room floor and out along the dragon-veins in every direction, north and south and deep under the Weeping Stair and out beneath the glacier where Corvane had once shown me the sleeping warmth. The mountain drank it. The mountain *wanted* it. Every vein lit like dawn moving across a landscape, and above us the Spire shuddered, and snow avalanched off distant peaks in long white ribbons, and it did not end, and it did not end, and then—

Quiet.

Real quiet. The kind that comes after a fever breaks.

Vhalorath lay still around the ruined throne, and he was not a dragon anymore, quite—he was going golden and translucent at the edges, becoming something like ember-light wearing the memory of a shape—but his eyes stayed open, and they were peaceful, and they watched the fire travel out through the dark mountains like a father watching children scatter happily across a field.

*Well done,* he said, softer than wind. *Now go and see what you have bought, and what it has cost you. And child—one more thing. The courier at the ford. He asked me, at the end, to tell you—*

But the light was already thinning, and the words thinned with it, and I caught only the shape of the sentence and not its contents, and something in me howled at that, and something older and calmer in me said: *later. There will be later. Go see.*

* * *

Aldric was awake.

He sat on the steps below the shattered throne, wrapped in Corvane's cloak, looking younger than his portrait coins and infinitely older than his years, the way men look when a weight they've carried so long it became their skeleton has finally been lifted. He was crying without any noise at all, just tears running steadily down his face, and when he saw me he tried to stand, failed, and settled for pressing his fist to his breastbone.

"Fourteen years," he said. His voice was rust. "Fourteen years I screamed inside my own head and the kingdom heard only wisdom. I begged it to stop. I begged the priests, the gods, anyone. And then a smuggler's apprentice walks in and cracks it like an egg." He laughed wetly. "What do I even owe you?"

"Nothing," I said. "It wasn't a transaction."

"It was *entirely* a transaction, and I intend to honor it at ruinous interest, starting with the fact that I can apparently hear the mountain now, and it is telling me, in no uncertain terms, that it wants its hot springs back from a defrocked priest currently bleeding on my floor."

"He's fine," I said. "He says it's nothing."

"It is *not* nothing," Corvane announced from the doorway with tremendous dignity, and then sat down rather abruptly, grinning like a fool. Around him, the unmade Hollow Men were stirring, wrapping each other in cloaks, weeping, laughing, asking each other's names like guests arriving late to their own reunion.

I stood in the middle of it all—the freed king, the fading dragon-light, the remembered faces, the mountain humming underfoot with borrowed fire—and I reached, as I had reached a thousand times on a thousand roads, for the map in my head. For the paths. The shortcuts. The sureness.

Nothing.

No roads. No village name. No mother's voice. No childhood at all—just a smooth blank stretch where twenty-some years used to be, like fresh snowfall over an old track. I knew facts about myself the way you know facts about a stranger in a story: born in Thornfell, raised by smugglers, trained by the Guild. I knew *that* I had been invisible. I could no longer remember how it felt, and God forgive me, some small traitorous part of me was relieved, and a larger part grieved the relief.

Corvane limped over and stood beside me, following my gaze out the blind windows, where the mountains glowed faintly along their hidden veins like coals under ash.

"Well?" he said. "How does it feel? Being no one in particular?"

I considered it honestly, which was all I had left, and it turned out it was enough.

"Like a road with no map," I said. "Which means, as of tonight, I suppose I'll just have to see where I'm going."

Corvane snorted. "Terrible line. You'll want to workshop that before you make it your motto." He winced, pressed his hand to his side, and nodded down the dark stairwell, where torches were sputtering alight one by one, all on their own, as if the building itself were waking. "Come on, then. First rule of surviving a miracle: get somewhere warm before the shock arrives. And August—someone has to tell that king what happened to his kingdom while he was dreaming. Forty ex-Hollow Men, a dragon-shaped sunset, and a girl who paid for it all. I'll handle the dragon theology. You handle—"

He stopped. Looked at me properly. And whatever he saw in my face made his grin soften into something gentler.

"You handle beginning," he said. "Whenever you're ready. There's no hurry. The mountain, I'm told, keeps excellent records."

Below us, the first true firelight in fourteen years climbed the walls of the Spire, and somewhere in it—warm, patient, unmistakably alive—I heard the mountains begin, very quietly, to remember me back.
