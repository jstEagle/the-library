## 20. Ashes and Embers

Corvane woke to the smell of bread.

This was, in itself, a miracle worth recording. For eleven years his mornings had smelled of sulfur and wet stone and, on bad days, of his own scorched vestments. Bread meant someone had baked it. Someone baking bread meant there was an oven. An oven meant fire that did not come from a dragon's dying throat, tended by human hands, in a place where people intended to keep living.

He lay still a while longer on the cot they'd dragged into the Spire's lower halls—still, because moving hurt, and because he wanted to memorize the moment before pain could editorialize. His chest was wrapped to the armpits in linen gone stiff with old salve. Every breath pulled at the wound the Hollow Men's captain had opened across his ribs, and every breath also carried woodsmoke and yeast and, faintly, the mineral warmth of the mountain itself.

The mountain was breathing. He could feel it through the floor now, a slow pulse under the stone like a sleeper's heartbeat. Three weeks since the Unbinding, and he still woke each dawn expecting the veins to have gone cold overnight, expecting to press his palm to the rock and find nothing but geology.

Instead: warmth. Instead: *Bram*, as the children were already calling it, the old word for ember, dug out of some grandmother's lullaby and set loose in the world again.

"About time," said a voice near his feet. "I was starting to think you'd learned to sleep like a proper priest. Lazy. Ceremonial."

August sat in the corner with her boots off—a sight that still unsettled him more than any rite—and a basket of bandages in her lap she was very pointedly not looking at. She'd taken to sitting with him during his waking hours without ever admitting she was sitting with him. She brought mending, or maps, or nothing at all, and if asked, she claimed she was guarding him, though from what, in a fortress full of convalescing soldiers and one abdicating king, she never specified.

"I'm not a priest," Corvane said, and swung his legs off the cot, and immediately regretted it with his whole body. "Priests serve something dead. I'm—" He searched for the word. It kept sliding away behind the pain. "A caretaker. A groundskeeper. A man who shovels after a very large animal."

"A shepherd," August said.

"I was going to say janitor."

"You would."

He grinned, which pulled at the stitches, which was worth it. Then he held out his arms like a child demanding lifting, because pride was for men whose ribs hadn't been opened by a faceless thing wearing a dead soldier's memory, and August sighed the sigh of the long-suffering and hauled him upright with the ease of someone who had once carried him up four hundred feet of vertical wall in a storm.

He would never stop being faintly humiliated by that. He would also never stop being grateful. The two feelings had settled into an arrangement, like old married couples.

* * *

The lower halls of the Spire had been servants' quarters once, before the Crown sealed everything below the throne room. Now they filled again: soldiers who'd woken from their hollowing with no idea what year it was; refugees drifting up from the valleys as word spread that the fires under the mountains had changed hands—or rather, had stopped having hands on them at all. Aldric's stewards, those few who hadn't fled when the Crown cracked, moved among them with ledgers and harried faces, trying to feed a growing town inside a tower built for one man and his parasite.

Corvane walked the halls slowly, leaning on a staff August had cut for him from ash wood and then whittled, over several evenings, into something that was either a snake or a very ambitious eel. He stopped at the warm seams in the walls—the new veins, thin as rootlets, glowing a soft orange where the stone had split to let the fire through. Each morning there were more of them. Each morning they'd crept a little further from the great chamber where Vhalorath had poured itself into the mountain's bones.

He pressed his palm to one and listened, the way he'd been trained to listen to dying things, and heard—not dying. Not living, exactly, either. Something between. A fire that no longer needed to be a creature to go on burning.

*You are welcome here,* the mountain seemed to say, in the way stone says anything, which is slowly and without much interest in your opinion. *Mind the mortar.*

"The Ash Choir used to fast forty days to hear less than that," he told the wall.

The wall declined to comment.

In the afternoons he taught. This had started as an accident: two children had followed him down to the vein-rooms out of curiosity, and rather than send them up—he'd never been good at sending children anywhere—he'd put them to work carrying water and salve, and by week's end there were nine of them, and by month's end thirty, sitting on benches in the old refectory while he explained how to read heat in stone, how to tell a vein that was settling from one that was searching, how to approach a warm seam with respect and a bucket of sand.

"You're rebuilding the order," August observed one afternoon, watching from the doorway with her arms crossed.

"I'm running a daycare with extra steps."

"Mm." She came in and took the chalk from his hand and drew, on the slate board, a quick sure line: the mountain's spine, the branching veins, the Spire at the crown of it all. She drew it from memory—which was to say, she drew it from the land itself, because since the Unbinding she didn't remember learning the mountains' shapes anymore; she simply knew them, the way you know where your own hand is in the dark. "The guilds are asking questions. Merchants want routes along the warm roads. Someone in Thornfell wants to bottle the spring water and sell it as a cure for baldness."

"Does it work?"

"No idea. I told them yes. They were annoying."

He laughed, and paid for it in stitches, and decided it had still been worth it.

* * *

Aldric came down on the first day of thaw.

Corvane heard him before he saw him—the particular uneven gait of a man whose body had spent twenty years being piloted by something else and was only now relearning ownership of its own feet. The king stood in the doorway of the vein-room holding his own hands as if unsure they were his, which, Corvane reflected, they barely were.

"Master Corvane." Aldric's voice was quiet and rough, a voice that had screamed itself hoarse somewhere far inside its own skull. "May I?"

"It's your house."

"It was its cage." Aldric crossed the room slowly and knelt beside the nearest vein, and Corvane watched the man watch the fire with an expression Corvane knew intimately from mirrors: the look of someone taking attendance of everything that had happened while they weren't consulted. "They tell me I signed edicts. Twenty years of edicts. I remember perhaps six of them." He turned his hand over, palm up, toward the warmth. "It tells me things, sometimes. The mountain. In dreams. What the fire saw while I was—away."

"What does it say?"

"That I wept, the first year. Every night. And then the Crown learned to take the weeping too, and after that I don't know what I did, because there wasn't a me left to do it." Aldric closed his fist, gently, as if around something small and alive. "Vhalorath tried to spare me, at the end. Did August tell you? It burned its own mind ahead of the Crown's, section by section, so there'd be a man left over when it broke. That's why it was dying faster than it should have. It was spending itself on my behalf."

Corvane said nothing. There was nothing to say that the fire in the wall wasn't already saying better.

"I mean to abdicate," Aldric said. "Not die—August has forbidden it, in terms I won't repeat in a holy man's presence—but step down. A council. The provinces, the guilds, the—" he hesitated, and something almost like humor surfaced in his ruined face, "—the waystations. There's a girl. Sera. Eight years old. August vouches for her, and I've learned that when August vouches for someone, arguing is a form of self-harm."

"Sera." Corvane smiled. "The one from the empty town. How is she?"

"Loud," said Aldric, with unmistakable reverence. "She has opinions about the council chambers. She says the chairs are too tall and the kings who sat in them were probably shorter than they claimed. She may be the sanest person in this building."

"She'll do."

That evening, in the great hall, Aldric set the Crown—the cracked, cold, emptied thing—on a table before the assembled survivors, and renounced it, and renounced every edict signed in his name, and asked pardon of no one and forgiveness of everyone, which Corvane thought showed a decent grasp of theology for a man who'd slept through two decades of it. When he finished, Sera climbed onto her too-tall chair and announced that the first law of the new council should be that nobody got to wear anything on their head that talked to them, and the hall laughed, and something in the stones laughed too, a low warm rumble that made the candle flames lean sideways.

Corvane felt it pass through him like a blessing and thought: *the Choir sang to a corpse for a thousand years, and it took a smuggler's apprentice and a child to make the mountain laugh.*

There was a sermon in that. He resolved never to give it. Some truths went sour the moment you preached them.

* * *

His ribs healed the way mountains heal: slowly, grudgingly, with visible scarring. By mid-spring he could walk the outer paths, and he began the work that would occupy the rest of his life whether he admitted it or not.

The veins needed tending. Not binding—never binding again—but tending, the way a riverbank is tended, not to own the water but to keep it from eating the village. Where a vein ran too close to a root cellar, you laid cooling stone. Where the ground swelled with new heat, you vented it gently into bathhouses and hearths and, in one memorable case, a cheese-maker's aging cave that produced, within a season, a wheel so transcendent that three separate religious movements claimed it as a sign.

Corvane wrote it all down. Rites, revised. Prayers, rewritten in the second person instead of the third, because you did not speak *about* a living mountain, you spoke *to* it. He struck the fasting requirements entirely on the grounds that hunger made priests stupid and short-tempered, a fact the old Choir had mistaken for holiness for centuries.

August found him one evening on the high terrace, copying by lantern-light, and dropped onto the bench beside him with the groan of a woman whose knees had opinions about stairs.

"Brenna sent a letter," she said.

He kept writing. "And?"

"And it's three pages long and mostly insults, and at the bottom it says the southern roads are open and the passes are safe and if I ever want to come down and see what's left of the warehouse district, there's a bed with my name on it." August turned the letter over in her fingers. "She doesn't apologize. She explains. As if those were the same thing."

"Are they?"

"No." A long pause. The lantern flame leaned toward the warm seam in the terrace wall, lazy as a cat. "But I think I'll go anyway. Not yet. After the thaw roads. Someone should walk them first and see what the land remembers." She glanced at him sidelong. "You could come. See the lowlands. Eat something that isn't mountain goat."

"Tempting. But the eastern vein is branching toward the village at Coldford, and if I don't teach them to vent it before summer, their well will boil and they'll blame me personally, and they'll be right to."

"You're staying."

"I'm staying." He said it and heard, with mild surprise, that it sounded like a choice rather than a sentence. Eleven years of exile, and exile had quietly become a post. "Someone has to shovel after the very large animal."

August snorted. Then, quieter, in the voice she used maybe twice a year: "For what it's worth—I don't remember why I climbed the wall. Carrying you. I know that I did it. I don't know why." She looked out at the darkening valley, where the new veins glowed along the mountainside like a constellation fallen to earth. "Sometimes I think I gave up the wrong memories. Sometimes I think there are no wrong ones. Only ones you build around."

Corvane set down his pen. "The Choir had a saying. *The fire does not mourn the log.*"

"That's terrible."

"It's a terrible order. We also had a saying about cheese, but it doesn't translate."

She laughed—really laughed, head back, the sound bouncing off the warm stone—and the mountain rumbled beneath them in what he had come to recognize, after months of listening, as its own version of the same thing.

Below them, in the halls of the Spire, a council without a king argued cheerfully about grain tariffs. Above them, the stars came out over a mountain that breathed. And in the morning, Corvane would rise, and eat bread, and go tend the fire that belonged to no one—which was, he understood at last, the only kind of fire that had ever truly belonged to everyone.

He picked his pen back up.

At the top of the fresh page, he wrote the first line of the new rites: *Here is what the mountain asks of you: nothing. Here is what it offers: everything. Here is how to say thank you—*

And paused, pen hovering, because he realized he didn't know yet.

He had a feeling August would.
