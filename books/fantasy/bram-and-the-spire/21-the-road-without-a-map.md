## 21. The Road Without a Map

The first thing I learned about walking without my memories was that my feet had not been consulted about the arrangement.

I discovered this three days out from the Spire, on a slope above the Ashvale, when I stepped around a slab of granite that I had no reason to step around—no memory of it, no name for it, nothing in my head but gray fog where the smuggler roads used to live—and my boots simply went where they needed to go. My left foot found the hidden ledge. My hand found the root. By the time my mind caught up, I was already past the place where the mountain liked to shrug travelers into the river.

"Huh," I said, to nobody.

Corvane, picking his way behind me with the careful dignity of a man who had nearly died four times and intended never to be casual about footing again, looked up. "Problem?"

"No," I said. "The opposite of a problem."

He climbed up beside me and squinted at the path ahead, which wound down through the pines in a way that no honest road should wind—doubling back on itself, slipping under deadfall, vanishing and reappearing like a rumor. To anyone else it would have been impassable. To me it should have been worse than impassable, because I couldn't remember learning it. Every trick Brenna's people had beaten into me as a child—the cut-throughs, the goat tracks, the ways over the Thornfell that didn't appear on any king's map—had gone into the map's ledger along with my mother's voice and the name of the village where I was born.

And yet here I was, walking them like breathing.

"It's the fire-veins," Corvane said, when I tried to explain it to him that evening over a fire that lit itself too eagerly, leaping up out of the ground wherever I crouched as if the mountain were leaning in to listen. He turned his hands toward the warmth. The scars across his palms glowed faintly gold in the dark, old dragon-blessing waking now that there was something nearby to bless. "You paid the map everything you knew. But you paid it *out*, do you see? Not destroyed. The roads are still in you somewhere—or rather, you're still in them. You gave yourself to the land, child. The land is only returning the courtesy."

"That's very poetic," I said.

"I'm a priest. It's practically fraud."

I laughed, and he pretended not to look pleased about it, and the fire crackled companionably between us. That was another thing I was learning: that grief did not vanish just because you'd spent it. It sat in me like an empty room in a house I couldn't quite picture. Some nights I reached for my childhood the way your tongue reaches for a missing tooth, and found nothing, and lay awake feeling the shape of the absence.

But other nights—more of them, now—I felt what had come instead. When I put my palm flat on the stone before sleeping, I could feel the slow pulse under the mountains, the veins of ember-light Corvane tended like a shepherd, running all through the Thornfell now, no longer hoarded in one dying chest on one high throne. The land remembered everything. It remembered the roads because I had walked them; it remembered my childhood because I had paid with it; and it seemed to feel some responsibility about the whole transaction, the way a good merchant feels when a customer overpays by accident.

So when I lost the trail the next morning—and I did lose it, standing at a fork where my memory offered two blank paths and my pride offered several unhelpful suggestions—I did the thing that would have been unthinkable a year ago.

I stopped. I closed my eyes. I asked.

Not aloud. Not exactly. More like the way you ask a door to open, or ask a friend to pass the bread—with intent, and trust, and a small willingness to be embarrassed if it doesn't work.

The ground under my feet warmed. And the way forward simply became *obvious*—not knowledge arriving, not a map unrolling, just the plain and sudden certainty that the left-hand path went down to a washed-out bridge and the right-hand one switchbacked up through a hollow full of rowan trees, and that I had hidden in those rowans twice before I was ten years old, except that I hadn't, because I couldn't have, because I didn't remember being ten years old—

"Stop that," Corvane said gently, from behind me. "You're doing the thing where you argue with a gift."

"The gift ate my childhood."

"And then apologized," he said. "Extensively. In fire."

I snorted and took the right-hand path. The rowans were there, gold-leaved and patient, and when I passed through them something in the light shifted warmly, like a nod from an old acquaintance whose face I couldn't place. I walked on, and the road came to me as I needed it—not remembered, but *recognized*, the way you recognize kindness when you see it even if you've forgotten every specific kindness ever done to you.

We made better time than I expected after that. Word, apparently, travels underground faster than above it. At the second waystation south of the Weeping Stair, the keeper—an old woman who pressed barley bread and pickled onions on us and refused payment—told me that travelers on the new paths had taken to leaving small offerings at forks and fords. A ribbon on a branch. A coin sunk in a spring. "For Bram," she said, using the old word for ember, the name the children had given the fire-veins. "For the roads. They say the mountain shows the way now, if you're honest about where you're going."

"And if you're not honest?" I asked.

She shrugged. "Then it shows you where you actually are. Which is usually worse."

I thought about that for a long time that night. It sounded, when I was honest with myself—which the mountain apparently insisted on—like a fairer bargain than any I'd ever struck before. Brenna's roads had required that I be nothing: unseen, unnamed, unremembered. Bram's roads required only that I be true. I had spent thirty years perfecting invisibility and three weeks learning that the world would meet me halfway if I let it be seen meeting me.

Which is why, when the southern trails finally brought me down out of the high passes toward Saltmere—the smoke-stained harbor town where I had grown up, except that I had no idea whether Saltmere was really where I'd grown up, since the name of the actual village was gone—I did not take the smugglers' bypass around the walls. I walked in through the main gate, in daylight, with my hood down, and let everyone see me.

Nobody did, much. Old habits die hard in a town built on not noticing things. But the gate guard glanced at me, and glanced again, and frowned the frown of a man trying to place a face, and waved me through with a troubled look. It was, I decided, a start.

* * *

Brenna's court had moved. Of course it had. But I didn't need to ask anyone where; I walked the waterfront with my senses open and the town told me—a tug of attention toward the old rope-walk, a warmth in the cobbles outside a shuttered counting-house, gulls wheeling with suspicious purpose over a warehouse that officially stored nothing. The land whispered, and I listened, and within an hour I stood before a door I had once slipped through a hundred times without being seen, and knocked on it. Loudly. With the iron ring. Like a person.

The peephole slid open. Shut. Bolts clattered, more of them than there used to be, and the door swung wide on Marta, Brenna's door-warden, who had taught me to throw a knife when I was—well, at some age I could no longer name. She stared at me for a long moment, and her hand drifted toward her belt.

"They said you were dead," she managed.

"Everyone did," I agreed. "It was a reasonable conclusion. Is she in?"

"She—" Marta's eyes went to my right hand, where the map's brand had faded to a pale silver whorl in my palm, like a scar left by moonlight. Whatever the rumors were, my hand confirmed them. Her face did something complicated, and settled on misery. "August. About the warehouse—"

"I know," I said. "I'm not here for that. Tell her I'll wait."

Brenna kept me waiting forty minutes, which for Brenna was practically an apology in itself; in the old days she'd have kept me waiting two days just to establish the weather. Then Marta led me up the stairs to the long room with the map table—empty now, its charts burned, its pins drawn—and Brenna stood at the window with her back to me, watching the harbor.

She had aged. That was the first thing, and it hit me harder than I expected, because in my memory—if I could be said to have a memory of her, which was mostly muscle-memory and a smell of tar and cardamom—she had been permanent. Mountains aged slowly. So did smuggler queens. But her shoulders had come down from their old confident set, and her hair was more gray than black now, and when she turned around, the shrewd dark eyes that had priced everything in my childhood had bags under them like a woman who wasn't sleeping.

"You look terrible," I said.

"You look like a legend," she said. "It's disgusting. Sit down."

I stayed standing. She noticed. She poured two cups of plum wine anyway and set one near me, and we performed the old ritual of ignoring each other's hospitality until it became rude, and then I picked up the cup, and something in the room loosened half a notch.

"The guilds sent word," she said at last. "About the Spire. About Aldric. About—" she gestured vaguely at all of me, "—whatever you are now. The council's sending envoys. Everyone wants to know what the woman who freed the king intends to do next."

"What do you want me to intend?"

"Don't do that," she said sharply. "Don't give it back to me. I don't get to—"

"You sold me," I said. Not loud. Not angry. Just setting the fact on the table between us, where we could both look at it. "Thirty years of raising me off the streets, teaching me, feeding me, calling me yours—and the moment the bounty came, you weighed me against coin and convenience and sold me. And I burned your warehouse getting out, and I've spent a lot of miles deciding what I felt about it, and here's what I've decided."

Her jaw tightened. "Say it, then."

"I don't forgive you," I said. "And I don't hate you. Both of those require something I gave away on top of the Spire, and I'm not sorry it's gone. What I have instead is this: I saw it. All of it. The pact the guilds made with the Crown, the way you groomed me for the road without telling me, the arithmetic where I was cheaper spent than saved." I took a sip of the wine. It was good. She always did have good taste in things she shouldn't own. "Nobody else remembers all of it. The guilds will tell it so it flatters them. You'll tell it so it flatters you. The Hollow Men who survived will tell it however their poor cracked heads can manage. But I was there, and I'm choosing to stay the one who was there. That's all I'm offering, Brenna. Not forgiveness. Not vengeance. Witness."

Brenna stared at me for a long moment. Then she laughed—one short, cracked sound with no joy in it—and sat down heavily in her chair, suddenly looking every hour of her age.

"Witness," she repeated. "Gods. That's worse than vengeance. Vengeance I could pay off." She rubbed her eyes. "You want to know the joke, girl? I told myself I was protecting you. Keeping you ignorant of what you were for—that was mercy, in my accounting. A blind beast goes to slaughter easier. And then the day came and I told myself the price was too high to refuse, and that was also protection, of a kind—of me." Her hands flattened on the table. "I've done sums my whole life. That's what a smuggler queen is, in the end. A woman who does sums. And every sum I ever did about you was wrong, because I kept putting myself on the side of the equation that mattered."

"Yes," I said. "You did."

She flinched. I let her. Witnessing, I was discovering, was not the same as being gentle. It meant letting people sit in the truth without hurrying to pull them out of it, and Brenna, of all people, deserved the full weight of that courtesy.

After a while she straightened, and some of the old steel came back into her spine—not the old self, exactly, but a new thing forged from the wreckage, which I supposed was the only way anyone got a self. "The council envoys arrive in a week," she said. "They'll want the guilds' old charters reviewed. The Crown-pacts torn up, probably. There's talk of the waystations going independent—your waystations, the ones at the Spire's foot." A pause. "There's talk of you a great deal, generally. Half of it nonsense."

"Only half? I'm improving."

"They say the children call the fire in the mountains *Bram*," she said. "That the roads move for honest travelers now. That a woman walks them who has no past, and the mountain makes up the difference." Her eyes searched my face, looking for the girl she'd raised, and I watched her fail to find her. I don't think it hurt her the way it would have hurt me, a year ago. I think it relieved her. "Is it true? That you can't remember?"

"Not the beginning," I said. "The rest I'm making as I go. Turns out that counts."

Brenna nodded slowly, and rose, and did something I had never seen her do in my entire life: she bowed. Small, and stiff, and unmistakable—a queen's acknowledgment of something she could no longer price. "Then witness this much," she said quietly. "Saltmere stands with the council. The guilds' pacts end here, in this room, tonight, if you'll stand witness to the burning of them."

"I will," I said. And then, because identity rebuilt from choice apparently included small choices too: "Also, I'm keeping the wine."

That surprised a real laugh out of her, rusty and startled, and for a moment—just a moment—the old court flickered back to life between us, warm and crooked, before settling into whatever new shape we would eventually become. Perhaps nothing. Perhaps something smaller and truer. The mountain, I suspected, would approve of either, so long as I was honest about which one I wanted.

I stayed three days. On the third evening, walking the seawall as the lamps came on across the harbor, I felt it: a pulse from the north, faint and bright, running up through the soles of my boots like a knock at a door. Bram, reaching all the way down from the Spire to touch my heel. Except—it wasn't only the mountain's usual hum. This was sharper. Directed. A thread of heat pulling north with the insistence of a hand tugging my sleeve, and beneath it, carried the way the land carries things now, a sense I can only describe as *someone asking for me*.

Corvane, I knew without asking, had felt it too. Sure enough, there he stood at the end of the seawall, silhouetted against the last light, his scarred hands already glowing faintly.

"August," he called. "You'd better come look at this. The fire's found something. Or—" he paused, and I heard the wonder in his voice even across the distance, "—someone's taught it to deliver messages. And it says there's a child at the foot of the Spire who's asking for you by name."

A child. Asking for me.

I turned north, toward the mountains, and the road home opened under my feet like a held breath.
