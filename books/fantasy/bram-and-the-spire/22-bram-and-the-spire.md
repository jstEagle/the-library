## 22. Bram and the Spire

The kettle sang before dawn, as it always did.

August Iversen had learned, in her years at the foot of the Spire, that a waystation lived or died by its mornings. A traveler who woke to cold porridge and silence would grumble all day and tell everyone at the next inn that the place was falling apart. A traveler who woke to fresh bread and the smell of juniper smoke on the hearth would remember the mountain kindly, even through the blizzard that nearly killed him on the Weeping Stair.

So she rose in the dark, as she had since she was—well, since she'd been anyone at all—and put the kettle on.

The children came down like an avalanche in slow motion. There were four of them now: Pip and Sela, who were brother and sister and argued about it constantly; Odo, who was enormous for eight years old and gentle as thawing snow; and little Wren, who had been left on the doorstep one spring morning wrapped in a blanket embroidered with a name no one could read but which August suspected meant *trouble*.

"Bram's awake," Wren announced, sliding into her chair. "I saw it."

"You saw steam off the south vein," said Sela, pouring milk.

"It's different when Bram's awake. It breathes."

August set down the bread knife and looked out the window. Below the terraced garden, below the switchback road, the mountainside glowed faintly through its seams of stone—a lattice of warm light running up toward the Spire like veins under skin. The old word for it, the word Corvane had taught her with tears in his voice the first time he'd knelt beside a vent and felt the heat answer his palm, was *bram*. Ember. The children had never known any other name for the fire-veins, because August had refused to give them one until they could say the old word properly, and by then it had stuck.

"Did you thank it?" she asked Wren.

"I thanked it," Wren said, with the gravity of a small priestess. "Bram doesn't mind if you forget sometimes. It's very patient."

"It is," August agreed. "More patient than your sister."

"Pip started it."

"Pip always starts it," Odo rumbled contentedly, already eating.

Outside, the first travelers of the season were picking their way up from the valley road: a wool merchant with two mules, a pair of pilgrims heading for the ruined monastery where Corvane still kept his choir—not of priests anymore, but of anyone who wished to learn the listening rites—and a nervous young surveyor from the lowland guilds who had written ahead asking, very politely, whether it was true that the paths through Thornfell changed overnight.

August had answered him honestly: yes, often, and that was rather the point.

She stepped out into the blue morning to meet them, and the merchant squinted at her the way people always did—the way of a man trying to place a face he was certain he should know. It happened less than it used to. The kingdom had mostly stopped looking for the woman who had unbound its king; there wasn't much point looking for someone whose own history had been spent as payment. But occasionally someone would stare too long, hunting for recognition, and August would offer them bread and let the moment pass.

"Roads are good this time of year," she told the surveyor, handing him tea while his hands still shook from the climb. "Better than good. They're *awake*. You'll want to walk the high path past the third cairn, not the old trade route—the old route follows a memory of the mountain, not the mountain itself."

"A memory of—" He blinked. "Forgive me, mistress, but how does one map a memory?"

"One doesn't." She smiled. "You ask the land nicely, and you write down what it says."

* * *

The school, if you could call it that, met in the afternoons in the long room with the south-facing windows. August taught whatever needed teaching: letters, sums, knots, the difference between weather that kills and weather that only threatens to. But the heart of every lesson was the walking.

They walked the lower trails together, all five of them strung out along paths that hadn't existed ten years ago, learning to feel for the faint warmth underfoot that meant Bram was near. The children pressed their palms to warm stones and listened—really listened, the way Corvane had taught her—with the unselfconscious seriousness of the young.

"The trick," August told them, for perhaps the hundredth time, "is that you don't find the path. The path finds *you*. Your job is only to be worth finding."

"How do you be worth finding?" Wren asked.

"Honesty helps. So does carrying extra biscuits."

Odo raised his hand. "My gran says you used to know every smuggler's road in Thornfell. That you could cross the whole range blindfolded."

"Your gran exaggerates," said August, who had no idea whether she did, because August had no gran she could remember, and no smuggler roads either. She knew what the books said about her—Corvane had written some of them, careful and loving accounts of the Unbinding, and Aldric himself, gray and quiet in his retirement, had confirmed the shape of it. But the inside of those years was gone. Her childhood, her mother's voice, the village whose name had been the first payment, Brenna's warehouse burning behind her—all of it spent, all of it gone into the map that had cracked open the Crown.

She had expected the loss to hollow her. Instead, most days, it felt like traveling light.

What remained was stranger and better: the land remembered for her. When she walked the high passes now, the mountain showed her the way—not as memory, but as simple sight, the way you see a door when you enter a room. The roads she had paid away lived on in Bram's warm veins, and Bram, generous beyond anything the old dragon-priests had dared hope, shared them freely with anyone who asked kindly.

That had been Vhalorath's last gift, though the dragon would have hated the word *gift*. The great beast, dying on its throne, had poured its fire not out but *down*—into the mountain's bones—so that no king could ever hold it, no crown could ever bind it, and no child would ever again be bred in secret to carry a map inked in blood.

No one owned the fire now. Everyone was warmed by it.

"Tell us about the Spire," Sela said, as they crested the ridge and the tower itself came into view above them—shattered at its crown where the Crown had broken, its upper halls open to the sky, gardens growing wild in rooms where kings had once schemed. "Did you really climb the outside wall? In a storm?"

"I really did."

"Were you scared?"

August considered the question honestly, as she owed it to be. "Terrified," she said. "But here's the thing about fear, Sela. It's just love pointed at yourself. And there are days when love needs pointing somewhere else."

Sela frowned at this the way she frowned at sums, which meant she'd be turning it over for a week. Good. August had been turned over for years herself, once. It had made her sturdier.

* * *

Corvane arrived at midsummer, as he always did, driving a cart full of books and honey and complaints about the state of the pilgrim trail.

He was older now, gray to the roots, his burn-scarred hands steadier than they'd been since the battle at the monastery gate where he'd held the door against thirty Hollow Men and very nearly died of it. The dragon-blessing in his scars had kept him alive; the years and the work had made the keeping worthwhile. He still wore the faded vestments of the Ash Choir, patched a dozen times, because he said a priest ought to look like his faith: durable, slightly scorched, mended often.

"You've let the garden go feral," he announced, kissing her cheek.

"I've let the garden be free," August corrected. "There's a difference. Ask any plant."

"Free things need pruning, August. That's the entire discipline of gardening. And of priesthood. And," he added, settling onto the bench in the kitchen with a groan, "of friendship, which is why I'm going to tell you that Aldric is coming to visit and you can't avoid him this time."

Her hands went still over the teapot.

"He writes," she said. "Every season. I write back."

"You write back three lines, unsigned, via a courier, like a woman hiding from a debt." Corvane accepted his cup. "He abdicated, August. He tends bees. He asks after you the way a man asks after a sister he wronged and cannot repay. Come and see him, or let him come and see you, or I will start telling the children embellished stories about your climbing technique until you surrender out of sheer embarrassment."

"You wouldn't."

"I have drafts," said Corvane, and she believed him.

She looked out the window, at the veins of light in the mountainside, at the Spire standing open-armed against the sky, at the road winding down into the green world where a former king kept bees and grieved and hoped.

"All right," she said finally. "Midsummer festival. He can come. But he eats in the kitchen with everyone else, and if he tries to kneel to me I'm dumping soup on him."

"That's the spirit," said Corvane, and stole a biscuit.

* * *

Aldric came on the second day of the festival, riding a plain mule with a straw hat and no escort, and if any of the pilgrims recognized him they had the grace not to say so. He looked smaller than the man in the histories—of course he did; the histories had a Crown to fill—but his eyes were clear, wholly his own, and when he saw August across the yard he stopped, and his face did something complicated and honest.

"Mistress Iversen," he said. His voice shook slightly. "You look well."

"I look tired," she said. "It's festival week. Have a seat. There's soup."

He sat. He ate soup. He did not kneel, though she watched him want to, twice, and each time he gripped his spoon instead and carried on, and by the third bowl they were simply two middle-aged people talking about weather and bees and whether Wren was old enough to begin the listening rites (yes, said Corvane; absolutely not, said August; they compromised on autumn).

It was near dusk, when the children had collapsed in heaps and the fires were being lit along the terrace, that Aldric spoke the thing he had ridden all this way to say.

"I dream of him still," the old king said quietly, watching the mountain glow. "Vhalorath. Not nightmares. Just... him. The weight of him. The terrible patience. Sometimes I wake and I can feel what it cost him, century upon century, holding the fire so we wouldn't starve. Do you know what he said to me, at the end? After you broke the Crown?"

"No," said August. She had been in the ritual's white heart at that moment, losing everything she was, and had heard nothing but the sound of her own childhood leaving.

"He said, *'You were a poor throne, little king, but a fair friend.'*" Aldric's laugh caught in his throat. "A thousand years of dragons, and that one chose kindness at the finish. I think about that every day. What it means that the worst binding in history ended in a blessing."

August followed his gaze upward, to where Bram's light ran silver-gold through the darkening rock, breathing, as Wren said. Patient. Awake.

"I think it means," she said slowly, "that fire doesn't care whose it is. It just wants somewhere to live that won't cage it." She turned her teacup in her hands. "People are like that too, maybe."

"People are exactly like that," said Aldric. "I was proof." He rose, bowed—just a small bow, a bow between equals, which was somehow harder to bear than kneeling would have been—and paused. "The council sends thanks again. The child, Wren's namesake—she speaks for the northern villages now. She remembers everything, you know. Everything the Hollow Men took. She keeps it for them."

"Good," said August softly. "Someone should keep it."

"And you?" he asked gently. "Do you miss it? What you paid?"

August thought about it the way she thought about all large questions: while doing something useful, in this case banking the fire. She thought of the empty places inside her where a village name should be, a mother's voice, a queen smuggler's laugh, the reason a girl once started climbing a wall in a storm. Empty places, yes. But not wounds. More like windows. Through them the wind blew clean, and nothing rattled, and the view was remarkable.

"I don't miss what I can't remember," she said at last. "And what I *can* remember seems to keep arriving just when I need it. The land gives back more than it took. Corvane says that's the miracle. I say it's just good manners."

* * *

Years passed, the way years do at the foot of a mountain—slowly enough to savor, quickly enough to surprise.

Pip grew tall and took over the mule routes. Sela went south to study with the surveyors and sent back maps so beautiful people framed them. Odo stayed, bless him, and became the spine of the waystation, the one who carried stranded pilgrims on his shoulders through the melt-swollen fords. And Wren—Wren learned to listen better than any of them, and one bright autumn morning stood at the mouth of a new vent with her hand outstretched and announced, with absolute certainty, "Bram says there's a road opening over the Weeping Stair. An easy one. For people who are scared of heights."

August climbed up that same afternoon to see for herself, and found it true: a path wound along the cliff face where once she had clung by her fingernails with Corvane bleeding in her arms, wide and sure and warm beneath the boots, as though the mountain had reached out and built a handrail out of its own heartbeat.

She stood there a long time, wind in her hair, watching pilgrims far below on the safe new road, and felt something settle in her chest that she supposed other people called peace and she had never had a word for, having misplaced most of her words along with everything else.

It didn't matter. The important things had come back to her over the years, in fragments, like embers carried on a wind—never the whole blaze, but enough to warm her hands by. The smell of rain on a hot road. The feeling of being invisible and choosing, later, to be seen. The weight of a burned man's hand pressing paper into hers at a river crossing.

And one thing more. One thing only, whole and perfect, that had returned to her the night the Crown broke—perhaps the map's final mercy, or perhaps the stranger's last strength, spent not on the message but on the messenger:

*a voice, ragged with blood and river water, saying* ***thank you.***

Two words. A dying courier's gift, kept in the vault of her longer than kingdoms, longer than crowns.

August stood on the Weeping Stair in the evening light, an old woman with young students waiting patiently below, and touched two fingers to her lips, and pressed them to the warm stone.

"Thank *you*," she told the mountain.

And Bram, patient as ever, glowed a little brighter, and showed her the way home.
