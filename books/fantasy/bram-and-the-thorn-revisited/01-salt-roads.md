## 1. Salt Roads

The trick to moving cursed goods through Thornfell, Edmund had learned in six years of apprenticeship, was to never carry anything worth cursing.

She patted the satchel slung across her chest as she climbed the goat track above Hollowmere. Inside: forty luck-charms pressed from river clay and dried rowan, a dozen fever-talismans stitched into boot leather, and one thing she'd never shown Aldous — a small iron key wrapped in wool that hummed against her hip like a sleeping wasp. Contraband, all of it. The kind of quiet magic that kept a village's children breathing through winter and cost the crown nothing but pride.

The crown hated pride being spent elsewhere.

Below her, Hollowmere smoked gently into the morning grey — a scatter of slate roofs around a millpond, too small for a tax collector's map and just big enough for a church whose priest looked the other way when charms changed hands after vespers. Edmund had left before dawn, as always, taking the smuggler paths Aldous called *the salt roads* because everything on them was worth its weight in seasoning and none of it appeared on any ledger.

"You walk like you own the mountain," Aldous liked to say. "Own it quietly."

She owned it quietly now, breath fogging, boots finding the frost-slick stone by memory. The pass ahead split at the Widow's Tooth, and beyond it lay three days of high country before the valley markets where a fever-talisman fetched two weeks' wages and nobody asked where the rowan came from.

Halfway up, she felt it.

Not heard — felt: a wrongness in the wind, the particular hush of a mountain holding its breath. Edmund stopped on the trail and went still, the way her gift had taught her to go still, listening with something older than her ears.

Riders. Many. Below, coming up the south road.

She should have turned back then. Six years of survival said turn back, melt into the scree, let the crown chase shadows. But curiosity is a witch's first sin, and she crept instead to the overlook above the switchbacks and looked down.

They moved like spilled ink along the white road — twenty riders in crown black, no banners, lances bare. Not soldiers. Soldiers announced themselves. These rode silent, and at their head flew a pennant Edmund knew only from stories mothers used to frighten gifted children with: a silver circle pierced by an arrow.

Witch-hunters.

Her mouth went dry. The sweep was supposed to be rumor — Aldous had laughed at it over their last cup of cider, saying the Lord Protector hadn't the men to search a single village, let alone Thornfell. But here they were, methodical as a comb drawn through hair, and behind them came carts. Cages.

Edmund backed away from the edge, heart hammering a rhythm against the humming key. They were heading for the pass. For the salt roads. And the only reason anyone would know there were salt roads to search—

*No.*

Aldous had known about the sweep before she did. Aldous always knew things first; that was his gift, or one of them. And Aldous had sent her out this morning with a heavier load than usual, and a route he'd chosen himself, and a smile that hadn't quite reached the lines around his eyes.

*You walk like you own the mountain.* 

*Own it quietly.*

He'd sold her route. Or worse — he'd sold *her*, and this was simply the delivery.

Edmund stood on the frozen trail with her satchel of small mercies and understood, with terrible clarity, that the comfortable isolation she'd built over six years — the cabin, the kiln, the coded ledgers, the mentor who was almost family — had been a pen all along. She'd been fattened in it. Kept valuable. Kept findable.

The wind shifted. Below, a rider's voice carried thin and clear up the rock:

"—the gifted one travels the high paths. Warrant says a woman. Warrant says *witch*."

She ran.

* * *

The salt roads were hers; that much was true. She took them at a dead sprint, cutting off the Widow's Tooth onto the ledge path where the mountain fell away sheer and the crows drifted below her boots. The hunters wouldn't ride that. But they didn't need to ride it — they needed only to know where it ended, and Aldous knew every ending, because he'd drawn half of them himself on maps he'd burned in front of her, laughing, saying paper remembers longer than people think.

By noon she'd gained the high shelf above Hollowmere Vale and allowed herself one look back.

Smoke. Not chimney smoke.

The riders had reached the village anyway. She watched, chest heaving, as figures were brought out of houses — not many, four or five, hands bound, and among them old Marta the midwife who had bought nothing from Edmund but bread and gossip. They put Marta in a cage cart, and the village watched from doorways, and nobody spoke, because speaking was how villages died.

They weren't hunting her specifically. They were harvesting. Every gifted soul between here and the capital, gathered like timber.

And Aldous had known. Had perhaps even pointed, and named a price.

Edmund turned north, away from the capital, away from the cages, toward the one border crossing the crown couldn't watch — the Stair, a crack in the mountains where smugglers passed goods and oathbreakers passed lives. Three days hard walking. She had food for one.

She made eight hours before the snow started, and twelve before she understood the trap.

It was the quiet that told her. The salt roads were never quiet — ravens argued over everything, the mountain muttered, the wind carried the millpond smell all the way up. But as dusk came down over the Shelf of Names, the silence arrived first, rolling ahead of something, and Edmund froze mid-stride with the instinct of prey.

Horses. Behind her. On the ledge path, which horses could not climb.

Unless someone had told them about the shepherd's stair cut into the cliff face at Raven Gap. Unless someone had drawn it.

She ran again, but running was arithmetic now, and she was losing. They'd entered the gap an hour behind her and gained it in the dark, because they had torches and she wouldn't light one — light meant charm-light meant magic meant proof. She stumbled through the freezing dark on smuggler's memory alone while behind her the torch-glow bloomed and spread like a wound.

At midnight she reached the place where the path forked: left to the Stair and freedom, right to the hollow under Hare Stone where a smuggler could wait out a week if he had food for a week.

She had food for a day. And behind her, closing, twenty riders who already knew every turn because her mentor had sold them the map of her life.

Edmund stood in the falling snow at the fork and finally let herself think the thought whole: *he gave me a head start on purpose.* A day's lead. Enough to feel free. Enough to make the capture clean, out here, away from witnesses, away from questions about why the witch's own teacher wasn't taken too.

Because he wouldn't be taken. That was the arrangement. One name traded for another's safety. She'd have done the arithmetic herself, once. That was the worst part — some cold, clear corner of her mind respected the deal.

The rest of her wanted to burn the mountain down.

She chose left, toward the Stair, because dying tired was still dying. The snow thickened. The torchlight behind her multiplied — they'd split, fanning across the shelf, and somewhere a horn sounded twice, short-short, a signal she didn't know, which meant coordination, which meant they'd done this before, in other mountains, to other runners.

The horn answered from ahead.

From *ahead*.

Edmund skidded to a stop so fast she nearly went over the lip. Ahead — down the slope toward the Stair, where no rider could be — torchlight bloomed in the trees, patient and unhurried, and she understood that the fork had never been a fork. Both roads ended in the same net. There had been no choice since Hollowmere. Maybe since the day she was born gifted, or the day Aldous found her and decided she was more useful alive than reported.

The snow settled on her shoulders like a hand.

She could run anyway. The ledge, the drop, the crows. Her gift stirred under her breastbone, waking from its long burial, offering itself — fire enough to blind them, wind enough to break the line, a whole buried life of power screaming to be used.

*Never show it,* she'd promised herself at fourteen, kneeling in the ashes of the last place that burned. *Never show it, never spend it, never let them see what you are.*

If she used it now, they'd know forever. If she didn't, they'd take her anyway — but they'd take a woman, not a witch. A prisoner, not a specimen. And whatever waited at the end of the cage-carts, it would go easier for a woman.

That was the arithmetic she chose, standing in the snow with her gift howling inside her like a chained dog. She unwound the satchel strap, knelt, and buried it — charms, talismans, the humming iron key — beneath the cairn of a smuggler whose name she'd never learned, marking the spot with a flat stone and a knot of red thread only she would read.

Then she stood, brushed the snow from her skirts, and walked down into the torchlight with her empty hands visible and her face arranged into something dull and frightened.

It wasn't entirely arranged.

The riders closed around her without hurry. At their center sat a woman on a grey mare — tall, dark-haired, armor plain as a coffin lid, and a warrant tube slung at her saddle like a sword. Her eyes went over Edmund once, top to bottom, cataloguing.

"Name," the woman said.

"Edmund," she said. Just that. Let them dig for the rest.

"Edmund." The hunter glanced at a folded paper, and something flickered across her face — not triumph. Something closer to distaste. "Apprentice to Aldous Fenwick, hedge-charmer, Hollowmere parish."

"He'll confirm anything you like," Edmund said. "For a price."

Something else flickered then, quick and unreadable, gone before it could be named. The hunter rolled the paper shut.

"I'm Captain Serah Vane," she said. "You'll ride to the capital. You won't be harmed if you don't make me harm you." She nodded at a soldier, who dismounted with a coil of rope. "Bind her wrists. Front, not back — she'll need her balance on the passes."

Even her cruelty was reasonable. Somehow that was worse.

As they cinched the rope, Edmund looked back once, up the slope, into the dark where Hare Stone stood sentinel over her buried life. Somewhere behind her, in a warm room in Hollowmere, Aldous was drinking to his health, and the snow was covering the last of her tracks, and the comfortable world she'd built was finished.

She'd buried more than charms under that cairn.

She'd buried the girl who trusted him.

The rope tightened. The column turned south, toward the capital, and Edmund rode into captivity with her gift coiled silent and secret beneath her ribs — untouched, unspent, unknown.

For now, it was the only thing in the world that was still hers.

And she intended to keep it that way.
