## 2. The Iron Warrant

They bound her wrists with iron, and that was how Edmund knew they meant to keep her.

Rope could be chewed through. Locks could be picked with a hairpin and patience. But cold iron sat against her skin like a held breath, dulling everything — the low hum of the Thornfell ley that she'd felt in her teeth since she was six years old, the warmth of the charm-satchel still slung across her back because nobody had thought to search a smuggler properly, the small bright thread of herself that she had spent eleven years folding smaller and smaller until it fit inside a lie.

Now it didn't fit anywhere at all. It just pressed against the inside of her like a bird against a shuttered window.

The witch-hunters made camp in the ruined waystation below the pass, the one with the collapsed roof and the old shrine to some road-god whose name nobody remembered. Eight of them, gray cloaks, iron at every throat and cuff. They built no fire. They ate cold rations in silence, and Edmund sat where they'd put her — on an overturned grain crate near the dead shrine — and did what she'd always done best.

She waited, and she watched, and she said nothing.

* * *

Captain Serah Vane was not what Edmund had expected from a witch-hunter.

She'd expected the Lord Protector's men as Aldous had described them: zealots, torch-bearers, men who enjoyed their work. What she'd gotten instead was a woman perhaps ten years her senior, weathered brown at the cheekbones, dark hair cropped short for a helm, who spoke to her prisoners in the same level voice she used to give orders. She'd checked Edmund's wrists herself after the first hour of riding, adjusted the cuffs so they didn't bite, and said only, "Iron chafes. Dead captives tell no secrets."

That was all. No sermon. No threats about burning.

Edmund hated how much it unsettled her. Fear was simple. This was not.

Vane came to sit across from her near midnight, unhelmed, turning something over in her gloved hands. It took Edmund a moment to recognize it: the cheap tin charm off her own satchel, the kind she sold to shepherds against night-hags, worth maybe two coppers.

"Yours?" Vane asked.

"Contraband," Edmund said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "If you're confiscating stock, there's forty more pieces in the bag. I'll want an itemized receipt."

Something flickered at the corner of the captain's mouth. Not quite a smile. "You sell these?"

"I move them. Someone else makes them." The lie was old and smooth and it tasted like ash now, sitting this close to iron. "I'm a courier. That's all."

"A courier." Vane turned the charm over. Tin, a knotted thread, a dab of sheep's tallow. Nothing in it — nothing but what Edmund had put there three months ago, a whisper of keeping, a hedge-mother's blessing strong enough to turn a nightmare. "Do you know what your cargo does?"

"It keeps shepherds sleeping sound. It's folk craft. Half the grandmothers in Thornfell make stronger."

"I know." Vane set it down between them on the crate, deliberately, within reach of neither of them. "That's what puzzles me."

Edmund kept her face still. Eleven years of practice. *Nothing to see here. Just a smuggler, just a girl, just a body with nothing inside it.*

"The warrant says 'the gifted,'" Vane went on, conversational, terrible. "Sweep the passes, take them alive. Do you know why alive?"

"Because corpses can't confess?"

"Because corpses can't serve." Vane studied her, and Edmund had the sudden vertiginous sense of being read like a ledger — line by line, entry by entry, the balance coming up wrong somewhere. "There's a war coming out of the east that the court won't name, and the crown is short on things it can't buy. The hunt isn't extermination, courier. It's conscription. Every gifted soul we bring in goes into the King's service. Sworn, bound, collared, useful." She paused. "You may find this hard to believe, given my office, but I think it's a waste. Most of them are hedge-crafters. Grandmothers, like you say. We put them in a registry and point them at problems."

"And if they refuse?"

"Then they're traitors, and the protocol changes." Vane said it flatly, without relish, which somehow made it worse. "I've executed four people in nine years. I remember all their names. Do you know how many of my colleagues can say that?"

Edmund said nothing. Inside her, behind the iron, the folded-small thing turned over in its sleep.

"You flinch when you look at the cuffs," Vane said suddenly.

"I flinch because they're cold."

"Mm." The captain rose, collected her tin charm, and pocketed it instead of returning it. "Get some sleep. We ride at dawn. Ten days to Corvethal, if the weather holds."

* * *

It did not hold. It snowed on the third day, a wet spring snow that turned the switchbacks above Greywater Gorge into grease, and one of the horses went down screaming with its rider half under it, and Edmund learned two things before noon.

The first was that Serah Vane dug the rider out with her bare hands and set his broken arm herself, splinting it with a tent stake and a strap, without once raising her voice. The man — a heavy youth called Brannic — wept with pain and shame, and Vane crouched beside him and said, quietly enough that Edmund almost didn't hear it: "You're fine. You did nothing wrong. The mountain did this, not you."

*The mountain did this, not you.* Edmund turned the sentence over all afternoon. It was not the kind of thing a zealot said.

The second thing she learned was about herself, and it frightened her more than any warrant. Because when the horse fell — in that first raw second, before thought — something in her had *reached*. Reflex, eleven years buried, older than the lie. A thread of wanting flung toward the animal's terror, toward the sliding stones, an instinct to *hold*, to *steady*, to help.

And the iron had slammed down on it like a door.

The backlash left her dizzy and sick for an hour, lips numb, fingers buzzing, while the hunters rode on around her oblivious. Nobody had noticed. Nobody ever noticed. That was the whole architecture of her life.

But Vane rode up beside her as they made the far rim of the gorge, and looked at her flushed face and shaking hands, and said nothing at all. Which was worse. Which meant she'd seen.

* * *

On the fifth night, camped in a hollow beneath a shattered watchtower, Edmund finally asked.

"Why fair?"

Vane was cleaning her blade by moonlight, each stroke slow and exact. She didn't look up. "Fair how?"

"Your office. The stories say you burn cradles."

"The stories say a lot of things." She sheathed the sword. "My mother was a midwife in the Fenmark. Knew more herb-lore than any licensed physiker in Corvethal, delivered four hundred babies, lost fewer than most. When I was fourteen, a hunter came through — proper warrant, proper seal. He tested her over a fire and a needle and declared her gifted." A pause. "He was right, as it happens. She was. Small craft. Warming fevers, easing births. She never hid it; she thought honesty would protect her."

"What happened?"

"She chose the collar. Served twelve years in the capital, registered, leashed. Came home when they let her, worn thin as parchment. Died of a winter cough the year I turned nineteen." Vane fed the blade-cloth to the coals and watched it curl. "So I know exactly what the collar does to people. And I know what refusing it does. And I joined the office anyway, because someone has to stand between the gifted and the ones who enjoy the work. Fair-mindedness isn't mercy, courier. It's the minimum."

Edmund lay back against her pack and stared at the wheeling stars through the broken tower, and felt the dangerous beginning of something she had no business feeling toward a woman whose warrant had her name on it.

Grudging curiosity. Maybe respect. Roots, if she wasn't careful.

"Ten days," Edmund said. "What happens when we get there?"

"Presentation at court. The Lord Protector takes inventory. Then the binding oaths, one by one." Vane wrapped her cloak around herself and lay down with her back to the fire's ashes. "Sleep, Montrose. You'll want your wits. Everyone does, in Corvethal."

"Everyone? Even you?"

A long silence. Long enough that Edmund thought the captain had gone to sleep.

"Especially me," Serah Vane said at last, to the dark. "I've seen the king's eyes twice now. Up close."

She said nothing more, and the fire burned down to red, and somewhere south beyond the mountains the capital waited with its iron and its oaths. Edmund closed her hands over the cold cuffs and, beneath them, over the small folded thing that was still hers, still hers, still hers —

and tried not to wonder what kind of man needed hunting witches to guard him.
