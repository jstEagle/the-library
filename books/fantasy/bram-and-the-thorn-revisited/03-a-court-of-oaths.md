## 3. A Court of Oaths

The capital announced itself by smell before it showed its walls.

Serah Vane had ridden out of Ostmere a dozen times in her years with the Hunt, and every return was the same: the tanneries first, souring the wind for a mile; then the river stink, the smoke stink, the crush of ten thousand lives pressed into stone. She breathed through her mouth and kept her eyes on the road.

Behind her, roped to the saddle of one of her own riders, Edmund Montrose rode with her chin up like she was arriving at a wedding instead of a tribunal. The girl hadn't spoken since dawn. She'd spent the last three days not speaking, actually — a silence so deliberate it was practically conversation.

"You could at least look impressed," Serah said over her shoulder. "Most people do."

"Most people," Edmund said, "haven't seen Thornfell."

Serah let that go. It was true enough. She'd grown up in the capital's shadow herself, in a customs clerk's house where the mountains were a rumor on the horizon and magic was a rumor under everything — feared, taxed, hunted, never once seen. Then she'd joined the Order at seventeen because it was the only institution that would take a clerk's daughter without a dowry, and she'd learned what everyone learns: that the Hunt was less about witches than about paperwork. Warrants, ledgers, quotas. The Lord Protector wanted numbers each season, and Serah delivered them, and most of her "witches" were herbwives and charm-sellers who'd have been fined anywhere else in the realm.

She'd told herself that made her decent. Three days on the road with Edmund Montrose had started making that harder to say.

* * *

They came through the Salt Gate at midmorning, into streets so loud the horses flattened their ears. Serah felt the shift immediately — the way eyes found them. Not the wary looks of mountain villages. Here people *looked away*, fast, the way you look away from a funeral cart. Word traveled ahead of riders; word always did. By the time they reached the Order's compound, half the city would know Captain Vane had brought in a live one from Thornfell.

A live one. That was the part that sat wrong in her stomach, and had been sitting wrong since the pass.

The old Hunt burned. Everyone knew the stories — pyres in the market square, children questioned with hot irons, the ash that used to drift over the river every spring. That ended forty years ago when the crown reformed the Order under the current charter. *Conscription, not extermination.* Gifted persons were assets of the realm. They were registered, trained, and bound into service. Serah had always read that as mercy. Progress. The difference between a civilized kingdom and the bad old days.

But on the road, Edmund had asked her one question — just one, in that flat smuggler's voice — that had lodged under her skin like a splinter:

"When they bind them into service, Captain, does anyone ever ask them first?"

Serah hadn't answered. She still hadn't answered. And the question had grown teeth in the silence.

The compound swallowed them. Grooms took the horses; clerks took the prisoners' names; a quartermaster with ink-stained fingers took Serah's warrant and stamped it without looking at her face. Edmund and the two other captives from the sweep — a stuttering boy who charmed luck into horseshoes, an old woman whose crime seemed to be knowing too much about weather — were marched toward the holding cells.

Edmund paused at the door. Looked back. Said nothing. The look said plenty.

*You know,* the look said. *You've known for days.*

Serah signed the transfer ledger and hated how steady her hand was.

* * *

The summons came within the hour, which told Serah everything about how badly the Lord Protector wanted this presentation. No rest for the prisoners, no interval for the paperwork to settle. A royal courier in black and gold, standing in the compound yard as if it were beneath him, announcing that His Excellency the Lord Protector Corvane required Captain Vane and her assets at the palace before evening court.

*Assets.* Even the courier's mouth curled around the word like it tasted of coin.

She dressed in full dress uniform — grey coat, silver thorn-and-flame of the Order at the collar — and went down to inspect her charges before transport. The boy was crying quietly. The old woman was praying, or muttering; hard to tell the difference. Edmund stood at the bars of her cell with her arms crossed, watching Serah approach with the patience of someone watching weather come in.

"Big day," Edmund said.

"Don't speak unless you're spoken to in there."

"Or what? You'll arrest me?"

Serah rested one hand on the bars. Up close, the girl smelled of woodsmoke and some green thing — rosemary, maybe, or something wilder. "Listen to me. In that hall there will be men who think your gift is a resource and your life is a rounding error. The only protection you have is being useful. So be quiet, be small, and don't show them anything you don't have to."

Something flickered across Edmund's face — surprise, quickly buried. "That almost sounded like advice."

"It's tactics."

"Right." But the girl's shoulders dropped a fraction, and she nodded once, and Serah walked away before either of them could make more of it than there was room to make.

* * *

The throne room of Vaelcrest had been built to make visitors feel like insects. Serah had presented prisoners here eleven times in her career, and it worked on her every time: the vaulted ceiling lost in gloom, the banners of nine provinces hanging like flayed skins, the long walk up the center aisle with every footstep audible. Courtiers lined the sides in silk and furs, murmuring behind fans. Somewhere above, in the high gallery, musicians played something slow and sweet that didn't match anything happening below.

At the far end, on the dais, sat the king.

And beside the throne, standing, in a coat of black brocade cut sharp as a knife: Lord Protector Corvane.

Serah had met Corvane perhaps six times. He was a handsome man gone slightly hollow at the cheeks, silver at the temples, with the kind of voice that made cruelty sound like policy. He ran the Order, the treasury, and — since the queen's death eight years ago — effectively the realm, while King Aldric sat above him like a figurehead carved out of something pale.

Except.

Serah slowed, halfway up the aisle, and stared, and hated herself for staring.

The king was crowned. That wasn't strange — kings wore crowns. But Aldric's crown was *blackthorn*. Living blackthorn, branches twined and interlocked into a circlet, and Serah could swear — could see, now that she forced herself to look properly — that the bark glistened. That tiny pale buds clustered along the branches like blisters. That one tendril had grown down past the king's ear and hooked, gently, behind his jaw, the way ivy takes a wall.

No one else seemed to notice. The courtiers bowed and murmured. The heralds called names. The whole glittering machine of the court turned and turned around a man wearing a growing thing on his head, and nobody saw it, or nobody said it, or —

Or it only showed itself to certain eyes.

Serah thought of Edmund Montrose, and her stomach went cold.

"Captain Vane." Corvane's voice carried down the aisle like oil poured on water. "Approach. Present your findings."

She climbed the steps. Knelt — the king did not acknowledge her; his gaze was fixed on the middle distance, on nothing, his hands folded in his lap with the stillness of a man in a painting. Up close she could see that he was breathing, barely. That his lips moved sometimes, shaping words too soft to hear.

"Your Excellency." She rose. "Three assets taken in the Thornfell sweep. One registered hedge-practitioner of significant strength—" she heard herself using the ledger language, hating it, "—one journeyman charmwright, one village cunning-woman. All compliant. All documented."

"Significant strength." Corvane smiled without warmth. "The Thornfell girl. Yes. We've been expecting her." He descended one step, close enough that Serah caught his scent — cedar and something underneath it, loamy and dark, like soil turned in spring. "Bring her forward."

Guards brought Edmund forward. She walked the aisle alone, ropes removed, hands free, chin level. The courtiers craned. Someone whispered *mountain witch* and someone laughed, softly, the way people laugh at things that frighten them.

Edmund stopped at the base of the dais. Did not kneel.

"Kneel before His Majesty," a herald snapped.

"She kneels when she chooses," Corvane said mildly, and the herald went silent as if a hand had closed over his mouth. The Lord Protector circled Edmund slowly, the way a buyer circles horseflesh. "Do you know what happens next, girl?"

"I can guess."

"Guessing is for peasants. What happens next is that you are measured. Tested. Catalogued." He stopped in front of her. "And then you are given the great honor of serving your king. Every gift in this realm flows to the crown, Edmund Montrose. That is the whole of the law now. Your talent is not yours. It never was. You simply held it in trust until we came to collect."

Serah watched Edmund's jaw tighten. Watched her swallow whatever she was going to say. *Be quiet, be small.* Good girl. Terrible that Serah was proud of it.

"And if I refuse?" Edmund asked quietly.

Corvane's smile widened by a degree. "Refusal is also catalogued."

It was then that the king moved.

It was small — a turn of the head, slow as a ship coming about — but the whole court felt it, the way animals feel weather. Aldric looked down at Edmund Montrose, and for the first time since Serah had entered the hall, his eyes focused. Focused on her with an intensity that had nothing vacant in it at all.

And Serah saw his eyes clearly for the first time.

They were brown. They should have been brown — portraits hung in every schoolroom, a kindly brown-eyed king. But threaded through the iris of each eye, radiating outward from the pupil like cracks in ice, ran lines of deep green-black. Veins. Roots. As if something had grown into the man's eyes from the inside and was still growing.

His lips moved. This time, standing this close, Serah caught the shape of the words. Two syllables, repeated, silent, over and over, a prayer or a plea or a signal fire:

*Unbind. Unbind. Unbind.*

Then the king blinked, and the vacancy rolled back over his face like a tide, and he turned his gaze to the middle distance again, and the court exhaled as one body, unaware it had ever held its breath.

But Edmund had seen. Serah knew it the way you know a struck bell has been struck — she watched it happen in the girl's face. The fear draining out. Something else pouring in, steady and terrible, filling the space behind her ribs. Edmund looked up at the ruined king, and then — slowly, deliberately — she turned her head and looked at Serah.

Not at her captor. At her. At the one person in this glittering rotting hall who had also seen.

Her expression said: *Now you know. Now what?*

Corvane clapped his hands once, briskly. "Take the assets below. Captain Vane — my study, after court. There are arrangements to discuss regarding the girl's testing." His eyes lingered on Edmund a moment too long, and Serah saw, or thought she saw, the blackthorn crown shiver — buds trembling like insects waking.

"Your Excellency is generous with his confidence," Serah said, and heard her own voice come out flat and careful, a bridge over deep water.

"I am generous with everyone who serves well." Corvane turned back to the court, already smiling at someone else. "Dismissed."

As the guards led Edmund away, the girl walked past Serah close enough to touch, and murmured, so low only Serah could hear it, in the accent of the mountains she'd tried to bury:

"He's still in there, Captain. And he's asking for help."

Then she was gone down the side passage, and Serah Vane stood alone on the dais steps in front of the whole court, with her oath in one hand and the shape of those root-threaded eyes burning behind her own, beginning to understand that very soon she was going to have to choose which one she believed in.
