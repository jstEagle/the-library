## 4. The Thing Wearing Him

They gave her three days to recover from the road, which Edmund understood immediately as a lie of courtesy. You didn't rest a tool before use. You oiled it.

On the fourth morning, two servants in palace grey came for her — not guards, servants, which was somehow worse — and walked her through corridors that grew older and quieter the deeper they went, until the plaster gave way to bare stone and the torchlight to a grey, sourceless glow. They stopped before a door banded in iron so old it had gone soft at the edges.

"The king will receive his scrying asset," said the elder servant, in the tone of a man reciting something he'd been made to memorize until it stopped meaning anything.

*Asset.* Not prisoner. Not guest. Asset. Edmund filed the word away with all the other small humiliations and said nothing. She'd learned on the road north that silence was a coin she could spend anywhere.

The door opened without being touched.

The chamber beyond was round, windowless, and cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature. Cold like a well. Cold like the inside of a locked box. In the center stood a chair — not a throne, though someone had clearly meant it to look like one; the gilt was peeling off the armrests in long curls — and in the chair sat the king of Thornmark.

Edmund had seen him once from the gallery at court, three days ago, during Serah's presentation of prisoners. She'd told herself then that the distance had tricked her. That no living man's eyes could actually look like that — flat and dark and patient as still water over something drowned.

Up close, there was no trick to blame.

King Aldric sat very straight, hands folded in his lap, and he did not move when they entered. He did not move at all. His chest rose and fell with the slow regularity of a man asleep, but his eyes were open, fixed on the middle distance, and around his brow — woven into his hair, threaded through it like ivy through a fence — grew blackthorn.

Living blackthorn. She could see the faint pulse of sap beneath the bark-dark stems. A bud near his left temple swelled as she watched, split, and opened into a flower the color of a bruise.

"Sweet saints," she breathed.

"He does not hear you," the elder servant said. "He has not heard anyone in eleven years. You will begin."

"Begin *what?*" But even as she asked, Edmund felt it: the pull. The room itself was shaped around whatever slept in that chair, the way a riverbed is shaped around the water that cut it. Her gift — buried these six years under smuggler's pragmatism and Aldous's warnings, buried so deep she sometimes pretended it wasn't there — stirred toward the king like a dog toward an open gate.

The younger servant produced a shallow bowl of water and set it on a stand before the throne. "Touch the water. Look for what threatens the realm. Report only threats." The words again had the flatness of liturgy. "You will be watched."

Edmund looked at the watchers — four of them now, positioned at the compass points of the room, faces carefully blank — and understood the shape of the trap. If she found nothing, she was useless, and useless assets were disposed of. If she found something, she revealed herself as gifted beyond dispute, and gifted people in this kingdom ended up either leashed or burned.

And if she touched the king's mind—

She thought of Aldous, somewhere in the cells below, sold and selling. She thought of Serah Vane's face at the presentation, watching the king with an expression Edmund had mistaken, at the time, for professional detachment.

She put her hands in the water.

The scrying came easy, insultingly easy, the way it always had — the surface stilled, deepened, went glassy and dark, and images rose in it like fish rising to feed. Border fires. Grain riots in the south. A ship lost off the Salt Coast. The ordinary weather of a kingdom's troubles. She reported them in a flat voice because that was expected, and the watchers wrote them down, and part of her mind — the smuggler's part, the survivor's part — noted exits and sightlines and the exact weight of the iron-banded door.

Then, on the fourth pass, the water showed her the king.

Not the room. Not the chair. The king, from inside — a vast dark space like the interior of a felled tree, ring upon ring of shadow, and at the center, small as a candle seen down a long corridor, a man.

He was curled around himself. His hands were pressed over his ears. And he was whispering.

Edmund had touched minds before — briefly, accidentally, the way you might brush a stranger's hand in a crowd. This was nothing like that. The moment her attention found him, the moment she *looked*, the whole dark space turned its attention on her, and she fell into it.

* * *

There was no floor. There were walls of thorn, grown dense as basket-weave, and between them a cold that bit through skin and muscle into the marrow. Edmund stood — or existed, or hung — in the hollow heart of the king of Thornmark, and everything in her screamed that this was wrong, that bodies did not fit inside other bodies, that she needed to leave, *now.*

The whispering was louder here. It came from the small figure at the center, the real one, the one curled around his own knees behind a lattice of thorn grown across him like a cage built by a careful gardener.

"*Unbind,*" he whispered. "*Unbind. Unbind.*"

Eleven years, she thought. Eleven years of that word, worn smooth as a river stone, repeated into the dark where no one could hear it.

"I hear you," Edmund said. Her voice sounded thin and mortal in all that vegetable dark.

The figure went still. Then his head came up, and his eyes — his true eyes, brown and tired and human, nothing like the flat black pools upstairs — fixed on her with an intensity that hurt.

"You're new," King Aldric said. His voice was rust and dust. "The others never see me. They see the court, the borders, the things it wants them to see. You're looking at *me.*"

"I didn't mean to."

"No one ever means to." He laughed, or tried to; it came out as a cough. "Girl. Hedge-witch. Whatever you are. Listen, because I don't know how long I can hold the shape of a sentence. It grows me a little more every year. The thorn. I can feel it in my hands now. Some days I think my thoughts in its rhythm." He pressed forward against the lattice, and the thorns bit him, and he didn't seem to notice or care. "It feeds on oaths. Every vow of loyalty sworn in this kingdom, every knee bent, every hand on every hilt — it drinks them. That's why it took a king. A king is a fountain of oaths. Do you understand?"

"I—"

"It cannot be killed while the oaths sustain it. It can only be *unbound.*" The word cracked out of him like a whip. "There is a rite. My wife knew it. She bound the thing to me herself rather than let it take the crown entire — she thought binding it to one man would starve it slow, keep it small. She died believing she'd bought us time." His face twisted. "Time. Eleven years. And the Lord Protector rules in my name, and the hunters ride, and every oath they swear to *me* — to the thing wearing me — feeds it more."

Behind Edmund, deeper in the dark, something shifted.

It was not a sound so much as a change in pressure, the way the air changes before a storm. The thorn-walls flexed. Somewhere far above — up, up, through layers of stone and ceremony and the body of a king — she felt the flat black eyes move.

*It noticed.*

"Aldric," Edmund said quickly, "the rite — your wife's rite — do you know it?"

"Half. She kept half." His voice was fraying, the human shape of him guttering like a candle in wind. "Her servant knew the rest. Her servant—" The pressure increased. The thorn-lattice groaned, growing even as she watched, new shoots weaving themselves across the king's face. "Aldous. His name was Aldous. Find—"

The dark *closed.*

* * *

Edmund came back to herself with her face in the water bowl and blood running freely from her nose, both hands gripping the stand hard enough to whiten her knuckles. Someone was saying her name. Several someones. The watchers had their hands on their weapons.

"She overreached," said the elder servant, in the tone of a man explaining weather. "It happens with strong assets. She'll be fine."

Edmund lifted her head. The room looked the same. The king sat the same, eyes flat, thorn blooming quietly at his temples. As if nothing had happened. As if nothing ever happened, eleven years running.

But as the servants walked her out — past the soft-edged iron door, down the grey corridors, back toward the wing where the conscripted gifted were kept — Edmund felt it, faint and unmistakable, like a splinter working its way toward the heart:

Attention. Directed. Patient.

The thing wearing the king had felt her touch its prison. And it was not alarmed.

It was *interested.*

That night, lying awake in her narrow cell of a room, listening to the palace settle around her, Edmund turned the king's last words over and over. *Her servant. Aldous. Find—*

Find what? The rest of the rite? Aldous himself?

She thought of her mentor in the cells below, old and frightened and treacherous, and felt something shift in her chest that she refused, for the moment, to name. Rage, maybe. Or the beginning of a plan.

Because here was the truth she'd carried out of that thorn-dark place, the truth that sat in her stomach like a swallowed coal:

The king was alive. The king was *asking.* And the thing that wore him had noticed her — had marked her, the way a gardener marks a promising cutting.

One of those facts was a death sentence.

The other might be a weapon.

Edmund stared at the ceiling until the grey light of dawn crept along the stones, and made herself a promise in the oldest language she knew, the wordless vow of a hedge-witch with nothing left to lose:

*I'm coming back down. And this time, I'm bringing the axe.*

She just had to find out what Aldous knew first.
