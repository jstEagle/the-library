## 5. What Aldous Knew

They kept Edmund in the third cell down, the one with a drain in the floor and no window. She'd counted her steps across it — eleven, heel to toe — until counting stopped meaning anything and became just another way of breathing.

The chains at her wrists were iron, but not cold-iron. Whoever ran these cells knew enough to keep a hedge-witch uncomfortable without killing what they wanted to use. She could feel her gift sitting under her skin like swallowed river water, dull and heavy and hers, and every hour she spent in the dark she made herself check on it the way you check on a coin sewn into your hem.

*Still there. Still mine.*

The door above groaned open. Light spilled down the stairwell, and with it came voices — two guards, arguing low about dice — and then a sound she knew better than her own heartbeat: a wet cough, the kind that comes from a man's chest being wrong for years.

Aldous.

They brought him past her cell and she saw him by torchlight, and something in her went very still and very old. He looked smaller. Not bent — Aldous had never been a tall man — but *folded*, like a letter someone had read too many times and shoved in a pocket. His coat was gone. His hands were chained in front of him, and he was looking at the floor like a man rehearsing an apology he already knew would fail.

They put him in the cell across from hers. Close enough to talk. Far enough that neither could touch the other through the bars, which told her somebody up top understood exactly what kind of prisoner they had.

The guards left. The torch outside gutted and steadied.

For a long time, neither of them spoke. Then Aldous said, "You've got his look."

"Whose?"

"The king's. When I saw them parade you through the hall." He coughed again, folded smaller. "Wrong eyes."

"Don't." The word came out flat as a blade laid on a table. "Don't you talk to me about looks. Don't you talk to me about *anything*." She stood, and the chain between her wrists pulled taut, and she let it. "You sold me. You sat across our own table, drank my tea, watched me mend your boots with a charm I hid from you for three years, and then you wrote my name on a piece of paper and handed me to Vane like a sack of salted fish."

"I know."

"You *know*?" She laughed, and it came out ugly. "Eleven steps, Aldous. That's how wide my world is now. Eleven steps and a drain."

He didn't flinch from it. That was the worst part. He just took it, the way he took everything — weather, prices, betrayal — with that same tired patience, and waited for the storm to spend itself against him.

"They offered me a choice," he said finally. "My name or yours. And I chose mine, and I'll burn in whatever hell keeps smugglers before I pretend otherwise. But you should understand one thing before you decide what I'm worth." His eyes came up, and for the first time since the pass, they weren't tired. They were afraid. "I didn't sell you because I'm a coward. I sold you because I recognized the warrant seal."

Edmund went still.

"The crown's hunting seals changed last spring," he said. "Everyone thinks it's just new ink. It isn't. It's a binding sigil — a feeding sigil. Old work. Older than me." He paused, and when he spoke again his voice dropped to almost nothing. "I'd seen it once before. On the door of a woman named Maren Cole."

The name meant nothing to her. She said so.

"She was the royal hedge-witch," Aldous said. "Before your time. Before mine, near enough. Thirty years ago she served the old queen, and when the old queen died and the young king took the throne — sickly boy, thorn-fever at seven, everyone said he wouldn't live out the winter — Maren saved him. Or everyone *said* she saved him." His hands turned over in their chains, helpless-looking. "She bound something to him. Something that got in during the fever. A thorn-thing, root-magic, the kind that grows where oaths are spoken. And the binding held for thirty years because Maren fed it her gift a little at a time, keeping it asleep, keeping it *contained*, until there was nothing left of her magic and the thing started waking anyway."

"And then?"

"And then it ate her." Flat. Final. "Not her body. Her *name*. She died in the palace gardens and by the time they found her, no one remembered what she was called. Records blank. Gravestone blank. Even I—" His voice caught, and he hated it, you could see him hating it. "Even I have to work to remember her face. I only keep the name because she gave it to me to carry, in case."

"In case of what?"

"In case someone ever came along with enough gift to finish what she started." He looked at her across the dark, across eleven feet of stone. "You're stronger than she was, Edmund. Stronger than me. I felt it the day you mended those boots and thought I'd hidden it well. I've known what you were for three years. Why do you think I taught you to hide?"

The rage in her didn't die. It shifted — turned heavier, colder, harder to hold. "You knew. You *knew* this thing existed, you knew it wore the king, you knew there was half a rite somewhere, and you let me run salt and charms through the mountains like none of it mattered—"

"Because running was *safe*." For the first time he raised his voice, and it cracked in the middle. "Because Maren asked me to find someone strong and I found you, and then I spent ten years hoping to God I'd never have to tell you why. Because the woman who did this work *died nameless*, girl, and the rite is only half-written, and I am a coward with bad lungs who couldn't—" The cough took him then, doubled him over, wracked him against the bars until Edmund's own chest hurt listening.

When he could breathe again, he pressed his forehead briefly to the iron. "Couldn't watch another one be spent."

Silence settled back over the cells like snowfall.

Edmund sat down slowly on the cot's edge. Her wrists ached where the chains rested. Under her skin, the river-water weight of her gift shifted, restless, as if it had heard its own name called.

"The rite," she said. "You have half of it."

"Maren gave me half before she went in to feed it the last time. The opening words. The unbinding's first movement." He fumbled at his shirt with chained hands, and Edmund realized he'd been wearing it against his skin this whole time — through the arrest, the road, the cells. A square of oilcloth, and inside it, folded small as a communion wafer, a scrap of vellum gone soft and brown with age.

He passed it through the bars. His fingers were shaking. Hers weren't, and she noticed that, and didn't know yet whether it was courage or shock.

The writing was cramped, urgent, in a hand that deteriorated line by line:

*What is bound by oath may only be loosed by truth. Speak the lie it feeds upon and starve it first. Then name the king — his true name, the one his mother gave him, not the crown's — and call him out of the thorn. The caster must give what holds the door shut. There is no other coin. God forgive me, there is no other coin.*

Edmund read it twice. Three times. The words didn't change. The cost didn't either.

"It wants the caster's gift," she said. "All of it. Permanently."

"Yes."

"And the second half? The closing? Where is it?"

"There is no second half written down." Aldous met her eyes. "Maren never finished it. She was inventing it as she went, girl — no one had ever unbound a thing like this. She gave me what she had and walked into the throne room knowing she'd be writing the rest with her dying breath." He smiled, and it was a terrible, gentle smile. "But she left one more thing. A word. The word the king has been whispering for thirty years, down in whatever cellar of himself he's buried in. Have you touched his mind yet?"

Edmund thought of the scrying session — the entombed man, the single syllable repeated like a pulse against her fingers.

"*Unbind,*" she said quietly.

"That's the word. That's the whole second half, or the seed of it. Maren believed the binding could only close from *inside* — that the king himself, awake, could refuse the thorn, refuse its food. Your job is to open the door. His job is to walk through it." Aldous's voice frayed. "She believed it because he whispered it to her, at the end. Same word. Thirty years, one word. A man asking to be let out."

Edmund folded the vellum along its worn creases and held it against her sternum, over the place where her gift sat waiting.

"Why give me this now?" she asked. "You could sit on it. Trade it. Buy your neck back the way you bought it with mine."

Aldous leaned his head back against the stone and closed his eyes.

"Because I owed her a carrier," he said. "And because I owe you a truth. And because tomorrow the Lord Protector's men come for the prisoners who've stopped being useful, and I'd rather die owing the right people than living owing the wrong ones." He opened his eyes. "Learn it tonight, Edmund. All of it. Every word."

* * *

She learned it through the dark hours, by torchlight guttering, whispering the opening lines against her knuckles until the rhythm lived in her mouth the way smuggling routes used to live in her feet. What is bound by oath may only be loosed by truth. Starve it first. Name the true name. Open the door.

Somewhere past the deepest hour of night, Aldous began to cough again, and didn't stop, and when the guards finally came clattering down the stairs at dawn — more of them this time, and armed, and moving with purpose — Edmund was on her feet at the bars with the vellum burned into memory and ash-colored dread in her throat.

The guard captain unrolled a list. Read two names.

Neither was hers.

"Aldous Fen," he said, and looked up at the old man folding himself off the cot, calm as Sunday. "You're wanted upstairs. The Lord Protector has questions about the previous holder of your office."

Aldous shuffled to the bars. Up close, Edmund could see his lips were gray, and that he'd been ready for this since the pass, maybe since the day he'd written her name on that paper.

"Girl," he said. "The true name. When you get to the throne room — the mother-name, the fever-name. Ask the king what his mother called him. Only he remembers it now. Not even the thorn knows."

The guards took his arms. At the foot of the stairs he stopped, and turned, and looked back at her one last time — not apologizing, not begging forgiveness, just *looking*, the way a man looks at the last fire he'll ever stand beside.

"Tell Maren," he said, "the debt's paid."

Then they took him up into the light, and Edmund stood alone in the dark with half a rite in her head, a dead woman's name on a scrap of vellum, and the sudden, terrible understanding that the next person to ask the king his true name might be the last person who ever could.
