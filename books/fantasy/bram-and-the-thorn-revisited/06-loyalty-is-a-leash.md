## 6. Loyalty Is a Leash

Serah Vane had taken the hunter's oath at seventeen, kneeling on cold flagstones with her hand pressed flat against a drawn blade, and she had never once broken it. Eleven years of warrants served, of gifted men and women marched to the capital in irons or buried in unmarked graves along the salt roads, and her conscience had stayed clean the way a sword stays clean — by never asking what it was for.

She was asking now.

The summons came an hour past midnight, delivered by a boy in Lord Protector Corvane's livery who wouldn't meet her eyes. She dressed in the dark, buckled her sword belt out of habit, and crossed the rain-slicked courtyards of the Citadel to the Protector's wing, where the candles burned behind leaded glass like trapped yellow moths.

Corvane received her alone, which should have been an honor. It felt like a trap being set.

"Captain." He sat behind a desk of black oak that had belonged to three Protectors before him, and he wore the same expression all three had worn: pleasant, patient, and utterly without warmth. "You've done remarkable work. The Montrose girl especially."

"She's cooperative," Serah said.

"I know. I've read the reports." He folded his hands. "Her scrying sessions with His Majesty have been... productive. More productive than any asset we've fielded. Which is why this next order will be difficult for you, so I'll say it plainly and only once." He leaned forward into the candlelight. "When their usefulness ends — hers, and the old man's — you will execute them both. Quietly. No tribunal, no record beyond what I write myself. You'll report it as an escape attempt gone wrong."

The rain ticked against the window. Somewhere below, a sentry's boots rang on stone.

Serah heard herself say, "The girl is the most valuable scrying asset the crown has ever held."

"And when the king is restored to health, we won't need her." Corvane smiled. "That's rather the point, Captain."

*Restored.* The word snagged in Serah's chest like a fishhook. Because she had stood in the shadow of the throne room three days ago, delivering the prisoner for another session, and she had seen the king up close — closer than protocol allowed, close enough to watch the blackthorn crown shift on his brow like something breathing. Close enough to see him mouth a word as Edmund was led away, a word no king should know needed saying.

*Unbind.*

She hadn't reported it. That omission sat in her gut now like a swallowed stone.

"May I ask," she said carefully, "what the physicians conclude about His Majesty's condition?"

"The physicians conclude whatever serves the realm." Corvane's voice didn't rise, but the temperature of it dropped. "Your oath is to the crown, Captain, not to your curiosity. The order stands. Both prisoners. Before the month turns. Do I make myself understood?"

"You do, my lord."

He studied her a moment too long, the way a man checks a knot he suspects is slipping. Then he dismissed her, and Serah walked back through the dark corridors with the stone still lodged in her throat, and understood that something in her had already decided — she just hadn't let herself know it yet.

* * *

She went to the records.

There was a wing of the Citadel where old warrants went to die, presided over by a half-blind archivist who owed Serah for looking the other way about certain smuggling in his nephew's name. She told him she needed the file on the previous royal hedge-witch, and he looked at her for a long moment with his one good eye before shuffling off into the stacks.

The file was thin. That was the first wrong thing. A woman who had served the crown for twenty years, who had bound wards around the palace itself, and the entire record fit inside a folio no thicker than a psalter.

*Maren of Thornfell. Appointed royal hedge-witch under Queen Alianor. Retained after the queen's death at King Aldric's request.*

Then the last page, dated eleven years ago: *Deceased. Cause: misadventure during rite preparation. Remains interred privately per His Majesty's instruction. File closed by order of the Lord Protector's office.*

Eleven years ago. Serah did the arithmetic without wanting to. Eleven years ago was exactly when court chronicles began describing the king as *pious*, *withdrawn*, *given to long solitary devotions*. Exactly when the annual progress through the provinces stopped. Exactly when the witch-hunts began — not under some zealot predecessor's decree, but signed by Aldric's own hand, in a script that grew shakier every year while the man himself, by all accounts, grew strangely serene.

Hunts that conscripted the gifted instead of killing them. Scryers brought to the palace in chains. Assets, Corvane called them. Fuel, said a colder voice in her head, a voice that sounded uncomfortably like Edmund Montrose's.

She thought of the thorn crown, alive and glistening. Of the king mouthing *unbind*. Of Maren of Thornfell dying mid-rite, and a Protector's office closing the file before anyone asked what rite, and why a hedge-witch's death required royal privacy.

Serah had spent eleven years believing she hunted monsters. Standing in the dead archive with a thin folder in her hands, she confronted the possibility that she'd been working for one — that the leash her oath had always been ran not to the crown but to the thing wearing it.

She put the folio back exactly as she'd found it, thanked the archivist, and walked out into the rain without feeling it.

* * *

The cells beneath the east tower were cold enough that breath showed, and Aldous Renwick had claimed the corner nearest the brazier grate with the shameless pragmatism of a man who had survived thirty years in Thornfell by always finding the warm spot. He looked up when her boots stopped outside the bars, and his eyes — quick, gray, appallingly honest for a traitor — went straight to the paper in her hand.

"You shouldn't be here," he said.

"No," Serah agreed. "Neither should you, by month's end."

She unlocked the cell because there was no one to see and because she had already stopped pretending to herself that she came as a jailer. Aldous shifted back to give her room, wincing at his bad knee, and waited with the patience of a man who had spent weeks learning exactly how much silence made captors uncomfortable.

"I need to know about Maren," Serah said.

Whatever he expected, it wasn't that. He went very still. "Where did you hear that name?"

"From a closed file. Signed off by the Protector's office eleven years ago. Misadventure during rite preparation." She held his gaze. "I'm going to ask you once, plainly, the way I'd want to be asked. What did she bind? And what happened to it?"

Aldous laughed, but there was no humor in it — just the exhausted sound of a man setting down something heavy. "You've seen the king," he said. "Don't tell me you haven't wondered."

"Wondering isn't knowing."

"No. Knowing is worse." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Maren found it in the deep records — a thing older than the dynasty, older than the mountain shrines. It eats oaths, Captain. Loyalty is its bread. Every vassal who kneels, every soldier who swears, every subject who says *long live the king* — it drinks. And it got hungry, and the king was the richest table in the realm, and Maren—" His voice cracked and steadied. "Maren tried to bind it out of him. Alone, because no one would believe her, because the thing had already eaten half the court's judgment. It killed her. And then it settled deeper, and it's been feeding ever since, and every year the hunts bring more of the gifted here because it wants them. Wants them close. Wants them *ripe*."

Serah's hand had drifted to her sword hilt without her noticing. Not from threat. From the old reflex of reaching for certainty and finding none.

"Your girl," Aldous went on, softer now, watching her face with terrible attention. "Edmund. Corvane keeps her sessions short and her strength measured, yes? Never lets her touch the king's mind longer than an hour?" He nodded at whatever he saw in hers. "That's not caution. That's a farmer rationing feed. When she's dug deep enough to show it everything it needs — every ward Maren left, every seam in its binding — she'll have outlived her usefulness. And so will I."

"The order came tonight," Serah heard herself say. "Both of you. Before the month turns. Reported as an escape attempt."

Silence. Then Aldous exhaled slowly, and something in his shoulders loosened — not relief exactly. Confirmation. "So. Now you know." He leaned forward. "And here's the question that decides what you are, Serah Vane. Your oath says protect the crown. But the crown is a thorn-crowned corpse wearing a king like a coat. Who does your sword serve when the two can't be the same thing?"

She didn't answer. She stood, locked the cell again out of pure habit, and walked away — and hated how much the locking sounded like an answer she wasn't ready to give.

* * *

She found Edmund at dawn, in the small stone yard where the prisoners were allowed an hour's air. The girl sat on the mounting block with her knees drawn up, watching light come over the eastern wall, and even hollowed down by weeks of scrying she had a stillness to her that Serah envied — the stillness of someone who knew exactly what she was, whatever name the world used for it.

"Captain." Edmund didn't stand. Her eyes narrowed slightly, reading Serah's face the way she apparently read minds. "Something's happened."

"Aldous talked to me."

"He does that." A ghost of a smile. "What did he cost you to listen?"

Everything, Serah almost said. Instead she crossed the yard, checked the sightlines herself — old habit, new purpose — and crouched to the girl's level, voice pitched under the wind.

"Two nights ago I saw the king mouth a word at you. *Unbind.* Last night the Lord Protector ordered me to kill you and Aldous within the month and bury it in paperwork. Today I learned the woman who bound the thing on his head died doing it, and the file was closed by the same office that just signed your death warrant." She held Edmund's gaze. "I have spent eleven years believing my oath pointed somewhere true. I no longer believe that. So I'm going to break it, or bend it, or whatever word lets me sleep — and I need to know if you're worth breaking it for. Tell me what you saw in his mind. All of it. And tell me what you need to finish what Maren started."

Edmund stared at her, and for the first time since the pass at Thornfell, Serah watched fear leave the girl's face and something harder take its place.

"You're serious," Edmund said.

"I'm asking."

Edmund glanced toward the wall, toward the throne room beyond it where a dead-eyed king sat crowned in living thorn, and when she turned back her voice was low and certain and changed everything.

"Then get me out of these yards, Captain. Because I know where Maren hid the rest of the rite — and it's time someone finished it."
