## 7. Thornfall

The whispers started an hour before the escape did.

Edmund heard them through three feet of granite and a locked cell door, thin as spider silk, threadbare as a psalm sung by someone dying. *Down. Down and left. The old ways remember.*

She pressed her palm flat against the cold stone and let the words come. Not her gift — that was buried deep, packed in ash and salt where Serah Vane's iron-cold scrutiny couldn't find it. This was something else. The king. What was left of him. A voice worn down to its last syllables, calling from inside his own skull like a man tapping on the lid of his coffin.

*They come at moonrise,* the whisper said. *The Protector sends for you both. After that there is no after.*

Edmund sat back on the cot. Across the cell block, in the cell they'd moved Aldous into once he'd stopped being useful, her mentor coughed — the wet, rattling cough of a man whose lungs had been negotiating with death for years and were close to settling terms.

"Aldous," she said quietly. "Wake up."

A pause. Then, dry as old paper: "If this is another lecture on gratitude, girl, I've heard it twice tonight already."

"The king is talking to me."

Silence. Then the shuffle of him coming to the bars, and his voice losing all its performance. "What does he say?"

"Escape routes. Ways under the palace." She looked at her hands. They were shaking, and not from fear. "He says your mistress built them. The royal hedge-witch. The one who bound the thing to him."

Another silence, longer. When Aldous spoke again, the arrogance was gone from his voice entirely, and what remained underneath it was older, and tired, and small.

"Maren," he said. "Her name was Maren. I was her second, thirty years ago, before I decided survival was a more interesting craft than loyalty." He laughed, and the laugh broke in the middle. "I told myself selling you out was different. It wasn't. It never is."

"Save the confession." Edmund stood. "The rite needs a place of binding. Where?"

"The old queen's chapel. Under the east wing, past the cisterns. Maren kept her tools there." He was quiet a moment. "The other half of the rite is in my head, Edmund. Has been since the night she died. I memorized it because I was too much a coward to use it, and too proud to forget it."

"Then you're coming with us."

"I'm coming with you," he agreed, "as far as I'm useful."

* * *

Serah Vane came for them at moonrise, exactly as the whisper had promised — but not with the escort of six the Lord Protector had ordered. She came alone, keys in one hand, sword at her hip, and a look on her face Edmund had never seen there: a woman standing on the far side of a bridge she'd already burned.

"They're moving you at dawn," Serah said, working the first lock. "Officially to new quarters. Unofficially—" The lock clicked open. "There won't be quarters."

"You're breaking your oath," Edmund said.

"I'm keeping a better one." Serah met her eyes, and there was no softness in her, only precision. "I swore to protect the crown. Whatever wears it now isn't the crown. It's a parasite wearing a crown, and I have been ordered to guard it while it eats the realm from the inside." She opened Aldous's cell next. "Move quickly. Speak quietly. And if either of you makes me regret this, I will personally deliver you to whatever comes next."

"Charming," Aldous wheezed. "You'd have made a fine smuggler, Captain."

"I'd have made a fine hangman. Don't test me."

They went up the service stairs two at a time, Serah ahead with a shuttered lantern, Edmund in the middle, Aldous last, his breath sawing in and out of him in short expensive bursts. Twice they froze in doorways while patrols passed — guards moving with the strange loose-jointed synchrony of people whose wills had been thinned by too many oaths sworn in the wrong direction. Once, passing a window, Edmund looked down into the great courtyard and saw the king standing alone beneath the blackthorn tree that had grown, impossibly, up through the flagstones, its branches crowned in pale blossoms that opened only at night.

He wasn't moving. But his head was tilted, slightly, toward the tower where she stood.

*Hurry,* whispered the voice in her bones. *It knows you are awake.*

* * *

The forgotten ways began behind a wall of stacked wine casks in the undercroft, behind which a doorway waited that no architect had drawn on any surviving plan. The stone around its frame was older than the palace above it — older than the kingdom, maybe — carved over with spirals that made Edmund's buried gift stir uneasily against its bindings, like a dog dreaming of running.

"Maren's work," Aldous breathed. "Or Maren's inheritance. She said the palace was built on the bones of something older, and the builders left the veins showing." He touched the spiral with two fingers. "These are wards, girl. Dead ones. She let them die so no one would ever look here again."

Beyond the door, stairs wound down into dark, and dark gave way to corridors: low, rough-hewn, dripping, threaded with the sound of water moving somewhere below. They walked in single file, lantern hooded to a slit of light. Hours passed, or what felt like hours. Edmund lost count of turnings. All the while the whispers came, fainter now, fraying at the edges like cloth rubbed too long.

*Left at the split. Mind the drop. The cisterns breathe — wait when they inhale.*

"He's getting weaker," she murmured to Serah.

"People who fight parasites alone for years do get weaker," Serah said. Her voice had an odd roughness to it. "It's remarkable any of him is left."

At the cisterns, the corridor opened onto a vast black chamber where still water mirrored nothing, and pillars rose out of it like the legs of drowned giants. They crossed the narrow causeway single file, and in the middle, where the dark was deepest, the whispers stopped.

Edmund stopped with them, heart slamming. "No. No, no — talk to me. Which way?"

Nothing. Only the drip of water, and Aldous's ragged breathing, and somewhere very far above, muffled through tons of stone, a bell beginning to ring.

"That's the alarm," Serah said flatly. "They've found the cells empty. Or they've found what I left in the Lord Protector's dispatch box." Almost, almost, she smiled. "Either way, we've stopped being a secret."

"Which way?" Edmund asked the dark again, and hated how her voice cracked.

Then, faintly — so faintly it might have been memory rather than sound:

*Follow the water. The water goes to the chapel. I held the map of him together as long as I could. Go, witch. Finish it.*

The bell rang on. They ran.

* * *

The gate was the problem.

Of course it was. The old ways ended at a barred iron gate set into the foundation wall of the ruined chapel, and beyond it, torchlight — hunters in the passage above, drawn by the alarm, sweeping toward the east wing with dogs and drawn steel. They could hear the baying through the stones. Ten minutes, maybe less.

The lock on the gate was ancient, fused, dead. Serah put her shoulder to it and gained nothing but a grunt. Edmund clawed at her own bindings, at the ash-and-salt shell around her gift, and felt it hold — not because she lacked the strength, but because breaking it here, now, would light her up like a beacon for every thorn-sworn hunter in the palace.

"It doesn't matter," Aldous said.

They turned. He was leaning against the wall, and he was smiling, and his face had gone the color of tallow.

"There's a release bar on the far side. Maren showed me, thirty years ago, when we used these ways to move unseen." He slid down the wall until he was sitting. "Someone has to go through the grate above — the drainage shaft, there. Climb through, lift the bar, open the gate. It takes time. Time we don't have, unless someone stays here and convinces the dogs the trail ends at this gate."

"No," Edmund said.

"Girl—"

"*No.*" Her voice came out raw. "You don't get to do this. You sold me. You get to live long enough to be sorry, that's the arrangement, that's the whole—"

"I know." He said it gently, and somehow that was worse than cruelty. "I know exactly what I did, and what it cost, and I have been sorry in every hour since the riders took you. Sorry isn't a coin you can spend, Edmund. It's just weight you carry." He coughed, wet and long, and wiped his mouth, and his hand came away dark. "But this — this I can carry instead. Let me trade up."

Above them, the dogs were closer. Torchlight flickered in the drainage grate.

Serah looked at Edmund, and said nothing, and that silence was its own terrible permission.

"Half the rite," Aldous said, gripping Edmund's wrist with the last of a smuggler's strength. "Maren's half is in the chapel, in her strongbox under the altar stone — the sigil of unbinding, cut in lead. My half is here." He pressed his other hand flat against his own chest, over his heart. "Listen once. You've always listened once and remembered forever. So listen."

And he spoke it — low and fast, the counter-verse, the words that would take her gift and burn it as fuel and leave her hollow and free — while Edmund stared at him and memorized it the way she'd memorized everything he'd ever taught her, hating him, loving him, unable to separate the two anymore, perhaps never having been able to.

"Good girl," he said when he finished. "Now open the grate and don't look back. Looking back is for people with time."

She climbed the shaft. At the top, wedged in the narrow dark, she looked down anyway — one last look — at Aldous Montrose pulling himself upright against the gate, drawing his belt knife, arranging himself between the hunters and the iron like a man arranging his own monument.

"Thornfell roads run true," he called up to her, grinning through the blood on his teeth. "Even the crooked ones. Especially those. Go!"

She lifted the bar. The gate swung. Serah caught Edmund's arm and hauled her through into the chapel dark, and behind them, faint through the closing stone, Edmund heard the dogs arrive, and shouting, and then Aldous's voice — clear and defiant and laughing — saying something she couldn't make out and would spend the rest of her life inventing versions of.

Then the passage sealed, and there was only the chapel: ruined roof open to the stars, altar stone cracked down the middle, dust and pigeon-quiet and cold.

Serah barred the inner door with a fallen beam and turned to face her, sword drawn, back to the wood. "How long do you need?"

Edmund knelt at the altar, fingers finding the seam of the altar stone, the hidden lip of Maren's strongbox beneath. Her hands had stopped shaking.

"As long as it takes," she said. "They'll come through that door. Whatever happens — whatever you hear — keep them off me."

Above the chapel, across the sleeping palace, the blackthorn tree shuddered in a wind that touched nothing else, and the thing wearing the king finally, fully, woke.
