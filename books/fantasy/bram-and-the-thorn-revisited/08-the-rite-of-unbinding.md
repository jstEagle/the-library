## 8. The Rite of Unbinding

The old queen's chapel had no roof. Rain had gotten into the stones over a hundred years and turned them green and soft as moss, and where the altar had been there was only a slab of cracked marble furred with lichen, and above it, climbing the wall like a frozen scream, the remains of a thorn-carving — a crown of blackthorn cut into rock by some mason who had either loved the old queen very much or feared her very much.

Edmund stood in the nave and felt the place listening.

Not the way a house listens when someone walks through it late at night. Not the way a forest goes quiet around a predator. This was older and more deliberate, the attention of a building that had been raised for one purpose — the making and breaking of oaths — and had never entirely stopped practicing.

"She's here," Edmund said. "The old queen. Or what's left of her."

Serah Vane came up beside her, sword drawn, which in a chapel struck Edmund as either blasphemy or prayer depending on how you looked at it. "The hunters are twenty minutes behind us at most. Maybe less. Tell me this can be done in twenty minutes."

"It took Aldous three days to write down half of it." Edmund pulled his notebook from her coat — water-stained, blood-stained, the ink of a dying man smeared where his hand had dragged across the page. "I'm going to do it faster."

"Can you?"

"I have to."

* * *

She laid out the rite on the altar slab the way Aldous had taught her to lay out charms: everything in its order, nothing touching that shouldn't touch, the whole arrangement legible at a glance so that under pressure she could read it like a sentence.

Salt, from the smuggler's pouch she'd carried over the Thornfell passes a hundred times. The same salt she'd used to hide charms in flour sacks and false-bottomed cheese wheels. It seemed absurd that it should end here, doing the largest work of her life.

A knife. Hers, with the horn handle worn smooth by her own grip.

A bowl of rainwater caught from the broken roof, because the chapel provided.

And the notes. Half a rite in Aldous's crabbed hand, the rest of it blank pages waiting for her instinct to fill them — because the woman who'd written the original had died before teaching anyone the whole of it, and Aldous had only ever been her apprentice's apprentice, twice removed from the source, carrying fragments he'd spent forty years pretending were complete.

"I need you to understand something," Edmund said, not looking up from the page. "Before I start."

Serah stood at the chapel door, angled outward toward the path, watching the treeline. "If it's about the cost—"

"It's about the cost."

"Then say it quickly."

Edmund made herself look at the words on the page instead of at Serah's back. *The unbinding requires a vessel of equal weight.* That was how Aldous had put it, in his last lucid hour, gripping her wrist hard enough to bruise. *You cannot pull a thorn out of a king with empty hands. The rite needs fuel, girl. Living fuel. Gifted fuel. And the gift does not come back.*

"The entity is bound to him by an oath," Edmund said. "Oaths are my whole trade — they're every hedge-witch's trade, we just don't call them that. Love is an oath. Loyalty is an oath. The gift itself is an oath, sworn before birth by powers I've never met, that I'll carry magic and magic will carry me. To break the thing's hold on the king I have to break an oath of equal strength." She turned the page. Blank. "Mine."

Silence, except for rain ticking off the broken stonework.

"You'll be hollow," Serah said. "Like him."

"Not like him. He was taken. This I'd give." Edmund heard how steady her voice was and distrusted it. "But it doesn't grow back, Serah. There's no second rite. Whatever I am after tonight — that's all I'll ever be."

"And if it kills you?"

"Then it kills me." She said it lightly and hated herself for it, because lightness was a kind of lying and she had promised herself, coming down off Thornfell, that she was done lying. "Better odds than the realm has otherwise."

Serah turned from the door. In the grey light her face was hard to read — witch-hunter's training, Edmund supposed, a face built for tribunals. But her eyes weren't hard at all.

"There's another way," Serah said.

"There isn't."

"We run. North, over the pass, back to Thornfell. I know roads the crown doesn't. We disappear, and the king stays whatever he is, and the Lord Protector keeps his leash—"

"—and every gifted child in the kingdom keeps getting conscripted or buried, and the thing wearing the king keeps eating oaths until there's nothing left of this country but the shape of loyalty." Edmund shook her head. "I ran once already. I was good at it. Ten years of running and hiding my gift under flour sacks, and you know what it bought me? Aldous in chains. You in front of me with a warrant. A kingdom going rotten from the head down." She put her hand flat on the salt. "I'm done buying safety."

Serah held her gaze a moment longer. Then she nodded, once, the way she did everything — economical, final — and went back to the door.

"Then do your work," she said. "I'll do mine."

* * *

The first part of the rite was Aldous's, and it worked exactly as written, which nearly undid her. She had braced herself for fragments, gaps, places where his memory failed and she'd have to leap. Instead his instructions unfolded clean as a psalm: salt in a circle broken at the east, water charged with three drops of the caster's blood, the name of the bound spoken backward into the bowl.

*Gnidniknu*, said the water, and shivered, and went still.

The chapel changed. Edmund couldn't have described how, except that the air acquired a texture, a grain, like wood about to split. The lichen on the altar seemed to lean. Somewhere above the missing roof, thunder moved without lightning.

The second part was hers.

She sat down on the cold marble, closed her eyes, and reached for the thread the king had given her in the scrying chamber — the whisper she'd carried out of his entombed mind like a coal in cupped hands. *Unbind.* One word, worn smooth by years of saying it into the dark where no one heard.

*I hear you,* she told him silently. *Hold on. I'm coming with tools.*

She opened her gift the way she hadn't since Thornfell — fully, deliberately, all the buried channels at once — and it roared up through her like spring meltwater, ten years of dammed power flooding loose, and for one dizzy heartbeat she understood why people sold their souls for this feeling.

Then she began to spend it.

The rite required a vessel of equal weight. So she poured. Every charm she'd ever woven, every hedge-blessing, every whispered ward over smuggled goods and frightened children and her own guilty heart — she unpicked them all and fed the threads into the circle, and the salt drank them and glowed faintly white, and the water began to turn like a slow eye.

It hurt. Not the way fire hurts. The way grief hurts — a leaving, one loss after another after another, each charm a small funeral. Her mother's voice teaching her the first knot-spell. Gone. The fox-charm that had saved her from a wolf at thirteen. Gone. The little warmth-magic she'd knitted into Serah's cloak two nights ago, wordlessly, while the other woman slept. Gone, and she hadn't known she'd miss that one most of all.

Outside, hooves. Many.

"Edmund." Serah's voice, level as a spirit-level. "Riders. A dozen or more. They've found the road."

"How long?"

"Minutes."

"Then keep them off me for minutes." Sweat ran into her eyes; the salt circle steamed. She kept pouring.

The third part of the rite didn't exist yet. She could feel the gap like a missing tooth — Aldous's half ended at the vessel, at the spending, and beyond it the original writer had died with the ending in her head. Edmund had to build the bridge herself, in real time, out of instinct and the chapel's patient listening.

*An oath needs a speaker,* she thought. *And a witness.*

She opened her mouth and gave the rite her name.

Not *Edmund Montrose*, the smuggler. Not the apprentice name, the runaway name, any of the names she'd worn like coats. She spoke the true one, the deep one, the name her gift answered to — the one her mother had whispered over her cradle and never told another living soul — and speaking it aloud, offering it into the circle, was like opening a vein.

The chapel took it. She felt the name leave her the way you feel a tooth leave your jaw: shockingly little pain, then an enormous wrongness, then absence.

The water in the bowl flared white.

The salt circle burned blue.

And far away — miles off, in the palace, in the throne room, under the crown of living thorns — something ancient and hungry lifted its head, because it felt the door begin to open.

* * *

"They're dismounting," Serah called. Steel whispered free of its sheath. "Edmund, whatever you're doing—"

"Almost." The word came out shredded. She was crying without noticing, tears cutting tracks through the blood on her face. What remained of her gift guttered in her chest like a candle in a draft — one flame left, the last of it, the seed of everything she'd ever been. The rite wanted that too. All of it. Equal weight meant equal.

She thought of the king, whispering *unbind* into his own dark for years.

She thought of Aldous, dying against a gate, buying her minutes with his life.

She thought of a girl on a salt road with flour-sack charms, so proud of her small hidden light.

*Take it,* she told the rite. *Take all of it.*

And the last flame went out of her — quietly, the way a lamp goes out, no roar at all — and the chapel filled with light that wasn't light, and the bowl of rainwater cracked clean in half, and somewhere beneath the world a door that had been locked for thirty years swung open on screaming hinges.

Edmund fell forward onto the marble, empty. Hollowed. Alive.

She had no magic. She had no secret name. She lay on the floor of a dead queen's chapel with nothing inside her but Edmund, whoever that turned out to be.

Boots hit the chapel steps. Serah's blade came up to meet them.

Through the doorway, framed by rain and torchlight, the Lord Protector smiled.

"Took you long enough," he said. "Bring her. The king is asking for her by name."

Edmund, still gasping on the stone, felt the words land wrong — felt the shape of them, the *craft* of them, the thorn-logic underneath.

The thing knew she was coming.

It had sent an invitation.
