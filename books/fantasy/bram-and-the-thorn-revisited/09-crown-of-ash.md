## 9. Crown of Ash

**Edmund**

The throne room had been built to make people feel small, and it worked on everyone but the thing sitting in the king's chair.

Edmund came through the doors with her hands open and her gift burning up her arms like she'd swallowed a bonfire. Behind her, Serah's boots rang on the marble, and behind Serah, the doors boomed shut — Aldous's last gift, the old lock mechanism he'd jammed with his own broken knife. He wasn't behind them anymore. He was in the dark of the forgotten ways, holding a gate closed with his body, and she would not think about that yet. She couldn't. If she thought about it, her knees would go, and the rite required her standing.

The Lord Protector stood at the foot of the dais, and the court had emptied around him like water down a drain. He didn't look surprised. That was the worst of it. He looked like a man who had been waiting for the bill to come due.

"Captain Vane," he said. "You're late to your own execution. Both of you."

"The king first," Edmund said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Move away from him."

"He is *my* king. I have kept him. I have fed him. Eleven years of oaths, girl, sworn at that altar, poured into him like wine — do you know what that buys?" The Lord Protector spread his hands, and Edmund saw, for the first time, the fine black threads running under the skin of his wrists, thorn-roots mapped in veins. He wasn't the thing's master. He was its first tether. "Stability. A realm that holds. You would unmake all of it for one hollow man's freedom."

"He's not hollow," Edmund said. "He's buried. There's a difference, and it's the whole point of me being here."

The king's head turned. Slowly, with the creak of a tree bending in wind, the thorn crown shifted, and the wrong eyes found her — and then, beneath them, for half a heartbeat, a man's eyes. Terrified. Awake enough to be terrified.

*Unbind*, he had whispered into her mind a week ago, and she had heard it every night since, like a stone dropped down a well.

She walked forward. The Lord Protector didn't stop her. She understood why a moment later, when the blackthorn on the king's brow *bloomed* — flowers of dark flesh opening along the branches, and the whole room filled with a smell like honey and grave-dirt, and the entity spoke through the king's mouth with a voice like roots splitting stone.

*Little hedge-witch. I have tasted your gift across a hundred miles. Come closer. I am always hungry, and you are carrying such a feast.*

"I know," Edmund said. "That's the idea."

She had spent the escape through the forgotten ways rehearsing this, and Aldous's notes were inked on her left forearm in her own blood, the way hedge-witches did the work that mattered. The rite of unbinding wasn't a spell, exactly. It was a *trade*. The old queen's hedge-witch had bound the thing to the king with her own gift as the knot, and it had eaten her alive from the inside over a single terrible night, and the binding had held because a binding made of a whole self holds longer than one made of words.

To unbind it, Edmund had to offer a knot of her own — and then, at the moment the thing took hold of the bait, *pull*, and burn the knot, and the thing with it.

Her whole gift. Her whole self, the self she'd been since she was seven years old and made a fever break with a whispered charm. Everything she was.

*Everything I was*, she corrected herself. She was about to find out what was left.

"Edmund." Serah's voice, low, at her shoulder. The witch-hunter's hand hovered near her sword, not drawing. "Tell me you have a way out of this."

"No," Edmund said. "But I have a way through. Hold the door."

"There's no door. Aldous jammed it."

"Then hold the room."

* * *

**Serah**

Serah had sworn four oaths in her life. To her father, to the Order, to the crown, and to the truth — the last one quietly, at nineteen, over a burned village that the Order's reports called a necessary measure. She had kept the first three and told herself the fourth was compatible with them.

The Lord Protector's order, delivered three days ago in a room that smelled of ink and rot, had finally made a liar of her.

*When the hedge-witch has served her purpose, Captain, you will put her down yourself. An example. The realm needs to see what happens to the gifted who reach the capital.*

She had said *yes, my lord*, because that was what captains said. And then she had gone to the cells and warned a smuggler-witch, and burned her career to the ground one honest sentence at a time.

Now she stood between Edmund and the throne room doors, sword drawn, watching the Lord Protector's face do something terrible. It wasn't rage. It was *calculation* — the look of a man counting oaths like coins and finding Serah's purse empty.

"Captain," he said. "Your oath. Your *sworn word*. Kneel, and all of this is forgiven. The crown is generous to its instruments."

"The crown," Serah said, "is currently a shrub." She heard Edmund laugh — a short, sharp, terrified sound — and was absurdly glad of it. "You want to know what I learned in eleven years of hunting the gifted, my lord? Most of them were hiding from exactly the thing sitting on that throne. I was the leash. You were the hand that held it. And I am done."

The Lord Protector moved, and it was wrong — too fast, too smooth, the thorn-threads in his wrists flaring black as the entity lent him its strength. He crossed the distance to Serah in two strides that should have taken six, and his hand closed on her sword-arm, and the grip was cold as a winter fence.

"You will kneel," he said, and the voice was half his and half the roots', "and you will swear, and you will *feed*—"

Serah did the thing no duelist would do and no witch-hunter would expect: she dropped her sword.

It clattered on the marble, and in the half-second of his confusion she drove her dagger — the short one, the one the Order said was for mercy — up under his arm, into the knot of thorn-roots she'd seen pulsing under his collar. Black sap sprayed across her face. It burned like nettles and smelled like the inside of a coffin, and the Lord Protector screamed in two voices.

"You swore," he gasped at her, folding, the threads in his wrists writhing. "You *swore*—"

"I know," Serah said. She picked her sword back up. Her hands were shaking. "That's rather the point."

* * *

**Edmund**

The thing in the king came for her gift like a flood coming for a lit candle.

Edmund stood at the foot of the dais and opened herself — every ward down, every buried charm unpinned, the hedge-witch gift she'd hidden for eleven years suddenly blazing out of her like a signal fire. It was the most naked she had ever been. The entity *lunged*, and the king's body convulsed, and the thorn crown grew a hand's length in a single shrieking moment, branches spearing down toward her—

—and she let it take hold.

The pain was immediate and total. It felt like being read. Every memory with magic in it — the fever she'd broken, the charms she'd smuggled, the night she'd first felt the gift rise in her chest like a second heartbeat — the thing drank them, catalogued them, *claimed* them. Her knees buckled. The marble was cold against her palms.

*Yes*, it breathed, everywhere, in the roots and the walls and the king's ruined mouth. *Yes. A whole self. A knot that will hold for a hundred years. I will wear you as I wore him, little witch, and your realm will be so *stable*—*

"Now," Edmund whispered.

She pulled.

The rite was not elegant. Aldous's notes had called it *the burning of the knot*, and it was exactly what it sounded like: she took the thing's grip on her gift — its roots sunk into the living fabric of her magic — and she *ignited* it, all of it, her gift as both the rope and the fire. The feedback went through her like a struck bell. She heard herself screaming and was distantly embarrassed by the sound.

The throne room went white.

Inside the white, Edmund found the buried king. He was small — the way a man is small at the bottom of a well, looking up — and he was *him*, gray-bearded and hollow-cheeked and weeping without sound. She took his hand. It was the only thing left to do that wasn't fire.

"Come on, then," she said. "Your realm needs you. It's in a state."

The white burned. The thing burned. She felt it tearing free of the king the way a root tears free of stone — slow, grinding, *reluctant* — and she fed the tear with everything, the fever-charm, the smuggled blessings, the first heartbeat, the last, until the well of her ran dry and kept pouring anyway, because that was what the rite demanded and she had said yes to it in a ruined chapel with ash on her hands.

The last thing the entity did was speak, one final time, directly into her mind, and it did not threaten.

*You will be no one*, it said, almost wonderingly. *I am eating the part of you the world could see. What walks out of this room will be only a woman.*

"Good," Edmund said, and burned it to ash.

* * *

**Serah**

When the light faded, Serah got to her feet off the marble — she didn't remember falling — and took inventory of the world.

The Lord Protector was dead, or mostly; the thorn-threads in his wrists had gone gray and brittle as winter twig, and he lay crumpled at the foot of the dais with her dagger still in him. The throne room smelled of smoke and, underneath it, faintly, of spring.

On the dais, the king sat slumped in his chair, and the blackthorn crown was crumbling off his brow, flaking into ash that drifted down like dirty snow. His eyes were his own. Old, watery, *human*. He was looking at the witch kneeling in front of him — at Edmund, who was on her hands and knees, gray-faced, breathing in ragged pulls.

"Girl," the king said. His voice was rust and disuse. "What did it cost you?"

Edmund lifted her head. She looked, Serah thought, exactly the same and completely different — the way a house looks the same after the fire's put out. She raised one hand, palm up, the way hedge-witches did when they called their gift, and nothing came. Not a flicker. Not a warmth. Her hand was just a hand.

She stared at it for a long moment.

"Everything," she said. Then, to Serah's astonishment, she laughed — a real one, cracked and exhausted and free. "And it was worth every coin. Your Majesty, you're welcome. Now if someone could help me up, I'd like to get out of this palace before anyone remembers I exist."

The king struggled upright, one hand on the arm of the throne, and looked at her with eleven years of buried grief and gratitude. "I will remember," he said. "I will remember *everything*. And the realm will know what was done here, and by whom—"

"No," Serah said, and stepped up onto the dais, sword still bare in her hand. "With respect, Majesty — it will. But not today, and not by decree. Today she walks out of here as no one, because that's what she wants, and after what she just did, what she wants is the first order I intend to follow."

The king looked at her. Then at Edmund. Then, at last, he did the thing Serah hadn't seen a monarch do in her entire career.

He nodded.

* * *

**Edmund**

They walked out through the great doors — unjammed now, swinging wide at a touch — into a corridor full of courtiers frozen mid-flee, and Edmund felt every eye on her and found, to her surprise, that it weighed nothing. She was no one's quarry. No one's apprentice. No one's asset, or example, or feast.

She was just a woman with ash on her hands and a witch-hunter at her shoulder, walking toward a salt road and a mountain pass and, eventually, home.

"Thornfell," Serah said, as they pushed through into the gray morning light. "You'll want to see what Aldous left you. He kept a ledger, Edmund. Eleven years of it. Everything he knew, everything he owed — and there's a page with your name on it that I think you should read before anyone else does."

Edmund stopped on the palace steps, the capital spread out below her, smoke still rising faintly from the throne room's chimneys.

"A ledger," she said. "Of course he did. That stubborn, cowardly, magnificent old man."

"Is that grief or fury?"

"Ask me again at the pass." She started down the steps. "Come on, Captain. We've a long road, and for the first time in my life, I'm walking it as exactly one person — and I intend to find out who she is."
