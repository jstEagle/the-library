## 10. No One's Apprentice

The king woke on the third morning after the burning, and the first thing he asked for was not a priest but bread and salt.

Edmund heard it secondhand, from a chambermaid with flour on her apron and gossip in her eyes. *He ate standing at the window,* she said. *He wept. He thanked every servant by name.* The palace, which had held its breath for years without knowing it, exhaled.

Edmund did not go to see him. She sat in the ruined chapel instead, in the old queen's dust, and waited to feel different.

She didn't. That was the thing no one told you about giving up magic: there was no wound. No scar where the gift had been. Just a quiet in her chest like a room after the furniture's been carried out — the shape of what was missing still printed on the air. She kept reaching for it out of habit. Reaching for the warmth behind her sternum that had answered her since childhood, the low hum under the world's skin. What came back was silence. Ordinary, mortal, human silence.

She found she could live inside it.

* * *

The summons came at noon, delivered by a page too young to hide his awe. *His Majesty requests the Lady Edmund.*

*Not a lady,* she almost said. But she followed the boy up through the palace's living levels — past the throne room, its blackthorn crown now a heap of grey ash swept into an urn, past the council chamber where men argued in loud, healthy voices about grain tariffs as if the world had always been sane — to a small solar with tall windows and a table set for tea.

King Aldric stood when she entered. That surprised her. Kings did not stand; kings were stood for.

"You're shorter than I imagined," he said.

"Most people imagine me taller."

"I imagined you older, too." He gestured to a chair. "The woman who pulled a god out of my skull should be eighty and terrible."

"The woman who pulled a god out of your skull was mostly improvising."

Something moved across his face — not quite a smile, but the ground a smile might grow from. Up close, his eyes were just eyes. Brown, tired, human. She hadn't realized how much she'd braced herself until she saw nothing behind them to brace against.

"Serah Vane tells me you refused a title," he said.

"I refused three titles. She's underselling me."

"A barony. A seat at court. And the office of Royal Hedge-Witch." He said the last one carefully, watching her. "Vacant these eleven years."

Eleven years. Since Aldous's mistress had bound the thorn-thing into a dying king's heir and paid for it with her life. Since the crown had begun feeding.

"Your Majesty," Edmund said, "I watched what that office costs. I read the notes of the woman who held it. She wrote her rite in the margins of a prayer book because she knew no one would ever be allowed to publish it. She knew the palace would eat her and call it service." She turned her cup in her hands. "You're offering me a leash with a gilded collar. I've worn enough leashes this year. I'd like to try going without."

"And if the realm needs you?"

"Then the realm can send a letter. I hear the salt roads carry post eventually."

The king laughed — short, startled, real. Then he was quiet a long moment, looking out the window at a capital still learning how to breathe.

"Aldous Vane died holding a gate," he said. "For a girl he sold once. Why?"

Edmund thought about it honestly, because he deserved honesty and she was done trading in anything else.

"Because betrayal is a debt," she said, "and he paid it late, but he paid it whole. Because he spent his life serving a woman who chose truth over safety, and when it was finally his turn to choose, he couldn't do anything else. Because—" she stopped. Started again. "Because love doesn't have to be loyal to be real. I think that's the only thing I learned all the way to the end."

The king looked at her for a long time.

"The office stays open," he said finally. "Not as a collar. As a door. If you ever walk back through it, it's yours."

"Thank you, Your Majesty. But don't hold it open on my account."

"Call me Aldric." He poured the tea himself, badly. "And drink that before you go. You look like someone who hasn't been offered anything freely in months."

She drank it. It was terrible. It was the best cup she'd ever had.

* * *

Serah was waiting in the courtyard with two horses and no escort, which told Edmund everything about how official this departure wasn't.

"You refused the king," Serah said. Not a question.

"I refused the king."

"He'll have your portrait painted anyway. They always do."

"Then I hope they give me better cheekbones."

Serah almost smiled. Almost. On her face it was practically a confession. She swung onto her horse — the same iron-grey gelding she'd ridden out of Thornfell with a prisoner roped behind her saddle, though neither of them mentioned that.

"You know," Edmund said, mounting up beside her, "last time we took this road together, you had a warrant with my name on it."

"I burned the warrant."

"I noticed the absence of paperwork."

"Burning things is becoming a habit in this kingdom." Serah turned her horse toward the gates. "Besides. Warrants are oaths written down. I'm retired from oaths."

"That lasted less than a day. Impressive even for you."

They rode out through the city gates in the gold of late afternoon, past market stalls reopening, past children chasing a dog between the legs of the guard, past ordinary life reassembling itself piece by piece over the bones of something monstrous. At the crest of the hill Edmund looked back once. The palace caught the light and threw it back, clean stone and clean glass, and somewhere inside it a man was eating bread and salt and remembering his own name.

She didn't feel smaller than that building anymore. That was the strange part. She felt exactly her own size, and it fit.

* * *

The salt road was slower going north. Of course it was; they let it be. No riders sweeping passes, no smuggler's schedule, no mentor's messages scratched in charcoal on fence posts. They stopped where they wanted. Slept when dark fell. Once, in a river town whose name Edmund never learned, Serah won money at dice by cheating transparently, and Edmund laughed so hard she had to sit down on the tavern step, and Serah pretended not to be pleased and fooled no one.

Her gift didn't come back. She checked, some nights, the way you touch a tooth that isn't there. Nothing. Just her own pulse, her own breath, her own small human weather. And yet —

In the third week, crossing a high meadow full of early gentians, she knelt to fix her horse's loose shoe and found she knew the animal was frightened before it shied. Not magic. Just attention. Just the slow, unglamorous work of watching a thing long enough to understand it. She talked the mare calm with a flat hand and a low voice, and when she stood up, Serah was watching her with an expression Edmund couldn't name.

"What?" Edmund said.

"Nothing," Serah said. "You used to do that with charms. Now you do it with horses."

"Now I do it with myself. Mostly."

They crested Thornfell's shoulder on a morning of hard blue sky, and there below them lay the pass — the smuggler paths, the hidden hollows, the little shrine to a nameless road-god that Aldous had maintained with more devotion than he'd ever shown a church. Edmund dismounted at the shrine. It was untended, snow-crusted, half-swallowed by thornbush. She cleared the snow away with her bare hands and set down the only offering she had left: the stub of candle she'd carried since the cells.

No flame leapt up for her. No warmth answered. She struck flint against steel like any mortal woman, and the candle caught, and burned, and that was all.

It was enough.

"Aldous," she said quietly. "Debt paid. Both directions."

Behind her, Serah waited without hurrying, the way equals wait.

"So," Serah said as they remounted. "What does the famous hedge-witch of Thornfell do now? With no hedge. And no witch."

Edmund looked down the long thread of the road — the pass, the valley, the smoke of chimneys where people who owed her nothing lived lives she might choose to touch or not. Nobody's apprentice. Nobody's quarry. Nobody's instrument. Just Edmund, holding a name that belonged to no one but herself.

"Whatever I want," she said. "Starting with breakfast. There's a widow in the lower village who makes honey buns worth committing minor treason for."

"And after breakfast?"

"After breakfast—" She nudged her horse forward, and the wind off the peaks came down to meet them, cold and clean and carrying nothing but the smell of snow. "After breakfast, I thought I'd teach you to read charm-signs. Not to use them. Just so you stop glaring at them like they insulted your mother."

"They usually have insulted my mother. Most of them are forged."

"See? This is why you need lessons."

They rode down into the pass together, side by side, two women with burned bridges and open roads, and the mountain closed green and bright around them — not hiding anything, not hunting anyone, just home.

Somewhere ahead, in the lower village, the widow was pulling honey buns from her oven, unaware that her quiet morning was about to acquire two boarders, one horse apiece, and opinions.

Edmund smiled, and rode on.
