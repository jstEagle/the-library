## 1. The Taster's Oath

The bells of the Regent's palace rang the hour of first light, and Wren Vane was already awake, already dressed, already tasting.

This was the order of things. The bells, then the taster, then—only after—the Regent's breakfast. A kingdom ran on sequence, and Wren had spent twenty-two years learning to be the least memorable part of any sequence they touched. The kitchen staff called them *the shadow in the whites*, though never to their face and mostly without malice. Cooks respected a person who swallowed poison for a living; it was one of the few honest trades left in the palace of Amberhold, where everything else on the menu came with a side of flattery or fear.

Wren stood at the long steel table in the tasting chamber—a narrow room off the main kitchens, windowless, lit by two lamps that turned everyone's face the color of old candle wax—and worked through the morning's service with the calm of a surgeon. Quail eggs poached in cream. Honeyed rolls from the southern ovens. A compote of mountain berries that smelled, faintly, of home.

They took a spoonful of each. Held each mouthful for the counted ten breaths. Rolled it against the tongue, because some toxins announced themselves only to the soft tissue there, and watched the light through the spoon's curve for the oily sheen that betrayed certain mineral salts. Then they spat into the brass basin, rinsed with watered wine, and moved to the next dish.

It looked like prayer. It wasn't. Prayer asked for something. Tasting only refused.

"Berry compote next," said Mistress Halloway, the under-steward who managed the tasters' schedule, her voice flat as slate. She was a broad woman with flour permanently ghosting her forearms and eyes that had stopped registering surprise sometime before Wren was born. "New supplier. Orchard cart out of Thornfell foothills."

Something flickered behind Wren's ribs—not quite recognition, more like a dog lifting its head at a distant whistle. Thornfell. The mountains rose blue and jagged beyond the palace's eastern windows every day of Wren's life, close enough to touch on clear mornings, far enough that no one who mattered ever looked at them twice. Everyone knew the Thornfell roads were closed since the Regency began. Officially, there were no roads at all up there. Officially, the mountains held nothing but goats and weather.

Wren knew better. Wren knew about the switchback called the Liar's Stair that doubled back on itself three times so convincingly that even army mappers had drawn it wrong. Knew about the hollow beneath the old shrine at Coldbrook where smugglers cached brandy and bolts of unmarked silk. Knew the smell of pine resin burned over juniper, which meant *safe passage tonight* if you happened to be walking past the third milestone marker at dusk. Knew all of this the way other people knew their own hands—because an orphan raised in the warrens beneath Thornfell learned the mountains' veins the way palace children learned their letters, and forgot neither.

None of which appeared on Wren's face. Wren's face appeared on nothing, most days. That was its whole craft.

"The compote is fine," Wren said, after the ten breaths and the spit and the rinse. "Slight bitterness in the skins. Underripe fruit, not preparation."

Halloway noted it down. "The Regent prefers them sweeter."

"The Regent," Wren said, "prefers to eat."

That earned the ghost of a smile, gone as quickly as it came. Halloway had been kind to Wren once, in the way of people too tired to sustain cruelty: extra bread at the end of shifts, a name remembered correctly on the second try. In the palace economy, this amounted to love.

There were four tasters in the Regent's household. Two had been with her since the coup fifteen years ago—gray men named Ostler and Crane who spoke of their work the way priests speak of liturgy. One was new, a nervous boy called Tam whose hands shook so badly he'd once spilled a full spoon of almond soup down his own whites and spent the afternoon convinced he was dying of cyanide when he was merely clumsy. And then there was Wren, who had passed the entrance examination five years ago with a perfect score and had been quietly remarkable ever since in the way that made superiors forget your name rather than promote you.

Invisibility was a discipline. Wren practiced it like scales on a fiddle. Stand in doorways, not beside them. Answer questions with just enough accuracy to be useful and just little enough personality to be forgotten. Wear the same expression for grief, boredom, and mild interest; the palace read faces the way scholars read texts, and a blank page told no stories. When required to attend the Regent's table in person—which happened on high feast days, when protocol demanded the tasters be displayed like silverware—Wren stood at the end of the row and let Ostler take the center. Let Crane answer for the group. Be furniture. Furniture survived.

Because here was the truth Wren had understood since childhood, first in the warrens and then confirmed daily in the palace: being nobody was the safest profession in any kingdom run by somebody. The noticed died of what they were. The unnoticed died only of accidents, and accidents could be dodged.

Usually.

* * *

The trouble began with the soup.

It was mid-morning service, the second seating, and the kitchen ran in its usual controlled roar—sixteen cooks, forty pots, Halloway shouting measurements across the steam. The Regent's table today would host the Lord Chancellor of the coastal provinces, a man rumored to be negotiating for a reduction in grain tariffs, which meant the menu had been designed to impress rather than nourish. White soup of young almonds, thickened with rice flour, perfumed with orange flower water. It was a delicate dish, expensive, and—Wren noted as they lifted the first spoon to their lips—prepared correctly in every visible particular.

Ten breaths.

At breath six, Wren's tongue began to burn.

Not the clean fire of pepper or the bright sting of strong spirits. This was a low, creeping heat, chemical, wrong, spreading backward along the soft palate toward the throat with the patient confidence of something that knew exactly what it was doing. Wren spat. Rinsed. Spat again, longer, working the water deep along the gumline where certain alkaloids hid, and felt the burn recede but not vanish, like a tide pulling back but leaving the sand wet.

"Halloway." Their voice came out steadier than their pulse. "Who prepared the white soup?"

The kitchen didn't stop, but it quieted, the way a room quiets when a word like *poison* passes through it even unspoken. Halloway crossed to the basin, sniffed, dipped a fingertip, touched it to her tongue. Her brow folded.

"Bitter," she said. "Almonds shouldn't—" She stopped. Looked up. "Tam's station. He supervised the blanching."

Everyone looked at Tam. Tam went white as his apron and started explaining that he'd done everything right, he'd checked the nuts himself, he'd *smelled* them—

"Nobody's accusing anyone," Wren said, and heard how the words settled the room, how even Halloway straightened slightly. This was the part of the trade they never taught in the examinations: the taster's authority extended past chemistry into diplomacy. Accuse wrongly and you'd start a purge among the cooks; accuse rightly and you'd start one anyway. Better to move like water. "Bitter almonds carry the toxin naturally. Sometimes a bad batch slips through. But I want the rest of the pot tested before it goes anywhere near the table. Silver needle, if we can find one."

They found one. Silver darkens in the presence of certain sulfur compounds, and while arsenic—the classic—wouldn't show, the needle's tip came away faintly gray regardless, which meant *something*. Not enough to kill. Enough to sicken, to blister the mouth, to put the Regent's guest abed for a week and the Regent's reputation in question at precisely the moment she needed the coast compliant.

Or—to be precise, and Wren's mind was always precise at moments like this, cold and itemizing as a ledger—it meant someone had dosed a dish with a mild irritant disguised as a natural flaw, in a quantity calibrated to embarrass rather than kill.

Calibrated. That was the word that wouldn't leave. Amateurs poisoned to kill. Professionals poisoned to send messages. But whoever did this had poisoned to *almost* kill—to come close enough to frighten, in a dish destined for a table protected by four tasters, any one of whom might have taken the first swallow.

Any one of whom. Wren stood very still amid the kitchen noise and let the thought finish forming, ugly and complete.

The soup hadn't reached the Regent. It had reached *the taster*. The first spoonful always went to whoever drew tasting duty, and the roster was posted the night before, and Wren's name had been on it for the white soup since yesterday evening. Anyone with access to the schedule—whoever wrote it, whoever read it—had known exactly whose mouth would meet that spoon first.

And the dose had been small. Small enough that a trained taster with Wren's particular constitution would suffer and survive. Wren had been swallowing controlled doses of the common toxins since childhood—part of the training, the immunity built dose by dose the way a smith builds callus—but immunity was a wall with limits, and whoever had seasoned that soup had known roughly where those limits lay. Known, or guessed well enough.

Someone inside the palace wanted a taster dead. Or wanted a taster frightened, or removed, or simply reminded that they were mortal. And had chosen Wren.

*Me,* Wren thought, and the strangeness of it sat in the chest like a swallowed stone. *Why me?* Of the four, Wren was the least. The invisible one. The blank page. You don't send messages to furniture.

Unless the message wasn't for the taster at all. Unless the taster was merely the envelope.

"I'll re-taste everything from the second seating myself," Wren said aloud. "Pull Tam off service for the day—he's shaken, and shaken hands make mistakes. Halloway, I want the roster for tomorrow posted *after* the evening meal, not before. If anyone asks why, tell them the steward's orders."

Halloway studied them for a moment with those flour-dusted, unsurprised eyes. "You think it was deliberate."

"I think," Wren said, "that bitter almonds are having an unusual year."

* * *

They finished the shift. That was the discipline of it—fear was a luxury the body couldn't afford mid-service, so Wren folded it small and tucked it behind the sternum and tasted fourteen more dishes with hands that did not shake, because shaking hands were a signature, and signatures got you noticed.

But afterward, when the lamps were trimmed and the kitchen exhaled into its afternoon lull, Wren did not go to the tasters' dormitory. Instead they climbed.

The palace of Amberhold had been a fortress once, before the Regency smoothed it into something softer and more surveilled, and its bones still showed in places the stewards preferred not to discuss. A stair behind the laundry scullery that went up three floors and stopped at a bricked archway. A gallery above the great hall accessible through a door painted the same gray as the wall. And above the eastern wing, a disused bell platform where the old alarm bell had been melted down for cannon during the coup, leaving an open iron frame with a view of everything.

Wren went there because it was the one place in the palace where nobody looked. From the platform, Amberhold spread below like a diagram of itself: the kitchens exhaling steam, the barracks marching yard, the administrative offices where clerks copied the rosters, the Regent's tower standing apart from everything else the way a blade stands apart from a scabbard. And beyond it all, east and endless, the Thornfells—blue in the afternoon haze, snow still clinging to their high shoulders even in summer, beautiful the way dangerous things are beautiful.

Home, Wren thought. Both of them. The mountains below my childhood and the palace above my adulthood, and I have spent my whole life learning how to disappear inside each.

The burn had faded from their tongue, but the memory of it hadn't—that creeping chemical patience, the sense of a dose measured by someone who knew. Wren ran the possibilities the way they'd run a taste: systematically, spitting out what failed. A cook with a grudge? Possible, but cooks poisoned the Regent, not the taster; killing the taster achieved nothing except a new taster, and there was always a queue. Tam? Too clumsy, too transparent. Ostler and Crane? They had seniority, position, the center of the row on feast days. What did they gain from Wren's death?

Nothing. That was the problem. Every answer came back *nothing*, which meant the motive lived somewhere Wren couldn't see—in some ledger Wren had never been shown, some account with Wren's name in it that they had never signed.

The wind off the mountains carried the smell of pine and cold stone, and for a moment Wren let themselves imagine the Liar's Stair, the cache at Coldbrook, the resin fires at dusk. Escape routes. They'd mapped them idly over five years of service—not seriously, not with intent, just the way a caged thing notices the shape of its bars. If I ever need to run, the knowledge sat patiently in some back room of the mind, I know the way.

They'd never needed it. They'd never expected to.

Below, a figure emerged from the administrative wing and stood a moment in the courtyard, looking up. Gray robes. The cut of them said *clerk*, but the stillness said otherwise—clerks hurried; this man simply waited, head tilted, studying the eastern wing with the unhurried attention of someone confirming a detail.

Then he turned and walked back inside, and Wren's skin prickled with a certainty that had no evidence attached to it whatsoever: *he knows I'm up here.*

That night, the new roster went up after supper as instructed. Wren checked it twice. Their name appeared on the morning service, ordinary, unremarkable, written in the same clerk-hand as every other name.

But beneath the ink, so faint it might have been a smudge, might have been the paper's grain, there was a mark. A small pressed seal, the kind made by a signet ring set carelessly into wax that had dripped onto the page—a circle bisected by a vertical line, half worn away.

Wren had seen that symbol before. Once. Years ago, scratched into the lintel of a smuggler's cache in the Thornfells, beside words in an alphabet no one in the warrens could read.

They stood in the lamplight of the empty corridor for a long moment, the palace settling around them into its nighttime hush, and understood that the safe life they had constructed—dish by dish, breath by counted breath, anonymity laid down like mortar—was thinner than they'd believed. Perhaps it had always been thin. Perhaps somewhere in this building or beyond it, someone had known Wren Vane's name for a long time, and today they had finally begun to write.

Wren touched their tongue to the roof of their mouth, where the ghost of the burn still lingered, and made the taster's silent vow: *I will know what I am swallowing.*

Starting tomorrow, that included everything.

They walked back toward the dormitory through halls that suddenly felt less like home and more like a trap that fed you well, and behind them, in the dark of the corridor, the lamp flame bent—though the windows were shut—toward the east. Toward the mountains.
