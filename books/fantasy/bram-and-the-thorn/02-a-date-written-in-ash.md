## 2. A Date Written in Ash

Wren Vane had a rule about running: never run. Running made people look. People who looked remembered. So when the bell of the palace kitchens rang the hour of the midday meal and Wren's hands were still shaking hard enough to rattle the tasting spoons against their tray, they did not run. They walked. Slowly. The way a person walks when nothing at all has happened to them.

Inside, though—inside was another country.

The poison had been a small one. That was what kept circling back through their thoughts as they crossed the servants' corridor, past the scullery boys hauling water, past the laundresses arguing over a torn sheet. A small poison. Almond-bitter at the edge, sweet underneath, the kind of thing that killed a sparrow in a quarter hour and a grown man in two. Wren had caught it on the fourth spoonful of the Regent's plum conserve—the way they'd been trained to catch things, tongue to palate, wait three heartbeats, spit—and for a moment the world had gone soft at its corners, and the stone floor had seemed very far away, and then the counter-agent in their own blood, the slow accumulation of twenty years of deliberate small doses, had pushed back like a tide reversing.

They had not died. But they should not have tasted it at all.

That was the part that made their hands shake. Not the nearness of death—a taster lived folded against death's shoulder, cheek to cheek, and grew numb to its warmth—but the arithmetic. The conserve had come up from the provisioning stores sealed and waxed. It had passed the under-taster. It had passed the kitchen steward's nose. Three checkpoints, all clean, and then poison sitting in it like a stone at the bottom of a well. Which meant the poison had entered between the seal and the spoon. Which meant it had entered inside these walls.

Someone in the palace wanted a taster dead.

Not the Regent. If the Regent wanted you dead you simply stopped being scheduled. Not the steward—too clumsy, too traceable. Someone careful enough to slip a dose through a sealed jar, and careless in exactly one way: they had forgotten, or never known, that Wren Vane was not an ordinary taster. Ordinary tasters died of small poisons. Wren had been drinking death by thimblefuls since before they could write their own name, building the immunity the way other children built towers of river stones, one on top of another, patiently, without knowing why. Master Corvel, who had trained them, had called it *the long table*. You sit at it your whole life, he said, and every dish is a lesson, and if you learn everything the table teaches, you will outlive us all.

He had been right about everything except himself.

Wren reached the door to the cold pantry, slipped through, and stood a moment in the dark among the hanging hams and the barrels of salted herring, breathing the cold air, letting the shaking move down from their hands into their shoulders and out. Think. Whoever it was would try again. The next dose would be larger, or subtler, or aimed at something they hadn't built a wall against. And there was nowhere in the palace to be safe, because the palace itself was the trap; the palace was a mouth and everyone in it was food, and Wren had known this for years in the way you know a thing without letting yourself look at it directly.

They needed somewhere outside the walls. Somewhere no one looked. Somewhere nobody.

There was exactly one place Wren knew of where nobody went willingly, and it happened to be downhill, through the laundry court, behind the disused chapel: the archive of Ilse Marrow.

* * *

The scholars' annex had once housed forty archivists and the crown's entire memory. Now it housed one disgraced old woman and the books too unimportant or too dangerous to burn. The Regency kept her the way a miser keeps a coin he despises—because getting rid of it would mean admitting it existed. Ilse Marrow catalogued forbidden histories in a room that smelled of dust and lamp oil, and she did it loudly enough that everyone knew to avoid her, which meant no one ever came, which meant it was the safest room in the palace, and Ilse knew it, and charged accordingly.

"Back again," she said without looking up when Wren let themselves in. She sat behind a desk stacked with folios, a tiny woman with white hair cropped short as a soldier's and ink stains to the elbow. "You have the look of someone who wants the back corridor."

"I need to not exist until the evening shift changes."

"That costs double on a feast day." Now she looked up, and her eyes—pale, quick, unsettlingly kind for a woman so famously unpleasant—narrowed. "You're gray around the mouth. What did you taste?"

Wren hesitated. Telling Ilse Marrow anything was like dropping a coin into a deep well; you never heard it land, but you always suspected it was being spent somewhere. On the other hand, Ilse was the only person in the palace who asked questions because she wanted answers rather than leverage.

"Plum conserve," Wren said. "Bitter almond under the sugar. Fourth spoon."

Ilse set down her pen with great care, the way she might lay down a knife. "Fourth spoon," she repeated. "And here you stand. Gray, but standing."

"I'm difficult to kill. It's a professional trait."

"Mm." Ilse rose, joints cracking like green wood, and shuffled to the shelf behind her desk—not the catalogued shelves but a loose stack of volumes leaning against the wall like drunks outside a tavern. She pulled out a slim book bound in cracked black leather, held it a moment without opening it, then put it back and took a different one instead, and then, muttering, took the first one again. "Sit down, child. No—there, in the window chair, where I can see your face. Light matters when a person is deciding how much truth to swallow."

Wren sat. The window gave onto the laundry court, gray cobbles, wet sheets. Below, a guard crossed the yard and did not glance up. Nobody glanced up. It was, Wren thought, the whole art of their life, distilled into a view: be the window nobody looks into.

"You know what I do here," Ilse said. She dragged her stool over and sat close, lowering her voice even though the room held no one else. "I catalogue what the Regency burned. Not everything—they were thorough, the burners usually are—but books travel, copies breed, and some things were hidden before the fires were lit. For eleven years I have read everything I could get my hands on about the kingdom that used to be where this palace is standing." Her eyes went briefly to the floor, as if she could see through stone to the foundations, to whatever lay beneath. "Velthmar."

"They say Velthmar never existed," Wren said carefully. "A fable. Old maps drawn wrong."

"They say the sun rises in the east, too, and most of them have never checked." Ilse smiled without humor. "Velthmar existed. It ruled these mountains for four hundred years. Then came the coup, and the new lords discovered you can conquer a country in a season but its memory takes longer to die, so they helped it along. Every record. Every name. Every street sign, every gravestone, every song that named a king. There are villages in Thornfell whose grandmothers still hum melodies no one remembers the words to. That is Velthmar, singing in the dark and not knowing what it's singing."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because of this." Ilse lifted the black-leather book at last, and opened it, and Wren saw that the pages were not paper but something older—thin sheets of pale hide, covered in a cramped, slanted hand, brown ink faded to the color of dried blood. "This came to me eight years ago, wrapped in oilcloth, sold to me for the price of a chicken by a dying smuggler who claimed it came out of the deep mountain. He didn't know what it was. He thought it was a saint's life. It is not a saint's life." She turned pages with the tenderness of a midwife. "It is a prophecy. Or a prediction. Or—I've argued with myself for years—a record written backward, by someone who knew how such things are done. It concerns Velthmar's fall and, more strangely, its return. And there is a page—" her fingers paused on a leaf darker than the rest, "—that I have read perhaps five hundred times, wondering, and today, seeing you gray around the mouth and alive anyway, I find myself unable to wonder any longer."

She turned the page around and laid it in Wren's lap.

At first Wren saw only the drawing: a door. Tall, narrow-arched, set into a wall of rendered stone, with a lintel carved—or so the fine pen-strokes suggested—with words too worn to read. Beneath the door, in that cramped slanted hand, ran several lines of text, and beneath those, a date, and beside the date—

Wren stopped breathing.

Beside the date, in the same century-old ink, were three words. A name.

*Wren Vane.*

The room did not tilt, exactly. It was worse than tilting. The room became suddenly, horribly detailed—the grain of the desk wood, the dust motes turning in the window light, the precise crack in the leather binding—as if Wren's mind, denied the option of fainting, had decided to memorize everything instead. Their name. Written a hundred years ago. In ink that had dried before their grandmother's grandmother drew breath.

"That's not possible," Wren heard themselves say. Their voice sounded far away, borrowed. "My name—it isn't—it's a smuggler's name. Vane. Half the warrens use it when they don't want their real names written anywhere. It means *nothing*. That's why I chose it."

"Chose it?" Ilse said softly. "Or were given it? By whom?"

And there was the vertigo, arriving late and all at once, like the delayed collapse of a tunnel: because Wren had not chosen it. They had woken in the foundling house at Thornfell with a tag pinned to their blanket—*Vane*, in a matron's hand—and the matron had said the name had come with them, that whoever left the baby had left the word, and Wren had carried it into the kitchens and the guild and the taster's oath like a coat that fit too well to question. *Wren* because they'd been small and quick and ordinary as a bird. *Vane* because it had already been theirs.

"Read the rest," Ilse said. "Read the date."

Wren read it. The script was old-fashioned, letters formed differently, but numbers keep their shapes across centuries the way stones keep theirs across rivers. The day. The month. Tonight.

"The rest of the line," Ilse prompted, and her voice had gone strange, reverent and frightened at once, the voice of a scholar watching her life's skepticism die on a table in front of her. "Translate it for me. Your schooling included old Thalassic, yes? The taster's guild likes its apprentices to read labels."

Wren's lips moved. The words came slowly, each one landing like a footstep on ice:

"*The forgotten child of the erased kingdom shall stand at the sealed door, having supped on death and lived. The door knows its own. Let no faction open it. Let no crown break it. It opens only to the one who was left behind on purpose.*"

Silence. A cart rattled past in the yard below. Somewhere in the walls, pipes knocked, the palace's old blood moving through old veins.

"No," Wren said. It came out flat and certain and completely useless. "No. I'm a food taster. I taste soup. My greatest ambition last winter was a better pillow. I am not—I was not left behind *on purpose*, I was left behind because leaving babies in foundling houses is what people do when they can't feed them, it happens every day, it's *nothing*, that's the entire point of my life, that it's nothing—"

"Wren." Ilse's hand covered theirs, dry and warm and surprisingly strong. "Look at the drawing again. Look at what's carved above the door."

Wren looked. The lintel, they saw now, was not merely decorated. The worn lettering had been rendered with loving precision, stroke by stroke, and Ilse traced one character with a fingernail without quite touching the page. "This is old Velthmari script. I've spent years on it. It reads *we remember what we were made to forget*. The same formula appears on three surviving fragments of Velthmar stonework. This door is real, child. It exists somewhere in Thornfell, or beneath it. And according to a dead man's book, you—" she tapped the name, gently, as though the ink might bruise, "—are going to stand in front of it. Tonight."

"Tonight is the feast of the Regent's Accession. The whole palace will be at table until midnight."

"Yes," Ilse said. "Everyone will be looking at the Regent. And someone in this palace tried to kill you this morning, which suggests that at least one person believes this page as much as I now do—and does not want you reaching any doors." She closed the book with a soft, terrible finality. "Think, Wren. Think like the survivor you have made yourself. If your life has been scripted by someone a hundred years dead—if your name, your trade, your *immunity* were arranged toward this night—then the person who poisoned your conserve is not the only one who knows the script. There may be others reading from older copies. Others who want the ending changed."

The vertigo came again, and this time Wren let it ride, let it show them the shape of their own life from above: the orphanage that had inexplicably fed its foundlings well; the guild examiner who had tested a nine-year-old's tongue with escalating doses and pronounced it a miracle rather than asking *why*; the palace appointment that had come easily, almost eagerly, to a warren rat with no patron. Doors opening, all their life, quietly, in the dark. All leading here.

"I'm nobody," Wren whispered. It was not a protest anymore. It was a eulogy—for a life that had apparently never been real, for the comfortable fiction of a self-built self. "I spent twenty years learning to be nobody."

"And tonight," Ilse said, "you discover that *nobody* is the most dangerous thing a person can be, because somebody wrote it." She was already moving, snuffing the lamp though daylight still filled the window, pulling a loose stone from beside her desk and drawing out a flat packet wrapped in oilcloth—the twin of the wrapping the smuggler had used, Wren thought distantly. "I have transcribed that page eleven times in eleven years, hoping I was mad. Take one copy. Burn it if you must, but do not lose it. And take the back stairs, down past the old chapel, out through the coal gate. The evening shift change leaves it watched by one man who owes me money."

"Why are you helping me?" Wren asked. "What do you get?"

Ilse paused, half-turned in the dimness, and for a moment the famous unpleasantness fell away entirely and left only an old woman who had spent her life cataloguing ash.

"A hundred years ago," she said, "someone hid this book so that the truth would survive the burning. I am doing the same thing. We are colleagues, separated by a century." She pressed the packet into Wren's hands and closed their fingers over it. "Go. Quickly. And Wren—if anyone asks, you were never in this room. Be nobody one more night. After tonight, I suspect nobody will ever manage it again."

Wren was three steps down the back stairs when the bells began—not the hour bell, but the alarm peal, harsh and fast, ringing from the gatehouse tower. Voices rose in the yard below. Boots. The word that carries furthest and fastest in any palace, cutting through every other sound:

*"Inquisitors."*

Black cloaks were coming through the front gate—six of them, ten, a whole company, moving with the unhurried certainty of people who already know which door they want. And at their head walked a tall man with a gray streak in his beard whom Wren had seen at a distance a dozen times and hoped never to see closer: Captain Dain Rook, the Regent's own hound, whose reputation was built on one simple fact—he had never once searched a place where he did not find exactly what he was looking for.

His head turned. His gaze swept the courtyard, the windows, the stairwell shadows where Wren stood pressed against cold stone with a dead man's book against their chest.

It passed over Wren like weather.

Then it stopped. Came back.

And Dain Rook began, unhurried, to walk toward the scholars' annex.
