## 3. What the Regent Burned

Ilse Marrow had spent forty years learning to hear the palace before it arrived.

First came the bells—never the great bells of the citadel, which rang for executions and coronations and were therefore honest, but the small bell of the gatehouse, rung twice with a pause between, the signal that meant *inspection* rather than *delivery*. Then the dogs. The archive kennels were supposed to be for tracking escaped debtors through the lower city, but Ilse had noticed years ago that the hounds grew restless a quarter hour before any inquisitorial visit, as if they could smell the leather and ash-and-iron scent of inquisitor's coats before the men wearing them had finished saddling their horses.

She heard none of it now. She was too busy watching the girl.

The girl sat at the long reading table with her hands flat on the wood, palms down, the way people sit when they are trying very hard not to bolt. Her name was Wren Vane; she had said so in a voice barely above breath, as if even here, in a room full of dead paper, the name might be overheard by someone who would make her regret it. She was thin in the particular way of people who ration themselves—not from poverty exactly, but from habit, from a lifetime of taking only what would not be missed. She had kitchen-calluses and the faint sweet smell of almonds that clung to everyone who worked the Regent's pantry. And she had come up out of the storm an hour past midnight asking, in the tone of someone bargaining for their life, whether Ilse Marrow still kept books that ought to have been burned.

Ilse had lit every lamp she owned.

Now the prophecy page lay open on the table between them, and Ilse watched the girl read it the way one watches a person read their own death warrant: first confusion, then the slow, terrible arrival of comprehension. The girl's finger hovered over the ink without touching it, the way one hovers a hand over a flame to test its heat.

"That's my name," Wren said. It wasn't a question, but it wasn't anything else either. It was the sound of a floor giving way.

"It is."

"And the date." Her finger moved to the marginal notation, the cramped old numerals. "That's tonight. That's *today*. That's—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Someone wrote this a hundred years ago."

"A hundred and six," Ilse said. "Give or take. Dating is imprecise in the early Velthmari script; they counted seasons, not years."

"Velthmari." The girl tested the word like an unfamiliar dish—a small taste, held on the tongue. "I've never heard that word."

"No," Ilse said gently. "You haven't. No one has. That is rather the entire point of everything I am about to tell you, and the reason we may both hang for it." She drew the lamp closer, and its light pooled gold across the page, across the drawing beneath the written lines: a door, tall and narrow, set into living stone, its lintel carved with a row of small figures whose faces time had rubbed to shadows. "So sit, child. Drink your tea. And let me tell you about the country you were born inside of and have never once seen."

Wren did not drink the tea. But she sat.

* * *

"Before Thornfell there was Velthmar," Ilse began. "Not a kingdom in the mountains—the mountains *were* the kingdom. Every vale, every pass, every river that runs down to the lowlands you know. The capital stood in a valley so deep and green that travelers wrote songs about the light there, how it came down filtered through the peaks like honey through cloth. They were weavers and winemakers and keepers of extraordinary archives—better than mine, though I flatter myself mine are better hidden. They kept records the way other nations kept jewels. Memory was their wealth."

"And then?"

"And then there was a coup." Ilse said it plainly, because she had learned long ago that plainness was the only mercy history allowed. "A century ago. The noble houses of the lowlands—lands Velthmar had absorbed or annexed or simply overshadowed—rose against the crown in a single season. There was a battle at the gates of the capital that lasted three days, and afterward there was something worse than a battle, which was silence. The victors did not merely conquer Velthmar. They *erased* it. Maps were redrawn. Records were burned in pyres that burned for a month—my predecessors among the archivists documented them before they fed the flames themselves, some of them, choosing to die beside their collections rather than watch them go. The language was suppressed. The songs were rewritten. Children born after the purge were taught that the valleys above the lowland kingdoms had always been empty, wild places, home to smugglers and nothing else."

"The Thornfell warrens," Wren said slowly. "Where I grew up. Everyone says no one remembers who settled them."

"No one remembers," Ilse agreed, "because remembering became a crime the year your grandmother's grandmother was born. The new regime called it the Great Correction, in those early decades. Now they don't call it anything at all, which is what success looks like, from their side of it." She turned the page toward herself, though she knew every line by heart; she liked to touch it while she spoke, the way the grieving touch a ring. "My family were archivists under the old crown. Minor ones—keepers of a provincial collection, not the great vaults of the capital. When the purge came, my great-great-grandmother did the unforgivable thing. She hid books. Carried them out in flour sacks, in coffin linings, sewn into the skirts of refugees, and buried them in cellars and caves across three generations of quiet, stubborn disobedience. The Marrows were ruined twice for it—stripped of position, stripped of property, and the second time my grandfather was taken by the inquisitors and did not come back. My mother raised me on two lessons. The first was that these books were worth more than our name. The second was that if anyone ever asked, we had never heard of Velthmar either."

Wren was silent a moment. In the lamplight her face had gone very still, the way faces go still around wounds. "And this page. The prophecy. Where did it come from?"

"From the capital itself—from before the end. It's a fragment of a longer work, a divination cycle commissioned by the last royal taster's guild, if you can believe such a thing existed. Tasters had standing, then. Guildhalls. Oaths." Ilse smiled without much humor. "The fragment survived the burnings because it was misfiled—bound inside an account ledger of grain shipments, of all things, where no censor thought to look. It passed through four hands I know of and eleven I don't before it reached my family's cellar. We call it the Vane Fragment, incidentally. That name comes from the first line, not from you—or rather, it comes from whoever *Vane* was meant to be, a hundred years before you were born to carry it."

"'The forgotten child,'" Wren read aloud, haltingly, working through the old orthography. "'Born of the taster's line, nameless-made-named, who shall stand at the door that remembers on the eve of the ash-fall, having eaten of seven bitter dishes—'" She stopped. Looked up. "I've eaten poison four times that nearly killed me. Small doses, training doses, and then—" Her voice thinned. "Yesterday. Something yesterday. Someone put something in the Regent's soup that should not have been survivable, and I drank it anyway, and I didn't die, and I have been trying all day not to think about why."

Ilse felt the cold move up her own spine, the old archival instinct that recognized pattern the way a hound recognizes blood. Seven bitter dishes. Four survived, one yesterday. Two remained. "Child," she said carefully, "when you say you didn't die—"

"I mean my heart stopped for the length of a prayer and started again. I mean I vomited black for an hour and walked to work the next morning. I mean whatever was in that soup has killed three foreign envoys in five years, I checked the records, and it took me like a bad winter." Wren's hands had closed into fists on the table. "I trained for immunity my whole life. Bitter herbs since I could walk. Microdoses of everything. My instructors said I had a gift. Nobody said *where* the gift came from."

"No," Ilse said softly. "They wouldn't have. The guild's methods survived the purge too, in fragments—in midwife's lore, in smuggler's medicine, in the kind of knowledge that passes mother to child without ever being written down, because writing it down is how things get burned. If your family carried the taster's line out of Velthmar, then your immunity isn't a skill you learned, Wren Vane. It's an inheritance. You were bred for this page. Perhaps a hundred years of careful marriages were made for it, quietly, without anyone left alive knowing why."

The girl stared at her. Outside, the storm worried at the archive's shutters, and somewhere below in the stacks the building ticked and settled like an old man in his sleep. When Wren finally spoke, her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of someone holding themselves together with both hands.

"So someone scripted me. A hundred years ago. Someone wrote down my name, and my date, and my—my dinner—and sealed it away, and everyone who kept it alive was killed for keeping it, and meanwhile I was raised to be nobody. To be invisible. To be *unremarkable*, that was the word, they said it like a compliment—" She laughed, one short broken note with no humor in it at all. "I built my whole life on being no one. And it turns out no one was a role. I was *cast*."

"Yes," Ilse said, because lying to her seemed suddenly obscene. "I imagine it feels like vertigo. Like looking down from a height and realizing the ground was never where you thought. I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. But understand this too: the people who wrote that page are a hundred years dead, and the regime that erased them is dying of its own rot, and neither of them gets to decide what you do next. Prophecy is a door, child, not a cage. Doors open from both sides."

She believed it when she said it. She had spent her whole life believing it—that memory was not a chain but a lantern. It was only later, in the smoke, that she would wonder whether she had been comforting the girl or herself.

* * *

The dogs began to scream at half past two.

There was no other word for it. The archive hounds, which barked at cats and carters with bureaucratic regularity, went off all at once in a ragged chorus of pure animal terror, and beneath it Ilse heard the thing she had been listening for all her life without wanting to hear it: hooves on the causeway stones. Many hooves. Unhurried. Men who did not need to rush had the loudest confidence in the world.

Wren was on her feet instantly, and Ilse saw with a strange, detached pride that the girl moved like a smuggler—low, silent, already cataloguing exits. "Back door?" Wren breathed.

"There is no back door. There hasn't been since the last inspection found the tunnel behind the map cases." Ilse was already moving, but not toward escape; her feet carried her, as they always had, toward the shelves. "Listen to me. Rook does not raid without warrant unless he's certain, and he is never certain unless he's been told, which means someone talked, which means—"

"Ilse." Wren caught her arm. Through the shutters, orange light was beginning to bloom in the courtyard below—lanterns, a dozen at least, and the wet gleam of armor. "Whoever told him knows I'm here. If I run, they follow me, and you're implicated either way. Give me to him and tell him I broke in. Tell him I held a knife on you. He'll believe it—I look like exactly the sort of person who'd hold a knife on a scholar."

Ilse looked at the girl—at the orphan of the warrens who had come up through a storm to ask a ruined woman for the truth, and who was now, at seventeen perhaps, offering to be arrested so that an old archivist's books might survive her. Something in Ilse's chest cracked along a fault line that had been forming for forty years.

"No," she said.

"Ilse—"

"*No.*" She pulled free and crossed to the reading table, and her hands were steady, gods help her, steadier than they had been in decades. She gathered the Vane Fragment into her arms—the prophecy page, the commentary leaves, the grain-ledger binding that had saved it once already—and looked around the room she had lived in longer than anywhere else on earth, at the ten thousand books her family had died twice to keep. "Rook will search. Rook searches well—it's why he frightens me more than the ones who enjoy it. He will find this room's secrets eventually no matter what I do. So the question is only what he finds *first*, and how long the finding takes, and whether it takes long enough." She thrust the fragment into Wren's arms. "Take it. Take the whole bundle. There's a coal chute behind the third case in the north wall—drop bar, slide, it opens onto the drainage culvert that runs down to the tannery district. Smuggler territory. You know those roads better than he ever will."

"I'm not leaving you to burn for me."

"You're leaving me to burn for *this*." Ilse touched the fragment, once, lightly, farewell. "Which I chose fifty years before I met you. There is a difference, child, and it matters, and you will remember it later when it matters to you." She was already dragging the lamp to the hearth, already pulling the drawer where she kept the accelerant she had sworn she'd never use—the resin-oil, the thick yellow stuff that caught hot and ate paper whole. Her mother had taught her the recipe alongside her letters. *A keeper's last duty,* her mother used to say, *is to choose what survives.*

Boots in the courtyard now. The main doors boomed—once, twice, the polite knock of men who intended to come in regardless.

"What do I do with it?" Wren's voice cracked high and young. "The page. If it's real—if tonight means something—what am I supposed to *do*?"

Ilse hesitated. All her life she had dealt in certainty, in citation, in the clean architecture of proven fact, and here at the end she had nothing but a scholar's honest ignorance. "Find the door," she said. "It's drawn true—the artist saw it. Somewhere in Thornfell there's a valley the maps forgot, and a door the stone remembers, and a hundred years of stolen sleep waiting behind it. Open it or don't. But decide for yourself, Wren Vane. That's the one thing no one—not the regicide's grandchildren, not the prophets, not the inquisitors—can take from you, if you refuse to give it."

The doors gave way below with a sound like a felled tree. Voices, ordered and calm. The jingle of a man unbuckling his sword to carry it indoors, which was somehow worse than a drawn blade.

Ilse poured the resin-oil over the reading table, over the false ledgers, over forty years of careful forgeries designed to bore any searcher into leaving. Then she struck the match, and the fire went up with a soft rushing sigh, almost gentle, and the light of it made the whole archive glow like the inside of a lamp.

"North wall," she said. "Third case. Go *now*, and don't stop at the tanneries, don't stop until you're under the tree-line. Find Bram Odell's crew if you can find anyone—he owes a Vane, though he'll curse me for telling you." She pressed one oil-stained hand briefly to the girl's cheek, the first time she had touched her. "You were never invisible, child. You were *hidden*. There's a difference, and the difference is that hidden things are meant to be found."

Down in the entrance hall, boots were climbing the stairs, unhurried, certain.

Wren ran. Ilse heard the drop-bar clack, the chute sigh open, the brief scrape of a body in the dark—and then nothing, which meant the girl had done it right, and Ilse turned back to the fire with tears she refused to spill and began, methodically, to feed it books.

* * *

Captain Dain Rook entered the burning room the way he entered everything: precisely, quietly, and too late by design.

He stood in the doorway with his coat steaming from the rain and watched the flames take the shelves Ilse had spent her life filling, and his grey eyes moved over the fire with the expression of a man auditing a ledger he suspected had been cooked. Behind him his inquisitors waited, torches guttering, saying nothing. Rook never permitted shouting in a search. Shouting, he liked to say, was what amateurs used instead of evidence.

"Scholar Marrow," he said. His voice was mild as milk. "Your archive is on fire."

"How observant, Captain. Do come in and inspect it."

He stepped over the threshold, and his gaze went where she knew it would go: to the reading table, to the empty space where the Vane Fragment had lain, to the single wooden cup still sitting there with tea going cold in it. One cup. He looked at it for a long moment. Then he looked at her, and Ilse saw the terrible thing she had always heard rumored and never witnessed—that Dain Rook was not cruel, and was not stupid, and had begun, somewhere in the deep rooms of himself, to doubt. Doubt was the most dangerous thing an inquisitor could carry. It made them unpredictable. It made them *thorough*.

"One cup," Rook said.

"Scholars drink alone. It's why we're scholars."

"Of course." He turned to his men. "Sweep the north wall. Check the coal chute. If it's been opened tonight, I want the culvert tracked to the tanneries and hounds on the trail within the hour." His voice never rose; orders fell out of him like water finding level. Then, to Ilse alone, quietly, almost kindly: "You'll come with us, Scholar. There will be questions."

"There usually are."

As they bound her hands—gently, which frightened her more than roughness would have—Ilse looked back once at the heart of the fire, where the last of her mother's forgeries curled and blackened, buying their final few minutes of confusion. Somewhere beyond the walls, beyond the rain, beyond the reach of dogs and doubt, a kitchen-girl was running into the mountains carrying a hundred years of stolen truth against her chest.

Burn it all, Ilse thought, watching her life's work become light. Burn every page. The words are already out of the cellar. The forgotten child is awake, and walking, and the ash is falling right on schedule.

Let the Regent learn what the Marrows always knew:

History is not what you write. History is what refuses, in the end, to stay buried.
