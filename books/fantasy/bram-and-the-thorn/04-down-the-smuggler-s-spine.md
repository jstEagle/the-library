## 4. Down the Smuggler's Spine

The hounds found Wren's scent at the tannery drain, which was annoying, because Wren had counted on the tannery's stink to smother it. They'd forgotten that Rook's hounds weren't ordinary hounds. They were pale, long-jawed things bred in the inquisitors' kennels below the palace, fed on nothing but ash and old cloth, and they didn't track smell so much as they tracked *absence*—the ghost of a scent, the hole a person left in the world. Which meant, Wren thought grimly, ducking beneath a sagging clothesline in the Warrens' lower lanes, that for once in their life, being nobody was working against them.

The hole Wren left was very deep indeed.

Behind them, three streets down and closing, came the belling of the pack—high, thin voices like cracked glass, harmonizing on one note that made the back teeth ache. Ahead, the lane ended at the tanner's wall, moss-slick, twelve feet of stone that every child in the Thornfell warrens learned to climb before they learned their letters.

Wren climbed it.

Their hands knew the holds without consulting them—the chipped ledge at shoulder height, the iron staple some smuggler had driven into the mortar forty years ago and no mason had ever thought to remove. At the top Wren paused, chest heaving, and looked back the way they'd come: the lantern-lit lanes of the capital's lowest quarter, smoke rising from ten thousand chimneys, the palace above it all with its windows burning gold against the dark like eyes that never blinked.

Somewhere up there, Ilse Marrow was feeding a prophecy to the flames. Somewhere up there, Captain Dain Rook was reading the ash.

*Run*, she'd said. So Wren ran.

* * *

There were four ways out of the city, if you asked the city. The south road through the customs gate, the river barge, the postern by the garrison, and the great north highway over the shoulder of the mountains. The city believed these were all the ways there were.

The city was wrong.

There was a fifth way. It had no name on any map, because maps got people hanged, but the smugglers called it the Spine—a seam of old mine-workings and goat tracks and collapsed chapel cellars that ran up through the Thornfell mountains like marrow through bone. Wren had learned it in pieces, over years, the way you learn anything worth keeping: from a deaf grandmother who sold eels, from a boy who died young, from a night spent hiding in a wine cellar while soldiers searched the floor above. No single person knew the whole of it. But a taster, Wren had discovered, was uniquely placed to collect fragments. Nobody watched a servant carrying a covered dish. Nobody remembered them. And so Wren had walked half the Spine already, in the plain gray livery of the Regent's kitchens, carrying consignments that were never inspected because the person carrying them was never seen.

Now those same roads would carry them out—or kill them. Either way, better than the kennels below the palace.

Wren went to ground first, in the cellar of the ruined Chapel of Saint Verity-of-the-Small-Print, where the smugglers kept their stores behind a false wall of stacked hymnals. They waited out two hours of darkness, listening to the hounds quarter the streets above, their voices rising and falling, frustrated, circling. Rook wouldn't give up. That was the thing about Rook that everyone in the palace whispered and no one said aloud: other inquisitors worked cases; Rook absorbed them. He would be following Wren still when the moon set and the frost came and his men begged to turn back. Wren had seen him do it with a tax-fraud case that took eleven months. Eleven months, over ledgers.

Over a prophecy naming Wren Vane by name, date, and dish, Rook would follow until the mountains themselves gave out.

So: no sleep. Wren ate a strip of dried pear from the chapel store—smugglers' toll, noted honestly in the little mental ledger Wren kept for exactly this purpose—and moved on, up.

* * *

The Spine began, properly, at the mouth of the old Delve, a mine-shaft the crown had officially sealed sixty years ago and unofficially left open because sealing things cost money. Wren dropped into it by rope, down past the rotted timbers, into air that tasted of cold stone and older dark. They lit no lamp. This was the part only a taster could do: navigate blind, by touch and memory and the taste of the air itself. Every passage in the mountain had its own flavor. Sulphur meant the fire-veins, avoid. Iron-and-wet meant water nearby, drinkable. A faint sweetness like bruised apples meant bad air, the kind that killed a man smiling, and Wren had learned its taste from watching another taster die of it years ago—a lesson delivered once, permanently.

They climbed for what felt like hours, hands finding rungs hammered in by miners three generations dead, and came up at last through a crack in the hillside onto a shelf of rock under a sky salted with stars. Below, the capital lay in its bowl of light. Above, the Thornfells went up forever, black against blacker.

Wren allowed themselves one breath of something almost like hope.

Then the hounds bayed, and the sound came from *below*—from the Delve they'd just climbed out of—and Wren understood with a cold drop of the stomach that Rook hadn't followed their trail at all. He'd simply known the Spine existed. Of course he had. Inquisitors read everything, even the things that were never written down. He wasn't tracking Wren through the mountain.

He was waiting ahead of them in it.

* * *

Dawn caught Wren in a place called the Kettle, a bowl of scree between two shoulders of the range where the smuggler path narrowed to a ledge barely wide enough for a loaded mule. Wren had been moving all night, eating as they walked—dried fish, a heel of bread, a pinch of cress-bitter leaf to keep the mind sharp—and running now on craft rather than strength, which was how tasters did everything. Strength failed. Craft didn't, not until you dropped.

It was craft that told Wren, halfway across the Kettle, that someone else was on the path.

Not the hounds—no belling, and the wind carried no ash-smell. Someone else. Wren froze against the rock face and let the ledge do what it always did: make the walker invisible to anyone not standing precisely on it. Below and around the curve of the mountain, voices drifted up. Several. Unhurried. The particular unhurriedness of people who owned the ground they stood on.

"—saying the pass is closed, I'm *telling* you it's closed, Pim drew the map, and Pim's maps are scripture—"

"Bram, your scripture doesn't move a landslide."

"It doesn't have to *move* it, Yara, it has to *route around* it, that's literally the entire profession—"

Wren closed their eyes briefly. Smugglers. A whole crew of them, coming around the bend, loud enough to wake the dead, in the one hour when quiet was survival. Wren pressed deeper into the shadow of an overhang and prepared to do the thing they did best: become scenery.

Then a dog—no, not a dog, something leaner, a ferret-creature riding a man's shoulder—went rigid, turned its small fierce head, and looked directly at the overhang where Wren stood.

"There," it said.

It *said*. Wren would think about that later, in the way you postpone thinking about things that break your understanding of the world. Right then there was no time, because the crew came around the bend—a big man in a patched coat leading a mule train, a broad woman with a soldier's stride, a slight figure buried in maps—and the big man stopped, and looked up, and grinned like sunrise breaking over a crime scene.

"Well," he said. "Hello, scenery."

* * *

His name, it emerged over the next terrible quarter-hour, was Bram Odell, and he talked the way rivers flood—constantly, cheerfully, and over everything in his path.

"Because here's my question," he said, pacing the little hollow where the crew had made camp under an outcrop, while the soldier woman—Yara—checked the mules' harness with the grim efficiency of someone who expected the world to end on schedule, and the map-buried figure—Pim, presumably—stared at Wren with unsettling, unblinking attention. "Here's my actual question. Palace grays don't climb the Spine. Palace grays take the south road and get robbed, or the river barge and get seasick. You're wearing kitchen gray, but you came up through the Delve, which means somebody taught you the mountain, which means—" he spread his hands, delighted, "—you're either a smuggler gone respectable, which is tragic, or a taster gone fugitive, which is *interesting*."

"I'm neither," Wren said.

"You're both," the ferret-thing said smugly, from Bram's shoulder.

"And who," Wren asked, "are *you*?"

"Sefa's familiar. Don't matter." It groomed one paw. "You taste of ink and burnt paper and fear. Interesting combination."

"The hedge-witch's familiar reads people," Yara said flatly, not looking up from the harness. "Ignore it. Bram. We're burning daylight."

"We're burning *nothing*," Bram said happily, "because the pass is shut, thanks to the landslide Pim predicted, which means we have time, which means I get answers. Names first. Yours."

Wren weighed it. The old calculus: give nothing, be nothing, survive. But the hounds were behind them in the mountain, and Rook was somewhere ahead of them in it, and this crew was the only moving thing between Wren and either fate. Sometimes invisibility wasn't the safest play. Sometimes the safest play was to be useful enough that people wanted you alive.

"Wren," they said. "I'm the Regent's food-taster. Or I was, until last night. There's a book, and a page in the book, and inquisitors burned down an archive over it, and now there are hounds in the Spine looking for me."

Silence, of a kind. Even Bram stopped talking, which Wren suspected was a historic event.

"A book," Yara said.

"A prophecy. It names me. By name, and by date, and—" Wren hesitated, hearing again the scholar's voice, dry and shaking: *and by dish, child. It names the dish.* "By what I ate for supper last night. Written down a hundred years ago."

The map-figure spoke for the first time—not aloud, but in quick gestures, hands cutting shapes in the cold air. Bram translated without appearing to look, the way you translate a language you've spoken since childhood.

"Pim says that's either the biggest lie ever told on this mountain or the biggest truth, and either way it's worth more than our last three cargoes combined." He crouched in front of Wren, and the grin faded into something sharper, more careful. "Here's my offer. We're going through the high passes, slow, because the low ones are watched. You know routes through this rock that I've been trying to buy off dying old men for ten years—I can see it in how you stand, taster, you *know* this mountain. You guide, we protect. Simple trade."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you go on alone," Bram said, spreading his hands, "with inquisitor hounds behind you and whatever's chasing prophecy ahead of you, and I'll mourn you briefly and beautifully at the top of the pass."

The ferret snorted. Even Yara's mouth twitched, one millimeter.

Wren looked at them: the flood-talking smuggler with sharp eyes underneath, the soldier counting her mules like rosary beads, the mute cartographer who had predicted a landslide and routed around it, the witch's familiar that tasted fear in the air. Strangers. Liars, probably. Criminals, certainly.

And the only family-shaped thing the horizon currently offered.

"The hounds track absence," Wren said. "Not scent. They'll find us faster if we're together—more holes in the world. If I'm guiding, I can split our trail across six false paths and buy us days. But you'll have to trust me about which paths are real."

Bram's grin came back, slow as dawn.

"Oh, taster," he said, sweeping an extravagant bow, "you misunderstand the entire arrangement. *I* don't need to trust you. The mountain does. And the mountain—" he patted the scree beneath his boots affectionately, like a horse's neck, "—answers to me."

Above them, with impeccable comic timing, the far side of the Kellet began to ring: high, thin, glass-cracked voices, rising up the switchbacks from the direction of the Delve.

The hounds. Hours ahead of any reasonable schedule. As if they'd smelled not Wren, but the decision.

Yara was already saddling. Pim was already unrolling maps. The ferret had already vanished into Sefa's hood, wherever the hedge-witch had been standing all along, silent as furniture.

"Right," Bram said, all business now, hauling the lead mule's rope. "High pass, false trails, days bought. Try to keep up, scenery."

Wren ran.

And behind them, faint but gaining, the hounds sang—and somewhere ahead, deeper in the white silence of the Thornfells, something else answered.
