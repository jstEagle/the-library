## 5. The Crew of Small Crimes

Bram Odell had a theory about strangers: they came in two kinds, the kind who slowed your crew down and the kind who got your crew killed. The ones who did neither were rare as honest tax collectors, and he'd learned not to trust rarity.

So when he led Wren Vane up the last switchback to the hollow where his people made camp, he did it at half a step behind, which was as close to watching someone's back as Bram ever admitted to. The stranger climbed like a person used to being quiet for their supper—no wasted motion, no noise, hands tucked into sleeves against the cold. Small. Sharp-faced in an unremarkable way, the sort of face you'd forget before it finished looking at you, and Bram had lived long enough to know that was never an accident. People built faces like that the way masons built walls. On purpose, with mortar.

*Vane*, the mountain roads had said. Or rather, hadn't said—the paths had simply decided, all at once and without consulting him, that this stranger went where they pleased. Forty years the Odells had worked Thornfell's spine, three generations of bribes and bartered whiskey and careful cartography, and Bram had earned exactly one miracle out of the whole enterprise: the day the old toll-road under Raven Split had opened for him when it should have swallowed him whole. He'd been nineteen, fleeing a Kellish debt collector with more patience than humor, and the crack in the rock had widened just enough, just long enough. His grandmother had spat and told him the mountain liked him. Bram had always suspected it wasn't him it liked.

Now here was this person, this taster from the Regent's own kitchens, walking routes that answered to no smuggler alive, with hounds baying somewhere below and a look in their eye like someone reading a letter they weren't sure they wanted delivered.

"You're staring," Wren said, without turning around.

"I'm assessing."

"Assess what you like. I don't know why the roads move for me either."

That was the other thing about this stranger. They answered questions Bram hadn't asked yet. It was disconcerting, like playing cards with someone who could see the backs.

The camp sat in a bowl of stone below a shelf of overhang, sheltered from the wind that came shrieking down the col every evening like a bill collector. A fire burned low and banked, giving off more heat than light—Sefa's work; she never let a flame show above the ridgeline if she could help it, and she'd woven something into the smoke that made it fall instead of rise, a grey curtain hugging the ground. Three bedrolls, a mule named Duchess who regarded Bram with her usual expression of judicial disappointment, and crates. Always crates. Bram measured his life in crates these days.

"Company," Bram announced, because his crew deserved warning. "Don't shoot."

Yara shot anyway.

It was a formality, more or less—a crossbow bolt that buried itself in the dirt a polite six inches from Wren's boot, thunk, conversational, the way Yara said good evening. She rose from behind the fire with the bow already reloaded, a big woman gone soft at the edges but nowhere it mattered, her soldier's close-cropped hair going iron-grey at the temples. Her eyes did the thing they always did: took in height, weight, weapon, exits, threat. Then stopped on Wren and stayed there, and her brow creased like she was trying to read fine print by moonlight.

"They're with me," Bram said. "Sort of."

"Sort of gets people buried, Odell."

"Sort of is how we all joined, if memory serves."

Wren, to their credit, hadn't flinched at the bolt. They looked down at it, then at Yara, with something almost like professional courtesy. "Good grouping. Most people aim center and hope."

"I don't hope," Yara said, and lowered the bow. "I aim." But she said it quieter than she needed to, and Bram saw her still studying the stranger, hunting whatever it was that had made the hair on her neck stand up. Twenty years in the Regent's border regiments before the incident Bram never asked about, and Yara could smell palace on a person through three layers of road grime.

"They're a food-taster," Bram said. "Palace-trained. Escaped. Inquisitors after them with dogs."

"A *taster*." Yara's mouth twisted. "Half my old company died of the kitchens. Poison in the stew at Marrow Ford, and the quartermaster swore on his mother the provisions were clean."

"The provisions were clean," Wren said. "The cook wasn't. Bay leaf paste in the rendering fat—it doesn't survive boiling, so it went in after, at the garnish. Whoever did it knew your company ate stew on rest days."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere above, a rock cracked with cold.

"That was eleven years ago," Yara said slowly.

"Yes."

"You weren't even—how old are you?"

"Old enough to have read the report," Wren said, "because I trained under the man who wrote it, and he kept copies of everything he was too much of a coward to fix." They crouched slowly, deliberately, and pulled the bolt from the dirt, offering it back to Yara handle-first. "I'm sorry. For what it's worth, the cook was tried and hanged. Quietly. The families weren't told it was poison because the army couldn't admit its kitchens were porous. Your dead died under a lie of logistics."

Bram watched Yara take the bolt. Watched something complicated move across her face—not forgiveness, nothing so fast as that, but recognition. Soldiers and tasters, he supposed, were both in the business of swallowing things so other people didn't have to.

"Well," Bram said into the quiet, clapping his hands. "This has been a lovely wake. Sefa! Pim! We have a guest and possibly a story worth money."

* * *

Sefa emerged from her tent the way she emerged from everywhere—already mid-thought, a small brown woman with ink stains to her elbows and charms braided into her hair that clicked softly when she moved, each one a different bargain with a different small god. She took one look at Wren and her whole face changed.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, you're *tangled*."

"Pleased to meet you too," Wren said.

"No, no—" Sefa crossed the camp in quick little steps, circling them like a hawk deciding, sniffing the air near their shoulder, near their hair. "You've got threads on you. Old ones. Someone tied knots in your life before you were born, and now everything you touch pulls. The roads, yes, I felt the roads shift this morning, the whole spine of the mountain *hummed*—that was you, wasn't it? That was you passing through."

"Sefa," Bram warned.

"Hush, Bram, I'm working." She caught Wren's hand and turned it palm-up, frowning at it, and whatever she saw there made her go very still. She looked up. Looked at the hand again. Looked at Bram.

"What?" Bram said.

"Later," Sefa said, and folded Wren's fingers closed with unexpected gentleness. "You'll want to sit down when I tell you, and there's no time, because Pim's already seen it, and Pim wants to talk to you."

Pim did not talk. That was the first thing anyone learned about Pim, usually the hard way, after they'd asked a question twice. Pim had come to Bram four years ago with a satchel of maps drawn in a hand so fine it looked stitched rather than written, and hadn't spoken a word since. They were young—younger than Wren, maybe twenty, maybe less, it was hard to tell with the shorn hair and the permanent air of someone listening to a frequency nobody else received—and they communicated through sketches, gestures, and a slate they carried slung on their back like a shield. Right now they were standing at the edge of the firelight holding the slate against their chest with both arms, staring at Wren with an intensity that would have been rude in anyone else.

They crossed the camp, wiped the slate with their sleeve, chalked something, and held it up.

**YOU'RE THE ONE THE ROAD WAS TALKING ABOUT.**

Bram's stomach dropped. "The road *what*?"

Pim ignored him entirely, chalk flying again. **THE DEEP ROADS. THE ONES THAT DON'T ANSWER TO ANYONE. I'VE MAPPED THEIR EDGES FOR FOUR YEARS AND THEY NEVER OPEN. THEY OPENED TODAY. FOR YOU.**

"How do you know the deep roads don't answer to anyone?" Bram demanded. "Who have you been *asking*?"

Pim gave him a look of vast, weary pity and turned back to Wren, adding beneath the first line: **I DREAMT THEM LAST NIGHT. DOORS UNDER THE MOUNTAIN. A DOOR WITH YOUR NAME ON IT.**

And there it was, laid out between the crates and the banked fire like a body on a slab. Bram looked around at his crew—at Yara's narrowed eyes, Sefa's knowing ones, Pim's slate hanging in the smoky air—and thought, with the particular despair of a man watching a plan die, *well, there goes any chance of talking them into leaving.*

Because here was the thing, the thing Bram had been chewing on since the ravine, since the moment Wren Vane had given their name and the ground had seemed to lean toward them: he knew that name. He'd known it for fifteen years, carried it like a coin sewn into his coat lining, heavy and private and never spent. And he had a very bad feeling that the ledger was coming due.

* * *

Supper was barley and salt pork and dried plums, which was to say, supper. Bram watched Wren taste it first out of pure reflex—one grain of barley, one sliver of fat, one plum, then wait, counting under their breath—and something in his chest went tight and strange. That wasn't training anymore, he realized. That was just how they lived. Checking every door. Measuring every spoonful for the dose meant for someone else.

"So," Bram said, when the bowls were scraped and the plums were gone. He turned his tin cup in his hands. "You want to know who I am, roughly? I'm a thief with delusions of logistics. I run goods the Regent's customs men can't be bothered to climb for. Tea, medicine, books when the mood takes me, once a season a crate of something that makes inquisitors sneeze. My crew is myself, a witch, a ghost, and a deserter. Any questions so far?"

"One," Wren said. "Why did you help me? You didn't know me. You had hounds on your scent because of me. That's a bad trade by any smuggling arithmetic."

Fair. Annoyingly fair. Bram took a pull of tea and bought himself a moment, and found that the moment didn't help, because the truth had waited fifteen years and apparently intended to come out regardless of his opinion on the matter.

"There was a woman," he said. "Named Vane."

He felt the whole camp go still.

"Fifteen years back. Maybe sixteen. I was young and stupid in ways that are mostly behind me—mostly—and I got a commission I shouldn't have taken. Moving a crate through the lowland passes for a man who paid too well, which should have been my first warning. Second warning was that the crate breathed." He stared into his tea. "It was a person. A woman, drugged, bound. Cargo for the inquisitors' archive—she'd done something, knew something, I never learned what. And I was nineteen and the coin was real and I told myself everyone carries something sometime."

"And?" Yara said quietly. She knew this story, Bram realized. Had known it for years, and had never once pressed him to finish it in front of strangers. There was a reason he loved this impossible woman.

"And halfway up the pass she wakes up," Bram said. "Works the ropes somehow—I still don't know how, the knots were mine and they were good—and she's out of the crate and gone into the trees before I can even shout. And I'm standing there doing arithmetic: report it, chase it, or walk away. And I walked away. Took the empty crate to the drop point, took the coin, told myself a person who could slip my knots didn't need saving."

He set the cup down. His hands wanted to shake and he wouldn't let them.

"Except she came back. Two nights later, found our camp, walked in past Yara's pickets like she owned the ground. And she looked at me—young, stupid, guilty—and she didn't curse me or cut me. She sat down and ate my supper and said, 'You hesitated. That's worth more than most people's courage.' And then she paid me. Paid me to carry messages, sometimes. To move things. Small things, at first—letters, then ledgers, then a sealed box I was never to open, only deliver. She taught me three roads through Thornfell I still use. She taught me how to listen at way-stations without being seen. Two years we worked, off and on. She called herself Vane. Never gave another name."

"What happened to her?" Wren asked. Their voice was very level. Too level. The levelness of someone holding a door shut.

"She got careless. Or cornered. Word came to us that she was taken in the capital—inquisitors. Except—" Bram frowned, chasing the memory. "Except the story didn't add up. The man who told us was a broker we used, and later he couldn't remember telling us at all. Swore blind he'd never heard the name Vane in his life. Like the whole thing had been... smoothed over. Erased." He looked up, and met Wren's eyes across the fire. "Like a lot of things in this country get erased."

The fire cracked. Duchess shifted and stamped.

"You said she paid you," Wren said finally. "For jobs."

"For years. And the last payment—" Bram reached into his collar and drew out a cord, and on the cord a small brass token, worn smooth, stamped with a sigil none of them could read: a circle broken by a vertical line, like a door seen edge-on. "She gave me this and said, 'One day a Vane will need you, and the debt runs through the child.' Those words. Fifteen years I've wondered what child. Thought maybe it was a figure of speech."

Wren was holding out their hand. Bram set the token in it and watched them turn it over, thumb moving across the sigil, and saw the exact moment recognition landed—the small stillness, the breath let out through the nose, the face arranging itself back into blankness a half-second too late.

"You know it," Bram said.

"My mother had one." Barely above the fire's murmur. "She kept it in a box with her work things. I thought it was a guild mark. Some taster society." Wren closed their fist around it, and Bram watched the stranger's shoulders come down half an inch, the first unguarded thing they'd done all day. "She was a taster. In the palace. Died when I was seven—fever, they said. I was raised in the Thornfell warrens after. Smugglers' charity. That's why I know the roads."

"Not all the roads," Pim's slate said, held up with something dangerously close to a grin. **NOT THE ONES THAT MOVE.**

"No," Wren agreed softly. "Not those."

* * *

Later, when the dishes were scoured with sand and the fire had been fed down to embers, Sefa sat beside Wren at the camp's edge, and Bram pretended to check the mule's leg while listening with both ears.

"You knew her," Wren was saying. "My mother."

"Everyone in the hidden trades knew *of* her," Sefa said. "The taster who could taste intention. Who could tell you not just what was in a dish but what the cook meant by it. Kings feared her. Well." A dry smile. "Kings fear very few women. But the men behind kings—they feared her plenty."

"Why?"

"Because a taster who reads intention isn't guarding stomachs. She's guarding secrets. Every court on the continent has a poison problem, child. Only Velthmar ever produced a solution." Sefa said it lightly, tossing it off like a charm, and Bram saw Wren go rigid at the name—the name from the burning page, the name from the archive, the name that got Ilse Marrow's world put to the torch.

But Sefa only patted Wren's knee and rose, joints cracking, and shuffled off to her tent, calling back over her shoulder: "Sleep, tangled one. The mountain's been waiting for you a long while. It can wait a few more hours."

Bram gave up the pretense of mule care and came to sit on Wren's other side. Below them the valley was a black sea of pine, and far to the south, faint as a scar, the lowlands glowed with the Regent's city—ten thousand lamps, none of them lit for Wren Vane.

"You could run," Bram said. "South. Over the border. New name, new face, new kitchen somewhere nobody's ever heard of prophecy. I'd give you a week's start and lie beautifully about it."

"I know you would." Wren turned the brass token over in their fingers. "You'd charge me for the lie, though."

"Market rate."

The ghost of a smile. Then it faded, and Wren looked at the ember-light with an expression Bram recognized from mirrors—the look of someone tallying a bill that keeps growing.

"Everything I did to be nobody," they said. "Every habit. Every flat voice and forgotten face. I thought I built it. And now there's a book that says I was *written*. And my mother's debts running through me, and doors with my name on them, and a witch who says I'm tangled, and—" They stopped. Breathed. "How do you stand it? Owing people. Being known."

Bram considered lying, considered charm, considered the dozen deflections he kept oiled for exactly this weather. Instead he said: "Badly. Mostly. You make a family out of the people who see you anyway, and you feed them well, and you steal enough to keep the wolves off, and some mornings it feels like a con you're running on the whole world." He shrugged. "But it's the only game in town where losing means you're alone, so you learn to play it straight."

Wren looked at him for a long moment. Whatever they saw, they seemed to accept it, because they nodded once and tucked the token away next to their heart, and lay back on the bedroll like a person setting down a load they intended to pick up again at dawn.

* * *

Bram didn't sleep. Bram sat up with the embers and did sums, and the sums were ugly.

Inquisitors with dogs on one side. Rebels—he'd heard the whispers at the last way-station, Corvess's people moving in the high passes, looking for something—on the other. A prophecy in the middle wearing his dead friend's name. And his crew, his ridiculous, precious, unmarketable crew, sitting in a bowl of stone directly in the blast radius.

He should wake them and move camp tonight. He knew it. He'd known it since the ravine.

Instead he found himself sketching in the dirt with a stick, the way he did when a route wouldn't resolve: the deep roads as Pim mapped their edges, the sealed sections, the places where the old maps showed passages marked only with that same broken-circle sigil as the token. Four years of Pim's edges. Fifteen years of a dead woman's roads. All of it converging, like water finding stone channels, on a door under the mountain with a Vane's name on it.

The stick stopped.

"Bram." Yara's voice, low, from the dark at his shoulder. He hadn't heard her move; he never did. "Trouble sleeping?"

"Didn't try."

She lowered herself onto a stone beside him and followed his finger along the scratched map. For a while neither of them spoke.

"You're going to take them wherever the door is," she said. Not a question.

"Looks that way."

"And the inquisitors follow, and likely Corvess's rebels too, and we—" she made a sound halfway to a laugh, "—four of us and a mule."

"Five," Bram corrected. "Duchess counts. She's the only one of us with savings."

Yara snorted. Then, quieter, in the voice she saved for things that cost her: "Marlow Ford. The taster knew things about the poisoning no report ever held. Eleven years I've hated the kitchens, and the first stranger through camp takes the hate right out of my hands and shows me the truth of it, plain as inventory." She shook her head slowly. "That's not accident, Odell. Nothing about that one is accident."

"I know."

"It scares you."

"Terrifies me," Bram agreed cheerfully. "I'm planning to be extremely brave about it starting tomorrow."

From the darkness by the cold fire came the soft scrape of chalk on slate. They both looked over. Pim was sitting up in their blankets, slate raised so the starlight caught the letters:

**WE SHOULD LEAVE BEFORE DAWN. THE ROADS WILL WANT TO SHOW THEM SOMETHING. ROADS THAT WAIT THAT LONG AREN'T PATIENT.**

Below it, after a pause, chalk scratching slower:

**AND I WANT TO SEE IT TOO.**

Bram looked at the words for a long time. At his ghost, wanting. At Yara, who'd stayed when staying cost her everything, still staying. At Sefa's tent, dark and humming faintly with the click of charms as the witch turned in her sleep. At Wren, flat on their back, breathing slow, a brass token rising and falling over their heart.

A crew of small crimes. Thieves, deserters, witches, ghosts. Nobody the kingdom would miss.

Well, he thought, standing and kicking dirt over his half-finished map. Maybe that was exactly who you wanted, when the job was remembering something the whole country had been paid to forget.

"First watch is mine," he said aloud. "We break camp at grey light, and somebody wake Duchess gently, she bites before coffee."

He settled against the overhang with his back to the stone and his eyes on the southern glow, and it was there—while he watched—that he saw it. Far off across the black sea of pines, up on the shoulder of the pass they'd need to cross at dawn: a light. Not a campfire. Too steady, too high, too deliberate. Then a second, flanking it.

Lanterns. Signaling. Somebody on the ridge, in the dark, in the cold, arranged in a line across Bram's only road south.

And beneath his feet, faint as a heartbeat through the stone, the mountain began—very softly—to hum.
