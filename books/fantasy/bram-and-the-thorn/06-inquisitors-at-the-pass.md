## 6. Inquisitors at the Pass

The checkpoint appeared the way bad weather does in Thornfell: first a smell, then a silence, then the thing itself.

Wren smelled it before anyone spoke—lamp oil and wet wool and the particular astringency of inquisitorial ink, which they had spent nine years learning to distinguish from every other ink in the palace because it was the ink that wrote death warrants. They stopped on the switchback trail, one hand rising, and Bram's crew stilled behind them with a discipline that surprised Wren; smugglers were supposed to be loud. These ones moved like a held breath.

* * *

They had been walking since the small hours, down the Smuggler's Spine and into the western folds of the mountains where the paths thinned to goat-logic. Bram had talked the whole way—not nervously, but the way some men whistle, filling the dark so it couldn't fill itself—and Wren had learned things about him in self-defense: that he'd once sold candles filled with stolen letters, that Pim could map a cave by dragging one finger along its wall, that Sefa's curses were mostly herbs and confidence. That Yara slept sitting up with her sword across her knees and woke at the sound of a name spoken kindly, which was why no one spoke hers at all after sundown.

Now the trail bent around a shoulder of granite, and below them, wedged between cliff and drop like a bone in a throat, lay Greywether Pass: a single stone bridge over a ravine that sang with meltwater two hundred feet down. And on the bridge—lanterns. A wagon. A brazier burning the wrong color, white and hungry.

And Captain Dain Rook, leaning against the parapet as though he had been carved there by an artist with strong opinions about patience.

He was not a large man. Wren remembered that from the palace—the way you remember a knife is not large. He wore the grey coat of his order without insignia, because inquisitors above a certain rank considered insignia a confession of limitation, and his hands were folded, and he was looking not at the trail but at the ravine, as if the water might tell him something useful.

"He can't know we're here," Yara breathed. "He's guessing."

"Inquisitors don't guess," Wren said. "They wait until guessing becomes true." Even as they said it, Wren felt the old palace instinct wake in them, cold and clear: *read the table*. Three soldiers by the far end of the bridge, trying to look casual and failing, because casual was not taught to conscripts. One hound-handler with two dogs lying flat and miserable, which meant they'd been worked hard and recently—which meant Rook had been close all night, closer than chance. The wagon was empty. Not empty-empty. Empty *expectant*.

Rook had come to collect something he already knew about.

"He knows," Wren said. "Or he knows enough. Someone reported a party coming through Greywether, and he's set the board."

Bram shifted up beside them, and for once he wasn't smiling. Up close, without performance, his face was younger than his voice—there was weather on it, but the boy underneath hadn't entirely drowned. "Options," he said quietly. "There's a goat track down to the river. It kills half the people who try it and breaks the legs of the rest."

"And Rook would love us to try it," Wren said. "Dogs don't mind cliffs. Men do, but dogs don't."

"So we fight."

"Four of us against six and a captain who burned Ilse Marrow's archive and looked bored doing it." Wren shook their head. "No. We do the thing nobody expects a fugitive to do."

"Which is?"

"Walk up and say hello."

Sefa caught Wren's sleeve. Her grip was surprisingly strong for someone built like a bundle of dried stems. "Child," she said—she called everyone child, including Bram, who visibly enjoyed it—"what are you?"

"A food taster."

"That's what you *do*. What are you?"

Wren looked at her. Hedge-witch, the others called her, and Wren had assumed it meant poultices and pregnancy charms and small profitable fears. But her eyes were the grey of things that had seen a lot of weather and outlasted it, and they were not asking idly.

"I'm nobody," Wren said. It came out automatically, the way a lock answers a key it was made for. "That's the whole craft of me. Nobody notices a taster. We're furniture that eats."

Sefa held their gaze a moment longer, then released their sleeve. "Furniture," she said, "doesn't bleed when you move it. Go say your hello. I'll be watching your hands, and if they make signs, I'll put a wind through this pass that will strip that man's dignity off him like bark."

"That's not a real spell," Bram said.

"Neither is his authority," Sefa said. "And yet."

* * *

Walking down onto the bridge was the strangest thing Wren had done in a life that included eating professionally for a living. The planks—or rather the slabs, the bridge was old Velthmari work, Wren realized, the stones fitted too well for anything modern—rang faintly under their boots, and the ravine below sang back, and Rook watched them come the entire way without appearing to move his eyes at all, which was a trick Wren had seen the Regent do.

"Wren Vane," Rook said, when they were ten feet away. He didn't reach for a weapon. His hands stayed folded. "You've been difficult."

"Captain." Wren stopped just outside arm's reach and gave him the small bow they gave under-stewards, precise enough to acknowledge rank, shallow enough to preserve dignity. It was a servant's bow, and it was also a message: *I am still playing my role. Are you?*

"You fled the palace the night of a fire in the Marrow archive," Rook said. "You fled with a banned text or knowledge of one. You have since attached yourself to Bram Odell's crew, whose warrant I have had in my coat for two years and whose neck I intend, eventually, to have as well." He tilted his head. "I have hounds, four soldiers, a narrow bridge, and all morning. Convince me of something."

Here was the thing about being invisible for nine years: you learned exactly how visibility worked. You learned what people saw when they looked at you, because you had spent your whole life curating it, the way a cook curates a plate. Rook expected a frightened runaway. Frightened runaways ran, begged, lied badly.

So Wren did none of those things. They stood comfortably, let their shoulders drop, and said, in the flat tone of a colleague reporting to a colleague:

"You're wasting your morning. I told you I'd send word if anything moved. Something moved. I'm delivering it in person, because your hounds spooked my contact and I lost two days."

Silence. One of the soldiers by the far parapet glanced over. The meltwater sang.

Rook's expression didn't change, but something behind it did—a recalculation, quick as an abacus. "Informants report through the office. In writing."

"Your office leaked," Wren said. "Someone wanted a taster dead in the Regent's own kitchens. You knew—I saw your face when you searched Marrow's archive. You weren't surprised we existed. You were surprised someone else had acted." Wren took a breath and gambled everything on a truth, because truths were the only currency left that couldn't be forged. "You're not hunting me for the Regent, Captain. You're hunting the leak, and I'm the only witness who survived it. Arrest me and you lose your only thread. Let me walk, and I'll keep pulling it."

It was beautiful, in a horrible way, how much of it was true. The poison attempt had happened. Rook's face in the archive *had* been unreadable in the specific way of a man performing unreadability. Wren simply took the facts and laid them in a different dish, the way you present yesterday's soup under a new name.

Rook unfolded his hands. Crossed them again. Behind him, the hound-handler shifted, waiting for an order like a man waiting to exhale.

"You understand," Rook said at last, mildly, "that even if I believed you—and note that I have not said I believe you—an informant who walks away with a known smuggler crew is worth nothing to me. Less than nothing. You're compromised."

"Then take my tie to the palace," Wren said, and reached into their shirt, and pulled out the thing they had carried for nine years: the taster's badge, a small silver spoon on a chain, worn smooth by their own skin. Their last possession from the life before. Their proof of belonging anywhere at all.

They had rehearsed letting go of it on the trail, telling themselves it was strategy. Standing there, it weighed what it actually weighed.

"This says the crown trusts my tongue," Wren said. "Take it. When I find your leak, I'll want it back—and I'll want the answer to why the crown's own kitchen tried to kill its own servant. Until then, I'm nobody. Which, as it turns out, is useful in your line of work too."

They held it out.

Rook looked at the spoon for a long moment. Then he took it—not snatching, but taking, deliberately, his gloved fingers brushing the chain—and Wren felt the loss go through them like a stitch pulled from living cloth.

"Greywether Pass is closed for repairs," Rook said loudly, for the soldiers' benefit. "All travelers turn back." Then, quieter, only for Wren: "You have until the new moon, Vane. After that, you're a fugitive again, and I stop being a man you can bargain with." He turned away, then paused. "Marrow burned the page. But she'd copied it. Scholars always copy. Find her copy before someone worse than me does."

And that—that was the thing Wren would turn over for nights afterward. Because it wasn't a warning you gave a prisoner. It was advice you gave an ally, and Dain Rook knew it, and gave it anyway, and walked back to his lanterns without looking to see whether Wren obeyed.

Wren crossed the bridge. The soldiers parted, confused. The hounds didn't even lift their heads.

Only at the far end, with the pass singing behind them, did Wren let their hands shake.

* * *

Bram waited until the bridge was out of sight, which cost him visibly, and then he whooped—actually whooped, arms flung wide, nearly going over the edge of the trail in his enthusiasm. "You *walked up to him*. You handed him your—Wren, you gave the man your *spoon*—"

"It was the price of the lie," Wren said. Their voice came out level, which they were proud of. "A badge means nothing unless someone takes it from you."

"It meant something to *you*."

"Yes," Wren said. "That's why it worked."

Yara fell in beside them on the descent, silent a long while, matching strides. She had a way of walking that suggested she could do it through walls. Finally she said, "I've watched a lot of people talk their way past men with swords. Most of them lied. Some of them got lucky. You did neither—you *paid*, and he *knew* you paid, and he respected it. That's rarer than luck." She clapped Wren's shoulder once, hard, and moved ahead, and that was the whole conversation, and Wren understood they had just been admitted to something.

They camped that night in a hollow beneath a shelf of rock, with a fire small enough to hide from the sky. Pim produced dried fish and a jar of pickled roots from somewhere in the cart that defied cartography, and Bram told a story about a customs officer and a wedding cake that was probably sixty percent true, and for an hour the day's terror became instead a good anecdote, reshaped by retelling, the way events become bearable by becoming narrative.

Sefa sat beside Wren at the fire's edge and said nothing for a long time. Then:

"Give me your hand."

"My palm's not interesting."

"Everything alive is interesting. Hand."

Wren gave it. Sefa turned it over in her knotted fingers, tilting it to the firelight, and her thumbs moved along the lines with the tenderness of someone reading a face. Wren expected theater. They had watched court fortune-tellers work the palace gardens, all flourish and flattery.

Sefa stopped. Her thumb rested in the center of Wren's palm, over the lifeline's beginning, and did not move again.

"What?" Bram said, leaning in.

"Quiet." Sefa bent closer. Firelight moved on her face. "This mark here, where the deep line crosses the fate line—child, you have a gate mark. I've seen two of these in sixty years. Both times on the palm of someone standing at a door." Her voice had gone strange, stripped of its performance, down to something old. "Except theirs were faint. Wishes. This one—" she looked up, and her grey eyes were afraid, which frightened Wren more than the checkpoint had—"this one is already *open*. The lines around it are moving, changing. Palm marks don't change, child. Yours changed since I last read you at the fire, four nights ago. Whatever that door in your book is—" she closed Wren's fingers over their own palm, gently, as if shutting a book herself—"it isn't waiting for you to arrive. It's reaching for you."

The fire cracked. Somewhere upslope, a night bird called three notes and thought better of the fourth.

Wren opened their hand and looked at it: an ordinary hand, a taster's hand, scarred faintly at the fingertip from a hundred small professional pricks, trained to detect bitterness before sweetness could lie about it. An invisible hand. A nobody's hand. Except now it bore a mark that moved, that reached back.

"I burned my badge today," Wren said—not quite to the others, not quite to themselves. "Gave it away. There's no road back to the palace now, none that doesn't run through Rook's mercy, and Rook's mercy has a new moon on it." They flexed their fingers, watching firelight pool in the lines. "So the question isn't whether I choose the road. The road chose itself when he took the spoon."

Bram was quiet, which was remarkable enough that everyone noticed. Then he said, "Then we'd best get you to whatever's reaching for you before it gets impatient," and the crew murmured assent, and the fire settled, and one by one they wrapped themselves in blankets and oilcloth.

Wren stayed awake longest, palm open to the dark.

And it was near dawn, in the grey hour when the mountain exhaled cold, that Yara—who had taken first watch—stood up suddenly, head cocked toward the eastern passes, and said one word:

"Smoke."

Wren rose and looked. Far off, beyond the shoulder of the world, thin black columns were rising against the paling sky—not one column but many, spaced with terrible deliberateness, like stitches being pulled from a seam. Villages burning? Signal fires?

Or something moving through the high country in force, methodically, closing doors behind it.

Bram joined them at the ridge, squinting, and all the humor drained out of his face by degrees. "Those aren't tax patrols," he said. "That's a net being drawn. Somebody doesn't want us to reach wherever we're going." He looked down at Wren, and for the first time since Greywether, Wren saw the smuggler under the charm—the man who survived. "Question is, friend: how does whoever's burning the high passes know where we're headed?"

Wren had no answer. But their marked palm tingled, faint and insistent, like a door knocking softly from the other side.
