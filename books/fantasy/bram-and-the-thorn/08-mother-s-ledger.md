## 8. Mother's Ledger

They kept Wren in a room with a window.

That was the first thing they noticed about the rebel hold—a squat tower of black Velthmari stone wedged into the shoulder of the mountain like a tooth that had never been pulled—and it was the thing that unsettled them most. Palaces did not give prisoners windows. Prisons in Thornfell were carved where no light could be bribed to enter. But General Corvess's people had put Wren in a chamber on the third floor with a shuttered casement that looked east over a fold of pine-dark valley, and they had left the shutter unbarred.

*They want me to look at the view,* Wren thought, sitting cross-legged on a cot that smelled of cedar and old wool. *They want me to imagine staying.*

Below, the hold went about its morning. Rebels drilled in a yard worn smooth by generations of boots—though Wren would have wagered the last generation of those boots had belonged to shepherds, not soldiers. Smoke rose from kitchen chimneys. Someone was singing, low and tuneless, a work song in a dialect Wren half-knew from the warrens, except the words were wrong. Older. Rounder. Words from before the Regent's tongue had been planed flat and uniform.

Velthmar's words. Sung by people who had been punished, a century ago, for remembering them—and who had taught them to their children anyway, in whispers, in lullabies, in the spaces between official sentences.

Wren pressed their palm against the cold sill and tried to feel what Corvess wanted them to feel. Kinship. Destiny. The pull of blood calling to blood.

What they felt was the particular nausea of a person who has just realized the trap has a second door, and both doors lead inward.

* * *

Corvess came at midmorning, and she came without ceremony—which was itself a kind of theater. No honor guard, no banners. Just the general in a plain grey coat, her silver-streaked hair bound back with a leather cord, carrying a wooden box under one arm as casually as if it held bread. Two guards took up posts outside the door and closed it behind her.

"You slept," she observed.

"I pretended to."

"Good." She set the box on the table between them and sat, unhurried, folding herself into the room's single other chair as though she had sat in a hundred prisoner cells and found them all adequate. "Pretended sleep is how I spent most of my twenties. You learn things in the dark that daylight hides from you." Her eyes were pale grey, almost colorless, and they did not blink often enough. "You want to know why you're here. Not the prophecy version—the real one."

"The prophecy version was plenty real," Wren said. "A book written before my grandmother was born knew my name and tonight's date. I've been vomiting with fear since Ilse Marrow showed it to me."

"Yes. Marrow." Something flickered across Corvess's face—not quite regret. "She was meant to bring you to us years ago, when she first translated the text. Instead she sat on it. Sentiment. Scholars are hoarders; they'd rather own a truth than spend it." She rested her hands on the box. "I am not a hoarder, Wren Vane. I am going to show you something your mother left behind, and then you are going to understand exactly what you are."

"She was a cook," Wren said. "In Lowmarket. She died of lung-rot when I was nine. She salted everything too much because she couldn't smell anymore, and she used to say—" Wren stopped. They had not said any of this aloud in fifteen years, and hearing their own voice carry her across it made their throat close. "She used to say taste everything twice. Once with your mouth and once with your doubt."

Something moved in Corvess's face then, and this time Wren could name it: grief, genuine and old, surfacing like a drowned body finally given leave to rise.

"Taste everything twice," Corvess repeated softly. "Yes. That was her."

She opened the box.

The ledger inside was small—a hand-width square, bound in grey leather gone soft as cloth with handling, its corners rubbed round. It had no lock, but it had three separate strings knotted around it in patterns Wren's fingers itched to read, smuggler's knots, warrens' knots, the grammar of things passed hand to hand in the dark. The first page bore no title. Only a single line in a fine, slanted hand:

*For whoever finds this: I was the taster of Velthmar, and this is the account of what we tasted.*

Wren did not touch it. Their hands stayed flat on their knees, pressing down, as if their body might otherwise rise out of itself and drift toward the book like smoke toward a draft.

"That's not possible," they said. "Velthmar fell before—"

"Before you. Before your mother was born, some say." Corvess turned the book gently, so the cover faced Wren: a stamped device, half worn away, of a door set in a mountain. "The kingdom didn't fall, child. It was *closed*. There is a difference, and the difference is the entire reason either of us is alive. Sit. Listen. I have rehearsed this speech for thirty years and I promise you mine is shorter than hers was."

She began, then, to talk, and Wren let the words come the way they had learned to let all strange food come: slowly, attentively, testing each morsel against the doubt.

Velthmar had not been conquered. It had been *negotiated out of existence*. A century ago, its ruling houses and the rising merchant princes of the lowlands had struck a bargain after a decade of grinding war neither side could win: Velthmar would erase itself. Its name would come off the maps, its records into the fire, its songs into forbidden mouths. In exchange, its people would be quietly absorbed—names changed, titles dissolved, histories renamed as myth—and one thing would be preserved beneath the mountain, sealed behind a door only its *forgotten child* could open. The memory of everything. Held in trust. Waiting.

"And who held the key to that door?" Corvess asked, though her eyes said she already knew Wren had answered it in their head. "Not a king. Kings can be bought. Not a general—generals die and their successors renegotiate. A taster." She laid her hand flat on the ledger. "The royal taster of Velthmar was the one person in the kingdom whose whole craft was *refusing to swallow*. Whose entire discipline was suspicion. Who was trained from childhood to detect poison, deceit, corruption—in the cup, in the dish, in the treaty. The founders of the seal understood something the Regent has spent a century proving true: the last defense of a people is not their army. It is someone whose job it is to say *no* to whatever the powerful are about to put in their mouths."

The room felt colder. Wren became aware of their own heartbeat, slow and deliberate, the way it went when they were tasting something dangerous—body calm, senses wide open, every faculty bent toward survival.

"My mother wasn't from Lowmarket," they said. It wasn't a question.

"Your mother was Maren Vane, last royal taster of Velthmar, and she sealed that door with her own blood and her own words, one year before you were born." Corvess said it without triumph, which somehow made it worse. "She was offered what I am offering you. More, actually—she was offered the choice to open it in her own lifetime. The loyalists wanted their crown back. The merchants wanted the memory destroyed forever so the bargain could never be undone. And Maren Vane looked at both of them and chose *neither*. She locked the door against everyone. Loyalists, collaborators, kings, crowds. She wrote it in the ledger—here, page after page, you can read her reasoning yourself—she would not hand a weapon to men who would use it to rebuild the same throne that got ten thousand people erased."

Wren's mouth had gone dry in the specific way it went dry before a poisoning—not fear exactly, but the body bracing for what the mind already suspected.

"Then she disappeared," they said. "Married a cooper in Lowmarket. Had me. Salted everything too much."

"She hid," Corvess agreed. "Badly, at the end. The Regent's archivists were hunting the taster's line even then—they understood, better than our own loyalists ever did, that the seal required an heir. Your mother ran the warren roads for two years with you on her hip. The routes you know—the ones you thought you invented, the ones no palace map shows?" For the first time, the general smiled, and there was nothing warm in it. "You learned them from her without knowing it. She walked them into you before you could walk. She sang the turns and landmarks into lullabies. *Taste everything twice*, Wren. She was teaching you to verify. Everything. Always."

The floor seemed to tilt very slightly, the way it did when Wren had taken a dose that was working through them. They gripped the edge of the cot.

"So my immunity—"

"Is not training." Corvess's voice gentled, and the gentleness was the cruelest thing yet. "You were dosed in the womb. Maren began your conditioning before your birth—microdoses of a dozen venoms, bitterleaf, greyscale, all of it, building resistance into your blood the way a smith builds temper into steel. Every meal she cooked you carried it. Every 'medicine' she gave you as a child. By six you could eat things that kill grown soldiers. You thought the kitchens trained you." She shook her head slowly. "Child, the kitchens *discovered* you already finished. You were bred for this. Born for it. You are the last link in a chain of purpose a century long, and every woman in that chain—your mother, her mother before her—was forged for a single hour that has now arrived."

Wren stood up. They hadn't decided to; their legs simply refused the chair any longer. They crossed to the window and shoved the shutter wide, letting the cold mountain air hit their face, letting the valley's green distance fill their eyes instead of the grey little room, because if they had looked at Corvess one more second they were going to do something with their hands that would cost them dearly.

Behind them, pages turned. Corvess was reading aloud, unhurried, as if she had all the patience in the world—as if she had, in fact, rehearsed this for thirty years and knew precisely which passages cut deepest.

"*Day forty-one of the sealing,*" she recited. "*They brought the prince's man again today with honey in his voice and gold under his cloak, asking after the door. I fed him a soup he will remember. Let the record show: I did not poison him to death. I poisoned him to humility. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole of my craft.*"

"Stop," Wren said.

"*My daughter kicks as I write this. She will never know this room or these roads, and I grieve that, and I am glad of it, both at once. If she reads this someday, let her know: I did not make her a tool. I made her a door, and doors choose—*"

"*I said stop.*"

Silence. Then the soft sound of the ledger being closed, and Corvess's boots on stone as she crossed the room to stand behind Wren—not touching, keeping a careful arm's length, the way one approaches a wild thing or a drawn blade.

"You're angry," the general said.

"I'm *nothing*," Wren said, and heard their own voice come out level and dead and far too steady, the voice they used for reporting poisons to the Regent's steward. "Angry would mean I know what I'm feeling. I don't. My whole life is a lie with furniture in it. My immunity is a recipe. My warren roads are a lullaby. My name is in a book older than my grave-mother. You tell me I was bred—*bred*, like a hound—for a door I've known about for four days." They laughed, one dry bark with no humor anywhere in it. "Even my doubt isn't mine. She taught it to me. In *lullabies*."

"Yes," Corvess said simply. "And now you understand why I waited. Why I couldn't just tell you the prophecy and expect obedience." She moved around into Wren's field of vision, and her pale eyes were steady, and for one unguarded moment Wren saw the exhaustion underneath the certainty—decades of it, a woman who had carried a dead country on her back since girlhood. "Everyone else who comes to that door will come to take from it, Wren. The Regent wants it burned. The loyalists want a crown. I—" she paused, and Wren tasted the pause, tested it twice, and found something honest in it, or something that had practiced honesty so long it had become indistinguishable, which might be the same thing. "I want my grandmother's street back. The one that doesn't exist. I want the names of the erased spoken aloud in daylight by ten thousand mouths at once. That is all I have ever wanted. But I cannot open the door myself, and I will not force you, because a forced key breaks."

She held out the ledger.

"It's yours. It was always yours. Read every word—including the parts where she argues with herself, including the parts where she is afraid, including the last page, where she writes to you directly." Corvess's voice dropped. "And when you've read it, I'll ask you for your answer. Not today. Not tomorrow. A door that opens to truth should not be opened to a lie, and a decision made in anger is always partly a lie."

Wren took the ledger.

It weighed almost nothing. That was the horror of it—that a whole life, a whole lineage, a century of erasure and hiding and dosed meals and sung roadmaps, could weigh less than a loaf of bread. They held it against their chest and felt it pulse with nothing at all, and outside the window the rebels drilled below in the yard, and somewhere in the kitchens someone was cooking with too much salt.

*Once with your mouth,* her voice said in Wren's head, in the accent they had never been able to place until this moment, *and once with your doubt.*

*You knew,* Wren thought at the dead woman across the gulf of years, and could not decide if it was accusation or mourning or both braided together like the knots on the ledger's strings. *You knew what I was before I had a name, and you loved me anyway, or you told yourself you did, and I will never know which, because you salted everything too much, Mother. Even the truth. Especially the truth.*

That night, by candle stub, Wren opened the ledger and read until the candle died, and then sat in the dark with their thumb resting on the final page, not turning it, because they understood—with a taster's instinct, with a daughter's dread—that whatever Maren Vane had written last was the strongest dose of all.

They were still sitting there, unmoving, when they heard the sound that froze their blood faster than any venom: soft footsteps on the stair, a whisper of familiar rough cloth, and Bram Odell's voice, low and furious, breathing through the gap under the door—

"Wren. Don't shout. We're getting you out tonight—but there's something you need to know about the general first, and you're not going to like it."
