## 9. Bargains in the Dark

The rebel hold had been a monastery once. Bram could tell by the arches—those long, patient curves meant to hold up prayers—and by the way the corridors swallowed sound, as if the stones themselves had been taught silence and never unlearned it. Now the arches held up banners of the old kingdom, blue and silver, dyed fresh over older dye, and the silence belonged to armed men who checked every shadow twice.

Bram Odell walked those corridors like he owned them, because walking like you owned a thing was half of owning it.

He'd dressed for the occasion. Not his road leathers with their patched elbows and honest grime, but the good coat—the one he kept folded in oilcloth at the bottom of his pack for negotiations, funerals, and the rare moments when looking poor would cost him more than looking rich. Silver buttons. A collar that bit his neck. Yara had laughed at him when he'd buckled it on, that dry soldier's laugh of hers, and said, *Going to buy her back or sell yourself?*

*Buy,* he'd said. And then, because lying to Yara was like lying to a wall, *Or steal. Whichever's cheaper.*

Now he stood outside General Corvess's study while two rebels patted him down for the third time, finding exactly what he wanted them to find: a knife, a coin purse, a letter of credit from a Thornfell fence worth more than most farms. They did not find the hairpin lockpick sewn into his left cuff, or the vial of dreamless sleep tucked behind his collarbone, or the fact that he had spent the last two days mapping this monastery the way Pim mapped everything—corridors, doors, guard rotations, which floorboards sang underfoot.

Sefa had done that part. He was just wearing her work.

"General will see you now," said one of the rebels, a young man with a farm boy's shoulders and a zealot's eyes, and Bram followed him down the arch-haunted dark to the room where Wren Vane was being kept like a treasure nobody had paid for.

* * *

Corvess received him at a table set for tea.

That was the first thing Bram noted, and he filed it away the way he filed everything: she wanted this to feel civilized. She sat with her back straight as a spear shaft, gray-streaked hair braided crown-tight, wearing the old Velthmari colors not as costume but as conviction. Two cups poured. Steam rising. On the table between them, weighted flat by a stone paperweight, lay a ledger with a cracked spine and a name burned into the leather.

*VANE.*

Bram looked at that ledger longer than was polite, and Corvess watched him look, and let him.

"You're the smuggler," she said. "Odell. The roads answer to you, they say."

"The roads are polite," Bram said, taking the offered chair without being offered it. "I'm just the one who asks nicely."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile. "You want to bargain for the taster."

"For Wren Vane." He let the name sit there, whole and specific, because everyone in this room had been saying *the key*, *the child*, *the door*, and he was tired of nouns standing in for people. "You've got my crew locked in a guest wing, my cook locked in a cell with a view of the courtyard, and me drinking your tea. I'd call that a bad opening position, so I'll skip to mine."

He slid the letter of credit across the table. Corvess didn't touch it.

"Money," she said, tasting the word like something spoiled. "You think we're doing this for money."

"I think everybody's doing this for something. You want a crown. Rook wants a clean conscience. The Regent wants an erasure finished. Me—" he spread his hands, "—I want my people out from under your roof before somebody decides they're inconvenient. The letter's a start. Add safe passage through the pass, and we're square."

"And the key?"

"Wren isn't a key," Bram said. "Wren is a person who tastes food and keeps their head down and has never asked anyone for anything in their life. The key business is your problem, not theirs."

Corvess leaned back. Outside the narrow window, evening was coming down over the mountains, and the light turned her face half bronze, half shadow. "Do you know what I was doing when Velthmar fell? I was nine years old. My father carried me out of the burning archive district on his shoulders, and I looked back and saw the Regent's soldiers feeding books into the flames like cordwood. A hundred years of laws, songs, genealogies, recipes—" her voice didn't waver; it sharpened— "recipes, Odell. My grandmother's kitchen. Erased. Because a kingdom that remembers itself cannot be ruled by thieves."

Bram drank his tea. It was good tea, which annoyed him. "That's a fine speech. I believe you even mean it. But you've got a person locked in a cell, so here's my counteroffer, and it's the only one coming: Wren walks out with us, free, tonight. Then, if they *choose*—and note the word choose—to open your door, that's between them and their mother's ghost. Not you. Not the prophecy. Them."

"No," said Corvess.

Just that. No anger, no theater. She picked up the ledger and rested her hand flat upon it like a woman resting her hand on an altar.

"This is bigger than your feelings, smuggler. The Vanes were made for this. Bred for this, if the records speak true—tasters down four generations, palates refined like racehorses, immune systems cultivated the way kings cultivate orchards. Wren's mother sealed that door with her own blood rather than let any faction have it, and she died for the trouble, and her child inherited both the lock and the debt. Wren doesn't get to walk away from that. Nobody gets to walk away from a hundred years of stolen memory because a thief has grown fond of them."

The word *fond* landed in the quiet like a dropped glass.

Bram heard it shatter somewhere behind his ribs and kept his face still, because he was very good at keeping his face still. Fond. As if it were small. As if it were nothing.

"Then we're done talking," he said, and stood, and took his letter of credit back off the table, slowly enough to be insulting.

"Bram Odell." Corvess's voice stopped him at the door. It had gone gentle, which was worse than sharp. "I've read the ledger. I know what Vane's mother did for your crew—or what she tried to do. Sentiment makes men stupid. Come back when it wears off."

"It won't," Bram said, and walked out into the dark of the praying arches.

* * *

He found Sefa in the stable loft, where the crew had been quartered under guard—a gilded cage, but still a cage. She sat cross-legged in a square of candlelight among her jars and bundles, grinding something that smelled like feverfew and burnt sugar, and she didn't look up when he climbed the ladder, because Sefa never wasted motion on things she already knew.

"He said no," she said.

"He said no."

"And now you're going to do something stupid."

"Now I'm going to do something *careful*," Bram corrected, dropping down onto the hay beside her. "Which looks stupid from the outside. There's a difference, and the difference is whether I get caught."

Sefa ground her pestle twice around the mortar and stopped. In the candlelight her face was all planes and patience, a hedge-witch's face, weathered by other people's emergencies. "Tell me the plan, then."

"First, the why." He pulled his knees up, wrapped his arms around them, and found himself looking at the candle instead of at her, because some stories went down easier if you weren't being watched. "You've all been dancing around it since the pass. Yara's too disciplined to ask, Pim can't, and Wren—Wren thinks they don't deserve to ask. So."

He took a breath. It caught a little, which surprised him. Twenty years on the roads and the words still caught.

"Wren's mother was named Maren Vane. Before that, she was Maren Vane, Royal Taster of Velthmar—which means nothing now, since there's no Velthmar, but back then it meant her palate decided whether the queen ate dinner. When the coup came, she was one of the ones who saw it early. Saw what the new regime would do with a door like that one, and what the loyalists would do, and the rebels after them. Everyone holding a sword wanted what was behind it."

"The kingdom's memory," Sefa said quietly.

"Everything. Every name they burned, every song they banned, every grave they dug in secret. And Maren Vane looked at all those hands reaching for it and decided none of them were clean enough. So she sealed the door herself—with blood, with craft, with whatever the royal tasters knew about poisons and antidotes and the space between them. And then she ran. Ran with a baby in her arms and the regime's hounds behind her, and she came to the warrens, and she needed someone to move her along the smuggler's spine fast and quiet, and the crew she found was mine."

Sefa had gone very still.

"My mother's crew," Bram said. "I was fourteen. I carried lanterns. And the regime caught up with them at a place called Hollow Bridge—we lost three people that night, and my mother took a crossbow bolt meant for Maren Vane. Died with her hand in mine, and her last words were, 'Get the lady and the little one over the mountain.' So we did. Maren Vane vanished south with her baby, and I grew up owing a dead woman a debt I couldn't pay, because she was dead and her son was gone and the world forgot her name inside a year."

He finally looked at Sefa. "Until a scholar wrote it in ash. Until a taster walked onto my road with that name. Odell, they said. And I thought, well. Here it is, then. Twenty years late. The bill comes due."

"That's not why you're stealing them back," Sefa said. It wasn't a question.

"No. That's why I started." Bram rubbed a hand over his jaw. "Somewhere between the checkpoint and the cell, it stopped being a debt. Debts end, Sefa. You pay them, they're finished, you walk away light. This isn't—I don't want it finished. I want—" He gestured vaguely at the air, at the loft, at the whole ridiculous shape of his life. "I want them to eat breakfast with us tomorrow. I want to hear them complain about my cooking, because they do, constantly, and they're right, and I hate it. I want them *safe*, which is a fool's wish in this business, and I want it so badly it's embarrassing."

Sefa reached over and closed her hand briefly around his wrist—an old hedge-witch's gesture, checking a pulse, granting a blessing, hard to say which. "You know what you call that feeling," she said, "when you find a word for it."

"I don't," said Bram Odell, who was many things but not a liar. "And I'm not going to. Words like that get people killed. Look at Yara—she loves this crew and she says 'good tactical positioning.' Look at Pim—they love us and they draw us maps home. I'll do the same. I'll say 'operational necessity' and mean every syllable wrong."

Sefa smiled, small and crooked. "All right. Operational necessity. Now show me how you're getting past forty rebels and a general who reads ledgers."

Bram grinned then, the real one, the one that had gotten him banned from three taverns and married once by mistake. He unrolled the cloth he'd brought—Pim's map of the monastery, drawn from memory in careful mute strokes, guard rotations marked in red, and in green ink, a thin line running beneath the laundry yard to the old cistern tunnel the monks had dug and the rebels hadn't found.

"Laundry goes out at dawn," he said. "We go out under it."

* * *

The warning came at midnight, and it came the way all the worst news in Bram's life came: folded small, delivered by someone frightened.

A boy—one of the scullery lads, seven or eight, all elbows—slipped into the stable loft where Bram was pretending to sleep, pressed a scrap of paper into his hand, and fled before Bram could offer anything but a whisper of thanks. No seal. No signature. Just a string of characters in a cramped, precise hand that Bram recognized from the checkpoint at the pass, from a document Rook had waved in front of Wren's face:

*V.R.M. 7-3-11. K. confirms. Move soon or lose.*

Bram stared at it for a long time. He didn't need a cipher book. Wren had taught the crew Rook's palace code over campfire dinners months ago, laughing at how clumsy the man's substitutions were—*he encrypts like a clerk, because he is one*—and the letters unfolded themselves almost eagerly.

*Vane. Regent knows. Move soon or lose.*

The Regent knew. Not suspected—knew. Confirmed, the code said, *K. confirms*, and Bram felt the cold slide of that down his spine, because whatever else happened, whatever Corvess wanted, whatever the door held, the most powerful person in the country had just entered the game, and she did not send coded warnings to people she planned to spare.

He thought about waking the others. Thought about it for a long moment, sitting in the dark with the boy's footsteps fading below.

Then he went and woke Sefa instead, and showed her the paper, and watched her face do what faces did when the ground moved.

"Dawn," she said. "We go at dawn, not after."

"At dawn," Bram agreed. He folded the warning small and tucked it inside his coat, against his chest, next to the vial and the hairpins and the unnamed thing he refused to name. Somewhere down the corridor, in a cell with a courtyard view, Wren Vane was sleeping the sleep of someone who believed themselves a prisoner of one enemy at a time.

They had maybe six hours before the arithmetic changed forever.

"Operational necessity," Bram murmured to the dark, and began to dress for theft.
