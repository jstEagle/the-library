## 10. The Poisoner's Kitchen

The rebels kept their kitchen in the belly of the hold, a vaulted cellar where the walls wept mineral stains and the ovens breathed heat like sleeping animals. Wren learned its geography in three days: which flagstones were slick with grease, which corner caught the drafts that carried smoke into the guards' corridor, which of the cooks would look away and which never did.

They had put Wren to work because Corvess was clever. A taster who could not be poisoned was a taster worth feeding; a captive with a craft was safer busy than idle. *Let her knead bread,* the general had said, or so the story went around the scullery. *A key is only dangerous when it's near its lock.* The kitchen sat far from the sealed door at the hold's heart—three staircases, two iron gates, and a garrison's worth of soldiers between—and so Wren was permitted flour and fire and the long hours of a prisoner with clean hands.

Clean hands. Wren looked down at them now, dusted white, knuckles scarred from years of palace work, and almost laughed. The Regent's kitchens had taught them that hands were instruments. Bram's crew had taught them they could be gifts. Here in Corvess's hold, Wren was teaching themselves something else entirely: that hands could also be weapons—not the kind that cut, but the kind that measured.

Because that was the thing no one understood about tasting. Everyone imagined it was the mouth—the tongue's delicate alarm, the palate's aristocratic judgment. But the mouth was only the last door. Before that came the eyes, the nose, the fingertips, the memory of ten thousand meals catalogued against betrayal. And before all of that came the arithmetic. Every poison was a recipe. Every recipe had proportions. And every proportion could be altered by someone who knew what they were looking at.

Wren knew. Wren had spent a childhood in Thornfell learning which berries killed slowly and which killed fast, and an adulthood in the palace learning how poison moved through a household—a pinch here, a delay there, the subtle choreography of harm. They had never once done it. The taster's oath was carved somewhere behind their ribs: *I taste so that others need not die.* It had felt like armor for years. Standing in this stolen kitchen, stirring a pot for the men who held Bram and Sefa and Pim in cells above, Wren finally understood it was not armor at all.

It was a scale. And scales could tip either way.

* * *

The first lesson of poisoning a garrison is that you do not poison the food.

Food was watched. Food was tasted—by Wren, ironically, each morning before the officers' mess, a ritual Corvess insisted on with a thin smile, wanting both the service and the humiliation of it. Wren performed beautifully. They lifted each spoon, held each flavor on their tongue like a jeweler assessing stones, pronounced each dish clean. Their immunity made the performance flawless. Let them believe the mouth was the whole of the craft.

But a garrison does not live on meals alone. It lives on the small consumables, the unwatched things: the wine casks broached for the evening watch, the vinegar barrel, the dried plums in the storeroom, the bitterleaf tea the night guard brewed by the bucketful to keep their eyes open. No one tasted the tea. No one tasted the wine but the soldiers themselves, and they drank like men drinking wages rather than pleasure—deep, fast, unquestioning.

And there was one more thing, the thing Wren loved best: the hold's water. Drawn from a mountain spring by aqueduct into a stone cistern, sweet and cold and trusted absolutely. Nobody guards water. Water is the one dish every guest believes is honest.

Wren stood at the cistern's edge on the fourth evening, ostensibly scrubbing pots, and watched the dark surface reflect a smear of lamplight, and thought about pinches.

* * *

They began with the plum store.

Not poison at all, the first move—just craft. Dried plums kept in a damp corner quietly grew a bloom of mold that was harmless to most stomachs but ruinous to a marching gut. Any competent cook would have caught it. So Wren, dutiful as a temple mouse, reported the spoilage themselves, loudly, to the quartermaster—and then, when replacement stores were fetched up from the deep cellars, volunteered to help carry them, and in the turning of two crates, shifted the new stock into the dampest corner of the shelf and the oldest, softest fruit into the dry. In four days the garrison would be eating fruit that tasted fine and turned their insides to water. Nothing lethal. Nothing traceable. Just sixty soldiers spending their nights walking to the latrines instead of standing watch.

The vinegar was next. Vinegar was the base of half the hold's sauces, splashed over greens, mixed into the soldiers' morning posset. Wren couldn't touch its source—but they could touch the crock of stewed onions that sat beside it, and onions steeped in certain roots from the hold's own herb garden went pleasantly sharp and left a man cramping by dusk. Sefa had named the root for them once, back when the world was campfires and shared lies: *gallow's bane—we use it for fevers, but too much and a strong man thinks he's dying.* Not dying. Thinking he was. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole point.

Because here was Wren's oath, rewritten by necessity, whispered to the steam of the soup pot like a prayer amended mid-verse: *I will not kill them.* Corvess's rebels were not the Regent's inquisitors. They were Velthmari, or said they were—orphans of an erased kingdom, people who remembered nothing except that something had been taken from them, and who had decided the taking entitled them to everything. Wren understood that hunger. Had nearly been eaten by it. Killing them would make Wren no better than the coup that had made them all.

But stopping them—that was mercy too. Every day the garrison stood strong was a day closer to Corvess forcing Wren's hand against the door, or worse, using Bram's crew as leverage to make Wren press it willingly. Sick men could not march. Sick men could not storm a chamber. Sick men lay in their bunks cursing the plums while a taster walked free among them with clean hands and a plan.

Justice outside any law, measured in pinches and hours.

* * *

"You hum when you cook."

Wren's knife paused over the onion. Marta—one of the hold's cooks, a broad woman with burn-speckled forearms and the flat stare of someone who had survived three regimes' worth of bad masters—leaned against the prep table, arms crossed.

"Do I?" Wren said.

"Like a kettle." Marta took the knife from Wren's hand, not gently, and tested its edge against her thumb. "Palace-trained?"

"Taster."

"Mm. Tasters don't usually hum. Tasters are the saddest cooks I've ever met—all technique and no joy. You chop like someone who used to be hungry." She handed the knife back. "Corvess says you're the key to the old kingdom. Says your blood opens doors." Her eyes were unreadable. "I say blood doesn't chop onions. Hands do. Yours are good hands."

"Thank you," Wren said carefully.

"Don't thank me yet." Marta pushed off the table and returned to her dough. Then, without turning: "Third cistern gate sticks. Lift, then push. If you're going anywhere at night, you'll want to know that."

Wren held very still, the way they'd learned to hold still when a dish arrived smelling faintly of almonds beneath its sauce. "Why would you tell me that?"

"Because I've fed this garrison since before Corvess marched in, and I've watched her turn a rescue into a siege in a month." Marta slapped the dough down. Folded it. Slapped it again, like she was hitting a man. "Because my brother's in one of those cells upstairs—he argued against holding children hostage, if you can imagine such treason. And because whoever's been rearranging my storeroom has been doing it well enough that I've decided not to notice." She glanced back, one eye, brief and hard. "I notice everything, taster. I just choose what gets noticed."

Then she went back to her bread, humming, and Wren understood that in this kitchen of all places, invisibility had run out of road. Someone saw them. That should have terrified them. Instead it felt like the first warm coal in a cold week.

* * *

The tea came next, and with it, Wren's masterwork.

Bitterleaf grew wild on the mountainsides above the hold; the night watch picked it fresh each dusk and simmered it in great dented kettles. Alone it was merely foul. Steeped too long—or steeped alongside the right companion leaves—it became a gentle thief. It didn't fell a man. It blurred him. Edges softened, hours slipped, orders given at midnight dissolved by dawn like sugar in rain.

Wren couldn't alter what the pickers brought down. But Wren could alter the kettle. Each evening the night brew sat unsupervised for twenty minutes while the guards changed shift—twenty minutes in a shadowed alcove where a taster carrying wash-water might pause, might set down a pail, might let a folded twist of cloth steep its contents for exactly as long as a heartbeat counts to forty. The companion leaf was common as weeds; Sefa had called it *sleeper's friend* and used it in poultices. In tea, in quantity, across a whole garrison, night after night—

By the sixth day, the watch rotations had gone soft. Orders repeated twice were forgotten by morning. Two sentries were found asleep at their posts and flogged for it, poor devils, and the flogging taught the rest of them to hide their drowsiness better, which served Wren fine. A garrison that hides its weakness grows weaker still.

Corvess noticed. Of course she noticed—she was not a fool, whatever else she was. Wren watched her stalk the corridors with her officers, snapping questions, demanding explanations, and felt the peculiar vertigo of watching consequences move through other people's bodies. This was what the Regent must feel, Wren thought, every time a taster swallowed and lived. This casual arithmetic of other people's flesh.

Except Wren had chosen every pinch. Chosen the dose, the hour, the target. Chosen *not* to kill when killing would have been easier and quieter. That was the difference, and Wren held onto it like a rail in a rockslide. Anyone can destroy. The craft—the whole inherited, bred-for, stolen craft—was in the measuring. In knowing exactly how much ruin a body could carry and come back from, and giving it precisely that much, no more.

Their mother had been the last royal taster of Velthmar. She had sealed a door with her own hands to starve every faction of a weapon. Somewhere in the dark behind Wren's ribs, he hoped—absurdly, furiously—that she would have approved. That somewhere in her ledger, between entries of court dishes and court poisons, there was a page like this one: *Sickened the garrison. Killed no one. Bought time.*

Time. That was the currency. Every blurred watchman, every ruined crate of plums, every cramping soldier was another hour bought for Bram, for Sefa and Pim, for whatever plan was forming in the cells above—or forming here below, in a kitchen that smelled of onions and rebellion.

* * *

On the eighth night, everything nearly came apart.

Wren was at the cistern—third gate stuck, lift then push, Marta's gift burning a hole in their pocket of knowledge—when boots rang on the stair. Not the guards' rhythm. Sharper. Certain.

Corvess.

Wren dropped the wash-pail, seized a scrubbing brush, and became, instantly and completely, a prisoner cleaning a floor. Head down. Shoulders dull. The oldest costume they owned.

The general descended with two officers, her boots striking sparks off the stone. Up close she was younger than her voice, handsome in a hard-carved way, with the restless hands of someone accustomed to being obeyed quickly. She stopped at the cistern's rim and stared into the black water as though it owed her money.

"The watch is drunk," she said to her officers. "Or drugged. Or cursed—I no longer care which. Three breaches in five days." Her reflection rippled. "Find me the cause."

"Yes, General."

"And check the kitchens." Her gaze swept the room—over the ovens, the racks, the steaming pots—and landed, briefly, on Wren kneeling in the wet. Something moved behind her eyes. Recognition? Suspicion? Or simply the habit of weighing everyone, always, as material?

Wren scrubbed harder. Let their breath go ragged and afraid—true enough, no acting required—and hunched smaller, the invisible posture, the *nobody here but a servant* shape they had worn for twenty years. The palace had taught them this too. Everything Wren had ever been forced to learn was suddenly, terribly useful.

"The taster?" one officer murmured.

"Immune," Corvess said shortly. "That's rather the point of her. Poison slides off that one like rain off slate." But she lingered a moment longer, and Wren felt her attention like a blade laid flat against the neck—cold, not yet cutting. "Still. Immune to poison isn't immune to ambition. Watch her."

Boots receded up the stairs. The door boomed shut.

Wren knelt on the wet stone, heart hammering against the oath, and made themselves count to forty—the sleeper's-friend count, the poison-count, the count that meant *enough time has passed*. When they rose, their knees were steady. Mostly.

She suspected. Not enough, not yet. But suspicion in a woman like Corvess grew the way mold grew in Wren's carefully dampened corners—quietly, and all at once.

Which meant the slow war of pinches was over. Whatever came next had to come fast.

* * *

Marta found Wren at dawn, kneading dough with hands that trembled only slightly, and set down a covered basket between them.

"Bread for the east cells," she said, loud enough for the passing guard to hear. Then, under the thunder of her own fists on the table: "There's a loose grate behind the flour bins. Old smuggler work, this hold—they built it on top of the Warren roads, did you know that? Your roads, mountain-child." She slid the basket toward Wren. Inside, beneath a linen cloth: bread, hard cheese, a stub of candle. And a strip of paper, inked in a hurried hand.

Wren unfolded it beneath the table's lip.

*East cell, second landing. The big one talks in his sleep—says the little mapmaker memorized the whole hold. Says there's a way out through the spring channel. Tonight. Burn this.*

No signature. None needed. Bram's handwriting slanted like a man running even when he sat still.

Wren read it twice, then touched it to the oven's coals and watched the words curl into ash, feeling the strangest sensation rise in their chest—not fear, not relief, but something with the weight of both. For twenty years, being nobody had meant that no one planned escapes *with* them. Plans happened around Wren, over Wren, despite Wren. Now a man who owed their mother a debt was whispering through stone walls, and a cook who noticed everything had armed them, and a garrison lay sick and blurred and buying hours because a taster had chosen mercy as a weapon.

Tonight, then. Through the spring channel, past the third sticking gate—lift, then push—with a candle, a basket, and eight days of quiet sabotage paving the road home to the others.

Wren pressed their floured palms flat against the dough and began to work it, folding and striking, folding and striking, and if the motion resembled anything besides bread-making, no one in the kitchen was fool enough to say so.

Above them, faint through the ceiling stones, a bell began to ring—the morning muster, the garrison dragging itself upright, half of it queasy, half of it fogged, all of it unaware that its downfall had been seasoned into its supper one careful pinch at a time.

Wren hummed while they worked. Like a kettle. Like a promise.
