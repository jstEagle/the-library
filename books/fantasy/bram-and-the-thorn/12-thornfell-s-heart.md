## 12. Thornfell's Heart

The maps were drawn in ink made of crushed lichen, and they glowed faintly when Pim breathed on them.

Wren hadn't believed it at first—had thought it a trick of the firelight in the waystation where the crew had gone to ground after leaving Rook at the crossroads with his confession still hanging between them like smoke. But there it was: Pim's long fingers unrolling the stiff vellum, exhaling across its surface, and the pale lines waking one by one, silver-green, like veins filling with blood.

*Deep roads,* Pim signed with his free hand, and Yara translated aloud from habit, though by now they all half-read him.

"Deep roads," she said. "Not smuggler roads. Older."

"Older than what?" Bram asked, though Wren suspected he already knew. Bram Odell liked to ask questions whose answers he could watch land on other people's faces.

Older than the kingdom above. Older than the Regent's grandfather and his grandfather's coup. The routes Wren had learned in the Thornfell warrens—the ones that had saved their life a dozen times, the ones they'd used to slip Rook's hounds at the Spine—all of them ran along the mountain's outer skin, like the lines of a face. These went into the skull beneath.

They set out before dawn, six of them: Bram leading because he couldn't bear not to, Yara at the rear because someone had to guard against the world behind them, Sefa murmuring to her charms, Pim with the maps strapped across his back like wings, Rook walking at the edge of the group as if unsure whether his company was wanted—and it wasn't, not entirely, not yet—and Wren in the middle, tasting the air.

That last was habit. That last was inheritance, though they were only beginning to understand what that word truly cost.

* * *

The entrance Pim had marked was not an entrance at all until you looked at it sideways.

It stood halfway up a cliff face above a frozen waterfall, a seam in the rock no wider than Wren's shoulders, and if you stared directly at it your eyes slid off like rain off oiled leather. Sefa found it by closing her own eyes and letting her palm hover over the stone until the hairs on her arm rose.

"There," she said. "It's shy."

"It's a door," Rook said quietly. "Inquisition records call them veils. We burned the maps of them." He said it flatly, without apology, because he had spent the last three days refusing the comfort of apology. "I burned most of these myself. I just never knew what I was burning."

"You never do, the first time," Yara said. She said it without cruelty. It was worse that way.

The seam opened at Pim's touch—or perhaps at Wren's; afterward none of them could agree who had touched it first, and Wren privately suspected the mountain had simply decided it was tired of being closed. Cold air breathed out, carrying a smell that stopped Wren's heart for three full beats.

Hearth-smoke. Bread. Something flowering.

Underground. Under stone that had not known the sun since before the coup. And it smelled like—

Like the kitchen behind Ilse Marrow's archive, the morning she'd baked them bread from a recipe she claimed to have invented. Like the gruel the matron at the warrens' charity house had made once a year on a day she could never explain. Like every small, orphaned kindness Wren had ever been handed without knowing why it felt familiar.

"Velthmar," Wren said, and their voice came out strange, thin, as if it had traveled farther than their mouth. "It smells like Velthmar."

"You've never been," Bram said. Not a question. He'd stopped asking how Wren knew things days ago; now he just noted each impossible knowledge like a coin added to a purse.

"I've never been," Wren agreed, and stepped through.

* * *

The deep road did not behave like a tunnel.

For the first hours it was honest about itself: rough walls, dripping ceilings, the dark pressing close enough that Sefa's witchlights were less a luxury than a lifeline. But somewhere past the third mile—Pim counted miles by the knots he tied in a cord; Wren counted them by the growing warmth of the air—the character changed. The walls smoothed. Not carved, exactly. *Remembered* into shape, Wren thought. As if the stone had been persuaded rather than cut.

Then came the lamps.

They hung in niches along the walls at intervals of thirty paces, glass globes gone gray with a century of dust, and when Sefa passed the first one, it flickered. By the fourth, it burned steady, a low amber bloom inside the grime. By the tenth, the whole road ahead was strung with light, and the company walked down a corridor of small resurrections.

"They're waiting to be lit," Sefa whispered. She sounded close to tears. "All this time, they were just waiting."

Rook walked with his head bowed. He had been doing that more often, Wren noticed—not penance exactly, but the posture of a man trying to make himself smaller in a country he'd helped erase. Once, he reached out and wiped dust from a lamp globe with his sleeve, gently, the way you'd wipe a sleeping child's face. He didn't seem to know he'd done it.

Yara kept checking the dark behind them. "Corvess won't be far," she said for the fifth time. "She let us walk out too easily."

"She didn't let us," Bram said. "I stole us. There's a difference, and I'll thank you to remember it."

"She let us think we stole ourselves," Yara said, unmoved. "That's worse."

Wren said nothing. They were listening—to the road, to the air, to something underneath both that had been growing louder since the seam. A sound below hearing. Not footsteps. Not water. More like the feeling you got standing outside a great hall where a feast was already underway: the sense of many voices, muffled by distance and stone, going on without you.

*Come*, the mountain seemed to say, in the voice kitchens use when they're full of people you love. *You're late. Come anyway.*

* * *

They camped at a place where the road widened into what had clearly been a rest-stop: alcoves worn smooth by generations of travelers, a dry cistern that still held sweet water, soot-blackened hearths that lit at Sefa's touch as eagerly as the lamps had. Someone had left a clay whistle on a ledge, shaped like a thrush, and Pim held it for a long time before setting it carefully back exactly where it lay, as if it might still be expected home.

Bram cooked. Of course Bram cooked; he'd appointed himself cook within an hour of Wren joining the crew, on the grounds that anyone who'd survived the Regent's table deserved to be fed by someone with better motives. Tonight it was barley and dried plums and a scandalous amount of salt pork, and they ate in the amber light of woken lamps while the mountain hummed its buried feast-song around them.

"Wren." Rook, across the fire. His voice had a new carefulness when he addressed them, as if speaking to a person he'd spent years failing to see. "May I ask something?"

"You can ask."

"The immunity. Your mother's ledger said the royal tasters of Velthmar were bred for it—trained from infancy, dosed, hardened. Is that what it feels like? Being bred for something?"

The fire cracked. Somewhere far off, water dripped in a rhythm almost like speech.

"No," Wren said. "Being trained feels like being trained. You learn which bitterness means nightshade and which one means bad well-water. You learn to hold poison in your mouth like a stranger's secret." They turned the tin cup in their hands. "But the other thing—the knowing things I shouldn't know, smelling a country I've never entered—that doesn't feel like training. It feels like remembering. Like the knowledge was always in me and the world keeps catching up to it."

"That's what the prophecy promised," Sefa said softly. "The forgotten child wouldn't learn the door. The child would recognize it."

"And I hate it," Wren said, before they could decide whether to say it. The others looked up. "I built my whole life on being nobody. Nobody notices the taster. Nobody follows the taster home. It kept me alive for twenty-six years. And now every road I walk was written for me before my grandmother was born, and every step feels less like choosing and more like—" They searched for the word and found it ugly. "*Reporting.* Like I'm reporting for duty to a dead kingdom."

Bram set down his bowl. "Say the next part," he said.

"What next part?"

"The part where you keep walking anyway."

Wren looked at him. Firelight moved in the creases of his face, all the laughter-lines and the newer ones. He wasn't mocking. He almost never mocked anything that mattered.

"I don't know why I keep walking," Wren admitted.

"Yes, you do," said Pim—and the whole camp went silent, because Pim did not speak. Pim had not spoken in years; his voice was a rumor, a relic, a thing the crew spoke of in the past tense. He sat there with his hands still, looking embarrassed at himself, as if his own voice had escaped without permission.

"Pim?" Yara breathed.

He swallowed. Wren watched him decide—watched him choose the words the way Wren chose pinches of powder, with exactness and consequence in mind.

"You keep walking," Pim said, hoarse and slow, "because being written isn't the same as being owned. I drew maps for the Inquisition for six years. Every line I made, they owned. Then I stopped drawing what they ordered"—he tapped the glowing vellum beside him—"and started drawing what was true. Same hand. Same skill. Different master." He looked at Wren. "The prophecy wrote your road. It doesn't get to write your feet."

No one spoke for a moment. Then Bram raised his cup. "To feet," he said gravely, "and their insubordination," and the toast broke the spell, and Pim flushed scarlet and would not speak again that night—but he smiled into his bowl, and Yara put her hand briefly on the back of his neck, and Wren sat with the gift of it, turning it over like a warm stone.

Later, on watch with Yara, Wren asked her the question that had been sitting in their chest since the rebel hold.

"You served the old border garrisons. Before the Regent folded them into her army. Did you ever meet a Velthmari? Anyone who remembered?"

Yara considered the dark. "There was a woman in my first company. Old cook, name of Maret. She used to sing a song none of us knew, some nonsense lullaby about a city under a blanket. When we asked, she'd laugh and say she made it up." Her jaw tightened. "Twenty years I thought she made it up. Now I'd give a year of my life to hear it again and know what I was hearing."

Wren's skin prickled. "What were the words?"

"I don't remember. Just the tune." Yara hummed four bars, low and uncertain.

Wren knew the tune.

Knew it the way they knew the smell of the lamps, the shape of the doors, the weight of the silence ahead. Knew it somewhere older than memory, in the bred-in marrow of themselves, and their mouth was moving before their mind caught up, supplying the words in a language they had never studied:

*Sleep, my city, under snow.*
*Keep our names where no one goes.*
*When the child forgets to hide,*
*we will open wide, wide, wide.*

The song hung in the cold air. Yara stared at them.

"That's it," she whispered. "That's her song. Word for word."

"I've never heard it before tonight," Wren said, and heard the lie even as they spoke it—not a lie of intent but of fact, because *never* had stopped meaning what it used to mean. "And I've known it all my life. Both those things are true. I'm starting to suspect everything about me works that way."

* * *

On the sixth day the road began to descend in great spirals, and the air grew warmer, and green appeared.

First moss, then ferns, then actual grass—grass underground, fed by some lightless spring, growing lush around pools that reflected the woken lamps like fallen stars. Fruits hung from trees that had no business fruiting without sun: pale, heavy, sweet-smelling. Sefa identified them with trembling hands as sugarplums, a variety recorded nowhere in any book above. Rook picked one and did not eat it; he held it in his palm like evidence, like contrition, like a piece of the crime scene that history had left still smoking, finally allowed to ripen.

And then the road opened its hand, and showed them what it had been carrying all along.

The vale was a bowl of stone two miles across, roofed by living rock a hundred fathoms overhead, lit from below—lit from *within*—by veins of soft gold running through the floor like the glow of embers seen through ash. Terraces climbed the walls, orchards and stairways and aqueducts, all intact, all dusted in a hundred years of perfect silence. And at the center, spilling down the slope in tiers, stood the city.

Towers. Bridges. Ten thousand windows, dark. A palace crowned with a dome like a closed eye. Banners of faded blue still hanging from a colonnade, stiff with dust, bearing a crest Wren had never seen and recognized instantly: a thrush carrying a key.

Nobody spoke. Even Bram, who talked the way rivers run, had nothing.

Wren walked forward alone—it seemed right, seemed *required*, though no one had said so—and stood at the edge of the upper terrace with the whole sleeping kingdom spread beneath them. The buried feast-song was loud now, no longer below hearing but all around, a murmur of ten thousand voices just behind the walls of ten thousand houses, patient as bread rising.

They had braced for awe. What ambushed them instead was homesickness.

A specific, unbearable homesickness: the ache of the warrens' charity house on festival nights, when the matron sang her nonsense song and gave them honey-cakes and told them, every year without explanation, *you eat first, little one, you eat first.* The ache of Ilse Marrow's kitchen. The ache of every place that had ever felt like home before Wren understood that home was supposed to be a place you chose. This city had been feeding them their whole life through a hundred borrowed hands, and they had never once been able to say thank you.

"Oh," Wren said, very quietly, to no one, to everyone asleep below. "There you are. I'm late. I'm sorry I'm late."

Behind them, Sefa made a sound like grief. Yara's hand found Wren's shoulder and stayed.

It was Rook who broke the moment, because it was always Rook's particular punishment to see danger first. He had gone rigid, staring not at the city but at the road they'd descended—the long spiral behind them, the string of amber lamps stretching up into the dark.

"Lamps," he said. "We lit them coming down. All of them."

"So?" Bram said.

"We lit them *all the way back to the entrance.*" Rook turned, and his face in the golden light was gray. "Every veil-door on every record I ever burned worked the same way. They answered to the seal-key. To blood." His eyes found Wren's. "You didn't just walk in, Vane. You rang every bell in the mountain on your way to the front porch."

Far above, faint as a struck match, a second string of lights kindled along the spiral road. Moving downward. Moving fast.

Corvess had found the door after all.

Or something else had—and either way, it was descending toward the sleeping city, and the only person in the world who could wake it was standing at its threshold with a stolen army at her back and a found family at her side, and no time at all to decide which of them the door should answer to.
