## 14. Corvess's Crown

They dressed Wren in silk.

That was the first sign of how badly things would go. Not chains, not the cold cell they'd been kept in since the rebels took the hold—silk. Two rebel women with gentle hands and hard eyes had come at dawn with a basin of water scented with some mountain flower Wren didn't know, and they'd washed the kitchen grease from under Wren's nails as if scrubbing away a crime, and then they'd combed their hair and dressed them in a tunic of deep green silk embroidered at the collar with silver thread in a pattern Wren recognized only from Ilse Marrow's burned book: the nine-pointed star of Velthmar.

"You'll want to look worthy," one of the women said, smoothing a wrinkle across Wren's shoulder with something close to tenderness.

"Worthy of what?" Wren asked.

Neither answered. They never did. The whole garrison had that quality about it since the sickness had run through the stores—a watchfulness, a suspicion passed from eye to eye like a coin nobody wanted to hold. Three days of vomiting soldiers, three days of stalled patrols, three days of Wren ladling soup with a face like an open book and a heart like a locked box. Nobody could prove anything. But everybody felt it, the way you feel weather coming in your knees.

The women led them up out of the cell block, through corridors Wren had spent a week memorizing—guard rotations, the loose grate over the cistern channel, the storeroom where Sefa's confiscated satchel sat under lock—and into the great hall at the heart of the old hold.

It had been a temple once. You could still see it in the bones of the place: the vaulted ceiling painted with faded constellations, the dais at the far end built for an altar rather than a chair. The rebels had hung their banners from the rafters anyway, black-and-red cloth with the same nine-pointed star, and beneath them General Corvess had set two chairs on the dais instead of one.

Two.

Wren's stomach did something complicated at the sight of them.

* * *

Corvess stood waiting at the base of the dais, and she was, as always, beautiful the way a drawn blade is beautiful. She wore Velthmari colors without irony—the old court dress cut in the old style, high-collared, silver-starred—and her gray-streaked hair was braided in a fashion Wren had seen only in illustrations. She had made herself into a portrait of a dead kingdom and called it loyalty.

"Wren Vane," she said warmly, spreading her hands. "Forgive the theatrics. Some moments deserve ceremony."

"Some moments deserve honesty," Wren said. "I haven't had any of that yet."

Corvess laughed—a real laugh, unoffended, which was somehow worse than anger would have been. She gestured, and the guards withdrew to the walls, leaving the two of them alone beneath the painted stars.

"Honesty, then." She began to walk, slow, circling Wren the way a jeweler circles a stone. "A hundred years ago, my ancestors served the crown of Velthmar. Not its kings—its *keepers*. When the coup came, when the Regent's grandfather's grandfather burned the maps and salted the archives, my family kept what remained. Names. Seals. The true history, hidden in kitchens and cellars, passed hand to hand down ten generations of people who were told they were traitors for remembering their own country." She stopped before Wren. "You know what that costs. Your mother paid it too."

"I know what you've shown me in your ledger," Wren said carefully. "I don't know what's true."

"The ledger is true."

"Ledgers can be forged. I've seen the palace do it. A column of numbers can hang a man."

Something flickered behind Corvess's certainty—irritation, quickly smoothed. "You're cautious. Good. Your mother was the same. It's in the blood." She turned and gestured up at the two chairs. "Do you know why I brought you here?"

"I imagine you're going to tell me."

"The door opens for the forgotten child. Only for them. We've known this for a century—we've *planned* around it—and now, by grace or accident or whatever force moves this tired world, the child exists, and is standing in my hall wearing Velthmar silk." Her voice softened into something almost reverent. "When we open that door, Wren Vane, we don't just recover a city. We restore a *rightful order*. And a restored order needs more than a general. It needs sanctity. It needs a symbol the people can see and believe in."

"And you're offering me the second chair."

"I'm offering you the *first*," Corvess said, and let that land. "I am a soldier. I know it. I can take a city; I cannot be one. But you—you are the prophecy made flesh. The name written in ash a hundred years before your birth. The child sealed in blood and bred immune to every poison, who will stand before the door that remembers. Let them crown *you*. Let me kneel. I will hold the sword so you never have to touch it. Velthmar reborn, with you upon its throne—not because you seized it, but because the kingdom itself wrote your name in fire and waited for you."

* * *

The hall was very quiet. Somewhere above, wind moved through the mountains and made the banner-hems whisper against each other.

Wren looked at the chairs. They were old, older than the rebellion, older probably than Corvess's whole lineage—dark wood worn pale at the armrests by centuries of gripping hands. One of them had a cushion. The other didn't. Someone had thought about that. Someone had decided, in advance, which chair Wren would sit in, and padded it accordingly, and that small domestic cruelty told Wren everything the speech hadn't.

They thought of the palace. Of the Regent's table, where Wren had eaten death a spoonful at a time so that great men could dine undisturbed. Of the oath they'd sworn at thirteen, kneeling on cold tile: *my body is the kingdom's shield.* They had believed, once, that this was service. Then they'd learned it was inventory. A tool doesn't get asked. A tool gets maintained.

Then the prophecy. A scholar's forbidden book with their own name inked in it, dated, precise, beside a drawing of a door—every step of their life laid out by a dead hand, so that even their flight through Thornfell, even Bram Odell's grinning rescue, even this moment had the flavor of a script already read aloud. Being chosen, Wren had discovered, felt exactly like being used. There was no seam between the two experiences. Chosen was just *used*, with better lighting.

And now this. Now a third hand reaching for them, dressed in silk and ancestor-worship, offering a throne as if a throne were a gift and not another kind of leash—one with a nicer room.

"Say something," Corvess said gently.

"You've rehearsed this," Wren said. "The speech. The chairs. The silk. You had those women wash me like I was being presented at market." They looked up at her. "Who decided the green? Was it you? It suits the star, I suppose. Very tasteful. Very dead."

"Careful—"

"No. You said honesty. Here's mine." Wren's voice didn't rise; they had been trained all their life to keep their voice level while saying terrible things, and it served them now. "You want to put me on a chair because a chair doesn't argue. A crowned symbol smiles at ceremonies and signs what generals put in front of it. I'd be the prophecy's mouthpiece, your seal of approval, stamped on whatever you decide is righteous. I've been an instrument my entire life, General. The palace tuned me like a fork. The prophecy aimed me like an arrow. And now you want to engrave your crest on me and call it coronation." They stepped back, out of her orbit. "I'm done being held by other people's hands."

For a long moment Corvess simply looked at them, and the beauty of her face went strange and still, like ice forming over deep water.

"You misunderstand what's being offered," she said at last. "This isn't a request I can withdraw politely, child. The door opens for you or not at all. If you won't wear the crown willingly—" She sighed, genuinely regretful; that was the horror of her, the regret was genuine. "Then you'll open it unwillingly, and we will find some other arrangement for afterward. Symbols can be mourned as easily as followed. Martyrs are cheaper than monarchs and twice as useful."

"There it is," Wren said softly.

"What?"

"The honest part. Underneath the silk." Wren smiled, and knew it wasn't a kind smile, and didn't care. "You never wanted a king. You wanted a doorstop with a pedigree. You'd have taken anyone the prophecy named—but it named me, so now you need me breathing until the seal breaks. After that—" They shrugged. "After that I'm a loose thread. Loose threads get cut. Ask my mother."

Color left Corvess's cheeks and came right back, disciplined. "Your mother lacked vision," she said. "She saw a door that could destroy us and so she locked it. I see a door that can *restore* us. History belongs to people willing to use it."

"History isn't yours to use. That's rather the point of it."

"Guards," Corvess said, not loudly, and the hall filled again with armed silhouettes. She straightened her collar, and the regret returned to her face like a mask settling back into place. "Take our guest back to their rooms. See that they're comfortable. Tomorrow we march for the vale, and the day after, Wren Vane, you and I are going to open a door together—willingly or otherwise."

As they took Wren's arms, Corvess leaned close, and her voice dropped to a register meant for no one else.

"For what it's worth, I hoped you'd say yes. You have your mother's spine. It will look lovely in the histories either way—heretics always write the prettiest versions of the people they break."

* * *

The cell again. The straw pallet, the bucket, the bar of grey light from the vent slit near the ceiling. The two gentle-handed women brought supper—bread, cheese, watered wine—and Wren ate none of it, not because they feared poison but because they needed their wits, and because eating a jailer's bread felt, tonight, like agreeing to something.

They sat against the wall and took inventory, the way their mother's ledger had taught them without meaning to.

Sefa was held somewhere in the east range—they'd heard her voice through a wall two nights ago, arguing scripture with a rebel chaplain, glorious and profane. Pim and Bram were kept apart, in cells Wren hadn't located; Yara they hadn't seen at all, and the not-seeing sat in Wren's chest like a swallowed stone. Rook was somewhere in the mountains with his hounds and his shame, carrying a coded warning Wren had never managed to answer: *the Regent knows about the door.* Which meant the Regent was coming. Which meant Corvess's timetable wasn't ambition—it was panic. The general was racing a crown that was already rolling downhill toward her.

And tomorrow they marched.

Wren pressed their palm flat against the cold floor and thought about doors. About hinges and seals and the weight a door carries on both sides. Their mother had sealed one to keep it from everyone. The Regent wanted it destroyed to keep it from everyone. Corvess wanted it opened to keep it for herself. Everyone, everyone, everyone wanted to decide what the truth was worth and who got to hold it, and every single one of them had looked at Wren Vane and seen not a person but a *keyhole*.

Well. Keys had edges too.

From the corridor outside came footsteps—the changing of the guard—and with them, faintly, a sound Wren almost missed: three soft knocks, pause, two knocks. Pause. Three knocks, pause, two.

Wren went very still.

It was nothing. It was smuggler rhythm, campfire rhythm, the knock Bram Odell used on the inside of a wagon wall to mean *still alive, stay low*. It couldn't be him; he was locked in some other hole of this hold. It was probably a sentry drumming bored fingers on a spear haft.

But the pattern came again. Three knocks. Pause. Two. Closer now, passing along the corridor like something being handed from hand to hand down a line of dark—and Wren realized it was being *passed*, guard to guard, cell to cell, all through the rebel hold, and that somewhere in the dark other palms were pressing against other walls, listening.

Someone was counting the crew. Someone was making sure everyone knew: *we are still here.*

Wren lifted their hand and knocked back—three, pause, two—against the stone, softly, the way you answer a question you're not sure you heard.

Somewhere below, muffled by floors and fear, someone answered.
