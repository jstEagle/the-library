## 15. Siege of the Vale

Bram Odell had been in a great many fights, and he had learned to tell them apart the way a sailor tells weather. There were bar-fights, quick and stupid and over before the second drink spilled. There were ambushes, which were only bar-fights with worse lighting. And there was the kind of fight that arrived quietly, the way frost arrives, so that by the time you noticed it, everything you loved had already gone white and brittle.

This was the third kind.

He felt it first in his boots. The vale under the mountain was silent in a way that no natural place was silent—it was the silence of a held breath, of a city asleep beneath stone and waiting—and into that silence, deep in the rock, came a vibration too low to hear. He felt it through the soles of his feet, up through his knees, into his teeth. Boots. Many boots. Marching in step, which smugglers never did and soldiers always did.

"They're coming," he said, to no one and to everyone.

The door chamber was a cathedral of the buried kind. The door itself stood at the far end, taller than three men, carved from a single slab of pale stone that seemed to drink the light of their lanterns rather than reflect it. Its surface was worked with a relief of hands—hundreds of hands, reaching, and at the center a single open palm the size of a shield, waiting. Sefa had spent the better part of two days reading it, her lips moving through dead syllables, and now she stood before it with her arms crossed and her face the particular gray she got when the news was bad and she was deciding how much of it to spend.

Wren stood beside the door with one hand hovering near the carved palm, not touching, the way you hover near a sleeping animal you've been told not to wake.

"How long?" Yara asked. She had taken up a position at the chamber's mouth with the ease of a woman who had defended worse ground for worse reasons. Her sword was already drawn. It had been drawn, Bram noticed, since before dawn, which meant she had known too.

"An hour," Bram said. "Maybe less. They're not hurrying. Corvess doesn't need to hurry. She's got two hundred of them and we've got—" he counted, because counting was the only arithmetic that had ever been honest in his life, "—five. And a scholar who's half a mountain away with a broken ankle."

"Rook's coming," Pim said.

Everyone turned. Pim had not spoken—Pim had not spoken in three years, not since the night Bram had found them in a burning waystation with their tongue cut and their maps scattered in the snow—but they were signing, quick and precise, their fingers carving the words into the torchlight. *Rook is coming. Alone. Ahead of them. Half a day.*

"You're sure?" Bram asked, though he knew better than to ask. Pim's certainty was a fixed star. You navigated by it or you drowned.

Pim nodded once. Then their hands moved again, slower. *He's bringing something. Heavy. On a cart.*

Sefa made a small sound, like a stitch tearing. "The Regent's not sending an army to open a door," she said. "She's sending one to close one. Permanently." She looked at Wren. "There's a ritual. I found it in the marginalia. To unmake the seal, you don't have to break the stone. You poison the memory. A draught poured into the palm—something brewed to kill remembering itself. The door would still stand. It just wouldn't *be* anything anymore. A wall with a story no one can read."

Wren's hand curled away from the carved palm, slow, as if the stone had grown hot.

"Then we hold the chamber," Yara said, "until Rook arrives, and we take whatever's on that cart."

"And then what?" Bram asked. "Two hundred against five?"

"Two hundred against five and a door," Yara said. "The door only opens one way. They have to come at us through that corridor." She nodded back at the passage they'd entered by—a throat of stone barely wide enough for three abreast, sloping up toward the rebel-held tunnels. "I've held worse."

"You've *said* you've held worse," Bram said. "I've never once seen the worse."

Yara smiled at him. It was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of a soldier doing arithmetic of her own, and Bram had spent enough years around soldiers to know that when they started smiling like that, they had already finished the sum and didn't like the answer.

* * *

They prepared the way Bram's mother had taught him to prepare a smuggler's run: not with speeches but with lists.

Water, at the spring that wept from the chamber's eastern wall, cold and mineral-bright. The rebels' own rations, taken from the supply cache Corvess's scouts had left at the pass—Bram felt no guilt about that particular theft; you couldn't rob a man who was planning to rob you of everything. Sefa went through her pouches with a surgeon's calm, laying out the last of her powders: the blinding salt, the choke-smoke, the little clay beads that burst into fire when thrown hard enough, of which there were four, which Sefa said was either plenty or nothing depending entirely on the throwing.

Wren worked beside her in silence, grinding something with a mortar stolen from the rebel kitchen, and Bram watched them without meaning to.

He had been watching Wren Vane for weeks now, and he had still not entirely forgiven himself for it. Watching people was a habit from the warrens—you watched everyone, always, because in Thornfell the ones who didn't watch were the ones who woke up robbed or dead—and Bram had told himself for years that he watched Wren the way he watched everyone: as a risk to be assessed, a variable to be managed. But somewhere between the pass and the deep mountain, the watching had changed its nature, and he knew it, and he hated knowing it, because Bram Odell had buried one Vane already and had sworn on her grave that he would never again care about someone the crown had marked, the prophecy had claimed, and the world had decided to use up.

And here he was, watching Wren's hands—those careful, taster's hands, scarred at the fingertips from a lifetime of sipping death in thimblefuls—grind a powder that might be the only thing standing between the door and oblivion.

"You're staring," Wren said, without looking up.

"I'm supervising."

"You're staring *supervisingly*."

"I contain multitudes," Bram said, and Wren huffed something that was almost a laugh, and Bram felt the almost-laugh land somewhere under his ribs where he kept the things he didn't name.

Yara came back from the corridor mouth and reported, in the flat voice she used for facts: torchlight in the upper tunnels. Not advancing. Waiting for full dark, or for whatever their general was waiting for. Corvess had sent one messenger down, alone, unarmed, with an offer: open the door, and the crew would live, honored, ennobled, remembered as the founders of the restored crown.

Wren had listened to the whole speech with their face perfectly still, and then said, "Tell her I've been somebody's symbol before. It didn't fit."

The messenger had gone back up. The torches had stayed. And the vibration in the floor had grown, hour by hour, patient as rot.

Now, as the last of their preparations wound down, Wren came and stood beside Bram at the chamber's edge, and for a while neither of them said anything, because the thing between them had long since outgrown the things they knew how to say.

"My mother sealed this door," Wren said at last, quietly. "She spent her whole life being the crown's creature so she could do one thing that mattered, and then she did it, and it got her killed, and she never told a soul. Not even—" Wren's jaw tightened. "Not even her own child. She let me grow up thinking I was nothing. So that no one would ever look at me twice."

"That's a hell of a thing," Bram said. "Being raised as a secret."

"It's a hell of a thing being *anyone's* plan." Wren looked at him then, and in the lantern light their eyes were the color of the deep water in Thornfell's cisterns. "Corvess has a plan for me. The Regent has a plan for me. The prophecy had a plan for me a hundred years before I was born. You know what I keep thinking about? The kitchen. The rebel garrison's kitchen. The three days I spent putting feverfew and bitterroot into their stores—not enough to kill, just enough to slow them down. That was the first time in my life I did something that nobody planned for me. Nobody scripted it. It was just mine." A pause. "If we get out of this, that's the life I want. Small things. Mine."

Bram looked at the door—the great pale slab with its hundred reaching hands—and thought about all the enormous things people wanted to do with it, and all the small things it was actually going to cost.

"If we get out of this," he said, "I'll teach you the Thornfell roads. The real ones. The ones even I only half know." He grinned, because grinning was armor and he had worn it so long it had grown into the skin. "Fair warning: I'm a terrible teacher. Pim learned the eastern passes from me and they still won't speak to me."

The joke left his mouth and died there, because Pim, at the far side of the chamber, looked up at the sound of their own name, and something crossed their face—something old and bruised—and Bram wished, not for the first time, that he could take back a sentence spoken years ago in a snowbound camp, when grief had made him cruel and he had told Pim that a cartographer who couldn't talk was a map with half the legend torn off.

Pim had not spoken since. And Bram had carried the silence like a stone in his boot ever since.

* * *

They came at midnight, and they came the way Corvess did everything: beautifully, and with overwhelming force.

The first sign was the torches in the corridor snuffing—not blown out but *drowned*, a wave of choke-smoke rolling down the throat of stone ahead of the rebels, and Sefa was already moving, hurling her counter-powders in great arcs that bloomed into clean air, and then the first ranks came through the smoke with shields locked and torches relit and Corvess's black-and-gold banners snapping, and the battle in the door chamber of a buried kingdom began.

It was, Bram would think later, less a battle than a tide against a breakwater. The corridor's narrowness was everything—Yara had been right about that—and Yara herself was the rest of it. She stood at the chamber mouth like something out of the old songs Bram's grandmother used to sing badly: a wall in a worn soldier's coat, her blade moving in short, economical strokes that wasted nothing, that gave nothing. The first three rebels who came at her went down. The next three learned caution. Behind her, Sefa's powders turned the corridor's choke-smoke back on its makers; behind Sefa, Wren moved among the wounded—both sides' wounded, because that was who Wren was—doing small, precise, life-saving things with those careful hands.

And Bram fought the way he'd always fought, which was badly and effectively: no form, no school, just a smuggler's knife and a smuggler's willingness to cheat. He broke a lantern over a sergeant's helm. He pulled a man's own banner-pole between his knees and introduced him to the floor. He fought, and he counted, and the counting was bad. For every rebel down, two more came through the smoke. Corvess was feeding them in a company at a time, letting the corridor grind them, and Bram understood with cold clarity that she could afford to lose a hundred men to take this room, and she would.

"Fall back to the door!" he shouted. "We can't hold the mouth past the next wave!"

"We fall back, they take the chamber," Yara answered, parrying, her voice barely winded, which frightened him more than the army did. "We hold."

"Yara—"

"*Hold.*"

The next wave came with hooks and chains, the way you fight a defended corridor when you've stopped caring about your own casualties—grappling irons to drag the defender off her feet—and Bram saw it coming and screamed a warning and Yara was already moving, already turning the first hook aside, and the second one took her across the thigh, and the third took her sword arm, and she went down fighting, dragging two rebels down with her, and Bram was moving before he knew he was moving, before any thought in him except *not again, not another one, I will not stand over another body—*

He reached her through a red haze. The chamber had gone strange and slow. Sefa was screaming his name from somewhere. He got an arm under Yara and hauled, and she was heavy the way the dying are heavy, all her soldier's weight surrendered at once, and there was a spear in her side that he made himself not look at, because looking was a luxury and hauling was the job.

"Corridor," she said, through her teeth, as he dragged her back. "Blow it. Blow the corridor. Sefa—the fire beads—the ceiling's rotten there, I mapped it, I *told* you I'd held worse—" She was laughing, actually laughing, blood in her teeth. "Blow it and they dig for a week."

Sefa was already there, the four clay beads in her palm, her face wet. "The blast will bring half that wall down. Anyone in the mouth—"

"I'm in the mouth," Yara said. "That's rather the point."

"No," Bram said. "No, we get you back, we—"

"Bram." Yara caught his collar with her good hand, and her grip was still a soldier's grip, and her eyes were still doing arithmetic. "You carried me. That's more than I had coming. Now put me down and let me finish my sum." She looked past him at Wren—at the door, at the pale palm waiting. "Everything we did. The pass, the checkpoints, Rook, all of it. It was always going to cost. I'm just the one holding the coin." She smiled her terrible smile. "Spend it."

They spent it.

Sefa threw the beads, all four, into the corridor mouth as Yara turned to face the oncoming wave with her broken sword raised, and the blast took the rotten ceiling and the corridor walls and the first rank of rebels and Yara Vane's war-sister with them, and the door chamber filled with dust and thunder and then, slowly, with a silence so total Bram could hear his own heart failing to keep time.

When the dust cleared, the corridor was gone. A wall of collapsed stone stood where it had been. And Yara was under it, and no digging in the world would reach her in time, and she had known that, and she had smiled.

Bram knelt in the dust with his fists against the rubble until Sefa's hands found his shoulders and held on, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing in any language they shared that was big enough.

* * *

They did not have long to grieve. The vibration came back within the hour—new boots, new tunnels, Corvess's sappers already hunting another way in—and it was Pim who found it, Pim who had spent the grief-hours the way Pim spent all hours, working: spread-eagled against the chamber wall with fingers pressed to the stone, reading the mountain's bones through the rock the way other people read letters.

Then Pim stood, and crossed the chamber, and did the thing that stopped Bram's breath.

They took Wren by both shoulders. And they spoke.

"Listen to me." Pim's voice was rust and river-stone, three years unused, cracked and quiet and absolutely steady. "The eastern wall. There's an old service way behind it—pre-purge, Velthmari masons cut it for the door-keepers. It runs up under the rebel tunnels and out to the high vale. Corvess doesn't know it exists. Rook's maps don't show it. Only the stone remembers, and I've been listening to the stone since we got here." Their hands were shaking, but their voice was not. "The sappers will breach the north wall within two hours. You cannot be here when they do. You have to go *now*, Wren, and you have to take the door with you."

"Take the—" Wren stared. "I can't move a door."

"You don't move the door. You move the *seal*." Pim's eyes were fierce and wet. "Sefa worked it out—the palm, the center carving. The door isn't the lock, it's the hinge. The seal lives in the one who opens it. Your mother didn't lock a door, Wren. She locked a *name* into a bloodline, and the bloodline walked out of the vale, and the door has been waiting ever since for its key to come home and speak." Pim's grip tightened. "Put your hand on the palm. Say the words your mother said, backward, the way Sefa taught you. The door will open. And what's behind it—"

"Is the kingdom," Wren whispered.

"Is the kingdom," Pim said. "Ten thousand sleeping memories. And when it opens, every soul under this mountain is going to wake up remembering Velthmar—the rebels, the Regent's army, all of them, at once, mid-siege. That's not a weapon anyone can hold. That's a weapon that holds *everyone*. Which is why it has to be you, and it has to be *now*, before Corvess breaches, before Rook's cart gets here, before anyone can put their hands on it and turn remembering into ruling." Pim let go, and their voice finally cracked, three years of silence breaking open all at once. "I watched a whole waystation burn because men wanted to own the truth of a map. I'm not watching it happen to a kingdom. *Go.*"

The chamber shook. Fine dust sifted down from the ceiling like the first snow of a very short winter. Somewhere beyond the new wall of rubble, hammers were already ringing against stone.

Bram caught Wren's arm. "I'm coming with you."

"No," Sefa said, and her voice was steel over grief. "Someone has to hold this room. Someone has to make Corvess believe the door is still sealed when her sappers break through, or she'll tear the vale apart looking for you. That's Pim and me. That's the last sum." She looked at Bram, and for once there was nothing hidden in her face at all. "You go with Wren. You've been going with Wren since the pass, you great blind fool. Finish it."

The north wall boomed. Dust jumped from the floor. And Wren Vane turned to the great pale door with its hundred reaching hands, and pressed one scarred palm to the open carved one, and began to speak.

Bram drew his knife, put his back to the eastern wall, and waited for Pim's hands to show him where the stone would open.

Behind the rubble, the hammers rang faster. And under his boots, the whole mountain drew one long breath—and held it.
