## 16. The Regent Arrives

The dust had not yet settled from the siege when the mountain itself went quiet.

Wren felt it first in their teeth—the same wrongness they'd learned to read in a suspicious broth, the stillness that meant something had been added to the pot. The clash of steel in the outer vale faltered. The rebels' war-horns stopped mid-blast. Even the wounded stopped crying out, as if the whole chamber were holding one long breath.

Then the sound came: boots. Hundreds of them. Marching in step down the pilgrim road that no pilgrim had walked in a hundred years.

"They're here," Rook said.

He stood at the shattered archway with his sword drawn and his back to the door, and Wren saw something in his face they had never seen there before—not the cold mask of the Inquisition, not even the raw shame of the pass where he'd laid his maps on the ground like an offering. This was older. This was a man watching his whole life walk toward him wearing its true face at last.

"How many?" Bram asked. He was bleeding from a gash along his forearm, Yara's blood drying black on his knuckles, and he did not seem to notice either wound.

"All of them," Rook said. "When the Regent moves, she moves entire."

* * *

They had perhaps a quarter hour, and they spent it badly, the way people always spend the last quarter hour of anything—arguing.

Sefa wanted to seal the door again, or try; her fingers were already chalked and trembling over the old Velthmari glyphs. Pim shook their head and sketched fast in the dirt: *the tunnels behind us are collapsed, we sealed our own retreat, remember?* They did remember. The memory of it was still in Wren's shoulders from hauling stone through the dark while the mountain groaned around them.

"We fight," Yara would have said. But Yara was dead, wrapped in her own cloak on the cold stone by the eastern wall, and every time Wren's eye found that shape they had to look away again, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

"She'll parley first," Rook said. "She always parleys. She likes to watch people understand what they've lost before she takes it."

"You know her that well?" Bram asked, and there was an edge under the question—not accusation, not quite, but close kin to it.

"I served her for nineteen years." Rook turned the sword slowly in his hands, studying the blade as if he'd never truly looked at it before. "I burned her letters for her. I burned a kingdom's letters for her. I thought I was serving order." He laughed once, short and terrible. "I was serving a woman who was afraid of a door."

The light in the chamber changed.

It came down the great stair in waves—lantern-light first, dozens of lanterns, then the white glare of inquisitor-lamps with their smoked glass and their cruel focus, and beneath all of it a colder radiance that Wren took a moment to identify: silver. Armor. Rank upon rank of it descending into the buried capital like mercury poured into a wound.

"Positions," Bram said quietly, and the crew moved—Sefa behind the door's plinth, Pim flat against the wall in shadow, Wren beside Rook at the archway because some stubborn instinct refused to let them hide anymore. That instinct had cost them everything. It might as well be allowed to finish the work.

The Regent came last.

Wren had seen her twice from a distance—at a distance being the whole architecture of Wren's former life—and both times she had seemed less a woman than a decision the country had made about itself. Tall, gray-cloaked, her face famously plain, famously unreadable, famous precisely for having no fame. Coins bore her profile and no one could describe it. Portraits hung in every courthouse and no two agreed.

Up close, in the white light of her own lamps, the Regent of Thornfell looked tired.

That was the first horror of her: how ordinary she was. A woman of perhaps sixty, with ink-stained fingertips and the faint stoop of someone who had spent decades bent over documents. She might have been Ilse Marrow's sister. She might have been anyone's grandmother, come down into the dark with a lamp to find a lost grandchild.

Except for her eyes. Her eyes moved across the chamber—the dead rebels, the wrapped body that had been Yara, the blood, the soot, the four ragged survivors standing between her and the door—with the unhurried thoroughness of a woman reading a ledger she had written herself.

"Captain Rook," she said. Her voice was quiet enough that the whole chamber leaned in to hear it. "You were ordered to destroy this place in the spring."

"The tunnels were unstable, Majesty."

"Mm." She descended the final steps. Her guards fanned out behind her in perfect silence, silver ranks filling the chamber's mouth like water filling a cup. "And instead you sent me coded warnings, surrendered your seals, and stood armed beside smugglers at the tomb of a kingdom you swore was ash." She stopped three paces away. Up close, Wren could see the ink stains were old, layered, years of them, a sediment of paperwork. "I want to understand you, Dain. Indulge me. When did it start?"

Rook's jaw worked. "The burning," he said finally. "The archive purge. You told me to burn everything, and I did, and afterward I couldn't—" He stopped. Started again. "I remembered too much of what I burned. That's all. A man who carries books to a fire reads them on the way."

"A dangerous habit."

"Yes, Majesty."

She studied him a moment longer, and Wren watched her arrive at a conclusion the way a taster watches poison bloom in a spoon: knowing exactly what was happening, powerless to stop it.

"Kill him," the Regent said.

* * *

It happened fast and it happened slow, the way violence always does.

Rook moved first—not away, but forward, into the space between the Regent and her nearest guard, his sword coming up in a guard-position Wren recognized from the checkpoint at the pass, the stance of a man defending something rather than attacking. The guard's halberd came down. Steel met steel with a sound like a struck bell, and then the whole chamber was noise.

"Behind the plinth!" Sefa screamed, and Wren ran, because running toward the door had become the only direction left in the world.

Bram fought the way he did everything—laughing, swearing, cheating. He'd gotten a knife into the knee joint of one armored trooper and was using the man's shield as a stepping stool to reach another's throat. Pim had vanished entirely, which meant Pim was doing something useful somewhere unseen. And Rook—

Rook held the center alone, sword against halberds, giving ground inch by inch but never breaking, and Wren understood with terrible clarity that he wasn't fighting to win. He was fighting to buy seconds. He had done the arithmetic of the chamber in the time it took the Regent to give her order, and he had decided what his remaining minutes were for.

"Dain!" Wren shouted it without deciding to.

"Don't waste them!" he called back, parrying, laughing—a real laugh, cracked and wild. "All my life I filed reports! This is the first honest work I've ever—"

The halberd took him through the chest.

He stayed up. That was the unbearable part—he stayed up, drove the point home into his attacker's throat, and turned, hand pressed to the wound, and looked at Wren across the chaos with an expression of furious apology.

"The maps," he said. "In your pack. Page nine. The—" Blood filled his mouth. He swallowed it and finished anyway, because he was the kind of man who finished things. "The counter-seal. She doesn't know about page nine."

Then the second halberd came down, and Captain Dain Rook of the Inquisition folded onto the stones of the kingdom he had helped erase, and did not get up.

* * *

"Enough," the Regent said.

One word, and the fighting stopped—not because her soldiers were winning, though they were, but because her voice cut through the chamber like a blade through cloth. The surviving guards withdrew to their ranks. Bram stood panting over two downed troopers, knife low, chest heaving. Sefa had a warding-sign half-drawn and nowhere to put it.

The Regent walked past Rook's body without looking at it.

She walked to the door.

Wren had expected triumph in her, or fury, or the cold appetite of a woman arriving at the thing she'd killed a country to prevent. Instead the Regent stood before the great sealed slab of Velthmar-black stone and looked at it the way Wren had once looked at their mother's ledger: as a person looks at the sum of everything they have done, hoping to find it justified.

"You don't know what's behind it," the Regent said, without turning. "None of you do. You think it's treasure. Freedom. A crown for General Corvess, whoever she's bribed into believing that. A truth to set the world right." Her gloved hand rose and hovered an inch from the stone, not touching. "I know what's behind it. I've always known. I was raised on the stories of it."

Wren's mouth was dry. "Then tell us," they managed. "You're going to kill us anyway. Tell us first."

For the first time, the Regent turned and looked directly at Wren. Her eyes were brown. Ordinary brown. That was somehow worse than anything.

"Yes," she said. "You're the taster. The Vane girl's child." Something moved across her face—grief, maybe, or its well-preserved corpse. "Your mother and I drank from the same court, did you know that? Before. She was the royal taster of Velthmar and I was the youngest daughter of a minor house, and we were both nobody, and we both knew it, and we made our nothings into weapons. She poisoned kings' enemies. I poisoned something larger."

"The kingdom," Wren said.

"The lie that was strangling it." The Regent's voice remained level, almost gentle, a lecturer's voice, a grandmother explaining arithmetic. "You've read Marrow's books, I assume. Pretty histories. Velthmar the golden, Velthmar the wise. Let me tell you what your mother's kings were. They kept memory-vaults, child. Under every city. Records of every debt, every crime, every name—so that nothing could ever be forgotten, which meant nothing could ever be forgiven, which meant every grudge in Velthmar was immortal. Families bound to blood-feuds a thousand years old because the vaults remembered and the vaults never lied. Do you understand? A kingdom where the past could never die is a kingdom where the future can never be born."

"So you erased it," Bram said hoarsely. "Every name. Every grave."

"I gave them mercy," the Regent said, and now something did crack in her voice, just slightly, a hairline fracture in porcelain. "I was a child of those vaults. I grew up knowing exactly what my grandfather had done, and his grandfather, and the weight of ten centuries of accounted sin pressing down on me before I could walk. When the coup came, the other lords wanted the royal line dead and the vaults emptied into their own keeping. I burned the vaults instead. All of them. Every record, every ledger, every name—including my own house's names, including my own crimes. I erased myself, Captain Odell. No one alive has paid more for this peace than I have."

"And the door?" Wren asked.

"The door remembers." The Regent finally touched the stone, flat-palmed, the way one touches a sleeping animal one means to slaughter. "The vaults I could burn. The door was older than the vaults—it holds the memory in itself, all of it, waiting. If it opens, every soul in Thornfell wakes remembering Velthmar. Every grudge. Every debt. Every descendant learning what their ancestor suffered and whose ancestor made them suffer. Ten million people waking with a thousand years of grief in their chests, all at once, forever." She turned back to face them, and her eyes were dry and clear and utterly certain. "I have seen what one remembered injustice does to a market square. I will not feed a nation a millennium of them in a single swallow. So yes, taster. I will kill the key. I have come to do it personally, because I owe your mother that much honesty, if nothing else."

The chamber was silent except for the lamps' hiss.

Wren found, to their surprise, that they weren't afraid. Or rather—they were afraid, thoroughly, in their marrow—but underneath the fear sat something harder and calmer, something that had been growing since a poisoned breakfast in the palace kitchens, since a date written in ash, since a palm read by firelight. Clarity. The Regent had unmasked herself, and what stood revealed was not a monster. It was worse. It was a reasonable woman. It was power explaining itself, and power always explained itself in the language of mercy.

"You're wrong," Wren said.

"Am I?"

"About what remembering does." Wren stepped forward, away from the plinth, away from Bram's grabbing hand. "You tasted the same fear your whole life that I did—that the truth is poison. But I'm a taster. I know poison, better than anyone alive, and truth isn't it. Truth is medicine that tastes like poison. The dose is the difference. And you—" their voice shook, and they let it, "—you never trusted your people to survive their own history. That's not mercy. That's contempt wearing mercy's clothes."

Something flickered in the Regent's ordinary brown eyes. Doubt, perhaps. Or the memory of doubt, buried as deep as her own name.

"It doesn't matter what either of us believes," she said. "Draw steel or kneel. Those are the choices."

"No," said a small voice.

Pim stepped out of the shadows by the eastern wall—Pim, who hadn't spoken in six years, who spoke in sketches and shrugs—and stood between the Regent and the door with a cartographer's compass clutched in one fist like a dagger.

"Pim, no—" Bram started.

"There's a third choice," Pim said. Their voice was rust and river-stones, unused and unmistakable. "There always was. You just never let anyone take it."

And behind them, with a sound like a held breath released after a hundred years, the great door began—very softly—to glow.

The Regent spun toward it. Her guards raised their weapons. Sefa was shouting words Wren didn't know, and the glow was spreading along the carved seams of the stone like dawn along a horizon, and Wren's hand—the key, the forgotten child, the dish the prophecy named—was already moving toward the light.

Page nine, Rook had said. The counter-seal. She doesn't know.

The Regent drew a slender knife from beneath her gray cloak and started forward, and Wren ran for the door.
