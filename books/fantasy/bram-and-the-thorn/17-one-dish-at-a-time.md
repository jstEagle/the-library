## 17. One Dish at a Time

The chalice was gold, and it was lying.

Wren knew both facts with the same certainty they knew the weight of a spoon, the temperature of a lie told over soup. The cup sat on a folding table of black oak at the center of the door chamber, and it gleamed like something loved—polished by hands that had polished it for generations, perhaps; carried down from some vault in the Regent's palace where treasures waited out their usefulness in the dark. Around its rim ran an inscription in Old Velthmari script, and even without Ilse Marrow's tutoring Wren could read enough of it now to know what it said: *That which is sealed may be unmade. That which is unmade may be forgotten twice.*

It was not a drinking vessel. It was a weapon shaped like a courtesy.

Wren stood three paces from it, wrists bound before them with silk cord—the Regent's touch, that; iron would have bruised and the Regent did not bruise her instruments—and watched the inquisitors work around the table with the brisk reverence of priests dressing an altar. There were six of them left. Rook's defection had cost her more than men; it had cost her the comfortable fiction that her order moved as one body. These six kept glancing at the space where Rook should have been standing, the way a tongue worries a missing tooth.

The door filled the far wall of the chamber.

Wren had been trying not to look at it since the Regent's soldiers dragged them in, because looking at it hurt. It was smaller than prophecy had promised—no towering gate, no monument, just a slab of grey-green stone set into the mountain's living rock like a keystone in a spine, maybe nine feet high, carved all over with the same dense script as the chalice's rim. And threaded through every line of the carving ran veins of something that was not metal and not light but both: a slow amber shimmer, like honey held up to a candle, moving so gradually that watching it made Wren feel the passage of years.

Behind that stone lay streets. Songs. Ten thousand sleeping memories, and a kingdom that had never actually died, only been asked—forced—to forget itself.

*Hold on,* Wren thought at it, absurdly, the way you'd speak to a person through a wall. *I'm working on it.*

"You're staring," said the Regent.

She had not raised her voice. She never needed to. Vessarine Aldemar, Regent of Thornmark by conquest dressed up as guardianship, stood at the edge of the ritual circle in travelling leathers and a plain steel circlet, and she looked at Wren the way Wren had spent thirty years making sure no one ever looked at them: fully, cataloguing, missing nothing. Up close she was smaller than her statues and her wanted-notices suggested, fine-boned, with her greying hair braided back in the old Velthmari style—which was its own confession, if anyone in this room had the wit or freedom to notice it. Wren noticed. Noticing was the whole of their craft.

"Forgive me, Your Excellency," Wren said. Their voice came out level. Thirty years of tasting for tyrants taught you how to make your voice a flat surface. "I've never seen a chalice that size used for anything but showing off."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. Almost amusement. She crossed to the table and laid two fingers against the chalice's stem, proprietary, gentle.

"Do you know what's in it?"

Wren considered lying and discarded it. Lies were for people who still had something to protect. "Given the company? Something that eats memory. Something distilled from the purge records, maybe, or whatever your alchemists scraped off the archive fires. Enough of it to burn the door's seal out of the stone permanently. To make sure no one could ever open it—not even by accident, not even in a hundred years when everyone who remembered why it mattered was dead."

The Regent studied them. "Ilse Marrow's student after all."

"She'd be flattered you think so. She says I read too slowly."

"And yet you read correctly." The Regent turned back to the door, and her voice dropped into something almost conversational, almost intimate, and that was worse than any interrogation voice could have been. "Do you know what I was, Wren Vane? Before the coup? I was a child of the Velthmari court. I danced at the Wintering Feast in the palace that is currently sleeping under your feet. I knew the words to songs that no one alive remembers." Her fingers tightened on the chalice stem until the knuckles whitened. "And I watched my family's name get struck from the rolls by a king who feared us, and I helped strike it deeper. Do you understand? I didn't destroy Velthmar because I hated it. I destroyed it because I loved it, and love, properly administered, is the most thorough poison there is."

Wren thought about that. They thought about their mother's ledger, and the careful hand that had recorded every dish, every dose, every sealing-word, and how that hand had trembled only once, on the last page. *Sealed to keep it from all factions,* the ledger said. *Including mine.*

"No," Wren said quietly. "You destroyed it because it frightened you. Love doesn't erase songs. Fear does."

The Regent's head turned, slow as a drawbridge. For one naked moment Wren saw what lived underneath the steel and the certainty: a woman who had spent forty years being right at the cost of everything, and who could no longer afford to be wrong. Then it was gone, folded away, and she smiled the smile she must have practiced in mirrors while her kingdom burned.

"It doesn't matter what you believe. In an hour none of us will remember believing anything." She gestured, and two inquisitors stepped forward with a basin of water and a bundle of dried herbs—hyssop, Wren noted automatically, and wormwood, and something darker beneath them whose smell raised the hairs on their arms. Ritual preparations. The destruction rite required a libation poured at the threshold, and then the chalice shattered against the stone, and the compound inside would do the rest. Ilse had described the theory once, bitterly, from a burned fragment of the purge protocols: *unmaking requires a witness, and the witness must drink.*

"The witness must drink," the Regent said now, echoing the protocol as though she'd plucked the thought from Wren's skull, and turned to look at them with terrible gentleness. "That's you, taster. You'll pour. You'll drink. And then you'll break the cup, and the door will forget itself forever, and so will you. A mercy, really. You've been nothing but a wound since the day you learned your own name."

* * *

They gave Wren an hour alone with the chalice.

This was the mistake. Wren understood it was a mistake even as it was being made—the Regent posting a single guard at the chamber's outer door and withdrawing to coordinate her remaining forces against the sounds of Bram's crew still holding the upper galleries—but understanding someone else's mistake did not help unless you could act on it, and Wren's wrists were bound, and the guard was armed, and the chalice sat on its table like a dare.

So they did what they had always done. They paid attention.

Thirty years in kitchens and courts had taught Wren that every poison had a signature, and every antidote a counter-signature, and that the space between them was where a taster lived. The compound in the chalice smelled, when Wren leaned close under the guard's bored watch, of burnt rosemary and cold iron and something older—a mineral reek, like the air in a tomb. Memory-eater. The purge alchemists' masterpiece. One sip would hollow a mind down to the walls.

But it had a signature, and signatures could be forged.

Wren could not reach the chalice. That was fine. Wren did not need to reach the chalice.

They needed the basin.

The ritual basin sat at the threshold of the door, already filled with spring water hauled up from the deep wells by the inquisitors' laborers. Into it the herbs would go, and the first libation from the chalice, and the mingled waters would be drunk by the witness before the shattering. Wren looked at the basin, and at the guard, and at the bound cord on their wrists, and began, very softly, to hum.

It was an old smuggler's tune from the Thornfell warrens, a counting song children used for keeping watch shifts, and Bram had sung it badly around a campfire eleven nights ago while Sefa threw pinecones at him. The guard's head lifted. He was young, this one—Rook's order had eaten its own replacements during the defections, and the Regent was scraping the barrel of her inquisition—and he had the particular restlessness of a soldier posted somewhere terrifying, guarding something he didn't understand, listening to sounds in the dark he couldn't name. Sounds from the upper galleries, where fighting still echoed. Sounds behind the door, where ten thousand dreams pressed against stone.

"That song," he said. His voice cracked on the second word. "Where—"

"My mother sang it," Wren said. Which was even true; the ledger's margins were full of such tunes, copied in a homesick hand. "Velthmari. You might know it. Everyone here might know it, soon."

"I don't know any—" He stopped. Swallowed. Looked at the door, and Wren watched the exact moment the amber shimmer pulsed and the young man felt it, felt something stir in his chest that had no name in the Regent's kingdom, and his face went through confusion and fear and arrived at longing. "What's behind there?"

"A country," Wren said.

He stared a long time. Then, because he was young and frightened and the mountain itself was whispering through the stone, he did the thing Wren had counted on: he crossed the chamber, drawn toward the door like a moth, and put out one tentative hand—

—and Wren moved.

Bound wrists or no, thirty years of kitchen work lived in their body: they swept the herb bundle from the altar shelf with their cuffed hands, dropped it into the basin, and then—this was the craft of it, the whole inherited art compressed into four heartbeats—they bit open the knot of a small oilcloth packet sewn into their sleeve lining, the one Sefa had given them weeks ago with a grin and the instruction *for emergencies, and also for fun*, and shook its contents into the water.

Salt. Iron filings. And powdered moonsbane root, which tasted of nothing at all and did exactly one thing: it turned any liquid it touched into a mirror of whatever had last been added to it. A mimic-poison. A taster's forgery tool. Whatever went into the basin next would take on moonsbane's properties—and moonsbane's properties, in sufficient concentration, were simple. Nausea. Blindness. Total collapse of certainty, in the body and, if the alchemical texts Ilse had smuggled Wren were honest, briefly in the self.

Not death. Wren had promised themselves that much, somewhere in the dark hours of captivity. They had poisoned a garrison's stores to sicken, not kill, and they would hold to that line even now, even cornered, because the difference between them and the Regent was precisely that difference, measured in pinches and hours.

"What are you doing?" The guard spun from the door.

"Purifying the water," Wren said pleasantly. "Ritual hygiene. Your mistress would be furious if the rite failed on a technicality."

He hesitated—because the alternative was admitting he'd abandoned his post for a lullaby and a question—and in his hesitation, boots sounded in the corridor. The Regent returning. The guard snapped back to his station, and Wren stepped away from the basin with their heart hammering a smuggler's rhythm against their ribs, and their face arranged into the flat, obedient blankness that had kept them alive through a thousand dangerous meals.

Let her come. Let her pour.

The Regent entered with two of her remaining inquisitors and paused, sensing—perhaps—the changed air, the way a hunter senses a shifted wind. Her eyes went from Wren to the basin to the door, narrowing.

"Is there a problem?"

"None, Your Excellency," Wren said. "We're ready."

* * *

The rite began at the turn of the hour, when the amber light in the door's veins dimmed to embers—as though the mountain itself held its breath.

The Regent lifted the chalice. She spoke the Old Velthmari words in a voice worn smooth with rehearsal, forty years of rehearsing, and Wren stood at the threshold with the ladle in their bound hands, playing the part of the witness to perfection, bowing their head at the proper beats, murmuring the responses Ilse had drilled into them. The first libation went into the basin. The water took it, and the moonsbane took the water, and the surface of the basin swirled a faint, telltale silver that only a trained eye would catch.

The Regent's eye was trained on grander things.

"Now," she said. "Drink, witness."

Wren raised the ladle to their lips.

And stopped—because this was the moment, the hinge of the whole long recipe, and they let it hang for one breath, two, watching the Regent's certainty stand there in her face like a loaded crossbow. She believed. She believed in the rite, in the compound, in her forty years of arithmetic, and belief was the one flavor she had never learned to test for.

Wren drank.

The moonsbane hit like winter. Cold flooded down their throat and into their skull, and the chamber tilted, and for one appalling instant Wren thought they had miscalculated—thought the diluted dose meant for the rite's mechanics was going to take them instead—and then the training caught them, the lifetime of microdoses and immunities bred into their blood by a mother's design, and the cold became merely terrible rather than fatal. Their knees buckled theatrically. They went down against the threshold, gasping, convulsing—selling it, selling every spasm, because a good poisoning always needed a performance to be believed.

"Witness!" The Regent dropped to one knee beside them, and there—it was there, just for a heartbeat, under the command and the ceremony: doubt. The first crack. Because the compound was supposed to erase gently, cleanly, and this looked like agony. "What did you—"

"Your Excellency," one of the inquisitors said sharply. "The rite. While she's down—the door—"

"Yes. Yes." The Regent rose. Doubt folded away again, buried under momentum, because she had come too far and sacrificed too much to stop at the threshold of finishing. She lifted the chalice in both hands, turned to the door, and spoke the final words of the rite, the ones that ended kingdoms:

"*That which is sealed may be unmade—*"

She drank.

Wren, sprawled at the threshold, watched her throat move, and felt the strangest grief and the strangest triumph tangled together in their chest, because the chalice had never been emptied into the basin—only a ladleful, only the libation—and the rest of the compound, undiluted, unmoonsbane-touched, pure memory-death, had gone down the throat of the woman who built it.

For a moment, nothing. The Regent lowered the chalice. She frowned, as if at a wrong note in a familiar song.

Then she reached for the door—and stopped.

Her hand hung in the air. Her brow furrowed, deeply, terribly, like a woman reading a letter in a language she had spoken all her life and finding the letters rearranged. "What—" she said. "What was—I was going to—" She looked at her own hand as though it belonged to a stranger. "Why am I holding this?"

"Your Excellency?" An inquisitor stepped forward.

"I was—" She turned in a slow circle, and the certainty was draining out of her face like wine from a cracked cup, and what rushed in behind it was worse than fear: it was emptiness, vast and howling, forty years of purpose evaporating in the space of a swallow. "There was a kingdom," she whispered. "There was a kingdom and I—" Her gaze snagged on the amber veins in the stone, and something in her lunged toward them, grasping, a drowning woman reaching for a shore she herself had bombed. "I danced there," she said. "I danced at the Wintering Feast. Why do I know that? Who put that in me?"

"You did," Wren said from the floor. They pushed themselves up on shaking arms, the moonsbane receding in waves, leaving them wrung out and clear-headed and colder than the poison had. "You put it there yourself, Your Excellency. Every year of it. You just spent forty years pouring the rest of the cup down your own people's throats. Tonight you finally tasted it."

The Regent looked at Wren. And Wren watched the last thing happen, the thing they had not planned for and could not have planned: the great architect of the erasure, hollowed of her mission, seized on the single thread remaining in her—the memory of dancing, the memory of the kingdom, the one thing the chalice could not take because it had never been written in any record she burned. It lived only in her body. In her bones. In the muscle-memory of a child turning under festival lanterns.

"I want to hear the songs again," Vessarine Aldemar said, in a small voice, in the voice of a girl of eight in a palace that wasn't rubble yet. "I remember the beginning of one. I can't find the end of it."

The chalice slipped from her fingers.

It struck the stone floor and shattered, and the compound within flashed and hissed and was gone—spent on flagstone, wasted on nothing, the grand weapon of a grand cruelty dying the death of spilled wine.

In the silence that followed, the door began to glow.

Not the slow amber pulse of before. This was faster, brighter, the veins filling like arteries, like a heart remembering its business, and Wren understood with a jolt that the door had heard everything—that it had felt its would-be destroyer falter, had tasted the truth in the room the way Wren tasted truth in a dish, and that it was responding to something. To the witness who had drunk and survived. To the seal straining against a century of silence.

To the sound, suddenly, of running feet in the corridor—Bram's voice, hoarse and triumphant and getting closer, yelling something about tunnels and Pim and *hold the door, Wren, whatever happens, hold the—*

Wren got to their feet. The moonsbane's last cold washed out of them, and what remained was steady. They wiped their mouth with the back of their bound hands, and looked at the glowing door, and at the Regent weeping silently on the floor of her own ruin among the shards of her golden lie, and thought: *One dish at a time. That's all justice ever is. One dish at a time, served by whoever's willing to stand at the table.*

Bram burst through the doorway with Sefa's knife-flash and Yara's ghost at his shoulder and Pim's map clutched in his fist, skidded to a stop at the sight of the blazing door, and stared.

"Well," he managed. "Don't open it without me."

Wren smiled—bloodied, poisoned, bound, and for the first time in their life entirely, dangerously visible—and reached for the stone.
