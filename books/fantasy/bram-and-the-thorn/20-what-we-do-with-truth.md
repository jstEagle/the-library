## 20. What We Do With Truth

The morning after the world woke, the vale smelled of rain and woodsmoke and something older underneath—stone that had been holding its breath for a hundred years.

Wren stood on the steps of the door chamber with a cup of barley tea they'd made themselves, watching the light come down into the hidden capital. It came differently here than in the Regent's city. In the lowlands, dawn arrived like an announcement, gilded and official. Here it filtered through the great crack in Thornfell's roof where the mountain had split to let the memory out, and it fell in long pale columns across streets that had not seen weather since Wren's grandmother was a rumor. The sleeping city of Velthmar lay open now—not awake, exactly, but no longer sealed. Its windows stood dark and clean. Its fountains ran again, fed by springs that had never stopped, and the sound of that water was the first honest thing Wren had heard in weeks.

Behind them, the door stood ajar. Just ajar. Wren had closed it as far as it would go without latching, because some part of them could not yet bear to shut it entirely—not after Yara, not after Pim's voice cracking through the collapsing tunnels like a bell rung for the first time in years. The seam of it glowed faintly still, gold along the carved names, patient as banked coals.

"You should eat," Bram said, coming up beside them. He looked terrible. He looked wonderful. There was soot in his hair and a bandage wrapped around his forearm where shrapnel from Corvess's second assault had caught him, and he was carrying two bowls of something that steamed and smelled of salt pork and wild onions, which meant Sefa was already cooking for forty people, because Sefa cooked for forty people when she was grieving or frightened or happy, and this morning she was all three.

"I am eating," Wren said, lifting the cup.

"Tea is not food. I've tasted it. It's a rumor of food." He pressed a bowl into their hands anyway, and they took it, because refusing Bram Odell's food had never once worked and because their hands were shaking and needed something to hold.

They ate in silence for a while. Below them, in the great square where Corvess's rebels had made their camp and then their siege and then their rout, people were moving about in the early light—and these were not rebels, not soldiers, but ordinary folk who had climbed down into the vale overnight. Farmers from the high terraces. Miners from the eastern galleries. A whole village's worth of people who had woken at dawn yesterday remembering a country that did not exist, and walked into the mountains to see it with their own eyes.

An old woman stood at the edge of the fountain, dipping her hand in the water over and over, weeping without any noise at all. A man knelt in the street running his palm along paving stones as though reading letters written there. Children were already climbing the dry fountain's rim, laughing, because children did not need to be told what a city was for.

"They're coming in hundreds," Wren said quietly. "By tonight it'll be thousands."

"Mm." Bram chewed. "And every one of them woke up yesterday knowing what was done to them. That's a lot of grief arriving all at once, love. Grief with nowhere to put itself."

"That's why I'm standing here instead of sleeping."

"You haven't slept."

"No," Wren agreed. "I haven't."

* * *

Corvess made her claim at midday, and she made it beautifully, because General Corvess did nothing that wasn't beautiful.

She had been taken in the rout—her soldiers, waking mid-siege with a century of stolen history pouring through their skulls, had mostly dropped their weapons where they stood. Some had sat down in the rubble and wept. Some had turned their blades on their officers. Corvess herself had been found kneeling before the door, not fighting, her hands empty, her face wet, saying nothing. They had bound her gently, because by then everyone understood that gentle and dangerous were not opposites.

Now she stood in the square before perhaps three thousand people, unbound—Rook had insisted on that, and no one had argued—and she spoke without amplification, in the carrying voice of a woman who had commanded armies in caves.

"I will not insult you with apology," she said. "I wanted the door opened. So did you. What differs between us is only what we meant to do afterward—and I say to you now: I am Velthmari blood. My line goes back to the third king. I have spent my whole life keeping the old ways alive in cellars and whispered songs so that this day would find someone to return to. If any soul here has earned the right to speak for Velthmar, it is I."

It was a good speech. Wren had to give her that. It had the shape of truth around its edges—the cellar songs were real; Wren's mother's ledger had mentioned the whisper-networks, the hidden midwives of memory. And Wren watched it work, watched faces in the crowd soften, watched the old woman at the fountain lift her head, hungry for someone to lead her out of the enormity of what she now remembered.

Then a miner near the front said, loudly enough to carry: "You starved us for eleven days to get here."

The square went quiet in a way that had teeth in it.

"You burned our stores," someone else called—a woman with a child on her hip, one of the rebel camp's own laborers, conscripted during the siege. "You put my boy in your supply line and told him if he ran, his mother would answer for it. I remember that now. I remember it twice—once as yesterday and once as everything I've ever known about you."

"And your siege killed a soldier defending this door," Bram said, stepping forward before Wren could stop him, his voice stripped of all its usual music. "Yara Vess. She held the tunnel alone so a mute boy and a taster could live. You'll not stand on the bones of my family and crown yourself."

"She died for the kingdom," Corvess began—

"She died because you brought an army to a door that never needed one," Wren said.

Their own voice surprised them. It came out level and cold and very clear, the way their voice went when they tasted something wrong. Three thousand faces turned. Wren descended the steps slowly, bowl still in hand, because they refused to perform this—they would not make a ceremony of it. They simply walked until they stood ten paces from Corvess, close enough to see the calculation running behind her eyes, the constant re-dealing of the deck.

"You want to know what differs between us?" Wren said. "Nothing, at the root. You wanted to own the door. The Regent wanted to own its absence. The prophecy wanted to own me. Everyone in this story reached for the same handle." They turned to the crowd. "But here's what I know, being nobody from nowhere: kingdoms don't come back through crowns. My mother sealed this door against every faction—including the loyal ones. Especially the loyal ones. She knew what I learned last night watching you fight for it: that the hunger to possess a thing of this size looks identical whether it wears black or gold."

"The people need leadership—" Corvess started.

"The people need bread," Wren said. "The people need the granaries inventoried and the wells tested and the dead buried and someone keeping honest records. The people need what every people have always needed, and none of it requires your bloodline." They paused. "You kept the old ways alive. That was real, and no one here will take it from you. But keeping a song is not the same as owning the singer."

For a moment Corvess's composure flickered, and beneath it Wren saw something almost young—almost the girl who had learned forbidden lullabies in cellars, waiting her whole life for a resurrection she intended to command. Then it hardened again.

"And who are you," Corvess said softly, "to judge? The forgotten child. The key. The prophesied one. You think you weren't reaching for the same handle?"

"Yes," Wren said. "That's exactly how I know its weight."

Silence. Then the woman with the child on her hip stepped forward and stood beside Wren. Then the miner. Then another, and another—not cheering, not chanting, just standing, a slow accumulation of bodies like sediment settling out of troubled water. Nobody crowned anyone. Nobody knelt. They just stood, in a loose and unglorious crowd, facing a general who had run out of army.

Corvess looked at them for a long moment. Then she laughed once, short and bitter, and it cracked something in the sound.

"My grandmother sang me those songs," she said, quieter now, only to Wren. "Every night. She said someday we'd get our country back." Her jaw worked. "I don't know how to want anything else."

"I know," Wren said. And found, to their own surprise, that they meant it kindly. "Neither do half the people here. That's what the next fifty years are for."

They left her standing in the square. Not imprisoned—there was, as yet, no agreement on what to do with anyone—but not followed, either. The crowd flowed around her like water around a stone, and stone was, Wren thought, exactly what she would become if she couldn't learn a new way to be useful. Perhaps she would. People surprised you. Rook had.

* * *

Which was the other thing waiting for them.

Rook had built the jury himself. That was the detail Wren kept turning over later, the way you turn a stone in your pocket: he had not waited to be seized, had not bargained, had not fled east over the passes where no one would ever have found him. Instead he had spent the morning walking the vale asking strangers—actual strangers, miners and farmers and two of Corvess's captured soldiers—to sit in judgment on him. Twelve of them had agreed, mostly, Wren suspected, because they wanted to look at him.

He stood before them in the archive hall of the sleeping palace, on a floor of green marble, beneath shelves of records that had survived the purge because they had been sealed inside the mountain alongside everything else. Ilse Marrow stood among the onlookers, freed only two days past, thin as a winter branch, her hands still ink-stained. When Wren entered, she touched their arm once, briefly, and said nothing. Ilse had developed a whole vocabulary of touches since the prison. This one meant: *I'm here. Watch what courage looks like.*

"I burned the archives of Velthmar," Rook said. No preamble. His voice filled the hall the way it always did, flat and precise, a blade laid on a table. "Eleven thousand volumes. Deeds, genealogies, poems, tax rolls, recipes, letters between sisters. I was twenty-two years old. I was told it was sanitation—the removal of a contagion of false memory that threatened the realm. For nineteen years I believed it, because believing was easier than opening one of the crates I personally carried to the fires and reading what was inside."

One of the jurors, an old man with miner's scars on his forearms, said: "Why didn't you?"

"Because the first crate I ever read was a schoolbook," Rook said. "A child's primer. And in the margin, in pencil, some child had drawn a picture of her teacher. I burned a child's drawing of her teacher, and I told myself it was sanitation." His hands hung at his sides, perfectly still. Wren recognized stillness like that; it was what their body did when they were swallowing poison. "Since then I have served the regime that ordered it. I hunted scholars. I hunted the crew that stands behind you now. I nearly killed the person who opened the door you all walked through yesterday. I offer no defense. I ask for no absolution. I ask to be judged by the remembering, because the forgetting is what made me possible."

The silence afterward was enormous.

Wren had expected rage. Expected demands for execution—had perhaps, in their darkest hours, wanted them themselves. But the jurors looked at each other, and what passed between them was slower and heavier than rage. These were people who had woken up yesterday owning a hundred years of grief they hadn't known was theirs. They knew precisely what forgetting cost. They were not going to pretend it was simple.

"We remember what you burned," the scarred miner said finally. "All of us, now. We can't unread it either." He looked around at his fellow jurors. "So here's what I say. Death is easy. Death is the regime's currency—it spent lives like copper. We're not spending ours the same way." He turned back to Rook. "You'll serve the restoration. Every record you remember, every name, every order you carried out—you'll testify it all, publicly, forever, and you'll rebuild what you burned. You'll spend the rest of your life giving back what you took. And if we ever learn you withheld one page—one—you'll wish we'd chosen the other road."

Rook bowed his head. "Agreed."

"That's mercy," said a woman near the end of the row, and her voice shook. "Don't mistake it for forgiveness. Forgiveness comes later, if it comes."

"It does come later," Ilse Marrow said from the edge of the hall, unexpectedly. Every head turned. "I lost my family to men like him. I have imagined his death more times than I can count." She looked at Rook, and her eyes were dry and terribly steady. "And this morning I watched him carry water for the cooking fires like a common laborer, and I thought: there is a version of justice that gives me back nothing, and a version that might give us all back something. I choose the one that might." She lifted her chin. "I'll help build the archive. He'll fetch and carry for it. That's my price, and I'll be checking his work."

Something moved through Rook's face then that Wren had never seen on it—something that might have been the beginning of relief, or the beginning of grief, or the place where the two shared a border. "Yes," he said. "Good. Check everything."

* * *

Evening came down into the vale like honey poured slow. Fires were lit in the square—honest fires, for cooking, for warmth, for people who had no roofs yet but had, as of yesterday, a country. Sefa presided over the largest of them, armed with a ladle, feeding anyone who came near, and Pim sat beside her sketching the crowd on scraps of salvaged paper: fast little portraits, faces rendered with the fierce attention of someone making up for years of silence. He had spoken four sentences today. Each one had landed like a coin dropped in a quiet room.

Wren found Bram at the fountain, sitting on the rim with his boots off and his feet in the water like a boy, watching the firelight move on the surface.

"They're talking about a council," Wren said, sitting beside him. "Not a king. A council of twelve, elected from the villages and the vale both. They asked if I'd sit on it."

"What did you say?"

"I said I'd taste their water and audit their grain stores, and they could find twelve better talkers."

Bram snorted. "That'll hold them off for a month."

"A week," Wren admitted.

The fountain murmured. Across the square, someone had begun to sing—an old song, one of the cellar songs, except now it wasn't contraband, and people were picking it up verse by verse as they remembered it, hesitantly at first and then all together, hundreds of voices finding a melody they'd each carried alone. The old woman from the morning was singing with her whole body, eyes shut, tears running freely into her smile.

"It's going to be hard," Wren said. "This part. Harder than the tunnels. There's no map for it. No prophecy even—the book ended at the door."

"Good," Bram said.

Wren looked at him. "Good?"

"Prophecies are just someone else's plan with better handwriting." He bumped their shoulder with his. "You spent your whole life being nobody on purpose, and the world kept trying to script you. Well. The script's burnt. Whatever happens next, happens next because we choose it. That's not a flaw in the story, love. That's the first page that's actually ours."

Wren wanted to answer. They got as far as drawing breath—

And that was when the runner arrived, up the switchback trail from the lowlands at a dead sprint, mud to her knees, one shoe missing, a scrap of paper crushed in her fist. She skidded to a stop before them, gasping, and thrust it at Wren because Wren was, apparently, the person things got thrust at now.

Wren unfolded it. The seal on the wax was the Regency's—but broken, torn, stamped over with a hasty mark Wren didn't recognize. The handwriting inside was Ilse's, rushed, the ink blotched:

*Word reached the lowland garrisons before we could stop it. Half have stood down. The other half have declared the memory a plague, sealed their gates, and named the Regent's heir—her nephew, Cassian Vael, whom we thought a nonentity—as rightful sovereign of the "true" kingdom. He has proclaimed the Vale a source of contamination. He is marching on Thornfell with four thousand men who believe they are defending the world from a lie.*

Wren read it twice. Then, because some habits were deeper than prophecy, they handed the letter to Bram and said:

"Somebody wake Rook. And somebody tell the council." They looked up at the mountain, at the thin ribbon of smoke rising where the pass met the sky, and felt the old familiar steadiness settle over them—the taster's calm, the calm of a person who has already decided what to do with the poison in the cup.

"The truth got us in the door," they said. "Now we find out if it can hold a wall."
