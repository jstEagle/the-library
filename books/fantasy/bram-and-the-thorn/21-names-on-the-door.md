## 21. Names on the Door

They buried Yara where the vale opened to the morning sun.

Wren had chosen the spot without thinking about it, and only afterward understood why: it was the first place in Velthmar where the light came down clean and unbroken, spilling through a seam in the mountain's crown like something poured. The grass there grew pale gold, untouched for a century by any boot or cartwheel, and it smelled the way bread smells just before the crust cracks—warm and patient and full of promise. It was the kind of ground a soldier would have liked, Wren thought. Ground that had been waiting.

There had been no shroud to spare. Sefa had offered her cloak; Bram had offered his coat, the good one with the silver buttons he'd stolen off a customs officer three years ago and never stopped bragging about. In the end they used both, because Yara had deserved better than either of them alone, and no one said this out loud but everyone arranged the folds as if it were law.

Pim dug the grave. No one asked him to; he simply took up the spare entrenching tool from the rebel stores and worked until the hole was deep enough, and then kept working, making it deeper than it needed to be, and Bram put a hand on his shoulder and Pim shook it off and kept digging anyway. Some grief needed somewhere to go. Wren recognized the shape of it. They had spent their whole life learning what to do with feelings too large for their container: you gave them labor. You let them pass through your hands as work.

The Regent's soldiers had been marched down out of the vale two days ago, disarmed and dazed, weeping in clusters like children at a funeral they hadn't known they were attending. Most of them remembered now—the purge, the coup, the names struck from the rolls—and some of them had knelt in the mud of the road and vomited when the memory arrived all at once, the way food arrives all at once when you've eaten too fast. Wren had watched them with something that wasn't quite pity and wasn't quite satisfaction. It was the feeling of a dish finally plated after too long in the kitchen. Whatever came of the tasting, at least it was served.

The rebels who remained had not been permitted near Yara's grave. Corvess herself had tried—had come walking across the vale on the second morning with her hands open and her voice pitched low and reasonable, the way she did everything, saying that she wished to honor the fallen, that the dead belonged to all Velthmar now. Bram had stood in her path with his arms crossed and said nothing at all, which from Bram was louder than shouting, and Corvess had looked past his shoulder at the fresh-turned earth and turned around. There would be trials. There would be questions. General Amara Corvess, hero of the resistance, architect of the siege, would answer for ordering the charge that killed Yara Odell—no, Wren corrected themselves, standing at the graveside. Not Yara Odell. Yara had never been an Odell. She'd been Yara Vess, once, of the Ninth Velthmari Lancers, and yesterday Pim had pressed a scrap of paper into Wren's hand with that name written on it in his careful cartographer's hand, and beneath it the words *she told me once. make sure it goes on stone.*

Stone. Yes. That was the other thing Wren had come to the vale to do.

But first: the burial.

Sefa sang. Her voice was thin and cracked and went flat in the middle of the second verse, and it was the most beautiful singing Wren had ever heard, because it made no attempt whatsoever to be beautiful. It was an old Thornfell road-song, one of the smuggler hymns that existed in no official book—a song about carrying burdens through the passes, about how the mountain keeps what you give it and gives back what you need. Half the words were probably invented. Sefa changed two verses on purpose, weaving Yara's name into places where the original song had said *the traveler*, so that by the end the mountain itself seemed to be keeping Yara, holding her, remembering her.

Bram spoke last. He stood at the head of the grave with his hat in his hands—he owned exactly one hat, and it had been rained on, burned near, and shot through the brim during the siege, and he held it anyway—and he looked at the assembled crew, at the handful of freed prisoners and defected soldiers who had asked to come, and he said:

"She pulled me out of the Wraithwater when I was nineteen. I'd been stabbed twice and I still owe the man who did it money." A rough sound moved through the listeners—not quite laughter, but adjacent to it, the way Yara herself had always been adjacent to solemnity. "She didn't ask my name. Just hauled me into the boat, called me 'fish-bait' for six months, and taught me how to hold a blade like it was an extension of my own bad temper. I have spent eleven years trying to be worth that rescue." His voice caught, and he let it catch, and Wren watched him decide not to hide it. "I never got there. But she said once—she was drunk, mind, so weigh it accordingly—she said the point was never arriving. The point was who's rowing beside you."

He set the ruined hat on the mound of earth. He stepped back.

They filled the grave together, everyone taking turns with the shovel so that no single pair of hands bore the whole weight, and when it was done Wren knelt and pressed their palm flat against the new-turned soil the way they had pressed it against the door, and whispered the sealing-words forward instead of backward—which Ilse had later explained was the traditional form, the words meant not to lock something away but to promise it would be found again.

*Rest, and be remembered. We are coming.*

* * *

Ilse Marrow came out of the Regent's prison blinking like something born underground.

Wren had gone for her personally—had insisted on it, over the objections of the provisional council that was already forming in the lowlands with the speed and self-importance of mold on bread. The council wanted process. Petitions, warrants, formal transfer orders stamped in triplicate. Wren wanted Ilse, and had learned from a lifetime in kitchens that if you waited for permission, the dish went cold.

So they had walked into the Spindlewood prison with nothing but their own name spoken clearly, and discovered that a name could be a key too, now. The prophecy had spent itself, but its echo lingered; every guard in the building knew who Wren Vane was, knew about the door, knew about the remembering, and half of them had families in the lowlands who had woken three days ago weeping over names they'd never been allowed to learn. One of the guards, a heavy woman with ink-stained fingers, had simply handed over the cell ledger and said, "Take whoever you're here for. Take whoever else wants to go. I'll report it as an escape."

The cell they found Ilse in was small, cold, and meticulously organized. Books—confiscated, but stacked in neat rows by subject rather than size—lined one wall. She had been writing on the backs of prison notices in a hand so small and precise it looked typeset. When the door opened she didn't look up right away. She finished her sentence first.

Then she raised her eyes, and Wren watched recognition arrive, and then disbelief, and then something that broke their heart: hope, carefully rationed, the hope of someone who had learned not to spend it.

"You're supposed to be dead," Ilse said. "Three separate prophecies indicated—"

"I'm a taster," Wren said. "I'm very hard to kill in small doses."

And Ilse Marrow, disgraced scholar, heir of a ruined family, keeper of a century of contraband memory, began to laugh—a dry, rusted sound, like a hinge opening after years—and laughed until she cried, and Wren sat down on the cell floor beside her and let her grip their hand hard enough to bruise, because some things needed to be held onto tightly before they could be believed.

Walking out through the prison yard, Ilse squinting against daylight she'd almost forgotten, she asked what had become of the archive. Wren told her the truth: the Regent's inquisitors had burned most of it during the evacuation. Ilse nodded slowly, unsurprised, and then said the thing Wren would carry for years:

"Burned books leave ash. Ash leaves traces. And traces—" she smiled, and it was the smile of a woman who intended to spend the rest of her life doing paperwork as an act of war, "—traces can be reconstructed. Every record Rook burned, I will rebuild. Every name, every date, every dish." She glanced sideways at Wren. "Every recipe."

"The recipes matter?" Wren asked.

"Wren." Ilse stopped walking. "You are a person who can read an entire regime in the contents of its pantry. Of course the recipes matter. History is written by whoever feeds it."

* * *

The door chamber had been cleaned, but not repaired.

That had been a decision, argued over and eventually settled by the provisional council's one wise member—an old mason named Grett who remembered, now, being a boy in Velthmar before the erasure, and who had stood in the chamber with his cap wrung in his hands and said: "Leave the scars. A kingdom that pretends it wasn't hurt will hurt again the same way." So the scorch marks stayed, and the crack where the Regent's ritual chalice had shattered against the threshold stayed, and the bloodstains—Yara's among them—were left dark in the stone, and Wren thought Grett understood something the council was still learning: that the door did not merely remember. It demanded witnesses.

Which brought Wren, on the seventh morning, to stand before it with a chisel, a mallet, and a list of names.

The wall beside the door was smooth granite, old and fine, and carving into it felt like blasphemy until Wren remembered that the wall itself was a lie of sorts—dressed stone laid over the living mountain, a facade. Truth could stand a little alteration. Truth, in fact, required it sometimes: required to be cut into, marked, made legible.

Pim had drawn the lettering. Of course he had. He'd produced a full design on his slate within an hour of being asked, each letter formed with the care he gave coastlines, and beneath the names he'd sketched small symbols: crossed lances for Yara, a coil of rope for the smugglers lost at the checkpoint, and—for the inquisitors who had died defending the door against their own order—a single quill, which had been Rook's suggestion, offered quietly, and accepted even more quietly.

Rook himself was not present. He was in the lowlands, awaiting trial, having insisted on surrendering to whatever jury the newly remembering people chose to convene. Before he left he had shaken Wren's hand—his grip careful, formal, a soldier's grip—and said, "I burned a kingdom's memory. Let me be remembered accurately, at least. Put me in the record however the record judges me. Just don't soften it."

Wren intended to honor that. But today the chisel was for the fallen of their own company.

Wren carved slowly. This was craft, and they respected craft: each letter cut clean, each stroke finished before the next began. Sweat gathered along their spine. Granite dust powdered their sleeves. Bram and Sefa and Pim stood behind them in silence, and occasionally one of them would lean in to check a spelling—Yara Vess, with the double-s; Torvin Kale, the smuggler who'd died at the pass; Merrin Dole, who had carried the maps through the flood-tunnel and drowned doing it—and Wren would wipe the stone and correct it and keep cutting.

When the last name was done, Wren stepped back, and the five of them looked at the wall together.

It was not beautiful, exactly. The letters were uneven; Wren was a taster, not a stonemason. But they were deep, cut to last, and above them Pim had carved—himself, taking the chisel without asking, his hands steady—one final line, in letters larger than the names:

**NONE FORGOTTEN. NONE UNNAMED.**

Below the names, in smaller characters, the date. And below the date, because Wren insisted, because it mattered, because a taster knows that provenance is everything:

*Carved by Wren Vane, child of Maren Vane, royal taster of Velthmar, who sealed this door so that it might one day be opened.*

"There," Bram said quietly. "Now the wall lies less."

"Now the wall tells the truth," Wren said. "All of it. Even the parts that cost us."

Sefa touched the stone, tracing Yara's name, and murmured something in her hedge-witch's tongue—too soft to catch, meant for the dead alone. Pim took out his slate and wrote: *she'd have hated the ceremony. she'd have loved the stone.* And Bram laughed, wet-eyed, and agreed, and for a while none of them moved, five people standing in front of a door that had remade a country, reading names into granite the way you'd read them into a family Bible—if the family were a smuggling crew, a hedge-witch, a mute mapmaker, a repentant inquisitor awaiting trial, and a food-taster who had once been nobody on purpose.

* * *

They camped that night in the high chamber above the door, in the room the old Velthmari records called the Hall of First Light, where dawn came through a shaft in the mountain and lit the floor in a slow golden sweep. The others slept. Wren sat by the embers of a small fire, turning a strip of dried apple over in their fingers, eating it the way they ate everything now: deliberately, gratefully, aware of taste as a privilege.

Bram couldn't sleep either. He never could, the first night after burying someone; Wren had learned that much about him. He settled onto the bench beside them without asking, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, far enough that it was deniable, which was the geography of everything between them.

"So," he said, after the fire had talked itself down to coals. "Prophecy's spent."

"It is."

"Door's open. Kingdom's awake. Regent's done, Corvess is caged, Rook's in line for a trial, Ilse is terrorizing the archives back into existence." He counted them off on his fingers. "The book named you, the date passed, the dish got served. By every measure that scholar ever cooked up, your part's finished."

"That's what it sounds like," Wren said.

"So what now?" He turned to look at them, firelight catching the edge of his face, and his voice did the thing it did when he was asking a real question disguised as an idle one. "You've spent your whole life being invisible, and then a month being the most important person alive, and now—" he gestured vaguely at the sleeping hall, the waking kingdom beyond it, the enormous unscripted future, "—now there's just... life. Enormous amounts of it. Unscheduled. What does Wren Vane do when nobody's written the next page?"

Wren considered the question honestly, because Bram deserved honesty and because they had genuinely been asking themselves the same thing since the night the memory flooded outward and the world changed shape.

"I don't know yet," they said. "That's the strange part. I've always known. Before the prophecy, I knew—I'd be invisible, I'd survive, I'd taste and swallow and live to tomorrow. During it, I knew—the date, the door, the dish. Every step was already written somewhere, even if I hadn't read it yet." They turned the apple strip over again. "This is the first time the page ahead of me is blank. And I keep waiting to feel terrified. But mostly I feel—" they searched for it, "—hungry."

"Hungry."

"Like sitting down at a table where I get to order for myself. For the first time." They looked at him. "What about you? Your debt to my mother's paid. The crew's free. You could take the roads south. Buy that boat you're always lying about owning."

Bram was quiet for a long moment. The coals ticked. Somewhere below them, faintly, the great city of Velthmar was stirring—bells, voices, the first carts of a market morning in a capital that had slept a hundred years under stone and woken to find itself loved.

"My mother," Bram said at last, "told me once that the mark of a good crew isn't whether they survive the job. It's whether anyone volunteers for the next one." He picked a splinter off the bench, flicked it into the fire. "I'm volunteering."

"For what?"

"Whatever comes next." He shrugged, elaborate and unconvincing, the shrug of a man hiding an entire heart behind a piece of stage business. "Somebody has to teach these Velthmari how to smuggle properly. Their trade routes are a disgrace. And somebody has to keep an eye on you, because you'll walk straight into danger holding a spoon and calling it diplomacy, and frankly the kingdom can't afford to lose its—" he stopped, recalibrated, "—its witness."

"Not its anything else?"

Bram looked at the fire. Then at them. And whatever he was going to say—the unnamed thing, the thing he'd been circling since a checkpoint in the snow—he tucked it away again, gently, like a coin saved rather than spent.

"Get some sleep, Vane," he said softly. "Blank pages are best faced rested."

But as Wren closed their eyes, they heard him stay where he was, keeping watch beside them, and they thought: *the script is finished. Everything from here, I write myself.*

And somewhere in the waking city below, a bell rang the hour, and the mountain—old, patient, generous—held all of them, and gave back what they needed.
