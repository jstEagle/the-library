## 22. The First Witness

The year turned the way seasons do in Thornfell—slowly, and then all at once—and on the morning of the anniversary, Wren Vane woke before dawn out of habit rather than necessity, lay listening to the rain on the slate roof, and realized they were happy.

It was such an unfamiliar discovery that Wren tested it the way they tested everything: carefully, with a small dose first, watching for symptoms. Happiness, it seemed, produced no numbness in the fingers, no bitterness under the tongue, no tightening behind the ribs where fear usually kept its lodgings. It simply sat there, warm as a stone left too near the fire, waiting to be believed.

Wren believed it. Eventually.

* * *

The chamber beneath the vale had become a place of pilgrimage, though Wren had fought that word from the start. Pilgrims came expecting incense and kneeling; what they found was a doorway of black stone veined with silver, flanked by two walls of names—Yara's among them now, carved deep in Pim's careful hand, beside the names of every soul the purge had erased. The people came, and they read, and some of them wept, and then they climbed back up the long stair into the daylight where the work waited.

Wren's work.

They had been offered titles. That was the season's great comedy, told and retold in the taverns of the waking kingdom: the orphan taster who refused a throne three times. Corvess had offered queenship at the door itself, and been refused. The provisional council had offered regency, when it became clear someone would have to hold the country's center while it learned its own name again. Wren had refused that too, gently, and watched relief move through the councilors like weather. Then the guilds had offered a chancellorship, the army a marshalcy, and one earnest delegation of farmers from the lowlands had arrived at the door with a petition to make Wren king outright, having apparently decided that gender was a detail kingship could survive without.

"No," Wren had told them all, in the same flat voice they'd once used to report a dish safe or unsafe. "I tasted for a tyrant for eleven years. I'm not going to be one."

What they wanted instead had taken longer to explain, and longer still for the council to accept, because it fit no office the old kingdoms had ever kept. But Ilse Marrow—freed from the Regent's prison thin as winter kindling and twice as flammable—had drafted the charter herself, and read it aloud in the council hall in a voice that made hardened officers look down at their boots:

*There shall be one Witness, keeper of the Door and of the Record, who holds no crown, commands no soldiers, and answers to no faction. The Witness shall taste every truth before it is served to the kingdom—the treaties, the histories, the pardons and the prosecutions alike—and shall say plainly whether it is wholesome. And when the Witness finds poison, the kingdom shall hear of it, whoever put it there.*

"Whoever put it there," Corvess had repeated bitterly, from her seat among the delegates she could no longer lead. She had tried, in those first months, to claim the restored Velthmar by right of blood, by right of prophecy, by right of everything except the consent of the people who now remembered exactly what she'd done at the pass, what her siege had cost, whose blood was on her hands. The awakened crowds hadn't shouted her down. They'd simply looked at her, with ten centuries of memory open behind their eyes, and she had understood that no throne built on a forgotten people could stand once the forgetting ended. "And who tastes the Witness?"

"The dead," Wren had said. "Every day. They're very thorough."

* * *

On the morning of the anniversary, Wren went down the long stair with a lamp and a ledger under one arm, as they did every morning, and found Bram Odell already there.

He was sitting on the third step from the bottom, which was his spot, though he'd deny owning a spot the way he denied owning anything—a smuggler's superstition he'd never managed to shed even after smuggling stopped being his trade. He had two tin cups balanced on his knee and steam rising off both, and when he saw Wren he held one up without a word, because after a year they had passed beyond the need for greetings the way old roads pass beyond the need for signs.

"You're up early," Wren said, taking the cup.

"I live here. You're describing my entire life as an aberration." Bram blew across his tea. "Pim wants you at the archive by midday. There's a shipment of recovered records coming up from the lowlands—three carts, Rook's escorting them personally—and Pim says the manifests don't match what's on them."

"Pim always says that."

"And Pim is always right." He shrugged. "Also Sefa sent a parcel. It's on your desk. Don't shake it."

"What is it?"

"If I told you, you'd shake it."

Wren drank the tea. It was over-steeped and slightly bitter, and it was the best thing Wren had tasted all year, which was saying something, because tasting had become the whole shape of the work. Every treaty clause went under Wren's tongue first—not literally anymore, though there were those who missed the theater of it—but every claim, every number, every comfortable lie dressed in official ink. Wren read the way they'd once sipped: a small dose first, patience, attention to the fingertips. Most documents were wholesome. Some were poisoned in small, deniable ways—a date nudged, a casualty rounded down, a name quietly absent from a list of the pardoned. Wren marked those in red and sent them back, and the kingdom had learned, slowly, to be grateful rather than annoyed.

It was strange, being thanked for suspicion. Wren was still deciding how it felt.

* * *

The door itself stood shut.

That had been the second great surprise of the year—that after everything, after the blood and the siege and Yara, after the memory had poured through Thornfell like spring melt and remade the world in a single terrible, glorious day—the door had closed again, softly, the way a book closes when it finishes telling its story. Beyond it, Wren knew, lay the sleeping city still: the streets, the songs, the ten thousand preserved minds of old Velthmar, resting now that their testimony had been given. Sefa said the seal reformed itself around a new condition. Not a forgotten child this time. A witness who stayed.

"It only opens for me," Wren had told the council, "and I'm not going in. Someone has to remain outside to remember what it cost."

Ilse had written that down too. Ilse wrote everything down now; it was her penance and her joy in equal measure, and the Record—the true one, the unburnable one, copied in triplicate and hidden in three places Pim had chosen and refused to disclose—grew fat under her hand.

Wren set a palm against the black stone, briefly, the way one touches a grave or a shoulder. Silver veins pulsed faintly under the touch, cool as river water.

"Not today," Wren murmured. "But thank you."

* * *

Midday brought the carts, and the mismatch, and the small crisis that Wren handled the way all the year's crises had been handled: with tea, ledgers, and an unnerving amount of patience.

Rook stood beside the third cart with his hands folded and his face arranged into official blankness, but Wren had learned to read him the way they'd once read the Regent's kitchen—by what was *not* there. No tension in the shoulders. No flicker toward the exits. Whatever was wrong with the manifest, Rook wasn't hiding it. He was waiting, with the particular stillness of a man who had surrendered himself to a jury of the newly remembering and been sentenced to exactly the work he deserved: hauling other people's buried crimes into daylight, cart by cart, for the rest of his natural life.

"Seventeen crates listed," Rook said. "Eighteen on the cart."

"Open it."

The eighteenth crate held parish registers from the lowland town of Ashenford—birth records, marriage records, and, tucked between them, a bundle of letters that made Rook's jaw tighten for the first time. Correspondence between a Velthmari magistrate and the Regent's own chancery, dated the month before the coup, arranging in polite bureaucratic prose for the disappearance of forty-one families. Not execution. Disappearance. New names, new towns, records altered so thoroughly that the families themselves, in most cases, had never known.

"They're alive," Rook said quietly. "Some of them. Their grandchildren are. Living under names that were issued to them like ration cards." He looked up. "The council will want to announce this. Restore the names. Make a festival of it."

Wren read the letters twice. Tasted them. Found, under the horror, something else—a fine grain of something not quite rotten but not clean either. Ambition, maybe. Or the simple human hunger to be seen doing good.

"They'll want to announce it *before* anyone asks the descendants," Wren said. "Before anyone gets asked whether they *want* the old name back. Some of those families have buried the old name so deep they'll grieve if it's dug up." They closed the ledger. "Send it back. Let Pim trace the living lines first. Let the council ask, not tell."

Rook studied them for a long moment. A year ago he had knelt in the mud of the pass and offered his sword and his shame together, and Wren had taken neither and accepted both. Now he simply nodded, once, the nod of a subordinate to a superior he had chosen freely—which was, Wren had come to believe, the only kind of authority worth the name.

"You'd have made a fine regent," he said, shouldering the crate.

"That," said Wren, "is exactly why I shouldn't."

* * *

Evening found the crew where the crew always gathered: the long kitchen above the archive, which had begun the year as a barracks mess and had become, without anyone deciding it, the beating heart of the household.

Sefa had arrived on the evening cart herself, smelling of woodsmoke and hedge-magic, and was presiding over a stew with the serene tyranny she brought to all her cooking. Pim sat at the corner table drawing maps that weren't maps—charts of the kingdom's remembering, neighborhoods shading from grey to gold as the Record spread—and looked up when Wren entered with an expression that, from Pim, constituted a full speech of welcome.

Bram was arguing with Sefa about salt, which was their longest-running war and their deepest form of affection.

"There," Sefa said, pointing her ladle at Wren like a verdict. "Tell him a taster knows salt."

"A taster knows *poison*," Wren said, hanging up their cloak. "Salt is a matter of opinion."

"Thank you—" began Bram triumphantly.

"—and his opinion is wrong."

Sefa cackled and gave Wren the largest bowl.

They ate the way they always ate now: crowded, loud, elbows overlapping, Pim's foot hooked around the leg of Wren's chair in the mute habit of a person who had once spoken words to save Wren's life in a collapsing tunnel and had never quite stopped anchoring afterward. Yara's empty chair stood at the head of the table. Nobody moved it. Nobody sat in it. Once a month Sefa filled a bowl for it anyway and set it outside the door for whatever walked the dark, and in the morning the bowl was always empty, and none of them ever discussed why, because some truths are better fed than explained.

"So," Bram said eventually, turning his cup in his hands. "Council session next week. They're going to offer you something again. Ilse let it slip. Something about a 'High Custodian of the National Memory.'"

"That sounds like a title invented by a committee that hates both words."

"It does. It truly does." He glanced up, and there was something underneath the humor, the way there always was with Bram, load-bearing and unnamed. "You'll refuse it?"

"I'll refuse it."

"Good." He exhaled. "Because if you took a palace, we'd have to visit you in it, and I've seen the palace kitchens. You'd starve out of principle within a month."

"Is that the only reason?"

Bram looked at his cup. Outside, the rain had started again, soft on the slate, and the fire popped, and for a moment Wren watched the man who had once claimed the mountain roads answered to him wrestle with a sentence the way he'd once wrestled with locked doors—skillfully, irreverently, and refusing to admit how much it mattered.

"No," he said finally. "It's not the only reason." He didn't elaborate. He never elaborated. But his knee pressed against Wren's under the table and stayed there, warm and deliberate, and Wren pressed back, and that was its own complete sentence—one they'd been writing all year in the margins of everything else, unhurried, unsigned, entirely theirs.

* * *

Later, when the others had gone to bed, Wren carried the lamp down one last time.

Not to the door tonight, but to the wall of names beside it. The lamp flame steadied, and the carven letters caught the light, row upon row upon row—the erased, the returned, and near the top, newer and deeper cut than all the rest: **YARA VESS**, and beneath her, in smaller letters, *she bought the hour*. Pim had insisted on the epitaph. Wren had insisted it be true, and it was; the whole kingdom stood in the hour Yara bought.

Wren touched the names the way they touched the door. Briefly. Gratefully.

A year ago, Wren Vane had been nobody on purpose—an orphan of the warrens, a mouth for other people's meals, a shadow trained to pass through rooms leaving no more mark than breath on glass. The prophecy had named them, the factions had hunted them, mother and regent and rebel general had each tried to make them an instrument, and in the end Wren had chosen the one thing none of them had planned: to stay. To stand outside the door and keep the record. To taste the truth of a nation, one document, one treaty, one hard morning at a time, and to say plainly what was wholesome and what was not.

An invisible person, finally seen—who had discovered that the thing they most wanted to do with sight was give it away, daily, to everyone else.

Wren lifted the lamp, checked the wick, and turned back toward the stairs and the lamplit kitchen and the family asleep above it, and paused.

Because the door was humming.

Faintly—so faintly that anyone else would have called it the mountain settling, the rain, the imagination of a tired mind. But Wren had spent a lifetime listening for the note under the flavor, the thing beneath the thing, and this was neither water nor stone. It was the sound the seal had made on the last night, just before it opened: a held breath. A page turning.

Silver light moved in the veins of the black stone, slow as a pulse, and quickened.

From far inside—deeper than the sleeping streets, deeper than the songs—came a sound Wren had heard only once before, on the day the kingdom woke, and had hoped never to hear again, because it meant the past was not finished asking things of the present.

Someone, on the far side of the door, was knocking.
