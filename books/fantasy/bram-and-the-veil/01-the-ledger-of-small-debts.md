## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The candle had burned down to its last finger's width when Mara Thornbury wrote the final figure of the evening: *nine hundred and forty-one coppers, tithe of the miller Aldous Vey, received in full.*

She set down the pen. Her wrist ached in that particular dull way it always did after six hours of copying — an ache she had learned to read like a clock, the way other people read church bells or hunger. Six hours meant the debt year had turned another degree. Four years, seven months, and some uncountable days remaining until the Thornbury account was settled and her name came off the temple rolls for good.

Nine hundred and forty-one coppers. She looked at the number a moment too long. It was such a small sum to have bought so much time from a life.

Outside the scriptorium window, the valley lay under its first real snow of the season. The temple of Saint Verity-of-the-Counted-Grace sat on its shelf of hillside above Lowmark town, and from here Mara could see the whole grey sprawl of rooftops smoking with hearth-fires, the frozen millrace glinting like a vein of tin, and beyond it all the pale walls of the valley itself — those sheer, mist-wrapped cliffs that the old texts called the Veil and everyone else simply called *the edges of the world*. Nothing crossed them. Nothing ever had. The valley was a bowl, and the world was whatever fit inside it, and Mara had spent twenty-six years learning not to want anything larger than that.

*Learning* was perhaps generous. She had spent twenty-six years practicing the wanting and failing at the not.

She rubbed ink from the side of her hand — it had dried in a crescent there, her own particular constellation, mapped fresh every day — and began stacking the finished pages in order. Tithes, tithes, tithes. Coppers from the miller, grain-sacks from the farmwives, a lamb from the shepherd Corren marked *received alive, bleating, in good health.* The temple kept accounts of everything because the temple believed, with a fervor the rest of the valley had mostly let go cold, that a thing unrecorded was a thing unloved by heaven. Saint Verity had been a scribe before she was a saint. They said she had counted every hair on the head of the dying god, so that nothing of him would be lost.

Mara sometimes thought that if she counted her own life carefully enough — every page, every hour, every copper — she might trick it into mattering.

The scriptorium was empty now. Brother Anselm had gone up to bed an hour ago, taking his cough and his disapproval with him; Sister Pelline had left earlier still, muttering about the cold in her joints. That left Mara alone with the shelves of ledgers rising around her like the walls of a paper canyon, forty years of the valley's small debts bound in calfskin and string. She knew every spine by touch. She had re-bound a third of them herself.

She should sleep. The bell would ring at dawn and there were harvest-ledgers waiting, three seasons behind, and Brother Anselm had already remarked — twice, in that gentle knife-edged way of his — that Sister Mara's hand had been growing *careless*. Careless. As though a woman who copied two thousand words a day without error could be careless. As though the word didn't mean what it actually meant: *watched, and found wanting, and reminded of it.*

Mara blew out the candle.

Darkness took the room except for the ember-glow of the banked stove and the blue-white wash of snowlight through the window. And in that half-darkness, standing among the ledgers with her hands still smelling of iron-gall ink, Mara allowed herself the one indulgence the temple never taxed: she imagined being elsewhere.

It was a practiced ritual, this. She did it the way pious women said their beads, in strict sequence, every night. First the pass through the mountains that appeared on no map but existed in three forbidden books she had read as a girl, before the elders caught her and added a year to her debt as correction. Then the sea — she had never seen the sea; no one in the valley had; but the pre-kingdom texts spoke of it as *the grey country that moves*, and she had built hers out of those four words the way a starving woman builds a feast out of a menu. Then a city where no one knew her name. Then a door, any door, that opened onto a room where the ledger of her life had never been written at all.

She got as far as the city tonight. She was just beginning on the door when someone knocked.

Three knocks. Soft, arrhythmic, wrong — not the confident double-rap of a temple functionary or the shy scrape of a townsman come about a debt. These knocks sounded like someone who had never knocked on a door before and was working it out from first principles.

Mara went very still.

Snowlight lay across the floor in a long pane. Through the high window she could see the courtyard below the scriptorium, silvered and unbroken — no footprints, no lantern, no soul. The gatehouse was locked at dusk. The ward-bell had sounded hours ago. Whoever stood outside the scriptorium door at this hour had either climbed the hill past the gatehouse without disturbing a single flake of snow, or—

Or they had been inside the grounds since before the snow began.

Another knock. Softer. Almost apologetic.

"Who's there?" Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "The scriptorium is closed until morning bell. Debts are collected at terce."

Silence. Then, from the other side of the door, a sound she had no category for: a thin, cracked, deliberate voice, speaking words she did not know and yet — this was the part that raised the hair along her arms — words that did not sound *foreign*. Foreign speech bounces off the ear like water off oil. This sank in. This landed somewhere beneath language, in the cellar where Mara kept the dead tongues she'd been punished for studying, and something down there stirred and turned over in its sleep.

"Brother Anselm?" she tried. No answer. "Sister Pelline?"

Nothing. Only the stove ticking as it cooled, and the wind worrying the shutters, and then — unmistakable, impossible — that voice again, patient, repeating itself exactly as before, syllable for syllable, as if repetition might accomplish what comprehension had not.

Every sensible instinct she owned told her to fetch Anselm. Wake the whole cloister, light lamps, open the door in company and daylight and numbers. That was what a careful woman did. That was what a woman with four years and seven months left on her debt did, a woman whose entire existence was structured around giving the temple no cause, none, not ever, to extend the account.

Mara lifted the bar.

Later — much later, in a place where names were currency and she would spend hers like a drunkard spends his last coin — she would try to remember why she had opened that door, and she would never find a clean reason. There was no reason. There was only the fact that she had spent twenty-six years counting things, and everything worth counting in her life fit on a shelf, and the knock on the door was the first unnumbered thing she had heard since childhood.

The door swung inward on cold air and snowlight.

The child stood in the courtyard doorway at the far end of the colonnade, which was wrong, because the child should have been standing at the scriptorium threshold, right where the knocking had come from. But there was no one at the threshold. There was only the small figure thirty feet away, at the outer arch, barefoot on the stones, snow gathering between its toes, wearing nothing but a shift of undyed wool that had been white once and was now the grey of old teeth.

A boy, Mara thought. Or a girl. Hard to say — six years old, perhaps seven, slight as a bundle of kindling, hair cropped close and dark against a face so pale it seemed lit from within by the snow. The eyes were the worst part. Not because they were strange — they were ordinary brown eyes, red-rimmed, exhausted — but because of how they looked at her. People looked *at* Mara. The abbess looked at her accounts. Anselm looked at her posture. The townsfolk looked at her habit. Everyone looked at the surface of her, the way you look at a wall to see if it will hold.

This child looked *through* her, the way you look through a window at weather coming.

"You can't be out here," Mara said. Her breath smoked. "The gates are locked. Where is your mother? Where—"

The child spoke.

It was the same phrase again — she understood that much now, recognized the shape of it, four or five syllables arranged with a strange formal gravity, like lines from a rite. The sound of it moved through the courtyard and seemed to make the snowlight flicker, which was nonsense, which was impossible, because this was the valley, and nothing happened in the valley. That was the whole covenant of the place, the founding bargain, the reason her grandmother had brought the family over the mountains three generations ago with nothing but a strongbox and a story: *in the Veil's shadow, no magic crosses.* The valley was the quietest ground in the world. Wonders did not grow here. That was its blessing and its whole identity, and Mara had built her patience, her grim arithmetic, her four-years-and-seven-months upon it.

And still the snowlight flickered. Or she blinked. One or the other.

"Come inside," she heard herself say. "You'll freeze."

The child tilted its head — a birdlike motion, considering — and then crossed the courtyard toward her, leaving small dark prints in the snow, and Mara saw with a lurch that the feet were not merely cold but *wrong*: not blue with chill, not bleeding, not marked at all. Clean. Unmarked as porcelain, as though the snow had declined to touch them.

The child stopped an arm's length away, at the exact border where lamplight from the stairwell reached, and looked up at her. Up close there was something else wrong, something she couldn't name yet, some quality of stillness — like the difference between a sleeping person and a painted one. The small chest rose and fell. The breath fogged. Everything performed its functions. And underneath the performance, something waited.

Then the child raised one bare hand and pressed it flat against Mara's forearm, against the ink-stained sleeve of her habit, and spoke again — the same phrase, the same grave syllables — but this time it ended differently. This time it ended with a name.

"*Bram,*" said the child.

The word dropped into Mara's chest like a stone into a well, and she listened to it fall, and fall, and never heard it land.

She knew the word. That was the horror of it, arriving a heartbeat after the relief — she *knew* it, the way you know a face glimpsed once through a crowd years ago. It belonged to the same tongue as the phrase the child kept repeating. It belonged to the texts in the sealed case in the undercroft, the pre-kingdom fragments written in an alphabet older than the valley's founding, older than the Veil itself, which Mara had copied out in secret at fifteen and burned the copies at sixteen when the fear got too large. A dead language. Dead as the god whose hairs Saint Verity had counted. Nobody living spoke it. Nobody living *could*.

Nobody living, she thought, looking down at the small bare feet on the frozen stones, unless nobody living included whatever was standing in front of her wearing a child like a borrowed coat.

Behind her, in the dark of the scriptorium, among the forty years of small debts, a page turned — though every shutter was closed and there was no wind inside.

The child's fingers tightened on her sleeve.

"*Bram,*" it said again, and this time there was something new in the ruined little voice, something that needed no translation at all, and Mara Thornbury — who had spent her whole life learning to want nothing, who had made an art of patience, who had nine hundred and forty-one coppers and four years and seven months and a shelf of ledgers to show for it — felt the first crack run through the wall she had built, and heard, faintly, on the far side of it, the sound of a world she had been promised did not exist.
