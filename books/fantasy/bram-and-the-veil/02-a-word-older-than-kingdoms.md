## 2. A Word Older Than Kingdoms

The child had not slept.

Mara knew this because she had not slept either, and through the thin wall between the scriptorium and the infirmary cell where Sister Vell had insisted the foundling be kept, she could hear the small sounds of wakefulness: the creak of a cot frame, the whisper of bare feet on flagstone, and beneath it all that voice, low and ceaseless, speaking to no one in a language that sounded like water deciding to become words.

Dawn came gray to the valley, the way it always did, the mountains west of Thistlehollow still holding their night in their folds while the temple bell counted out the hour of first copying. Mara sat at her desk with the tithe ledger open before her and had written nothing for the better part of an hour. Her quill had dried at its tip. She kept meaning to dip it. She kept not dipping it.

*Three years, four months, eleven days,* she thought, the old arithmetic rising automatically, the way a tongue finds the roof of the mouth. That was what remained of her debt. Eleven hundred and ninety-two days of copying other people's accounts of grain and candle-wax and coffin-nails before the Thornbury name was struck from the temple's book and she could walk out of the gate and be no one's debtor. She had counted those days the way misers count coins, hoarding them, spending them carefully, never letting one slip past unnoticed. It had been enough. It had been a life, if a small one, and small lives were not to be despised; she had told herself so every morning for six years.

Then last night a barefoot child had come knocking at the scriptorium door through the snow, and now the arithmetic would not hold still.

The knock came again — not from the outer door but from the inner one, the one connecting the scriptorium to the elders' corridor, and Mara was on her feet before it finished, smoothing her scribe's smock, composing her face into the expression of dutiful blankness that had kept her out of trouble for six years.

Brother Aldous entered without waiting for invitation, which was his habit and his privilege. He was First Elder of the temple of Thistlehollow, a spare man built like a winter branch, and he carried the morning's frost in the seams of his face. Behind him came Sister Vell, round and worried, wringing her hands as though trying to squeeze the worry out through her fingers.

"You saw it first," Aldous said. Not a question. He never wasted syllables on questions when a statement would serve to accuse.

"I opened the door," Mara said. "It was snowing. The child was standing in it."

"The child." Aldous let the word sit between them like something dead on a table. "Sister Vell tells me it has not slept. Has not wept. Has eaten only bread soaked in milk, and only after touching the bread first, all around the rim, as though testing it for—"

"For poison," Mara said quietly. "Or for enchantment."

Vell made a small distressed sound. Aldous's eyes, pale as skimmed milk, fixed on Mara. "You have an opinion about enchantment, Scribe Thornbury?"

"I have an opinion about children," Mara said. "They are cold, and hungry, and frightened in that order."

For a moment the only sound was the wind working at the shutters, and under it, faintly, the child's low river-voice going on behind the wall.

Aldous turned toward the sound as if he'd been struck by it. "That noise. It has not stopped since midnight. Vell says it speaks *at* people — at the wall, at the floor, at its own hands — as though expecting to be answered."

"It is expecting to be answered," Mara said, and then wished she had not, because Aldous's head came around slowly, the way a hawk's does, and she understood she had just spent more than she intended.

"You understand it."

"No, Brother Elder."

"Mara." His voice did not rise. It never rose; that was what made it terrible. "I am sixty-one years old. I have buried two abbots and outlasted three Warden-Captains, and I have watched you copy tithes for six years with the patience of a spider, and I know precisely how much a spider notices. You looked at that child last night the way I have seen starving women look at bread. Do not tell me there is nothing in your mouth worth saying."

Mara held his gaze. Six years of practice held her spine straight and her face smooth. But inside her chest, something was beating against its enclosure like a moth against glass — the same thing that had beaten there all her life, in the temple schoolrooms where she finished her lessons too fast and hid the finishing, in the scriptorium where she read three lines of every ledger beyond the one assigned and told herself it was diligence. *Curiosity* was not a sin in the temple of the Veiled Valley. But it was a debt, and debts compounded, and Mara Thornbury knew everything there was to know about compounding.

"There are sounds in it," she said at last, carefully. "Vowel shapes. Cadence. It reminded me of nothing I know, which is itself unusual. I have copied in nine scripts and read in five tongues. Nothing I know. But it was not *nothing*, Brother Elder. It was structured. It was grammar wearing sounds I don't own."

"And where would a debt-scribe of Thistlehollow have learned to hear grammar?" Aldous asked softly.

"In the copying of it," said Mara. "One learns the shape of a thing by tracing its outline ten thousand times."

This was true, and it was also a deflection, and they both knew it, and Aldous let it stand — which frightened her more than pursuit would have. He turned to the window, where the gray light was strengthening over the snow-crusted courtyard.

"The Veilwardens will come today or tomorrow," he said. "Captain Hesk keeps a garrison at Millbrook now; word travels fast even in this valley, and faster than it should. A mute country has one law: nothing crosses the Veil, nothing stirs it, nothing whispers through it. And into our lap, in the deepest snows in twenty years, falls a child that does nothing but whisper." He clasped his hands behind his back. "The temple's position is simple. We found it. We sheltered it. We surrender it to the Wardens' keeping and wash our hands of whatever it carries. That is the safe road, Scribe. That is the road this valley has walked for three hundred years, and it is why everyone born here dies in their beds instead of on pyres."

"May I ask what happens to it afterward?" Mara asked. "The child. In the Wardens' keeping."

"You may not," said Aldous, "because I do not know, and because knowing is the kind of thing that makes a person responsible." He moved to the door, then paused. "It will be taken at midday. Have your ledgers in order by then. Whatever else you are, Thornbury, you are mine until the debt clears, and I will not have my scriptorium tangled in this." 

He left. Vell lingered, wringing, and whispered — "It asked me for something in the night. I couldn't understand, but it pointed at its chest, then at the floor, over and over, and wept without any tears coming. What kind of child cries dry, Mara? What kind of child—" Then the bell rang for second hour and she hurried away to hide her worry in work, as good sisters do.

Mara stood alone in the scriptorium with her dried quill and her open ledger and the sound of the child's voice through the wall, and felt the moth in her chest beat harder.

*Have your ledgers in order by then.*

She looked down at the tithe accounts. Grain, wax, nails. Three years, four months, eleven days.

Then she looked at the far end of the room, at the iron-banded cabinet against the north wall, the one with the lock whose key hung around Brother Aldous's neck, and her heart began — very quietly, very dangerously — to make plans.

* * *

The restricted archive of the temple of Thistlehollow occupied a single vaulted chamber beneath the scriptorium, and it contained perhaps two hundred volumes, which was, in the magicless valley, two hundred volumes more than any honest library required. Most were administrative: land grants, abbatial correspondence, the genealogies of families who had paid handsomely to have their names remembered correctly. Some were theology. Four were dangerous.

Mara knew about the four because she had catalogued them herself, three winters ago, when Aldous — in one of those lapses that made her suspect the old man was not so much rigid as tired — had set her to inventorying the collection. She had copied each title onto her list with steady hands and had memorized each shelf-position with unsteady ones.

*The Concordance of Rivers*, pre-kingdom, author unknown, provenance disputed. Shelf thirteen, second rank, wrapped in oiled leather against the damp.

She had thought about it for three years the way one thinks about a locked box in a dead relative's house — occasionally, guiltily, and always with the certainty that she never would. Now she stood before the cabinet with a hairpin bent into the lock and her whole body ringing like a struck bell, and understood that *never* had simply been waiting for its moment.

The pin slipped twice. On the third try the lock gave with a click that seemed to echo up the stairwell, and Mara stood in the dark breathing hard, listening for footsteps that did not come.

Inside, the air smelled of tallow and time. She took the lamp, took the stairs, took the corridor with the unhurried walk of a woman carrying nothing more illicit than ink, and did not exhale until the archive door was barred behind her.

The *Concordance* was heavier than she remembered. She unwrapped the oilcloth on the reading table and the smell of it rose up — smoke and riverbank and something older than either, a smell like the inside of a word. The book was a miscellany, really; some dead hand had gathered into it fragments of speech from before the kingdom, before the Veil, from the age when the valley had been a crossing-place rather than a hiding-place. Glossaries. Fragments of songs. Half a liturgy. A merchant's phrasebook for trade with peoples who no longer existed anywhere but here, in these pages, pressed like flowers.

Mara had read it once, three years ago, kneeling here in the dark with her heart hammering, understanding almost nothing and thrilled by almost nothing else in her life. She had gone back to her tithes and told herself it was enough.

Now she laid the pages open and waited for the child's voice in her memory, and began, line by line, to hunt.

It was slow work, slower than fear wanted. The script was archaic; half the glosses were in Old Liturgic, which she read at the pace of a woman wading upstream. Outside, the light through the arrow-slit climbed from gray toward white. Somewhere above, the temple bells marked third hour, and she flinched, doing the arithmetic she did not want to do: midday was coming. Midday, and the Veilwardens, and a child handed over like a misfiled ledger entry.

*Nothing crosses the Veil.* She traced that maxim in her mind, carved over the temple gate for three centuries. But something had crossed. Something had come *down* the mountain, out of the snow, to the one door in the valley where a girl who read dead languages happened to be sitting with a lamp lit. Coincidence was a comfort offered to people who could afford it. Mara had never been able to afford anything.

And yet — page forty — her finger stopped.

*Ae-ram. Ae-bram.*

The gloss read: *a name-offering; lit., "the breath given over"; used in binding-oaths of the mountain courts.*

Her mouth went dry. She read it again. Then again, aloud, barely voicing it, tasting the shape: the vowel opening wide like a door, the consonants closing after it like a latch.

Last night, standing in the snow, blue-lipped and silent for so long that they had all assumed muteness, the child had finally spoken — one word, one utterance, aimed at Mara alone with the terrible directness of an arrow finding the one gap in armor:

*Bram.*

She had thought it a name. Now, staring at the glossary, she understood it was a name the way a wound is a doorway — technically true, and useless as comfort. *A name-offering. The breath given over.* Given over to whom? To what court, on what mountain, under what terms? The fragment on the facing page spoke of bindings, of souls *filed* like deeds of land, of a tribunal beneath Mount Caelum where everything owed was collected and nothing forgiven—

The lamps flickered.

Not the wind. The flame leaned toward the stairwell door and steadied, leaned and steadied, like a dog scenting its master, and Mara heard — very far off, or very deep, she could not say which — a sound like many pages turning at once.

She slammed the *Concordance* shut. Wrapped it. Returned it to shelf thirteen, second rank, her hands steady by some mercy or muscle-memory, and barred the archive door behind her with the lock re-set and the hairpin hidden in her sleeve.

In the corridor, she met Sister Vell running.

"They're here," Vell gasped. "The Wardens — early — grey cloaks at the gate, and Captain Hesk with them, and Brother Aldous wants the child brought down to the courtyard *now*, Mara, now —"

Through the high windows Mara could see them: four riders in the courtyard snow, and their leader dismounting, a tall man moving with the unhurried certainty of someone who has never once been told *no*. Even at this distance, Warden-Captain Hesk seemed less to arrive than to *claim* the ground he stood on.

And below, being led out into the snow by two sisters, small and barefoot and unresisting, the child lifted its head — found Mara at the window as surely as a compass finds north — and spoke. Clearly. Deliberately. One word, pitched to carry across the courtyard, across the walls, across every law the valley had ever passed:

"Bram."

Not a plea. An introduction. As though to say: *this is who I am; this is who I am not; do you see the difference?*

Mara's hand pressed flat against the cold glass. Three years, four months, eleven days, she thought. A small life, patiently counted. A safe road, walked for three hundred years.

Below, Hesk turned his head toward the child, then — slowly — upward, toward the window, toward her.

Mara stepped back from the glass, heart slamming, and reached for the one thing she had never dared reach for: not the hairpin, not the forbidden book, but the decision itself, forming whole and terrible in her chest like ice taking hold of a pond.

*I can parse the word. I may be the only person in this valley who can.*

Which meant she was not evidence.

She was the translation.

And translations, she thought, pulling her smock straight and walking toward the stairs with the unhurried tread of a debt-scribe going to ruin, are not surrendered. They bargain.
