## 3. What the Veil Keeps Out

The goats knew before Odile did.

She was elbow-deep in a kettle of nettle-render when the whole herd went silent at once — six shaggy heads lifting toward the tree line, ears swiveled like weathervanes. Odile had kept goats for eleven years, and in eleven years they had screamed at foxes, at thunder, at the color red, and once, memorably, at a turnip. They did not go silent. Silence in a goat was the sound of something the animal could not name, and Odile had learned long ago to trust an animal's namelessness over her own certainty.

She wiped her hands on her apron, took the sickle off its peg, and went to stand in her doorway.

Two figures were coming up the drover's path from the valley floor: a woman in a scribe's grey, mud to the knee, carrying a child who was not asleep and not awake but somewhere in between — the particular limpness of a body that had been running on fear and had finally, mid-flight, run out. The woman walked like a debt: shoulders braced against an invisible weight, eyes counting the ground ahead of her as though the ground might present an invoice.

Odile knew that walk. She had invented it.

"You're the hedge-witch," the woman said when she reached the gate. Not a question. Her voice was scraped thin, and there was a bruise of exhaustion under each eye, but her chin was up, which meant she still believed she had choices. Odile gave her three days before that belief wore through.

"I'm the woman who keeps goats," Odile said. "The hedge-witch is a rumor started by people who steal my rosemary."

"They said you'd help."

"They said wrong." Odile looked at the child — a girl, or a boy, it was hard to tell under the dirt and the woolen cap, and it didn't matter; a soul in a body was a soul in a body, and she'd stopped sorting them by shape years ago. The child's eyes were open. They were fixed on Odile with an attention that had nothing childlike in it, and when the wind moved through the birches, the child's lips moved too, shaping sounds in a tongue that made Odile's back teeth ache.

She knew that ache. She had felt it once before, thirty years ago, on the far side of the Veil, before she crossed and left all of it behind.

"Put the child down," Odile said. "You look like you're about to drop her, and my gate is older than your whole temple."

"It's a he," the woman said, but she set him down on the bench by the door all the same, and her hands shook when she let go.

"I'm Mara Thornbury," she added, as if the name were a key she expected to work.

"It's not," Odile said. "But come in anyway. The kettle's on."

* * *

The inside of the cottage was small and crowded and smelled of tallow, thyme, and the particular mineral tang of things kept too long in iron. Mara sat where she was pointed, a stool by the hearth, and watched the hedge-witch move through her clutter with the economy of someone who had lived alone long enough to resent every extra movement.

Odile had a face like a map of somewhere harsh — all ridges and weather — and hands that were surprisingly gentle with the child, tipping water into his mouth a spoonful at a time. The boy swallowed without waking. His lips kept moving. That same low murmur, syllables worn smooth like river stones.

"He's been doing that the whole way," Mara said. "Two days. He doesn't sleep properly. He eats if you put it in front of him, but he doesn't ask for anything. He never asks for anything."

"What does he say?"

"I don't know. Some of it. A little of it. There's a text in the temple archive — pre-kingdom, older than the Veil, older than the valley — and the script matches the sounds. Not all of them. Maybe one word in ten." Mara's hands were in her lap, and she was wringing them like laundry. "One word I'm sure of. He says it constantly. *Bram.*"

The spoon stopped.

Odile set it down on the table with a care that was louder than any clatter could have been. She looked at the child for a long time — at the too-still hands, the breathing that was a half-beat too slow, the eyes that had rolled back not in sleep but in some private, interior dark.

"Where did you find him?" she asked.

"He found us. The scriptorium. Snow on his feet, frost in his hair, and he walked straight past four elders to my desk." Mara gave a laugh that had no humor in it, only disbelief still burning itself out. "They wanted to give him to the Veilwardens. I took him instead. I don't — I keep telling myself I had a reason. I don't know that I did."

"You had the only reason that matters," Odile said. "You looked at him and your gut said *wrong*, and you listened to it. Most people drown their gut in doctrine by the time they're twenty." She crouched by the hearth and fed a split of birch to the fire. "The elders wanted to hand him to the Wardens because they didn't know what he is. You took him because you didn't either, but you knew it mattered. That's a better instinct than half the clergy I've met, and I've met clergy in three kingdoms, so I'm qualified."

"What *is* he?" Mara asked.

Odile was quiet for a while. The fire cracked. Outside, the goats had resumed their complaining, which meant the world had resumed being ordinary, which meant the thing she was afraid of was not standing in her yard.

It was already inside her house, sleeping on her bench.

"You're a scribe," Odile said. "Temple-trained. So you'll know the difference between what a thing is called and what a thing is. What is the valley called?"

"The Vale of Caelum. The Quiet Valley. The — " Mara stopped. "The Peaceful Vale. That's the liturgical name. *Vallis Pax.*"

"And do you know why it's peaceful? Truly peaceful — no hexes, no charms, no cunning-folk, no blessings that take, no curses that stick. A valley the size of a small kingdom, and in eleven years I have not been able to make a wart vanish. Not one wart. Do you know what that does to a witch's reputation?" She said it dryly, but there was an old flint under the dryness, something that had been struck against stone so many times it had forgotten how to spark.

"I assumed the Veilwardens enforced it," Mara said slowly. "The peace. They punish — irregularities."

"The Wardens are shepherds," Odile said. "They herd the sheep and call it the fence. No. The peace isn't enforced. It's *imposed* — by the Veil itself. There is a boundary, girl, drawn around this valley by hands older than any kingdom in your archive, and across that boundary magic does not pass. Not flows. Not trickles. Does not *pass*. A witch on the far side of the line can boil a river dry. A witch on this side can't warm her own tea with a word." She held up her hands. "Everything I was, I had to leave at the border like a knife at a temple door. I crossed thirty years ago with a satchel of charms that turned to buttons by the time I reached the far side of the pass. Buttons. I keep them in a jar. I keep looking at them."

Mara was staring at her. "Magic can't cross into the valley."

"Now she's a scribe again. Say it as a consequence, not a fact."

Mara's brow furrowed — and Odile watched the thought move through her like weather, watched the color change. "Then nothing magical can be *sent* here either. Nothing can be hidden here that the Undercourt — " She stopped. Her hand went to the sleeping child's shoulder, and her fingers closed on the wool of his coat as though he might be taken from under it. "He was sent here to be *unreachable*."

"Someone paid a great deal," Odile said quietly, "to put that boy where no power could follow him. The Veil keeps things out, scribe. But walls that keep things out also keep things in, and anything carried across the line — carried in a body, in blood, in bone — becomes the safest thing in the world. Or the most abandoned." She looked at the child. "I've never seen a soul carried across the Veil. It's supposed to be impossible. Souls are the one currency the boundary was built to stop. Which means whoever did this either found a way to break the oldest law in the world —"

"Or the soul was already inside the body when it crossed," Mara said.

"Or that." Odile sat back on her heels. "And that, girl, is the answer I like least, because it means the body itself is the vessel, and vessels are made to be *opened*."

* * *

The child woke at dusk, and the first thing he did was look at Odile.

Not at Mara, who had fed him, carried him, wept over him in the way of someone who had not yet learned to ration her tenderness. At Odile. He looked at her the way one professional looks across a room at another — and then he said something in that worn-smooth tongue, four or five syllables, and pointed one small finger at the jar of buttons on the shelf.

Odile went cold from the scalp down.

"What did he say?" Mara whispered.

"I don't know." That was a lie, and Odile was not a good enough liar to make it sound like anything else. The truth was that she had understood perhaps one word in ten — the same ratio Mara had reported — and one of the words she had understood was *witch*, and another was *buttons*, and the way the child had said *buttons* had been with something that in an adult she would have called pity.

"Your archive text," Odile said. "The pre-kingdom one. How old?"

"Older than the founding. The elders call it the Root-Tongue. They say it was spoken before the kingdoms, before the courts — " Mara stopped. Odile watched her arrive at it. "Before the Undercourt."

"Everything under the mountain is older than everything above it," Odile said. "Stone remembers first." She crossed to the child and crouched, bringing her eyes level with his. Up close, the wrongness was worse: his pupils were fine as pinpricks, and in their centers there was a depth that did not belong in a face that young. "Who are you?" she asked him, in plain valley speech, and then, because thirty years of bitterness is not so easily discarded, she said it again in the old tongue — the one she had sworn never to use again, the one that had cost her everything on the far side of the line.

The child answered.

He answered in the old tongue, fluently, patiently, as one speaks to a slow student, and Odile understood perhaps one word in eight, and the words she understood were these: *hidden*, *thirteen*, *moon*, *owed*.

And then he said the word. *Bram.* And he put his own small hand flat against his own small chest.

Odile sat down hard on the floor.

"That's not his name," Mara said. "That's — he's been saying it for days, I thought — "

"It's not his name," Odile agreed. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "It's the name of the boy who *is* him. Or was. Or is supposed to be." She looked at Mara across the firelight, at this tired, stubborn, debt-ridden scribe who had thrown away her whole careful life for a child she couldn't understand, and Odile felt the old thing stir in her chest — the thing she had buried with her charms, the thing that had made her a witch in the first place, which was not power at all but the unbearable inability to walk past a wrong thing and leave it wrong.

"Here is what I know," Odile said. "The Veil keeps magic out of this valley, which means it keeps the Undercourt out too. The courts can't collect what they can't reach. Someone hid this child's soul on the one patch of earth where his creditors have no jurisdiction. Clever. Cruel. The kind of clever that costs someone else." She took a breath. "And here is what I don't know, and what I suspect you're not going to like: the Veil is not a wall, girl. It's a *ledger line*. Everything on this side is accounted for. Everything that crosses is entered. And in thirty years, nothing has ever been carried across that was not meant to be found."

"Found by whom?"

"That," said Odile, "is the question I've been not-asking since you walked up my path."

* * *

They did not sleep, any of them. Mara dozed in a chair with the child in her lap, and Odile sat at her table with a candle and a sheet of scrap vellum, doing the one kind of work the valley still allowed her: writing.

She wrote down every fragment she had understood. *Hidden. Thirteen. Moon. Owed. Bram.* She drew the boundary of the valley from memory — she had walked that border more times than she would admit, in the early years, testing it, hating it, pressing her palm against the invisible line like a woman pressing against glass. She marked the passes. She marked the old road. And then, because the child had said *thirteen* and *moon* and her skin had crawled when he said it, she marked the moon's phases, counting forward.

Thirteen turns of the moon was not a year. It was a season and a hand of weeks. It was soon.

She was still counting when Mara spoke from the chair, not quite awake, not quite asleep, her voice frayed down to the thread.

"Why are you helping us? You said you wouldn't. You said they said wrong."

Odile did not look up from her vellum. "I said the rumor was wrong. I never said anything about the witch."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Odile agreed. "It isn't." She set down the pen and looked at the woman in her chair, this stranger with a stranger's child, and found that the truth was already in her mouth, waiting, like a charm she'd been keeping under her tongue for thirty years. "When I crossed the Veil, I left things on the other side that I shouldn't have left. Debts. A sister. A name I can't say in this valley without the Wardens coming to ask polite questions. I told myself I was done — that the peace was a fair price for the quiet." Her hand closed around the jar of buttons, just for a moment, just to feel them. "And then a soul walks across a border that souls cannot cross, and lands on my doorstep, and asks me about my buttons in a dead language, and I think: the quiet was never the price, girl. The quiet was the *cell*. And I think I would very much like to know who built the cell, and why, and what they're owed."

Mara was fully awake now. In the firelight her eyes were the color of strong tea, and there was something in them Odile recognized from her own mirror, decades ago: the look of a person standing at the exact spot where obedience ends.

"The temple will report me," Mara said. "By now, they already have. And the Wardens don't ask polite questions — I've seen them ask questions. They'll come here."

"They'll come everywhere," Odile said. "Which is why you're not going back down the valley, and why you're not staying here past tomorrow's dark. There's a road through the high pass — the old road, the one that predates the Wardens' maps — and it runs toward the mountain, toward Caelum, toward everything under it that your child is hiding from." She said it flatly, the way one reads a bill. "I swore an oath thirty years ago never to go back up that road. I've been breaking small oaths ever since to practice."

The child stirred in Mara's lap. His eyes opened — that too-old dark — and he looked from Odile to Mara and back again, and then he said something soft, two syllables, in the Root-Tongue, and pointed one small finger at the window, where the moon was rising over the shoulder of Mount Caelum, already a night older, already a night closer to thirteen.

Odile understood that word. She had understood it the first time he'd said it, and she wished with everything left in her that she hadn't.

The word was *soon*.
