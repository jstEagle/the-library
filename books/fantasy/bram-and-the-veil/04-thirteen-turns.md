## 4. Thirteen Turns

The moon had been whole when the child came to the scriptorium door, and now it was a bitten crescent hanging over the temple of Saint Aldhelm's like a fingernail paring, thin and pale and counting itself down.

Mara had learned to count in moons the way other people counted in years. Her debt ran by seasons — seven years of service for her mother's apothecary debts, four already spent — but she had begun measuring everything smaller against the sky. The child had been with them nine nights now. Nine nights of the strange syllables that poured out of him like water from a cracked jug, endless and shapeless, and no closer to meaning.

He slept in the novices' infirmary on a pallet too large for him, curled into himself like a comma, and every night Sister Berna checked his breathing twice before dawn because there was something about the way he slept that unsettled even the old women who had watched children die before and did not rattle easy. He breathed too slowly. Or perhaps it was that he never turned over. He lay down as he lay down and rose in the same position, as though sleep were something he performed rather than something that took him.

Tonight Mara sat beside the pallet with Odile's borrowed book open across her knees, and the candle burned down between them, and the child talked in his sleep.

He did this every night. Not nightmares — there was no thrashing, no crying out. Just a low, continuous murmuring, syllable after syllable after syllable, sometimes for hours, in that dead tongue that made the hair on Mara's arms rise because it was not the sound of a child's voice at all. It was deeper than it should be. Older. Like listening to a well speak.

"Say it again," Odile said.

She sat on the floor across the pallet, cross-legged, her grey shawl pooled around her, and she had not moved in an hour. She looked, in the guttering light, like something the temple had grown rather than invited — all angles and stillness, her eyes half-lidded, her hands resting palm-up on her knees as if waiting to catch something falling.

"He says the same passage every night," Mara whispered. "I've told you."

"I know what he's said *before*. I want to hear whether it changes." Odile's eyes opened fully, black and unblinking. "A soul under contract repeats its terms. That's how you know it's a contract and not just grief. Grief wanders. Contracts recite."

Mara bent toward the sleeping boy. His lips moved. The words came, low and level and terrible in their patience:

*Vael-ith korram. Vael-ith korram. Senn aveth Bram. Senn aveth Bram. Torren-vail, torren-vail, torren—*

"There." Odile sat up so fast the shawl slid off one shoulder. "*Torren.* Say that word again. No — don't wake him. Just — count. How many times does he say it?"

Mara listened. The child murmured through the phrase again, the same phrases in the same order, and then: *torren-vail, torren-vail.*

"Twice," she said. "Every cycle. Twice."

"And how many cycles has he done tonight?"

"Four. Always four. Four cycles, then he goes quiet for an hour, then he begins again." Mara closed the book slowly. A coldness was gathering behind her sternum, the particular cold of a scribe recognizing a pattern she did not want to recognize. Numbers were her trade. She knew what it meant when a thing repeated exactly. It meant it was written down somewhere. "Odile. What is he?"

"A ledger," Odile said quietly. "He is a ledger that walks."

* * *

They worked through the rest of the night in the scriptorium, where no one would look for them before matins and where the walls were thick enough to swallow a whisper. Odile spread the child's recorded speech across three sheets of Mara's finest paper — Mara had transcribed everything since the first night, obsessively, in her small precise hand, without quite admitting to herself why — and weighted the corners with ink pots and a cold tallow stub.

"It's not a language I know," Odile admitted. She said it grudgingly, the way she admitted most things, as if each confession cost her a coin. "It predates my training. Predates most training. But contracts have a grammar older than any language that carries them. Debts are spoken the same way everywhere, because debt is older than speech. Listen." She tapped the page. "This word here — *korram* — appears at the start of every cycle. And this one — *aveth* — sits next to the name. In every bargain-tongue I've ever seen, the opening formula states two things: the holder of the debt, and the debtor. *Korram* will be 'held' or 'kept.' *Aveth* will be 'belonging to' or 'owned by.'"

"And *Bram* is the name."

"*Bram* is the name," Odile agreed. "But which one? Is the debt held by Bram? Or does the debt belong to Bram?" She pulled the transcription toward her and stared at it until Mara thought her eyes would wear holes in the vellum. "That distinction is the entire question. Everything else is decoration."

Outside, the wind worried the shutters. Somewhere below, a novice coughed in her sleep. The temple settled around them, stone and routine and the smell of lamp oil, and Mara felt suddenly how fragile it all was — how it had always been fragile, and she had simply never stood close enough to the edge to see the cracks.

"Read it to me again," Odile said. "All of it. Slowly. I need to hear the order."

Mara read. *Vael-ith korram. Senn aveth Bram. Torren-vail, torren-vail.* Then a longer passage she could not parse at all, a flood of syllables that changed slightly from night to night while the frame around them never varied. Then the closing phrase, always last, always delivered with a slight drop in pitch that made the hairs on her neck stir: *Il-maren. Il-maren. Il-maren.*

Three times, that one. Every night. Three times, like a bell rung for a death.

"*Il-maren,*" Odile repeated. Her voice had gone strange. Flat. Careful. "That one I do know. Old road-speech. My grandmother used it for curses she didn't want me understanding." She looked up. "It means *thirteen*. Or it means *the thirteenth*, depending on the case. And the case here—" she tapped the final line, "*il-maren* with the falling ending — is the counting case. The case you use for things running out."

"Running out of what?" But Mara already knew. She felt it land in her chest like a stone dropped down a well, falling and falling, waiting for the water.

"The moon." Odile rose and went to the narrow window, pushing the shutter wide. The crescent hung above the mountain, Caelum black and vast against the stars, wearing its ring of cloud like a hood. "Count with me. The night he arrived, the moon was full. Full is turn one of the waning — no, wait. No." She stopped herself, pressed her knuckles to her mouth, and Mara watched her actually calculate, watch the numbers move behind her eyes. When Odile spoke again her voice had dropped to almost nothing.

"No. Full isn't the start. Full is the end of the last turn. The new moon after — that's turn one." She turned from the window. "Mara. How many nights has he been here?"

"Nine."

"And the moon dies completely in—" Odile's mouth worked. "Four nights. New moon in four nights. Which makes the night he arrived the—" 

Mara counted backward and felt the well-stone hit water. "Turn five."

"Five turns gone. Eight remain." Odile said it very evenly, the way people say unbearable things when they cannot afford to say them otherwise. "On the thirteenth turn — the thirteenth new moon from whatever turning began this — the contract comes due. Whatever he is now, whatever vessel they poured him into—" she gestured at the sleeping child down the corridor, as if he could hear her through stone, "—it fails. It was built to fail. A vessel with a term of use, like a lease. They didn't hide the boy in the valley, Mara. They *stored* him. Until the court collects, or until the term expires and the contents spoil."

The silence afterward was total. Even the wind seemed to hold its breath.

"Spoil," Mara repeated. The word tasted of ash. "You mean die."

"I mean worse than die, probably, but yes." Odile rubbed her face with both hands, and for a moment she looked every year of her age and several more besides. "The Undercourt doesn't kill, girl. Killing wastes. They *foreclose*. If the term runs out before someone pays or someone cheats, the soul gets rendered down for whatever parts are still legible — memories sold separately, years stripped and resold, the name filed under salvage. There'd be nothing left of your Bram to save. Nothing to bargain back. Just stock."

"How do you know all this?" Mara asked, and heard her own voice come out sharp. "How does a hedge-witch at the edge of nowhere know the commercial practices of the Undercourt?"

Odile looked at her for a long moment. Candlelight moved in her black eyes.

"Because I read the same forbidden texts you do, only I didn't have a temple to confiscate them afterward," she said finally. "And because everyone at the edge of nowhere knows someone who went up the mountain to make a bargain and came back short. You'll learn, living here long enough. This valley is a fence around a wound. Everything magicless inside it, everything hungry outside it, and every few generations somebody finds the seam." She began rolling up the transcriptions, her movements abrupt, angry. "Nine turns. We have nine turns, and we've spent five standing in a temple arguing with deaf old men."

"We haven't argued yet. I haven't told anyone."

"You haven't told the elders?"

"They want to give him to the Veilwardens." Mara heard the words leave her and was surprised by their steadiness. "Sister Berna sent the request three days ago. A Warden delegation is expected before week's end. Officially, they're coming to assess an anomalous foundling. Unofficially—" she swallowed, "—they're coming to collect him. Take him over the pass to some Warden holding. Get him out of the valley."

"Out of the valley," Odile said slowly. "Where the Veil ends. Where magic pools. Where the Undercourt can reach him again." She laughed once, a dry sound with no humor in it at all. "Of course. Of course. The temple thinks it's saving him. It's delivering him. The kindest thing these people know how to do is hand the wounded over to authority, and authority here is a door that only opens outward."

Mara looked down at her own hands. Ink stained the cuticles; it always had, for as long as she could remember, her mother joking that Mara had been born holding a quill and would die owing for the ink. Seven years of debt. Four spent. Three remaining, if she served them out faithfully, copying tithes in a temple that had just decided to hand a child to the men who would feed him to the mountain.

She thought about her grandmother's house in Lowmarsh, the good roof, the orchard. She thought about how a family that poor had gotten a house that fine, and pushed the thought away before it finished forming, the way you push down a lid on something that rattles.

"What do we do?" she asked.

"Now?" Odile tucked the rolled papers into her bodice. "Now you go to bed, and you sleep if you can, and tomorrow you act like nothing has changed, because the moment the temple smells fear on you, they'll lock the infirmary door and post a novice outside it and call it protection. And I—" she paused at the scriptorium door, silhouetted against the dark corridor, "—I'm going to walk the valley floor and find out how fast a message can travel to the pass, and how slow the Wardens can be persuaded to ride. Nine turns is time, Mara. Time is a currency. Spend it carefully."

Then she was gone, and Mara sat alone among the ink pots and the cold candle, listening to the child breathe his slow, patient breaths, and outside the window the moon hung thinner than it had the night before, and the night before that, a white wound closing over the world.

Eight more times, she thought. Eight more nights it would narrow. On the thirteenth, nothing.

* * *

The Veilwardens came early.

Not at week's end — two days after Odile slipped away down the mountain path, three days after the night of counting — and they came in force, six grey riders in oiled leather, their horses' hooves wrapped in felt so that the temple heard nothing until the knock came at the gate. Mara was in the tithe office when the bell rang the alarm pattern she had never once heard used in her four years of service, and her quill left a blot on the ledger that she would remember afterward, a fat black stain blooming across the column of candle-wax tithes like a hole burned in the page.

Warden-Captain Hesk filled the abbess's receiving room the way winter fills a valley. He was not a large man, but he had the quality of largeness anyway — a stillness, a completeness, a sense that nothing about him was provisional. His grey cloak bore no sigil. The Veilwardens needed none; their uniform was the absence of ornament, and every commoner in the valley knew that absence on sight and stepped off the road.

Mara stood at the back of the room with Sister Berna and two elders, summoned as the foundling's keeper, watching the Captain read the temple's report with an expression of mild, professional interest, as though reviewing a shipment manifest.

"A child," Hesk said. "Found at the scriptorium door. Barefoot. Snow on the ground." He turned a page. "No frostbite. No illness. Speaks no known tongue. Sleeps without moving." He looked up, and his eyes were the color of river ice, and they went past Sister Berna entirely and settled on Mara as though she had been marked in advance. "And the temple has concluded this falls within Warden jurisdiction because...?"

"Anomalous persons crossing the Veil boundary fall to the Wardens by covenant," said Elder Cassian, puffing himself up. "Article nine."

"Article nine concerns smugglers and hedge-craft," Hesk said. "This is a child." Something moved behind his eyes — not softness, Mara thought, but a kind of tired accounting, the look of a man checking a figure twice. "Still. Anomalies are anomalies. Where is he now?"

"The infirmary," Mara said, before she could stop herself.

Hesk looked at her properly then. Up and down, unhurried, the way a man reads a document he intends to quote later. "Your name?"

"Mara Thornbury. Scribe, second grade. Debt-bound."

"Thornbury." He said it once, tasting it, and wrote nothing, and somehow that was worse than writing it. "You found him?"

"I heard knocking. I opened the door."

"Alone. At night. During curfew hours." He folded the report closed. "Curfew violations are also article nine, Scribe Thornbury. Interesting week you're having."

"Captain." Abbess Severine stepped forward, all silver authority. "The temple will of course cooperate fully. We ask only that the child be treated gently. He is frightened, and he is—"

"He is a question," Hesk said, "that someone went to considerable trouble to place inside the Veil, and questions placed that carefully are usually expensive ones." He turned to his lieutenant, a younger woman with a scarred jaw. "Bring the child. Gently, as the Abbess asks. Saddle a spare horse."

And Mara felt the floor tilt beneath her, felt the whole tidy architecture of her life — the ledgers, the debt-years, the patient countdown toward a small free life in some market town — slide sideways like furniture in an earthquake, because they were going to take him *now*, today, this hour, and Odile was a day's walk down the valley and the moon was dying overhead and there were eight turns left, seven now, and no plan at all except a stolen book of dead grammar and a hedge-witch's word that contracts could be cheated.

The lieutenant went out. Boots sounded in the corridor, moving toward the infirmary.

Hesk caught Mara looking at him. For a moment — one held breath, no longer — the Captain's ice-colored eyes did something unreadable. Almost like recognition. Almost like pity.

"Scribe Thornbury," he said quietly, so only she could hear. "When the records are reviewed — and they will be — make sure your hands are clean of ink that isn't yours."

Then the lieutenant returned, and her face was wrong.

"Captain," she said. "The child is gone."
