## 5. The Debt Collector Comes at Dusk

The lamps in the scriptorium were lit early that evening, and Mara noticed the wrongness of it before she understood its meaning.

Brother Aldous lit the scriptorium at dusk on feast days and at dawn on working ones — never the reverse. But tonight the tallow candles burned in their iron sconces while the light outside still held a bruised amber, and the great copying tables had been cleared of everything except her ledger of small debts, squared to the table's edge with a precision no scribe would bother with. Someone had prepared the room for an audience.

Mara stood in the doorway with her ink-stained fingers curled around a fresh quill she did not remember picking up. Behind her, down the cloister, two novices hurried past with their heads bent, and neither one looked at her. That was the second wrongness. Novices always looked at her; she was the debt-bound girl, the curiosity, the cautionary tale they whispered about in the dormitory after lights-out. *Thornbury. Nine years left. Don't end up like her.* Tonight they looked through her as though she had already begun to fade.

"The child," she said to the empty corridor, and her voice came out smaller than she intended. "Where is the child?"

No answer but the sound of the mountain wind worrying at the shutters, and beneath it, from the direction of the guest wing, a voice she did not know giving orders in the flat, unhurried cadence of a man accustomed to being obeyed.

She found Elder Sareth in the passage outside the refectory, standing very straight beside a window, her hands folded in the manner she used when delivering bad news about tithes. The old woman's face was composed into something almost like kindness, which was how Mara knew, before a single word was spoken, exactly what kind of evening this would be.

"Child," Sareth said. "There are men here from the Wardency."

"I saw." Mara made herself breathe. "They've come about the boy."

"They've come about *irregularities*." Sareth's eyes flicked away toward the window, toward the darkening valley, and did not come back. "Questions have been asked of us, and we are obliged to answer them honestly. That is all this is. Cooperation. The temple has nothing to hide."

*We*, Mara thought. Not *I*. Already the institution had closed around itself like water around a dropped stone, and she was outside it, watching the ripples.

"Where is he?"

"In the infirmary, under supervision. He is quite safe, Mara. No one is going to hurt him." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on cloth. "Warden-Captain Hesk would like a word with you. In the scriptorium. He asked for it to be there, specifically. I believe he wishes to see where you work."

Mara heard what lived underneath the sentence: *he wishes to see the scene of the thing.*

* * *

He was not what she expected.

She had built Warden-Captain Hesk out of the stories — a big man, loud, cruel in the way of men who enjoy their authority. The man standing at her copying table turned when she entered, and he was none of those things. He was lean and grey-templed, perhaps fifty, with a face like a folded letter: much written on it once, now pressed flat and sealed. His uniform was immaculate without being ostentatious, the Veilwarden grey so dark it read nearly black in the lamplight, and the silver chain of office at his collar caught the candleflame and gave back nothing warm.

"Mara Thornbury," he said. Not a question. "Sit, please. Or stand. Whichever keeps your hands steady."

Her hands were not steady. She sat anyway, at her own table, in her own chair, feeling like a stranger who had wandered into someone else's life and been mistaken for the owner.

"You copy tithes," Hesk said, gesturing at the ledger before him without touching it. "Grain, wool, wax, lamp-oil. Eleven years of it in this hand alone. Very fine work. Very consistent." He turned the ledger a few degrees, studying her penmanship as though it might confess something. "Consistency is a virtue in a scribe. Consistency is also a virtue in a liar, incidentally. The best forgeries come from the steadiest hands."

"I don't understand what—"

"Three nights ago," Hesk said, "a child arrived at this temple's gate speaking a language that predates the kingdom. Two days ago, you were seen carrying forbidden material out of the restricted archive — a text catalogued four hundred years ago and never lawfully removed since. Yesterday, you asked Brother Aldous for the temple's records concerning Mount Caelum. This morning, the child was found attempting to leave the valley by way of the mill road, and when prevented, spoke — in plain trade-tongue, I'm told — the words *'she promised.'*" He looked up. "Did you promise?"

Mara's mouth had gone dry. She could hear the wind again, or thought she could; more likely it was her own pulse in her ears. "I never touched the mill road. I've been at my desk since second bell yesterday. Ask anyone."

"I have asked everyone." He said it without heat, without accusation, and that was somehow worse. "That is rather my profession. And everyone has been remarkably helpful. Your elders most of all." He let that sit between them for a moment, then drew a folded paper from inside his coat and laid it on the table, squaring it to the edge with a small, unconscious gesture that echoed her own habits so precisely it made her stomach turn.

"Do you know what this is?"

"It appears to be a report."

"It is a report. Filed by the temple administration of Vael Hollow, addressed to the Wardency, regarding irregularities in the conduct of one bound scribe, M. Thornbury." He unfolded it but did not turn it toward her; he seemed to feel no need. "It cites unauthorized archive access. It cites questions asked of junior clergy regarding pre-kingdom languages. It cites, and I quote, 'an unhealthy attachment to the foundling,' and notes that the attachment began within hours of the child's arrival." He paused. "Filed this morning. Before noon. Before the child reached the mill road."

Mara stared at the paper as if it might burn if she looked long enough. "I've served this temple for eleven years. I've copied every tithe, kept every ledger, never taken so much as a candle-end that wasn't allotted to me. My debt—"

"Is nine years, four months, and some days," Hesk said quietly. "Yes. I read your indenture. I read everything. It's how I know that your grandmother's estate settled your mother's debts into yours, and that the temple has held that paper for twenty-two years, and that in all that time you have given them nothing but exactness." He folded the report again, slowly. "And this morning they sold you. For nothing, near as I can tell. No favor asked, no concession granted. They simply preferred to be the ones reporting rather than the ones reported upon. Institutions are like that. Self-preservation looks like virtue from the inside."

The word *sold* went through her like a nail through soft wood. She thought of Sareth's folded hands, of the novices who would not meet her eyes, of the cleared table prepared like an altar for this interrogation. Eleven years of lamplight and aching wrists and grim patience, counting her debt years down like beads, telling herself that exactness would eventually purchase her freedom — and they had handed her name to the Wardens over breakfast, unasked, unpriced, unweighed.

"Why?" Her voice cracked on it, and she hated that. "Why would they—"

"Because you're useful to them only as long as you're obedient, and obedient only as long as you're afraid," Hesk said. "And somewhere in the last three days, you stopped being afraid of them. Fear redirected is worse than fear lost, from an institution's point of view. You started fearing *for someone else*. That makes people unpredictable. Unpredictable makes them dangerous. Dangerous makes them paperwork." He said it all evenly, like a man reading a weather report, and Mara realized with a cold settling in her chest that he was not being cruel. He was being accurate. He had seen this so many times it no longer required any feeling at all.

"The child," she said. "What happens to him?"

"That depends on what he is." Hesk rose and walked along the row of her copying tables, trailing one finger above the wood without touching it. "You recognize his speech. The report says as much — says you parsed words from it. Pre-kingdom tongue, possibly older. Which raises the question of how a debt-scribe raised in a magicless valley comes to half-read a dead imperial dialect." He glanced back at her. "Temple training, the elders say. Temple training in things the temple forbids. Their reports are admirably self-contradicting. It's almost artistry."

"My grandmother taught me letters. Old letters. Before she—" Mara stopped. She had never spoken of her grandmother to anyone at the temple. The fact that she wanted to now, wanted desperately to explain herself to this man, told her exactly how frightened she was.

Hesk watched her stop, and something moved behind the folded-letter face — not sympathy, exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man watching someone step onto ice he has fallen through himself.

"I'll tell you what happens next," he said, "because I find that people lie less when they know the shape of the trap. Tomorrow morning the child goes down-valley to the Wardency house at Ferrow Bridge. There he'll be examined. If he's what he appears to be — a foundling, mute-adjacent, displaced — he'll go to the foundling rolls and be fostered out by spring, and none of this will ever have mattered." He turned fully to face her. "But if he's what I think he might be — if there's anything *attached* to that child that crossed a border it shouldn't have — then the examination becomes something else. There are procedures. I won't describe them to you. You'd only lose sleep, and I suspect you'll lose enough as it is."

"And me?"

"That also depends. Cooperate, and the worst you face is censure and a notation on your indenture — years added, perhaps. Obstruct, and..." He shrugged, a small economical motion. "Obstruction of a Wardency inquiry carries penalties I've never yet had occasion to apply to a scribe. I'd prefer to keep my record clean."

The lamplight flickered. Somewhere in the temple a bell rang the hour, and Mara counted the strokes automatically, the way she counted everything — years, debts, pages, days — because counting was how she had survived, because a thing counted was a thing that would eventually run out.

Nine years, four months, and some days. She had worn the number smooth as a river stone. And these men would add to it, or take her whole future, over a barefoot child who had done nothing except arrive and speak and trust her.

"May I see him?" she asked. "Before tomorrow. Say goodbye, if nothing else."

Hesk studied her for a long moment. The candle between them guttered and steadied.

"No," he said. "And I want you to notice, Thornbury, that I'm answering honestly, even though honesty costs me your cooperation. That should tell you something about the difference between me and the people who filed that paper." He tucked the report away inside his coat. "Go to your cell. Lock your door — not that locks mean much tonight. And think carefully about the shape of the trap, the way I described it. People who understand the shape of the trap sometimes find the part of it that isn't nailed down."

* * *

She did not go to her cell.

She walked the cloister instead, in the last grey remnant of the light, past the chapel where the evening offices murmured behind closed doors, past the infirmary where a Warden stood at the door and looked through her the way the novices had. The child was in there. Twenty feet of stone wall between them, and she could not cross it, could not even slow her steps without confirming every line of that report.

*An unhealthy attachment.*

Was it unhealthy? She turned the phrase over as she walked, testing it the way she tested an unfamiliar word in a dead text, sounding out its roots. Attachment to what? To a shivering scrap of a boy who spoke in a tongue older than kingdoms? To the single word she had pulled from his mouth — *Bram* — and the way he had gripped her sleeve afterward, as though the syllable were a rope thrown across a chasm? To the strange, furious tenderness that had opened in her chest like a crack in a wall she had spent eleven years mortaring over?

Or attachment to the possibility — and this was the thought she had been refusing to look at directly, the one that now stepped forward into the lamplight of her mind and refused to step back — that for the first time in her careful, counted, debt-ridden life, someone needed her for something no ledger recorded?

She stopped at the cloister's corner, where the mountain showed itself above the chapel roof: Mount Caelum, black against the last indigo of dusk, wearing its first snows like a crown of ash. The texts said the Undercourt lay beneath it. The texts said a great many things that the Veil had made irrelevant, that the valley's peace had buried under tithes and ledgers and nine years, four months, and some days.

*The shape of the trap,* Hesk had said. *Find the part of it that isn't nailed down.*

She stood there until the stars came out hard and cold over the peak, and she made herself think it through like a column of figures, because arithmetic had never lied to her the way institutions did.

If she stayed: the child went down-valley at dawn, into whatever the examinations were, and she spent her remaining years copying tithes with the memory of his grip on her sleeve sitting in her chest like a swallowed coal. If she ran: she confirmed the report, forfeited whatever mercy existed, added years or lost them entirely, and became — by tomorrow's sunset — exactly the criminal the temple had described.

Both columns ended in ruin. That was what Hesk had not quite said, and what Sareth's folded hands had said perfectly: there was no version of this where Mara Thornbury came out whole. The trap was not designed to catch her doing wrong. It was designed so that caring at all was the crime.

So the only question left was which ruin she could live inside.

She thought of the child saying *she promised.* She had not promised — not aloud, not in words. But he had looked at her across a language older than kingdoms, and she had understood him, and understanding, she was beginning to learn, was its own kind of vow.

Behind her, in the direction of the elder's quarters, she heard Sareth's voice, low and unhurried, giving some instruction she could not make out — and then, unmistakably, the word *seal-ring,* and the word *vault,* and Mara felt the last warm thing in her chest go still and clear as frozen water.

The temple kept its seal-ring in the vault beneath the chapter house. The ring that made a document true. The ring that could open doors, sign permissions, command obedience from every gate and tollhouse in the valley's jurisdiction.

Eleven years of exactness, and they had sold her for a morning's safety. Very well. She had learned bookkeeping at the knee of a woman whose name appeared in no temple record she'd ever been allowed to read, and bookkeeping's first lesson was simple:

Every ledger balances. The only question is who pays.

Mara turned away from the infirmary, away from her cell, and walked toward the vault stairs with the dark rising around her like a tide, and she did not look back once.
