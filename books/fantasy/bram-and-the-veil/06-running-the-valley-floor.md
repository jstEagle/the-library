## 6. Running the Valley Floor

Mara had copied ten thousand ledgers in her nine years at the temple of Saint Aldhelm's, and she knew precisely how a theft was supposed to look. It was not supposed to look like this — like a woman standing in the dark of the reliquary with her heart slamming against her ribs and the seal-ring of the Provost's own hand warm in her palm.

The reliquary smelled of tallow and old vellum, that smell she had loved for nearly a decade, the smell of order, of everything filed and counted and safe. Moonlight came through the horn panes in pale squares. Somewhere above her, the night brothers were singing compline, their voices rising through the floorboards like water through a cracked jar. *Deus, in adiutorium meum intende.* God, come to my assistance. She had sung it every evening of her adult life, and tonight the words felt like a door closing.

The ring sat in its velvet cradle on the Provost's writing desk because the Provost was vain as well as careless, because he believed no one in his house would ever dare. The seal bore the temple's authority: the sunburst of Aldhelm split by a quill. With it, she could sign travel writs. With it, she could open the postern gate past curfew. With it, she could become, for one impossible night, someone whose orders were not to be questioned.

She thought of the child asleep in the infirmary cot, small hands curled under his chin, breathing in that too-shallow way he had, as though his body were only borrowed and he knew it.

*Thirteen turns of the moon,* Odile had said. *Count them if you don't believe me. Count what's left.*

Eleven remained. Maybe ten.

Her hand trembled over the cradle. Not from fear, she realized — that was the strange part. From something closer to hunger. Nine years she had been patient. Nine years of copying tithes and mortuary rolls, of subtracting one year from her debt each Michaelmas, of watching the elders' faces when the mute child arrived speaking a dead tongue, of hearing Brother Anselm say, *the Veilwardens will know what to do with him,* and feeling something in her chest go quiet and cold and certain all at once.

The Veilwardens would know what to do with him. They would take him apart looking for whatever was inside him, the way you took apart a clock to find the thief.

Mara picked up the ring.

It was lighter than she expected. Most terrible things were, she suspected. She slid it onto her thumb — it fit nowhere else — and stood a moment longer in the moonlit square of light, letting herself feel the full weight of what she was doing. When they discovered the theft, and they would discover it by morning, her remaining debt years would be forfeit. Her name would go into the discipline rolls. Her hands, which could reproduce any script in the valley, would be barred from every scriptorium between here and the coast.

She found she did not care. That was the strangest revelation of the night: that beneath nine years of grim patience there had been waiting, all along, a person willing to burn.

* * *

The postern gate opened to the seal without protest, its lock turning with a small, obscene click that sounded louder than compline. Mara pulled the hood of a novice's cloak over her hair and lifted the child onto her hip. He was heavier than he looked, and warm, and he put his arms around her neck with the unhesitating trust of the very young or the very desperate.

"Stay quiet," she whispered, in the dead tongue, the only words she had that were truly theirs. *Sleep now.*

He made a sound against her ear. Not a word. Almost a word. She had stopped trying to parse his half-language days ago and started simply listening, the way you listened to rain, but tonight one syllable snagged her: something soft and doubled, like *mama*, like *Mara*, though she told herself it was coincidence, told herself firmly, and carried him out into the snow.

The valley at night was a different country. By day it was the ordered world — hedged fields, tithe roads, the Veilwardens' white cloaks moving along the walls of Caelum-town like milk poured over stone. But at night the mountains leaned close, and the snow squeaked underfoot, and the stars came down so low she felt she could have gathered them like fruit. She kept off the tithe road and followed the drainage ditches south, where the ground fell away toward the fens, toward the valley's ragged edge, toward the hedge-witch who had looked at her across a table of tea leaves and said, *You'll have to choose, scribe. Everyone always thinks they have more time to choose than they do.*

Twice she heard hoofbeats on the high road and crouched in a ditch with the child pressed under her cloak, counting her own heartbeat until the sound faded. Once a patrol lantern swung within thirty feet of her, close enough that she saw the Warden's breath plume white, saw the silver chain of office glinting at his throat — Hesk's men, or Hesk himself, hunting already, though surely the theft hadn't been discovered yet. Unless it had. Unless the elders had sent word ahead, unless the whole valley was already closing around her like a fist.

The child stirred against her neck and breathed one clear word into the dark: *"Bram."*

"I know," she whispered. "Hold on, Bram. We're going somewhere the law can't follow."

Whether that was true she did not know. She was beginning to suspect that very little of what she had believed was true — that the valley was peace, that the temple was shelter, that debts were honest things paid honestly. Somewhere behind her the bells began the midnight peal, and she walked faster.

* * *

Odile woke before they knocked.

That was the hedge-craft in her, older than the temple, older than the Veil itself maybe: the sense of a wrongness approaching up her path, two heartbeats and a small third one, stumbling through the fen ice. She was out of bed and dressed and putting water on to boil before the first knock came, because it would not do to greet fugitives with a cold hearth; frightened people needed hot tea the way wounded ones needed binding.

She opened the door on Mara Thornbury, snow to her knees, a temple novice's cloak hanging off her shoulders like a stolen flag, and the child strapped against her chest inside it. Behind them the fen stretched silver and silent to the black line of the valley wall.

"You stole it," Odile said. "The seal."

"I stole the seal."

"And the child."

"The child stole himself. I'm only the getaway." Mara's voice shook on the last word, and Odile watched her master it, watched the scribe's spine straighten by pure will. "They'll find out by morning. I need — I don't know what I need. A place to stand still for one hour."

"Come in, then. Stand still. Drink your tea. Then we're leaving, because an hour is exactly how long it takes a Warden-Captain to ride from the temple to my door once somebody remembers I exist."

The cottage was one room and a lean-to, crowded with the paraphernalia of a disgraced trade: bundles of dried herbs hanging from the rafters, jars of tinctures, a cauldron gone green at the rim, charms — hedge-charms, little woven things of straw and thread and bone — strung along every beam like the molting feathers of some enormous bird. The child's eyes went around the room and fixed on the charms, and he reached out one hand toward them and made a sound of recognition so naked that both women went still.

"He knows them," Mara said.

"His soul is older than the Veil," Odile said shortly, pouring tea. "Everything in this room is older than the Veil. Of course he knows them." She set the cup in Mara's hands and knelt in front of the child. "Hello, Bram."

"Bram," the child agreed gravely, and then a stream of the dead language, soft and urgent, and one word recurring in it that Odile did not know and Mara, listening, translated with visible reluctance: *"Thirteen. Thirteen. Thirteen."*

"Yes," Odile said. "I know. We're going."

"Going where?" Mara asked. "Out of the valley? The Wardens guard the passes—"

"Out of the valley isn't far enough. You could walk a thousand miles and the court's claim would follow that soul like a debt follows blood." Odile rose and began pulling bundles down from the rafters, working fast now, her earlier wryness burned away. "No. We go *up*. Mount Caelum. To the Undercourt itself. You can't outrun a contract, girl. You can only renegotiate it."

"That's madness."

"That's arithmetic." She said it without looking at him, and Mara, holding her tea, understood that Odile had done this arithmetic long before tonight, perhaps the first night, perhaps alone in this room with her tea leaves, and had already grieved the answer.

Then Odile did the thing Mara never forgot as long as she lived — which, given later events, was a longer time than either of them could have guessed. She took down her charms. All of them. Every woven thing of straw and thread and bone, eleven years of careful hedge-craft, the only magic permitted her inside the Veil, the small warm magics of finding lost goats and easing fevers and keeping the fen fog off her roof — she took them down, one by one, and fed them into her hearth.

The smoke that rose was wrong. Not gray but silver, and it moved against the draft, curling out under the eaves and spreading across the fen like frost given intent.

"What are you doing?" Mara's voice cracked. "Those are yours. Those are everything you—"

"They're a scent," Odile said flatly, feeding another bundle to the flames. "Every charm I've ever worked carries my signature, same as your handwriting does. Any hedge-reader the Wardens bring could track me across the valley by them in an afternoon. So we burn the handwriting." She glanced back, firelight carving her face. "A trail you can't follow starts with nothing left to smell."

"It's eleven years of work."

"It's eleven years of hiding." For a moment something moved under her words, something old and starved. "I've been keeping those charms the way a starving man keeps crumbs. Time to stop being starving." The last of the straw caught, flared, and the silver smoke thickened, sheeting outward, erasing. "There. Now the fen says nobody lives here. Let Captain Hesk read *that*."

Outside, far off across the silvered marshes, a lantern glow bobbed along the valley road — one light, then two, patient as tide. The Wardens were awake, then. The hunt was on.

Odile shouldered a pack, wrapped the child in her thickest shawl, and looked at Mara over his head.

"Well, scribe. Last chance to be sensible. Give the boy back, confess your little burglary, serve out your debt. They might even be gentle. The temple does love a repentant prodigal."

Mara looked at the child — at Bram, asleep again inside the shawl, one hand escaped and curled loosely around Odile's finger as if he'd already decided whom to trust. She thought of the reliquary, the ring on her thumb, the bells still faintly tolling midnight behind them. Everything she'd had was ash now, and the astonishing thing was how little the ashes weighed.

"No," she said. It came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "Where do we cross the fen?"

Odile grinned, quick and wolfish, and kicked open the door onto the smoking silver dark.

"Northwest, along the drowned causeway. And mind your footing — the old road has opinions about who walks it."
