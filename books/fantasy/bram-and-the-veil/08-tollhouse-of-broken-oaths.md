## 8. Tollhouse of Broken Oaths

The mountain did not announce itself. It simply grew — first a gray suggestion beyond the valley's orchards, then a wall of scree and pine that drank half the sky, until by the third day of walking Mara's neck ached from looking up at it. Somewhere up there, past the tree line, past the snow, was the boundary of the Veil. Somewhere up there, if Odile's maps could be believed, magic pooled like rainwater in a stone basin.

Somewhere up there was the only place the Undercourt could not follow them — and, Odile said darkly, the only place where everything else might.

Mara kept her eyes on the road instead. The old road wound upward through the foothills in switchbacks worn smooth by centuries of feet, and it had a habit of doing strange things to sound: her footsteps arrived a heartbeat late, as though the ground were considering each one before admitting it. The child rode on her back, arms looped around her throat, weightless in a way that should have frightened her more than it did. Three days of travel had taught her the child's small vocabulary of tugs and pressures — this tug meant *stop*, this pressure against her shoulder meant *listen* — and she had learned, without deciding to, to obey both.

Now the pressure came, insistent, twice.

Mara stopped. Ahead of her, where the road bent around a shoulder of granite, Odile stood very still, one hand raised, her gray braid stirring in a wind that seemed to come from nowhere at all.

"Tollhouse," Odile said, without turning. "You can smell the smoke."

Mara smelled it too, faint under the pine resin: woodsmoke, yes, but beneath it something sweeter and wronger, like sugar burned onto iron. Her stomach, which had been living on foraged sorrel and Odile's suspicious trail bread, clenched with a hunger that felt almost embarrassing given the circumstances.

"We go around?" Mara asked.

"There is no around." Odile finally turned, and her face had the particular set it got when she was about to say something Mara would hate. "This is the Kestrel Road. Every pass through these foothills narrows here, into a gorge the locals call the Throat. The Wardens know it too. Their cordon sits above the gorge — you can see their signal fires at night if you're fool enough to look. The tollhouse is the only way through that isn't a two-week climb over ice that has killed better climbers than me." She paused. "Well. There are ways through that don't involve the road. But they involve things I'd rather not do while carrying a child."

"The child isn't carrying—" Mara began, then gave up. Arguing with Odile about precision of language was like arguing with the weather. "What kind of tollhouse charges what?"

"The interesting kind." Odile smiled, and it was not a comforting smile; it was the smile of a woman remembering every bad decision she'd ever made fondly, the way other people remembered summers. "Come and meet Ferren."

* * *

The tollhouse squatted in the mouth of the gorge like a toad that had decided, after long consideration, to become architecture. It was built of black timber and older stone, three stories tall in a place where nothing taller than a man should have bothered standing, its upper windows shuttered against the wind, its lower windows glowing amber with lamplight. Smoke curled from a chimney crooked as an old finger. A sign hung over the door, so weathered its letters had surrendered to suggestion: **TOLL** — and beneath it, in fresher paint, someone had added: **ALL ROADS PAID IN FULL OR NOT AT ALL.**

Odile pushed the door open without knocking.

Inside, the tollhouse was warm in the aggressive way of places heated by a single enormous stove, and dim, and cluttered past sense — ledgers stacked on ledgers, brass instruments whose purposes Mara could not begin to guess, a chandelier made of antlers, a stuffed owl wearing what appeared to be a tiny hat. And behind a counter built from the door of some grander building, a man sat with his boots up, shelling nuts into a bowl and dropping more on the floor than in it.

He looked up. He was perhaps fifty, lean as a whip handle, with a face like a topographical map of somewhere difficult and eyes the pale gray-green of river ice. His gaze went to Odile first — recognition, and a flicker of genuine pleasure quickly buried under professional indifference — then to Mara, then, last and longest, to the child on her back.

"Well," he said. "Odile Ashgrave. They told me you'd been hanged."

"They tell everyone that. It keeps the price of my company high."

"And now you've brought me customers. Two women and a—" He tilted his head at the child. "What is that?"

"A child," Mara said, before Odile could answer with something worse.

"Mm." Ferren cracked a nut between his teeth and considered her over the wreckage. "Funny sort of child. Funny sort of woman, too. Scribe's ink on your fingers, temple-cut hair growing out ragged, and shoes worth more than my stove. You've run a long way in expensive shoes, mistress whoever-you-are." He gestured with a shell fragment at the stools before his counter. "Sit. Nuts?"

"No, thank you."

"Suit yourself. They're good nuts." He swept a fresh handful off the counter into the bowl, missed the bowl entirely, and continued as though the conversation had not paused. "So. You want through the Throat. Past the cordon. Which means either you're smugglers, in which case welcome home, or you're fugitives, in which case also welcome home, but the rates differ."

"Fugitives," Odile said cheerfully.

"Thought so. You've got the look. Guilty people always stand like the floor might be listening." He leaned forward, elbows on the counter, and for the first time the pleasantness dropped away like a mask slipping, and underneath was something quick and cold and entirely awake. "Here's how it works. The Wardens' cordon covers the high trail and the low trail and the ford below the gorge. What it doesn't cover is the drover's path — an old thing, half-collapsed, runs behind the waterfall. I know it because I cut half of it myself twenty years ago when I was young and stupid instead of old and stupid. I can take three people up it in a night, if none of them scream, and if none of them slow down, and if the child—"

"Will not be a problem," Mara said.

Ferren looked at the child again. The child looked back. Something passed between them that Mara could not see, and whatever it was, it made the smuggler sit back down slowly.

"Huh," he said. "Right. Rates."

"Which are?" Mara asked.

"For anyone else? Silver. For you—" he spread his hands, apologetic and predatory at once, "—I don't think silver covers it. You see, mistress scribe, I've heard what's coming down the road behind you. Warden-Captain Hesk himself. Six riders. Questions asked at every shrine between here and the valley floor, and rewards posted that would make a bishop blush. That means the people hunting you are serious, which means you are *very* valuable, which means anyone who helps you isn't just taking a risk, they're taking a side. And I don't take sides for silver."

"What do you take sides for?" Odile asked, and there was an edge under her lightness now.

"Insurance," said Ferren. "Something that makes helping you cheaper than not helping you. Something that, if Hesk catches me anyway, buys me a story worth telling." His eyes came back to Mara, and stayed there, patient as water finding a crack. "You stole out of a temple, mistress scribe. I can smell temple on you the way you can smell my smoke. Temples have seals. Seals have rings."

Mara's hand closed over her pocket before she could stop it.

She had told herself, on the third night of flight, huddled in a shepherd's hut with the child asleep against her ribs, that the ring was practical. That the temple's seal-ring, lifted from the prior's desk along with a purse of coin and a map of the passes, was a tool like any other — proof of authority if she needed authority, tradeable if she needed trade. She had told herself it didn't matter whose desk it came from. Prior Elsbeth, who had signed the order to hand the child to the Veilwardens. Who had signed, eleven years ago, the indenture papers that bound a fatherless girl with a talent for dead languages to thirty years of copying tithes. Whose signature Mara had copied so many times she could reproduce it blindfolded, in her sleep, in the dark, with her non-dominant hand.

She had told herself all of that. But her hand still knew what it held, and it held on.

"You want the seal-ring of the Temple of the Quiet Word," she said.

"I want," Ferren corrected gently, "the *authority* of the Temple of the Quiet Word, which happens to live in a ring. Do you know what I can do with that? I can forge writs. I can open sealed roads. I can walk up to Warden checkpoints and hand them paper with your temple's own mark on it and watch grown men salute. That ring isn't jewelry, mistress. It's a skeleton key with a God's blessing stamped on it." He cracked another nut. "And it's the least valuable thing you're carrying, which is why I'm asking for it rather than the child."

The silence stretched. The stove ticked. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard groaned under a weight that may or may not have been a person.

"Mara," Odile said quietly, using her name the way one uses a rope thrown to someone sliding toward a ledge. "We can find another way."

"There is no another way. You just finished telling me there's no another way."

"I'm allowed to hope. It's one of my vices."

Mara looked down at her hand, still fisted in her pocket, knuckles white through the worn wool of her glove. And she found — this surprised her most of all, more than the child in the snow, more than the word *Bram*, more than any of it — that she was smiling.

Because she understood, suddenly, what Ferren was actually buying. Not the ring. The ring was brass and blessing; the temple could void its authority with a letter, and probably already had. What Ferren wanted was the *story*: the fugitive scribe who paid a smuggler with the church's own stolen seal. He wanted to be part of the tale, and he thought the price of admission was hers.

But Mara had spent eleven years learning how stories were built, line by line, ledger-entry by ledger-entry, and she knew a secret about them that most people never learned: whoever holds the record holds the truth. And she was, whatever else had been taken from her, still the best record-keeper in the valley.

"Before I give you anything," she said, "we make a contract."

Ferren's eyebrows rose a fraction. "Go on."

"A written contract. Your terms and mine, in full, signed and witnessed. And a copy for each of us, in our own hands, in ink we mix ourselves tonight." She pulled out the stool at last and sat, folding her hands on the counter like a woman settling in for a long day's auditing. "Because I've read enough smuggler's law to know how your profession handles memory, Master Ferren. Debts get forgotten. Promises get misremembered. Witnesses get bought. So we write it all down — the route, the price, the conditions, what happens if you betray us, what happens if the Wardens take us anyway — and we both sign, and we each keep a copy, and if you ever think about selling us to Hesk, you'll remember that somewhere there exists a piece of paper in my handwriting that says exactly what you agreed to do, and exactly what you'll lose if you don't." She tilted her head. "Unless you'd prefer the deal stay verbal. I hear verbal deals are very popular among men who get hanged."

For a long moment the smuggler said nothing at all. Then he laughed — a short, sharp bark of genuine delight, startling the stuffed owl.

"Oh, I *like* you," he said. "That's rare. That's genuinely rare. Do you know how many clever people come through here telling themselves they're the cleverest thing on the mountain?" He stuck out a hand across the counter, palm callused, fingers nut-greased. "Terms accepted. Ring on completion of the crossing — not before. If I take payment up front, what stops me from leaving you at the waterfall?"

"What stops me giving you a fake?" Mara countered. "Completion. Simultaneous exchange. At the top of the drover's path, on safe ground, ring for passage. Written, signed, witnessed."

"Witnessed by who? Your witch?"

"By the contract itself. Two copies, identical, in both our hands. That's witness enough for anyone who matters."

Ferren chewed on that, and on a nut. Above them, the antler chandelier swayed slightly in some draft from the mountains' heart, throwing wheeling shadows across the ledgers on the walls — hundreds of them, thousands, Mara realized, whole shelves of the tollhouse's own accounts, bargains struck and broken and struck again, the entire sedimentary history of everyone who had ever needed to cross this gorge and couldn't do it honestly.

Including, soon, her.

"One condition of mine," Ferren said at last. "The child stays where I can see it on the path. No offense meant. But I've carried cargo that whispered before, and I don't care for surprises."

"It's not cargo," Mara said, and heard her own voice go flat and cold as slate. "It's a person. It has a name."

"Has it?"

"Bram," said the child.

Both adults turned. The child had slid down from Mara's back and stood on the floor between them, small bare feet — they had never managed to keep shoes on it — planted on the tollhouse boards, looking up at Ferren with the grave patience of a judge. And it said the name again, clearly, in its strange accented voice that always sounded as though it were speaking through water:

"*Bram.*"

The fire popped. Ferren's face did something complicated.

"That," he said finally, "is a dead name. That's a name off a gravestone in the old country. My grandmother used to spit salt saying names like that, keep 'em from sticking to the house." He looked from the child to Mara, and whatever he saw in her face made him exhale slowly through his nose. "Right. Fine. Bram stays where I can see him, and I'll pretend I noticed nothing, which is the most valuable service I offer anyone." He hauled a ledger across the counter, blew dust off it, and shoved it toward her along with a pen and a stoppered bottle of ink. "Write your contract, mistress scribe. Make it vicious. I'll enjoy signing it."

* * *

They wrote it by lamplight while the wind worried the shutters and the stove ate wood with small greedy sounds. Mara drafted; Ferren dictated amendments with the relish of a man dictating his own legend; Odile sat on a barrel sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening and interjecting clauses of her own, several of which involved Ferren being thrown off his own roof.

*The party of the first part (hereafter FERREN) agrees to guide the party of the second part (hereafter THORNBURY, ASHGRAVE, and BRAM) via the drover's path behind the falls, past the Warden cordon, to the high trail above the Throat, beginning at moonrise and completing by dawn...*

*In consideration whereof THORNBURY shall deliver unto FERREN one seal-ring of the Temple of the Quiet Word, gold, bearing the mark of the Prior's office, upon arrival at safe ground...*

*Should FERREN betray, abandon, sell, or surrender any member of the party before completion, FERREN forfeits [here Odile insisted] his teeth...*

*Should any member of the party slow, scream, or otherwise endanger the crossing, FERREN may abandon them without penalty or refund...*

Mara hesitated over that clause. Ferren watched her hesitate.

"Fair's fair," he said. "You wouldn't want a guide obligated to die with you."

"No," Mara said. "I suppose I wouldn't." But she added one line more, in her smallest, neatest script, at the bottom of the page, where a careless reader would skim past it: *and should the party be abandoned, FERREN shall carry forward to the party's intended destination such intelligence as may aid them, gratis.* Ferren read it. Read it again. Grinned like a wolf being handed a lamb and finding the lamb holding a knife.

"Sneaky," he said approvingly, and signed.

They mixed the ink together — lampblack, wine dregs, a pinch of ash from the stove — and made the two copies side by side, page for page, stroke for stroke, until even Mara could barely tell which was which. Then each took theirs. Ferren stowed his somewhere inside his coat with a pat, like a man pocketing a weapon. Mara rolled hers and tied it against her ribs, next to the folded pages of forbidden pre-kingdom text and the little bundle of the child's — of Bram's — few possessions.

Then she took the ring from her pocket and set it on the counter between them, and for a moment neither of them touched it.

Up close, by lamplight, the seal-ring looked smaller than it had on Prior Elsbeth's hand. Gold gone soft with wear, the temple's mark — a circle enclosing a single horizontal line, the sign of the Quiet Word, speech laid to rest — worn nearly smooth by decades of pressing into wax. Eleven years of her life had flowed through that little circle, tithe after tithe, entry after entry, debt compounding on debt. She had hated it and needed it and dreamed of it. Now it lay on a smuggler's counter among nutshells, waiting to be spent like a coin.

"You're sure?" Odile asked softly, at her shoulder. "There'll be no going back to the temple after this. Using their seal to slip the Wardens — that's not irregularity anymore, Mara. That's heresy with paperwork."

Mara looked at the ring. She thought of Prior Elsbeth's voice, dry and mild, reading the elders' decision: *the child goes to the Veilwardens, Thornbury. The law is the law, and your feelings are not in it.* She thought of the indenture ledger, her own name entered year after year like a prisoner's tally. She thought of Hesk's questions waiting somewhere behind them on the road, and of the moon overhead, invisible through cloud, grinding through its thirteenth turn.

"Good," she said, and meant it, and the meaning of it frightened her a little — how easy it was, once the choice was actually in front of her, to burn a bridge she'd spent eleven years believing was a home.

She picked up the ring. It was lighter than she expected. Everything stolen turned out to be lighter than expected; that, she suspected, was how they got you.

"Not yet," Ferren reminded her, palm out. "Safe ground first. Contract's contract." But his eyes glittered as he watched her weigh it, and he rubbed his thumb across the worn mark of the Quiet Word when she showed it to him for verification, almost tenderly, the way a man touches a key to a door he's been locked outside of all his life.

Outside, the clouds tore open, and moonlight fell down the gorge in a white sheet, and Mara saw for the first time the waterfall they would climb behind — a silver thread plunging out of darkness into darkness, roaring its one endless syllable. Above it, faint on the ridgeline, orange pinpricks: the Wardens' signal fires, six of them, patient as stars.

Moonrise, said the contract.

"Time to go," said Ferren, snuffing the lamps. "Stay close, step where I step, and whatever you hear behind the water — and you *will* hear things behind the water — keep walking."

Mara settled Bram onto her back. Small arms closed around her throat. Against her ear, the child breathed one word, soft as a prayer, and this time it wasn't a name.

It sounded, Mara thought, terribly like *hurry.*

They stepped out of the lamplight into the moonlight, and the mountain swallowed them.
