## 9. Where Magic Pools

Odile had not crossed the Veil in nineteen years, and she had told herself a hundred lies about why.

The first and most comfortable lie was that she didn't need to. A hedge-witch's craft was small by design — knots and charms, weather-sense in the joints, the gentle persuasion of milk not to sour. The valley's magicless peace had never troubled her much because her magic had always been small enough to live inside its borders like a cat under a floorboard, unseen and mostly forgotten. That was the second lie: that being forgotten was the same as being free.

The third lie was the one she'd fed to Mara on the road out of the valley, walking with her staff in the frozen ruts while the child rode her back with his arms looped loose around her neck: *I know where the boundary runs. I'll feel it before we cross.* That part was true enough. What she hadn't said was what it would feel like.

They climbed through the last of the pinewood as the afternoon went thin and blue around them. Snow squeaked under their boots. The child — she still thought of him as *the child*, though Mara had begun saying *Bram* in that careful, half-apologetic way of someone handling another person's grief — had gone quiet over the last hour, his cheek pressed against Odile's shoulder, watching the trees with an attention that felt older than his body. Twice he had lifted one small mittened hand and pointed at nothing at all.

"Stop doing that," Odile muttered, though there was no heat in it. "You're making my neck crawl."

"What does he point at?" Mara asked. She walked ahead breaking trail, and even now, two days out from everything they'd burned and abandoned behind them, she moved like a scribe — precise steps, shoulders squared, as if the snow were a page and she was afraid of smudging it.

"I don't know," Odile said. It was true, and it wasn't the whole truth. She could feel it too, faintly, the way you feel a draft from a door left open in a far room. Something ahead of them. Something waiting to be let in.

The pines thickened, then thinned all at once, and they came out onto a shelf of bare granite where the wind had scoured the snow into long gray banners. Below them the valley floor unrolled — the smoke of the temple town a smudge on the horizon, the river a seam of white. Above them, Mount Caelum shouldered into the clouds, wearing its glacier like a hood.

And across the mountainside, from granite shoulder to granite shoulder, ran the line.

Odile had seen it once before, as a girl of seventeen with a satchel of charms and more courage than sense, standing exactly where she stood now. Back then she had taken it for weather — a shimmer in the air, a band where the falling snow moved strangely, each flake hesitating mid-fall like a word a speaker has forgotten. Nineteen years later she knew better. The Veil was not weather. The Veil was a scar, and scars are made by something cutting.

"That's it?" Mara asked. She had stopped three paces short of the shimmer, and Odile watched her take in the way the light bent along it, the way the world on the far side looked subtly wrong — colors a half-shade too rich, shadows leaning the wrong way against the rocks.

"That's it," Odile said. "The peace of the valley. No magic crosses it. Not mine, not theirs, not anything with teeth." She shifted the child's weight on her hip. "Which is why somebody put your boy inside it. Can't be collected if the collectors can't reach you."

"But we're going *out*." Mara's voice did something complicated on the word *out* — hunger and fear braided together so tightly Odile couldn't tell which strand was holding. "We're going toward the thing that sold him."

"We're going toward the thing that can give him back," Odile said. "Different door. Same house." She looked up at the shimmering line above them, closer now than she liked, close enough that she could see it wasn't a line at all but a curtain, hanging from the sky itself, moving without wind. "There's something I have to tell you before we cross. And I want you to hear all of it before you look at me like that."

Mara folded her arms. Waiting. She was very good at waiting; Odile had noticed it early and trusted it, in the way you trust a lock that actually locks.

"Nineteen years ago I crossed this line going the other direction," Odile said. "Fast. Bleeding. With people behind me who wanted my hands for kindling — long story, not tonight. And when I crossed back into the valley, the magic went out of me like water out of a cracked jug. All of it. Not dampened. *Gone.* Do you understand? A hedge-witch is a small thing, but she is still a witch, and what they took from me wasn't power. It was—" Her jaw worked. She hadn't said this aloud in nineteen years and it came out rusty. "It was the sense of me. You know how you know your own hand is yours even in the dark? That. They cut off the knowing."

Mara's face had gone carefully still, the way it did when the temple elders said something cruel in a reasonable tone. "And crossing out again?"

"Should pour back in," Odile said. "That's the theory. Magic isn't destroyed by the Veil, only barred. Step outside the bar and—" She shrugged, and hated how casual she made herself sound. "We'll see if the jug still holds water after nineteen years dry."

"You're afraid it won't."

"I'm afraid it will," Odile said, "and I won't know what to do with it. There's a version of me that got very good at being nothing, girl. I've had practice." She hoisted the child higher. "So we cross quick, we don't linger, and if I start acting strange, you hit me with the flat of your staff and keep walking. Agreed?"

Mara almost smiled. Almost. "Agreed."

* * *

Crossing the Veil felt, Odile would think later, like being born, if birth remembered itself.

One step she was in the valley — cold, ordinary, her joints aching in the honest way of old joints in old snow — and the next step the curtain slid down over her like a waterfall made of light, and the world *arrived.*

It came all at once. That was the cruelty of it. Not a trickle, not a slow return, but the whole flood at once, nineteen years of it backed up behind the scar and released the instant her boot broke the boundary. Magic poured into her the way blood pours back into a limb that's slept wrong — a roaring, burning rush of *knowing*, and she heard herself make a sound she didn't recognize, high and cracked, and her knees went, and then Mara had her by the arm and the staff both, shouting her name, and Odile was laughing or sobbing or both, face turned up to a sky that was suddenly *loud* with color.

Because that was the thing no one warned you. Magic wasn't sparks and incantation. Magic was attention. It was the world turning to look at you and finding you worth looking at. Every stone on the mountainside had a name and she could feel them murmuring it. The snow held the memory of every footstep ever pressed into it, layered like rings in wood. The wind coming off the glacier tasted of iron and old prayers. Her own hands — her own plain, chapped, clever hands — blazed with purpose, every knot-charm she'd ever tied lighting up along her knuckles like a rosary.

"Oh," she said. Just that. "Oh, you *beautiful, terrible—*" and she couldn't finish, because the mountain finished it for her, the whole slope singing its low granite note straight up through her boots and into her teeth.

"Odile!" Mara had her face in both hands now, forcing her to focus. "Your eyes—"

"What about them?"

"They're—" Mara faltered. "Luminous. Like banked coals."

"Are they?" Odile laughed, wild and unsteady. "Nineteen years I looked at myself in trough-water and saw nothing worth the seeing. Let them burn." But even as she said it, she was reining herself in, hauling the flood back behind its banks the way she'd been taught before she'd ever been taught anything else — *small craft, Odile, small craft, big magic draws eyes*. Because a witch who lit up like a signal fire on an open mountainside, with Veilwardens somewhere behind them and whatever lived under Caelum somewhere ahead—

The child screamed.

Not cried. *Screamed* — a sound with no childhood in it at all, raw and adult and terrified, and he tore himself off Odile's back so fast he nearly went over the granite edge, and stood there in the trampled snow with his arms flung wide and his head thrown back, screaming at the sky in a voice that was not a child's voice.

It was a boy's voice. Fourteen, maybe fifteen. Cracked at the edges with adolescence, hoarse with running, full of a terror so specific and so old that Odile felt it land in her chest like a thrown stone.

"*No — no, don't take it, don't take it, I didn't sign, I didn't sign, Mother, Mother, they're counting, they're COUNTING—*"

Then it stopped. Cut clean, like a door shutting.

The child swayed. His arms dropped. He looked down at his own small mitten-covered hands as though they belonged to a stranger, and when he raised his face to them his eyes were wet and utterly blank, and he was breathing in little hitches like a swimmer come up too fast.

"Bram?" Mara whispered. She was already kneeling in front of him, careful, careful, the way you approach a bird with a broken wing. "Was that — did you—"

The child shook his head slowly. Once. Then, after a moment, he reached up and touched his own throat, wonderingly, and said something in the dead tongue — soft, questioning, two syllables — and Odile, whose ears were suddenly open to a great many things they had not been open to for nineteen years, caught the shape of it even before Mara translated in a hushed voice:

"He asked... 'whose voice was that?'"

Nobody answered. The wind moved the curtain of the Veil behind them, and far below, tiny as beetles, something dark moved along the valley road. Wardens, maybe. Maybe nothing. Odile filed it away and crouched beside Mara in the snow.

"Listen to me," she said quietly, to Mara, but really to both of them. "What just happened — that wasn't the mountain. That was the magic touching him." She turned the child's chin gently with one finger, studying his eyes. Ordinary brown. Child's eyes. But underneath, deep down where even a witch's sight had to squint, there was something else, something *folded*, packed tight as a letter sealed against tampering. "He's been kept in a place with no magic since before he could walk. This is the first raw magic he's ever touched. And there's a soul in him that remembers what magic *did* the day it was taken."

"The Undercourt," Mara said. "Taking him. Whatever they do when they collect."

"Or the selling," Odile said. "Whatever happened in that room, however old he was. Magic's a key, girl, and that soul is a locked box, and just now we rattled the lock." She blew out a breath that steamed in the blue dusk. "Which means every mile we walk through the wild lands, we rattle it again. And I don't know what comes out when it finally opens."

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a boulder field, a half-mile beyond the Veil, close enough that Odile could still feel its pressure against her back like a hand between her shoulder blades — the valley's silence, pushing. She built a fire the old way, with a charm instead of flint, just to feel it work: a whisper of intent, a curl of warmth drawn out of the wood's own memory of sunlight. Small craft. The flames came up gold and obedient and she could have wept at the simple rightness of it.

Mara fed the child broth and got him wrapped in both blankets, and he fell asleep between one spoonful and the next, mid-word, the way exhausted children do. For a while the two women sat on either side of the fire saying nothing.

"He called for his mother," Mara said at last. She was staring into the flames with her scribe's stillness, but her voice had frayed. "In Bram's voice. He said *they're counting.*"

"Aye."

"Moon turns are counted. Thirteen." Mara pulled her knees up. "Odile, when the magic touched him — did it touch *Bram*, or did it touch whatever the Undercourt put inside him? Are they the same thing? Is he—"

"I don't know." It cost her to say it plainly and she said it anyway. "Here's what I know, for what it's worth. Souls aren't coats you can swap. If they've stuffed something else in that body, it's wearing Bram like a glove. But if what I felt under there is Bram — folded up, sealed, *waiting* — then the screaming wasn't the thing inside him. It was him. Remembering. Reaching." She poked the fire harder than it deserved. "Either way, girl, the answer's under the mountain, and we're walking toward it, so save your dread for when it can be useful."

But she didn't sleep well, and near moonset she woke to find the child sitting up, blankets pooled around his waist, watching the moon with his head tilted like a bird's. And he was talking. Low, continuous, conversational — the dead tongue flowing out of him in his sleep-child voice, but the cadence all wrong, too fluid, too *practiced*, a boy telling a story he'd told himself ten thousand times in the dark.

Odile lay very still and listened with her newly-opened ears, and caught fragments — a dog's name, a river, a sister, a game played with stones on a slate — and understood, with a certainty that settled over her like frost, that the child was naming things. Listing them. Keeping inventory of a life the way a drowning man keeps count of his breaths.

*Mother, I didn't sign. They're counting.*

She rose softly, went to him, and laid her hand — her blazing, remembering, witch's hand — on top of his head.

"Hush now," she murmured, in the oldest dialect she knew, the pre-kingdom speech that Mara fumbled through from forbidden books and Odile had drunk with her mother's milk. "No one's counting tonight. Sleep. I'll keep the tally."

The child stopped. Looked up at her. And smiled — a real smile, brief and blazing, a boy's smile surfacing through a child's face like a fish through dark water — and spoke one clear word in the old tongue before his eyes closed and he slumped back into sleep:

*Soon.*

Odile stood a long time in the cold, her hand still resting on his hair, watching the moon slide down the shoulder of Mount Caelum. Thirteen turns, and the twelfth nearly spent. *Soon,* he'd said. Either a promise or a warning, and no way under heaven to tell which.

Behind her, the Veil shimmered against the stars. Ahead, the mountain waited with its court of counted things. And somewhere below them both, on the valley road, the dark shapes had stopped moving — gathered now, Odile realized with a chill that had nothing to do with the cold, directly beneath the spot where they had crossed.

Someone had found the seam in the world. And they were looking at it.
