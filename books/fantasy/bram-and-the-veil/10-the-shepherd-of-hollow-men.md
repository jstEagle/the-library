## 10. The Shepherd of Hollow Men

The man who found them at the treeline carried a lantern that gave off no light.

Mara noticed it first as a wrongness — a brass lantern swinging from his fist, wick trimmed, glass clean, and nothing within it but darkness. He walked toward them through the pines without hurry, snow to his ankles, and when he was close enough that she could see his face she understood the lantern was not broken. It was simply empty. Whatever had once burned inside it had been taken out, carefully, like a stone from a fruit.

"You'll be wanting shelter," he said. His voice was pleasant, unhurried, the voice of someone who had long ago stopped being afraid of anything in the dark, because he had already sold the part of himself that knew how to fear it. "Night's coming down hard, and the mountain doesn't forgive the unsheltered. Follow me. Mind where I mind."

"Where are we going?" Mara asked, pulling Wren — the child — closer against her side. The boy's small hand was ice inside hers. Since crossing out of the valley he had been speaking more, fragments of Bram's life spilling out of him in that dead tongue: *the river with two names, the dog called Sorrel, my mother's hands smelled of beeswax.* Each word made him more real and less a child, and Mara did not know which frightened her more.

"To the shepherd," the man said. "Everyone comes to the shepherd eventually."

Odile shifted at Mara's shoulder. The hedge-witch had barely slept since the Veil's edge, since magic had flooded back into her starved body and the child had screamed in another boy's voice until dawn. Her eyes were rimmed red, and there were new lines around her mouth that hadn't been there a month ago, carved by things she would not name. "And who's the shepherd?" she asked, in the flat tone she used when she already suspected an answer and wanted it said aloud anyway so she could argue with it.

The man smiled. It reached his eyes, which somehow made it worse. "The one who keeps what we couldn't carry."

* * *

The settlement sat in a fold of the mountain like a coin dropped into a purse — a dozen low houses of stone and timber, roofs heavy with snow, chimneys smoking thin and blue. It should have looked welcoming. It looked, instead, like a painting of a village with one detail wrong in every frame. A woman hung laundry with movements too smooth, too practiced, as if grief had once slowed her hands and now something else swung them. A boy of perhaps ten stood at the edge of the yard watching them come, and when a crow startled up from the fence beside him he did not flinch, did not blink, only tracked it with the dull patience of a statue. Mara realized after a moment what was missing: no one here glanced over their shoulders. No one listened for anything behind them. Fear, in all its small daily postures, was absent from every body she saw.

"They've traded," Odile murmured, so low only Mara heard. "Pieces of themselves. Look at their shadows."

Mara looked. In the failing light the shadows pooled strange beneath the villagers — some too short, some split oddly at the edges, one woman's shadow bearing a gap at the heart, a hole where a shadow's chest should hold its darkest weight.

"The Undercourt," Mara said. Not a question.

"Aye." Odile spat, delicately, into the snow. "I'd heard of such places. Way-stations for the soul-broke. Folk who went down the mountain whole and came back up missing something they only noticed later." She pulled her patched coat tighter. "We shouldn't stay. We should walk past."

But Wren had stopped walking. The child stood in the trampled snow, head tilted, staring at the settlement with an expression Mara had never seen on that small face — not Bram's memories, not the mute terror of the temple nights, but recognition. Pity. As if the boy saw the hollow places in these people the way Mara saw ink errors in a ledger: instantly, helplessly, line by line.

"He wants to stay," Mara said.

"The boy doesn't want anything," Odile snapped. Then she caught herself, and her voice cracked along its middle. "Forgive me. I'm tired, and this place puts teeth in my spine. But Mara — listen to me. These are people who made bargains. Every bargain with that court has a hook in it somewhere. We're carrying exactly what they'd all want most, standing right here in the open." She nodded, grimly, at Wren. "A soul. Free and clear. Do you understand what that's worth to them?"

Mara did. She thought of her ledger years in the scriptorium, the neat columns of debt and payment, how a number owed was never merely a number — it was a leash. And she looked down at Wren, whose cold hand gripped hers with the absolute trust of someone who could not speak to say otherwise, and she heard herself answer:

"We stay one night. Behind locked doors. And in the morning we go."

* * *

The shepherd's house was larger than the others, built into the hillside itself, and warm in the way of a place where the fire is fed by habit rather than hope. Their guide left them at the door with a bow that was almost courtly. Inside, the shepherd was already pouring tea.

She was old in a way that didn't match her face — her skin smooth, her eyes ancient, as though time had been traded off her body and settled elsewhere. She wore grey wool and no ornament, and she moved through her single room with the economy of someone who owned nothing that could be repossessed. When she set three cups on the table she placed Wren's first, without being told there was a child, without looking.

"Sit," she said. "You're the scribe, the witch, and the vessel. I'm Mother Cassia, though shepherd serves as well. Ask your questions while the tea steeps. Afterward you won't want to talk."

"How do you know us?" Mara asked.

"I know everyone who comes down this road toward the gate. They come in threes or they don't come at all — one who owes, one who knows, one who's owed for." She nodded at each of them in turn. "You're the owed-for. The interesting one." Her gaze settled on Wren, gentle and terrible. "Or rather, the boy inside him is. Sit, child of the valley. Drink. Nothing here will take from you tonight; I forbid it, and the house obeys."

There was a weight to the words that made Mara believe them absolutely. She sat. The tea tasted of pine smoke and something sweeter underneath, something that loosened the knot between her shoulders by one careful turn.

"Why do they call you shepherd?" Odile asked. She had not touched her cup. Of course she hadn't.

"Because I gather them." Mother Cassia folded her hands. "The ones who come back up from the court missing pieces. Courage. Grief. Memory. The ability to weep, in one poor woman's case — she came back able to feel sorrow perfectly well, you understand, just unable to let any of it out. She spent six years smiling at funerals before she found her way to me." She spoke without judgment, without pity, in the tone of a woman reciting a harvest count. "Someone must keep watch over the hollow ones. Someone must remember what they sold, since half of them can't. That's my trade now. I was a midwife, once. Delivering souls instead of children isn't so different. Both arrive screaming. Both change everything they touch."

"And yours?" Odile asked. "What did you trade?"

For the first time the shepherd paused. "My hope of getting anything back," she said. "It seemed prudent at the time. You cannot be cheated out of a thing you have already relinquished." She turned to Mara then, and her ancient eyes did the thing that old eyes do — stripped away pretense, read the pages beneath the page. "You've seen the archive."

Mara's cup stopped halfway to her mouth. "How—"

"You flinched when I said *trade.* Not puzzled. Flinched. And your hands—" Cassia gestured lightly. "Ink under the nails that scrubbing won't lift. Archive ink stains differently than temple ink. Deeper. It gets into the quick." She leaned back. "So you know. You've walked among the shelves. Tell me, scribe — what did you see there?"

Mara set the cup down because her hand had begun to shake and she would not let them see it. She thought of the vast dark hall, the shelves that climbed beyond lamplight, the clerks with their quiet voices and their ledgers full of everything anyone had ever surrendered. She thought of a contract with a seller's mark she had traced with her fingertip again and again in the sleepless dark, unable to stop touching it, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

*I saw my grandmother's name,* she did not say. *I saw that everything my family owns was bought with a child.*

"I saw that justice there is arithmetic," she said instead. "Nothing more. Nothing moral. Things balance, or they don't. People are line items."

"Yes." Cassia closed her eyes briefly, as if the confirmation cost her something. "Then you're ahead of most. Most come down that road believing the court can be persuaded. Argued with. Moved by suffering." She opened her eyes. "It cannot. I'll tell you what I tell every hollow one who passes through, and I'll tell you freely, because you seem like folk who pay attention, and attention is the only currency the court respects and the only one it can't confiscate."

* * *

What Mother Cassia told them, she told plainly, in the voice of a woman laying out bones on a table so the shape of the animal could be seen.

"The court does not lie to you. Understand that first — it's the trap. Everything it tells you is true. *You will live longer.* True. *Your grief will end.* True. *Your debts will be forgiven.* True, in the letter, always in the letter. What it never says is what the truth will cost in context. The man who sold his courage — Tam, he sleeps in the third house — he asked to stop being afraid of his creditors. He lives longer now, yes. He works harder, fears nothing, and last winter he watched his own daughter fall through the ice and walked toward her at the same pace he walks everywhere, because urgency is a cousin of fear, and he sold the whole family of it." She lifted her cup at last and drank, unhurried. "They fished her out. She lived. He stood on the bank the entire time with his lantern that gives no light, and he felt — nothing wrong. That's the horror of it, scribe. Not pain. Absence. He came to me after and asked me what was wrong with him, and I had to tell him: nothing. Nothing at all. That's what's wrong."

Wren made a sound. Small, high, the first noise the child had made since entering the house. Everyone turned. The boy was looking at Tam's lantern, still hanging by their guide's abandoned cloak near the door, and tears were running silently down his small face — tears, Mara realized with a lurch, that were not entirely the child's own. Something in Bram remembered fear. Something in Bram grieved for the man who could not.

"Hush, love," Mara whispered, gathering him in.

"Don't hush him." The shepherd's voice was soft but absolute. "He's the only honest crier in the room. Let him teach you." She turned back to Mara and Odile both. "Here is what I need you to carry down the mountain, and I'll only say it once. The court will offer you a way to save the boy. There is always a way; the court loves a transaction, and transactions require two parties. But hear me." She set down her cup with a click like a small door closing. "Every path it offers you ends with you owing it. That is not cruelty. That is architecture. The court is built the way a web is built — every thread leads to the center, and the center is appetite. If you leave that place having paid only what you planned to pay, you will be the first in recorded history. So before you cross the threshold — decide, now, sober, in advance — what you are willing to lose. All of it. Name it to yourself and mean it. Because the moment you stand in that archive with the contract in front of you, you will not be able to think. The shelves think for you. The ledgers lean on you. I have known scholars who went down sharp as knives and came back signing away their childhoods to cover a fare."

Odile's jaw was tight. "You talk like someone who tried to fight it."

"I talk like someone who watched better people than me try." For a moment the shepherd's composure thinned, and Mara glimpsed beneath it something vast and worn smooth, like a stone the sea has worked for centuries. "I had a sister. Cleverer than me, kinder than me. She went down to buy back her husband's years. She budgeted for it, scribe. Drew up terms in her own hand, fair ones, generous ones. The court accepted every clause." Cassia looked at the fire. "She came back up with his years in a jar and none of her love for him. She'd written *love of husband* into her collateral without noticing. The clerk pointed it out to her afterward, very politely, and complimented her penmanship." The pause stretched. "She gave the jar back. Walked straight down and gave it back, and the court kept both — the years and the love — because returning a purchase is not a refund, and she had failed to write that clause. She's still down there, I think. Or some filing of her is."

Silence held the room. The fire ticked. Outside, wind worried the eaves.

Mara became aware that Wren had gone rigid against her side — not weeping now, but trembling, and his lips were moving, shaping words in the dead tongue over and over, a phrase she had not heard before tonight. She caught it by rhythm if not meaning, four syllables rising and falling like something rehearsed across years, a prayer or a promise, and when she looked at Mother Cassia she found the old woman watching the child with an expression that had finally, at last, cracked into something human.

"What's he saying?" Odile asked hoarsely.

"I don't know," Cassia said. "But I know this much, witch: I have sat in this room with forty hollow souls, and I have never once seen the hollow ones weep for anyone but themselves." She rose, joints cracking like green wood, and took a candle from the shelf. "You'll have the back room. It locks from inside. Sleep if you can — and scribe, a word before you go, just you."

* * *

At the door to the back room, with Odile and the sleeping-weighted child already settling onto the narrow bed, Mother Cassia caught Mara's sleeve. Her grip was stronger than it should have been.

"You recognized the mark on that contract," she said quietly. "The seller's mark. Don't tell me — your face did already, days ago, I'd wager, and it hasn't healed." Her ancient eyes searched Mara's. "Listen to me, girl. I've delivered enough souls to know how blood-debts work. The court holds family lines the way a miller holds grain — it grinds slowly, generation by generation, and it always saves the last of the line for last. If your grandmother sold that boy, then you are not merely his rescuer. You are the outstanding balance."

Mara's throat closed. "What are you saying?"

"I'm saying the court may not want you to pay for him." The shepherd's voice dropped to almost nothing. "I'm saying it may already consider you *paid with.* Decide what you're willing to lose, Mara Thornbury. Tonight. Before the mountain decides for you."

She released Mara's sleeve, took up her lightless lantern from its peg, and walked back into the dark of her keeping-house, and Mara stood alone in the doorway hearing the words turn over and over like the ledger-pages of a book she could not stop reading — until, from the bed behind her, Wren stirred and spoke clearly in his sleep, in plain valley speech, in a boy's voice that was not the child's own:

"*Tell her the debt collector already knows her name.*"
