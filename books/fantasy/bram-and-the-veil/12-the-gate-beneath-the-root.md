## 12. The Gate Beneath the Root

Ferren had led pilgrims to the mountain before. He had led lovers fleeing disapproving families, debtors fleeing less forgiving creditors, and once — he did not like to remember it — a woman carrying a jar of grave-dirt and insisting it was her mother. He knew every kind of fear the human face could make when it looked up at Mount Caelum: the white-eyed terror of the hunted, the hungry terror of the desperate, the quiet terror of people who already knew what waited for them at the top and had decided to go anyway.

He had never seen anything quite like what the scribe woman's face was doing.

Mara Thornbury walked three steps behind him with a child on her hip and her chin set like a chisel against stone. She wasn't terrified. She wasn't desperate. She was *counting* — Ferren could see it in her eyes, the way they ticked from landmark to landmark, from his shoulders to his hands to the knife at his belt, tallying everything, filing everything away in that ledger of a mind she carried behind her forehead. She was memorizing him. She thought he didn't notice.

He noticed. It made him want to laugh and also made him deeply uneasy, which was, he reflected, roughly the correct way to feel about scribes. In Ferren's long experience of the world's professions, the dangerous ones were never the soldiers or the priests. Soldiers killed you quickly and priests killed you slowly, but a clerk would unmake your whole life with one well-placed line of ink and then ask you to confirm the spelling of your name.

The child was worse. The child said nothing — hadn't said a word since the tollhouse — but watched Ferren with eyes too old and too still for that small face, and twice now, when the wind came down off the glacier in its long grey breaths, the child's mouth had moved silently, shaping sounds Ferren was glad not to hear.

And the witch — Odile — she talked enough for all of them, which Ferren supposed was how hedge-witches survived: by filling silence with chatter so the silence couldn't fill itself back with magic. She'd been talking about lichen. For an hour. Lichen. He'd learned more about lichen than any man needed to know outside of a famine.

"Third time you've checked that rope," Odile observed now, falling into step beside him as the path narrowed along a shelf of black rock. Below them the valley floor lay wrapped in evening haze, small and warm-looking as a hearth. "If it were going to fail, love, it would have done it while we were hanging off it."

"Ropes fail slow," Ferren said. "That's the trick of them. They look fine right up until they aren't."

"Mm." She glanced sideways at him. "Like guides?"

Ferren smiled without showing teeth. "Exactly like guides."

* * *

They made camp in the mouth of a cave where an old avalanche had bitten into the mountainside, and Ferren built a fire small enough to hide under his cupped hands if Wardens' lanterns swept the slopes below. The cordon was still down there somewhere — Hesk's men strung across the passes like beads on a wire — but the smugglers' routes ran above the roads and below the patrols, through cracks in the world that only existed because nobody official had ever bothered to look at them.

Mara fed the child dried plums and watched Ferren over the fire.

"You're going to leave us," she said. Not accusingly. Almost conversationally, the way one might note that it looked like rain.

Ferren kept his eyes on the flames. "Am I?"

"At the gate. You'll take us to the threshold and no further. You won't come inside." She turned a plum over in her fingers. "I've been reading you since the tollhouse. You keep touching your left breast pocket — the one with the charm in it, the one you haven't burned. Everything else about you is practical, but that pocket is sentiment, and sentiment means you have a line you don't cross. Guides who take people to the Undercourt and walk in with them don't come out with their names intact. You know that. So you'll stop at the door."

The fire popped. Somewhere far below, ice cracked off a cornice with a sound like a distant bell.

Ferren looked at her for a long moment. Then he laughed — a short, genuine bark of a laugh that startled even him. "Gods preserve me. You *are* an auditor."

"I'm a scribe."

"In the Undercourt there's no difference." He fed a twist of bark to the flames. "You're right, of course. I'll take you to the root and not one stride past it. That was the bargain. My conscience stays on this side of the door, and my purse gets heavier, and everyone goes home happy or doesn't, but either way it isn't mine to carry."

"And if we die in there?" Odile asked. She was sharpening a knife that didn't need sharpening; she'd been doing it all day, and Ferren understood it was less about the blade than about keeping her hands busy so they wouldn't start drawing sigils in the dirt. "Doesn't that weigh on the conscience? Just a little?"

Ferren considered the question honestly, because she'd asked it honestly, and because there was nothing left to lie about now — they were two days past the last point where lying would have helped anyone.

"I've carried eleven people to that gate," he said. "Four came back out. One of those four I'd call lucky. The others came back *changed* — missing things, though they couldn't always say what. And every one of them chose to knock on that door. I don't open doors for people. I just show them where they are." He nodded at the child, asleep against Mara's shoulder, breathing in the shallow, careful rhythm of something that wasn't entirely sure it wanted to be alive. "That little one didn't choose anything. That's the part that sits wrong with me. But it's not wrong enough to make me walk in there, and I'll thank you not to pretend otherwise."

"No one's pretending," Mara said quietly. "We're just asking you to know what you're walking away from."

"Oh, I know it." Ferren pulled his blanket around his shoulders and settled back against the cold stone. "That's the trick of me, scribe. I know exactly what I'm walking away from. Been doing it my whole life. I'm very good at it."

But sleep took its time finding him, and when it did, he dreamed of a door made of roots, and of knocking on it from the *outside*, forever, while voices on the other side counted something that sounded terribly like his own name.

* * *

They reached the hollow on the third morning, in that hour when the dawn light comes down blue and grainy as watered milk.

Ferren had described it to them — he always described it, partly to prepare them and partly because describing it aloud reminded *him* that it was real and not some fever he'd caught young — but description had failed him, as he'd known it would. There was no preparing a person for the tree.

It stood in a fold of the mountain where no tree should grow, above the treeline, above the scree, in ground that was more stone than soil. It was dead — had been dead, by Ferren's guess, longer than kingdoms — and yet its roots lived on in the only sense that mattered, plunging down and down through the mountain's bones, vast as rivers, silver-grey and smooth as poured metal where they broke the surface. Over centuries the earth beneath them had washed away, or been eaten away, or perhaps simply *given up*, until the roots arched overhead like the ribs of some buried cathedral and the space beneath became a hollow, a hall, a throat.

And down at the bottom of the throat, half-swallowed in living wood, was the gate.

It wasn't grand. That was the thing that undid first-timers, Ferren had learned — they expected the Undercourt's door to be terrible, and instead it was merely *exact*. A rectangle of darkness perhaps eight feet high, framed in root-wood carved so finely with script that the letters seemed to move when you weren't looking directly at them. No hinges. No handle. Nothing so vulgar as a knocker. Just a doorway standing in the middle of nowhere, in the belly of a dead god of a tree, breathing a faint cold air that smelled of paper and iron and, underneath it all, of deep places where water drips onto stone in the dark.

The child went rigid in Mara's arms.

Then the child screamed.

It was not a child's scream. It came out low and cracked and full-grown, a young man's voice torn raw with terror, and the words in it were none that belonged to any living tongue — except that Mara stiffened as though each one had struck her body like thrown stones, and Ferren saw her lips moving, translating, catching fragments the way a man catches knives.

"What's it saying?" Odile demanded, hands already glowing faintly, hedge-light leaking between her fingers like blood through a bandage.

"'Don't—'" Mara's voice shook. "'Don't count—don't let them finish counting—'" She pressed her cheek to the child's hair. "Bram. Bram, it's Mara. We're here. We're going in *with* you. Nobody's leaving anybody."

*Except me,* Ferren thought. *Somebody is leaving somebody. Me.*

The child — Bram — the boy — whatever he was, whoever he was, the poor wracked vessel of a soul sold like a sack of grain — turned his head and looked at Ferren. Directly. With those old, still eyes. And though the voice that had screamed was a grown man's, the look was a child's, the naked accusatory look of a child who has just learned that adults lie about the important things.

Ferren held the gaze for as long as he could, which was not long.

"The path ends here," he said, and his voice came out rougher than he intended. He cleared his throat. "Gate opens for blood or bargain. Don't touch the frame. Don't say your true name within earshot of it — whisper it, mumble it, swallow it if you can. Once you're through, keep left; there's a gallery, a corridor of shelves, follow the sound of writing. Somebody will find you. Somebody always finds you. They can smell intent through that door like sharks smell blood." He dug in his pack and pressed a stub of tallow candle into Mara's hand. "Burns cold. Light it when your eyes start playing tricks. The dark in there isn't empty; it's *full*, and your eyes will try to make shapes of it."

"Why are you telling us all this?" Odile asked softly. "You said you don't open doors."

"I'm not opening it. I'm telling you which parts of it bite." He shouldered his pack and stepped back, one pace, two, putting the roots between himself and them — no, putting himself outside the roots, outside the arch, on the side of the world where things stayed named and solid and paid their debts in coin instead of souls. "This is where I get off. As agreed."

Mara set the child down, keeping a hand on his shoulder, and faced Ferren across ten feet of frozen ground. She didn't plead. He'd half expected pleading; he'd armored himself against it, rehearsed his refusals on the climb. Instead she studied him with those ledger-keeper's eyes one last time, and then she inclined her head — a small, precise, scribe's bow, the kind given to a colleague across a desk.

"You showed us where the door is," she said. "That's more than most people ever do for anyone."

"It's less than it looks," Ferren said.

"Most things are." She lifted the child's hand and closed the small fingers around the candle. "Come on, Bram. Left corridor. Sound of writing."

They walked toward the gate — the witch with her knife drawn and her jaw set, the scribe with her spine straight as a ruled line, the child stumbling between them with his face wet and his fists clenched. At the threshold Mara stopped. Turned back once.

"Ferren. If a Warden-Captain named Hesk asks you which way we went—"

"He'll ask me, and I'll sell him the truth at a fair price, and by the time he's haggled me down you'll be three galleries deep and beyond anyone's reach." Ferren spread his hands. "My conscience and my purse stay separate, lady. That's the whole of my religion."

Something that might have been a smile crossed her face — brief, tired, real. Then she turned, and the three of them stood before the exact and patient darkness, and Odile said something in her low working-magic voice, and the gate — which had no handle, no hinge, no knocker — opened anyway, folding inward like a page turning, and the cold paper-and-iron breath of the deep places rolled out over the snow.

They stepped through. The gate folded shut behind them. The root-script stilled.

And Ferren Colm, smuggler, guide, keeper of a carefully separated conscience, stood alone under the ribs of the dead tree with a heavier purse and a lighter heart and told himself both of those things were true.

He made it eleven strides back down the trail before his legs stopped working.

He sat down hard on a rock and stared at the place where the gate had been, and he thought about the child's eyes, and about the four who had come back changed, and about the seven who had not come back at all, whose names he had never asked because asking would have put them in his ledger, and Ferren had spent forty years making absolutely certain his ledger stayed clean.

Eleven people. Four out. He'd never once gone back to find out which four.

Until now he had never once wanted to.

Far below, down through the blue morning haze, a speck of movement caught his eye — riders on the mountain road, lanterns winking even in daylight, a thin line of them climbing with the patient inevitability of water finding its level. Veilwardens. And at the head of them, riding a horse too good for him, a big square-shouldered man whose posture even at this distance spoke of duty worn like armor over something softer.

Hesk. Coming on fast, following the trail Ferren had left for him to find — the trail Ferren had promised, out loud, to sell him.

Ferren got up. Brushed off his breeches. Looked once more at the silent gate in the roots, then at the riders winding up the road, doing sums in his head the way the scribe would have done them, weighing prices and distances and the odds that a bought lie would hold long enough to matter.

Then he turned and started down the slope — toward the road, toward Hesk, toward the sale he'd sworn to make — and found, to his considerable annoyance, that he was already composing the lie.
