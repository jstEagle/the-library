## 13. Court of Counted Things

The dark did not end. That was the first thing Mara understood, and the thing she would carry with her longest: the dark beneath the mountain was not an absence but a *place*, and it had architecture.

They descended for what felt like a day and might have been a year. The root-passage Ferren had abandoned them at narrowed and widened like a throat swallowing, and the walls were not wood anymore — they had become something between bone and paper, veined with lines of faint gold script that moved when she looked away from them and settled again when she looked back, like insects pretending to be still. Odile walked close behind her, one hand on Mara's shoulder so they would not lose each other. The child rode in a sling against Odile's chest, and he did not cry, though his breathing came quick and shallow, and once — only once — he said a word in that dead tongue, soft, like a man in sleep asking after someone he loved.

Mara had stopped trying to count turns of the passage. She counted other things instead, because counting was what she had. Steps. Breath. The number of times her heel caught a root. Four hundred and eleven steps before the gold script began to glow brighter. Six hundred before she heard the sound.

It took her a long moment to identify it, because it was a sound from her old life, transposed into this impossible place: the rustle of pages. Thousands upon thousands of them, turning all at once, everywhere at once, a sound like wind through an infinite wheat field made of vellum.

Then the passage opened, and she saw.

* * *

There was no sky. There was no ceiling either, or if there was, it stood so far above that the lamplight failed to reach it, and the darkness overhead was not empty but crowded — shelves, tier upon tier of shelving, rising out of sight, each level hung with lamps whose flames burned without color. The archive floor stretched before her in long avenues of desks, and at every desk sat a figure writing.

She had expected monsters. She realized, standing there with snowmelt still drying in the seams of her boots, that she had expected the Undercourt to be a court — a throne, a judge, chains, screaming. What she saw instead was a *scriptorium*. It was the temple's copying hall scaled up past reason, past nightmare, past anything the architects of Mount Caelum's temple could have imagined, and every desk held a clerk bent over a ledger, and every ledger held a life.

"Everything is filed here," Odile whispered. Her voice had gone strange — smaller, and older, as if the air itself pressed years onto whoever breathed it. "I told you. Everything. Not coin. Coin is nothing. They trade in what things actually are."

A clerk detached itself from the nearest avenue and came toward them, and Mara's heart slammed once, hard, and then went oddly calm, because the clerk was only — a person. A thin person in grey robes gone greenish under the lamp-light, with ink-stained fingers and spectacles perched on a nose that seemed assembled from spare parts of several noses. Its eyes were the worst thing, and even those were merely tired: the deep, patient tiredness of someone who has read everything twice.

"Arrivals," the clerk said. It did not ask a question; it stated a category, the way one writes a heading before filling in the entries beneath. "State your holdings."

Mara opened her mouth to say *we have nothing*, and the scribe in her caught the words before they left her tongue. Nothing was a filing error. Nothing would be corrected, and corrections in this place would be made by hands other than hers.

"I am Mara Thornbury," she said, and heard her own voice come out flat and official, the voice she used for tithes and ledgers. "Debt-bound scribe of the temple of Caelum Valley, fourteen years served of twenty. These are my dependents." A pause, half a heartbeat, while something in her chest tore and was immediately filed away. "I'm traveling under audit authority."

The clerk's pen stopped moving. It had been writing the whole time she spoke — she could see the entries appearing in its ledger without the pen touching paper — and now it looked up over its spectacles.

"Audit authority," it repeated. "From whom?"

"The Office of Irregular Holdings," Mara said.

There is a particular silence that follows a lie told well, and Mara knew it intimately, because she had spent fourteen years listening for it in others' voices across the tithe-desk. This silence lasted three full seconds. Then the clerk turned to a fresh page.

"Name."

"Mara Thornbury."

"Debt?"

"Six years remaining, temple-bound, interest accruing annually at the rate of—"

"We don't use the temple's rates," the clerk said, with the first flicker of something almost like distaste. "We use ours. Continue."

"Dependent one: male child, age indeterminate, mute by contract—" she invented it smoothly, hating herself and marveling at herself in the same breath, "—speech rights currently held elsewhere. Dependent two: Odile of the hedge, profession revoked, assets: charms, burnt; reputation: forfeit."

Odile made a small sound beside her that might have been outrage or might have been laughter strangled at birth.

"Purpose of entry," said the clerk.

"Investigation of an irregular holding," Mara said. "A soul-account, misfiled. I'm given to understand your records show a discrepancy, and discrepancies are my entire profession. I count things. I find where the counts go wrong." She met the clerk's tired eyes and did not blink, because she had spent fourteen years not blinking at men who owed money and wanted her to look away first. "If I'm wrong about the discrepancy, you may file me wherever you file people who waste the court's time. If I'm right, then somewhere in this building there's a ledger that doesn't balance, and I imagine—" here she took the smallest risk of her life, "—that bothers you."

The silence this time lasted longer. Somewhere in the vast dark above them, a page turned by itself.

"Clerks do not feel bother," the clerk said at last. But it wrote something down, and it gestured with its pen toward the avenues of desks, and it said, "Auditor Thornbury will be assigned a reading desk. Access: restricted stacks, third gallery. Dependents will be held in the waiting alcove."

"They stay with me," Mara said. "I don't work without my references present."

Another silence. Another entry. And then, in a tone that Mara would think about for many nights afterward — a tone that was almost, almost respect — the clerk said:

"Fourteen years at a tithe-desk. You'll do well here. That's the tragedy of it."

* * *

Her desk was real oak, older than any tree in the valley, and the chair fit her back as though it had been measured for a woman exactly her height who had never existed. From it she could see three avenues of clerks, the base of the shelf-tiers vanishing upward into lamplit fog, and, to her left, the waiting alcove where Odile sat with the child, behind a rail of black metal that hummed faintly, the way a struck bell hums long after the sound should be gone.

The restricted stacks of the third gallery were brought to her in wheeled carts by clerks who did not speak, and she learned quickly not to thank them — thanks, she gathered, was a transaction here too, and created obligations, and obligations created entries, and entries were how this place ate you.

So she worked.

Gods forgive her, she *worked*, and it felt like coming home. She had thought, walking down through the roots, that she was entering a monster's belly. Instead she had entered the largest accounting house in existence, and every skill the temple had ground into her over fourteen years of debt-slavery turned out to be currency here. She reconciled columns of holdings that had not been reconciled since kingdoms fell. She found a double-entry error in a shipment of memories — forty years of summers belonging to a shepherd dead nine centuries, recorded twice, claimed twice, being litigated between two branches of the court — and flagged it, and the flag was accepted, and a senior clerk paused by her desk and looked at her for a moment the way her old mistress used to look at a page of clean figures.

"You count the way we count," the senior clerk said. It was neither praise nor threat. It was worse than both: it was recognition.

"What happens to the discrepancies?" Mara asked, keeping her eyes on her columns. "When you find them. The double-claimed summers. The misfiled names. What does the court do?"

"The court corrects," the senior clerk said. "Correction is justice. Justice is balance. Balance is the whole of the law." It drifted away down the avenue, robes whispering, and Mara stared after it and felt cold settle into her bones, because she understood now, truly understood, why Odile had warned her: there was no cruelty here to fight. There was no villain to expose. There was only arithmetic, perfect and pitiless, and arithmetic did not care whether Bram deserved his soul back. Arithmetic cared only that the numbers balanced — and by the court's own ledgers, the numbers already did.

Unless they didn't.

That was the word the clerk had accepted without question, the crack she had talked her way through the door on: *discrepancy*. Somewhere in these endless shelves was the account of a boy named Bram, sold by a mark she hadn't yet seen, held under terms she hadn't yet read — and if there was one truth Mara Thornbury had learned at the tithe-desk, it was that every ledger, no matter how holy, no matter how ancient, no matter how vast, was written by hands.

And hands erred. Hands grew tired. Hands could be guided.

She pulled the next cart toward her and began, methodically, terribly patiently, to search.

* * *

The child was brought to her at intervals — she learned the intervals were called visitation windows, and that they were themselves entries, billable ones — and each time he climbed into her lap among the ledgers and went still, pressing his ear against her chest as though listening for something. The second time, he took her pen-hand in both of his small ones and moved it across the blotting paper, tracing shapes. Letters. Dead letters, from a language older than kingdoms.

She knew four of the five characters. They were in the forbidden pre-kingdom text she'd pored over in the temple's sealed case, the night this all began: *the vessel keeps what the owner gave.*

The fifth character she did not know. She copied it carefully into the margin of her working notes, and the ink seemed to sink into the page more slowly than ink should, as though the paper were tasting it.

On her ninth session in the archive — she stopped measuring days; the lamps never changed — she found the index.

It was not hidden. That was the horror of the Undercourt: nothing was hidden, because nothing needed to be. The index of soul-holdings stood open on the end-cap of the third gallery's last aisle, a book the size of a door, chained not to prevent theft but apparently to prevent it *falling on someone*. She ran her finger down the B folios with her heart going slow and enormous, and there it was, in copperplate finer than any temple hand could produce:

**BRAM — [surname redacted by original contract] — soul, whole, unencumbered at time of sale. Vendor: private party. Purchasing arm: Court of Counted Things, Office of Held Persons. Status: ACTIVE. Term: thirteen lunar turns, vessel-dependent.**

Vendor: private party. No name. Of course no name — the seller had paid, surely, for anonymity; anonymity was a holding like any other, purchasable, filed. But contracts had marks, and marks were not names, and marks could not easily be bought off a page.

She needed the contract itself. The index gave her the call number: **HP-114-BRAM**, held stack, fourth gallery, *restricted access — auditor petition required*.

Petition required. Which meant forms. Which meant a queue. Which meant time, and the moon overhead — the real moon, the thirteenth-turn moon, rising somewhere in the world of weather and snow that she had traded for lamplight — was running out.

She was copying the call number onto a petition slip when she felt it: a change in the light of the colorless lamps, a ripple down the avenues, clerks lifting their heads all at once like a flock of birds. Down the great central aisle came a procession of six figures in robes the grey of ash, and between them, bound in cords of black metal humming the same note as the alcove rail, walked—

Odile.

Her hands were tied. Her chin was up. There was blood at her hairline, dried brown, and her eyes swept the avenues of desks with the fierce, contemptuous attention of a woman taking inventory of her captors, and Mara understood in one lurching instant what had happened: the diversion. Odile had made her diversion, bought Mara her hours of undisturbed searching, and paid for it in the only coin this place respected.

And then Odile's gaze crossed Mara's desk, and passed over her, and returned, and narrowed — and Mara watched, with her heart cracking cleanly in two, as the hedge-witch's face performed the small, deliberate blankness of a stranger looking at a stranger. *Don't know me,* Odile's eyes said. *You're an auditor. Auditors don't know criminals. Play your part or we've both spent ourselves for nothing.*

Mara bent back over her columns. Her pen did not shake. She had fourteen years of practice at not letting them see the pen shake, and for the first time in her life she was grateful for every one of them.

The petition slip lay finished under her hand. **Requesting retrieval of record HP-114-BRAM, for audit purposes. Auditor: M. Thornbury. Reason: suspected discrepancy in vendor attribution.**

Suspected discrepancy in vendor attribution. She had written it before she fully knew why, following the instinct that had kept her alive since the scriptorium door: find where the counts go wrong. Because a soul sold thirteen turns ago, by a private party whose name the court itself had filed away — that was not a clean transaction. That was a thread, and threads, pulled, unraveled.

She slid the slip across her desk to the collection tray, and watched it sink into the wood as though the desk were water.

Somewhere above her, past tiers of shelving beyond sight, past the false dark where the real moon was climbing toward its final turn, a bell rang once — a soundless bell, a bell that rang in entries rather than air — and the tray accepted her petition.

Now, she thought, bending back to her columns with steady hands and a heart hammering like a trapped bird, now we learn who sold a child.

The tray, silent, gave back one word in return, inked in copperplate on a slip of pale vellum that had not been there a moment before:

***Pending.***
