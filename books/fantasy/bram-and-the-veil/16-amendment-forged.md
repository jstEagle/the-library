## 16. Amendment, Forged

The Undercourt did not sleep, and so neither could its forger.

Mara learned this on her third night in the archive, when she discovered that the lamps along the shelving aisles burned without oil and the clerks at their high desks turned pages with hands that had been dead longer than her grandmother. The Court of Counted Things kept its hours the way a miser keeps coin: jealously, endlessly, every moment accounted for and none of them wasted on rest. Somewhere above the archive — or below it; direction meant nothing beneath the root — a bell tolled the hour, and no one looked up.

She had been an auditor for six days now. Long enough to know where the contract ledgers lived: the deep stacks, past the shelves of forfeited years and the drawers of unlived lives, in a vault whose lock answered only to a clerk's mark pressed in wax. Long enough to have memorized the rotation of the night wardens, two figures in grey who walked the aisles with candle-stubs that guttered but never went out. Long enough to be trusted, which was the strangest part. Her forged credentials had passed inspection because they were not forged at all — she had simply written herself into existence as what she claimed to be, in the court's own hand, in the court's own ink, and the court believed its own records the way the devout believe scripture.

*Auditor Third Class, Thornbury.* She had chosen the surname deliberately. If she was going to lie, she would lie close to herself.

Now, on the sixth night, she knelt before the vault door with a stolen wax stylus trembling in her fingers, and told herself the tremor was the cold.

The seal took the impression of the sleeping clerk's mark cleanly. The door swung inward on hinges that made no sound at all, and the smell that came out was old paper and something sweeter underneath, like fruit going soft in a cellar. Contracts. Thousands of them. Each one a soul's price set down in a hand steadier than any living scribe's.

*Bram.*

She found him under *B*, between a ferryman who had sold his fear of drowning and a bride who had sold her first love for a dowry. His contract lay flat in its drawer, and when Mara lifted it into the lamplight, the paper was warm, like a sleeping animal.

It was short. That was the first cruelty. She had expected pages, clauses, the baroque machinery of damnation. Instead there were four lines.

*Soul of Bram Hollis, aged nine years, tendered by the undersigned. Term of service: thirteen turns of the moon, renewable at the pleasure of the Court. Vessel provisioned per clause seven. Consideration received in full.*

Below it, the seller's mark: *S. Thornbury.* Her grandmother's name, in her grandmother's hand. Mara had copied that hand ten thousand times in the temple scriptorium, practicing letters from a woman who had died believing herself respectable. Below the name, the consideration itself, itemized with the court's terrible precision:

*Received: one valley, charmed against ingress; one bloodline, prospered; one debt of grain, forgiven.*

One valley. The Veil itself — the magicless peace the whole world praised — had been the purchase price. Her family had not merely sold a child's soul. They had built the prison that hid him from his own rescue, and then they had lived comfortably inside it for sixty years while he screamed in a borrowed body in the snow.

Mara sat down on the vault floor with the contract in her lap and let the shaking come, properly, for the first time since the temple. When it passed, she unrolled her kit: blank amendment leaves cut to the court's exact dimensions, ink mixed from the archive's own well, a pen she had ground from a raven quill Odile had given her at the gate — *for luck,* Odile had said, *and because ravens are thieves and so are you now.*

An amendment, she knew from six days of reading, was a small mercy the court permitted itself. Contracts could be amended if both parties consented, or if one party was dead and the other party's interest could be established. Bram's soul had no voice here. But the vessel clause — clause seven — specified that the vessel must survive thirteen turns, and the moon was nearly full, and a contract that failed through the court's own negligence could be amended by any party acting to preserve the court's investment.

So she would amend. She would write Bram out of service and into release, forge the countersignature of a magistrate three aisles away who had not moved in a hundred years, and walk out of the vault with a boy's soul folded into her sleeve.

It should have worked. Every element was correct. She had checked the letter-forms against a hundred genuine amendments until her eyes burned. She wrote the first line in the dark of the vault, by lamplight shielded behind her back:

*Amendment the First to Contract B-1149: the term of service herein is concluded—*

And the ledger woke.

She felt it before she understood it — a pressure behind her eyes, a taste of copper, the sense of being *read*. The contract in her lap grew cold as riverbed stone. Ink crawled on the page beside her pen, letters rearranging themselves with the dry patience of insects, and where she had written *concluded*, the word dissolved and reformed:

*Falsified.*

Then, beneath it, in the same dispassionate hand, a second line appeared:

*The Court accepts true statements only. Value tendered must equal value owed. What is offered falsely is void. What is offered truly binds.*

Mara stared at it until the words blurred. She tried again — rewrote the clause, varied the phrasing, mimicked the magistrate's slant and spacing down to the little hitch he made before every terminal flourish. *Falsified. Falsified. Falsified.* Each refusal struck her like a hand laid flat on her sternum, gentle and absolute. The ledgers did not detect lies the way a temple elder detected a crooked tithe row. They detected them the way skin detects a blade. There was no fooling them because there was nothing in them to fool; they were not records *of* truth, they were truth, filed.

She sat among the contracts until the bell tolled some hour she did not count, and she thought about the shepherd community on the mountain road — the man without his courage, who flinched from shadows and wept without knowing why; the woman who had traded away her grief and so had lost, with it, every memory worth grieving. *The Court does not cheat you,* Odile had translated from the hollow man's halting speech. *That's the horror of it. You get exactly what you asked for, priced exactly right, and you discover too late what you should have asked instead.*

Priced exactly right.

A boy's soul. A whole soul, unsold, restored to a living body, freed of a debt sixty years deep. What equaled that? What did the court weigh souls against? Not gold — gold was filed under *trinkets*. Not years — she had seen years traded by the fistful, cheap as onions. Not labor, not secrets, not the Veil itself, which had already been spent once and spent lavishly.

She thought of the ferryman's fear. The bride's first love. The items people brought here when they had nothing else, the things a person carried that could not be replaced and therefore could not be refused.

A name.

The thought arrived quietly, the way the worst thoughts do, dressed as an ordinary observation. In the intake hall on her second day, she had watched a debtor surrender his name to settle a gambling contract, and she had watched the clerks receive it with visible satisfaction — more satisfaction than they had shown for the man's house, his boat, his teeth. Names were currency here. Not trinkets. Currency. A name was how the court found you, held you, remembered you; a name was the handle by which your whole self could be lifted out of the world and set on a shelf. It was the one thing a person owned that the court valued above all others, because a name freely given could not be reclaimed, disputed, or forged.

*A name freely given.*

Her pen hovered over the amendment leaf. She understood, suddenly and completely, why the ledgers rejected her forgery — not as failure but as instruction. The court was not telling her *no*. The court was telling her the price, over and over, in the only language it spoke. *Value tendered must equal value owed.* It had never once said the value could not come from her.

She laughed — a small, cracked sound in the vast silence of the vault, and somewhere in the dark stacks something turned a page.

No. Not yet. There had to be another way; there was always another way; Odile would find one, Odile was finding one even now somewhere above her, trading secrets to magistrates to buy time. Mara gathered her kit with steady hands and left the amendment unfinished, unsigned, tucked inside the false binding of an audit ledger where no eye would look. She walked the long aisle back toward the clerks' desks, past the shelf of forfeited names — she made herself look, made herself see the little drawers, hundreds of them, each labeled in a hand like frost — and she memorized the layout of the intake hall, the wording of the surrender rites, the exact phrase the clerks required:

*Freely given, fully owned, irrevocable upon signature.*

At the edge of the archive, where the lamplight thinned into root-shadow, she stopped and pressed her palm flat against the cold stone wall, and let herself feel the size of it. Thirteen turns. The moon would complete its circle in five days. Bram — the child, the vessel, the boy who said one word in the snow — had perhaps five days of borrowed breath left unless she paid.

Five days to find another way, or five days to decide.

She climbed the root-stairs toward the chamber where Odile was held, rehearsing cheerful lies she did not yet know how to tell. Behind her, in the deep vault, the unfinished amendment leaf stirred once in the still air, and across its face, in the court's patient hand, a single new line wrote itself — unasked, unprompted, waiting:

*The auditor knows the price. The Court awaits her answer.*

* * *

Odile was sitting cross-legged on the floor of her cell when Mara reached it, sharpening a stick with a shard of pottery, because even imprisoned hedge-witches apparently needed something to do with their hands. She looked up, and her sharp face did its sharp work — reading Mara in one glance the way she read weather, trails, the truth in a smuggler's grin.

"Well," Odile said. "You've found the price."

Mara opened her mouth to deny it, to deflect, to do all the competent lying she had done for six days — and found she could not. Not to this woman. Not with the moon nearly full.

"It can't be me," she said instead. "There has to be something else. Something I haven't read yet, some clause, some loophole — you're the one who says the court runs on loopholes—"

"The court runs on loopholes the way a fish runs on water," Odile said, setting down the stick. "It swims in them. It doesn't leave them lying around for strangers." She rose, joints cracking, and came close, and gripped Mara's wrist hard enough to bruise. "Listen to me, girl. Whatever number you've got in your head, whatever the ledgers whispered to you in the dark — you hold it loose. You hear me? Because the moment you sign yourself away in that place, there is no clever clause, no loyal friend, no god under this mountain who gets you back. The Court does not sell twice."

"I know."

"You don't. Nobody knows until—" Odile stopped. Something crossed her face, quick and old, a grief she had evidently decided at some point in her life to keep rather than spend. "I knew someone who gave the Court her name," she said quietly. "Before the Veil. Before any of this. I've spent forty years not remembering her properly, and I only worked out last week that the hole in my memory has a shape, and the shape is a person." Her grip tightened. "So you hold the price loose, Mara Thornbury. And you tell me what it is, so that two of us are carrying it."

Above them, far up through the root and the stone and the sleeping mountain, a bell tolled. Mara counted the strikes without meaning to, the way a scribe counts anything, and arrived at twelve, and thought: *thirteen minus one.*

"The price," she said, "is everything I am. Filed under T."
