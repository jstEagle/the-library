## 17. The Price of a Name

Mara found the answer the way one finds a splinter in one's own hand: not by looking for it, but by feeling it every time she moved.

She had spent four nights in the archive beneath Mount Caelum, four nights among shelves that breathed. The Undercourt kept its records in ledgers bound in hide she refused to identify, and the ledgers kept themselves — pages turning when no wind stirred, ink crawling into new columns while she watched, the whole vast room murmuring in a language of arithmetic. Everything here was counted. Everything here was owed. She had learned the court's grammar the way she had once learned the dead tongues of the temple: by surrender, by repetition, by letting the strange syllables rearrange something behind her ribs.

Bram's contract lay in the seventh gallery, in a case of black glass that sweated cold. She had read it so many times she could recite it in the dark. *One soul, whole and unencumbered, sold by Vessa Thornbury of the valley floor, in consideration of three hundred crowns, two fields, and the remission of all debts outstanding against the house of Thornbury, in perpetuity.*

In perpetuity. Her grandmother's mark sat beneath those words like a scar — the same looped, impatient *V* that Mara herself made when she signed the temple tithe-rolls. She had inherited the hand along with the blood. She had inherited the debt along with the name.

And the name — that was the splinter. That was the thing she felt every time she moved.

Because she had forged an amendment. She had spent three nights drafting it in the court's own style, matching the clerk's slant, aging the ink with ash and tallow, and the ledgers had rejected it before her pen left the page. The black glass of the case had fogged white with refusal. A line of script had risen through the vellum of her draft like a body rising through a lake: *FALSE ENTRY. THE COURT WEIGHS WHAT IS GIVEN, NOT WHAT IS WRITTEN.*

What is *given*.

She had stood in the seventh gallery with the failed forgery crumpling in her fist and felt the thought arrive whole, the way the child's first word — *Bram* — had arrived through the scriptorium door on a night of snow. Some truths do not need to be reasoned toward. They simply walk in and sit down, and after that you cannot imagine the room without them.

The court traded in everything: years, memories, courage, grief, the color of a childhood afternoon. But all of it was denominated in one currency, because all of it was *held* by one instrument. The ledgers did not record souls. The ledgers recorded names. A name was the hook on which the court hung everything it owned — Bram's name, filed and stamped and accruing interest for thirteen years. Erase the name and the holdings fell. She had seen it in the margin of a two-hundred-year-old contract, a clerk's annotation in faded rust-brown ink: *Name surrendered by the debtor of his own free will; all claims lapse; the file cannot be maintained against a man who was never entered.*

The court could not refuse a name freely given, because the court was built on the principle that everything had a price, and a name was the one commodity whose value was infinite and therefore always sufficient. It was the one payment no ledger could call insufficient. It was the one bargain the court was *obliged* by its own law to accept.

And a name freely given did not merely transfer. It *unmade*. She had read enough of the court's procedure to know what surrender meant: not that Mara Thornbury would walk out of the mountain nameless, but that Mara Thornbury would never have been. Every record that held her would shed her like water. Every memory that carried her would close over the space where she had been, smooth as a pond after a stone. Her mother would not grieve her; her mother would not remember bearing her. Odile would wake in their camp and find a stranger's bedroll beside the child's and feel, perhaps, a grief with no name attached — a splinter in a hand that had never touched anything.

The child. That was the thought that had kept her from the pen for two days. The child would forget her too. The child, who slept in the alcove behind the archive's false wall where she had hidden him, who pressed his forehead to her wrist each night like a seal pressed to wax, who said the word she had half-translated on the second day — a word from the pre-kingdom tongue, a word that appeared in no lexicon she had ever copied, a word that meant, as nearly as she could render it, *the one who reads me back into the world*. The child would forget. Everyone would forget. That was the price, and the price was the point, because a sacrifice that left a residue was not a sacrifice; the court would smell the remainder and refuse the trade, and Bram would be erased at moonrise on the thirteenth turn, and the child's small body would fail with him, and the snow outside the mountain would go on falling on a world that had never contained either of them.

So: everything, or nothing. The court did not deal in fractions of a self.

* * *

She went to the alcove on the last night before the moon completed its turn. The false wall was a shelf of ledgers that swung when you pressed the spine of the oldest one — a ledger of tolls from a kingdom whose name had itself been surrendered, which struck her now as a dark joke the court had been making all along.

The child was awake. He was always awake lately; the nearer the moon came to fullness, the more of Bram bled through him, and Bram, she understood, had never been a boy who slept well in strange places. He sat with his knees drawn up, and in the light of the tallow stub she had left him he looked less like a child than like a portrait of a child painted by someone who remembered him fondly and had not seen him in years. Thirteen years. The soul in him had been thirteen years old when it was sold, and it had not aged a day in the dark of the court's keeping, and now it was pouring back into this borrowed vessel like water into a cup too small for it, and the cup was beginning to crack at the seams. She had seen the cracks that morning: a fine gray webbing at the corners of his eyes, like the crazing of old glaze.

"Tell me about him," she said, and sat down across from him, folding her hands in her lap the way she did at the scriptorium desk, the posture of a woman prepared to copy out whatever came.

The child looked at her. And then — this was the thing that undid her, this was the thing she would carry into the dark if anything could be carried — he began, in his broken, half-dead tongue, to tell her.

He told her about a river with a red bridge, and a dog named after a saint who had turned out not to be a saint. He told her about a mother who sang off-key on purpose to make him laugh. He told her about the day the men came with papers, and how his mother had hidden him in the flour chest, and how he had breathed flour and wept without sound, and how when he came out his mother was gone and the house was very tidy, tidier than it had ever been, because the buyers had required the property be surrendered in good order. He told her about the Undercourt's antechamber, the long gray room where souls waited to be filed, and how he had been small enough, even at thirteen, to hide from the clerks for three days, and how on the fourth day a clerk had found him and said, not unkindly, *You are a very well-made contract, little one*, and how he had not understood until much later that this was the truest thing anyone had ever said to him.

Mara copied none of it down. For the first time since she had entered the mountain, she did not want to be a scribe. She wanted only to be a listener, and to hold the listening in the one ledger the court could not seize.

When he finished, he said the word. The untranslatable one. He said it looking directly at her, and his hand came up and touched the place below her collarbone where, in the old pre-kingdom script she had once studied in a forbidden text, the sign for *name* was written on the bodies of kings.

She understood him perfectly. That was the cruelty of it. She understood that he was telling her: *I know what you are going to do, and I know what it costs, and I am giving you the only thing I have, which is the knowledge that you were here.*

"No," she said, in his own tongue, badly. "No. You keep that. You keep it even when you don't know what it is. Promise me. Whatever's left of me, it lives in that word, and you don't give it to anyone, not even the court. *Especially* not the court."

The child considered this with the gravity of the thirteen-year-old soul inside him, and then he nodded once, the nod of a boy closing a bargain, and Mara felt something in her chest go very quiet and very bright, like a room after the lamp is trimmed.

* * *

The Office of Voluntary Surrender was on the ground floor of the archive, which told her everything about how often it was used. It was a small room, almost humble, with one desk, one lamp, one chair for the clerk and one for the supplicant. The clerk was ancient in the way furniture is ancient — worn smooth, indeterminate of age, shaped by use. He did not look up when she entered. He set a single sheet of vellum on the desk and a pen beside it, and the pen was the plainest object she had seen in the entire court: no silver, no bone, no ornament. Just a quill and a nib, the tool of her whole former life.

"You understand the terms," the clerk said. His voice was dry paper.

"Say them anyway," Mara said. "I'm a scribe. I believe in reading the contract aloud."

Something moved at the corner of the clerk's mouth — not quite a smile; the court did not deal in smiles. "The supplicant offers her name, freely given, without reservation, remainder, or condition. The court accepts, and by acceptance is bound to apply the full value of the offering against the designated claim. The claim against the soul entered as *Bram, son of Liss, of the valley floor* is discharged in full. The supplicant's name is struck from all records. All memories held by all persons, living, dead, filed, or pending, are amended. The supplicant will not be mourned, because the supplicant will not have been known. There is no appeal. There is no remainder. There is no coming back." He looked up then, and his eyes were the gray of the antechamber where the souls waited. "The court is obliged to tell you that no one has ever used this office twice."

"I should hope not," Mara said, "given the terms."

"The court is also obliged to tell you" — and here the clerk paused, and the pause had weight, the way a scale has weight before it tips — "that the offering must be *free*. If any part of you is coerced — by fear, by debt, by the hope of being remembered for the sacrifice — the offering is impure, and the ledger will know. The ledger always knows. We rejected your amendment, scribe. We will reject your name if there is anything held back in the hand that signs it."

Mara stood very still. She thought of the temple scriptorium, the smell of ink and cold stone, the tithe-rolls she had copied for six years while counting down a debt she had never chosen and never questioned. She thought of her grandmother's looped, impatient *V*. She thought of Odile's crooked grin and her burned hedge-charms and the way she had said, on the valley road, *I've been nobody's for thirty years, girl, and I'm not starting with the Wardens* — and how Mara had understood, in that moment, that she had never been anybody's either, and how much she had wanted, suddenly and ferociously, to be somebody's. Chosen. Chosen family, chosen loyalty, chosen road.

She was choosing now. That was the whole of it. That was the free part. Not the absence of fear — the fear was in the room with her, filling it like water — but the presence of choice, made with her eyes open, made *because* of the cost and not despite it. The court wanted a name without remainder. She could give it one, because the remainder she was leaving behind was not held back from the offering; it was the *purpose* of the offering. A boy, whole. A word, kept. A loophole, planted — because she had written the failed forgery to be *found*, had left it in the seventh gallery with its rejection-script showing, had trusted Odile to be captured near it, had trusted Odile's cunning the way she trusted the dawn. The court would take her name. The court would not know it had taken a seed.

"Bring me the claim," she said. "I want to see Bram's file struck through with my own eyes before I sign."

The clerk produced the black-glass case without comment. Inside, the contract lay open to its final page, and beside it, the ledger-entry: *Bram, son of Liss — held, accruing — 13th turn imminent.* Mara took the plain pen. She dipped it in the plain ink. And she wrote, beneath the discharge-clause the clerk had drafted, in her own hand — the hand the temple had trained, the hand that was the only inheritance she chose to keep — her name.

*Mara Thornbury.*

She watched the letters as they dried. She watched the ink take them the way the snow had taken the child's footprints on the first night, and she felt — this was the strange thing, this was the thing she would have written down if there had been time and a page — she felt *lighter*. Six years of debt. A lifetime of being a line in someone else's ledger. And now, at the end, one line that was hers alone, written in a hand no one would ever read, and it was the truest sentence she had ever composed, because it was the only one whose author and subject were the same person and the price was the same as the payment.

The lamp guttered. Somewhere far above, through a thousand tons of mountain, the moon was rising on its thirteenth turn.

The clerk took the page. The letters of her name lifted off the vellum like ash off a page of burning paper — gently, without resistance, almost gratefully — and dissolved into the gray air of the little office, and the black-glass case fogged white and then cleared, and the ledger-entry read: *Bram, son of Liss — DISCHARGED.*

"Done," the clerk said. He was already turning to other papers. "The trade executes at full moon. You should return to your — " he paused, the smallest hesitation, the first human thing he had shown — "to the child. The court finds that supplicants who linger in this office after signing tend to regret the time wasted."

Mara was already at the door when he spoke once more, without looking up.

"Scribe. The court is obliged to note one irregularity. Your offering was pure. It was also — " the dry paper voice paused — "unusual. The ledgers do not often receive names given *in the shape of a forgery*. Someone will find that seed you planted. I am obliged to hope, against the interest of my office, that they find it in time."

She did not answer. She was running, up through the galleries, past the breathing shelves, toward the false wall and the alcove and the child, because the moon was rising and the trade was executing and she had perhaps a quarter of an hour left in the memory of the world, and she intended to spend every second of it holding his hand.

Behind her, in the seventh gallery, the crumpled forgery she had left deliberately unburned lay where she had dropped it, its rejection-script glowing faintly, waiting — as she had designed it to wait, as only a scribe would know how to make a document wait — to be found by the one person in the mountain stubborn enough to read a lie until it told the truth.

Above the mountain, the moon cleared the peaks, whole and white and final, and began, at last, to turn.
