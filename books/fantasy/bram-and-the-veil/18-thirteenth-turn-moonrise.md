## 18. Thirteenth Turn, Moonrise

The mountain held its breath.

Mara felt it in her teeth first — a pressure, a stillness, as though the whole ridge of Mount Caelum had drawn in a lungful of night and refused to let it go. Above them the sky was a bowl of hammered tin, cloudless and cold, and the moon hung at its center like a coin someone had nailed there: swollen past fullness, silvered almost white at its rim, a hair's breadth from completing its thirteenth turn since the child had come stumbling barefoot out of the snow.

They waited in the hollow beneath the great root — the same root-system that had swallowed them into the Undercourt days ago, now exhaling faint warmth through its broken gates. Odile had built a fire small enough to cup in two hands, feeding it twigs of rowan she'd carried from the valley, because some habits survived even here, on the far side of the Veil where magic pooled in the low places like rainwater. The child sat between them, wrapped in Odile's patched cloak, watching the flames with eyes that were not quite a child's eyes. They never had been.

"Tell me what happens," Mara said. Not *what will happen*. She couldn't bear the future tense tonight. "Tell me what happens."

Odile didn't look up from the fire. Her hands were steady; they always were when it mattered most, which Mara had learned was the opposite of how steadiness worked in most people. "The contract executes. That's all. The court doesn't send heralds or trumpets. It's arithmetic." She turned a twig between her fingers. "The moon finishes the turn, the term of the sale ends, and the soul goes home to the vessel it was purchased for. The body you see—" she nodded at the child, gently, without shame, "—was only ever a shell. A place to keep him while the interest accrued."

"And me?" Mara asked. She kept her voice level. She had practiced this question for three nights, against the stone walls of the archive corridors, until it came out flat and clean as a copied line of text. "And my part of it?"

Odile looked at her then. In the firelight her face was all angles and ash-grey hair, and her eyes held the particular grief of a woman who had spent her whole life reading the fine print of other people's bargains.

"You know your part," Odile said quietly.

The child stirred. He had spoken more these last days than in all the weeks before — Bram's memories rising in him like sediment shaken loose, names surfacing one by one. His mother's name. The name of a dog. The name of a river he'd drowned in, or nearly drowned in, before the men with the ledger-books had found his father weeping over debts that could not be paid any other way.

Now he looked up at Mara and said, in that dead tongue older than kingdoms, a single word.

She had translated it once, kneeling among forbidden texts with her heart hammering, certain she was wrong. She was not wrong. It was her name. There was no reason for a boy dead eighty years to know her name. There was no reason except that souls in the court's keeping drift close to the ledgers, and ledgers record everything, and somewhere in those vast grey stacks a line existed that read: *sold by Thornbury blood, to be repaid by Thornbury blood.*

He knew. He had always known. And still, every night since the archive, he had reached for her hand in the dark and said it — her name, that one untranslated word — like an apology he couldn't finish.

"You could stop it," Odile said. It was not encouragement. It was not counsel. It was simply the truth, laid on the fire like a log. "Walk away from the gate. Live unnamed in the high country. People have done worse things to stay themselves."

"I know," Mara said.

"He won't remember you. No one will. I won't." Odile's voice did not waver, but something behind it did — something old and buried, the way a river moves under ice. "I've been trying to prepare myself for that, girl, and I'll tell you honestly: I don't know if it can be done. To lose someone and not know what shape the loss is. It's like losing a word mid-sentence. You feel the hole. You just can't say what fit there."

Mara reached across the fire and took the child's hand. His fingers were cold, always cold, cold as the day he'd arrived at the scriptorium door.

"Then remember this much," she said. "If you find yourself grieving and don't know why — if you're standing in my schoolroom someday, or passing the temple steps, and your chest goes tight for no reason — that's me. That's the shape of the hole. Don't go looking for a reason. Just know I was worth it."

"That's a cruel thing to ask of an old woman," Odile said.

"It's the only thing I have left to give."

Above them, imperceptibly, the moon moved.

* * *

Bram dreamed.

It was not sleep exactly — the shell-body did not truly sleep anymore, not since the magic had flooded back into the world beyond the Veil and begun burning him slowly from the inside like frost melting off a window. But he drifted, and in drifting he was eight years old again, standing on the frozen mill-pond with his boots skidding, and his mother was calling him in for supper, and the light through the kitchen window was gold as honey, and none of it had happened yet or had happened eighty years ago or was happening right now in a ledger somewhere filed under *assets, mortal, minor*.

The court had told him nothing, all these years. The court did not speak to its holdings. But he had heard things, drifting near the shelves where his contract slept: whispers of an amendment being drafted. Whispers of an auditor with ink-stained fingers who worked nights and asked dangerous questions. Whispers of a price being weighed on scales so old no one remembered their maker.

He had tried to hate her, once, when he understood whose blood had sold him. It was easier to hate a name than to hate a grandmother long dead, and easier still than hating the court itself, which was too vast to hate properly, the way you cannot hate winter.

But then she had knelt beside his cot in the hollow of the root and read to him from a primer of the dead tongue — his tongue, the tongue his mother had sung in — stumbling over the vowels, getting half of it wrong, and laughing at herself, and he had understood that she was paying a debt she hadn't incurred, with a currency that would cost her everything, freely, without being asked. He had stopped hating anything after that. There wasn't room.

The moon completed its turn.

There was no sound. That was the terrible thing, Bram would think later — that the greatest transaction of his existence, the unmaking and remaking of a soul, passed without a single note of ceremony. The pressure in the air shifted, like a door opening in a distant room. The cold in his bones turned warm. The warm became heat, the heat became light, and the shell around him — the borrowed child, the vessel, whatever patient nameless thing had walked in his place all these months — folded away like paper in water, and Bram Fairweather, aged eight years and eighty, opened eyes that were entirely, humanly, his own.

The first thing he saw was firelight. The second thing was a woman with dark hair pulled back from a face streaked with soot and exhaustion, and she was looking at him with such naked wonder that he knew her instantly, though he had never seen her true face before, only glimpses through the shell's dim windows.

"Mara," he said. Her name came out whole, unbroken, in his own voice.

And she laughed — a cracked, disbelieving sound — and gathered him up off the ground and held him against her shoulder, and he was crying, and she was crying, and above them the moon stood perfect and complete and white as a fresh page.

"Mara," Bram said again, into her hair, because he could. Because saying it was like setting down a weight carried across eighty years. "Mara, Mara—"

"Mm?" she answered. "What is it? What do you need?"

"Nothing," he said. "Nothing. I just—"

He never finished the sentence. He never got to finish any sentence addressed to her again.

Because mid-word, mid-embrace, with her arms still warm around his shoulders and her heartbeat still quick against his cheek, Mara Thornbury stopped existing.

There was no flash. No cry. Her arms did not fall away so much as cease to have ever been around him. One moment Bram was held; the next he was embracing empty air, pitching forward onto his knees in the dirt, and the fire crackled on, and the mountain went on holding its breath, and the space where a woman had been was simply space, ordinary and indifferent, as though no one had filled it for longer than a heartbeat.

Bram screamed. He was hoarse from eighty years of silence, and he screamed anyway, screaming the only word he had, the word that was hers, and the word fell into the night and broke apart into sounds that meant nothing — syllables without a name attached, a key for a door that no longer stood anywhere in the world.

* * *

Odile came awake to a boy sobbing in the dirt and a fire burning low and a hollow feeling under her ribs she could not account for.

She had been asleep — or something like sleep, the shallow doze of an old woman keeping watch. Now she was on her feet with a charm half-formed on her tongue and no memory of reaching for it. The boy — and he *was* a boy now, whole and solid and breathing, transformed, the bargain executed, the thing they had crossed a kingdom and broken laws to accomplish — the boy was clawing at the ground where someone wasn't.

"Boy." She crouched, took his shoulders. "Bram. Look at me. Look at me."

"She was here." His voice was wrecked. "She was *here*, she was holding me, she—"

"No one was here." The words came out of Odile before she thought them, and even as she spoke them, they felt wrong in her mouth, like wearing another woman's coat. "No one came with us past the tollhouse, child. It was you and me and Ferren, and Ferren left us at the gate. You know this. You—"

But she stopped. Because she was counting, the way she counted everything, hedge-witch habits dying hard, counting heads and herbs and coins — and the count came out wrong. Three bedrolls lay by the fire. Three cups sat on the flat stone. She had brewed willow-bark tea before moonrise, and she had measured out four portions, because that was the number that lived in her hands, the number her fingers had known since before the mountain — and she could not, for the life of her, remember why four.

"There was someone," Bram whispered, watching her face. "You know there was someone. Say it. Say her— say the—"

He groped for the word and could not find it. She watched him grasp for it the way a drowning man grasps water, watched the word slide through his fingers again and again, and something in Odile's chest tore along a seam she hadn't known was there.

"I can't," she said. Her voice came out ragged. "I can't say it, boy. Whatever it was — whoever it was — the ledger has it. All of it. Every record, every memory, every—" She pressed her fist against her sternum, against the ache beneath it, the hole-shaped ache, the missing word mid-sentence. "Every trace. She gave it all. Do you understand what I'm telling you? She gave everything she was, and the court accepted it, and the court does not give change."

Bram stared at the empty bedroll. At the fourth teacup. At the fire, where the heat of another body should still have been lingering in the packed earth.

"She bought me," he said. "With herself."

"Aye." Odile sank down beside him, old knees cracking, and pulled him against her side the way she had once pulled her own children close, a lifetime and a disgrace ago. "She bought you with herself. So you'd better live, lad. Live loudly, live long, and be worth every stroke of that pen — because somewhere in the grey stacks of that court there's a blank space where a woman used to be, and I am going to spend whatever years are left to me finding it, and I am going to write her back."

Bram lifted his head. Through his tears his eyes had gone sharp and strange, the eyes of a boy who had spent eighty years listening at the doors of archives.

"How?" he said.

And Odile — who did not remember Mara's face, or Mara's voice, or the taste of the tea she'd brewed for four — looked down at her own hands, ink-stained and clever, and felt the hole-shaped ache shift inside her, almost like a hint. Almost like a hand pointing.

"By cheating," she said. "The same way she did."

Far above them, the moon began, very slowly, to wane — and in the deepest vault of the Undercourt, in a ledger older than kingdoms, a single line of text flickered where no line should be able to flicker:
