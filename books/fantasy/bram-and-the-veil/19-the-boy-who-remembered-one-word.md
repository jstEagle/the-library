## 19. The Boy Who Remembered One Word

The first thing Bram knew was the weight of his own hands.

They lay on a blanket of rough grey wool, and they were small — smaller than he remembered hands being, though memory itself came to him strangely, like light through water. He flexed his fingers and watched them move, and the moving of them felt like a sentence being completed after years of silence. *These are mine. These have always been mine.* Somewhere beneath the thought, older and deeper than thought, something settled back into its socket with an almost audible click, the way a shoulder goes home after dislocation.

He was sitting up before he understood that he had been lying down. The room around him was low-ceilinged and lamplit, smelling of tallow and dried herbs and woodsmoke, and there were two people in it: a tall woman with iron-grey hair braided like rope, standing very still by the hearth as if she were afraid movement might break something; and a boy — no, not a boy, a child, younger than himself, though the child's eyes held nothing young in them at all. They were watching him with an expression Bram could not read, because it was not any expression he knew. It looked like grief and relief and terror sharing one face.

"Say something," the woman said. Her voice cracked on the second word.

Bram opened his mouth. His throat worked. What came out was not the dead tongue, not the syllables that had lived in this borrowed mouth for thirteen turns of the moon — it was his own voice, rusty and thin from disuse but *his*, pitched the way his voice had been when he was nine years old and shouting across the barley fields at Tamsin Cole.

"I'm hungry," he said, and then, absurdly, because hunger was not what the moment demanded, "and I don't know where I am."

The woman made a sound that was half laugh and half sob and pressed both fists to her mouth. The child did not laugh or sob. The child simply kept looking at him, and Bram felt the look land somewhere under his ribs like a stone dropped down a well.

*You gave me your body,* he wanted to say. *You carried me all this way.* But he did not know how he knew that, and he did not know who to say it *to*, because when he searched the child's face for a name, for a single fact to hold onto, he found —

He found a word.

It rose in him unbidden, the way a song rises, the way a smell can drag a whole summer up out of the dark. It was not a sound so much as a shape that wanted to be a sound: four beats, soft at the edges, hard in the middle, tasting faintly of ink and cold air and someone laughing with her head thrown back. He could feel it sitting behind his teeth, complete and perfect and utterly unpronounceable, because it was not built of any alphabet that any living mouth used. It was a residue. It was the one thing the trade had failed to scrub clean.

"Mara," he said.

Except he didn't. What came out of his mouth was a small confused noise, and then, in plain valley-speech, "Who — who was she?"

The child's face changed. Something flickered there, quick as a fish turning in shallow water — recognition, loss, a hand opening over emptiness — and then it smoothed away into nothing, into the pleasant blankness of a person remembering nothing at all. The tall woman frowned. "Who was who?" she asked. "Bram, lad, there's only us here. You're safe. Do you know your name?"

"Bram." It came easily, gratefully. "Bram. I'm Bram."

"And do you remember anything else? Anything about how you got here?"

He tried. He reached inward, down through the fresh bright layers of *I am awake, I am hungry, my hands are mine*, hunting for whatever lay beneath, and what he found was a country of absence. There should have been months — years — of something between the last thing he remembered (his mother calling him in for supper, the smell of bread, the mountain going blue with evening) and this lamplit room. Instead there was a smooth blank wall, polished and seamless, like ice over a lake. He pressed against it and felt, rather than saw, shapes moving on the other side. A road. A tollhouse. A fire burning charms. A scribe's desk with a lamp guttering over a ledger. Hands writing. A voice saying, patiently, over and over, a word he couldn't hear.

"It's gone," he said slowly. "Most of it. All of it, maybe. I remember my mother's kitchen. I remember — Tamsin. Tamsin Cole, she threw apples at me. After that it's just—" He touched the wall of ice inside himself. "There's something behind it. I can feel it. Like hearing talking through a door."

The woman exchanged a glance with the child. "That'll be the Court's work," she said grimly. "They take the memories along with everything else. Clean transaction, no loose threads." She said it bitterly, the way a person says a thing they have personal reasons to hate.

But the child had gone very still.

* * *

They told him the story over supper, which he ate like a starving animal and was not ashamed of. The woman was called Odile, and she was a hedge-witch, which apparently meant she was both more dangerous and less respectable than she sounded. The child — the body he now wore, though Odile chose her words carefully around that fact, as if walking on river stones — had been found barefoot in the snow outside a temple scriptorium, speaking a language no one in the valley knew.

"You spoke it too," Odile said. "Before. When we crossed the Veil and the magic hit you. You screamed in a voice twice your size and told a Warden exactly where he could put his cordon." A ghost of a smile. "Colorful boy, whoever you were."

"I was nine," Bram said. "When they took me."

The words surprised him. He hadn't known he was going to say them; they'd simply surfaced, pushed up through the ice like a bubble finding air. "Nine. My father owed money. Not to a lender — to *them*. To the Undercourt. He'd traded something, I think, years before I was born, and the debt came due, and the only thing left in our house worth a soul's price was—" He stopped. Looked down at his own small hands again. "Me."

Odile said nothing. The fire popped. Outside, wind moved through the pines above the cottage with a sound like slow breathing.

"The contract would have been filed," Bram went on, and heard his own voice go flat and careful, the voice of someone walking a plank. "Everything's filed there. That's what it is, underneath all the rest of it. A court, yes, but really it's a *ledger*. Every soul, every bargain, every year and memory and name anyone ever sold them, written down and cross-referenced and kept forever." He frowned, because he did not know how he knew this either, and yet he knew it the way he knew his own heartbeat. "Which means somewhere in that archive there's a page with my whole life on it. Everything they took. Filed away like tax records."

"That's a cheerful thought," Odile said dryly.

"No," Bram said. "It's a *true* one. I can feel it." He put a hand flat against his sternum, against the place where the word lived. "There's something they missed. One thing. It's still in me."

"What kind of thing?"

And here was the trouble, the trouble that would follow him up out of the mountains and down into the valley and through every day that came after: he could not say it. He opened his mouth and reached for the word — that four-beat, ink-and-cold-air word — and found that it would not come out. It was not that he forgot it. It was that the word seemed to sit *sideways* to speech altogether, angled into some dimension his tongue could not enter. He could feel every part of it. He could feel its weight, its temperature, its terrible tenderness. And it would not be spoken.

"A word," he said at last. "A name, maybe. Someone's name. It's the only thing left of — of whoever brought me here. Whoever walked me up the mountain." His throat closed. "I think they gave everything. I think they gave *all* of themselves, and the trade took even the memory of them, and somehow this one word fell through the cracks. And I can't say it. I can't write it. I can't even hum it. It just sits inside me like a stone in a shoe, and nobody else knows it's there, and if I die it dies, and then they'll be gone *completely*, won't they? Erased twice."

Odile had stopped eating. She was looking at him with an expression he would learn to know well in the years after: the look of a woman whose clever mind has caught fire and is trying very hard to hide the smoke. "Words don't fall through cracks," she said quietly, mostly to herself. "Not in that place. Nothing falls. Everything is *filed*."

"Then why is it still in me?"

"Because," Odile said slowly, "maybe it isn't a leftover. Maybe it's a record." She stood abruptly, crossed to the window, and stared out at the dark. "A ledger has to record what was given. That's the law under everything, the law even the Undercourt obeys, because without it their whole archive is worthless. If someone gave a name — truly gave it, freely, as payment — then somewhere in those books there must be a line that says so. A line with a hole in it shaped exactly like—"

She stopped herself. Shook her head. "Eat your supper, boy. We'll talk tomorrow."

But Bram barely tasted the rest of the meal. Behind his teeth, the word pulsed, patient and warm, like a coal that refused to go out.

* * *

Sleep did not come kindly.

He lay on a pallet near the fire in the cottage's single back room, and the dreams came not as pictures but as weather. Warmth moving past him — a presence, laughing, always just leaving a room when he entered it. Ink stains on fingers. Snow, and bare feet in snow, and someone carrying him although he was far too big to be carried, carrying him anyway because no one had told her arms what was impossible. A temple desk. A lamp. A voice reading aloud in the dead tongue, stumbling and then less stumbling, learning it for him, learning it *because of him*, and every time he turned toward the voice in the dream it stopped, and the warmth withdrew, and there was only the smooth ice wall and his own reflection staring back at him from it, nine years old and utterly alone.

He woke gasping in the grey hour before dawn and lay listening to the cottage breathe.

The child slept on the far pallet. In sleep, the strange old eyes were closed, and the face was only a child's face, and Bram felt a complicated ache looking at it — gratitude and guilt tangled together like briars. *You carried me. You spent yourself to buy me back. I don't even know your name.*

*I do,* said the word behind his teeth. *I'm keeping it.*

He sat up carefully and looked at his hands in the dying firelight. Small hands. A child's hands. Thirteen turns of the moon, Odile had said, and the vessel would have failed — would have crumbled like a pot too long in the rain, and Bram with it. Someone had run out the clock. Someone had beaten the deadline with days to spare and paid a price he could feel but not see, the way you can feel a missing tooth with your tongue.

"They took your name," he whispered to the sleeping child, to the empty air, to the word. "Didn't they. That's what a name costs. That's what you paid."

The word answered the only way it could: by pressing against the inside of his ribs, once, firmly, like a hand squeezing his.

He made a decision then, in the cold and the ash-smell and the grey light, a decision he would keep for the rest of his life with the same stubbornness that other boys kept faith or tempers. He could not speak the word. He could not write it. No one else could hear it, and the world's every record had been scoured clean of the person it belonged to, and by every law of heaven and the Undercourt she was gone.

But he was not a record. He was a boy, and boys are worse than ledgers — boys remember what they love whether the universe approves or not. So he would carry it. He would carry it the way you carry an ember in cupped hands through wind: carefully, stubbornly, refusing to let the cold have it, feeding it breath whenever breath was needed, until he found someone clever enough to do what he could not.

Because he had seen Odile's face when he described it. He had seen the fire catch behind her eyes. Hedge-witch, disgraced, half-starved for magic and twice as sharp for it — she believed words could be filed and lines could be found and holes in pages could be filled. And Bram had spent one night among the living again and already understood the most important thing about being alive:

You do not let go of people.

He got up, wrapped the blanket around his shoulders, and sat by the hearth until dawn, holding the word.

* * *

Morning brought porridge, and questions, and the beginning of an argument that would define them all.

"We go down the mountain today," Odile said, packing with the brisk violence of a woman who hated staying anywhere too long. "Valley's safest. Veil keeps the Court's reach thin below the line, and Hesk—" she paused, "—Hesk owes us, though don't ask me to explain how I know that. Point is, you need feeding up, schooling, a life. You've been dead thirteen months, boy. Time to start being alive properly."

"All right," Bram said. "One condition."

Odile's eyebrows climbed.

"When we're settled — when I'm fed and schooled and alive properly — we go back. Down into that archive. Both of us." He met her eyes, and he was nine years old and his voice shook and he did not care. "Someone paid everything for me. The Court erased her so well that even *she's* probably forgotten herself, wherever she is, if she's anywhere at all. But there's a word left, Odile. One word. It survived the whole machine. Which means the machine isn't perfect. Which means there's a seam somewhere, and seams can be picked."

For a long moment the hedge-witch said nothing. The fire ticked. The child watched them both from the corner, silent as ever, wearing that unreadable expression that was not quite blankness — and Bram noticed, for the first time, that the child's hand had drifted up to rest flat against his own chest, right over the heart, as if something in there were being listened to.

"You felt it too," Bram said softly. "Whatever she left. You felt it the whole way here, didn't you?"

The child's eyes glistened. He nodded, once.

Odile looked from one of them to the other — the boy who would not forget and the child who could not explain — and something in her jaw set like mortar drying. "Ledgers record what's given," she murmured, half to herself, and there was a new edge in her voice now, a whetstone edge. "And a given name doesn't stop being given just because the givers forget. Debts run both directions in that place. It's the one law they can't escape, because they wrote it themselves."

She slung her pack over her shoulder and shoved open the cottage door, and morning poured in cold and gold, and the mountain loomed above them with all its buried courts and buried bargains.

"Come on, then," Odile said. "Both of you. We've a long walk and I've a loophole to go pick."

Behind Bram's teeth, warm as a banked coal, the word beat once — twice — like a heart remembering how.
