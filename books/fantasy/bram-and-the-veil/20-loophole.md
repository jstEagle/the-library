## 20. Loophole

The grief had no name, and that was the cruelest part of it.

Odile had mourned before. She was sixty-one years old, a hedge-witch who had outlived two husbands, one daughter, and every friend she'd made before the age of forty, and she knew grief the way a farrier knows horses: intimately, practically, with an understanding of its weight and temper. Grief had a shape. It had edges you could learn to walk around, calluses that formed over time until the raw thing beneath stopped bleeding at every touch.

This grief had no edges at all.

It sat in her chest like water in a cracked jar — present in everything, contained by nothing, spilling into every hour of the day without reason or rhythm. She would be stirring the pot at midday and find tears running down her face into the broth. She would be mending Bram's coat — he'd grown since the mountain, the boy ate like a threshing crew — and her hands would stop moving, and she would sit staring at nothing for a quarter of an hour, aching over something she could not name.

Because there was nothing to name. That was the whole obscenity of it.

She remembered the journey up the mountain. Every mile of it. She remembered Ferren's crooked smile and his crookeder tollhouse, the hollow men and their hollowed faces, the flood of magic that had nearly knocked her off her feet after twenty years of starving outside the Veil. She remembered the gate beneath the root, the archive like a city built inside a cathedral, the magistrate with his ink-stained fingers. She remembered bargaining for time while Mara worked the ledgers, remembered being dragged away by court-clerks with faces like closed books, remembered the cold of a cell that did not exist on any map.

And then she remembered walking down out of the mountain at dawn with a living, breathing boy beside her, whole and real and blinking at the sun like a man waking from a long illness — and no third person between them. No gap where anyone should have been. Just a road built for three, walked by two, and a hole in her heart shaped like nothing at all.

Bram remembered a word.

That was the other cruelty, or perhaps the only mercy; Odile had never decided which. The boy carried it with him everywhere, tucked behind his teeth like a stone from a river he alone had visited. *Mara.* He said it sometimes in his sleep. He said it when he woke. He said it standing in Odile's garden looking at the rowan tree as though the word were written somewhere in its branches, in letters only he could read.

"Say it again," Odile said to him, one evening in early spring, because she had learned to ask instead of flinching.

"Mara." He pronounced it carefully, the way he pronounced all the words of the dead tongue he still half-spoke, giving each syllable its full weight. "It doesn't mean anything here. I've asked. There's no word like it in any language the valley speaks."

"And what does it mean where *you* come from?"

He looked at her with those eyes that were too old for a boy of eleven — eyes that had been someone else's for thirteen turns of the moon. "It means a person," he said. "A specific person. But it also means—" His brow furrowed. He reached for it the way a man reaches for a dream upon waking, grasping at warmth already fading. "It means *the one who wrote herself out*. Or maybe it means *sacrifice*, but not the kind they do at altars. The kind you do at a desk." He shook his head, frustrated. "It's a name. That's what it is. A name that became a meaning."

Odile's hands had gone cold around her teacup. "A name," she repeated.

"A name." He nodded. And then, because he was a child still, despite everything: "Do you think whoever lost it misses it?"

*Whoever gave it,* Odile thought. *You don't lose a name the way you lose a glove. You give it, or you sell it, or you spend it. Names are currency down under the root.*

"I don't know," she said aloud, which was the truth, and drank her tea, which was a lie.

* * *

It took her three weeks to see it, and she saw it, as she saw most things worth seeing, through the bottom of a bottle.

Not drink, exactly — she'd sworn off the strong stuff after the second husband — but the Undercourt wine Ferren had pressed on them as a parting gift, sour black stuff in a stoneware jug that tasted of cellars older than kingdoms. She'd been saving it for an occasion, and then realized she was waiting for an occasion that would never come, for a celebration whose guest of honor she couldn't remember, and so she opened it on an ordinary Tuesday and drank two cups and stared into the fire and let her mind go wandering the way it did when the hedges were asleep and the wards were quiet.

Wandering. That was the trick of it. Hedge-witchery wasn't power, not really — not the way temple-trained thaumaturges had power, all structure and syllable. Hedge-craft was attention. It was noticing which way the smoke bent, which hen went broody early, which neighbor's cough meant more than a cough. Twenty years outside the Veil had sharpened her attention to a blade, because attention was all she'd had.

And so, drunk on dead-cellared wine and staring at the fire, she noticed the thing she had been circling for weeks without landing on.

The ledger must record what was given.

That was the law beneath all the Undercourt's laws, the axiom from which the clerks derived everything else. She'd heard it in the magistrate's mouth while she bargained for time: *We are not cruel, hedge-witch. We are accurate. The court cannot lie, because the court is a record, and a record that lies stops being a record, and then it is nothing.* Everything in the archive existed because it had been entered. Every debt, every trade, every soul sold and filed. The court's power was its bookkeeping, and its bookkeeping was honest the way a scale is honest — it weighed exactly what was placed upon it, no more, no less.

Mara had forged an amendment. Odile knew this the way you know the shape of a room you've slept in — dimly, structurally, without being able to point at any single wall. The girl had worked nights in the archive, scribe's hands and scribe's patience, drafting some alteration to Bram's contract. And the amendment had held. That was the miracle nobody could explain: the ledgers detected lies — Odile had seen a clerk feed a false statement into the great book once, during her captivity, and watched the ink crawl and blister like skin burned — and yet Mara's work had stood. Which meant it hadn't been a lie. Which meant—

Odile set down her cup very slowly.

Which meant the girl had found the one thing the court could not refuse: a true offer, freely given, of equal value. Not forgery at all, in the end. A genuine transaction. Something given, honestly recorded, irrevocably entered.

Given. Recorded. Entered.

The ledger must record what was given.

"Ah," Odile said to the empty kitchen. Her voice came out rough. "Oh, you clever, stupid, brave girl."

Because if the ledger recorded what was given — truly, accurately, in the court's own incorruptible hand — then the record of that giving was itself a fact, and facts in the Undercourt were tradable. That was the entire architecture of the place. Memories were filed and sold. Years were banked. Names were currency. Nothing in the archive was fixed except the accuracy of the entries themselves.

And a name that had been *given* — not sold, not seized, not forfeited, but given, which was the purest form of transfer the court recognized — a given name was a gift. And gifts, in every legal tradition older than the Veil, could be inherited back.

She was on her feet without remembering standing. The fire spat. Bram's door creaked upstairs as he turned in his sleep, and faintly, half-dreaming, she heard it again: *Mara.*

"You're going to get me killed," Odile told him softly. "Again. Both of us, probably. All three of us, if we're counting properly."

She counted properly. Then she started packing.

* * *

The road to the mountain was easier the second time, which frightened her more than the first time's difficulty. Fear was a resource, and she was spending too little of it.

Ferren's tollhouse still stood at the pass mouth, and Ferren still sat in it, mending a net that would never hold fish, and when he saw her coming up the track with a staff in her hand and murder dressed as determination in her face, he laughed until he had to sit down.

"The witch returns," he wheezed. "Alone, even. What happened, did your pet scribe finally tire of your company?"

Something must have moved through Odile's face, because Ferren's laughter died in his throat. He looked at her a long moment, and smugglers are connoisseurs of grief the way priests are connoisseurs of sin, and whatever he read there made him stand and pour her a cup of the bad tea without being asked.

"She went into the book," Odile said. "Didn't she."

"I keep payment and conscience separate," Ferren said, which was what he always said, which was the closest a man like him ever came to a confession. "But I'll tell you what I told no one, since you're walking back toward the thing everyone else walks away from. I saw the entry go in. Everyone in the lower galleries saw it — the whole archive lit up like a struck bell. A name, given freely. First free gift entered in four hundred years." He slid the cup across the counter. "The clerks didn't know what to do with it. They kept checking the entry for error. There was no error. There's never any error. That's the problem with the place."

"That's the opportunity," Odile said.

Ferren raised his eyebrows, and Odile laid it out for him the way she'd laid it out in her own head a hundred times on the road: the ledger records what was given; the record of a gift is itself an asset; assets can be transferred; and a name given freely carries no taint of sale, no lien of debt, no clause of forfeiture — only provenance. Clean provenance. In a court where everything was mortgaged twice over, a clean name was worth more than a kingdom, and the court knew it, and the court would therefore have protected that entry, sealed it, guarded it against claim.

"But here's the flaw," she said, leaning forward, and her old teacher's cadence crept into her voice, the one she'd used on apprentices fifty years ago, "the flaw is that protection requires classification. To guard an entry, you must label it. And the only honest label for a freely given name is—"

"*Inheritable*," Ferren breathed. His net slipped from his fingers. "Gods below. You're going to make them inherit it back."

"I'm going to have the rightful heir claim it," Odile said. "Through the front door. With papers."

"And who," said Ferren slowly, "is the rightful heir to a name given by a woman no one remembers?"

Odile smiled, and it was not a kind smile, and she was glad no one was there to see it but a smuggler, who appreciated such things.

"The boy who received her last gift," she said. "Her sacrifice bought his life. In every law the courts have ever written, a life saved stands heir to the life that bought it. Bram inherits. The court's own precedent will argue my case for me. They wrote those precedents. Let them choke on them."

* * *

The gate beneath the root had not changed, but Odile had, and she felt the difference in her bones as she descended: the first time she had come down this throat of earth, she had been bargaining for minutes, spending secrets like a woman throwing coins to a flood. Now she came as a petitioner with a claim, and claims, in the Undercourt, were almost sacred.

The archive rose around her, shelf-stacks like cliff-faces, clerks moving between them like gulls over a harbor. The same magistrate found her — or perhaps summoned her; in the Undercourt the distinction was decorative — within a quarter hour, ink-stained fingers laced together, expression unreadable as a closed ledger.

"Hedge-witch. You were released. Release is permanent. This is one of our few gifts."

"I'm not here to complain about the gift. I'm here to contest an entry."

Something flickered in the magistrate's face — the smallest movement, a fish turning under ice. "Contest."

"One entry. Freely given name, entered—" she named the date, because she had memorized it, because of course she had; she had spent the whole road south doing sums in her head like a merchant's widow. "I represent the inheritor."

"There is no inheritor. There is no—" The magistrate stopped. And Odile watched, with a satisfaction that felt like sunlight after twenty years of shade, as the court's own honesty turned on it like a well-trained dog. The magistrate could not say *there is no one to inherit* — because the entry existed, and the entry was true, and the truth of it included the giver's identity, and the giver's identity included the fact of the gift, and the gift had purchased a life, and the life lived, and inheritance law was inheritance law, and the court could not lie.

"The court does not lie," Odile said gently. "So check your own precedents. Life-bought stands heir. Bring me the forms. I'll wait."

They made her wait nine days. She suspected it was punishment; she treated it as vacation. She slept in a reading alcove, ate archive-bread that tasted of dust and honey, and read everything she could about the mechanics of name-transfer, filling three notebooks with a scribe's neatness she'd learned from a girl she could not picture.

On the tenth day, they brought her the forms, and the pen, and the entry-book, and a witness-inheritor's signature line, and a summons for the boy, who arrived two days later, travel-worn and furious and clutching his one word like a talisman.

"It's her name," he said, before anyone else could speak, standing in the great hall with the shelves rising above him like weather. "It means her. Write it back."

The magistrate dipped the pen. Handed it to Odile. And Odile Fairweather, disgraced hedge-witch, twenty years starved outside the Veil, took the pen in her cracked right hand and wrote *Mara Thornbury* into the Undercourt's ledger in letters that glowed like banked coals —

— and the whole archive rang, once, like a struck bell.

Far above them, dawn was breaking over Mount Caelum, and something that had been blankness for a season began, at the edge of the light, to take shape.
