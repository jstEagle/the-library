## 4. Spring Owes No One

The door out of the country that was not stood at the top of a hill made entirely of held breath.

That was how it looked, anyway. A long slope of white rising toward a gate of black iron, and every flake on that slope hanging in the air exactly where it had fallen years ago — not drifted, not melted, not touched. Rafael climbed through them the way a swimmer climbs through water. Each step disturbed nothing. Behind her, Bram's paws made no prints at all, and she had stopped pretending not to notice.

The god waited at the gate.

It did not look like anything so grand as a god. It looked like an old woman in a shepherd's coat, frost in her eyebrows, hands folded like someone who has been waiting so long that waiting has become a posture rather than an act. But when Rafael came close enough to see her eyes, they were winter-colored all the way through, and there was no bottom to them.

"You've brought him," the old woman said.

"I've brought what you told me to bring." Rafael kept her voice level. She was a mercenary; she had negotiated with warlords and tax collectors and one memorable archmage over a disputed invoice for resurrected oxen. "I want to hear the terms again. All of them. Plainly."

"Plainly, then." The god unfolded her hands. "The door opens when the debt is paid. The debt is a memory of equal weight to the death I swallowed. You have only two things left of that weight. Your vows — already spent, sealing this place. And him."

She nodded at Bram.

The wolf sat in the snow, tail curled around his feet, watching Rafael with the patient attention he had worn since the guildhall door. Since before the guildhall door, probably. Since wherever she had found him and forgotten finding him.

"If I keep him," Rafael said slowly, working it through like arithmetic, because arithmetic was safe, "the winter holds."

"The winter holds. The people stay as they are — mid-laugh, mid-prayer, mid-farewell. No one dies twice. No one lives once more, either." The god's voice was not cruel. That was the worst part. It was gentle the way deep snow is gentle: it simply covered everything. "You would walk out with your wolf beside you and a warm thing to carry home, and every person in this country would stay a held note forever."

"And if I let him go—"

"They die honestly. What should have ended, ends. And then—" the god spread her hands, and somewhere far below, under miles of unmelting snow, Rafael felt something shift like a sleeper rolling toward wakefulness, "—spring does its work. Grief first. Then green."

Rafael looked at Bram.

He looked back, and there was nothing of the animal in it, nothing of the trick or the trap she had feared on the road. There was just him. Whoever he had been. Whatever name she could not say because saying it would cost her everything she had rebuilt herself from.

"You knew," she said. "From the first night. You knew what you were."

The wolf's ears tipped forward. When he spoke, his voice was the same warm rough voice that had argued her out of despair outside Vell's Crossing, that had hummed off-key to a bottled lullaby because she'd asked him to prove he could:

"I'm the last page of your ledger, Rafa. I've been walking beside you waiting for you to read me."

"Don't call me that." Her voice cracked on it. "I don't remember why I hate it. Or love it. Don't—"

"That's the point," Bram said gently. "You gave me up once already. This is just the second time. And the second time, you get to do it awake."

* * *

She sat down in the snow — snow that did not melt under her, snow that did not move — across from the wolf, and she made herself do the accounting. It was the only prayer she knew anymore. Numbers didn't lie. People lied, gods lied, ledgers rewritten themselves overnight, but if you added honestly, the sum was true.

Item: one country, several hundred thousand souls, held in stasis against their consent, saved from a death they never got to refuse.

Item: one memory, unnamed, unweighable, the last warm thing she owned. The shape of a voice calling her Rafa. The reason she flinched at campfires and always slept facing doors. Every soft thing in her that she'd assumed was temperament and now understood was scar tissue laid over an absence.

Item: the truth about herself, which was that she was not a woman who had lost her god. She was a woman whose god had gone silent because she had helped bury a whole nation under it.

The columns balanced. They balanced horribly.

"There has to be another way," she said, and hated how small it sounded. "I trade well. I have things. Bottled lullabies, second chances at discount — I can get you—"

"I don't want your inventory," the god said. "I want what was paid to me once. Paid gladly. He remembers that part, even if you don't."

Rafael turned to Bram. "Paid gladly?"

His tail moved, once, slow. "You loved me," he said. "Past tense is yours to decide, not mine. And when the god came collecting — when the country started to fall and someone had to hold the winter shut from inside — you didn't hesitate. You said, *take the best of me.* So it took me. Everything you remembered of me. It walked you out the other side, wiped the borders off every map but one, and set you down in a city where nobody knew your name, and you built a life out of the wreckage. Ledgers. Order. Small debts, tallied clean."

"I built a cage," Rafael said.

"You built a shelter," Bram corrected. "It worked. You survived me. Most people don't survive losing the best of themselves. You just forgot you were surviving."

The wind moved through the held snowflakes without moving them. Somewhere beneath them, faint as a pulse under bandages, Rafael heard it again: the bell-hum of the border, the country's heart, beating slow.

"How long?" she asked the god. "How long have they been frozen?"

"Forty-one years," the god said.

Rafael did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding. Forty-one years. She was thirty-six.

Whatever face she made, the god answered it. "Time runs strangely around a sealed thing. You were twenty when you made your vow. You are thirty-six now, and you were also twenty yesterday. Ask your wolf."

Bram's golden eyes held steady. "You're older than your ledger says, Rafa. But not by much. The Guild found you nine years ago. Before that—" he huffed, almost a laugh, "—before that is the part you'll be trading away. Again. Lucky you."

"It isn't fair," she said. "Any of it."

"No," the god agreed. "It never was. Fairness was the first casualty. I could only save them from dying twice. I could not save them from being robbed. Neither could you. You just tried anyway." Something moved in the winter-deep eyes — respect, or its fossil record. "It's why I chose your vow to seal the door. Gladly given grief holds better than any iron."

* * *

Rafael stood. Brushed off her knees. Did not cry, because she was saving it, the way you save coin, the way you save breath, the way you save the last warm thing until the exact moment spending it matters most.

"Terms," she said. "Before I pay. I'm still a bookkeeper."

"Name them."

"One. When spring comes, they come back to their names. All of them. Written back into existence, into maps, into memory — including mine. I will remember this country exists. I will remember what I did here."

"Granted."

"Two. The dead die honestly, and the living grieve honestly, and neither is hidden. No more beautiful lies. If this country wakes to forty-one years of loss, it wakes to the truth of it."

"Granted. Truth is the only seed that grows after a winter that long."

"Three." Her voice went rough. She let it. "When he goes — when the memory unwrites — I want it to hurt. All of it. No mercy numbness, no blessed forgetting of the forgetting. If I gave him gladly once and blindly, I'll give him gladly twice with my eyes open, and I will feel every coin of it."

The god was quiet a moment. "That term costs you more than the payment itself."

"I know. That's why I'm naming it." Rafael crouched down, eye level with the wolf, close enough to see the frost caught in his fur, close enough to count the gray starting at his muzzle that she had never let herself wonder about. "Hey," she said. "Last chance to tell me your real name."

He pressed his forehead against hers. Warm. Impossibly, stubbornly warm, the way he had been every cold night on the road, and she understood suddenly that he had been warm because she remembered warmth through him and nothing else.

"You'll give it back to yourself," Bram said. "On the other side. First entry in your own hand. That's the deal you're making, even if you haven't said it yet."

"I hate that you know me better than I know myself."

"That's what the best of someone is for."

She closed her eyes. Opened them. "Goodbye, then. Whoever you were. Thank you for finding me twice."

"Thank you for paying twice," he said. "Gladly."

And she opened her hands, and gave him back.

* * *

There was no light show. No tearing of the sky. The god simply reached out with both frost-browed hands, and lifted something out of Rafael's chest the way you lift a coal out of a fire, and the cold where it had been was total and clean.

Rafael screamed. She had asked to feel all of it, and she felt all of it: a name — *his* name, blazing bright for one instant before it guttered — a hand on her shoulder in a burning doorway, laughter, an argument about theology settled over cards, the word *Rafa* said in a hundred tones, the vow, the gladness, the gladness, the gladness—

Then snow.

Then the snow shuddered.

Then the bell-sound broke — not faded, *broke*, like ice going out of a river all at once — and the winter fell upward.

It went in sheets, in veils, forty-one years of held snow rising off the hills like breath finally released, and beneath it the land emerged brown and waiting and starving for sun. On the slopes below the gate, the frozen villagers finished their gestures. A woman mid-wave completed the wave. A boy mid-run hit the ground running. A priest mid-prayer arrived at amen. Some of them wept where they stood. Some of them called names into the warming air. Some of them simply sat down in the wet grass and breathed, raggedly, like people surfacing from deep water, alive, alive, alive, and done pretending otherwise.

The rain began — soft, ordinary, miraculous rain — and Rafael stood at the open gate with empty hands and a hollow chest and let it soak her through.

The god was already fading, unraveling into weather, into warmth, into the smell of thawing earth. At the last, she looked less like a shepherd and more like a woman finally allowed to sit down after forty-one years of standing.

"You kept your terms," she said. "So will I. Check your pocket, bookkeeper."

Rafael's fingers found paper. Her ledger — the small one, the travel copy — dry against her skin despite the rain. She opened it. The pages had stopped lying. Every rewritten debt was struck through in a hand like melting frost. And on the final page, blank white, waiting, a single line of instruction:

*Write what you bought.*

She had no pen. She used the edge of her thumbnail dipped in mud and rain, and her handwriting was terrible, and she wrote it anyway, pressing hard enough to tear:

*Bought: one country, returned to its name.*
*Sold: Bram — [the space where his name had been] — given gladly, twice.*
*Balance owed: none.*
*Carried forward: spring.*

Below it, without letting herself think, without giving the grief time to argue, she wrote four more words. Not a debt. An entry. The first line of whatever ledger came next:

*He liked to be called Wolf.*

She read it and something in her broke open and something in her knitted shut in the same motion, grief and belonging passing through each other like travelers at a border.

Behind her, the country went on waking. Ahead, the road ran down out of the hills into a world that had a new-old nation on its maps, a Guild that would need someone to audit what happened when prayers got answered, and rain falling on everything equally, generously, like something owed and finally paid.

Rafael Underhill closed the ledger, tucked it against her heart where the cold spot was already learning to be just a scar, and walked out of a country that existed, carrying nothing but spring.

Somewhere behind her — or ahead, or nowhere at all — a wolf barked once, bright and brief, like punctuation.

She smiled, and did not look back, because looking back was for debts, and she owed no one.

But she marked the sound down anyway. In her own hand. In ink that would not wash out.

Some entries, she suspected, she would be carrying forward a very long time.
