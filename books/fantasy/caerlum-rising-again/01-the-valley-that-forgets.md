## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The goats of Caerlum Valley are the only congregation I've kept these ten years, and frankly they're the better audience. They don't ask questions about my past, they don't flinch when lightning rolls over the peaks — well, mostly don't; Bramble is a coward and we all know it — and they have never once suggested that I might be responsible for a royal death, a brother's death, or the general moral decline of the eastern provinces.

I was mucking out the byre when the pilgrim came.

You learn to read weather in Caerlum, even dead weather. The valley sits in a bowl of grey stone where magic went to die a thousand years ago, which is precisely why they send people like me here. Storm-callers, hedge-witches, one very memorable necromancer who now makes excellent cheese two farms over. Nothing supernatural survives the crossing. You can stand at the northern pass with your palm full of fire and watch it gutter out like a candle in a rain barrel. The valley forgets you were ever anything but flesh and bone and bad decisions.

So when the air above the eastern ridge went *wrong* — not storm-wrong, nothing so honest as that, but wrong the way a room goes wrong when someone's been crying in it — I set down my pitchfork and stood very still, because ten years of numbness had taught me exactly one skill worth keeping: recognizing trouble before it introduced itself.

He crawled into view an hour before dusk. A pilgrim, from the rag of a traveling cloak still clinging to his shoulders, though the cloak had lost its argument with whatever had happened to him somewhere on the mountain road. He dragged himself through the bracken like a man swimming upstream, leaving a dark thread behind him on the stones.

"Goat girl," he rasped, when I reached him. He'd mistaken me for something younger and less bitter, which was flattering. "Goat girl, you have to — the gods are answering again."

"Well," I said, kneeling beside him, "that's nice for them."

He laughed, and the laugh cost him blood at the corner of his mouth. He was maybe fifty, sunburnt to leather, hands calloused in the pattern of a man who'd walked roads his whole life. Three wounds in him that I could count without trying: a slash across the belly that had been bound with a shirt torn into strips and soaked through twice over, a puncture below the collarbone, and something internal that made his breathing sound like a bellows full of gravel.

"Who did this?" I asked.

"Doesn't matter." His fingers found my wrist with surprising strength. "Listen. Listen to me. I prayed. Do you understand? I lay dying on the road above the pass and I prayed to Vessar the Just, the way my mother taught me, the way nobody's taught in thirty years because everyone says the old gods are sleeping or dead or never were. And something answered."

"People say lots of things when they're bleeding out," I told him gently. "I once heard a man swear the moon owed him money."

"It healed me." He pulled aside the sodden bandage with shaking hands.

I want to be precise about what I saw, because precision was all I had left after the trial took everything else. In Caerlum, wounds do what wounds do. They bleed, they fester, they close slowly if you're lucky and kill you if you're not. What I saw instead was the gash across his belly drawing closed while I watched — not knitting, not scarring, but *sealing*, edges flowing together like wax under a flame, and along the seam ran a thin line of light. Gold-white, warm as lamplight, utterly impossible.

My hands went cold. Not with fear, exactly. With recognition. That light had a texture to it, a flavor, the way storms have a smell before they break. It was power. Real power, moving through a valley where power came to be forgotten.

"That's not possible," I said.

"I know," said the pilgrim, and grinned up at me with bloody teeth like this was the best joke he'd ever been in on. "That's why I walked down here instead of dying on the road. I wanted to see it fail. I wanted to watch it *not work* somewhere." He coughed. "It worked anyway."

"Where did you pray?" I asked. My voice had gone strange and flat, the voice I used at trial, the voice that got me through ten years of goat shit and silence. "Above the pass, you said. Where?"

"There's a shrine." His eyes had begun to wander, fixing on things behind me — the ridgeline, the sky, some middle distance only he could see. "Old one, off the pilgrim road, half-swallowed by the hill. My grandmother's grandmother prayed there. I thought — I was dying anyway, so I thought it couldn't hurt to try." He gripped my wrist again, harder. "But it isn't Vessar answering. Or it is, and it isn't. It answers too fast. Too *gladly*. Like a dog that's been starving behind a door and finally someone opened it. And it doesn't ask anything back, goat girl, it doesn't ask for sacrifice or penance, it just — takes. I could feel it taking. Every prayer in the kingdom, funneling down into that hill like water into a drain, and something drinking."

"What does it take?" I asked.

"Everything," he whispered. "Faith. Memory. The king—" and then he shuddered all over, and the light along his healing wound flickered like a lamp in wind, and I understood with a clarity that felt like falling that whatever had answered him was still holding the thread.

"Rest," I said. "I'll bring water. There's a woman two valleys over who knows bonesetting better than any surgeon—"

"No time." He caught my sleeve. "No time, no time. Listen. If it answered *me*, it'll answer anyone. Anyone. Do you understand what that means? Thirty years of silence and suddenly every desperate soul who kneels gets an answer, and the answers are sweet, oh they're sweet, they heal you and lift you and tell you everything you're suffering is for a purpose—" His breath rattled. "Sweetness like that has a bill attached. Sweetness like that always has a bill."

"You've met my last decade, have you?"

He almost smiled. Then his face changed — went slack, then focused, then afraid, three expressions in the space of a breath, like a door being tried from the other side. The light beneath his skin pulsed once, twice, and I swear on Tam's grave that for a moment I could hear something underneath the pulse. A rhythm. Patient. Enormous. A heartbeat the size of a cathedral, beating somewhere far below the valley floor.

The pilgrim's mouth moved. What came out wasn't his voice. It was layered, doubled, a choir compressed into one throat, and it said:

"*Storm-child.*"

Every hair on my body stood up. Bramble, forty feet away and safely stupid, bolted for the far fence.

"You shouldn't be able to reach me," I said. Which was true, and insufficient, and we both knew it. "This valley is dead. Whatever you are, you can't follow me here. That was the whole arrangement."

"*Dead,*" the voice agreed, amused, terrible. "*Forgotten. But forgetting runs both ways, little caller. And the crown has been praying very loudly indeed.*"

The pilgrim gasped, and he was himself again — or mostly himself, staring up at me with his own terrified human eyes. "Did it speak? Did it speak to you?"

"It said my name wrong," I lied. It hadn't gotten my name wrong at all. *Storm-child* was what my mother called me before anyone knew what I was, before the trial, before the exile, before Tam.

"The king," the pilgrim whispered, urgent now, hauling himself half upright against my shoulder. "I heard it say — I heard prayers going *up*, goat girl, thousands of them, from the capital, from the court itself, and the thing drinking them wears — it wears—" He seized. Not metaphorically. His whole body arched, the impossible light blazing along every seam of him, bright enough to throw shadows at dusk, and through his clenched teeth came that doubled voice again, closer now, patient as rot:

"*Tell her the crown is hollow. Tell her the chapel eats. Tell her—*"

And then, blessedly, horribly, the light simply went out. All of it, at once, like a snuffed candle. The pilgrim collapsed against me, breathing shallow but alive, his wounds — I checked — still sealed, still whole, a gift he hadn't asked for sitting in his flesh like a hook.

For a long moment I just knelt there in the bracken, holding a stranger who smelled of road dust and blood and something sweeter underneath, something like incense gone to rot. Above us the first stars came out over Caerlum, indifferent and grey, and the valley did what the valley always does: forgot. The air smoothed over. The wrongness drained away like water through sand.

Except it didn't, quite. Because I could still feel it, faint as a scar-ache before rain. Something on the other side of the mountains, drinking. Something wearing — the pilgrim had tried to tell me. Wearing what? A crown, maybe. A king.

Ten years I'd spent learning to be nobody. Jonah Fairweather, goat-tender, storm-caller retired, sister of the late and lamented Tam, whose death sat on my conscience like a stone I'd swallowed and learned to digest. Ten years of the valley's merciful forgetting, and in one evening a dying pilgrim had crawled into my life carrying proof that the world outside had teeth again — and worse, that the teeth knew *my* name.

"Goat girl," the pilgrim murmured, drifting toward real unconsciousness now. "There's a knight looking for witnesses. Sent to silence them. He'll come down the pass within days. You should run."

"A knight," I repeated flatly. "Sent by whom?"

"By the throne." His eyes fluttered shut. "By whatever wears it."

I sat there until full dark, doing the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the light first flickered along his wound. I could drag him to my cabin, keep him alive, ask more questions. I could leave him in the bracken and go back to my goats and pretend the last hour had been a fever dream, the way I'd pretended away ten years of dreams about thunder and my brother's face.

Or I could walk up to that shrine on the mountain road and find out what had come back to the world, and whether it needed a storm to put it down.

I'm not proud of how long I considered option two.

In the end I got the pilgrim upright, got his arm over my shoulders, and started the long stagger toward my cabin, Bramble watching us from the fence line with the profound judgment available only to livestock.

Behind us, far to the east, past the pass, past the hills, something vast turned over in its sleep and began, softly, to pray in a thousand borrowed voices.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat by the fire with the pilgrim breathing on my floor and Tam's memory sitting on my chest like the old familiar weight, and I listened to the wind test the shutters the way it did every night, harmless wind, dead-valley wind, and I thought: *it spoke to me. Out of everything living, it picked me.*

Because it knew what I was. Because somewhere out there, prayers were being answered again, and the last storm-caller in the world had just been found.

Toward dawn, the pilgrim woke, lucid and grey-faced, and gripped my hand.

"One more thing," he said. "Before the knight comes. Before anything." He swallowed. "The voice. When it wore me. It said a word I didn't understand until later, thinking on it. It said: *unbound.* It said the unbound one was hiding in the valley that forgets." His eyes searched mine, and whatever he saw there made him go pale in a new way. "You know what that means, don't you?"

I fed another stick into the fire and watched it catch.

"Yes," I said. "Unfortunately, I think I'm beginning to."
