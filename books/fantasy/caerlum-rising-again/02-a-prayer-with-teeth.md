## 2. A Prayer With Teeth

The pilgrim died at dawn, which was considerate of him, because I had goats to milk and grief doesn't wait for anyone's convenience.

I buried him behind the terraced wall where the soil runs thin and gray, the way all soil in Caerlum runs, and I said the words my grandmother taught me — the old words, the ones that used to mean something before the crown decided meaning was a threat to public order. *Ash to ash. Breath to wind. The road goes on without you.* Then I sat back on my heels, wiped my hands on my apron, and admitted to myself what I'd been refusing to admit since he crawled out of the mist two days ago.

His wounds had closed.

Not healed. Healed is a slow thing, a human thing, a scab-and-pus-and-time thing that apothecaries can help along with poultices and patience. What happened to that man's side was different. The gash had been open to the bone when he staggered into my yard, and by morning it was a seam of new pink skin, smooth as a scar from a wound taken years ago. And around that seam, faint as breath on cold glass, his skin had glowed.

Nothing glows in Caerlum.

That's the whole point of this valley. That's why they sent me here ten years ago instead of sending me to the headsman, though some days I've wondered which was crueler. Caerlum eats magic. It swallows prayer like sand swallows rain. Whatever power a person carries walks into this valley and lies down and does not get up again. For ten years I have been a storm-caller with no storms in me, a weapon rusting quietly in its sheath, and I have been *grateful* for it. Grateful the way only someone who has killed with her own hands can be grateful for disarmament.

And then a dying man crawled into my yard glowing like a votive lamp, whispering that the gods were answering prayers again, and I did not know what frightened me more: that it was true, or that some small starved part of me wanted it to be.

I covered him with stones and went inside and made tea and drank it staring at nothing, and when the tea was gone I packed a bag, because apparently I am the kind of coward who cannot sit still in her own dread.

* * *

The pilgrim had come from the north, up the old shepherd tracks, and dying men do not wander far from their paths. I followed his trail the way you follow any story: by what people leave behind. A torn scrap of pilgrim's cloth on a hawthorn branch. Boot prints in the mud of the ford, one foot dragging. And then, as the valley narrowed toward the cliffs where Caerlum forgets itself into stone, something stranger.

Flowers.

Snowdrops, out of season, growing in a perfect line along the track like a procession. In magic-dead soil. I crouched over them for a long moment, feeling the way you feel when a dog growls at an empty corner of the room. Nothing there. Everything wrong.

"Right," I told them. "You're not flowers. You're breadcrumbs." And I kept walking, because the alternative was going home to my goats and pretending, and I'd done ten years of pretending and it fit me like a boot two sizes too small.

The shrine sat in a hollow beneath the cliffs, half-swallowed by ivy, so old that the carvings on its lintel predated the kingdom itself. I knew the style from my grandmother's stories — the First Gods, the ones worshipped before Aldric's line crowned themselves and appointed heaven a government department. The hollow should have been silent. Hollows are always silent. Instead I heard humming.

Not a voice. Not quite. A sound like a struck bell heard through three walls of earth, low and constant, and underneath it, rising from the shrine's mouth like smoke, a thread of light so pale you'd miss it if you weren't looking for the absence of dark.

Offerings lay piled at the threshold. Bread, still fresh. Coins. A lock of hair braided with red ribbon. A child's shoe.

As I watched, the light took the shoe. It didn't grab it; it didn't move at all, really. The shoe simply stopped being *here*, the way a word stops meaning anything if you say it too many times. One moment it rested among the offerings. The next it was elsewhere, drawn upward along the thread of light into whatever waited above or beyond, and the hum deepened by a fraction, satisfied, fed.

I am forty-one years old and I have called down lightning and lived, and I stood in that hollow with my heart hammering like a girl's because a shoe disappeared.

Prayers are supposed to go up. That's the arrangement. You speak your fear and hope into the air, and something on the other side hears it, and sometimes — rarely, honestly, rarely — it answers. But answers cost. Every child knows the old gods take payment, and every adult pretends they don't remember learning it. This thing wasn't taking prayers. It was taking *objects*. Things touched by love. Things carried close to skin and heart. It was eating devotion straight from the source, and leaving snowdrops in return like a merchant making change.

"That's not answering," I said aloud, to no one, because saying things aloud keeps you sane in hollows. "That's fishing."

"You shouldn't be here."

I turned. I'll grant him this much: he moved well for a big man, coming around the cliff path with his hand nowhere near his sword, which is where trained killers keep their hands when they want you to think they're friendly.

He was maybe fifty, built like a door, wearing travel clothes that had once been very good and were now very carefully mended. His cloak pin was cheap iron shaped like a sword — the mark of a knight without a house, which in this kingdom meant either traitor or corpse-in-waiting, sometimes both. His face had the particular weathering of a man who has spent years being rained on and disapproving of it.

"I live here," I said. "Technically, you're the one who shouldn't be here. Caerlum's exile land. No knights allowed."

"I'm not a knight anymore." He said it flatly, the way you'd say *I'm not tall* — a fact worn smooth from handling. "Brannic Vael."

"Jonah Fairweather." I watched his face for the flinch. Everyone knows my name; it's the name mothers use to frighten children about storms. To his credit, the flinch was small. "Let me guess. You're here for the view."

"I'm here because a knight came north last week and hasn't reported back, and I'm the sort of man they send when reports stop coming." His eyes went past me, to the shrine, to the thread of light, and something crossed his face that I recognized, having worn it myself for ten years. Shame has a shape. It sits differently on everyone but it's always the same weight. "Did you touch anything?"

"The shoe vanished on its own."

"It takes what's been loved hardest." He said it like a man quoting scripture he hated. "The longer it feeds, the bigger its appetite gets. Bread first. Then keepsakes. Then—" He stopped.

"Then what?"

"Then it doesn't need to wait to be offered." He looked at me directly for the first time. "It starts taking people who pray."

The hum beneath us shifted pitch. Just slightly. Like a throat clearing.

We both stepped back from the shrine at exactly the same moment, which would have been funny under other circumstances, and Brannic gestured up the cliff path with the weary courtesy of a man inviting me to his own execution. "Walk with me. Please. Not because I'm ordering — I don't order anyone anymore. Because in about an hour, six men in royal colors are going to come down this track, and their orders are simpler than mine were."

"Which are?"

"Kill everyone who's seen it." He started walking, and after a moment, hating myself a little, I followed. "Including each other, if it comes to that. Silence travels cheapest by subtraction."

* * *

We walked most of the afternoon, and he talked the way guilty men talk — in pieces, sideways, offering fragments and watching to see which ones I picked up.

He'd been sworn to House Aldric for thirty years. Shield-arm to the king's father, then to Aldric himself. "A good man, once," he said, staring at the track ahead. "Stiff. Proud. The kind of king who thinks mercy is a tax he pays grudgingly. But his own. When he spoke, it sounded like him."

"And now?"

"Now it sounds like him better than he ever did." Brannic's jaw worked. "A year ago the court noticed the king had become devout. Praying at dawn, at dusk, at midnight. Answering petitions personally — personally! — with wisdom no one knew he had. The priests wept with joy. The chancellors wept harder, because a pious king signs whatever the temple puts in front of him." He laughed, and it was an ugly sound. "I saw him mid-audience turn to a widow begging grain for her village, and his eyes went gold like lamplight through honey, and he told her everything she needed to hear, and she left weeping with gratitude, and I stood at the door with my spear and thought: *that is not a man being helped by a god. That is a man being worn.*"

"So you spoke up."

"I spoke up." His mouth twisted. "I was given a choice. Kneel at the new chapel and swear to the god in the king, or take a pension in the provinces and forget what I saw. I took the pension." He glanced at me. "Cowardice keeps surprisingly well. Like salted meat."

"They sent you to silence witnesses anyway."

"They send me because I'm deniable and thorough, and because I'm already damned, so what's one more?" He shrugged. "But I got here first, and I found the shrine, and I found the pilgrim's blood on the track, and I sat outside your valley for two days deciding whether I could do it. Whether I could kill a woman tending goats because she'd seen a light."

"And?"

"And then you talked to the flowers," he said, "and I thought: she's mad, or she's honest, and either way I can't put a sword in her." He stopped walking. We'd reached the ridge above my valley, the whole gray bowl of Caerlum spread below us, my croft a smudge of smoke against the evening. Behind us, far off on the north track, I could see dust. Riders. Six of them, maybe seven, moving fast.

Brannic looked at the dust, then at me, and I watched thirty years of oaths finish dying behind his eyes.

"I won't fight them," he said. "I know all their names. I trained half of them." His hand finally went to his sword, but loosely, the grip of a man holding onto something rather than reaching for it. "But I can stand between you and them long enough for you to run. That's not courage. That's arithmetic. One disgraced knight against six — it buys minutes, not safety."

"Why?" It came out harsher than I meant. "You don't know me. You knew my name and you still followed me up a mountain. Why spend your last shreds of honor on me?"

He was quiet for a long time. Below us the light failed; the riders' dust caught the last orange of it like a banner.

"Because at the trial," he said finally, "ten years ago, when they condemned you — I was in the second row. Full armor. And I believed it. Storm-caller kills her own brother; of course she did; power like that is a loaded crossbow in a nursery. I nodded along with everyone else." His voice dropped. "Last week I watched a god eat a child's shoe and call it grace, and I understood that believing easy things is how kingdoms rot. Somebody lied to me about you, Jonah Fairweather, or somebody lied to everybody, and either way I helped hold you under the water while they did it."

The wind came up the valley, smelling of stone and goat and ten years of my small careful life, and I thought about Tam, and about how truth arrives — never politely, never when you've tidied up for it, but crashing through the kitchen door tracked in mud.

"Minutes, then," I said. "Buy me minutes."

"That's the plan."

"And after the minutes run out?"

"After that," Brannic Vael said, drawing his sword at last and turning to face the riders coming up the track, "I imagine we improvise."

Down in the valley, faint across the distance, I could have sworn I heard the shrine begin to hum.
