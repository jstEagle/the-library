## 3. What Wears the Crown

I have killed men for less than what I'm about to tell you, and I've lied to kings for more, so understand that what comes out of my mouth next costs me something. A knight's whole trade is knowing which oaths to keep and which to bury in the yard at midnight with a shovel and no witnesses. I buried one oath ten years ago. I was about to dig up another.

Jonah Fairweather walked beside me through the capital's rain-slick streets with her hood up and her hands in her pockets like a woman who had decided the world could come get her if it wanted, but she wasn't going to make it easy. She hadn't spoken since the shrine. Since Brannic — that's me, mind, best not forget it — had put his sword down in the mud instead of through her ribs.

"You keep looking over your shoulder," she said finally.

"I keep *seeing* things over my shoulder. Different problem entirely."

"Then say what you dragged me out here to say. You didn't break twelve years of knighthood to give me a walking tour."

Fair. The woman had a way of cutting straight to the bone of a thing. It was, I suspected, what got her exiled in the first place — storm-callers are not known for their talent for small talk.

So I stopped under the eave of a chandler's shop, where the tallow smell covered us from passing ears, and I told her.

"The king hasn't spoken in his own voice for a year."

She went very still. Rain ran off the eave between us like a curtain.

"That's not true," she said. "Aldric addressed the solstice court. My mother's letters—"

"Your mother's letters describe a king who speaks in measured, holy cadences, who weeps during prayers, whose words come out smooth as poured honey." I watched her face do the arithmetic. "That's not Aldric. Aldric swore like a dockhand when the council wasn't listening. He laughed too loud. He once ended an audience because his boot heel snapped and he thought it was hilarious. The thing on the throne now doesn't laugh. The thing on the throne now *blesses* people."

"Blesses them."

"With both hands, Jonah. Like a priest. And the court eats it up. They call it piety. They call it his grief for the old queen ripening into holiness. Ser Vayne calls it a miracle of devotion and has the man's words copied down and nailed to chapel doors across six districts." I heard my own voice go flat and bitter. "I stood honor guard at the winter audience. Eleven months ago now. A grain merchant came before him, begging relief on tariffs — his daughter was sick, he needed coin, he was weeping, snot and all, the kind of crying you can't fake. And the king looked at him. Just looked. And his eyes went gold."

"Gold," Jonah repeated. Not a question so much as a door opening somewhere behind her eyes, and whatever was behind it, she didn't like the room.

"Like lamp-oil catching. Like something looked *out* through him. And then the king spoke, and his voice was wrong — too many harmonics, like a choir squeezed down one throat — and the merchant's sickness lifted right there on the marble. Knitted away. The whole court fell to its knees weeping, and I stood there holding a spear thinking, *that's not healing, that's fishing.* You don't answer a prayer like that unless you want more prayers."

Jonah's hand had gone to her collarbone. Old habit, I'd learn — she touched the place where a storm-caller's oath-mark used to sit like other people touch a scar.

"The pilgrim," she said quietly. "In the valley. His wounds knit with light. I thought the valley was failing."

"The valley isn't failing. It's being *reached*. Whatever wears the crown has been growing hungrier, and hungry things reach further." I pushed off the wall. "Come on. If I'm committing treason, I'd rather do it indoors."

* * *

The sealed royal chapel sits behind the Hall of Audiences, connected by a corridor the servants call the Whisper Walk, because sound carries strangely there and everyone pretends it doesn't. Three locks guarded it, once. Now there were four, and the newest one bore the inquisitor's mark — Ser Vayne's little sunburst, all spikes, like a promise made with teeth.

I had the keys. This is the part that should shame me most, so let me be quick about it: I had the keys because for two years I helped maintain the seal. I checked the locks. I reported nothing. I told myself the chapel was closed for sanctity, for mourning, for any of the comfortable lies a tired knight tells himself at the end of a long watch. The rot doesn't start with the locks, Jonah. It starts with the man who holds the keys and decides not to turn them.

Inside, the air was wrong.

I don't mean cold. Cold is honest. I mean the air was *thick*, the way air gets before lightning, except there was no storm coming and never would be — not in this kingdom, not anymore, not since the last storm-caller walked out of it ten years ago and took the sky's permission with her. The light through the stained glass fell in colors but landed colorless, as if the windows were being drained on the way down.

And the walls —

Jonah stopped three steps past the threshold and made a sound low in her throat, the sound of someone meeting a fear they'd already been introduced to.

The walls were veined. Pale gold threads running through the stone like sap through bark, converging toward the altar, thickening as they went. And along those veins, moving slow as honey in winter, traveled — I still reach for the word and come back empty. Prayers. That's the only word that fits. Little bright knots of something, drifting up through the veins, upward always upward, into the rafters, into the dark above the altar where the gold gathered dense as a hive.

"They're being harvested," Jonah said. Her voice had gone strange and quiet. "All those prayers people send up. They're not reaching anything. They're being *eaten*."

"By what's upstairs wearing the king," I said. "Or by what's downstairs wearing the chapel. I honestly couldn't tell you which end of the leash the dog is on."

She crossed herself with the old sign, the one farmers use against blight, then seemed embarrassed about it. "How long?"

"Vayne sealed the public chapels first. Then the private ones. Anyone caught praying outside a consecrated house is fined. Fined for *praying*, Jonah. Do you know what that means?" I gestured at the golden veins, at the slow bright freight moving through them. "It means the supply is managed now. Every whispered plea from every sickbed and scaffold and soldier's ditch in this kingdom gets funneled here, bottled, and drunk. The god answers some — the ones it wants answered, publicly, loudly, to draw the faithful closer. The rest it just... keeps. Interest on the principal."

"And the court?"

"The court kneels deeper every season. Vayne preaches that unanswered prayers are tests of faith, and answered ones are proofs of it, and either way you should pray harder. He's not stupid, Vayne. He's worse than stupid. He's *sincere*."

Jonah walked toward the altar. I should have stopped her. I want that recorded — the part of me that was still, technically, a knight knew I should have stopped her. But my feet had stopped taking orders from my oaths somewhere around the second lock.

Up close, the gold in the walls pulsed. Slow. Rhythmic. And I realized, watching her stand beneath it with her head tipped back, that it pulsed in time with something. Not a heartbeat. Slower than that. Deeper.

"Brannic." Her voice came out scraped. "This rhythm. Count it."

I counted. Sixty-two beats to the minute, maybe sixty-three. A resting heart. A large man's resting heart.

"It's synced to him," she said. "To Aldric. Whatever this root is, it beats inside him. Every prayer they harvest feeds it, and everything it takes, it pours into keeping him — keeping the body alive, keeping the voice sweet, keeping the gold eyes hidden between audiences." She turned to look at me, and in the drained light her face was ten years older than it had been that morning. "He's not possessed, Brannic. Possession is a fight. This is a *marriage*. Something climbed into the king and the king let it, or was too broken to stop it, and now neither of them can leave."

I thought of Aldric as I'd known him — loud, kind, hopeless at cards, ruined by grief after the queen died. A man with a hole in him shaped exactly like the thing that filled it. That's how they get in. Not through gates. Through grief.

"So we go to the council," I said, and hated how thin it sounded even as I said it. "We document this. Half the lords would turn on Vayne tomorrow if—"

"Would they?" Jonah asked. "Which half? The half whose tariffs he lightens? The half whose dying children he heals on festival days, in front of crowds, with light pouring off the king's hands while the mother screams thanks to a god that's fattening itself on her neighbors' despair?" She shook her head slowly. "You don't fight a thing like this with evidence, Brannic. Evidence requires people willing to look. Everyone who matters is being *fed*."

The truth of it settled over the chapel like snowfall. Somewhere above us, faint through the vaulting, came the sound of the evening service beginning in the great nave — hundreds of voices rising together in petition, and beneath my boots I swear I felt the veins in the stone quicken, eager, the way a table quickens when dinner is carried in.

"We need a storm-caller," I said.

"You're looking at the valley's finest goat-tender."

"I'm serious, Jonah."

"So am I." She looked down at her own hands, flexed them, as though checking whether the tools were still attached. "Whatever I was, it died with my brother. What's left of me can't call a drizzle."

"Then we find what's left of you," I said, "and we find whoever else this thing has orphaned, because I'll tell you what I know that the court doesn't: Vayne has started drawing up lists. Storm-blooded families. Names going back generations. And tomorrow at dawn, the purge begins — quietly, house by house, before anyone can connect the disappearances."

Jonah went white. I watched her do it — watched the blood leave her face in real time, watched her think of names I didn't know yet, people she'd spent ten years trying not to think about.

"How long until dawn?"

I looked toward the narrow window, where the drained light was failing toward violet.

"Four hours. Maybe five."

She was moving before I finished answering, and for the record — since apparently I'm confessing everything tonight — I followed her without hesitation, without one backward glance at the keys in my hand or the oaths they belonged to. Some doors, once opened, only open outward.

Behind us, in the dark above the altar, the harvested prayers drifted upward into the gold, patient as sediment, and far beneath the chapel floor — though neither of us knew it yet — something ancient turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to listen.
