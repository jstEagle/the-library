## 4. The Company of the Unwanted

The purge began on a Tuesday, because cruelty in Vaelmark always kept good administrative hours.

I learned about it the way I learned most things those days — from a stranger running faster than dignity allows. She was a laundress with her sleeves still pinned up, and she grabbed my arm outside the tannery as though we'd been friends since the cradle.

"They're taking the storm-blooded," she said. "Every house with the mark. They came for the Corvane family at dawn. The *children*, Jonah."

I should have run then. Any sensible exile would have. But Brannic had gone to the records hall that morning to pull whatever remained of my trial documents, and Sefa was across town delivering Maren's tinctures, and Old Ivo had been arrested twice already this year for offenses so obscure even the inquisitors needed a glossary. My found family had scattered itself across the capital like spilled coins, and I discovered that ten years of careful numbness dissolved instantly in the presence of other people's danger.

So instead of fleeing, I spent the day doing the single most stupid thing available: collecting them.

* * *

Sefa I found first, sitting cross-legged on the apothecary's step with her hands over her ears and an expression I recognized from mirrors — the look of someone hearing something the world insists isn't there.

"The gods are loud today," she said when I crouched beside her. Not a question. "Like bees. Angry bees. Someone's praying very hard and getting *nothing back*, and they're furious about it."

"Whose prayers?"

"A lady in a tall house." Sefa's dark eyes fixed on me with that terrible child-clarity that made lying to her feel like lying to weather. "She has storm-blood too. Jonah, they're going to her door right now."

We ran.

We did not arrive in time. By the time we reached the tall house, the lady — a soft-spoken widow named Corvane, whose only crime was a grandmother who'd once called rain onto a drought field — was being walked out in irons while neighbors watched from behind shutters with the practiced blindness of people who intended to survive the decade. A knight held her daughter by the wrist. The girl couldn't have been more than eight.

Something in my chest, some door I'd nailed shut over Tam's grave, rattled on its hinges.

"We can't fight six knights," Sefa whispered.

"No," I agreed. "But we can be witnesses." Because that was the other thing the valley taught you: what it costs to look away.

I stepped into the street. Unremarkable Jonah Fairweather, goat-tender, tanner's widow-in-law, nobody — except the youngest knight saw my face and went pale, and I understood with cold clarity that my description was circulating through the city faster than bread prices. *Storm-caller. Exiled. Do not approach alone.*

They didn't arrest us. They marked us. The knight drew a small sigil in ash on our doorway as they left, and Sefa stared at it and said, very quietly, "That's a hunting sign. That means *tonight*."

* * *

Maren was packing when we got there, which told me she'd heard before we had. The apothecary moved with the economy of a woman who'd been burned out of three homes before this one — literally burned, once, in Alderholt, for the heresy of treating a plague god's victims with medicine instead of prayer. Her forearms still carried the scars; she never covered them, calling them "receipts."

"Back door," she said by way of greeting, thrusting a satchel at me. "Poppy milk, willow bark, coagulant paste, and the good brandy, which is medicinal if you're sad enough. Take the front nothing. We leave through the graves."

"The graves?"

"Old Ivo's tunnel starts under the memorial wall. He'll complain the entire way but he'll come." She paused, looking at me with those flat gray eyes that missed nothing. "You're going after Brannic."

"He'll be at the records hall until dusk. He doesn't know—"

"He knows. That man reads danger the way priests read scripture. Go. Sefa stays with me." And when I hesitated: "Jonah. You cannot save anyone if you spend yourself like a drunkard spends coin. Fetch your knight. Be quick. Be *unremarkable*."

I want it noted, for posterity, that I was unremarkable for nearly four whole streets.

* * *

Brannic wasn't at the records hall. He was in the alley behind it, holding off two of the king's men with his back against a wall and a stolen quarterstaff, and he looked — there's no kind way to put this — delighted. Disgraced knights miss their work the way retired hounds miss the hunt, and Brannic Ossian had been given permission, at last, to stop pretending he was sorry about anything.

I broke a clay pot of Maren's coagulant paste over the first man's head. It was undignified and effective, which describes most of my life since Caerlum.

"Took you long enough," Brannic said, hauling me upright by the arm.

"I brought a child and an apothecary and a heretic elder. You brought a stick."

"My stick has killed eleven men."

"Our stick has killed zero and I'm very proud of it."

He almost smiled. Then the alley mouth darkened with more boots, and the smile became something harder, and we ran together through streets I'd lived in for a year without ever learning their escape routes, because Brannic had learned them all in his first week. Disgraced knights plan their retreats early. It's the one honest habit the crown ever gave him.

Old Ivo met us at the memorial wall, already prying up a slab with a crowbar, muttering rites under his breath that were half prayer and half complaint. "Seventy years I kept the old ways alive in this city," he grumbled, "and now I flee through my own ancestors' bones like a rat in a hymnal. Get in. Mind the third step, it's Tam's cousin's grave and he hated being stepped on in life too."

* * *

The tunnel smelled of earth and candle smoke and, faintly, of the shrine — that wrong sweetness I'd come to dread since the pilgrim crawled into my valley with light knitting his flesh. Sefa felt it too. Halfway through the dark she stopped walking and said, in a voice not entirely her own:

"*The crown is a mouth. The mouth is full.*"

Nobody spoke for a moment. Then Ivo relit his candle and said, gently, "Come along, little prophet. Prophecies keep. Bread doesn't."

That, more than anything, is how we ended up where we ended up: a smuggler's cellar beneath a derelict tannery on the river's wrong side, rented from a woman with no name and paid for with Brannic's last honest silver. A fire in a barrel. Six blankets between five people. Maren brewing something that smelled like pine tar and forgiveness. Ivo laying out a supper of hard bread, river fish, and pickled things he claimed were traditional.

And for one evening — one impossible, borrowed evening — it was warm.

Sefa fell asleep against my shoulder mid-sentence, mid-argument with Ivo about whether the god below the chapel was "old-mean or new-mean." Brannic cleaned his quarterstaff with the tenderness other men reserve for infants. Maren dosed everyone against infection and despair in equal measure. And I sat in the firelight listening to these people — a burned healer, a defrocked knight, a mad old ritualist, a child who heard the divine like weather — and understood that I had done the thing I swore off doing in Caerlum.

I had let myself belong somewhere again.

"You're thinking loudly," Brannic said, without looking up.

"I'm thinking that every person I've loved has been taken from me by something wearing authority."

"Mm. And?"

"And I'm considering making that expensive for it."

He nodded slowly, approving, the way you nod at a storm cloud that's finally decided to get on with it. "Good. Because I've had word from a friend still inside the palace." His jaw tightened. "Tomorrow night is the solstice rite. The king himself will open the chapel doors. And Jonah—" he finally looked at me, and the firelight made gold shadows in his eyes, "—the guest list includes every surviving storm-blooded family in the capital. He's gathering them, Jonah. All of them. In one room. Under one roof."

Maren's ladle stopped mid-stir.

"Why?" I asked, though the cold crawling up my spine had already answered.

"Because whatever wears Aldric," Brannic said quietly, "is hungry tomorrow. And it likes its meals gathered."
