## 5. Storm-Blood

I have bled a great many people in my time, most of them grateful for it. Leeching, lancing, the letting of humors — there's a whole philosophy of medicine that amounts to *hurt them until they improve*, and I abandoned it years ago along with my faith and my good name. But I had never bled anyone the way I bled Jonah Fairweather that night: with a needle of black iron, a bowl of river water, and three drops of her blood, while Old Ivo chalked sigils on the smuggler's hut floor that made my hands shake just copying them.

"You're certain this is safe," Jonah said. She sat on an upturned crate with her sleeve rolled back, arm out like a woman donating to charity rather than feeding a ritual older than the kingdom.

"No," I said honestly. "That's rather the point. If it were safe, it wouldn't tell us anything."

"That's not comforting."

"I'm an apothecary who was burned at the stake, dear. Comfort was never my trade." The brand on my forearm ached in the damp, as it always did, and I rubbed it absently. "Heresy, they called me. For suggesting that sickness came from bad water and not bad character. So you'll forgive me if I've developed a professional interest in things people insist are dead."

Sefa watched from her corner, knees pulled up, eyes enormous. The child hadn't spoken much since we'd fled the capital — since the purge began, since knights with white hoods went door to door through the storm-blooded quarters asking questions no one survived answering. She'd taken to holding Jonah's hand when the nightmares came, which was often, and neither of them pretended otherwise.

Old Ivo finished the last sigil and sat back on his heels, joints cracking like green firewood. "The rite is old," he said. "Older than the chapel. Older than the crown. Blood remembers what the mouth forgets."

"Poetic," Jonah muttered.

"I'm ninety-one years old. Poetry is all I have left."

I held the iron needle over the candle flame until it glimmered clean, then took Jonah's wrist. Her pulse was steady. Too steady. Ten years of tending goats will give a person calm, but this was something else — the stillness of a pond frozen so deep nothing moves underneath.

"Small pinch," I lied, as every healer has lied since the dawn of needles, and drew the blood.

Three drops into the river water. I swirled the bowl, set it in the center of the sigils, and waited.

For a long moment, nothing. The water stayed red-clouded and dull, and I felt the first cold thread of disappointment — perhaps the valley had truly scoured her, perhaps ten years of Caerlum's silence had done what the trial intended—

Then the water *moved*.

Not stirred. Not sloshed. It rose. Three thin threads of red lifted out of the bowl like roots growing in reverse, climbing the air, and where they touched the candlelight they caught it and burned blue-white, and the whole hut filled with a sound I felt in my teeth before I heard it — a low rolling mutter, distant thunder heard through a mountain.

The candle guttered. Sefa made a small sound. And the blood hung above the bowl, glowing, alive, *awake*.

"Well," said Old Ivo softly. "There it is."

"There what is?" Jonah's voice had gone strange. "What am I looking at?"

I stood very still, watching the blood turn slowly in the air like a small red galaxy. "Your power isn't dead, Jonah. It's sleeping. There's a difference, and the difference matters." I looked at her. "Dead magic leaves nothing. Yours is *fed*. Something has been keeping it alive all these years."

"Caerlum is magic-dead. That's why I chose it."

"Yes, and yet." I gestured at the hanging threads. "Magic doesn't feed itself. Grief does. Storm-calling runs on grief — I read that in books they burned me for owning. Every year of mourning, another log on the fire under the ice." I swallowed. "You haven't been preserving your exile, child. You've been *banking* it."

Jonah stood up so fast the crate tipped over. The blood dropped back into the bowl with a slap, and she stared at it as if it had accused her of something.

Which, I suppose, it had.

* * *

She didn't sleep that night, and neither did I, so we ended up sharing the small hours by the dead hearth, and she talked, and what she talked about was Tam.

"I was seventeen," she said, to the ashes more than to me. "Tam was fourteen. We'd gone up past the shepherd's ridge because he wanted to see lightning close — he was always wanting things closer than was safe. And the storm came early. Off-season, wrong season entirely, and I felt it building in my chest like a second heartbeat, and I thought — I thought if I could just hold it, shape it, send it out over the empty moor where it couldn't hurt anyone—"

Her hands were shaking. I reached over and covered one of them with mine, and she let me.

"It slipped. Or I pushed too hard. I've told myself both stories for ten years and I don't know which one is true anymore." She laughed, and there was nothing funny in it. "The bolt hit the ridge above him. The whole face of it came down. He was laughing when it happened, Maren. He was laughing because he thought I'd done it *on purpose*, for him. A show. For his birthday."

"Oh, child."

"The trial took three days. They asked me forty times whether I meant it. And do you know what I said? I said *it was an accident*. Because the alternative — saying yes, some part of me wanted to show off for him, wanted him to see what I could do — that wasn't an answer anyone would forgive. So I swore the accident story like a sacrament. And the tribunal gave me exile instead of the rope, and I walked into a valley where nothing magical could follow me, including the truth."

The fire had gone fully dark. In the last gray light before dawn I saw her face, and it was the face of a woman standing at the edge of a crack in the ground and realizing it runs beneath her whole house.

"And now you're telling me all that grief — his laugh, the trial, the lie — it hasn't been killing me. It's been *feeding* me."

"Grief makes poor mortar but strong fuel," I said. "I won't pretend to know which way that cuts."

"It cuts like a knife, Maren. It always cuts like a knife."

* * *

It happened two nights later, at supper, and none of us were prepared, though Ivo later claimed he'd half-expected it, which is easy to claim after the fact.

We were eating barley soup around the fire — Brannic had brought salt-pork from gods-knew-where, and even Jonah had admitted it wasn't terrible — when Sefa stopped chewing. Set down her spoon. Stood up.

"Sefa?" Brannic said carefully. He was gentle with her in a way that always surprised people who only knew his sword-hand.

She turned toward Jonah, and her eyes were wrong. Not gold — that would have been almost comprehensible, after everything Brannic had described in the king's court. No. Her eyes were *storm-colored*, gray-green and moving, clouds seen through a keyhole, and when she spoke her voice came out layered, a child's voice with weather behind it.

"*The unbound one sleeps in a valley of forgetting.*"

Jonah went white. "Sefa. Sefa, come back to us."

But the child kept speaking, slow and ringing, each word placed like a stone in a cairn:

"*Grief-fed, oath-sworn, liar by law and truth by blood. The unbound one. She will kneel where prayers are eaten, and she will swear what she has never sworn, and the sky will remember her name.*"

Then Sefa blinked, shivered all over like a wet dog, looked at the seven horrified faces around the fire, and said, in her own ordinary voice: "What? Why is everyone looking at me? Is there more soup?"

Nobody answered. Nobody could.

Jonah rose without a word and walked out into the rain, and I let her go, because some doors a body has to open alone.

But I stood at the window and watched her stand in the downpour with her arms at her sides, head tilted back, letting the storm fall on her face — and I saw, plain as a brand, that she wasn't hiding from it anymore.

She was listening.

And far off across the hills, thunder answered — once, soft, patient, like something that had waited ten years and could wait a little longer, but not forever.

I turned from the window and found Old Ivo beside me, and his old voice was grim as a grave-marker. "Get your kit ready, apothecary. Whatever comes for her next—" he nodded toward the rain, toward Jonah, toward the east where the capital lay like a wound under the horizon— "we'll want to be moving before it arrives."
