## 6. The God Below the Chapel

I am ninety-one years old, and I have spent most of those years learning which doors the gods keep locked and which they leave ajar out of carelessness. Tonight I opened one of the careless ones, and what came through it was neither god nor door anymore, and I want it noted, by whoever keeps the ledger of my sins — probably me, at this point — that I did warn them.

"You'll want to turn back," I said, at the mouth of the catacombs beneath the royal chapel, holding my lantern up like a coward's halo. "There's no shame in it. Well. There's some shame in it. But there's more shame in dying."

"Lead on, Old Ivo," said Brannic, who had once been a knight of the realm and still walked like one, shoulders squared to a wall that wasn't there yet.

"You're all fools," I said, and led on.

That is the shape of my life, if you're wondering: warning fools, then following them into the dark.

* * *

The catacombs predate the chapel that squats above them, and the chapel predates the kingdom, and the kingdom predates every honest thing left in it. My grandfather's grandfather helped dig these tunnels back when digging under a god's house was considered piety rather than treason. The stone down here is old enough to have opinions. It hums. Not with sound — with attention. You learn to feel it after sixty years of forbidden rites and confiscated books: stone that is *listening*.

The others felt it too. Sefa stopped walking first.

"It's loud," she whispered. She is nine years old and hears god-speech the way other children hear their mothers calling them home for supper, which is a gift, or a sentence; we hadn't decided which yet. "It's like — like everyone talking at once but nobody saying anything."

"That," I said, "is the sound of ten thousand prayers going somewhere they shouldn't."

Jonah Fairweather looked at me over her shoulder. She has eyes the color of weather coming, and a face that has practiced not hoping so long it's forgotten how. Ten years in Caerlum will do that to you. Ten years anywhere, probably.

"The pilgrim's wounds knitted themselves shut," she said. "In the valley. Magic isn't supposed to work in Caerlum. So whatever answered him reached *in*. What reaches into a place built to keep it out?"

"A starving thing," I said. "Starving things don't respect walls. They respect food." I lifted the lantern higher. The tunnel ahead sloped downward, slick with condensation, older than the masonry around it. "Come along. And do try not to pray. Any of you. Even small ones. Even 'gods, my feet hurt.' Down here that's not a figure of speech. It's an invitation."

Maren muttered something about her feet hurting, purely to spite me, and I loved her a little for it. Then she went pale, because she understood the joke, and I loved her less. That's marriage, essentially, and we were never married, and never will be, which has kept us both very happy.

* * *

We descended for the better part of an hour. The tunnels narrowed, widened, narrowed again — dug by different hands in different centuries, some reverent, some desperate, one section clearly gnawed at by something patient. Brannic went first once he saw the way, sword drawn, though I could have told him steel would be about as useful as a strongly worded letter. Jonah stayed close to Sefa. The girl held her hand and didn't let go, and Jonah didn't make her, and if you'd told me two months ago that the storm-caller of the trial — the woman whose name they say in pulpits the way you say plague — would be holding a child's hand in the dark like it was the only solid thing left, I'd have called you a liar and charged you extra for the story.

Then the tunnel opened, and I stopped telling stories, because the true thing was in front of us.

* * *

It is difficult to describe the root without lying about it. Language was built for describing bread and grief and the price of mules. But I'll try, since someone has to write this down and everyone else present was either crying, praying despite explicit instructions, or drawing steel at nothing.

The chamber was vast and round, older than any human stonework, its walls veined with channels like the inside of a heart. And in the center stood Vessar.

Not a statue. A presence wearing the shape of one — a figure of grey stone perhaps forty feet tall, kneeling, arms open, palms upward. The pose of welcome. Of receiving. Once upon three thousand years ago, that open posture meant something holy: *bring us your burdens and we will carry them.* Now the arms were open the way a trap is open. Now the palms caught everything that fell and gave nothing back.

Light ran up through the channels in the floor — thin gold threads, pulsing slow as a heartbeat, and each pulse carried something with it. I've handled enough relics to recognize prayer when I see it moving. It looked, if you must know, like light and smoke having an argument. Every thread fed upward into the stone god, into those cupped hands, and vanished.

"He was ours," I heard myself say. My voice came out smaller than I intended. "Before the crown claimed him. Before the chapel. Vessar the Listener. Farmers prayed at his roots for rain and got rain, or didn't, and either way they slept well. He carried what people couldn't. That was the whole of the faith. Carry it for me a while, until I'm strong enough to carry it myself."

"And now?" Maren asked. Her voice shook. Maren does not shake. She once stitched a man's bowel while insulting his cooking.

"Now he carries nothing for anyone," I said. "Now he just *takes*."

Brannic had gone the color of tallow. "The king," he said. "You said the root was bound to Aldric. Where—"

I pointed my lantern at the base of the statue, where the stone knees met the floor. There, half-hidden by the pulsing channels, was a figure. A man, fused into the stone from the waist down, arms raised in the same open gesture, head bowed against the god's knee like a child hiding from thunder.

Aldric. What was left of him.

He was breathing. That was the worst part, though there was competition. His chest rose and fell in time with the golden pulses. His lips moved constantly, silently, shaping words — and Sefa made a small broken noise and pressed both hands over her ears.

"What's he saying?" Jonah asked quietly.

Sefa lowered her hands. She was crying without seeming to notice. "Please," she said. "He's saying please. Over and over. Please stop, please let go, please — please don't use my voice anymore." She swallowed. "And underneath that. Underneath the please. He's counting."

"Counting what?"

"How many prayers are left."

Nobody spoke for a while after that. The chamber filled the silence for us — that low humming attention, the pulse of stolen devotion, ten thousand voices going nowhere, feeding something that had forgotten what it was for.

* * *

Jonah stepped forward. I should have grabbed her. In my defense, I am ninety-one, and awe makes old men slow.

She walked to the edge of the channels and stood looking up at the god's face — serene, blank, beautiful the way a famine can be beautiful from a distance — and the gold light bent toward her. Just slightly. Just a lean, the way flowers lean at sun.

"You know me," she said. It wasn't a question.

The humming changed pitch. Sefa gasped. "*It's speaking,*" she said. "Not to us. To her. It says—" her face screwed up with the effort of translating something that didn't want translating. "It says *storm-child. It says last of the last. It says it has been waiting.*"

"Waiting for what?" Brannic asked, and his hand found his sword hilt again, useless, comforting, human.

Sefa listened. Whatever she heard, she didn't want to say it. Jonah turned and looked at me instead, and I sighed the sigh of a man who has read every banned book in three kingdoms and knew exactly which page this was.

"Tell them," I said. "I'm tired of being the bearer."

I set the lantern down on the channel-stone, where the gold light threw our shadows enormous against the walls — five ragged shapes, and behind us the kneeling god, and inside the god the kneeling king, and I thought, not for the first time, that history is mostly a matter of who kneels where.

"The binding was made by storm-callers," I said. "Three thousand years ago, when Vessar first began to take more than he was given. They couldn't kill a god — nothing kills a god, believe me, people have tried with great enthusiasm. So they bound him. Chained him to a mortal vessel, generation after generation, so that his hunger would be leashed inside a human soul instead of loose across the world. And the binding was woven with an oath. A storm-caller's oath, sworn in truth. That was the lock."

"So another oath unbinds him," Jonah said slowly. "Sworn in truth."

"Yes."

"Well." She almost smiled. Almost. "Good. We found the truth-teller, then. Swear the oath, tear him off Aldric, done before supper."

"There's a wrinkle," I said.

Maren snorted. "There's always a wrinkle. With you there are wrinkles inside the wrinkles."

"The oath doesn't just unlock the chain, girl. An oath sworn in truth has to be *paid for in truth*. That's the whole architecture of the old bindings. Truth is the only currency gods can't counterfeit. Whoever swears it gives up the truest thing they have — the thing the oath turns on. For the ones who bound him, it was their names. Their lineages. They swore away their own existence from the record, and that's why there's no song of the binders, no tomb, no name. They paid themselves."

The chamber hummed. The gold pulsed. Somewhere in the stone, a king counted.

"And for me?" Jonah asked. Her voice was very steady. Steadier than mine would have been, and I've buried everyone I ever loved twice over. "What's the truth I'd pay?"

"I don't know," I lied.

Because I did know. I'd known since Caerlum, since the pilgrim, since the moment I saw her standing over a miracle with her brother's death hanging off her like a wet coat. The oath would turn on the thing she'd spent ten years refusing to say. The storm. Tam. The word *mine*. She would stand in front of a court and a god and confess the truest thing in her, and the binding would drink it, and she would be — emptied? Unmade? Paid out entirely? The books disagree, and the books that agreed with each other most closely were the ones the crown burned first.

But I watched her look up at that beautiful, starving thing, and at the man fused to its knee, counting prayers, whispering *please*, and I saw her decide — not consciously, not yet, but the way stone decides to fall — and I thought: she already knows the price too. She's just not ready to say it out loud.

None of us are ever ready. That's why it costs.

"We should go back up," Brannic said hoarsely. "Plan. Rest. This is — there has to be another way."

"There isn't," I said. "There never is. There's only the way and the bill."

* * *

We climbed back toward the world for a long time in silence. Near the top, Sefa tugged Jonah's sleeve.

"It says one more thing," she whispered. "It says it knows what your truth is. It says it will be gentle when you say it."

Jonah stopped on the stair. Above us, faint through the chapel floor, came the sound of evening bells and, beneath them, the murmur of thousands gathering for the solstice rite — thousands of throats, thousands of prayers, rising like smoke toward cupped stone hands.

"No," Jonah said softly. To the girl. To the dark below. To herself. "When I say it, it won't be gentle at all."
