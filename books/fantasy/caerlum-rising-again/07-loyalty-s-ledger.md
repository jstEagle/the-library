## 7. Loyalty's Ledger

I have broken a great many oaths in my life, and I can report that the breaking itself is easy. It is the accounting afterward that ruins a man.

Take the oath I swore at nineteen, kneeling on cold marble before the Crown: to protect the realm, its king, and its laws. Three things in one breath, as though they were the same thing. Nobody warns you that one day they will pull in three directions until you tear. I spent twenty years believing they were braided together. Then I watched King Aldric's eyes go gold mid-audience like two coins dropped into a dead man's face, and I understood the braid had rotted from the inside, and that everyone still kneeling was pretending not to smell it.

So when Inquisitor Halvard Vane sent word that he held Sefa — our Sefa, eight years old, gap-toothed, prone to naming storms in her sleep — and that he would trade the child for the storm-caller Jonah Fairweather, I did what any man of honor would do.

I lied.

* * *

The meeting place was the ruined tithe-barn at Corrow Ford, half-swallowed by ivy, which suited me fine because ivy makes excellent cover for men who have decided to commit treason before supper. Vane arrived with six knights and a cage.

Not a cart. Not a wagon with blankets and a worried guard. A cage, iron-barred, sized for something smaller than a hound. Sefa sat inside it with her knees pulled up, and she was not crying, which frightened me more than screaming would have. Children who stop crying around Vane have learned what crying earns them.

"Sir Brannic," Vane said, warm as a banked fire, which is the most dangerous temperature an inquisitor can manage. "You look well. Treason agrees with you."

"I've been eating properly."

He smiled. Vane has a face built for smiling, all soft lines and reasonable eyes, the kind of face that makes you feel foolish for suspecting him right up until he burns your village. He gestured at the cage with the tenderness of a man showing off a niece.

"The god-speaker. Small thing, but the Chapel wants her catalogued. You understand. Everything answered must be accounted." He spread his hands. "Bring me Fairweather, and the child walks free. Unharmed. Fed. I give you my word."

"You gave Ser Ollery your word once," I said. "They found his head on the Chapel gate."

"And I wept." He said it without a flicker of shame, which I confess I admired. There is something almost holy about a man so thoroughly done with pretending. "Brannic. Old friend. Old *knight.* Look at where you are. Sleeping in ditches with a heretic apothecary and a rites-keeper old enough to remember when the gods ate people properly. For *her.* For a woman who killed her own brother and got exiled instead of hanged because even the old king flinched at drowning a girl." He leaned closer. "You know why the crown purges storm-bloods now? Because the king asked. And the king asks only what *it* tells him to ask. You've seen the gold eyes. Bring her in, help us end this quietly, and there may yet be a place for you back inside the walls. Your oaths restored. Your name scrubbed clean."

My name. As if it were a stain on a floor rather than the only thing I had left after I'd spent everything else.

Behind Vane, Sefa looked at me through the bars. She didn't say anything. She didn't mouth *help me* or make big wet eyes at my conscience. She just looked, the way she does, like she could hear the ledger being tallied inside me — every debt, every entry, both columns — and was waiting, patiently, to see which column won.

Here is the entry nobody else could read:

*Nineteen years ago, a trial. A girl of seventeen standing in the dock with storm-scars down her arms, accused of calling the gale that killed Tam Fairweather. Twelve witnesses. One of them, a young knight named Brannic of the Vale, who testified that he'd seen her hands raised on the cliff road, that he'd seen lightning answer them.*

*I had seen her hands raised. I never said what else I saw — that she was reaching for her brother, that grief moves like weather, that a raised hand is not always a summons. I left that part out. The tribunal needed a clean account, and I gave them one, and the girl went into exile in the valley where magic dies, and I told myself for ten years that exile was mercy.*

That is the entry. That is the weight in the column marked *owed.*

Vane was still talking — something about discretion, about how the Chapel preferred quiet endings — when I hit him.

I will not pretend it was elegant. I am fifty-one years old and my left knee sounds like gravel in a sack. But I have been hitting men since before Vane could grow the beard he thinks frightens people, and the first blow put his nose sideways, and the second put him into the ivy, and then it was simply arithmetic: six knights, one disgraced knight, one barn full of shadows. I took a sword-slash across the forearm that I'll be honest about later. The fourth knight I dropped with a hay hook, which felt thematically appropriate. The last two ran when the horses spooked — Jonah's doing, I learned afterward; she'd come anyway, against every agreement, because of course she had, because the woman treats plans as suggestions made by lesser minds.

I broke the cage lock with the pommel of a dead man's sword.

Sefa climbed out, looked at Vane groaning in the nettles, and said, in a voice that was not entirely hers, flat and old as grave-dirt: "*The ledger balances only once.*"

Then she blinked, was eight years old again, and asked if I had any of Maren's honey-bread.

We walked out of Corrow Ford with the sun going down red behind us, and I thought, with the specific exhaustion of a man who has just made everything worse in order to make anything better: *well. That's another oath gone. At least the collection is consistent.*

* * *

Jonah was waiting at the treeline, and I saw the truth land on her before I reached her — saw it in the way she looked at me, at my bloody sleeve, at Sefa tucked under my arm. She'd heard. Of course she'd heard; the knights had been shouting my name like a curse, and besides, Jonah Fairweather has spent ten years learning to recognize betrayal the way farmers learn to recognize rain clouds.

"You were there," she said. "At the trial."

Not a question. I stopped walking. Some distances you don't cross while lying.

"I was."

"Witnesses for the crown. Twelve names read aloud." Her voice was very level, the way it goes level right before weather. "Yours was fifth."

"It was."

Sefa, bless her, wriggled free and went to stand near Maren's mule, pretending profound interest in the saddlebags. Even a child knows when to let adults bleed.

"They asked you what you saw," Jonah said. "On the cliff road."

"Yes."

"And you told them."

"I told them what served the verdict." I made myself hold her eyes. I owed her that much and less. "I saw your hands raised. I didn't say you were reaching for him. I didn't say grief looks like lightning. I gave them a clean account, and they wanted a clean account, and a girl went into the valley, and I slept fine for ten years, which I think is the worst part. Not the lying. That I could lie and then *sleep.*"

The wind moved through the trees above us, and for one mad moment I thought she'd call something down, and I found I wouldn't have blamed her.

"Why are you telling me now?" she asked.

"Because Vane offered me my name back today. Scrubbed clean. All my oaths restored." I laughed, and it came out ugly. "And I realized the name he was offering wasn't mine. It was the name of the young idiot who testified. Brannic of the Vale, crown's man, clean account. That man died somewhere between the chapel catacombs and teaching a child to skip stones. What's left doesn't want restoring. It wants—" I gestured vaguely at my own chest, at the whole ruinous inventory of it. "*Auditing.*"

"So you betrayed the inquisitor," Jonah said slowly, "to pay a debt to me."

"No." This came out faster than I intended, and truer. "I betrayed him because he put a child in a cage and called it accounting. The debt to you is separate. The debt is why I'm telling you the rest of it instead of letting you find out worse, later, at a worse time. Loyalty that hides the ledger isn't loyalty. It's just debt with good posture."

Maren appeared at the edge of the camp with her arms crossed, having heard approximately everything, because Maren hears everything short of thoughts and is working on the remainder. "For what it's worth," she said dryly, "he also hit the inquisitor twice before drawing steel. There's poetry in it."

"There's *concussion* in it," Jonah said. But some of the weather had gone out of her voice. She looked at me a long moment — the same long moment she'd given the tribunal, I realized, except that this time I was asking to be weighed honestly instead of conveniently.

"Sefa said something strange on the walk out," I told her, because she deserved the whole ledger, top to bottom. "In a voice that wasn't hers. She said—"

"*The ledger balances only once,*" Jonah finished quietly.

The fire crackled. Old Ivo looked up from his mending with the expression of a man who knows exactly one more thing than everyone else and finds it tiresome.

"That's a root-word," Ivo said. "Chapel liturgy, the deep rite. It means the binding holds until one true account settles it. One confession, sworn whole, no clean versions." He threaded his needle without looking up. "Which is why the god keeps everyone lying. Lies are mortar."

Jonah's hand had gone, unconsciously, to her own forearm — to the storm-scars she still wears like a sentence.

"One true account," she repeated.

And I understood, with a cold that had nothing to do with the evening, whose account it would have to be. Mine was settled. Hers — the cliff road, the raised hands, the brother — was the one still open, still bleeding entries, still keeping the mortar wet.

Across the fire, Sefa caught me looking at her. She smiled around a piece of honey-bread, gap-toothed and ordinary and eight.

But behind her eyes, briefly, something ancient turned a page.
