## 8. The Long Night of Prayers

I have been offered many things in my life. A goat named Duchess once offered me her opinion of my singing, at length. Maren has twice offered me poultices I did not want and one apology I did not expect. Old Ivo offered to teach me a funeral rite for a man still breathing, which is the sort of gift you only give family.

But no one had ever offered me my brother back.

Vessar did it the way a pickpocket does his work — while my attention was elsewhere, gentle as weather. We had made camp in the burned shell of a granary on the capital's edge, and Sefa was asleep with her head on Maren's knee, and Brannic was sharpening a blade that did not need sharpening because Brannic sharpens things when he cannot say what he means. And into that ordinary evening, into the space behind my eyes where the god's voice had learned to live, Vessar said:

*You could have him back.*

Not Tam's name. Not *your brother*. Just — *him*, the way you'd say *bread* or *warmth*. Like it was something owed to me.

"Get out," I said aloud, and Duchess would have been proud of how little my voice shook.

*You misunderstand. This is not an intrusion. This is an invitation.*

"It's a leash with better manners."

The god laughed, and I felt it the way you feel thunder through the soles of your feet before you hear it. *Tomorrow night, when the court kneels for the solstice rite, you will walk into my chapel and swear your oath, and I will let you believe it worked. You will tear nothing loose, Jonah Fairweather, because there will be nothing left of you to do the tearing. Or —* and here the voice went soft, went kind, went *human*, which was the cruelest thing it could have done — *or you can walk away. Let Aldric keep wearing me. Let me keep answering prayers. And I will reach back ten years and unmake the mistake that broke you.*

"You can't," I said. "Nobody can. He's dead. Dead is dead."

*I wear a king. Do you imagine death is difficult for me?*

And that was the moment, gods forgive me, that I stopped arguing. Not because I believed him. Because some traitorous cellar of my heart threw open its doors and said *what if*, and what if is a room you can't sweep clean again.

* * *

I couldn't sleep after that, so I did what I always do when I can't sleep: I made it everyone else's problem by leaving. Brannic watched me go from across the fire and said nothing, which is Brannic for *I'll follow at a distance and pretend I'm not following*. Ten years ago he helped condemn me at trial; now he shadows me through dark streets like a guilty dog, and the strange truth is that I've stopped minding. That's loyalty for you. It grows in the worst soil.

The capital on solstice eve was a city holding its breath. Garlands on every lintel, wax everywhere, the whole place smelling of tallow and pine and fear dressed up as festivity. And underneath all of it — under the carols and the market bells and the drunken singing — the prayers.

I heard them the way you hear a river when you live beside it long enough: constant, then suddenly deafening.

*A cure. Please, just one more winter.*
*My son marched north. Bring my son home.*
*I'll be good. I'll be good. I'll be good.*

They rose off the city like steam, like smoke, and every thread of them ran toward the chapel hill, and I understood with sick clarity what I was hearing. This was the harvest. Ten thousand desperate hearts, pouring out their need nightly, and the thing in the king drinking it down and dribbling back just enough miracle to keep them kneeling. A farmer doesn't kill his cows. He keeps them fed and frightened and grateful.

A woman knelt at a street shrine ahead of me, forehead pressed to cold stone. Younger than me. Hands cracked from laundry work.

"He answered my sister," she whispered. "The fever broke in a night. Everyone said pray to Vessar and the fever breaks. So why won't he answer me?"

Because your sister's cure cost someone else's child their luck, I thought. Because answered prayer is just theft with good bookkeeping. Because the god below the chapel isn't listening — he's *eating*.

I didn't say any of it. What would it buy her? Her sister lived. Somewhere else, some other mother wept into her hands, and she'd never know why. That's the arithmetic Vessar runs. It balances beautifully if you never count who paid.

*Do you see?* The voice slid in, warm as spilled wine. *Look at them. Look what they need. Your oath would take this away from them — the cures, the mercy, the answered nights. You'd leave ten thousand people alone with a silent sky, because of one dead man and your own guilt. Is that justice? Or is that just grief wearing armor?*

"That's not mercy," I muttered, stepping around the praying woman. "That's farming."

*Everything is farming,* said the god. *The question is only ever who eats.*

* * *

I ended up — and I swear this wasn't planned, though maybe everything tonight was planned by someone other than me — at the shrine where Tam used to leave offerings. Our mother's habit, inherited: a copper coin for safe roads. He was fourteen the last time he did it. He pressed the coin flat with his thumb like it mattered, like the weight of his thumb could tip the scales of heaven, and I laughed at him, and six months later I killed him with weather.

Here's the confession I've never said out loud, the one I've been carrying like a stone in my boot for ten years: it wasn't entirely accident. Yes, the storm came wrong. Yes, the power went through me like a flood through a dam. But there was a moment — half a heartbeat, maybe less — when I felt the lightning choose, and I didn't fight it. Tam had taken my place in our father's love without either of us asking for it. Half a heartbeat of *why him and not me*, and the storm heard it, and my brother died of my jealousy wearing the costume of mischance.

I told the tribunal it was accident. They exiled me anyway, for the calling itself. Everyone got the story wrong, and I let them, because the true version was worse.

So when Vessar says *I can unmake it*, what he is really saying is: *I can unmake the worst thing about you, the thing you've never confessed, the reason you flinch at your own name.* And I stood at that shrine in the freezing dark with copper-colored tears freezing on my face and understood that this is how you corrupt a person properly. Not with threats. With exactly what they've prayed for, wrapped in reasonable-sounding arithmetic. *Ten thousand saved against one dead boy.* The math even works. That's the horror of it. The math works.

"You're lying," I told the empty shrine. "Or worse, you're telling the truth, and either way I lose."

*Then don't decide tonight,* the god murmured, almost tender. *Decide tomorrow, on the chapel floor, when the whole court watches. You'll know my offer stands until your oath leaves your lips. Even gods keep their word, Jonah. It's the mortals who disappoint.*

Behind me, boots on frost. Brannic, arriving with the terrible tact of a man pretending he hasn't been standing thirty feet away for an hour.

"How much did you hear?"

"Enough." He came to stand beside me at the shrine, big and awkward as a barn. "He offered me the same thing once. When I first found the chapel. My commission back. My name cleaned."

"What did you say?"

"I said yes." He looked at the stone where Tam's coin had sat a decade ago. "That's how he kept me quiet for two years. Then I saw what 'yes' cost other people, and I took it back, and taking it back is the only brave thing I've ever done." He turned those gray eyes on me. "Whatever he offered you — and I don't need to know what — remember that he pays in a currency he prints himself. Everything Vessar gives is taken from somewhere. Usually from whoever's standing nearest."

"I know."

"Do you?" He said it gently. "Because you're crying, and the last time I watched someone cry at one of these shrines, she went home cured and never asked where the cure came from."

I wiped my face with the heel of my hand. "Go back to the others, Brannic."

"They're not my orders to take anymore." But he went, slowly, pausing at the corner of the lane. "Jonah. Tomorrow, whatever you choose — Sefa told Maren something before she fell asleep. She said the voice in her head is scared of you. Said it called you *the honest thunder*." His jaw worked. "I don't know what that means. But gods lie, and children with god-speech mostly don't."

He left me alone with the shrine and the frost and the enormous soft weight of the offer sitting on my chest like a sleeping cat I couldn't bring myself to move.

Tam. Fourteen years old, thumb flat on a copper coin, laughing at me laughing at him.

Ten thousand people on their knees, feeding a parasite their fear because the alternative was silence.

*Choose,* said the god, everywhere and nowhere, patient as winter. *You have until the oath.*

And far above us both, past the garlands and the bells and the smoke of a city preparing to worship its own disease, the solstice clouds were rolling in — black, heavy, and mine, if I still wanted them — carrying on their backs the first taste of a storm that felt less like weather than like an answer.

I looked up at it for a long time.

Then I went back to camp, and lay down between my ragged, impossible family, and did not sleep at all.
