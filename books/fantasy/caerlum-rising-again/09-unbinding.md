## 9. Unbinding

They dressed me in white for the occasion. Court fashion, apparently, for a woman about to commit treason in front of two hundred nobles: unbleached linen, no embroidery, the sort of garment you'd bury someone in. Maren had smuggled it into my cell that morning along with a vial of willowbark and a look I didn't like.

"You could still run," she said. "Brannic has the postern key. Sefa drew us a way out that doesn't involve the catacombs, which, given how the last catacomb excursion went, feels wise."

"And go where? The valley's gone, Maren. Caerlum let magic back in the moment Vessar started answering prayers on this side of the mountains. There's nowhere left that forgets me." I pulled the linen over my head and tried to laugh. It came out sounding like something with a broken wing. "Besides. I've spent ten years running from one storm. It seems rude not to introduce it to another."

She didn't laugh either. She took my hands instead — an apothecary's hands, burned once for heresy and steady as bedrock now — and pressed them flat between her own.

"When you swear," she said, "don't perform it. Don't shape it pretty. Ivo says the binding hears the difference between truth and theater."

"I know."

"Do you? Because you've been rehearsing your confession all week like a courtier rehearsing a toast, and if you walk in there with a *speech*, Jonah—"

"It isn't a speech," I said. And then, because she deserved better than a lie: "It was a speech. For six days it was a speech. Last night it stopped being one."

Her grip tightened. "Good," she said, which is a terrible thing for a friend to say when what you mean is *I'm going to stand in front of a kingdom and admit I killed my brother on purpose*, but Maren had always understood that medicine tastes worst right before it works.

They came for me at dusk.

* * *

The royal chapel of Aldric's line had never been beautiful. Brannic told me once that the first king built it deliberately ugly, out of gratitude — a thank-you to gods who answer honestly, which rarely means prettily. Somewhere in the last year, someone had gilded it. Gold leaf crawled up the pillars like lichen. The windows had been reglazed so that every saint depicted there wore the same face: mild, benevolent, faintly smiling, and identical down to the crease at each eye. Vessar's face. The god below had redecorated.

The whole court was assembled. I'd expected soldiers; I hadn't quite braced for the *audience*. Rows of lords and ladies in their solstice finery, the High Cleric enthroned to one side like a judge, and above them all, on a dais beneath a canopy stitched with prayers — actual prayers, thousands of little paper slips sewn into the fabric until it hung heavy as chain mail — King Aldric.

Or the thing wearing him.

Up close, the wrongness was worse than Brannic had described. The king sat straight and still, too still, the stillness of something conserving motion for later. His face was composed into kindness. His eyes were gold — not flecked, not glinting, *gold*, the whole iris, like coins sunk in a well — and they found me the moment the doors opened, the way a hook finds a fish that has finally, finally taken the bait.

"Jonah Fairweather," he said, and his voice was warm and resonant and utterly wrong, the voice of a man who had learned speech by listening through a wall. "The storm-caller returns. We have prayed so long for this."

"I'll bet," I said.

A ripple of scandal through the court. Somewhere in the ranks of guards, I heard a very small snort that I chose to believe was Sefa, disguised in a helmet three sizes too big, positioned exactly where Old Ivo said the binding's roots ran thinnest. My entire ragged family was scattered through this hall wearing stolen livery and expressions of religious solemnity, and every single one of them was here against my explicit instruction, and I have never loved anyone more in my life.

"The rite proceeds," the gold-eyed king said. "Kneel, storm-caller. Swear your oath before the assembled faithful, and be forgiven."

I did not kneel. I walked to the center of the chapel floor, where ten years ago a tribunal had stood me in irons while they pronounced the word *exile* like a sentence of mercy, and I turned to face the court, and I began to shake. Not from fear. From the pressure behind my ribs, the thing Maren had found sleeping in my blood, waking up all at once like a tenant who has heard the landlord is dead.

"Ten years ago," I said, "my brother Tam went onto the ridge above our village during the worst storm in a generation, because I asked him to."

Silence. The good kind, the listening kind. Even the gold eyes narrowed with something that might have been attention.

"They told you at my trial that I called that storm by accident. That a child's power slipped its leash. That Tam died because I was careless." My voice held. I kept waiting for it not to, and it held anyway, which felt like a small miracle being spent on me personally. "That was a lie. I want you to understand what kind of lie, because everything that follows depends on it.

"I called the storm *on purpose*. I was fourteen and furious, and our father had sold our mother's prayer-charms to buy drink, and I wanted the sky to punish someone, anyone, everyone. I raised my hands and I *asked*. Tam followed me up the ridge to talk me down. He was always the one who talked me down. And when the lightning came — when it came down toward *me*, toward the girl who had invited it — he stepped between."

The chapel was absolutely still. Two hundred nobles had come expecting a pageant, an execution dressed as forgiveness, and were getting instead the one currency Vessar could not mint: the plain, ugly, unbought truth.

"My brother did not die of my accident," I said. "He died of my intention. And I let a tribunal call it an accident because the truth would have meant the headsman, and I was a coward, and cowardice bought me ten years of goats and silence in a valley that forgets." I turned then, slowly, to face the dais. To face the gold. "So here is my oath, sworn in front of gods and court and whatever is squatting inside that man's soul:

*Tam's death was mine. Not the storm's. Mine. I called it, I meant it, and I have carried it badly, and I carry it now, and I set it down here where everyone can see its weight.*

*I am guilty. Let the thunder hear it.*

*Let the thunder answer.*"

The canopy of prayers shuddered overhead. Ten thousand slips of paper rustled at once, though there was no wind — yet — and from somewhere far beneath the flagstones came a sound like a great beast shifting its weight, and the gold eyes went wide, and King Aldric's borrowed mouth opened and said, in a voice suddenly layered with itself, suddenly ancient and hungry and afraid:

"*You cannot. Truth binds the binder, little storm. Swear the easy oath. Kneel. Take back what you—*"

"No," I said.

And I raised my hands the way I had on the ridge ten years ago, the same gesture, the same fury — except this time there was no fury left in me, only grief, only love, only a brother's laugh I could finally hear without flinching — and I called the storm.

* * *

You should understand what it means, a storm-caller swearing off storms. It isn't a vow like wine or war. It is a vow against your own heartbeat. For ten years I had lived with the sky pressing against the inside of my skull like weather against glass, and I had said *no* to it ten thousand times a day, and every *no* had been a small death, and I had believed the deaths were penance.

They weren't. They were the cage. And the oath — the true oath, the costly one, the confession that cost me everything I had left to hide — was the key.

The lightning did not come down through the roof. It came *up*. Out of the roots of the world, out of the catacombs where Old Ivo had shown us the bound god writhing beneath the altar, out of every honest prayer ever whispered in this ugly old chapel by kings who meant their thanks. It rose through the flagstones in white branches, through the gold-leafed pillars, up into the canopy of harvested prayers, which caught fire — all ten thousand slips igniting at once, not burning but *releasing*, every desperate plea Vessar had swallowed pouring back out into the air like birds from a net.

And Vessar came apart from Aldric.

I will not describe it kindly, because it was not kind. The god tore free of the king the way ivy tears free of a wall — taking stone, taking mortar, taking years. Aldric screamed with his own voice for the first time in a year, a raw human sound, and the golden light poured out of his eyes and mouth and gathered above the dais into a shape that was almost a man, almost a king, almost a god, vast and dwindling and shrieking in a language older than the chapel, and the storm met it there.

Honest thunder does not negotiate. That is the whole of what I learned on the ridge at fourteen, standing over my brother's body with rain in my hair, and it is the whole of what I learned now. Vessar had fed on purchased worship, on prayers it answered to itself, on faith curated like a garden. It had nothing left when the sky demanded payment in the only coin that matters. The truth I had sworn rang through the binding like a bell through glass, and the god — the parasite, the prisoner, the poor twisted remnant of something that had once been worshipped justly and justly answered — came undone in the lightning, unraveling into rain.

Just rain. When it ended, when the light guttered and the wind died and the last burning prayer-slips drifted down like black snow, it was simply raining in the chapel, warm and ordinary, falling through the shattered roof onto two hundred stunned faces and one kneeling king.

Aldric looked up at me. His eyes were brown. Tired, lined, ordinary brown, and wet, though that might have been the rain.

"What," he croaked, "have I done?"

"That," I said, "is going to take a very long conversation."

Then my knees gave out, because unbinding a god turns out to cost approximately everything, and the last things I saw were Brannic shoving through the guards with his sword still sheathed and his face wrecked, and Maren dropping beside me with her healer's hands already moving, and Sefa pulling off her stolen helmet, crying and laughing, saying something I couldn't quite catch.

Something about the valley.

Something about Caerlum rising.
