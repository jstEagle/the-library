## 10. Caerlum Rising Again

The first thing you should know about hearing god-speech is that it never stops being rude. Gods interrupt. They talk over your breakfast, over your prayers, over the one decent nap you've managed in a week. Before Vessar died — before Jonah *unmade* him, which is a sentence I still can't say without shivering — every voice in my head was him, or an echo of him, or something wearing his borrowed light like a stolen coat. Now there are others. Small ones. Old ones. A river-god who hums. A hearth-mother who nags like Maren but with better reasons.

And lately, on clear nights when the wind comes down off the mountains smelling of rain that hasn't fallen yet, I hear something new. Something that sounds like thunder clearing its throat.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Sefa's rule of storytelling: start where your feet are.

My feet were in Caerlum, which is funny if you think about it, because this was the valley that forgot. The valley where magic came to die and found the soil too poor even for that. Ten years ago it swallowed Jonah Fairweather whole and kept her safe by keeping her small — goats, silence, a cottage with a roof that leaked in exactly one spot she'd memorized. Now the king's own writ hung nailed to the chapel door in Harrowgate declaring her exile voided, her name restored, her storm-blood not merely pardoned but *needed*, and Jonah had read it twice, folded it into a square small enough to lose, and used it to prop up the wobbly leg of the milking stool.

"That's treason," Brannic said, delighted.

"That's carpentry," Jonah said.

I loved her for that. Not because it was clever — though it was — but because I understood what the stool-leg meant. I'd spent a year listening to a god wear a king like armor, so I know what borrowed voices sound like, and Jonah's voice, saying "that's carpentry" over a piece of royal parchment, was the most honest sound I'd heard since the night the sky tore open over the capital.

* * *

You want to know what happened after the storm? Everyone does. They stop me in the market now, people who wouldn't have spat on me six months ago, and they ask about the lightning, the gold-eyed king collapsing like a puppet with cut strings, the moment Vessar came apart in the air above the chapel — unmade by honest thunder, Ivo calls it, and Ivo was there, so he'd know. And I tell them the parts they want: yes, the court screamed; yes, half the stained glass went to shards; yes, Aldric wept for an hour afterward, a grown king weeping in front of everyone, saying *thank you* over and over in a voice gone rusty from disuse, like a hinge nobody had oiled in a year.

What I don't tell them is the part that mattered more. Three days later, Aldric came — not with knights, just four of them as escort, and one of those was Brannic's old squire who'd cried when they reunited, which Brannic denies and everyone else confirms — and he knelt. In the mud of our dooryard. King of the Nine Valleys, kneeling in goat muck, asking Jonah's forgiveness. Not for what Vessar did through him. For what he'd let himself become hollow enough to be worn.

Jonah looked at him a long time. Then she said, "Get up. You'll catch your death, and then I'll have two kingdoms' worth of trouble." And she pulled him upright by the arm like he was Tam — no, I don't know who Tam was to her, not really, none of us do, she keeps him folded up inside her the way she folded the writ — and she fed him stew, and he ate it sitting on our bench like any tired man, and when he left he didn't offer her a title.

She didn't ask for one. That told me everything.

* * *

The gods answering honestly again changed everything, but slowly, the way spring changes things. Prayers still go up. But now what comes back down is true: sometimes yes, sometimes no, sometimes *not yet, child, and here's why*. The old shrines that had been eating offerings into Vessar's root now give change back — a coin's worth of luck, a fever broken, a dream with actual instructions in it instead of dread shaped like instructions. Ivo has taken to leaving written complaints at the river shrine, which the river-god answers in humming, which I translate, badly, and which mostly amounts to *stop leaving complaints at my shrine*.

Maren says the apothecary's guild sent a letter rescinding her heresy charge. She burned it. Then she felt bad about the waste of paper and burned a heretical pamphlet instead, for symmetry.

Me — I'm learning. That's the word I keep landing on. Learning, like it's letters or knots or a song. The gift didn't leave when Vessar died; it turns out he'd only been shouting over everyone else. Now I hear the small true voices, and I'm learning to carry them without letting them carry me. Some days I get it wrong. Last week the hearth-mother spoke through me during supper and I complimented Brannic's beard in a voice like warm bread, and he hasn't let me forget it, and honestly neither will the hearth-mother, who found the whole business hilarious.

* * *

But here is what I actually sat down to tell you, and I'll get to it properly now, because it deserves proper telling.

The boy's name is Wren. He's nine, gap-toothed, all elbows, from a fishing family down-valley whose nets started coming up full of static. His mother brought him to our door in the third week of autumn because the priests were kind now but confused, and confused priests historically end up dangerous, and someone in her village remembered that the woman who killed the parasite god lived up past the goat track with a burned heretic and a disgraced knight and an old man who argued with rivers.

Jonah took one look at Wren — at the way the hairs on his arms stood up when clouds gathered, at the way he flinched from his own shadow's crackle — and I watched something cross her face like weather. Grief first. Then fear. Then, arriving last and staying longest, something I can only call *recognition*, the look of a person meeting their own ghost walking around in a smaller body.

"He's a storm-caller," she said quietly.

"I figured," his mother said. "That's why I'm here."

"It's not a gift I'd wish on anyone."

"No," his mother said. "But it's his, isn't it? Best somebody teach him how to hold it before it teaches him."

Jonah looked at her hands. I've learned to watch her hands; they say more than her face ever will. They'd been steady for weeks — steadier than I'd ever seen them, since the oath, since the truth she swore in front of the whole court, the confession that cost her everything she'd built her exile out of. But standing there with Wren looking up at her like she was made of lightning, her hands shook.

Then they stopped. And she crouched down to his height and said, "First lesson: the storm isn't angry at you. It's just loud. There's a difference, and learning it will save your life."

* * *

She teaches him in the high pasture, away from the goats, who have opinions about atmospheric electricity. I go along partly to help and partly because the god-speech is quieter up there, and partly — fine, I'll admit it — because watching Jonah teach is like watching someone come back from the dead in slow motion and decide they like the weather.

She doesn't teach him power first. She teaches him grief first, or rather she teaches him *about* grief, carefully, the way you'd show a child a knife without letting him hold it. "It will feed on the worst day of your life," she tells him. "It will try to convince you that the worst day is all you are. It lies. Remember that it lies." And Wren nods with the terrible solemnity of nine-year-olds, and asks questions like "what was YOUR worst day," and Jonah goes quiet, and the wind goes quiet, and then she says, "One day I'll tell you. Not today. Today we're going to learn to feel rain coming before the clouds do."

Some lessons I sit in on. In the ones about holding the power, I help — my gift and his apparently braid, a little; when he reaches for the sky I can hear the sky reaching back, and I tell him what it's saying. Mostly it says things like *faster* and *more*, because storms have no manners either. Jonah says I'm the first storm-caller's listener in three hundred years, and that together we might be something new, and she said it in a tone of voice that made me both proud and deeply nervous, the way prophecy usually does.

Brannic builds Wren a practice yard out of old chapel stones, the shattered glass melted smooth, and pretends he's just doing it to have something to do with his hands. Maren makes the boy tonics for the headaches, and grumbles that magic is a medical condition and someone should study it properly. Ivo takes Wren to the shrines and teaches him the old courtesies — how to thank a thunderhead, how to apologize to a field you've scorched. The boy learns all of it hungry, the way we all learned each other: out of necessity, and then out of love, in whatever order necessity allows.

* * *

Here's the thing I couldn't have known a year ago, back when the voices in my head belonged to a god who wore kings: belonging isn't something that happens to you. I used to think family was a thing you were born into or rescued by. But ours got built. Brick by brick, out of disgraced knights and burned healers and stubborn old men and a woman who propped the kingdom's pardon under a stool leg because the stool needed it more.

Last month a herald came up the goat track with an official invitation. The capital wants Jonah. There's talk of a new order, storm-caller advisors, a seat at the king's right hand, honors and lands and a name spoken in every church in the Nine Valleys. Aldric signs it himself, in his own hand, his own voice — I could hear the honesty of it from across the room, and it was a good voice, a tired and decent one.

Jonah read it standing at our table. We all pretended not to watch. Then she looked around — at Brannic whittling, at Maren sorting herbs, at Ivo arguing with the hearth-mother through me, at Wren asleep with his head on a sack of grain and his hair still faintly crackling from the afternoon's lesson — and she did the thing she does with papers she means to keep forever.

She folded it into a small square. And I swear on every small true god I know, she reached for the milking stool.

But she stopped. Her hand hovered there, and then she set the writ down on the shelf instead, above the mantel, where the light would find it.

"Not today," she said, to no one, to all of us, maybe to herself.

Wren stirred in his sleep. Outside, far off over the western hills, thunder cleared its throat — friendly, patient, waiting its turn. Jonah smiled at the window like an old friend had knocked.

"Tomorrow," she promised it. "We've got lessons."

* * *

So that's where we are. That's where I am: in a valley that forgot magic and is remembering it slowly, one lesson, one prayer, one honest answer at a time. I'm Sefa. I carry god-speech. I live with outcasts who chose each other, and I chose them back, and tomorrow morning I'll climb to the high pasture with a storm-caller who was supposed to be the last one, to watch her teach the boy who proves she wasn't.

Except.

There's one thing I haven't told them yet. One voice I've been hearing for three nights now, down beneath the river-hum and the hearth-nagging, deep and slow and old, older than Vessar, older than the chapel stones. It doesn't shout. It waits. And last night, finally, it said my name — my real one, the one nobody uses — and then it said:

*Tell the storm-caller: her brother's grave is not empty.*

I haven't told her. Not yet. My feet are where they are, in this kitchen, in this family, in this hard-won peace, and I know that once I speak it, the weather changes.

But I'm learning, see. And the first lesson Jonah ever taught was: the truth isn't angry at you. It's just loud.

There's a difference.

I take a breath. "Jonah," I say. "There's something you need to hear."
