## 1. The Ledger of Small Crimes

The moon came up over Thornfell like a coin someone had polished to spite the dark, and Mirabel Yarrow hated it on principle.

Full moons were bad for her trade. They turned the passes silver and legible, made shadows honest, gave every watcher on every ridge an hour of free light in which to count heads and cargo. She had planned this crossing for the new moon, three weeks gone, but the buyer had been insistent and the pay had been obscene, and obscene pay always meant one of two things: either the cargo was worth dying for, or the client was. She suspected both.

"Yarrow." Tam Vess's voice came low from beside her, barely louder than the wind combing the scree. "You're certain about this cut?"

She didn't look at him. Looking at Tam Vess was a mistake she'd stopped making months ago; he had a face built for being trusted, all easy angles and crooked half-smiles, and she'd watched it work on customs men, toll-keepers, and one unfortunate sergeant of the river watch who now owed his career to having believed it. Mirabel did not trust faces. Faces were the first thing she'd taught herself to stop believing in, back when she'd still had one worth believing in.

"The map says there's no road here," she said. "That's how I know it's safe."

"That's either very wise or very stupid."

"It's both. That's what wisdom costs up here."

Behind them, the caravan creaked and muttered—six wagons, fourteen mules, nine men who smelled of tallow and fear, and one boy who kept asking questions until Tam paid him in silence with a look. The wagons were supposed to be carrying brandy. Mirabel had counted the crates when they loaded at Duncarrow, out of habit rather than suspicion, and had noted immediately that brandy does not usually weigh so little or sit so square. Brandy sloshes. Whatever these crates held sat like a secret: compact, deliberate, packed by someone who cared more that it arrived than that it looked innocent.

She had said nothing. In three years of walking roads no map admitted existed, she had learned that the most dangerous question is not *what are we smuggling* but *why do you want me not to know*, and she asked neither. Curiosity was a debt, and debts were the only currency the world would still accept from her. She paid none. She collected none. She was, on paper and off it, no one at all.

*No surname spoken,* she reminded herself, as if reciting a catechism. *No debts owed. No name in any ledger.* Three years since she had signed anything. Three years since anyone had been able to prove Mirabel Yarrow existed, which meant three years since anyone could make her exist for their purposes—for a conscription roll, a tax assessment, a marriage contract, a gallows warrant. Invisibility wasn't freedom exactly. It was the closest thing to freedom that a woman with her history could afford, and she guarded it the way misers guard coin: obsessively, joylessly, counting it in the dark.

The path narrowed where the mountain shouldered in from the east. She raised one hand—not her left—and the caravan stilled behind her, mules blowing softly, wheels ceasing their complaint. Below the trail the valley dropped away into blue-black nothing, and above it the full moon laid the scree bare as a ledger page, every stone accounted for, nothing hidden.

Which was why she had chosen this route anyway. Not despite the moonlight. Because of it.

Anyone watching the obvious passes tonight—the Salt Road, the Wether Gate, the long switchbacks under Harrow Beacon—would be watching for smugglers, because everyone knew smugglers avoided moonlight, and therefore everyone knew smugglers moved on the dark nights, and therefore the dark nights were exactly where the Chancellery's riders would be. The Chancellery of Records did not chase people. It anticipated them. It read the patterns of the lawless the way priests read scripture, and it found them by finding what they thought was clever. Mirabel's entire livelihood rested on being cleverer in the wrong direction: predictable in her unpredictability, hiding precisely where the hunt expected her not to bother looking.

A road that appeared on no map, walked under a full moon, in plain silver sight. It was arrogant. Arrogance was safe, if you could afford it, and she had spent three years buying this particular arrogance one pass at a time.

"Move," she said, and the caravan breathed again and went on.

* * *

The Thornfell passes were older than any kingdom that claimed them, which was why they appeared on no map. Maps belonged to kingdoms, and kingdoms wrote down only what they controlled, and no one had ever controlled Thornfell—not the old empire whose boundary stones still leaned drunkenly along the high ridges, not the marcher lords who'd fought eleven wars over valleys they never once held through winter, and certainly not whatever authority currently pretended to govern the lowlands. The mountains remembered everyone who had tried. They kept the memory the way they kept everything: in paths worn into rock by feet that official history insisted had never walked there.

Mirabel knew thirty-one such paths. She had learned them the way she learned everything—by paying attention when no one was watching her do it. As a girl in her master Odara's cottage she had memorized the parish rolls for practice, then the toll ledgers, then the grain accounts, and Odara had caught her at it one evening and laughed and said, *You collect other people's books like other girls collect flowers,* and Mirabel had felt, for one unguarded moment, seen. That was before she understood what being seen cost.

Now she collected roads instead. Roads were safer than books. A road couldn't be confiscated, couldn't burn, couldn't be used to hang you—though the Chancellery, she'd heard, was working on the last one, mapping the wild places with instruments that hummed, erasing the unofficial routes one survey at a time. There had been a path over Cold Saddle two years ago. There wasn't anymore. Not destroyed—simply *unrecorded*, and once unrecorded, the Chancellery's scholars claimed, a thing began to fade, as though existence itself required a witness with ink.

She thought that was superstition, mostly. Mostly.

The trail crested below a leaning boundary stone, older than the empire that had carved it, its inscription weathered to a suggestion of letters. Mirabel paused beside it, as she always did, and let her fingers rest on the stone's flank without reading it, because there was almost nothing left to read, and because some part of her—a part she didn't examine—always hoped that this time her skin might feel something under the lichen. A warmth. A hum. Anything.

Nothing. Stone was stone. She moved on, and told herself she hadn't been hoping.

* * *

They made the descent by midnight and camped in the hollow called the Shepherd's Tooth, a fold of land walled on three sides by rock so that even the moonlight had to queue to get in. Tam set watches without asking her opinion, which she appreciated; he ran his crews the way she ran herself, quietly, efficiently, without the theater of command. It was one of the reasons she still worked with him. Not trust—she wanted to be clear with herself about that, even in the privacy of her own skull, especially in the privacy of her own skull—but professional compatibility. Trust was a debt. Compatibility was just arithmetic.

"You ate?" Tam asked, appearing at her shoulder with bread and hard cheese, because he also noticed things, which was another reason she kept him at arm's length. People who noticed things eventually noticed *her*, and she could not afford the weight of being known.

"I ate," she lied, and took the bread anyway, because refusing would have been a conversation.

He sat beside her against the warm stone, close enough to talk, far enough to pretend he wasn't. For a while they watched the firelight move on the wagons and the men drowsing around it, and the boy finally slept mid-question, mouth open, young enough that sleep ambushed him like weather.

"The buyer sends word," Tam said. "Corvane. He'll meet us at Millbrook ford tomorrow, not Greyharbour. He moved the rendezvous up."

"Moved it how much?"

"A day. Maybe two."

Mirabel chewed slowly. Buyers did not move rendezvouses up. Buyers who were expecting brandy had no reason to hurry; buyers who were expecting something else had every reason, and every reason made her spine itch. "Did he say why?"

"He said the season's turning."

"The season turned a month ago."

"He also sent this." Tam passed her a folded slip of paper, and she read it by cupped firelight, careful that the flames saw nothing. It was a receipt, of sorts—a confirmation of contract, written in a clerk's hand so fine it looked printed: *One item, sealed, delivered whole, condition verified upon exchange. Balance payable in certified credit.*

Not *goods*. Not *crates*. *Item. Sealed.*

"Vess," she said. "What did we carry over Thornfell tonight?"

"What we were told we carried."

"Brandy doesn't have a seal condition."

"No," Tam agreed pleasantly. "It doesn't." And he smiled his crooked smile, the one built for trusting, and she understood that he had read the paper too, had probably read it first, and had decided—as she was deciding now—to let the knowing sit between them like a third man at the fire, acknowledged and unspoken.

She should have left it there. That was the rule, her rule, the whole architecture of her life: see, note, forget. But the crates were twenty feet away, stacked under a wagon's tilt, and the fire was low, and the watch was Tam's own cousin, and the moon stood overhead like an open eye that had already seen everything there was to see of her.

Habit did the rest. Habit, and something older than habit that she refused to name—the same part of her that touched boundary stones hoping they'd hum.

* * *

She told herself she was only checking the lashings. That was even true, in the way that all good lies are anchored to something true: she did check lashings, every night, because a loose crate on a mountain switchback killed mules and mules cost money and money was the only god who answered her reliably. She moved along the wagon line with a lantern shuttered to a knife-slit of light, testing knots, and when she reached the second wagon—the one with the square, silent crates—she checked the knots, found them sound, and then, with the ease of a woman picking a lock she'd picked a hundred times, lifted the lid of the topmost crate.

Inside, nested in wool packing, lay a book.

Not a ledger, not a folio of invoices. A book bound in leather gone the color of dried blood, closed with clasps of tarnished metal, and sealed—not tied, *sealed*—with wax so old it had gone the amber of honey held too long near a flame. The seal bore a device she did not recognize: a circle broken by a vertical line, like a door seen edge-on. Someone had pressed it with care, long ago, and the wax had kept its shape the way things keep shape when no one has disturbed them for a very long time.

The book was heavy in a way that had nothing to do with weight. She felt it the moment her hands closed on it, a pressure behind the breastbone, faint and wrong, like standing too near a struck bell. The lantern-flame bent toward it. Or she imagined it did. She was tired. Tired women imagined things on mountains.

She should have put the lid down. She was, after all, an expert. Three years of expertise: she did not touch contracts, did not sign receipts, did not learn buyers' names, did not ask what was in the crates. She had built her invisibility plank by plank, and every plank was a refusal, and here was the largest refusal of all lying open under her hands, humming like a hive.

But the book was a book, and Mirabel Yarrow had learned her letters from a hedge-witch who traded healing for stories, and there had been a time—three years and a lifetime ago—when a book had been the only place she existed without penalty. On the page she had been allowed to be anyone. Off it, she had become no one, and no one was safer, but safety, she was remembering now, in the cold, with the bell-hum in her chest, was not the same as wanting to be alive.

Out of habit, she said to herself, later. Out of habit, out of greed, out of the oldest hunger she owned.

She pocketed the book.

It fit inside her coat against her ribs, warm as a stolen loaf, and she closed the crate, retied the lashings, and walked back to the fire with her heart going like a drum in a procession. Tam glanced up. She met his eyes and gave him nothing, and he gave her back the same nothing, and the nothing between them was so complete and so practiced that it was almost a kind of intimacy.

She did not sleep. She lay with her back to the stone and the book against her side and listened to the caravan breathe, and somewhere past the black heart of night she became aware of two things at once, arriving together like strangers holding hands.

The first was that the humming had stopped—or rather, had settled, the way a dog settles when it comes home, and that she could feel the book through her coat the way you feel a sleeping animal, alive and patient and *waiting*.

The second was that the full moon, sliding free of the peaks to the east, was throwing her shadow long across the hollow—and that the shadow fell across the leaning boundary stone above the camp, and that for the length of a single held breath, as the light touched the weathered carving, Mirabel would have sworn on anything she had left, which was nothing, which was exactly the point—

—that the stone grew warm.

That the worn letters filled, just slightly, just enough, with shadow.

That she could almost, almost read them.

Then the moon climbed clear of the ridge, the angle shifted, the stone was only stone, and Mirabel Yarrow lay alone in the dark with a stolen book against her ribs and the sudden, vertiginous certainty that she had just picked up a thread that someone, somewhere, had left dangling for a very long time—waiting, perhaps, for a hand with exactly her shape to find it.

Tomorrow they would reach the buyer. Tomorrow the book would have to go back in the crate, and the crate would change hands, and the small crime of her curiosity would be erased as cleanly as if it had never happened.

Unless.

She watched the fire die and did not decide anything, which was itself a decision, and the oldest one she knew: to keep the thing, and see.
