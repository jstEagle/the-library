## 2. A Date Written Before Her Birth

The inn at Hollowmere had exactly one lamp worth the oil it burned, and Mirabel Yarrow—no, Mirabel, just Mirabel, the way she had been for three years now—took the corner table beneath it without asking, because asking was a kind of signature and signatures were debts. She set her pack on the bench beside her where no one could sit against it, ordered stew she didn't taste, and waited for the common room to empty the way a river empties at dusk: gradually, then all at once.

The book lay in her pack like a held breath.

She had taken it out of habit. That was the honest word for it, though honesty was another thing she rationed. A smuggler who left sealed cargo sealed was a smuggler who stayed employed; a smuggler who checked was one who lived long enough to retire or died young enough to be interesting. Mirabel intended neither. But when Tam Vess had handed her the crate in the dark below Thornfell's third pass—*don't open it, don't weigh it, don't even look at it*—she had felt the old pull of her apprenticeship, Odara's voice in her ear: *Everything that hides is hiding something specific, girl. Find out what, or you're not guarding it, you're just standing near it.*

So she had opened it. Not the crate—the book inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, bound in leather the color of dried blood, with no title and no lock but a wax seal cracked so old it flaked at her touch. She had read perhaps a page by moonlight while the caravan slept, understood nothing except that the pages were written in a hand that made her chest ache with recognition, and then—out of habit, out of instinct, out of something older than either—she had put it in her own pack instead of back in the crate.

Tam hadn't noticed. Or he had noticed and said nothing, which with Tam amounted to the same thing and was somehow worse.

Now the last drunk staggered up the stairs, the innkeep banked the fire, and Mirabel turned the lamp's key until its flame stood tall and steady, a small yellow room of light in a large dark world. She unwrapped the book and laid it flat.

The paper surprised her. After a century—it had to be a century; the seal's style had gone out before she was born—she'd expected foxing, brittleness, that smell of sweet rot old paper gets. Instead the pages were white as cut apple, supple, cool to the touch, as if they'd been written yesterday and waiting ever since. The ink was brown-black, iron-gall, applied by someone who wrote fast and corrected nothing. Marginalia crowded the edges in a second hand, smaller, more patient: notes, cross-references, small sketches of boundary stones and knotwork.

She turned past the opening leaves—a dedication she couldn't parse, an alphabet she half-recognized from Odara's grimoires—and found herself reading a list. Not a poem, not a prophecy in the thunderous style the traveling players sold to gullible towns. A list, dry as a ledger:

*Item: the millstone at Werle's Ford shall keep the left face of the seal, worn but legible, and a wight shall keep the millpond, hungry for names.*

*Item: the harvest song of the Ashcombe reapers carries the second fragment in its fourth verse, which the singers have forgotten means anything.*

*Item: the door stands where three boundaries meet, on the high shoulder of Thornfell, and opens under the thirteenth moon.*

Mirabel sat very still. She knew Werle's Ford. She had camped there eleven days ago. There *was* a wight in the millpond—or something that pulled at her ankles when she washed, something cold and curious—and there *was* carving on the fallen millstone, half-worn, that she'd looked at for exactly as long as it took to decide it wasn't worth the risk of studying.

Her mouth had gone dry. She turned the page.

And stopped.

At the head of the page, centered, ruled off from the text below like a heading in a merchant's daybook, was a date.

She knew dates. Knowing dates was survival—knowing when the Chancellery's auditors made their rounds through the valley towns, when tolls doubled, when the full moon would hang fat over the passes because smugglers moved by its light and so did the men paid to catch them. Tonight was the twenty-third night of the Waning Year. She had counted to it herself, alone, in her head, the way she counted everything, because a woman with no ledger of her own kept her ledgers where no auditor could burn them.

The page said: *Twenty-third night of the Waning Year.* And beneath it, in that same fast, unrepentant hand:

*A thief crosses the Thornfell tonight, red-haired, her left hand scarred from wrist to knuckle where the trap took two of her fingers' grace. She carries what was lost. Let her not be found by the record-keepers, for she is the finding.*

Mirabel read it twice. Three times. Her scarred hand lay on the table beside the book, and she became aware of it the way you become aware of your own heartbeat—suddenly, unpleasantly, unable to stop noticing. The scars ran wrist to knuckle. A snare, nine years ago, back when she'd still had a master and a name and a habit of reaching into other people's property. Two fingers had kept their full bend; the others had never quite forgiven her.

Red-haired. She caught her reflection in the dark window glass beside the lamp—thin face, freckles gone pale with winter, hair the color of rust and temper shoved under a hood. A thief crossing the Thornfell. Under a full moon. Tonight.

*She carries what was lost.*

"No," she said aloud, softly, because the room was empty and some things needed saying into air to prove they were real. "No, that's not—" She stopped. Arguing with a page was madness. Reading a page this closely was worse.

Because here was the thing she could not put down, the thing that lifted her slightly out of her body and let her look down at the woman at the table as if she were a stranger in a story: the handwriting matched.

Not approximately. Not in the way all trained hands resemble each other. It matched the notes in Odara's effects—the bundle of papers she'd burned one by one after the funeral, weeping, because Odara had left her nothing else and instructions to leave nothing behind. *Burn them all, Miri, every scrap, and don't read them twice.* She had obeyed, mostly. She had read some three times. The cramped marginalia in this book—the smaller, patient hand that annotated the fast one—that was Odara's hand. She'd know it anywhere. She had spent six years learning her letters by copying it.

But the main text, the prophecy itself, the fast uncorrected script that had described her hands and her hair and her road and her *night*—

That hand appeared in Odara's papers too. Once. A single sheet she'd burned last of all, reluctantly, because it was only a fragment: a few lines about *the queen's bargain* and *the price outside the page*, signed with a symbol like a door seen edge-on. Odara had called it an heirloom when Mirabel asked. Had said it came from *family*. Had watched her with an expression Mirabel had never learned to name and changed the subject.

Mirabel's tea went cold. The lamp hissed. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard settled with a crack like a knuckle.

She did the arithmetic because arithmetic was safe, arithmetic always held still: the seal was a century old. The ink described events of tonight. Therefore either the book was newer than it pretended, or the man who wrote it had known, one hundred years ago, that on the twenty-third night of the Waning Year a red-haired woman with a ruined left hand would cross the Thornfell carrying something lost—and had known it the way you know tomorrow's sunrise, flatly, without wonder, listing her like an item between a millstone and a song.

*Let her not be found by the record-keepers, for she is the finding.*

The Chancellery. Her spine prickled. Three years of careful nothing—no surname, no debts, no contracts in her own hand, wages taken in goods and gossip, every transaction arranged to leave no line in any ledger that any clerk could trace back to a person rather than a ghost named Mirabel. She had built that invisibility plank by plank, the way Odara taught her, and it had held. It had *held*. And now here was a book that knew her better than any ledger could, that had known her before her grandmother was born, that had pinned her to this date and this pass and this lamplight like a moth to a card.

Her whole life, she had thought of herself as unwritten. Free. A blank margin walking around in boots.

The page said otherwise. The page said she was a line in someone else's hand, and had been since before anyone alive remembered.

She should burn it. That was the sane move, the Odara move—Odara had burned everything, Odara had hidden this book somewhere so deep it had taken a Chancellery buyer and a smuggler's caravan to drag it back into the light, and Odara had told her *never let it be read aloud*—no. Wait. That wasn't Odara. That wasn't anything Odara had said. Where had that sentence come from?

Mirabel pressed both hands flat on the table and breathed until the gray at the edges of her vision receded. She was tired. She was road-tired and moon-tired and she had eaten bad stew, and tired minds borrowed sentences. That was all.

Except the book was warm. She noticed it now—had maybe been noticing for minutes without admitting it. Warm through the table, warm like a sleeping animal, warm in a way that leather and paper had no business being in a cold common room far from any fire. As if it were pleased. As if, having finally been opened by the right pair of ruined hands, it had exhaled.

*She carries what was lost.*

What was lost. The cargo was supposed to be brandy. Tam had said brandy with the particular flatness he used for lies he didn't expect her to believe, and she hadn't cared, because the pay was good and the route was hers and caring about cargo got smugglers hanged. But nobody paid Corvane-money—because that was whose seal had been on the delivery chit, she realized now, the Chancellery's own auditor, the man with the gray gloves who'd inspected the caravan at the pass and looked at her one beat too long—for brandy. You paid that money for things that had been recorded once and must never be read again.

Things like books. Things like this book. Things that the Chancellery bought not to destroy, necessarily, but to *hold*—to keep locked in some archive where the words could do no work.

And they had hired her caravan to move it. Which meant they trusted the route. Which meant they trusted *her*.

Which meant that somewhere in the Chancellery's endless files, there might already be a line about Mirabel. Not the ghost-Mirabel she'd constructed, but the real one—red hair, scarred hand, the road over Thornfell—and if the Chancellery had that line, then the book was right in the worst way, and if the Chancellery didn't have that line, then the only record of her in the entire world was this one, written a century ago by a dead man whose handwriting lived in her memory like a scar of its own.

Neither possibility was comfortable. Both pointed east, oddly enough, toward morning, toward the low country where Tam would be waking to count his crates and find one book short—if he'd counted at all. Tam missed less than he admitted and admitted less than he missed. She'd need to decide, before dawn, whether the book went back in the crate or stayed in her pack, and every instinct she owned said *stay*, and every instinct she owned had been wrong before, usually loudly.

She wrapped the book again, slowly, giving her hands work so her mind would stop circling. Under the oilcloth her thumb found the back cover, and stopped.

There was writing on the inside of the cover. Small. Patient. Odara's hand—but younger, she thought, the letters rounder, from before whatever years had bent them. A single line, pressed hard enough to emboss:

*Miri — if you are reading this, then I failed to burn the right things. Come home. Bring nothing you love.*

Mirabel stared at it for a long time. The lamp burned steady. Outside, over Thornfell's black shoulder, the full moon stood exactly where a dead scribe had promised it would stand, and it looked, for the first time in her life, less like a coin than like an eye—an eye that had been open for a hundred years, watching a road, waiting for a red-haired woman to walk into the frame.

Come home. Odara's cottage had been a ruin for two winters. There was nothing there. There was never anything there.

That was what frightened her. She knew, with the certainty of the scarred hand on the table, that she was going anyway.
