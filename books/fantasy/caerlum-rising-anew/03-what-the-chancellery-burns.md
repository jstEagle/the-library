## 3. What the Chancellery Burns

Tam Vess had learned to read danger the way other men read weather: in small signs, in the way a morning sat wrong on the shoulders of the world. The morning the auditor came to Millbrook, the sky was the color of dishwater and the birds had gone quiet along the hedgerows, and Tam knew before anyone said a word that something was about to be subtracted.

He was sitting on the tailboard of the second wagon, nursing a cup of tea he'd let go cold out of habit, watching Yarrow count crates at the far end of the yard. She counted like a woman saying prayers she didn't believe in—quick, precise, lips barely moving. Three years he'd run with her now, three years of passes and toll roads and the particular silence she kept around her name. He'd stopped asking about it somewhere in the second year. Some people were luggage they carried themselves.

The buyer was due at noon. Corvane, the broker in Lowmarsh had said, a Chancellery man, which was unusual—Chancellery auditors didn't usually trouble themselves with brandy shipments, even good ones, even ones that had crossed Thornfell under a full moon without paying so much as a whisper of duty. Tam had raised it with Yarrow. She'd shrugged in that way of hers, as if the whole arrangement were someone else's problem she'd agreed to supervise.

Now, an hour before noon, the birds went quiet, and Tam looked up the road toward the ford and saw the rider coming.

Not a courier. Not a tax man. Couriers rode like they were being chased; tax men rode like they were bored. This one rode like the road belonged to him personally and he was merely permitting everyone else to use it. Grey coat, grey horse, grey gloves, and a satchel slung across his back that looked less like leather than like something grown—a smooth dark thing, seamless, with no buckle or strap that Tam could make out from fifty paces.

"Yarrow," he said, not loudly.

She'd already seen. Of course she had; she always saw first. But she didn't stop counting. She finished the crate, chalked its number, closed the lid of her tally book, and only then turned to look down the road with an expression Tam had never seen on her before. It took him a moment to place it, because it wasn't fear exactly. It was recognition—the flat, cold recognition of a woman seeing a face she'd been rehearsing against for years without knowing it.

"That's early," Tam said.

"Yes," said Yarrow.

"You expected him?"

"I expected *a* buyer." She set the chalk down. "I didn't expect that."

"What is that?"

"A Chancellery auditor," she said, "arriving ninety minutes before his appointment, alone, in a village where nobody important lives." Her voice stayed level, but Tam had spent enough nights sharing campfires with her to hear the wire pulled taut underneath. "Auditors don't come early, Tam. They come when there's nothing left to wait for."

* * *

Corvane dismounted at the edge of the yard the way a man steps into a room he owns. He was younger than Tam had imagined—not young, but unlined, with the smoothness of a face that had never once argued with a mirror—and his eyes did something Tam found genuinely unsettling: they moved across the yard, the wagons, the hired drivers, the crates, and seemed to take an inventory, not of what was there, but of what ought to be there, comparing the two with faint disappointment.

"Master Vess." His voice was pleasant. Everything about him was pleasant, in the way a well-kept knife is pleasant. "And you must be the guide. Yarrow, was it? No surname?"

"Just Yarrow."

"Mm." He smiled. It reached all the way to his eyes, which somehow made it worse. "The Chancellery appreciates economy."

Tam watched Yarrow lie to him, and it was beautiful, and it frightened him more than anything else that day would do.

She gave him the whole performance—crates opened, seals shown, the manifest recited from memory with the dry competence of a woman who had done this a hundred times. Brandy, forty casks, Thornfell crossing, duties assessed and paid at the Lowmarsh station, receipts available on request. Every word true except the ones that mattered, because Tam knew perfectly well that eleven of those forty casks held salted eel and lamp oil and one sealed parcel Yarrow had taken off the wagon two nights ago and hadn't spoken of since. And she lied with no flicker, no swallowed breath, no tell he could find even though he knew where to dig. She lied the way water lies—smoothly, completely, taking whatever shape the container demanded.

Corvane listened to all of it with his head slightly tilted, like a man enjoying music.

"Remarkable," he said when she finished. "Thornfell under a full moon, no losses, no witnesses worth recording. You must know those roads very well."

"Well enough."

"And yet the maps show no road through the Thornfell passes at all. Not since—" He paused, delicately, the way a man pauses before a word he has decided not to say. "Well. Not since."

"Maps are written by people who stay home," Yarrow said.

"They are indeed." Corvane smiled again. "That is rather their purpose."

It was then that Tam noticed the villagers. Millbrook was a small place, forty souls and a parish church with a slate roof, and word of a grey-coated stranger had traveled fast, as it does. They gathered at a respectful distance near the well—two or three farmers, the smith, old Mother Hesk with her market basket—and they watched the auditor with that particular rural stillness that isn't curiosity so much as a hope of remaining unnoticed. Tam had grown up in villages. He knew the posture. It meant someone remembered soldiers, or plague carts, or collectors of some kind, and had decided invisibility was the better part of valor.

Corvane noticed them too. He looked at them the way he'd looked at the crates.

"Is there a parish record house here?" he asked Yarrow, pleasantly.

"There's the church. Father Aldous keeps the registers himself."

"Naturally." Corvane turned, and Tam saw the satchel shift on his back—that smooth dark shape—and saw, for the first time, that the satchel was watching too. There was no eye on it, nothing so crude. But it moved, subtly, the way a cat's ear moves toward a sound, orienting itself toward the church with a hunger that had nothing to do with leather.

"I'm afraid I'll need to inspect the registers," Corvane said. "Standard procedure. Debtor verification."

"It's brandy," Tam said, before he could stop himself.

Corvane regarded him with mild interest, the way one regards a dog that has spoken. "Everything is everything else, Master Vess. Brandy is grain is debt is names is paper. The Chancellery concerns itself with the whole chain." He adjusted one grey glove, finger by finger. "There is a man in this parish—Hobb, farmer, east field—who owes the Crown four pounds, eleven shillings, entered in your Father Aldous's ledger some nine years ago. The debt has been purchased. Under the new instruments, purchased debts are resolved through record reconciliation."

"He's paid half of it back," Mother Hesk called from the well, before anyone could stop her. "Hobb. Paid it slow, but paid it."

Corvane turned to her with genuine warmth. "Then his account will balance beautifully."

Tam didn't understand yet. He wants to note that clearly, because later—after, when he lay awake in a hundred different beds replaying this hour—he would try to reconstruct the exact moment comprehension arrived, and it was not when Corvane walked up the lane to the church. It was not when Father Aldous met them at the door, pale, wiping his hands on his cassock. It was when Corvane stepped inside, and the satchel came off his shoulder, and Tam, watching through the churchyard gate from thirty feet away, saw the priest's mouth form the words *but the register*, and saw Corvane nod gently, agreeably, like a man being asked if he takes sugar.

Then the burning started, and it was not fire.

Or rather—it was fire, eventually. There was smoke, there was flame, there was the crackle and stink of burning vellum rising from the church windows. But before the fire, there was the other thing. The satchel opened—not unclasped, simply *opened*, like a mouth—and what came out was a darkness that moved across the parish register the way ink moves across wet paper, drinking it. Page by page. Entry by entry. Tam could see, through the door Corvane had left open, the ledger lying open on the altar rail, and he could see the entries fading—not crossed out, not obscured, but *unwriting*, the ink lifting off the vellum like breath off glass, until the pages stood blank and innocent, as if no hand had ever touched them.

And the people—

The people changed. It happened slowly enough that Tam could watch it work, entry by entry. A name faded from the register, and across the churchyard a woman standing by the lych-gate frowned suddenly, looked at her own hand as though it had gone numb, and then looked around at her neighbors with polite puzzlement, because she could no longer quite remember why she had come here today, or who had asked her to stand witness at a wedding that had happened—when was it? Someone's wedding. Whose?

Hobb's own name went last. Tam watched the farmer himself trudge up the lane, summoned by a neighbor, and stop dead at the churchyard gate with the expression of a man arriving at the top of his own stairs and finding no memory of climbing them. He looked at Corvane. Corvane smiled at him, kindly. Hobb looked back at the village, at the fields he had farmed for twenty years, and Tam saw him reach for something—his own name, surely, his own self, the plain fact of being Amos Hobb of Millbrook—and find his fingers closing on air.

"Can I help you, sir?" Father Aldous asked him, gentle, bewildered. "Are you lost?"

"I—" Hobb said. And then, after a long moment, with the terrible placidity of a man whose debts have finally been forgiven along with everything else: "I don't think so."

They burned the blank register afterward. Corvane insisted on it personally, striking the match himself, feeding the empty vellum into the church's own stove while the priest watched with his hands folded, agreeing to everything, remembering nothing worth objecting to. Fire, Tam understood now, was just the tidying. The deletion had already happened. You couldn't burn what wasn't written.

No one screamed. No one ran. That was the worst of it—Tam had seen violence, real violence, the kind that leaves blood and widows and reasons to fight back. This left none of those things. This left a village of forty people who now numbered thirty-nine and didn't know it, standing in their own churchyard in the smell of smoke, feeling vaguely that the day was shorter than it should have been.

*Power exercised as deletion.* He'd heard the phrase once, years ago, from a smuggler too fond of his own voice, describing the Chancellery the way sailors describe storms—with awe dressed up as contempt. Tam had laughed at the time. Deletion, he'd said, was for clerks.

He wasn't laughing.

* * *

When Corvane came back down the lane, brushing ash from one grey glove, Yarrow was waiting for him at the wagon with the manifest still open in her hands, and she did the single bravest thing Tam ever saw her do, which was nothing at all. She stood still. She let him look at her.

"Your business is concluded?" she asked.

"Entirely." He took the manifest she offered, read it without appearing to read it, and signed. His pen made no sound on the paper, which Tam would think about for a long time afterward. "You've handled this efficiently. The Chancellery values efficiency above most virtues. Loyalty, for instance, can be manufactured. Efficiency cannot."

"We aim to please."

"Do you." Corvane folded the manifest, and paused, and Tam saw the pause cost the man something—an appetite suppressed, a line item deferred. "Guide. Yarrow. May I ask how long you've worked these roads?"

"Three years."

"Three years." He repeated it softly, tasting it. "Before that?"

"Before that I worked other roads."

"Mm." He handed back the manifest. For a moment—one heartbeat, no more—his pleasant mask shifted, and Tam saw beneath it something older and hungrier and very tired, the look of a man doing arithmetic that never ends. "You have remarkably little history, Yarrow. Most people leave traces. Footprints in ledgers, entries in registers, names on leases and marriage lines and tax rolls. You are—" he considered, choosing the word with visible pleasure— "*unrecorded*. It's rare. It's almost professional." He mounted his grey horse. "Almost. No one is entirely unwritten, you know. The Chancellery keeps a list of the nearly-blank. We call it the Quiet File. It's not a punishment. It's an acknowledgment that some people are interesting precisely because they are missing."

He gathered the reins.

"I'll be reading yours," he said, and rode away down the ford road with his satchel riding behind him like a well-fed animal, and the birds did not come back to the hedgerows for a full hour after he was gone.

* * *

Tam found Yarrow at the far wagon, repacking crates that needed no repacking, her hands steady, her face composed, doing the work of a woman to whom nothing unusual had occurred.

"Hobb," Tam said. "The farmer. He's gone. Everyone's forgotten him. His own wife was standing right there and she looked through him like weather."

"I know."

"And you stood there and lied to him. To *that*. Like he was a toll collector."

"He was going to burn us either way," Yarrow said quietly. "The question was whether we'd be worth a second visit." She smoothed straw over a crate lid. "Now he thinks we're efficient."

"He knows your name isn't a name. He said—" Tam lowered his voice, glancing at the hired drivers, mercifully out of earshot. "He said there's a file. The Quiet File. He said you're in it."

Something moved behind Yarrow's eyes—there and gone, quick as a fish under ice. Not surprise. Confirmation.

"I know what he said," she answered.

"How long have you known you're on a list?"

For a moment he thought she wouldn't answer. Then she looked east, past the smoking chimney of the parish church, toward the fens and the low hills beyond them, with an expression Tam had never learned to translate—grief and resolve and something like homesickness braided together, aimed at a country that appeared on no map he'd ever bought.

"Longer than you'd believe," she said. "And shorter than I need." She closed the wagon's tailgate with a click that sounded, in the quiet, like a lock turning. "Get the drivers fed, Tam. We're leaving tonight instead of tomorrow."

"Why tonight? What's east?"

Yarrow shouldered her pack, and inside it, wrapped in oilcloth and hidden among spare shirts, something heavy shifted—a book-shaped weight that Tam had noticed two days ago and been told, smoothly, completely, was nothing.

"A cottage," she said. "A grave. And some questions my dead teacher is going to answer for me, whether she's alive to do it or not."
