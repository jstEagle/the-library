## 4. The Hedge-Witch's Last Lesson

The cottage had been dying for three years, and it was nearly finished.

Mirabel saw it first from the ridge above the valley, the way you see a body from a distance before you're close enough to recognize the face. The thatch had slumped in the middle like a tired animal settling down to sleep. Ivy had taken the eastern wall entirely, working its way through the mortar with the patient violence of anything that grows. The chimney still stood, which was somehow worse—everything else falling while the one thing built to hold fire kept holding nothing.

She had not come back. That was the rule she'd made at the graveside, standing in rain with her hood up and Tam Vess waiting at the road with his horses stamping, three years ago now: *never go back to a place that knows your name.* Odara was dead; the lesson was over; whatever the old woman had poured into her—herbs and hexes and half the alphabet of roads that existed nowhere on paper—could be carried without returning for refills.

But the book lay against her ribs inside her coat, wrapped in oilcloth, and it had been whispering to her all night.

Not words. She wanted to be clear about that, even alone on the ridge with no one to lie to. Not words, not voices, none of the things a sensible person would report to a priest or a physician. It was more like the sensation of a name on the tip of the tongue that would not resolve, sustained for hours, until her own thoughts felt like they were being spoken slightly out of order. And underneath that, steady as a pulse, the knowledge that the handwriting on last night's page—the page dated for a full moon over Thornfell, describing a red-haired thief with a scarred left hand—had matched, stroke for stroke and flaw for flaw, the notes she'd found in Odara's effects after the burial.

She had told herself it was coincidence. She had told herself a great many things since the caravan crossed into the passes, and she noticed, with the detached professionalism she brought to everything, that she was getting worse at believing herself.

*Go back,* the book did not say. *Go back and look.*

So she went back.

* * *

The door was swollen shut with rot and she went through the window instead, the way she used to as a girl, when coming home late meant choosing between Odara's temper and the nettles under the sill. The nettles were taller now. Everything was. A fallen rafter had crushed the corner where the medicine shelves stood, and what the weather hadn't reached the mice had—the bundles of dried yarrow and feverfew long since shredded into bedding. The smell hit her like a hand across the face: old smoke, old herbs, old woman, all of it faded but not gone, the way grief fades but does not.

"You could have told me," Mirabel said to the empty room.

Her voice sounded obscene in here. She hadn't spoken aloud in two days except to bargain with a ferryman, and the cottage took each word and gave back silence with interest. Dust hung in the light from the broken roof in slow golden columns, turning when she moved through them, closing behind her like water.

Three years of careful nothing. Three years of paying cash, using no surname, letting innkeepers forget her face by morning, keeping her left hand gloved so the scar stayed hers alone. She had built her invisibility the way Odara had taught her to build a hedge-charm: small stitches, tight knots, nothing ornamental enough to attract attention. And all that time, apparently, there had been a book in the world with tonight written in it. A page with her walk across Thornfell described before she was born.

She knelt where the floorboards were soundest—beside the hearth, because if Odara had hidden anything, it would be there. Not under the bed like an ordinary coward, not in the herb loft like a clever one. Under the hearthstone. Because Odara Vetch had believed, with the ferocity of the genuinely paranoid, that everyone searches a house but nobody disturbs a fire's grave.

The stone came up easier than it should have. Someone had loosened it recently—or maintained it recently—and that thought arrived cold in her stomach a full second before her hands understood why. Beneath it, nested in oilcloth gone stiff with age, was a tin box. On the lid, scratched with a blunt nail in the shorthand Odara had invented for her own accounts, a single instruction:

*Mirabel. Only.*

* * *

She sat back on her heels in the dust of her old life and let herself feel it properly for a moment, which was a luxury she rationed like everything else.

Odara had known she would come. Had loosened the stone—no, had kept it loose, checked it, tended it, the way you tend a wound you expect to reopen. Which meant the old woman had spent some part of her last years expecting Mirabel to be driven back here by exactly this kind of discovery. Expecting the book. Perhaps expecting the Chancellery riders Corvane would send crawling up the valley within the day.

Which meant Odara had known about the book. Had known, and taught Mirabel everything else—road-craft and herb-lore and how to be no one—and never once said *there is a story with your shape already drawn in it, child.*

"Only me," Mirabel said bitterly. "You couldn't even leave me a straight answer."

She pried the lid.

Inside: a letter, folded twice, sealed with candle wax and no signet. Beneath it, wrapped separately, a small bundle of bones—not human, she noted with relief; bird, probably crow, strung on wire in the old way Odara used for scrying. And beneath those, a second object that stopped her breath: a scrap of parchment, very old, brown as autumn, covered in a script she didn't recognize. Angular, dense, beautiful in the way that fortress walls are beautiful. It ran to perhaps twenty lines and ended mid-word, torn away, as though the rest had been ripped off and taken elsewhere.

She unfolded the letter.

*Mirabel—

If you are reading this, then the book found you, which means the moon is right and my time ran out before I could decide whether to tell you myself. I am sorry. I was a coward twice: once when I hid this, and once every year afterward when I chose another year of hiding.

You will want to burn the book. Don't. You will want to run. You will anyway; do it toward the fragments, not away from them. There are pieces of a name scattered through the world, and the world has forgotten they are pieces. Gather them before the thirteenth moon. After it, the door closes for another hundred years, and I will not be there to loosen stones for you.

You are the heir of the Scribe of Caerlum. Say nothing aloud until you understand what saying means. Above all:

NEVER LET THE BOOK BE READ ALOUD. Not by you, not by anyone. The words in it are not descriptions. They are instructions wearing descriptions' clothes. Whoever reads it aloud gives it a voice, and a voice is a door, and doors open from both sides.

I loved you as well as I knew how, which was badly, and I am sorry for most of it.

—O.V.*

Mirabel read it four times. By the fourth reading her hands had stopped shaking, which surprised her; by the fourth reading she had also noticed the thing she should have seen at once, the thing sitting in the middle of the letter like a stone in a shoe.

*I was a coward twice.* Not once. Twice.

And: *my time ran out before I could decide whether to tell you myself.* As though she had been deciding for years. As though the hiding wasn't a single failure but a practice, renewed annually, like taxes or sin.

The grief she had carried for three years—clean grief, honorable grief, the grief of a student for a hard teacher who had nonetheless given her everything—began, in the dust and the golden light and the smell of faded smoke, to curdle.

"You knew," she said quietly. "You knew what I was. What I'd be walking toward. And you taught me to hide instead of teaching me to fight."

The cottage said nothing. Somewhere in the walls, mice rearranged their empire around her intrusion.

* * *

She took the box outside because the cottage had become unbearable, and sat on the doorstep where she'd sat as a girl shelling peas, and unwrapped the old parchment again in the honest daylight.

The angular script resisted her. But not entirely. Odara had drilled her in paleography among everything else—"a thief who can't read a ledger is just a burglar, child"—and some of the letterforms were cousins of the old high-church hands, and once she found the rhythm of it, words began surfacing like drowned things.

It was not a complete text. Twenty lines, torn. But the surviving fragment read, in part:

*...the queen has made her bargain and the ink will not hold her. I write what remains while writing remains to me. If any hand finds this: the kingdom is not destroyed, only unrecorded. Unrecorded is not unmade. The name survives in twelve...*

Twelve. Twelve what? The tear took the rest.

And below, smaller, pressed hard enough to score the parchment:

*If my blood reads this: I am sorry for what I set in motion. The thirteenth moon shall find the door. Do not forgive me quickly.*

*Do not forgive me quickly.* Mirabel turned the scrap over, hoping for a signature, finding only foxing and age-spots. But she didn't need one. The book in her coat, the letter in the box, and now this—the same story told three times in three hands across a century, converging on a red-haired thief who had spent three years learning to be no one.

"I don't forgive you quickly," she told the Scribe, whoever he had been, wherever his bones lay. "I don't forgive anyone quickly anymore."

The sun was dropping toward the western ridgeline, and with it, somewhere behind her in the passes, she knew without knowing how she knew, riders were moving. Corvane had smelled the book the way wolves smell blood—not through evidence, but through the particular silence a hunted thing makes. She had perhaps a day. Less, if the ferryman talked, and ferrymen always talked.

She repacked the box with the care the dead deserve: letter, bones, parchment. Then she paused, and took out the letter again, and read the warning one final time.

*Never let the book be read aloud.*

She thought of the night in the caravan camp, the full moon laying itself across the pages like a finger pointing, and how she had caught herself mouthing the opening lines as she read—how the words had seemed to *want* her mouth, to arrange themselves for breath. She thought of the sensation all night, the name-on-the-tip-of-the-tongue, and understood suddenly that it hadn't been the book whispering to her at all.

It had been her almost speaking.

Cold walked up her spine on small feet. She wrapped the book tighter, buried it deeper beneath her coat, and stood.

There was one more thing to do before she left. In the ruined herb loft, under a floorboard she had helped Odara pry up a dozen times for no reason the old woman ever explained, she found the cache exactly where muscle memory said it would be: a purse of coin, a knife with a good edge, and a folded map of the valley drawn in Odara's hand—with one road marked that appeared on no other map Mirabel had ever seen, running east out of the valley toward the fens, annotated in the old shorthand with a phrase she had to work to translate:

*To Quill, who drew what wasn't there.*

East, then. Toward the fens. Toward a cartographer who mapped things that officially never existed.

She looked back once from the ridge, at the chimney still standing over the ruin of everything Odara had been to her, and felt the grief and the anger settle into something she could carry—side by side, uneasily, like strangers sharing a cart.

"You owed me the truth," she said. "I'll collect the rest of it myself."

Then she turned east, with the book silent against her ribs, the letter burning in her pocket, and the thirteenth moon six turns away and rising.
