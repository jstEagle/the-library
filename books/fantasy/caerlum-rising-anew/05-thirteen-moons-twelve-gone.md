## 5. Thirteen Moons, Twelve Gone

Dawn came to the ruined cottage grey and thin as old linen, and Mirabel sat in the wreckage of her teacher's life with the prophecy book open on her knees and ink on her fingers that was not hers.

She had not slept. She had meant to—she had even lain down on the pallet in the corner where Odara had slept for thirty years, breathing the smell of dried yarrow and lamp-oil that still hung in the blankets like a ghost refusing eviction—but sleep would not come while the book lay there on the table, shut, patient, radiating its quiet terrible fact: *tonight's date, written a century before tonight.*

So she had got up and opened it again by candlelight, because if she was going to be unmade she intended to understand the instrument first.

The book was not, she now saw, one book. It wore the binding of a ledger—the same plain calf-skin the Chancellery used for tax rolls—and inside, the pages were a palimpsest of hands. The newest hand she knew better than her own: Odara's, cramped and slanted leftward, the hand that had corrected her sums and her pronunciation of dead languages and, once, memorably, her posture. Odara's notes crawled through the margins like ivy over an older wall. And beneath them, faded to the brown of dried blood, ran the older script—the Scribe's hand, formal and beautiful, each letter placed as though it were being paid by the hour.

Mirabel turned to the page headed with last night's date. *A red-haired thief, scarred of left hand, shall cross the Thornfell under the full moon.* She looked down at her own hand, at the pale seam of the scar across her knuckles, and felt the vertigo rise in her again—not fear exactly, but the sensation of standing on a stair that wasn't there.

*All right,* she told herself. *If it knew last night, what else does it know?*

She read forward. The next dated entry was four days hence, and it was short, and she read it three times before her mind agreed to hold it.

*On the thirteenth turn of the sealing moon, the door shall stand where the stone remembers. Let the heir gather what is scattered, or let all be gathered into silence.*

Thirteenth turn of the sealing moon.

There was a colophon at the back—a scribe's habit, signing off—and beneath the flourish of the older script, a date. A century old. And beside it, small, in Odara's hand, a single word: *sealed.*

Mirabel did sums the way other people prayed. She pulled Odara's almanac from the shelf—dust sheeting off it into the candle-flame—and worked backward through the years, month by month, counting full moons the way a miser counts coins. Her lips moved. The candle guttered and steadied. Outside, the valley lay silent under the aftermath of last night's moon, which had been fat and white and already three-quarters spent when she'd crossed the pass.

Twelve moons since the sealing had come and gone.

Which meant the thirteenth—

Her pencil stopped.

The coming moon. The next full moon. Twenty-eight days, give or take the almanac's honesty about the sky, and the sky was never honest.

Twenty-eight days.

She sat very still. Somewhere in the cottage walls a mouse went about its small business, indifferent to the rearrangement of history. Twenty-eight days to find a door she'd never heard of in a country that did not exist, gathering *what is scattered*—and the book declined, with the serenity of all true prophecies, to specify what that was or where.

"Master," she said aloud, to the empty room, "you could have mentioned this."

The room, being empty, said nothing. But her eyes fell on the hearth—the cold hearth she'd knelt at last night, where she'd found the loose stone and the letter behind it—and she thought about the way Odara had always taught her to read a text: *not what it says first, girl. What it says last, and what it's hiding in the middle.*

*Never let it be read aloud.* That had been the letter's sharpest warning, along with the word *heir*, which still sat in her chest like a swallowed coal. Never read aloud. Which implied someone might try. Which implied—

A sound outside. Not the mouse. Hooves, more than one horse, far down the valley road but closing, the particular disciplined rhythm of animals ridden by men who were paid to ride them.

Mirabel was moving before she finished thinking, which was the whole trick of staying alive: let the body start while the mind catches up. Candle out. Book wrapped in oilcloth and shoved inside her coat, against her ribs, where it beat against her side like a second heart. Almanac. Pencil. The letter from the hearth-stone, already folded against her breast. Then she stood in the middle of the ruin, in the grey light, and made herself look at it once.

Thirty years of her life lived in this room. Every lesson. Every winter. The woman who had found her thieving bread in a market town at eleven years old and said, not unkindly, *you steal like someone apologizing; we'll fix that.*

They couldn't leave her here. Bones-in-a-box or no bones-in-a-box.

Because that was the other thing. Under the floorboards where Odara had kept the good brandy—the bottle Mirabel had been too respectful to touch until this morning—she'd found not brandy but a cedar box, and in the box, wrapped in wax-cloth, the clean dry bones of a human hand. Six finger-bones. Two from the palm. A note in Odara's writing: *His. He'll want them where he's going. So will you, though you don't know it yet.*

Odara had known. Had known for years, maybe decades, and had taught her herb-lore and lock-picking and the hundred small deceits of an invisible life, and had never once said *by the way, you are the blood of the Scribe of Caerlum, and a book in my keeping has your death date in it.*

Grief curdling into betrayal, she'd thought last night. This morning it was simply fuel.

She took the box. She took the bones of a man a century dead whose handwriting she knew intimately and whose face she had never seen, and she put them in her pack beside the almanac, and she went out the back way through the nettles, down the slope toward the goat-track that the valley's few maps showed as nothing at all.

Behind her, above the cottage, the riders crested the ridge. She didn't look. Looking was a luxury. She counted instead: six horses, the jingle of Chancellery-issue harness—she'd heard it once before, two years ago, in another life—and then, faintly, a voice giving an order, clipped and precise.

Corvane. It had to be. The auditor from the caravan, the man who burned parish records the way other officials filed them, and he had not needed six days to trace the book. He had needed less than one.

*He knows the roads no map admits exist too,* she thought, dropping low along the goat-track as the path bent behind a shoulder of gorse. *Or he owns whoever drew them.*

The track descended into birch scrub, and the birches gave cover, and Mirabel ran the way Odara had taught her—never flat-out, always at the pace you could keep till dark—with the box bumping her spine and the book pressing its old leather warmth against her ribs, and somewhere below, at the meeting of the fen-road and the river, a figure waited beside a cart that neither of them would call a rendezvous, because calling it that would make it trust, and trust was a debt, and Mirabel Yarrow owed no debts.

Except, she supposed, to the dead. Those were accruing.

* * *

Tam Vess saw her before she saw him, which he counted as a professional achievement given that she'd once picked his pocket as a demonstration. She came down through the birches at a lope, pack high and tight, hair escaping its braid in red wisps, and she moved like water finding the downhill—no wasted motion, no glance wasted either, but every glance landing where it needed to.

"You're late," he said.

"You're early." She swung into the cart's shadow, breathing hard, eyes already sweeping the road both ways. "That's worse."

"It's called initiative."

"It's called a man sitting alone in the open with a cart full of nothing, looking guilty." She climbed up onto the bench and took the reins from his hands without asking, which was also becoming a habit. "Riders on the ridge. Six. Chancellery harness. They'll reach the cottage within the quarter-hour and find a cold hearth and warm ash, and then they'll do arithmetic."

"Arithmetic?"

"Two travelers bought passage over Thornfell. One of them is a known associate of a dead hedge-witch. One plus one." She clicked her tongue at the mule and the cart rolled out onto the fen-road, wheels hissing through the wet gravel. "We're the sum."

Tam glanced back. The ridge was empty—for now. "Where are we going, exactly? Since apparently we're going somewhere."

"The river. South. Away from anywhere Corvane's map covers." She was watching the road ahead, jaw set, and Tam studied her sidelong, because something had changed in her overnight and he was a smuggler, and noticing what changed overnight was how he'd stayed alive past thirty.

"You've read it," he said. "The book."

"I've read some of it."

"And?"

She was quiet long enough that the mule's hooves filled the silence, clop and hiss, clop and hiss. When she spoke, her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice she used for prices and border reports.

"There's a deadline. Twenty-eight days. And after that—" Her hands tightened on the reins. "After that, everything the book promises gets gathered into silence, and I don't think I'm excluded from the promise."

"That's vague."

"It's a prophecy. Vagueness is load-bearing." She shot him a look. "You can drop at the ferry crossing. This stopped being a smuggling job last night."

"Did it." He let the words sit. "And what did it become?"

"A problem," she said, "that I'm going to solve alone."

Tam looked at the road. The fen spread out ahead of them, silver channels and black reeds, and somewhere south in that wet labyrinth lived a drunk cartographer who owed him money, and somewhere east of that—though he couldn't have said how he knew—something was pulling at the woman beside him like a tide pulling at a moored boat.

He thought about the arrangement they'd made on the road two nights ago, neither of them naming it, her saying *I need a driver and a man who knows which bridges are bribed,* him saying *I need paying,* both of them lying by omission in perfect harmony. He thought about the ledger-debt he carried that she didn't know about, the one with Corvane's name in it, and felt it shift against his conscience like a knife worn against the skin.

"Alone," he repeated.

"Yes."

"Right." He settled back against the bench. "Well. The ferry crossing's a day south, and you'll want someone to watch the road while you sleep, and someone who can lie to toll-keepers, and someone who—" he gestured vaguely at her entire person, "—can talk to people without threatening them, which you're famously bad at."

"I'm excellent at threatening people."

"You are. It's your best quality. But it doesn't get ferries crossed." He shrugged. "So. Alone, but I'll be here. Doing nothing. For free."

Mirabel stared at him. In the grey morning light, with the fen opening around them and the riders somewhere behind and twenty-eight days burning down like a counted fuse, she looked—briefly, before she caught it—young, and tired, and frightened, and grateful, in roughly that order.

"This isn't trust," she said finally.

"No."

"We're clear on that."

"Crystal." He tipped an imaginary hat. "Purely commercial arrangement between professionals."

The cart rolled south along the fen-road, and behind them the smoke of a chimney being fed something other than wood rose thin over the birches, and neither of them looked back at it, because looking was a luxury, and the clock had started, and the thirteenth moon was already climbing toward its night.
