## 6. The Cartographer Who Drew Nothing

The fen town was called Mireholt on the signposts and nothing at all on the maps, which Tam Vess had always taken as a fair assessment of the place. It sat where the road drowned itself in reeds and thought better of continuing, a scatter of black-timbered houses on stilts above brown water, connected by walkways that creaked like old men complaining about weather. The air smelled of peat smoke and rot and, faintly, of fish that had given up on dignity some time ago.

"You said she'd be in a tavern," Mirabel said. She had not spoken much in the two days since they'd fled the valley, and when she did speak it was in that flat, careful voice she used when she was counting something—coins, exits, betrayals. She had the book wrapped in oilcloth inside her coat, against her heart, like a second heartbeat she didn't trust.

"She's always in a tavern," Tam said. "The question is which one. She rotates. Says it keeps the bailiffs honest."

"There are bailiffs in a town that isn't on any map?"

"Especially there. Bailiffs love places nobody's watching." He steered them left at a walkway fork, past a shrine to some river god whose statue had been repurposed as a coat rack. "Ilexa Quill. Remember the name, and don't laugh when you meet her. Everyone laughs. She bites."

"I don't laugh."

"You did. In the pass. When I slipped on the scree."

"That was a cough."

"It was a laugh, and it was deserved, and I've forgiven you." He held out a hand to steady her over a gap in the planking where a plank had simply decided it was done with its career. Her scarred left hand closed around his wrist—brief, businesslike, gone again. He tried not to notice how much he noticed.

The tavern she meant was called the Drowned Sexton, and it earned the name by being half-sunk, its lower floor permanently flooded, its patrons drinking on the upper story with their boots propped on a rail to keep them out of the water that lapped at the floorboards below. A boy fished from the window. Nobody remarked on this.

Ilexa Quill sat in the corner with her back to the wall, which told Tam she'd been a soldier or a criminal before she'd been a disgrace, or possibly all three in rotation. She was a broad woman gone soft at the edges, with a cartographer's hands—ink-stained in the creases, permanently, the way a smoker's fingers remember the habit—and a face that had once been handsome in the way of a well-drawn map: strong lines, honest proportions, and now somewhat water-damaged. She was on her fourth or ninth drink. Her eyes, when they found Tam, were quick and clear and full of contempt, which he understood was her resting state.

"Vess," she said. "You've got the look of a man who's about to ask me for something."

"Ilexa. You look well."

"Liar. Sit down before someone notices you're standing. Both of you." Her gaze slid to Mirabel and stayed there a moment too long, the way a man's gaze stays on a loaded crossbow. "Who's your friend? No—don't tell me. Let me guess. It's more fun." She squinted. "Red hair. Scarred left hand. Walking like someone taught her that roads are a language and she's fluent. And carrying—" she sniffed the air theatrically, "—paper. Old paper. Under oilcloth. Gods, you're the one from the book, aren't you."

Tam felt Mirabel go still beside him, the stillness of a drawn blade.

"What book," Mirabel said.

"The one everyone's been whispering about since the Chancellery posted the notices." Ilexa drained her glass and held it up; the barman, a man shaped like a question mark, refilled it from a distance, sliding it along the rail. "Three hundred crowns for information leading to the recovery of a stolen historical document. Description: female, twenties, red-haired, scarred left hand. Traveling with—" she jerked a thumb at Tam, "—a smuggler of no fixed principles. That's you, Vess. You're famous. Congratulations. I'd ask for an autograph but I'd only sell it."

"When did the notices go up?" Mirabel asked.

"Four days ago."

Tam did the arithmetic and didn't like the sum. Four days. They'd been in the fens two. Which meant Corvane had posted the notices before they'd even reached the valley's edge—or rather, that the notices had gone up the moment the book changed hands, as if the Chancellery had been waiting a century for it to move.

"You're not going to turn us in, then," Tam said.

"Course not. I'm a disgraced royal cartographer. The Chancellery struck my name from the guild rolls eleven years ago. As far as they're concerned, Ilexa Quill never held a commission, never surveyed a border, and never—" she raised her glass in a bitter toast, "—drew anything worth drawing. I'm a woman who drinks in fen towns. That's my entire official existence. It's very restful." She looked at Mirabel again. "Sit down, red. You want to know about Caerlum. That's why you're here. Vess wouldn't cross a fen for anything less, and he certainly wouldn't cross one for me."

* * *

They sat. The Drowned Sexton's afternoon crowd was the kind that heard everything and repeated nothing, because repeating things got you audited, and in Mireholt *audited* was a word mothers used to frighten children. Ilexa drank and talked, and Tam watched the two currents in her—the performer and the professional—braiding and unbraiding.

"Ask your question," she said finally.

"Caerlum," Mirabel said. "Was it real?"

Ilexa laughed. It was not a kind laugh. It was the laugh of a woman who had been laughed at by better people for worse reasons. "That's the question, isn't it. That's the question they asked me at my hearing, eleven years ago, in a room with no windows. *Cartographer Quill, was Caerlum real?* And I said yes, and they said, *show us the records,* and I said, *the records are the problem,* and they said, *then you have nothing,* and I said—" she drained the glass, "—something unwise. I don't remember what. It was a long time ago and I'd had a drink."

"Tell me what you found," Mirabel said. "All of it. Please."

Something in the *please* got through. Ilexa set the glass down, and for a moment the drunkard left her face and the surveyor looked out—the woman who had once stood on cold hills with a theodolite and cared about truth down to the width of a pencil line.

"I was twenty-six," she said. "Commissioned to resurvey the eastern marches. Routine work. The Chancellery had just finished its grand consolidation—every ledger, every parish roll, every tithe record in the nine provinces brought under one roof. Wonderful for my trade. You can't draw a border you can't tax." She turned her glass slowly on the rail. "So I'm out east, past Thornfell, in country so empty the birds file flight plans, and I find a boundary stone. Old. Older than the provinces. Carved with a seal I didn't recognize—a tower with thirteen stars around it, half worn away. And the stone stands on a line. Not a road. A line. You could feel it, walking it, like walking a scar. The land on one side of it and the land on the other didn't agree about something."

"Agree about what?"

"That's what I went looking for. I spent two years on it. Two years, off-commission, my own coin, because a surveyor finds a line that isn't on any chart and she either follows it or she's no surveyor at all." Ilexa's voice had gone quiet and hard. "I found the line ran two hundred miles. I found place-names along it that matched no living tongue but turned up, broken, in harvest songs and children's rhymes and the nonsense syllables of lullabies—*caer-lum, caer-lum, the moon counts one*—you know the kind of thing. I found a millstone with half a seal on it in a village that had been grinding grain on their king's face for a hundred years without knowing what it was. And then I went to the Chancellery archives to find the records that would explain it all."

She stopped. Tam had seen men stop like that before—at the edge of a drop, in the dark, before saying a thing they couldn't take back.

"And?" Mirabel said.

"And the archive has a founding act," Ilexa said. "The very first document. Charter of the Chancellery of Records, sealed a hundred years ago, signed by the first Chancellor and witnessed by the nine provinces. It's a boring document. Mostly it's about standardizing weights and measures and the spelling of surnames. But there's a clause. Article One. The founding clause. And Article One says—" she recited it the way you recite something you've hated for eleven years, with perfect, corrosive accuracy, "*'That which is recorded may be amended; that which is amended shall be as though it had never been; and no record of the amended thing shall be retained, in any form, in any place, upon pain of amendment.'*"

The tavern noise seemed to recede. Tam found he was holding his breath without knowing why.

"Amended," Mirabel said.

"Recorded out." Ilexa's mouth twisted. "That's the archivists' term. Not *destroyed*—destroyed is crude, destroyed leaves ash, ash can be read. *Recorded out.* You go through every ledger, every map, every parish book, every gravestone inscription, every song written down, every letter in every family's cedar chest, and you amend. You strike the name. You scrape the vellum. You burn the copy and correct the citation that referenced the copy. And when you're done, the thing doesn't just cease to exist. It ceases to have ever existed. There's no gap in the record, red. That's the horror of it. A gap you could investigate. There's no gap. Where Caerlum was, the records show—empty land. Unclaimed. Always. Two hundred miles of nothing that was never anything, held by no crown, owed to no one, *since forever.*" She lifted her glass and found it empty and set it down with exaggerated care. "The founding act didn't erase a country. It erased the *possibility* of the country. And then it made erasing the founding act's own first duty, forever, so the wound would never scab."

"How do you know it was a country?" Tam asked. "Maybe it was nothing. Maybe you found an old border and built a nation out of a boundary stone and some nursery rhymes."

"Because of the census," Ilexa said.

"The what?"

"The tax rolls of the provinces, going back before the Chancellery, before the consolidation. I got hold of a provincial roll—pre-Chancellery, ninety-eight years old, misfiled, which is the only reason anything survives in that place. And in the eastern province's roll there's a revenue figure. A big one. A third of the province's whole assessed wealth, listed under a heading that's been scraped clean. Not torn out. *Scraped.* Someone went through that document with a knife and a pumice stone and removed one word from one heading, and left everything else—the sums, the dates, the signatures—untouched. Because the sums were needed. The tax was real. Only the name had to go." She looked at Mirabel. "You can scrape a word off a page. You can't scrape a hundred years of grain, and wool, and tin, and soldiers' wages off the world. Somebody *paid* those taxes, Vess. Somebody's grandchildren are alive right now, in villages along that line, singing a lullaby about a moon that counts to thirteen, and they don't know why, and their priests tell them the words are nonsense, and the Chancellery tells the priests the same. A hundred thousand people, give or take, walking around with a country in their mouths and no idea it's there."

Mirabel had gone very pale. Her hand—the scarred one, the one from the notices, from the book—had come up to rest flat against her coat, over the oilcloth, over the paper heartbeat.

"The book," Tam said, carefully. "The one they want. It's a prophecy. Written a hundred years ago. It describes her."

"Of course it does." Ilexa said it the way a man says *of course the river flows downhill.* "You don't record out a kingdom and leave no thread. The Chancellery missed one document. One. Because it was never *in* the record to begin with—it was written before the founding act, sealed, and carried out of the country by the last man who remembered everything. The Scribe of Caerlum. He wrote down the true name in fragments and hid them where amendment couldn't reach—not in ledgers, not in archives, but in *people*. In songs. In stones. In the one archive the Chancellery can't audit." She tapped her own temple. "Memory. Bad memory. Drunk, singing, half-remembered memory. The Chancellery can burn a book in a second. It's still trying to burn a lullaby after a hundred years."

The barman drifted past with the question-mark slouch and refilled Ilexa's glass without being asked. Tam realized the woman had ordered nothing. He realized, also, that the corner of the tavern had gone quieter around them—not empty, not pointed, just quieter, in the way of a room giving privacy to people it had decided not to see.

"Why did they strike you from the rolls?" Mirabel asked. "For finding a stone?"

"For drawing the map." Ilexa smiled, and it was a terrible thing to see, that smile—pride and ruin in the same expression. "I drew it. Two years of work. Every mile of the line. Every village. Every name I could dig out of songs and stones and one scraped tax roll. A complete map of Caerlum. The first in a hundred years. I brought it to the Chancellery myself. Carried it through their doors like a fool carrying a wedding gift." She turned the glass. "They were very polite. They thanked me. They said my survey work was *excellent*—those were the words, *excellent, Quill, first-rate technique.* And then a clerk took the map into the back room, and a senior archivist came out and explained Article One to me, and asked whether I had made any copies. And I said no. And they said, *then there is no map, and there never was a map, and you are a surveyor of the eastern marches who found empty land, and here is a stipend for your trouble, and here is a form confirming that Ilexa Quill holds no commission and never has.*" She drank. "They gave me a pension, Vess. A pension! For a woman who doesn't exist professionally. I sign for it every quarter. My name on the line. It's the only document in the world that says I was ever anything, and it says I was *nothing*, and it pays for the drink."

Nobody spoke for a moment. Out the window, the fishing boy pulled up a fish, considered it, and threw it back with the air of a critic.

"The fragments," Mirabel said at last. Her voice was steady but Tam had spent enough years reading cargoes and faces to hear the strain in it, the sound of a hull taking water below the waterline. "The Scribe hid the true name in fragments. You said songs. Stones. You found the millstone."

"I found the millstone. Half a seal. The other half of the name would have been cut on the matching stone, or on the lintel of the mill, or somewhere the Chancellery's knives missed." Ilexa's eyes were suddenly sharp and sober, all the drink burned off by something hotter. "And here's what you need to understand, red, before you go chasing them. Every fragment you take makes you louder. The name wants to be whole. It's been waiting a hundred years, and when you start gathering it, it starts *singing*—and the Chancellery hears singing. They have people whose whole profession is listening for the thing Article One forbids. They call them auditors. They're not accountants. They're hounds."

"I know," Mirabel said. "There's one already. Corvane."

Ilexa's glass stopped halfway to her mouth.

"Corvane," she repeated. "Corvane of the Ninth Audit. Tall? Grey-eyed? Smiles like he's grading you?" She set the glass down untouched. "He was at my hearing. Eleven years ago. Junior then. He took notes the whole time—wrote down every word I said in that beautiful hand of his, and I remember thinking, there's a man who loves writing more than he loves what's written. He's the one who suggested the pension." She looked from Mirabel to Tam and back. "He posted the notices himself, didn't he. Your auditor. He's not doing his job. He's doing his *vocation*."

* * *

They left the Drowned Sexton at dusk, when the fen went gold and then grey and the walkways became the only honest roads in the world. Ilexa walked them as far as the town's edge, steady on the planks in a way that suggested the drink was a costume she wore indoors.

"The millstone's at Harrow Ford," she said. "Three days east, if the water's low. The village won't stop you—they've been guarding that stone for generations without knowing it's a guard duty. But there's a records-wight bound to it. The Chancellery set them on anything that survived Article One. It won't fight you. That's not how they work." She paused at the last plank, where the fen opened into reed and open sky. "It'll *read* you. Everything you are, everything you've done, out loud, in your own voice, to anyone within earshot. And then it'll ask if you still want what you came for, knowing. That's all. That's the whole horror of them." She smiled her terrible smile. "Most people walk away."

"And the ones who don't?" Tam asked.

"The ones who don't," Ilexa said, "become interesting." She pressed something into Mirabel's hand—a folded paper, soft with age. "My map. Or what's left of it. They burned the original in front of me. I redrew it from memory over eleven years, a little every night, in taverns, on the backs of bills. It's not as good as the first one. My hand shakes now." She shrugged, enormously. "But it's the only map of Caerlum in the world, and I'm giving it to a thief, and I'd like it noted somewhere, in some record, that Ilexa Quill died a cartographer."

"You're not dying," Tam said. "You're buying the next round."

"Vess. Sweet, dishonest Vess." She turned back toward the town, then stopped. "The book she's carrying. The prophecy. When it's done—when the name's whole or the moon's set—bring it to me. I want to see the last page. I've spent eleven years redrawing a country from songs. I want to know how the story ends."

Then she was a shape in the dusk, and then a sound of a door, and then the fen had her.

Tam looked at Mirabel. She stood at the edge of the walkway with the folded map in her scarred hand, and she was not looking at the map, or the fen, or him. She was looking east, where the first star was out over the drowned country, and her lips were moving—very slightly, silently, the way a person's lips move when they are reading something written on the inside of their own eyes.

"Three days to Harrow Ford," Tam said.

"Yes," Mirabel said. "And Tam—" she folded the map with the precision of someone handling a living thing, and put it inside her coat with the book, two heartbeats now. "The lullaby Ilexa mentioned. *The moon counts one.* I know it. I've known it my whole life. Odara used to sing it when she thought I was asleep." Her voice went very quiet. "I always thought it was nonsense."

She stepped off the walkway onto the fen path, heading east, and the reeds closed behind her like a page turning.
