## 7. Fragments of a Name

They left Harrow Ford before the tide did, which Ilexa said was the only sensible way to leave anywhere.

"Water remembers who crosses it," the old cartographer muttered, cinching the straps of a pack that looked too heavy for her bird-boned frame. "Mud remembers longer. That's why I live here." She spat into the channel for emphasis, a woman who had spent forty years being wrong on purpose and had grown proud of it. "Everything else forgets too fast. Mud at least holds a grudge."

Mirabel walked behind her along the raised causeway, the fen unrolling on either side in sheets of grey-green water and rusting reeds, and tried to understand how a person could look like this—drunk for a decade, exiled, forgotten—and still walk like someone who had once drawn the edges of the world for a living. Ilexa Quill's boots found the firm ground without her eyes ever dropping to it. Her hands, when she gestured, moved in the shorthand of maps: a curve here, a boundary there, the world sketched in the air.

Tam brought up the rear with the mule, and Mirabel did not look at him, because looking at him meant deciding something about him, and she was not ready to decide.

They walked until the fen town was a smudge behind them and the causeway became a dyke and the dyke became a sheep-track, and then Ilexa stopped at a leaning boundary stone so old its inscription had worn to a suggestion, and said, "Here's as good a classroom as any. Sit. My knees have opinions."

They sat. The wind came off the water carrying the smell of peat and rot and, faintly, something sweeter—bog-myrtle, maybe, or the memory of it.

"Now," Ilexa said, "you've read the book. The Scribe's book. So you know what the door is. What you don't know—and I only learned it because I was stupid enough to map the border that wasn't supposed to exist—is *how* a kingdom gets erased, and what's left over after."

"Tell me," Mirabel said.

"Records aren't mirrors, girl. Everyone thinks the Chancellery just *looks* at the world and writes it down. Wrong. Writing is a load-bearing wall. You write a thing down, and the thing leans on the writing, and the writing leans on the thing, and after enough years nobody can tell which is holding which up." Ilexa's eyes were bloodshot and sharp as awls. "The Chancellery's founding act—there was an act, there's always an act, they love their acts—struck Caerlum from every ledger, every charter, every map, every parish roll from the Salt Coast to the Thornfells. Not burned. *Struck.* There's a difference. Burning leaves ash, and ash can be read. They cut the name out and closed the wound behind it, neat as surgery."

"And Caerlum—"

"Caerlum fell over." Ilexa said it flatly, the way you'd say a house fell over. "When you take the writing away from a thing, the thing has nothing to lean on. The kingdom didn't get destroyed. It got *unsupported.* It's still there the way a note is still there after the bell stops ringing—present, but only if you know how to listen."

Tam shifted against the boundary stone. "Then it's gone. You can't gather an echo."

"That," Ilexa said, "is where you're wrong, smuggler, and I'll thank you to be wrong more quietly." She leaned forward. "The Chancellery is thorough, but the world is sloppy. Names leak. They always leak. A kingdom a hundred years old doesn't live in ledgers—it lives in *people,* and people are the worst archives ever built. They misremember. They sing. They carve things on stones when no clerk is looking. The name of Caerlum survived the way water survives a drought: in the deep places, in the cracks, in the nonsense."

"The lullaby," Mirabel said.

Ilexa looked at her for a long moment, and something in the old woman's face softened—not kindness, exactly, but recognition. "The lullaby. *The moon counts one, the moon counts two,* and so on, and every nurse from here to the Thornfells sings it without knowing why, and every child learns it without knowing what it counts." She held up one gnarled finger. "Thirteen. It counts to thirteen, and then it stops, because the woman who wrote it—the Scribe—only knew there would be thirteen moons. He wrote the song into the mouths of mothers so the calendar would survive even if the book didn't. That's what a fragment *is,* Mirabel Yarrow. It's a piece of the name of Caerlum, hidden in something too small and too beloved for any auditor to think worth cutting out."

"Hidden how?"

"Every way there is. A verse in a harvest song. A carving on a boundary stone. Half a royal seal cut into a millstone in a village that thinks it's a good-luck charm. Nonsense syllables in a children's rhyme that are only nonsense if you don't know Old Caerlish." Ilexa's mouth twisted. "The Scribe scattered the true name like a man scattering seed before a fire comes. He couldn't save the harvest, so he saved the *seed.* And the prophecy says the door opens when the name is whole again. Gather the fragments. Speak them together. That's the door."

The wind moved through the reeds, and Mirabel thought she heard, underneath it, a rhythm like a lullaby, and made herself not hum along.

"And the fragments," she said. "When we take them. When we gather them. What happens then?"

Ilexa's expression did something Mirabel had not seen on it before: it went careful.

"That's the price," the old woman said. "You want to know it now, before you start, because I've seen people start and then wish someone had told them. A fragment is a piece of the name that has spent a century being *quiet.* Quiet is safety. The Chancellery's clerks can't cut out what they can't hear. But the moment you gather a fragment—carry it, speak it, wake it up—it starts to make noise. It starts to *pull.* Other fragments feel it. And the Chancellery feels it too, because the Chancellery is built out of the same writing the name is built out of, and when a struck name starts to come back, every ledger in the country itches." She tapped Mirabel's coat, over the book, with one finger. "Every fragment you take makes you louder. Not metaphorically. You will be able to *feel* yourself getting louder, like a bell being struck in a room full of people listening for bells. Corvane won't need informants by the end. He'll just need to follow the ringing."

Tam was quiet. Mirabel was quiet. The fen ticked and gurgled around them, indifferent.

"Then we'd best be loud on our own terms," Mirabel said at last. "Where's the first one?"

Ilexa smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. "Millbrook. Two days north. There's a mill on the River Cael—don't react, smuggler, the river kept its name because nobody thought to ask what it was named *for*—and the millstone there is the original, older than the mill, older than the mill's grandmother's mill. Cut into the stone, on the underside where the axle covers it, is half the royal seal of Caerlum. The miller's family has polished it for six generations because they believe it keeps the flour sweet." She stood, knees cracking like green kindling. "Which it may. Things that remember tend to be lucky."

"And the wight," Tam said.

"Ah." Ilexa shouldered her pack. "Yes. The wight."

* * *

They reached Millbrook on the evening of the second day, and Mirabel understood immediately why Ilexa had sent them at dusk.

The mill stood where the River Cael bent, a squat stone building with a wheel that turned slow and heavy in the failing light, and the whole valley around it had the hushed quality of a place that was used to being listened to. The miller's family—three generations of broad, flour-dusted people—fed them bread and cheese and asked no questions, because Ilexa had sent word ahead, and because millers, Mirabel was learning, were a class of people who understood that some stones are not to be discussed with strangers.

The old millstone sat in the yard, half-sunk in the earth, its axle-hole dark, its rim worn smooth by a century of hands that had never once wondered what they were polishing.

"It wakes at full dark," the miller's daughter said. She was perhaps fifteen, with flour in her eyebrows and an air of tremendous seriousness. "Grandda says the stone keeps a dog under it. A paper dog. It doesn't bite if you don't touch the stone wrong." She looked at Mirabel with sudden fierce curiosity. "Are you the one the song's about? Grandda says the song got a new verse. It came up the river last week, from the fen. Nobody taught it to anybody. It just *arrived.*"

Mirabel's mouth went dry. "What verse?"

The girl hummed it—four notes, rising—and Mirabel felt them land in her chest like a hand pressing on a bruise. The lullaby had grown. The name was spreading on its own now, the way a fire spreads once the wind takes it. *Louder,* Ilexa had said. The fragments were already talking to each other.

"Go before full dark," the girl said. "Or after. Grandda says the between-times are when the dog is awake."

* * *

They went at full dark, because Mirabel had read the prophecy's marginalia three more times on the road and the Scribe's hand had been very clear: *the thirteenth moon shall find the door, and the door shall be found by night, when the record sleeps.* The record slept at night. Whatever a records-wight was, it was a thing made of record, and records, like everything else in the Chancellery's country, kept office hours.

The mill yard was silvered with the first suggestion of moonrise. The stone waited.

"Describe it again," Tam murmured. "The seal."

"Half the royal seal of Caerlum," Mirabel said. "Ilexa drew it for me. A tower with a moon above it, cut off down the middle. The other half is somewhere else—another fragment, another hiding place. The Scribe split everything. No single piece tells the whole name." She knelt beside the stone. The axle-hole was a circle of deeper dark. "The carving's on the underside of the rim. I have to reach in."

"With your hand," Tam said. It was not a question. His voice had gone flat and careful, the smuggler's voice, the voice that priced danger before naming it. "Into the hole where the paper dog lives."

"With my hand," Mirabel agreed, and was distantly surprised to find it steady.

She thought of Odara's cottage. She thought of the letter hidden in the wall, *never let the book be read aloud,* and the way grief and betrayal had spent three days taking turns wearing her face. She thought: *I have been no one for three years. Tonight I put my hand into a hole in the world and take back a piece of a kingdom. This is what being someone costs.*

She reached in.

The stone's underside was cold and dry and carved—the Scribe's work, she knew it the way she knew her own handwriting, the same sure strokes she'd found in the margins of the prophecy book. Her fingers traced the half-tower, the half-moon. The stone hummed under her touch, a vibration too low for ears, felt in the wrist bones, in the teeth.

And then the dark in the axle-hole *opened its eyes.*

They were not eyes, quite. They were the pale rectangles of ledger pages, two of them, floating in the dark, and they turned toward her hand with the patient attention of a clerk reviewing a column of figures. The wight unfolded out of the stone the way a document unfolds out of a drawer—flat, then dimensional, then *present,* a figure of layered greys, its body a shifting palimpsest of old records: Mirabel glimpsed shipping manifests, a marriage register, a tax roll, all of it moving across its surface like water moving over stones.

*UNAUTHORIZED RETRIEVAL,* it said. Its voice was the voice of a stamp coming down on paper.

"I'm authorized," Mirabel said, and her voice did not shake, and she would remember that with pride for the rest of her life. "By the Scribe of Caerlum. By the prophecy. By the thirteenth moon." She did not withdraw her hand. The half-seal burned cold under her fingers. "Check your records. I'm in them. I've been in them longer than I've been in them, if you understand me."

The wight's ledger-eyes flickered. Somewhere inside it, pages turned. And Mirabel felt the thing *read* her—the scar on her left hand, the book against her ribs, the blood in her that had come down a hundred years from an exiled man's pen—and she felt the moment it found her, because the whole grey body of it went still as a closed book.

*HEIR,* it said. And then, in a different tone, one that had almost—almost—the texture of relief: *EXPECTED.*

"Then you'll let me take it," Mirabel said.

*I WILL LET YOU TAKE IT,* the wight said, *BECAUSE I HAVE BEEN HOLDING IT FOR ONE HUNDRED YEARS AND I AM TIRED.*

The half-seal came free of the stone with a sound like a sigh, and it was small—a disc of pale stone the size of a coin, cool as riverbed, and the moment it touched Mirabel's palm the whole world *shifted.* She felt it the way Ilexa had promised: a note struck in a silent room. The lullaby in her head gained a word. The direction of *east* became a physical fact, a pull behind her sternum, gentle and irresistible, like a hand on her back. And somewhere far to the south, she felt—she had no other word for it—*attention* turn toward her, vast and patient and indexed, like a great eye opening behind a great desk.

The wight folded itself back into the axle-hole. Before it went, it said one more thing, in its stamp-of-a voice, and the words followed Mirabel out of the yard and into the dark and did not stop following her for days:

*THEY WILL HEAR YOU NOW. RUN WELL, HEIR.*

* * *

They were three miles north of Millbrook, walking through a beech wood with the fragment wrapped in wool inside Mirabel's coat, when Tam stopped and put his head up like a deer.

"What," Mirabel said.

"Lanterns," Tam said. "Behind us. Two miles, maybe. Moving fast. Organized." He looked at her, and the moonlight made his face unreadable, and Mirabel thought, not for the first time: *what aren't you telling me.* "Riders don't ride that fast through beech woods at night unless they're following a sound."

Mirabel put her hand over the coat, over the small cold disc that had been quiet for a hundred years and was quiet no longer. Behind them, faint but unmistakable, someone was singing—the lullaby, the new verse, the one that had arrived up the river without being taught—sung not by a nursemaid but by many voices, in unison, in the dark, like a search party using a name for a net.

"They're not following us," Mirabel said quietly. "They're following *it.* Following the ringing." She turned east, toward the pull behind her sternum, toward the next fragment, wherever the Scribe had hidden it. "Then we run, Tam. And we keep running, and every time we get louder, and every mile makes it worse."

She started to run, and the beech woods took them, and behind them the singing grew clearer, note by patient note, like a page being turned toward the place where their names were written.
