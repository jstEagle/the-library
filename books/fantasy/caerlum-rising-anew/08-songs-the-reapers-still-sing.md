## 8. Songs the Reapers Still Sing

The village was called Hallowmere, though the sign at its edge had been repainted so many times that older names ghosted beneath the letters—*Hallowmear*, and beneath that something in a script Mirabel did not recognize, all loops and broken staves, like a song someone had tried to write down and failed.

She noticed it because she had trained herself to notice such things. Three years of being no one taught you to read a place the way you read a face: which doors were freshly painted, which wells had new rope, which signs had been painted over rather than replaced. A village that kept repainting an old sign rather than carving a new one was a village that remembered more than it admitted.

"You're staring at the post again," Tam said behind her. He had the cart's reins looped loose around one fist and was watching the fields instead—the wide gold sweep of barley going down to stubble, forty reapers bent along its length in a line that moved like something breathing.

"I'm reading it," Mirabel said. "There are words under the words."

"There usually are." He said it lightly, but he'd been saying things lightly for two days now, ever since the millstone, ever since they'd pried the first fragment free while the records-wight screamed itself apart into paper ash around them. Every fragment they took made them louder—Ilexa had warned them of that too, drunk and certain by turns in her fen-side shack—and Mirabel could feel the truth of it in her own skin. She felt *watched*. Not vaguely. Specifically. The way you feel watched when someone has your description in hand.

She'd cut her hair shorter in Millbrook. It hadn't helped. Nothing helped, lately, except moving.

"Harvest's late here," Tam said. "Barley should've been down a week ago."

"Or they're slow because they want to be." She nodded toward the field. "Listen."

He cocked his head. Over the scrape and whisper of sickles came the reapers' voices, rising and falling in a work-song with the easy worn rhythm of something sung ten thousand times—call and answer, the men's lines low, the women's higher on the refrain. Mirabel couldn't catch the words at distance, only the shape. And the shape was wrong. Work-songs kept time with labor; this one dragged, stretched, held certain syllables a beat too long, as though the singers were carrying something heavy inside the tune and didn't know what.

"It's a threshing rhythm," Tam said. "What of it?"

"Threshing songs don't have refrains that change key halfway through." She climbed down from the cart seat, brushing straw from her skirts, and felt the satchel against her ribs shift its weight—the book, warm as a sleeping animal, always warm since the boundary stone. "Nobody's threshing. They're cutting barley. Somebody changed the words to fit the work, and the tune remembers what it used to say."

Tam looked at her for a moment with that expression she still couldn't parse—half appraisal, half something softer he took care to hide. "And you can hear what it used to say."

"Not yet." She started toward the field. "But I'm about to be very friendly to a lot of strangers."

* * *

They worked for their welcome, which was how Mirabel preferred it. Travelers who offered labor asked fewer questions and inspired fewer lies. By midmorning she had sickles in both hands—her scarred left one aching in the cold metal, as it always did now, as though the scar itself were listening—and Tam had talked his way onto the wagons, being Tam: useful, charming, forgettable in exactly the way he chose to be.

By noon she knew the song.

It was called nothing, or the reeve's wife said it wasn't—"just what we sing, dear, my mother sang it, and hers"—and that answer, that shrug, told Mirabel more than any name would have. Songs without names are songs nobody wrote. Songs nobody wrote are songs everyone kept. The reapers sang it in verses, and the verses had been pruned over generations to fit the barley, but the refrain—the long strange refrain that bent the key—had survived untouched, because children learned it before anyone thought to explain it, the way children learn prayers before anyone thinks to translate them.

Mirabel sang along by the third pass through the field. She was careful, at first, to mouth the words she didn't have. But the refrain pulled at her, physically pulled, the way the millstone fragment had tugged at her palm when she'd first touched it—a lean, a yearning, eastward and slightly south, steady as a compass needle made of sound.

*Gold and gone, gold and gone,*
*the door stood where the road was drawn—*

She stopped mid-verse. Her hands had stopped too, sickle frozen above the stalks.

The woman beside her, broad and sunburnt, laughed. "First time singing it? Everyone goes queer at the old lines. We don't rightly know what door they mean. There's no door on this road."

"No," Mirabel said. Her voice came out strange. "There wouldn't be."

*Gold and gone,* she sang again, quieter, testing, and the pull in her chest swung like a plumb line finding true. Eastward. South of east. Toward something that wanted the words back.

Because that was the thing Ilexa had explained in her fen-town kitchen, waving a bottle, terrible and brilliant: Caerlum persisted wherever its name survived—not whole, not anywhere, but *distributed*, scattered into songs and boundary stones and lullabies' nonsense syllables, a country hidden the way a message is hidden in plain sight across ten thousand carriers who never knew they carried it. The fragments weren't pieces of an object. They were pieces of a name. Gather them, and the name could be spoken whole. Speak it whole under the thirteenth moon at the door, and—

Well. What happened then, no one living knew. But the book warmed against Mirabel's ribs every time the refrain passed her lips, and the book, whatever else it was, was honest.

That evening, in the long amber light, she stood at the field's edge with the harvest folk drinking weak cider and sang the full refrain through, alone, watching their faces go soft and puzzled the way people look at a smell from childhood. And when she finished, the oldest man there—a reaper with hands like tree roots, who'd barely spoken all day—set down his cup and looked at her with sudden terrible clarity.

"That's not how we sing it," he said. "You sang the middle verse back in."

"The middle verse?"

"The one my gran said never to leave out, and no one remembered why." He hummed three notes, frowning. "*The name half-spoken keeps the sleep.* Something like. My gran said if the song's sung wrong too many years running, something bad wakes up hungry." He shrugged, embarrassed by his own superstition, and the moment closed like water over a dropped coin.

But Mirabel's left hand was burning, and the book against her ribs was hot as a hearthstone, and she knew—knew with the flat certainty that had been arriving more often these days, arriving like weather—that the second fragment was here. In the song. In the verse this village had kept without knowing why, the way the millstone village had kept the carved seal without knowing what it sealed.

They stayed the night. She paid the reeve's wife for lodging honestly, overpaid, watched the coins disappear with the particular gratitude of people for whom harvest had come late and thin. And after midnight, when the house slept, she sat by the banked fire with the book open on her knees and sang the refrain in a whisper, all verses, the middle one restored from the old man's grandmother's memory as best she could reconstruct it—and as the last syllable left her, the page turned itself.

Not turned, exactly. The ink *moved*. Words surfaced on the vellum like drowned bodies coming up through dark water, and Mirabel read them by firelight with her heart hammering:

*Second of five: the song kept wrong.*
*Spoken whole, it leans toward home.*

And the pull in her chest—eastward, south of east—became so strong she gripped the chair arms. Somewhere out there past the dark hills, something was leaning back toward her.

"What did you do?" Tam stood in the doorway, blanket around his shoulders, hair flattened on one side. He'd been sleeping in the stable; he'd taken to doing that since Millbrook, and she hadn't asked why, and he hadn't offered, and the not-asking had started to feel like a wall between them built entirely out of politeness.

"Found it," she said. "The second fragment. It was in the song." She looked up at him, and the firelight caught her face, and she realized she was smiling and couldn't remember starting. "Tam—it's *real*. All of it. The kingdom. The door. Everything. It's real and it wants to come back."

He looked at her a long moment—at her lit face, her burned hand pressed to the book—and something crossed his expression that she read, wrongly, as tiredness.

"Then we should move," he said. "Tonight. Before whatever wants it back finds out we know."

* * *

They left before dawn, and Corvane arrived before noon.

Mirabel would learn this afterward, the way you learn things about places you've fled: from a drover on the east road who'd doubled back, from the reeve's wife's cousin, from the particular silence that falls over a region when something has happened in it that everyone saw and no one will say aloud.

Corvane came with six riders and a warrant that named no crime, because the Chancellery of Records did not require crimes. It required discrepancies. And Hallowmere had become discrepant: an auditor's informer in Millbrook had reported a red-haired woman with a scarred left hand asking questions about harvest songs, and when Corvane cross-referenced the parish ledger against the tithe rolls, he found that Hallowmere's records went back further than any parish's had a right to—unbroken, uncorrected, *unreviewed* for four generations. Unreviewed meant ungoverned. Ungoverned meant suspect.

The reeve met him at the village well, as custom required, with bread and salt.

His name was Aldous Wren. He was fifty-one years old. He had served Hallowmere as reeve for nineteen years, and in that time he had falsified nothing, stolen nothing, and hidden exactly one thing—which he hid not from the Chancellery but from his own children: the fact that the harvest song's middle verse, the one his grandmother had sworn must never be dropped, referred to a door, and a name, and a kingdom that the histories said had never existed. He hid it because his grandmother had made him swear, and because some inheritances you keep even when you don't believe them.

Corvane asked him, politely, standing by the well with the bread untouched between them, whether any traveler had passed through asking unusual questions.

Aldous Wren considered lying. He was not good at it; he had spent a life keeping accounts that balanced. And besides, the red-haired woman had overpaid for her lodging, and the tall smuggler had fixed his wagon axle without being asked, and the Chancellery's riders had already searched the stable and found straw still warm.

"They stayed one night," he said. "They went east on the low road."

Corvane thanked him. Then he asked about the song.

This was the question that decided everything, though no one in the square understood it yet. Because Aldous Wren—who had lied to tax assessors, lied to a bishop's surveyor, lied once magnificently to a company of press-gang recruiters—looked at the auditor's mild grey eyes and heard his grandmother's voice saying *never let the middle verse drop*, and he said:

"We sing what our grandmothers taught us. Nothing more."

It was, technically, true. It was also, Corvane understood instantly, an evasion, because a truthful village answers questions about its songs freely—songs are nothing, songs are air—and a village that guards its songs is a village that has been guarding something. He had burned a parish's records in Thornfell for less. He did not burn Hallowmere's records. Records, after all, could be seized, corrected, reviewed. But a song lived in mouths, and mouths could not be audited—only *incentivized*.

He ordered the reeve hanged from the beam of his own tithe barn, in front of the harvest-gathered village, on a charge written up on the spot: *obstruction of record*. The warrant was signed before the rope was fetched.

"He withheld information relevant to a Crown audit," Corvane told the assembled villagers, in the reasonable voice he used for arithmetic. "The Crown does not punish villages. It punishes omissions. Remember the difference, and there will be no further corrections."

Aldous Wren died at midday with the barley still standing in the eastern field, unfinished, because no one could bear to sing while they worked, and the work could not be done in silence—that was the old law of harvest, older than any of them, older than memory—and so the crop stood golden and uncut for three days while the village mourned a man for the crime of keeping a promise to his dead grandmother.

* * *

Mirabel heard about it at dusk, two days east, from a drover whose cart they'd flagged down for water.

She listened standing very still, the way she'd learned to stand when the world tilted—one hand flat on the cart's rail, the other curled around the strap of the satchel. The drover told it plainly, because drovers trade in news the way other men trade in wool, and by the end Tam had gone a grey color she'd never seen on him, grey as old parchment.

"They hanged him?" she said. "For withholding information?"

"For *not telling him about the song*." The drover shook his head, spat. "Chancellery man. Didn't burn nothing, didn't take nothing. Just—made an example. Said the word *correction* like he was talking about weather." He gathered his reins, eager to be away from the subject. "You two best watch yourselves on the low road. Word travels faster than carts."

He rolled off eastward, and the dust settled, and the evening got very quiet.

Mirabel did not speak. She walked to the roadside and sat on a milestone—an actual milestone, its carved face worn nearly smooth—and put her head in her hands.

*Aldous Wren.* She hadn't even known his name until now. She'd overpaid his wife for a bed, eaten his bread, admired his orchard, and walked out of his life at dawn, and twelve hours later a man in a grey coat had opened his throat with a rope because of what she'd carried away in her chest. Because of the song. Because the reeve had refused to give up the one thing she'd already taken.

"He covered for us," Tam said quietly. He'd come to stand beside her, and she couldn't look at him. "He guessed what we were and gave us a night's start anyway. That's not your fault, Mira. That's his courage. There's a difference."

"Is there?" Her voice came out flat and strange. "Because it doesn't feel different from where I'm sitting. He's dead because a stranger sang his grandmother's verse back to him and he chose kindness over caution. If we hadn't come—if I'd never heard the song—" She stopped. Breathed. The milestone was cold through her skirts. "Every fragment we take makes us louder, Ilexa said. She didn't say it makes them *heavier*. That everyone who helps us pays for it."

Tam crouched beside her. Close enough that she could see the exhaustion under his eyes, the way his hands—always so restless, always jingling coins or turning knives—were perfectly still.

"Then stop," he said simply. "Put the book in the river. Burn the satchel. Walk away, find some village with a stupid sign and a slow harvest, and be no one again. You were good at it. You could be good at it again." His voice was gentle, and underneath the gentleness ran something else, something almost pleading, and she thought: *he wants me to quit. Why does he want me to quit?* But grief had made her dull, and she filed the thought away unexamined, next to all the other small unexamined things about Tam Vess that had begun accumulating like sediment.

"I can't," she said. "You know I can't."

"Why? Because the book pulls you? Because the prophecy says?" He shook his head. "Say it plain, Mira. Why does a woman who spent three years learning to be invisible suddenly need a kingdom?"

She looked at him then, really looked, and the answer rose in her like groundwater, undeniable and cold.

"Because I was never no one. I was just *unwritten*. There's a difference, and Aldous Wren knew it, and he died keeping a piece of my name alive without ever knowing it was mine." She stood. Her legs were steadier than she expected. "I owe him. Not guilt—guilt is cheap, guilt changes nothing. I owe him the rest of the song. All five fragments, and the name, and whatever comes after. That's the only payment that means anything now."

Tam rose slowly beside her. For a moment something worked behind his face—something large, struggling, almost breaking surface—and she braced herself for confession, for argument, for anything.

Instead he said: "East, then. The low road forks at Stonebridge. One way follows the river, the other climbs toward the old quarries." He paused, and when he spoke again his voice had gone carefully casual. "Which way does your hand say?"

Mirabel flexed her scarred fingers. The pull was stronger tonight than it had ever been—eastward, south of east, and now with a new quality to it, a *familiarity*, as though whatever waited ahead of them had begun to recognize her approach.

"Quarries," she said. "Definitely the quarries."

Something flickered across Tam's face—there and gone, fast, but she caught it this time. Not fear. Calculation. And beneath the calculation, buried deep, something that looked horribly like pity.

"Quarries it is," said Tam Vess, and turned to load the cart, and did not meet her eyes again all evening.
