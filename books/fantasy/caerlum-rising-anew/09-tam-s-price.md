## 9. Tam's Price

Tam Vess had lied to better people than Mirabel Yarrow and slept fine afterward.

That was the thought he kept reaching for, the one he'd polished smooth over years of small betrayals—smugglers lied, it was the trade's first tool, same as a pry-bar or a false bottom in a cart. He'd sold out partners before. He'd sold out a partner once to save his own skin and had not lost a single night's sleep, because the partner had been a brute named Hollis who'd have done the same to him by morning.

But lying in the dark of their camp below the Thornfell ridge, listening to Mirabel breathe three feet away with the prophecy book wrapped in oilcloth between them like a sleeping animal, Tam found the old polish didn't take. The lie sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. It had been there for months now, since long before the book, since long before he understood what she was carrying—and that was supposed to be his excuse, wasn't it? *I didn't know.* He turned it over in the dark and found it had no weight at all.

He got up quietly, checked the horse lines, checked the fire, did all the things a man does when he cannot bear to be still.

* * *

It had started, as most of his sins started, with Denny.

His brother had never been clever. That was the kindest way to put it, and Tam had spent thirty years finding kind ways to put it, because the unkind ways were all true as well. Denny was big and cheerful and believed whatever the last person told him, which made him useful muscle for smugglers and useless at anything requiring judgment. Three winters ago he'd signed himself into a debt contract with a wool merchant in Greyharbor—a "fair arrangement," the merchant had said, and Denny had smiled and pressed his thumb into the wax, and the ledger had begun to eat him.

Tam knew how those ledgers worked. Debts in Greyharbor were recorded, and what was recorded could be *amended*, and what could be amended could be grown. A day late became a penalty; a penalty became interest; interest compounded until a man who'd borrowed forty crowns owed four hundred and his children's names appeared in a collateral column he'd never read. The Chancellery of Records didn't run the debtors' ledgers directly—they farmed that out, the way they farmed everything—but they audited them, and an auditor's stamp on an account was final. No court would look twice. The record said what it said. The record was the world.

Denny had come to him last autumn with the fear finally visible on his face, holding a copy of his own page like it might bite. Four hundred and eleven crowns. Collateral: his labor, his house-share, and—"pending review"—his person.

"So I work it off," Denny said. "Right?"

Tam hadn't been able to answer. He'd seen men work off debts before. He'd seen what came out the far side: gray men with ledger-numbers where names used to be, walking when told, stopping when told, because every hour of their lives was written down somewhere and writing, once it has you, does not let go.

That was when Corvane had come through Greyharbor.

The auditor had a reputation even among smugglers, and smugglers collected reputations the way other men collected coins. Corvane of the Chancellery of Records: the man who burned parishes. Not the people—the records. He would arrive in a town, quiet as dust, request access to the archives, and burn whatever displeased him. A debtor erased from the rolls didn't die; worse, or better, depending on your view—he simply ceased to owe. His debts vanished. His name vanished. His marriage record, his birth record, his children's claim to his surname—all of it, gone, as if he'd never existed. Some men paid Corvane fortunes for exactly this. Others begged him not to do it. The ones who begged had usually watched it happen to someone else and understood, dimly, that being unwritten was its own kind of dying.

Corvane also bought information. Everyone assumed he only *took* it. But Tam had learned otherwise, the way smugglers learn things—through a man who knew a man, through a note left under a specific loose stone in a specific tavern wall. The auditor paid well for routes, cargo manifests, the movements of persons of interest. And he paid magnificently—paid in the one currency Tam needed—for amendments to the debtors' ledger.

*One name,* the arrangement had offered. *One account struck clean. In exchange: timely intelligence.*

Tam had spent two weeks telling himself no.

Then he'd gone home and found Denny sleeping in the stable because the merchant's collectors had taken his room as interim collateral, and Tam had gone to the loose stone, and that was that.

The intelligence he'd sold, in those first months, had been nothing. Caravan schedules of other outfits. A customs officer's mistress's name. He'd told himself he was selling sand—filler, noise, the harmless gristle of the trade. And then, in the spring, a job had come through Rennick the fence: guide wanted for a Thornfell crossing, moon-dark roads, good pay, discretion required. And the guide, when Tam met her, was a woman with red hair and a scar across her left hand who introduced herself with no surname at all, who moved through the passes like water finding cracks, who knew roads that Tam—who'd run the Thornfells for fifteen years—had never heard named.

And Tam, professional to the bone, had sent word of her route to the loose stone.

He hadn't known about the book then. He told himself that nightly, like a prayer with no god behind it. He hadn't known she was carrying anything but her own careful emptiness. He'd sold a route, a name-that-wasn't-a-name, a description. *Red-haired woman, calls herself Mira, scarred left hand, knows the old paths.* If Corvane wanted her, that was Corvane's business, and business was business, and Denny's page in the ledger had come back amended within the month—balance zeroed, collateral released, the whole ugly arithmetic wiped clean. Denny had wept. Their mother, if she'd still been alive, would have lit candles.

Then Mirabel had opened the sealed book by lamplight in a way-stable, and Tam had watched her face change, and later she'd shown him the page—the date, the description, the scarred hand, the thirteenth moon—and he had understood, with the slow cold slide of ice going out underfoot, exactly what he'd done.

He hadn't just sold a route. He'd sold her to the thing that was hunting the book.

* * *

Now they were camped in the shadow of the Thornfells again, two days east of the harvest village where a reeve hung dead from his own gatepost because he'd talked to them and Corvane had learned it, and Tam could not make the old polish take no matter how hard he rubbed.

They'd argued that evening—not loudly; they never argued loudly, both of them too practiced at quiet—but about whether to push over the pass at night or wait for dawn. Mirabel wanted night. She'd been wanting night more and more lately, moving like something hunted, and Tam knew why, and she didn't know that he knew, and the knowing sat in him like the stone in his chest, heavier every mile.

"You're favoring the high track," she said now, appearing beside the horse line without sound, which was another thing about her that should have warned him from the start. People who are truly nobody don't move that quietly. Nobody moves that quietly except someone practicing.

"High track's faster if the fog holds."

"Or it's the track where riders can see us against the skyline."

"It's the track where *we* can see *them*." He tightened a girth that didn't need tightening. "You want my opinion or my obedience?"

"Neither, apparently. You've been strange for days."

"I'm always strange. You noticed this late?"

She almost smiled. That was the cruelty of it—she was starting to trust him. He could see it happening, the way you can watch frost form: gradual, delicate, and irreversible. After the reeve, after the hanging, after she'd stood in the road with her fists clenched and said *this is because of us, because of what we're doing*, he'd put his hand on her shoulder and said *we'll be faster than him*—and she'd let him, and something in her had leaned a fraction of her weight onto him, and every ounce of that weight was resting on a lie.

Tell her tonight, he thought. Tell her by the fire. *I sold your route. Months ago. Before the book, before any of it. I didn't know what you carried, but that's not absolution, because I didn't ask either, and a man in my trade knows better than to not ask.*

He opened his mouth.

"Tam." Her voice had changed—flat, quick, the working voice. "Firelight. We're showing it from the ridge."

He looked up. She was right, of course. The hollow they'd chosen hid the flames from the valley road but not from the heights, and against the stars the fireglow painted a small orange wound on the black of the pass. Anyone watching from above—

Anyone like the rider he'd glimpsed that afternoon, two ridges back, wearing travel-grays cut in the Chancellery style. He hadn't mentioned it. He'd told himself it was nothing, a courier, a shepherd. He'd told himself so many things lately that the words had worn thin as old canvas.

"Kill it down to coals," he said, grateful for orders to give. "We move now. High track."

"You just said—"

"Fog's coming up from the valley. I can smell it. Twenty minutes and the skyline's blind." He was already kicking earth over the flames, and the lie inside the order—that he'd smelled anything but fear on himself—was the smallest lie he'd told in months, and somehow the one that stuck worst.

* * *

The fog came as promised, rolling up the valleys in grey rivers, and they took the high track with the horses on lead-ropes and the world reduced to ten feet of visibility and the memory of the path underfoot. Mirabel walked ahead. She had a navigator's instinct that bordered on the uncanny—she read the mountain the way Corvane read ledgers, he thought, finding the true line beneath the confusing surface—and he followed the shape of her and hated himself in a steady, low-burning way that had become almost familiar.

At the saddle below the summit cairn, she stopped so suddenly he nearly walked into her.

"Lanterns," she breathed. "Below. On the low road."

He eased forward beside her. Through a tear in the fog, far down and eastward, a string of lights moved along the valley road—six, maybe eight, spaced like disciplined men, with a lantern at the head burning white and steady. Not a shepherd's light. Not a courier's. Chancellery riding-lanterns burned a special flame, oil treated with something that made the light hard and colorless, and Tam had seen that light once before, months ago, from a distance he'd chosen carefully.

"That's not following us," Mirabel whispered. "That's ahead of us. Waiting."

"They don't know our route." Even as he said it his stomach turned over, because *they don't know our route* was the one sentence in the world he had no right to say.

"They know something." Her eyes came around to him in the dark, and he was grateful the fog ate the firelight, because he could not have survived her seeing his face just then. "Every time we think we've got a day's lead, they're a half-day back. Every time we pick a road, they've asked questions in the town behind us. Tam—I've been invisible for three years. Three years, and in five weeks this man has crossed my trail six times. That's not competence. That's information."

The wind moved the fog, and the white lanterns below swayed like drowned stars.

He could tell her now. Here, actually, was the place for it—dark, cold, honest country. *You're right. It's information. It's me. I sold them your route in the spring, for my brother's debt, and I've been feeding them enough truth to keep them close and enough misdirection to keep them off you ever since, and I don't know anymore which side of that knife I'm standing on.* He felt the confession rise in him like sickness, actual physical pressure behind the breastbone, the words lining up—

And then he thought: *if I tell her here, in this dark, with riders below and miles to cover, she will either walk away from me into the fog or freeze, and either way we may both die on this mountain, and the book dies with her, and everything dies with the book.* It was a reasonable calculation. It was the exact sort of calculation Corvane would make. He recognized that, and hated it, and made it anyway.

"Not tonight," is what he said. "Tonight we go over. Tomorrow, when there's daylight and distance, I'll tell you what I know about how he moves."

Her silhouette went very still. "What you know."

"What I know."

A long moment. Then she turned uphill. "Keep up, then."

They climbed. The fog thickened until the lanterns below were smothered, and Tam followed Mirabel's ghost-shape over the saddle and down the western scree, and all the while the stone in his chest ground smaller and sharper, because he'd had his chance, and he'd done the math instead of the right thing, and the math had worn his own voice.

* * *

They made the far valley by grey dawn, horses lathered, and put eight more miles behind them before Tam called the halt at a shrine—an old boundary marker, pre-Chancellery, a waist-high stone with carvings worn to suggestion, where the road forked toward the fen country. Mirabel watered the horses. Tam walked to the fork and stood looking down the southern branch as if consulting some inner map.

"We should go south," he said. "Through Marleford."

"Marleford's a records-town. Waystation clerks, a sub-archive. Corvane will have eyes there."

"He'll have eyes everywhere. But he'll expect us north—everyone runs north from the Thornfells, it's the smuggler's habit, open country and old friends. So he'll weight his riders north." Tam heard his own voice doing what it did best: confident, granular, built of true details arranged around a false center. "South buys us two days while he figures it out. Two days is worth a risky town."

Mirabel studied him. She had a way of looking at a man as though reading fine print, and Tam had watched her use it on fences and forgers and once, memorably, on a customs inspector who'd aged ten years under it. Now it settled on him and he held still and let it, the way you hold still when a dog decides whether you're friend or meat.

"All right," she said at last. "South through Marleford. But we skirt the archive quarter wide, and if you're wrong—"

"If I'm wrong, we're dead, and you can haunt me."

Something flickered in her face at that—something that wasn't quite a smile—and she turned to the horses, and Tam exhaled slowly and looked south down the road he'd just chosen for her.

Because here was the rest of it, the part he hadn't said even to himself in plain words until this moment: the message he'd sent to the loose stone three nights ago, before the harvest village, before the hanging. *Party moving northeast toward the Thorn passes, two riders, likely crossing at Wolf Saddle within the week.* Northeast. North. Every word true, and every word together a lie, steering Corvane's company up into the high country while they slipped south—and buying time, yes, real time, but time bought with a coin that was running out. Because Corvane was not a fool. Corvane had read Tam's reports for months, and a man who reads records for a living learns to read the gaps in them, and sooner or later—sooner, if the northern passes came up empty—he would lay all those little truths side by side and see the shape of the one large lie, and then he would not send riders after Tam Vess.

He would send something worse. Or he would come himself, and he would bring the ledger.

Tam swung up into the saddle. Ahead, Mirabel was already moving, small and straight-backed on the southern road, the oilcloth-wrapped book strapped behind her like a heart she was carrying for someone else. He'd sell himself to keep that safe now. He understood that much. Whatever the price ran—Corvane's wrath, Mirabel's contempt, the stone in his chest growing until it cracked him open—he'd pay it, and pay it gladly, and never once be forgiven.

The road bent south through wet green country, and Tam followed, and behind them, far up in the greying passes, unseen and patient, the white lanterns began to move north—exactly where he'd told them to look.

It would hold for two days. Maybe three.

Then the record would catch up with them both.
