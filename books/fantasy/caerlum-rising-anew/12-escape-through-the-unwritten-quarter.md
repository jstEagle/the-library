## 12. Escape Through the Unwritten Quarter

Tam Vess had set fire to a great many things in his life—a granary in Duskwater that turned out not to be empty, two rival smugglers' boats, a marriage certificate he'd rather not discuss—and he had a professional's respect for flame. Fire was honest. It took what it touched and asked nothing in return except everything. The Chancellery of Records, by contrast, had never burned anything so useful as a debt file. They erased. They *unmade*. A man who owed them money simply woke one morning to discover that no one remembered lending it to him, and no one remembered him either, and his chair at his mother's table stood empty with a fine grey dust settling on it like snow.

Which was why, standing in the third-floor stacks of the Grand Archive with a stolen lamp-oil flask in his pocket and his brother's life folded flat between two pages somewhere above him, Tam felt the particular nausea of a man about to fight a monster with its own teeth.

*Burn the file,* Mirabel had said, three nights ago, in the fen camp, her voice low and certain in a way that made him both proud and afraid. *Not Corvane. Not the building. The file. If they think their records are vulnerable, they evacuate everything. You don't burn down a bank—you start a rumor of smoke.*

She was right, which was becoming an uncomfortable habit.

He found the ledger on the fifth floor, in Debts Secular, Subsection Nine: Obligations Heritable. His brother Wren's name sat in a neat column of ink, third from the top, followed by the figure that had eaten six years of Wren's wages and would have eaten six more. Beside it, in the margin, a small notation in red: *collateral: person.* Not Wren's house. Not Wren's tools. Wren himself, held against the sum, transferable, sellable, deletable if defaulting.

Tam touched the page once, lightly, the way you'd touch a wound to learn how deep it went.

Then he unstoppered the flask and poured oil into the spine of the shelf, and struck his flint, and did not watch it catch. Professionals didn't watch. Watching was for amateurs and arsonists with consciences, and he'd surrendered the second title years ago.

The alarm, when it came, was not a bell. It was silence—the particular, dreadful silence of a thousand quills stopping mid-stroke, as though the whole building had drawn one breath and forgotten to release it. Then a voice from everywhere at once, dry as old vellum:

*"Containment protocol. All readers will exit in an orderly fashion. All records are safe. All records are always safe."*

*All records are always safe.* Even now, even burning, they couldn't stop lying.

* * *

Mirabel heard the silence first.

She had been in Reading Room Fourteen for six hours, wearing the grey smock of a junior clerk, her scarred hand hidden in a copyist's glove, her head bent over a stack of shipping manifests she had no earthly reason to read. Invisibility was her craft and her religion, and for six hours she had practiced it flawlessly—shoulders rounded, pen moving, face arranged into the vacant diligence of a woman whose entire inner life consisted of margins and dates.

Then the pens stopped.

She looked up. Across the reading room, forty clerks sat frozen over their desks like a tableau of industry carved in wax, all of them listening to the same absence. Somewhere below, a door slammed. Then another. Then the sound Tam had described to her in the fen camp with grim relish: the sound of the Chancellery being afraid.

*When the archive panics,* he'd said, *it evacuates. And when it evacuates, every eye in the building is on the records, not the people. That's your window. Thirty minutes, maybe less. Be at the east stairwell.*

She rose with her manifests still under her arm, because a junior clerk leaving without her work would be noticed, and walked toward the east stairwell at the pace of a woman going nowhere in particular. Around her, the building was waking into controlled terror. Auditors in dove-grey coats moved through the corridors in pairs, checking seals on doors, speaking to each other in clipped phrases that were not quite words. She passed a window and saw, far below in the courtyard, wagons being loaded with ledgers under tarpaulins while clerks formed human chains to pass books hand to hand. They were saving the paper before they saved themselves.

Of course they were, she thought. Of course.

The east stairwell was dim and smelled of cold stone. Tam was already there, flattened into an alcove, dressed in the livery of a courtyard porter, looking exactly as trustworthy as a knife left lying on a table.

"You're late," he said.

"I'm early. You're just incapable of waiting."

"Both true." He pressed a bundle into her hands: a porter's coat, heavy and shapeless. "Put this on. We're going down through the loading yard, out past the wagon queue, and then—" he grinned, and there was something wild in it tonight that she didn't wholly like— "then we're going somewhere they can't follow."

"Which is where?"

"The part of the city that doesn't exist."

They descended. Below the ground floor the stairwell continued, past a landing marked SUB-VAULTS — AUTHORIZED PERSONS ONLY, past another marked FLOOD EQUIPMENT, and then the stairs simply ended in a brick wall that had been whitewashed so many times the mortar lines had vanished. No door. No sign. Just a wall, and a single guttering lamp, and the distant thunder of the evacuation overhead.

Tam put his shoulder to the wall where the whitewash was thinnest, near the floor, and pushed. A section of brick swung inward with a groan of disused hinges, exhaling air that smelled of river mud and neglect.

"There's a tunnel?" Mirabel said. "Under the Chancellery?"

"There's a district," Tam said, holding the lamp up so its light fell into a corridor of raw stone, older than anything above it, older maybe than the city itself. "And a district needs roads. Nobody maps what nobody wants to find. Come on."

* * *

The Unwritten Quarter, as Tam called it—not aloud, never aloud, but she caught the shape of it in the way he moved through those streets—was the city remembering itself badly.

It lay along the river's southern bend, a sprawl of leaning houses built from salvaged stone, connected by alleys that bent where no alley should bend, as though the ground beneath them had been folded and never quite smoothed out. There were people here. That was the first shock. Lamplight in windows, laundry strung across gaps, a woman beating a rug on a sill three stories above a street that appeared on no map Mirabel had ever seen or forged. The people did not look at strangers. They had the practiced blindness of those who survived by being scenery.

"How does a place like this stay hidden?" Mirabel whispered. "A whole quarter. In a capital city."

"Because the Chancellery drew the map," Tam said. "And when they drew it, they drew what should be there, not what was. There was a fire, a hundred years back—officially. A quarter burned down, everyone relocated, end of entry. Only it didn't burn, or only half did, and the half that didn't got left out of every survey since. You can't be taxed from a street that isn't on the tax rolls. You can't be conscripted, counted, recorded." He glanced back at her, lamplight carving his face into planes and shadow. "You'd love it here. It's three years of your life, laid out in streets."

She wanted to argue and found she couldn't. The place felt like the inside of her own skull made solid: functional, shadowed, surviving by omission.

They cut through a yard where goats regarded them with ancient indifference, ducked under an aqueduct whose arches predated any dynasty she could name, and came out at last onto a raised embankment overlooking the harbor road. Behind them, across half a mile of rooftops, the Grand Archive stood lit like a bonfire that hadn't started yet, its towers ringed with lanterns, wagons crawling away from it in a line of torches.

"They'll count heads," Mirabel said. "At the evacuation. They always count. When I'm not among the clerks accounted for—"

"Then we're already gone, and it won't matter."

"It'll matter to Corvane."

Something crossed Tam's face when she said the name. It was small—a tightening at the jaw, a half-blink—but she had spent three years learning to read people the way other folk read weather, and she caught it the way you catch a wrong note in a song you know by heart.

It was not fear. Fear she knew on Tam; she'd seen it cross his face a dozen times, usually right before he lied to someone dangerous, which was often. This was different. This was *recognition*. The look of a man hearing the name of someone whose face he already carries.

"When did you meet him?" she asked.

Tam kept walking. "Meet who?"

"Don't do that." She stopped on the embankment path, and after two strides he stopped too, because he was many things but he was not a fool. "Corvane. When did you meet Corvane?"

"At the caravan," he said. "Chapter and verse. The auditor came, he burned the parish records, you lied to him beautifully, we left. You were there. Better memory than mine."

"That's not what I asked." She stepped closer. Down in the harbor road, a cart rattled past, and its noise filled the pause like water filling a footprint. "I've watched you around powerful men my whole time knowing you. Smugglers, magistrates, that customs officer in Marrowgate who could have hanged us both. You get careful. You get polite. You become the most boring man alive until the danger passes." Her voice dropped. "Tonight, saying his name, you flinched. Not like prey. Like a debtor."

For a long moment Tam said nothing. Above them the sky was moonless—three nights shy of full—and the dark felt like a held breath. When he finally spoke, his voice had the flat evenness of a man walking on ice he hasn't tested.

"He interviewed me. Months ago. Before the Thornfell job, before any of it. Standard audit—all licensed carriers get pulled in eventually. He asked about routes, contacts, whether I'd ever carried cargo I couldn't name." He shrugged, and the shrug was good, it was nearly perfect. "I told him what I tell them all. Nothing worth writing down."

"You told me that interview lasted an hour."

"It did."

"And he remembers your face."

"Everyone remembers my face. It's my best feature."

But he smiled as he said it, and the smile was the mistake. Because Tam Vess lied best when he was serious, and when he smiled while lying it meant the lie had cost him something, and she filed that knowledge away in the same cold room where she kept everything else she couldn't yet afford to feel.

"Come on," he said softly. "We've a boat at Graystairs, and a tide that won't wait for your suspicions."

She let it go. Not because she believed him. Because the Archive behind them was beginning to glow at its edges like a coal breathed upon, and because some questions keep better than others, and she had learned—had been taught, had been *trained*, though by whom she was only beginning to understand—to survive first and grieve later.

* * *

They reached Graystairs as the false alarm curdled into a real one.

Later, Tam would learn that a junior archivist, fleeing the smoke rumor, had tripped and knocked a genuine lamp into a genuinely dry stack of petitions, and that the fire had taken the west wing's roof before dawn. Tonight, all he knew was that the sky over the Archive turned orange while they were still in sight of it, and the bells began—not the orderly chime of evacuation but the ragged urgent clanging of a city seeing its most feared building burn—and every soldier, clerk, and auditor within a mile turned toward the light.

Except one.

Tam saw him from the top of Graystairs: a lone figure in dove-grey standing motionless in the middle of the evacuated crowd-flow, facing *away* from the fire, toward the river, toward the dark southern bend, toward them. Even at that distance, even in that light, the stillness identified him. Corvane did not look at fires. Fires were merely records changing state.

And Corvane was looking directly at Tam Vess.

Not searching. Not scanning. Looking—at him, alone in a crowd of thousands, with the calm certainty of a man confirming an appointment. And then, slowly, deliberately, he inclined his head. A small bow. The kind you give an equal. The kind you give an *asset*.

Tam's blood went to ice water.

"Tam." Mirabel's hand closed on his sleeve. "The boat. Now."

He went. He took the steps two at a time, untied the line with hands that were steadier than he deserved, and poled them out into the black current while Mirabel crouched in the bow watching the shore. The current took them. The Archive burned behind them, beautiful and terrible, raining ash on the water like grey snow, and Corvane's small figure shrank and shrank on the stairs until the dark took it too.

Only when they were well out on the river, when the burning city was a smear of orange on the horizon and the fen country's reeds rose black around them, did Mirabel finally speak.

"He bowed to you," she said quietly. "Before we ran. I saw it."

"The man was stretching," Tam said. "Auditors have bad backs. All that sitting."

"Tam."

The current carried the word away and brought it back. He shipped the pole and looked at her across the water-dark space between them, at this woman he had sold for his brother's freedom and followed into a burning archive and could not, apparently, stop following anywhere at all, and he opened his mouth to say *I sold you, I sold your route, I sold you months before I ever knew what you carried, and Corvane has been reading my reports ever since, and that bow was a receipt*—

What he said was: "Get some sleep. Long pole ahead."

She held his eyes a moment longer. In the ash-fall light her scarred hand rested on the gunwale, and the prophecy book's weight was a hard rectangle against her ribs beneath her coat, and she nodded once, slowly, the way a person nods at a door they intend to open later.

Tam turned and poled, and the reeds swallowed them, and behind them the Chancellery burned its own history, and ahead—east, always east now, pulling at them both like a hook behind the breastbone—the thirteenth moon climbed toward its fullness over a kingdom that did not exist, carrying in its belly whatever came next.
