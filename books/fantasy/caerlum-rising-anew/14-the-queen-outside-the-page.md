## 14. The Queen Outside the Page

The fragments pulled east.

Mirabel had learned their pull the way she'd learned Odara's moods—by ache rather than instruction. The millstone fragment rode wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of her pack, and when they camped, it lay against her spine like a second heart, beating slightly out of time with her own. The reapers' verse lived in her mouth now; she caught herself humming it while walking and stopped, unsettled by how much of Caerlum she was becoming without ever having seen it. The third fragment—the page from the Scribe's journal, torn free in the archive's burning dark—she kept sewn into her coat lining, over her left breast, where she could feel its faint warmth like a coal that refused to die.

Three fragments. Three pieces of a name that had been cut apart a century before either of them was born.

And all three of them, this morning, leaned eastward so insistently that Mirabel's scarred hand had gone numb from wrist to fingertip, as if the blood in it were being drawn toward something.

"You're limping," Tam said behind her.

"I'm favoring my left side because my hand is trying to leave my body." She flexed the fingers. The scars across the knuckles—old ones, honest ones, from a life before she'd learned to be no one—had begun to glow a pale silver in the dawn light, like veins of ore in grey stone. "Is that normal?"

"Nothing about you is normal. That was established the night you stole a book out of my cargo."

"I didn't steal it. I *liberated* it preemptively."

They were following an old drover's road along the shoulder of the Thornfells, where the mountains softened into hills and the hills into moorland that no map bothered to insult with detail. The land grew strange the way deep country grows strange: not dramatically, but in accumulations. A bridge with no river under it. A field of wheat that ripened in every direction except one, where the stalks bent away from a low hollow in the earth as if from a draft of cold air—or a presence.

It was there, at the lip of that hollow, that the fragments went quiet.

Not silent. *Quiet*—the way a room goes quiet when someone is already listening at the door. The millstone stopped its heartbeat against her spine. The verse died on her tongue mid-hum. And the page over her heart warmed so suddenly that Mirabel gasped and pressed her palm flat against her coat.

"What is it?" Tam's hand went to his knife, which was Tam's answer to most questions.

"She's here," Mirabel said, before she understood she'd said it. Then, testing the certainty: "*Sorrel* is here."

"The queen who traded a kingdom for immortality and let a century of deletion happen on her watch? Good. I have questions, and I've been told my questioning technique leaves marks."

"Tam." Mirabel looked at him. He was thinner than he'd been three months ago, harder at the edges, with new grey coming in early at his temples that she suspected had nothing to do with age. He had burned his brother's debt file in a Chancellery archive and pulled her out through streets that officially did not exist, and he had never once explained how he'd known Corvane's face well enough to walk past it in the dark. She had chosen him anyway—at the fen camp, at dawn, with her eyes open—and choosing him daily was becoming a discipline, like prayer or knife-sharpening. "Whatever we find down there. Whatever she says. We listen first. We decide after."

"That's what worries me about you. You always want to *understand* people who are trying to kill us."

"No one's tried to kill us today."

"It's not even noon."

The hollow opened below them like a cupped hand. It was not a natural valley—that much Mirabel knew immediately, with the same instinct that told her which roads no map admitted existed. The land here had been *persuaded*, long ago, to forget itself. The slopes curved too smoothly, the grass grew too evenly green, and around the rim ran a ring of standing stones whose carvings had been worn deliberately smooth—not by weather, but by hands.

At the bottom, in soil so black it looked warm, grew a garden.

Mirabel descended the slope slowly, Tam a half-step behind and to her left, the way he always positioned himself when he expected trouble. The garden resolved as they came closer into rows of plants she could not name, and Mirabel had spent three years learning names—the false ones and the true ones both. There were flowers with petals like folded letters. There was a shrub whose leaves bore fine printed veins, legible if she tilted her head, though the words dissolved whenever she tried to read them directly. There was a climbing vine heavy with fruit shaped like small sealed books, and a bed of what might have been wheat except each stalk ended in a tiny quill instead of a seed head.

None of these things existed. She was certain of that the way she was certain of north. These plants belonged to no taxonomy, appeared in no herbal, answered to no name in any living language. They grew here anyway, lush and deliberate, tended by—

A woman knelt among the letter-petaled flowers with dirt to her elbows.

She did not look up. She wore a plain grey dress, practical, mud-hemmed, the kind of garment worn by a hundred thousand farmwives across a hundred thousand valleys, and her hair—a red so deep it was nearly bronze—was bound back with a strip of cloth. Her hands moved among the plants with the unhurried competence of someone who has done this work for a very long time, longer than work should take.

"Mind the eastern bed," the woman said, without turning. "The inkworts bruise easily, and I've only the one crop."

Mirabel stopped. The fragments against her body had gone still in a way that felt less like quiet and more like *attention*—three pieces of a broken name holding their breath.

"You know who we are," Mirabel said.

"I know who *you* are." The woman sat back on her heels and turned, and Mirabel's certainty about everything she had carried for months—every righteous anger, every clean conclusion—tilted sideways like a cart on bad road.

Because Queen Sorrel of Caerlum was young.

Not preserved-young, not uncannily unlined the way painted portraits stay young. She was young the way Mirabel was young: mid-twenties perhaps, freckled across the nose, with a smudge of dirt on one cheekbone and eyes the color of strong tea. If they had met at a market, Mirabel would have taken her for a gardener, a herbalist's daughter, a woman who worked with her hands and slept well. There was no crown. There was no coldness. There was only a tired, wary kindness in her face, and beneath it something that Mirabel recognized with a jolt because she saw it in her own reflection sometimes:

The look of someone who has been alone for so long that company feels like a language going rusty.

"A century," Mirabel said. "You erased your kingdom a hundred years ago. You should be dust."

"And yet." Sorrel rose, brushing off her knees. Up close she smelled of loam and something sweeter underneath, like pages kept in a dry place. Her gaze moved to Mirabel's left hand—to the glowing scars—and something in her expression cracked, very slightly, the way ice cracks before it gives. "You have her hand. Did you know that? The Scribe wrote about it. *The heir will carry the mark on the left hand, and the mark will light when the door is near.* I used to think he was being poetic. He was never poetic. It was the thing I liked least about him."

"You knew Odara?" Tam asked sharply.

"I knew Aldous." Sorrel's mouth did something complicated. "Odara was the name he took when exile got comfortable. He was my scribe for eleven years. He held the pen when I—" She stopped. Started again. "He held the pen."

"The pen that unwrote Caerlum," Mirabel said. It was not quite a question.

"The pen that saved it." Sorrel walked past them, back toward a small stone cottage at the valley's far end, and spoke over her shoulder as if continuing a conversation she'd been having alone for decades. "You'll want tea. Everyone wants tea when they arrive angry. It's the arriving angry that tells me you've read the prophecy book. He always did write to provoke."

* * *

The cottage interior was one room, and it was the strangest room Mirabel had ever stood in, because it was utterly ordinary. A hearth with a kettle. Two chairs. A bed with a patchwork quilt. Shelves of jars labeled in a neat, patient hand. On the wall hung a single framed embroidery: a map, stitched in faded thread, of a kingdom whose borders Mirabel now knew better than her own heartbeat.

Caerlum. Stitched by someone who loved it, stitch by stitch, over years.

Sorrel made tea and handed them chipped cups and sat across from them with her own, and for a moment nobody spoke, and the silence was so domestic it was obscene. A hundred years of erasure. An empire's conquest averted by the unmaking of a nation. A smuggler and a thief drinking nettle tea with the woman responsible, while outside her window plants that did not exist turned their lettered leaves toward the sun.

"Tell me what you think happened," Sorrel said at last.

"We know what happened," Tam said. "We found the empire's invasion plans. Preserved in amber memory, in your keeping. Caerlum was going to burn. Every city, every soul. So you made a bargain—you traded the kingdom's recorded existence for your own life outside the page. The Chancellery grew from the seed you planted. Corvane deletes people. Villages lose their parish records and their dead stop counting. And you—" his voice roughened— "you gardened."

"Yes," Sorrel said simply. "That is what happened. Would you like to see?"

She led them out behind the cottage, to a hollow in the garden's eastern wall where a single tree grew—leafless, silver-barked, wrong in some way Mirabel couldn't name until she realized its branches cast shadows that fell in directions the branches themselves did not point. Suspended among the bare limbs, like fruit, hung a sphere of amber light.

"Amber memory," Sorrel said. "One of seven. This one holds the night of the bargain." She reached up and touched it, and the sphere bloomed open like a flower of light.

Inside the light, images moved. Mirabel saw them as though standing in the memory itself: a war-room lit by storm-light, maps weighted with daggers, and men in imperial uniform moving small carved armies across the shape of a kingdom she recognized. A general's voice, flat as ledger-arithmetic: *"Total annexation is inefficient. Burn it. Salt it. Let the histories record that Caerlum was never worth recording."* And then Sorrel—younger, crowned, furious, terrified—standing in a tower room with a book open before her and a scribe's hand trembling at her shoulder, and her voice, steady in the way voices are steady when they have passed through terror and come out the other side:

*"Then let there be nothing to burn. Let them conquer a country that isn't there."*

The amber closed. Sorrel stood beneath the leafless tree with her hands at her sides, waiting.

"So you see," she said quietly. "I did not trade Caerlum for my life. I traded its *record* for its *people*. The Chancellery's founding act was mine—but understand what it was for. An empire writes history to justify what it does. So I made sure there was no history to justify anything. No borders to redraw. No archives to burn, because there were no archives. My people scattered with songs they couldn't explain and lullabies full of nonsense syllables, and the empire marched into an empty valley and declared victory over geography. Every person in Caerlum lived. That was the bargain. That was always the bargain."

"And the price," Mirabel said. Her voice sounded very far away to herself.

"The price was me." Sorrel smiled, and it was the saddest thing Mirabel had seen since Odara's funeral. "The bargain required a keeper. Someone anchored outside the page, tending what remained—tending *this*." She gestured at the impossible garden. "Every plant here is a piece of Caerlum that couldn't survive the erasure. Songs. Names. Boundary-stones. They fall out of the world's memory, and they fall here, and I keep them alive. I am the compost heap of a nation, Mirabel Yarrow. It is not a heroic job, but someone had to do it, and the alternative was watching my people burn in histories written by the men who lit the fires."

Tam set down his cup. His face had gone carefully blank—the blankness Mirabel now knew meant he was feeling something too large to show. "Then why fight us? Why send Corvane?"

"Send—" For the first time, Sorrel looked genuinely bewildered, and the bewilderment frightened Mirabel more than any mask could have. "Child, I haven't sent anyone anywhere in a hundred years. I can't leave this valley. The bargain binds me to it. I don't command the Chancellery—it grew from my act like rot from a wound, and it serves whoever holds its ledgers now. Corvane is not mine. Corvane is what happens when a tool outlives the hand that made it and learns to love the cutting."

Mirabel's mind was rearranging itself violently, certainties collapsing like wet paper. The villain. She had built her whole flight around a villain, a single face to hate, and the face kept refusing the role. "Then help us end it," she said. "Help us restore Caerlum. Speak the name with us. You owe it that much—"

"No." It came out fast, and sharp, and afraid.

"Sorrel—"

"*No.*" She set down her cup with a hand that was not quite steady. "You don't understand what restoration means. The bargain wasn't just erasure. It was a substitution. As long as Caerlum stays unwritten, the debt stays paid—the empire's claim stays void, my people's survival stays purchased. But if you speak the full name aloud, the original contract comes due. All of it. At once." She looked at Mirabel, and her tea-colored eyes were wet and fierce and ancient despite the young face. "The name cannot return unless the keeper returns to the page. Do you understand? To write Caerlum back into the world, something must be written *in*. The ink demands a body. Mine."

The wind moved through the garden of nonexistent plants, and the letter-petals shivered, and somewhere in the silver tree the amber memories hummed like struck glass.

"You'd die," Tam said flatly. "Restoration kills you."

"Restoration *completes* me," Sorrel corrected softly. "There's a difference, and I've had a century to learn it. I have watched every person I loved scatter into diaspora and forget why they sing what they sing. I have tended the scraps of my country in dirt for a hundred years like a widow tending a grave. And you come down my hill asking me to help you undo the one thing that keeps all of it—" her voice finally broke, just barely— "the one thing that keeps all of it *paid for*."

Silence. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Mirabel became aware that she was gripping her scarred hand with her right, hard enough to hurt, and that the silver light in the old scars had not dimmed since they entered the valley—if anything it burned brighter here, at the center of everything, closer than she had ever been to the door.

"There's another way," Mirabel said. She heard her own voice and was surprised by how calm it was. "There's always another way. The prophecy doesn't say the keeper dies. It says the thirteenth moon shall find the door. It doesn't say who opens it."

"The prophecy doesn't say," Sorrel agreed, "because Aldous hoped. Hope makes poor documentation." She crossed the room and stood before Mirabel, close enough that Mirabel could see the fine lines at the corners of her eyes that a century should have deepened into ravines and hadn't—could see, beneath them, the terrible patience of the long-alone. "But I'll tell you what the prophecy does say, girl, and what I've known since before your grandmother drew breath. The door opens from the inside. And the door needs a hand on *both* sides."

Her gaze dropped, deliberate and slow, to Mirabel's left hand. To the scars, glowing like a signature in silver fire.

"You carry her mark," Sorrel whispered. "The Scribe's mark. Which means when the time comes, the door may accept more than one kind of payment. It may accept—" she swallowed— "*an exchange.*"

The word landed in the room like a dropped knife.

"An exchange," Tam repeated. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning," Sorrel said, and turned away to tend the kettle as if the question were a small thing, as if her hands were not shaking, "that if the heir stands inside the door when the moon finds it, the page might take her instead of me."

Outside, beyond the ring of worn stones, the sun climbed toward noon, and eastward—far eastward, over hills no map admitted existed—the thirteenth moon waited, already rounding toward full.
