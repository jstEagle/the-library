## 15. Sorrel's Version of the Truth

Tam Vess had spent his whole life learning what people looked like when they were about to lie. His mother had lied with her chin up and her hands still. The debt-collectors in Saltmarsh had lied with a smile that arrived half a beat too early. Corvane lied the way a ledger balanced—flawlessly, without a single visible seam. And Mirabel, God keep her, lied like breathing: small lies, necessary ones, *I'm nobody, I want nothing, I'm just passing through*—lies she'd worn so long they had stopped being lies at all and become skin.

Queen Sorrel did not lie.

That was the first thing Tam noticed, sitting on a root that was probably older than every kingdom on the map, watching an immortal woman pour tea from a pot shaped like a sleeping fox. She told the truth the way other people told it too—openly, generously, with her whole face. Her eyes were the color of honey held up to firelight, and they did not flicker, and her hands were steady, and everything she said had the ring of a coin struck true.

*Which is exactly why you should be worried,* he told himself, turning the cup she'd given him without drinking. *Because you've never once met a liar who couldn't tell a true story.*

The garden around them was wrong in ways his eyes kept trying to correct. Flowers with petals like folded letters. A vine whose leaves turned, slowly, to follow not the sun but Mirabel as she walked the rows. Trees bearing fruit he had no name for, because there was no name for them anywhere outside this hollow valley—and inside it, apparently, names were optional. The air smelled of rain that hadn't fallen yet.

"You have questions," Sorrel said. It wasn't a question.

"Always," Tam said. "It's cheaper than answers."

Mirabel came back down the row, brushing soil from her scarred hand, and sat cross-legged on the grass with the prophecy book open across her knees. "You said you'd show us why," she said. "Why you did it. Why Caerlum."

"I said I would show you." Sorrel set down the teapot. "There is a difference between telling and showing, scribe's heir. Your ancestor taught me that. He was always after me to *write things down*, as if ink made a thing more real." Something moved through her face then—not quite pain, not quite contempt. Grief wearing its oldest clothes. "Ink makes a thing editable."

She rose, and the garden seemed to lean aside for her, and Tam felt the hair rise on his arms. Not magic, exactly. More like the valley itself remembered being ruled.

"Come," she said. "I'll show you the last night of your kingdom."

* * *

She led them to the far end of the hollow, where the hillside opened into a shallow cave—or not a cave; Tam corrected himself as they stepped in; the walls were too smooth, too deliberate, ribbed like the inside of a shell. And there, set into a basin of stone at the center, was the amber.

He'd seen amber before. Smugglers used it sometimes—a lump of it, sea-gold, with a fly caught mid-struggle inside, sold to merchants' wives as a curiosity. This was nothing like that. This was a block the size of a coffin lid, and inside it was not an insect but a *night*.

A city under stars. Walls he didn't know, towers with banners he didn't know, streets lit with lanterns—and beyond the walls, filling the horizon edge to edge, fires. Hundreds of them. Thousands. An army's encampment stretching past the curve of the world, and above it, hanging in the smoke, the shapes of siege engines tall as churches.

"That's—" Mirabel started.

"Caerlum," Sorrel said. "The night before the end. I stood on my own wall and looked out at that, and I knew we could not win. The Verrecan Empire had crossed the mountains with sixty thousand men and a decree: no city that resists would be left standing. They had already done it twice. You will not find those cities either, if you go looking. The Chancellery did not invent erasure." Her voice stayed level, warm even. "They inherited it. From me."

Tam leaned closer to the amber. Inside it, tiny as a painted miniature, he could see figures moving along the walls—soldiers, a woman in white at the rampart's center. He could see the fires reflected in the river below the city. It was not a painting. It was not a memory told in words. It was a night, kept.

"How?" Mirabel asked quietly.

"The bargain lets me hold what was. Not change it. Hold it." Sorrel touched the amber's surface, and the scene shifted—the view sweeping down off the walls, into the war room beneath the gatehouse, where maps lay on a table and men argued in voices too small to hear. "But I can show you what they never let you see. Here. The empire's own plans. Captured from their forward camp by my scouts three nights before."

The scene inside the amber changed again. Now it was a tent, lamplit, and a table spread with documents, and Tam found himself bending close despite himself, reading the elegant Verrecan script upside down through forty layers of golden time. *Order of Operations, Reduction of Caerlum.* Reduction. A word for arithmetic applied to a city of ninety thousand souls.

He read fast—he'd always read fast, a smuggler's skill, contracts and manifests and other men's ledgers—and what he read turned his stomach cold and quiet.

*Population to be dispersed. Structures to be fired. Archives of the rebellious province to be seized and destroyed entire. No record of the name Caerlum to be suffered to remain in any chronicle, map, or registry within imperial territory. Let it be as though the place had never been, that no grievance may attach to ground which holds no history.*

"They weren't going to conquer us," Sorrel said softly. "They were going to *delete* us. Every song, every stone, every gravestone. Ninety thousand people, and then a hundred years later, no one would even know to mourn them. Their children would grow up on land with no past, singing no songs, and the Empire would call that peace." She turned, and the firelight of the trapped night played gold across her face. "So yes, Mirabel Yarrow. Yes, Tam Vess, who is wondering whether to believe me—I traded our recorded existence away. I went to powers older than empires and I bought the one thing their decree could not take: I made sure there would be nothing left to burn. You cannot delete a country that has already been unwritten. You cannot grieve a name no one remembers. I took the wound into myself so the Empire's knife would close on air."

The silence afterward was enormous. Even the garden sounds outside—the impossible birds, the whispering vines—seemed to hold off, listening.

Tam understood, in that moment, why Mirabel's face had gone soft. He understood the pull of it. He'd spent years hating the ledgers that owned him, the files that decided who he was, the Chancellery stamp that could turn a man into a debt and a debt into ash. And here was a queen who had beaten all of it—beaten the pen itself—by stepping outside the page. It was a beautiful story. It was told beautifully.

And that was precisely when he started looking for the seam.

* * *

He circled the amber slowly, the way he'd learned to walk a warehouse before signing for cargo. Not because he doubted the sight of it. Because he'd once watched a man sell stolen brandy in honest barrels with honest seals, and the seals had been genuine, and the brandy underneath had been water and turpentine. The best cons are built entirely out of true things.

"Show it again," he said. "The war room. Slowly."

Sorrel raised an eyebrow. Mirabel shot him a look that said *what are you doing*, but he kept his voice easy, apologetic, a smuggler's shrug made audible. "No offense meant, Majesty. It's just—if I'm going to carry this story, I'd like to know it the way I know a road. Every turn."

Something flickered behind the queen's honey-colored eyes. Just a flicker. But Tam had spent three years reading Corvane's face across a campfire, hunting the half-beat delay, and he knew what a flicker cost a person.

"Of course," Sorrel said, and touched the amber again.

The tent bloomed back into being. Documents on the table. Lamplight. Tam walked his eyes across it the way he'd walk a ledger line by line, and there—there, at the edge, where the light fell off—

The table's corner. In the first showing, a courier's satchel had rested on it, buckled shut, stamped with the Verrecan double-eagle. He'd barely registered it. In this second showing, the corner was bare. Clean wood.

He said nothing. He made himself breathe. He watched the rest: the order of operations, the decree, the beautiful damning language about letting it be as though the place had never been—all of it present, all of it true, he'd stake his life each word was real. But the satchel was gone. And when Sorrel swept the view toward the tent flap, in the first version there had been a guard standing there holding a dispatch case; now the flap hung empty on empty shadow.

Small things. Things a grieving viewer would never check. Things only a man who checked *everything*—because checking everything was how he'd stayed alive and out of ledgers his whole crooked life—would ever catch.

"Again," he said. "Please."

"Tam." Mirabel's voice had gone flat and careful, the voice she used when she thought someone was about to embarrass himself in front of a buyer. "What is it?"

"One more pass, Majesty. Indulge me. I'm slow."

Sorrel's smile did not waver. That was almost the worst part—how well she wore it. She touched the amber a third time, and the tent returned, and this time Tam counted. Eleven documents on the table in the first viewing. Eleven in the second. Nine now. Two gone, and in their places, blank paper of the same size, same cream color, laid in the same neat stacks—as if the memory had been *re-typed*, carefully, the way a clerk falsifies a manifest by replacing pages rather than removing them, so the weight stays right.

His mouth had gone dry. He looked at Mirabel, and she was watching him now properly, her head tilted, catching the thing in his posture the way she always caught things—she'd survived three years as nobody by noticing everything. He gave her the smallest shake of his head: *not here, not yet.*

"So," Sorrel said, drawing her hand back. The amber dimmed to a warm sleepiness, the doomed city folding itself away like laundry. "Now you've seen it. The choice I was given. Burn entire and be remembered as ash—or step out of the page, unrecorded, and live. Any mother would have done the same. Any king. Your Scribe called it monstrous, and he left, and he wrote his book, and I have missed him every year of forever." Her voice frayed, just slightly, on the last word, and Tam believed that part completely. "Ask me anything. I have nothing left to hide."

*Nothing left to hide.* The phrase landed in Tam's ear like a coin he knew was clipped.

Because that was the thing—the thing he'd turn over all night, the thing that sat in his chest like swallowed ballast. She wasn't lying. Every word she'd spoken was true; he'd have staked the boat, the crew, his brother's freedom on it. The invasion was real. The decree was real. The horror was real. Sorrel had stood on her wall and faced annihilation, and she had chosen the only door left open, and it had cost her everything, and she had been alone with that cost for a century.

And she had edited it.

Not much. Not enough to notice, unless you were a man who counted satchels. A document removed here, a case gone there—details that complicated the story. Details that might have shown negotiations, alternatives, a faction at court urging surrender, something. The memory was curated the way the Chancellery curated a parish register: not by inventing, but by *selecting*. Leaving in what served. Lifting out what didn't. The Chancellery hadn't learned deletion from ancient powers or imperial decrees.

They'd learned it from her. She was the first editor. The founding act of the Chancellery of Records had merely scaled up what Queen Sorrel had already done alone in a valley with a block of amber: decide what the truth should contain, and make the truth match.

Even victims, Tam thought, watching her gentle, truthful, grief-lined face as she offered Mirabel tea again—even victims corrupt when they're the only ones holding the pen. Maybe especially then. A hundred years of being the sole keeper of your own story—who could resist tidying it? Who could resist deciding, night after night alone, that the story was cleaner without the satchel, simpler without the guard, truer without the two missing pages? She probably no longer knew she'd done it. That was the deepest cut of it: she likely believed, absolutely, in the memory she showed them, because she had edited it so long ago and so gently that the edit had become the truth.

Just like Mirabel's small lies had become her skin.

*We're all of us curators,* he thought. *Her. Corvane. Me.* And the thought had teeth, because it pointed inward, at the secret he was carrying like contraband in a false-bottomed crate—the route he'd sold, the ledger debt he'd bought his brother out of, the confession lodged sideways in his throat where it had been stuck for weeks. *You've edited yourself too, Vess. You've been showing Mirabel a memory with the worst pages lifted out, and telling yourself it's kindness.*

The tea steamed. Mirabel was asking something—about the Scribe, about the bargain's terms, her voice careful and hungry both—but Tam barely heard it. He was watching Sorrel's hands as she poured. Steady. Graceful. The hands of a woman who had never once been caught, because there had never been anyone left to check her work.

Until now.

"Well?" Sorrel said suddenly, and her honey eyes came up and fixed on Tam directly for the first time since the amber dimmed. The warmth in them hadn't changed. The steadiness hadn't changed. But there was a new attention there, sharp as a customs seal, and Tam felt it land between his shoulder blades like a hand. "You've been circling my memory like a man checking a cargo manifest, Master Vess. Three times through. You count everything, don't you? Your file says so."

His blood went cold. "My file?"

"The Chancellery keeps files on everyone worth keeping files on." She smiled—that same true, open, terrible smile. "Corvane sent riders through this valley eleven days ago. He knows where you are, both of you. He knows what you carry. And he knows, Mirabel Yarrow, exactly which fragments you've gathered—because the millstone wight talks, and songs travel faster than horses." She set down the teapot with a soft click. "So before we speak another word of doors and names and thirteenth moons, I think your smuggler should tell you what else he counted in my amber. And then perhaps you should both tell me why you think I brought you here at all."

The garden outside had gone silent. Every impossible bird at once.

Tam looked at Mirabel, and Mirabel looked at him, and the confession he'd been swallowing for weeks rose all the way to his teeth—and beside it, new and worse, rose the other thing he'd counted: the two missing pages, the vanished satchel, the edited dead. One secret was his to give. The other was hers.

And the queen was waiting, patient as amber, to see which of them broke first.
