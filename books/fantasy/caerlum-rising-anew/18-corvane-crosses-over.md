## 18. Corvane Crosses Over

Auditor-First Corvane had crossed the Thornfell passes forty-one times in his career, and he had never once needed a map. Maps were for people who trusted their eyes. He carried instead a ledger—leather-bound, brass-clasped, updated hourly—and the ledger told him where he was by telling him what was owed. A road existed to the precise degree that someone had been taxed for walking it. A village existed in proportion to its tithe rolls. This was not, as the sentimental liked to sneer, mere bureaucracy. It was ontology, disciplined. The world, properly kept, was an account book that balanced.

The pass ahead of him did not balance.

He reined in at the boundary stone and felt it the way a man feels a missing tooth with his tongue: an absence with edges. The stone itself was unremarkable—grey granite, lichen-crusted—except that the ledger in his saddlebag had no entry for it. Worse: it had an *anti-entry*, a line struck through in red so old the ink had gone brown, and beneath the strike a single marginal notation in a hand Corvane did not recognize and which should not have existed, because every hand in the Chancellery's founding generation was catalogued, indexed, and filed.

*Let this stand unrecorded,* the note said. And then, smaller, almost apologetic: *It will record you back.*

Corvane closed the ledger. Behind him, thirty auditors sat their horses in file, grey-cloaked, silent, ledger-chains clinking with the small music of authority. He had chosen them carefully. Not one of them had family worth mentioning. Not one of them owned anything he could not replace. Deletion work required men who understood, in their bones, that identity was a clerical convenience.

"First Warden Vell," he said.

A rider detached from the column. Vell was twenty-six years into the service and had personally unwritten four hundred and eleven individuals—a modest number; Vell was thorough rather than ambitious—and his face had settled long ago into the particular blankness of a page prepared for writing. "Auditor-First."

"What do your instruments say about this pass?"

Vell consulted the slate-and-stylus apparatus strapped to his wrist, the one that measured the density of recorded fact in a given location. He consulted it twice. His brow, which never moved, moved.

"The needle," he said, "is spinning."

"It would be." Corvane dismounted. His boots found the frozen mud of the pass and he stood a moment before the boundary stone, feeling—for the first time in thirty years of service—something adjacent to fear, though he would have called it professional caution, because Corvane had edited fear out of his own file decades ago. That was the secret none of his subordinates knew: he had begun with himself. Before he ever deleted another soul, he had opened his own record and struck through the grief, the doubt, the childhood terror of the dark. What remained was efficient. What remained was *him*, or near enough.

But standing before this stone, he felt the struck lines stirring. Like scars itching before weather.

"The Yarrow woman is inside," he said. "She has gathered fragments of a name we spent a century burying. By tomorrow night's moon she may speak it." He turned to face his company. "Understand what we do here. We are not burning a library. We are not hanging a debtor. We are going to delete a country at its source—the echo of it, the residue, the place where it still remembers itself. When we are finished, there will be nothing left for prophecy books to describe, nothing left for songs to smuggle, nothing left for thieves to steal." He let his gaze travel down the column. "And there will be no more thirteenth moons. Ever."

Thirty faces looked back at him with the smooth obedience of well-kept records.

He touched the stone.

* * *

The crossing was not like passing through a door. It was like being *read aloud*.

Corvane stepped through and the world turned briefly into language—he felt himself described, itemized, pronounced—and then he was standing in a road under a grey sky, in a country that smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and something older underneath, something like the inside of a book left shut too long.

The road was empty. The hills rolled away green and impossible, because these hills had been erased from every survey in the empire, and yet here they stood, wearing their grass like a garment kept in mothballs for a hundred years. In the distance, a town: rooftops, a bell tower, smoke rising from chimneys. As they rode closer, Corvane saw that the smoke rose and did not dissipate, hung in the air like ink dropped in water and refusing to spread.

The town was inhabited and uninhabiting. Figures moved in doorways—mid-gesture, mid-sentence, mid-life. A woman shook out a tablecloth that fell forever without landing. Two children played a game whose rules had been forgotten even by the game. An old man stood at a well with his mouth open around a word he had been speaking since before Corvane's grandfather was born.

"Auditors," Corvane said quietly, "observe protocol. Nothing here is real. It is residue. We do not speak to residue. We locate the source of the echo—the boundary stone's interior anchor, likely the old palace or the chronicle-house—and we apply the Redaction Protocol at the root. Thirty of us, working in relay, can unwind this entire country before moonrise."

They dismounted and worked. And it went beautifully, for eleven minutes.

Vell's team reached the marketplace first and began the procedure: name the thing to be deleted, cite the authority, strike the line. Vell stood before a fountain that had run dry for a century and read out its catalogue designation from the confiscated survey of 1143, and the fountain listened, and the fountain began, gratefully, to forget itself—stone paling, basin thinning to outline—

Then the fountain remembered Vell.

That was the only way Corvane could later describe what he saw. The water-less water arched upward and did not fall, and in its hanging curtain appeared reflections, and the reflections were of Vell—not as he stood, but as he was *kept*: every deletion he had performed, all four hundred and eleven, each one a person looking out of the water at him with their actual faces, the ones the Chancellery files reduced to inventory numbers. A miller. A girl who had forged her father's signature to flee a marriage. A tax clerk who had simply known too much about a rival's accounts.

Vell screamed. Corvane had never heard Vell scream. Vell's file contained no capacity for screaming; Corvane had seen to that personally during the younger auditor's induction, striking through *tremor of voice* and *excess of imagination* with the same red pen.

The fountain spoke with a hundred mouths. "You struck us," it said. "Now we will read you."

And Vell began to be edited.

Corvane watched, and made himself watch, because a leader must. It happened the way a candle gutters: Vell's edges went first. The grey cloak lost its buttons, then its seams, then its grey. His hands blurred at the fingers. His face—his careful, blanked, professionally emptied face—suddenly flooded with everything that had ever been struck from it, all at once, every deleted fear and grief and love surfacing like drowned bodies finally given leave to rise, and the sheer *volume* of it broke him. A man cannot contain four hundred and eleven murders plus his own original self arriving simultaneously. Vell came apart into sentences. Then into words. Then into a smell of ink.

Where he had stood, a page lay fluttering on the cobbles. On it, in Vell's own handwriting, was a single line:

*I remember my mother's name.*

Then the page burned with cold fire and was gone.

* * *

"Fall back," Corvane ordered, and his voice, thank the ledgers, did not shake. "Form the square. No one works alone."

They formed the square in the market square of a town that did not exist, thirty auditors back to back, pens drawn, citation-books open, and Corvane took stock. Twenty-nine. Vell was not recoverable; Vell was not even *absent* in the usual way. Absence could be annotated.

"Auditor-First." Second Warden Hask approached, and Hask—who had once deleted a plague village of two thousand souls over a weekend and slept soundly after—Hask's stylus was trembling. "The citations aren't holding. I named the bell tower, cited chapter and verse, and the bell tower—" He swallowed. "The bell tower rang. Auditor-First, there is no bell in that tower. There hasn't been a bell in a hundred years. It rang anyway, and the sound knew my name."

"It knows all our names," Corvane said. "We wrote them ourselves, into our own files. Every fact we have certified about ourselves—that is what it reads. That is how it finds the seams." He looked up at the bell tower, where a bell that did not exist swung in a windless sky, tolling notes that landed in the chest rather than the ear. "This kingdom was erased imperfectly. Queen Sorrel's bargain removed it from the record but not from *remembering*. Everything here survives as memory, and memory is not orderly. Memory keeps what we discard."

"Then we delete the memories first," Hask said. "Burn the houses. There's nothing real to burn, but the gesture—"

"No." Corvane said it flatly, and then, because honesty had become tactically necessary, added: "Burning is a form of emphasis. Fire makes things memorable. That is why we stopped burning records three centuries ago and started striking them instead. Here—" he gestured at the hanging smoke, the unfinished tablecloth, the wordless old man at the well— "*everything* is already emphasized. This whole country is a margin note that refused to die."

He walked the square while his auditors held formation, and he thought, and thinking led him somewhere he had not planned to go, down a corridor in his own mind he had sealed and struck-through years ago.

Because here was the truth he had never spoken aloud, the truth that lived in the redacted margins of Corvane's own file: he had not joined the Chancellery to bring order. Order was the recruiting poster. He had joined because when he was nine years old, in a debtor's tenement in Lowmarsh, soldiers had come for his father, and his father's name had gone out of every book, and within a season nobody in the street could say what the man had looked like—not his mother, not his neighbors, not Corvane himself, though Corvane had *loved* him—and the pain of that erasure had been so total that young Corvane had made the only bargain available to a child: if he could not keep what was written, he would become the one who held the pen. Custody instead of grief. If everything remembered passed through his hands, then nothing could be taken from him again without his own signature on the deed.

Order was the lie he told the empire. What he wanted—what he had always wanted, what he had climbed thirty years of ledgers to reach—was to be the last reader. The sole custodian. When the final audit was done, when every record in the world had been either confirmed or struck, one copy would remain, and it would be in his keeping, and then no one—not emperors, not queens outside pages, not prophecy-mongering thieves with scarred hands—could take a single thing from him ever again.

Caerlum, restored, would be a country that remembered itself. Unacceptable. Caerlum, deleted *at the source*, with the deletion witnessed only by him—

That was the prize. That was what waited past the fear.

"Auditor-First!" The shout came from the east end of the square. Teller-Rank Ostrey, youngest of the company, was standing very still beside a shopfront, and the shopfront's window glass—which showed nothing but grey sky—was showing Ostrey something else. Corvane arrived in time to see it: the window displaying, with terrible clarity, a small room, a small boy, a father's hands teaching small letters by lamplight. Ostrey's room. Ostrey's father. Ostrey had been six when the debt-collectors' fire took his family, and he had entered the Chancellery orphanage and asked, at nine, the question every orphan there asked: *can you make it so it didn't happen?*

And the recruiters had said yes. They always said yes. They said: we cannot restore what is lost, but we can ensure loss is *orderly*. We can make meaning of it. Come hold the pen.

Ostrey was reaching toward the window with both hands.

"Step away," Corvane said.

"She's in there," Ostrey whispered. His face was doing things faces were not trained to do. "My mother. She's in the glass. The kingdom has her. It kept her. It kept everyone—we *struck* them but it *kept* them—" His voice rose, cracked, became a child's voice. "Auditor-First, we've been deleting people who go *here*. They don't stop. They come here. They're all still—"

The window took him gently.

There was no other word for it. The glass rippled like struck water and Ostrey leaned forward into it the way a tired man leans into a doorway, and the shopfront accepted him, and the lamplit room in the glass brightened, and a woman's silhouette turned from a stove, and Ostrey—Teller-Rank Ostrey, deletion number 9,204 in his own certification log—stepped out of his uniform and into his mother's arms, and the window went dark, and on the cobblestones lay his ledger-chain and his citation-book and a single page that read, in a child's careful hand:

*I'm home.*

Twenty-eight auditors stood in the square of a dead kingdom, and not one of them moved, and Corvane understood with perfect clarity that he had perhaps four minutes before the same understanding reached each of them: that the Chancellery had never deleted anyone. It had only ever *filed them here*. That every strike of the red pen was a deportation to a country the Chancellery pretended did not exist. That the philosophy they served was not order but amnesia, and amnesia, faced with its own memory, devours itself.

"Hask," Corvane said. "Read them Article One. Now."

Hask lifted the citation-book with shaking hands and recited the founding text, the words every auditor memorized at induction: *What is not recorded cannot rebel. What cannot rebel need not be feared. Peace is the silence between entries.*

And the town laughed.

Not cruelly. That was the worst of it. The laughter came from the doorways, the windows, the unfinished gestures—a hundred voices, warm and rueful, the laugh of people who had heard a joke a hundred years ago and only now gotten to deliver the punchline. And in the laughing, Corvane heard words braided through it, words aimed with great precision:

*Peace is the silence between entries. And what, little clerk, do you suppose fills the silence?*

The bell tower rang again. Somewhere eastward, faint through the grey air, came the sound of the palace district—or what had been the palace district—and with it a pressure, a pull, like a tide going out. The kingdom was gathering itself. Tomorrow night the moon would rise full for the thirteenth time since the sealing, and Mirabel Yarrow would stand somewhere in those echoing halls with fragments of a true name in her scarred hand.

Corvane drew his red pen. Around him, his auditors breathed in the cold unrecorded air, and he could hear, in their breathing, the beginning of mutiny or the beginning of prayer—he honestly could not tell which, and for the first time in thirty years, Auditor-First Corvane did not know which side of the ledger he himself was standing on.

"We proceed to the palace," he said. "Single file. Touch nothing. Answer nothing."

Behind them, in the darkened shopfront window, very softly, someone began to sing a lullaby in a language the Chancellery had struck from every tongue.

And every auditor in the company, without choosing to, remembered the tune.
