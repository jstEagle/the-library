## 21. What Remains Written

Tam Vess had smuggled brandy, salt, gunpowder, and once—for six terrible weeks—a live snow leopard with opinions about his driving. He had crossed borders at night that existed only on the maps of men who'd paid him not to look too closely. He considered himself a man who knew what cargo was worth.

He had never carried anything like this: a kingdom in a cart.

The road down out of Thornfell was still deciding what it was. Where it passed through old Caerlumite territory, it widened and straightened as though remembering a century of cart traffic, and then, where it crossed into land that had never stopped being anyone's, it narrowed again into a sheep track, embarrassed by its own ambition. Tam drove slowly. The mules kept shying from nothing—or rather, from the edges of things, the places where hedgerows grew in two directions at once and a field of barley ended mid-row in raw grey grass that no farmer had planted because no farmer remembered planting it.

Behind the cart, walking, came the returners.

They straggled along the verge in twos and threes, carrying what they owned on their backs or in handcarts: a woman in a fishwife's apron with three children hanging off her like burrs; an old man in a soldier's coat fifty years out of fashion; a pair of brothers who hadn't spoken to each other since some quarrel in a country whose name Tam couldn't pronounce, now arguing cheerfully about which of them would claim their grandmother's orchard, neither knowing where the orchard was. They had all woken one morning—three weeks ago now, the morning Mirabel spoke the name—with a homesickness they couldn't account for, packed up whatever was nearest, and started walking east.

None of them could say why. That was the thing Tam couldn't get used to. He'd asked the fishwife, somewhere back near the pass, just making conversation to keep his hands steady on the reins.

*Why here? Why Caerlum?*

She'd looked at him as if he were simple. *Because my gran's gran sang us the lullaby,* she said. *About the river with nine names. You don't forget a song like that.*

The lullaby. Everyone coming down out of the passes sang it, or fragments of it, or hummed it while they worked. *Nine names the river keeps, and none for sleeping*—nonsense syllables to most of them, filler between verses. Except now the syllables meant something. Now there was a river running through the middle of a half-built town, and if you stood on its bank at dawn you could hear, very faintly under the water-sound, the other eight names turning over in their sleep like old men remembering their youth.

The children didn't know any of this. The children just sang.

Tam pulled the cart around the last bend and there it was: Caerlum Town, capital of a country eleven weeks old, looking like a camp meeting a city halfway and both losing. The walls had come back first—the stones had simply been where the stones had always been, waiting—and so the town had a fine grey ring of ancient masonry enclosing mostly tents, timber frames, mud streets laid out by committee, and one stone bridge over the nine-named river that everyone agreed was beautiful and no one had built. Smoke rose from cookfires in ragged columns. Somewhere a hammer rang. Somewhere else, someone was singing the lullaby badly, and a dozen voices corrected him without thinking about it.

Mirabel was on the bridge.

She was easy to find in any crowd these days, which was its own strange miracle. Three years of practice at being nobody, undone by a red head that caught every light and a way of standing now—square, planted, watching—that made strangers step around her instinctively. She wore the same coat she'd worn through the whole mad season, patched at the elbow where Corvane's auditor's knife had found it. Her left hand, scarred, rested on the bridge rail over water that remembered being sacred.

Tam brought the cart to a halt and climbed down, working the stiffness out of his back. Eleven weeks, and his body still hadn't accepted that the quest was over. Some mornings he woke reaching for a sword that he'd never actually carried, in a fen camp that had dissolved like sugar in rain.

"You're late," Mirabel said, without turning around.

"Road argued with me." He came to stand beside her at the rail. Below, the river ran green-grey and quick, and a boy fished from the far bank with a willow wand and no bait, catching nothing and perfectly happy. "Cargo's delivered. Flour, nails, lamp oil, and forty pounds of salt. Also," he added, digging in his coat, "a letter from Ilexa."

"Ilexa can't write letters. She drinks them."

"She dictated. To a very patient nun." He handed it over. Mirabel broke the wax—Ilexa's seal was a lopsided compass rose she'd carved herself—and read, and Tam watched her mouth twitch through whatever the old cartographer had said, ending in something that made her snort.

"Well?"

"She says the new surveys are going well. She says the eastern border is 'arguing' but she'll win. She says—" Mirabel folded it away, and the smile went somewhere private. "She says thank you for getting her drunk enough to tell the truth for the first time in thirty years. She says don't do it again."

"A fair bargain."

Below them, the fishwife's children ran past onto the bridge, chasing a dog, singing. *Nine names the river keeps.* One of them stumbled on the fourth name—it was a hard one, full of consonants like stones knocking together—and an old woman passing behind them corrected the child absently, the way you'd correct grammar, without stopping, without knowing how she knew.

Mirabel watched that happen with an expression Tam had learned to read carefully over a hard autumn. It wasn't quite happiness. It was happiness with weight behind it, like a coin heavy enough to be worth more than its face.

"They don't know," she said quietly. "Eleven weeks, and they're building a whole country on a song they think is nonsense."

"Better foundation than most," Tam said. "Most countries are built on less than a song. Most are built on a lie somebody won."

That earned him a real look, wry and tired. "When did you become a philosopher?"

"When the alternative was being honest about how scared I am of you."

It came out lighter than he meant it, and heavier. Mirabel turned back to the river. The water made its small endless argument with the bridge pilings.

"I'm not scary," she said finally.

"No," Tam agreed. "That's exactly what's frightening about you."

* * *

The Council—if five people in a tent could be called a council, and they insisted it be called that precisely because calling it a Parliament felt like pretending—met in the afternoon in the largest structure in town, a half-finished hall whose roof was canvas and whose floor was packed earth that had been, according to the oldest returners, the floor of the Queen's Justice Court, and before that a temple, and before that a market, because Caerlumites had apparently never met a building they didn't want to argue in.

Tam sat at the back, as was becoming his unofficial station. He'd tried leaving twice in the first month and had come back within a day both times, and by the third time he'd stopped pretending the leaving was real. There was work for him. Roads, mostly—roads and carts and the movement of goods, which was smuggling wearing a clean shirt. The new country needed everything moved from everywhere, and half of everything didn't officially exist yet in any ledger anywhere, and Tam Vess was the only man in living memory who could navigate a border that had spent a century refusing to hold still.

At the front, Mirabel stood before the five councilors and the crowd of perhaps two hundred who'd crammed in to listen, and read aloud from a paper she held in her scarred left hand.

"—and therefore, the office of Keeper of Records is established, with the following duties: to keep true and complete the record of Caerlum's laws, debts, births, deaths, boundaries, and agreements; to make that record available to any citizen who asks; and to destroy nothing."

A murmur went through the hall. *Destroy nothing.* In a world where the Chancellery had burned parish records and unmade debtors and edited a queen's memory like a rough draft, those two words landed like a vow of war.

"The Keeper holds no army," Mirabel went on. "No power of arrest. No authority to punish. If the Keeper lies, or hides, or edits, the office dissolves and the records pass to—" she paused, and Tam saw her jaw set, "—to whoever the people choose, by whatever method they agree on, even if the method is shouting."

More murmurs. A woman near the front—a weaver named Brannoch who'd appointed herself the voice of the tent-dwellers' quarter through sheer volume—called out, "And who watches the Keeper?"

"Everyone," Mirabel said. "That's the point."

"And who *is* the Keeper?" someone else shouted, and there it was, the question the whole hall had come to hear answered. Two hundred faces turned toward the woman on the platform, and Tam saw her take one breath, the kind of breath you take before cold water.

"I am," she said. "If you'll have me."

Silence, of the loud kind. Then Brannoch again, arms crossed: "You erased yourself for three years. We've heard the stories. A woman with no name in any ledger in four countries. Why should we hand our records to someone who's *good* at disappearing?"

Tam held his breath. He'd heard Mirabel answer versions of this question a dozen times in eleven weeks—to Ilexa, to returning families, once at three in the morning to the dark, practicing. But this was the first time she answered it in public, standing in a roofless court where a queen had once traded a nation for survival.

"Because I know exactly what a record is worth," Mirabel said. "I spent three years proving you could live without one. It nearly killed me, and worse—it nearly made me small enough to fit inside the Chancellery's idea of safety. A record is how a person resists being deleted. A record is how a country comes back from nowhere." Her voice didn't rise; it dropped, and the hall leaned in to hear. "I'm not offering you my skill at vanishing. I'm offering you the opposite. Everything I keep, you can see. Everything I write, you can check. And the day I break trust—" she lifted the paper, "—this document says you take it from me. I wrote that part first."

Brannoch stared at her a long moment. Then the weaver shrugged, elaborately, theatrically, for the benefit of the hall. "Rules written by the person they bind," she announced, "with a clause for her own removal. I've lived under worse kings. I say yes."

And the hall—two hundred tired, muddy, hopeful people who had walked out of other people's countries because a lullaby told them to—roared its agreement, and Tam, at the back, found his eyes stinging and blamed it on the canvas roof leaking October light into them.

* * *

The swearing-in happened at dusk, on the boundary stone at the top of Thornfell's highest pass—the stone that had been a door, that had opened onto a grey echo of a kingdom, that now stood as just a stone again except when you touched it, when you could feel, faintly, like warmth held in brick, the memory of everything that had passed through it.

Just the three of them. Mirabel, holding the oath written in her own hand. Tam, because she'd asked him, in the specific words: *I need a witness who knows what I look like when I lie.*

And Ilexa Quill, who had ridden three days from the fens, sober for the occasion and visibly suffering from it, to draw the ceremony—because, she said, a thing that isn't drawn might as well not have happened, and she'd spent thirty years drawing things that officially hadn't, and she was done with it.

"Repeat after me," Mirabel said, facing the stone, the wind pulling at her red hair. "I, Mirabel Yarrow—"

"You're saying your own name in your own oath," Ilexa observed. "Bold."

"It's mine. I'm keeping it."

"I, Mirabel Yarrow," Tam echoed when prompted—he was witness, not oather, but she'd insisted the words be shared, that the oath belong to more than one throat—"keeper of the record of Caerlum, swear to keep it true, to keep it open, to destroy nothing, to hide nothing, and to surrender the office the moment I fail."

The wind came down off the mountain and moved through the pass, and Tam would swear until his dying day that it sounded, briefly, like a great many voices saying *yes*—the recorded-out dead, maybe, the presences they'd walked among inside the echo, finally with somewhere to be. Or maybe just wind. He'd stopped needing to know which.

Then it was done, and Ilexa sketched them fast against the fading light—Mirabel with her paper, Tam with his hands in his pockets, the boundary stone between them and the whole visible sweep of a country putting itself back together below—and declared herself satisfied, and asked, hopefully, whether anyone had thought to bring anything drinkable.

* * *

Later, much later, after Ilexa had been installed by the fire in the Keeper's new quarters—a single stone room above what had been a guardhouse, containing one desk, one chair, two shelves, and a lock that Mirabel had insisted on installing facing outward, so the door could always be seen opening—Tam found Mirabel on the steps, wrapped in a blanket, looking east toward the river.

He sat down beside her. Neither of them spoke for a while. This was, by now, an established language between them: the silence that wasn't avoidance, the sitting that wasn't a promise. Since the marsh at dawn—since she'd walked away from him and come back and said, *not forgiveness, witness*—they had been careful with each other in a way that was either cowardice or craft, and Tam honestly didn't know which, and had decided he could live not knowing.

"Corvane's file on me burned with the Chancellery," Mirabel said at last. "Three years of notes on how invisible I was. Gone."

"That bother you?"

"It should. Someone spent years studying me, and I'll never read what he found." She turned her scarred hand over, examining it in the firelight from the window above. "But no. Let it burn. I'm done being written by other people." A pause. "Mostly."

"Mostly?"

"There's still you." She said it evenly, watching the dark. "You're writing me all the time, Tam. Every day. Whatever you decide I am to you—that gets written into me whether I consent or not. That's the last ledger I don't control."

Tam looked at his hands. Big hands, rope-scarred, good with knots and locks and lying. Bad at this. Always bad at this.

"I sold your route," he said. Again. For the third time since the confession, because some things needed saying until they were true instead of just admitted. "To Corvane. Before I knew what you carried. I bought my brother out of a debtors' ledger with the price, and I'd do parts of it differently, but I did it, and you know it, and you stayed anyway, and I have never understood why."

"Because I watched you misdirect him at real risk to buy back what you'd sold," Mirabel said. "I watched you burn your brother's file and lose him his safe life to pull me out of that archive. Loyalty chosen with eyes open, Tam. That was the whole lesson. Sorrel loved Caerlum and lied to it. Corvane served order and ate it. You betrayed me once, told me the truth about it, and have been trying to earn it back ever since, badly, visibly, without guarantees." She finally turned and looked at him, firelight and starlight mixing on her face. "There's no version of anyone worth trusting that comes without that history. Anyone who looks clean has just had their record edited."

The words hung there. Tam felt something in his chest unlock that had been locked since a fen camp in autumn—not forgiveness exactly, not yet, maybe not ever in the simple form he wanted—but a door, at least, unlatched.

"My brother wrote," he managed. "From the free cities. He's apprenticed to a chandler. He says—" Tam had to stop, clear his throat, start over. "He says he doesn't remember why we always sang that lullaby either. But he sings it to his daughter now."

Mirabel smiled, slow and real. "Good," she said softly. "Keep it in songs. Keep it in stones and songs and stubborn people. That's how it survives. That's how it came back." She looked east again, at the dark shape of the town, the scatter of fires, the river running nine-names-deep through the middle of everything. "That's how it'll survive me, someday, when I'm dust in the record. Not the office. The song."

Above them, through the window with its outward-facing lock, the shelves waited—empty tonight, but not for long. Tomorrow the first crates would arrive: recovered fragments, returned diaries, the rebuilt archive of a nation that had learned exactly what forgetting cost. Tomorrow Mirabel Yarrow, Keeper of Records, would sit at her desk and begin the chronicle of Caerlum anew.

Tonight she leaned, very slightly, until her shoulder touched Tam's shoulder, and let it stay there.

Neither of them called it anything.

Down in the town, a child began the lullaby, and other voices took it up, verse by verse, name by name, until the whole valley was humming it—the river keeping its ninth name secret even now, saving something for tomorrow, for the page not yet written, for the keeper who would write it true.
