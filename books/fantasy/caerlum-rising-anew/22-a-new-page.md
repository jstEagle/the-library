## 22. A New Page

The ink was still wet when the wind came through the open window, and Mirabel put her palm flat over the page—not to smudge it, but the way one steadies a bird that has just landed, uncertain whether it means to stay.

One year. She had been Keeper of Records for one year and eleven days, and the office she occupied had been, until recently, a linen closet. That was not a complaint. She had insisted on it. When the restored kingdom's council had offered her the old Chancellery hall—vaulted ceilings, marble floors, a hundred desks where clerks had once unmade lives with a stroke of the pen—she had laughed in the councilor's face, and then, when she saw he was not joking, laughed harder, until the laugh turned into something else and Tam had walked her out into the courtyard and stood beside her in the cold until she could breathe again.

So: a linen closet, with the shelves cleared and a window cut into the eastern wall, because Mirabel Yarrow had written the rules of her own office and the first rule was that every record-keeper must be able to see the sky while they worked. The second rule was that no entry could be amended after signing. The third rule was that the door stayed unlocked. Tam had argued for a fourth rule—something about locks, about guards, about the world being what it was—but she had told him that the Chancellery had begun with locks too, with reasonable men locking reasonable doors, and she would not build the same house on the same foundation and simply paint it a kinder color.

The page beneath her hand was the first page of the new chronicle of Caerlum. Not a restoration of the old records—the old records were gone, burned or unwritten or scattered into songs, and Mirabel had come to believe that this was not entirely a tragedy. A kingdom that remembered only its own glory would grow cruel the way old men grow cruel, repeating their best stories until the stories hollowed out. What Caerlum needed was not its past restored but its present recorded, honestly, from the first day forward.

She lifted her palm. The ink had dried.

She read the entry once more, the way she read everything now—twice, aloud in her head, checking it the way Odara had taught her to check a tincture, not for poison but for carelessness.

*In the first year of the Restoration, on the third day of the barley moon, the mill at Alderbrook reopened. The miller is a woman named Sef who came back from the coast with nothing but her grandmother's name and a song she did not know the meaning of. The song was the one. She sang it while she worked, and the millstone—our millstone, the one with half the seal—turned for the first time in a century without complaint. Sef asked me why everyone in the village cried when she sang the fourth verse. I told her the truth: because they had been singing it to their children for a hundred years without knowing why, and now they knew.*

*This is the whole of the entry. There was no battle. There was no omen. A mill opened. A woman sang. We wrote it down.*

*Recorded by Mirabel, Keeper of Records. Witnessed by Tam Vess.*

She signed it the way she signed everything now: *Mirabel Yarrow.* The surname had come back to her slowly, like feeling returning to a frostbitten hand. For three years she had been no one—*the guide, the woman, hey you*—and for the first months after the Restoration she had signed only *Mirabel*, the way a convalescent takes small steps. But a chronicle signed by a woman with no family name was just another kind of erasure, and she had promised herself, in the linen closet, on the first day, that this book would contain no erasures at all. Not even hers. Especially not hers.

Yarrow. A weed. A flower that grew in ruins and road margins and places too poor for gardens, and healed wounds when crushed into a poultice. Her mother had told her that once, before—before everything—and Mirabel had spent her invisible years ashamed of it, and now she wrote it at the bottom of the kingdom's first honest page and found that it fit her hand like a glove broken in long ago.

* * *

She left the chronicle open on the desk to dry—rule five: never close a book on wet ink, let the words breathe—and took the prophecy book down from its shelf.

It sat between a seed ledger and a child's primer of letters, because Mirabel had decided that the book that had started everything should live among ordinary books, shelved where a clerk could reach it without ceremony. Its cover was cracked leather gone soft as old bread. The Scribe's handwriting filled it, page after page of a future written before it happened, and in the margins, in two other hands—Odara's quick slant, and beneath that, faint as breath on glass, the older script that Mirabel had come to recognize as Queen Sorrel's—the book argued with itself across a century. *No,* Sorrel had written beside one prophecy. *It will not come to that.* And Odara, decades later: *It came to that.* And Sorrel again, in ink that had somehow not faded: *Then I was wrong, and I am sorry, and I am still here, and I do not know how to stop being here.*

The last page of prophecy was the one Mirabel had read a hundred times since the thirteenth moon. It described the speaking of the name—she had stopped flinching at it around midwinter—and then, beneath the final line, blankness. Not the blankness of a page waiting. The blankness of a page finished, the way a field lies finished after harvest.

She had thought, in the beginning, that she would burn the book. There had been a night—she remembered it clearly, the frost on the linen closet window, Tam asleep in the chair he pretended he didn't sleep in—when she had stood in the yard with the book in her hands and a lantern at her feet and every reason in the world to feed it to the flames. A book that wrote people before they lived. A book that had made her life feel like a line in someone else's ledger. Corvane had wanted custody of what was remembered; the book was custody of what was *going* to be remembered, which was worse.

She had not burned it. She had stood there until the lantern guttered, and then she had understood—not in words, but the way you understand a road under your feet—that the book had never written her. It had *watched* her. There was a difference, and the difference was everything, and it had taken her a year and a kingdom to learn it. The Scribe had seen her crossing the Thornfell under a full moon and had written it down the way she now wrote down mills and songs and the names of millers: because it was true, and because it deserved to be kept. The Chancellery wrote to own. The Scribe had written to witness. She was the Keeper of Records because she had finally learned which kind of writing she wanted to do.

But she was not going to keep the book in the office anymore either.

Today was the day. She had known it for a week, the way you know a date written before your birth—she smiled at that, at herself, at the woman she had been—and she wrapped the book in oilcloth and put it in her satchel and told Tam where she was going.

"I'll come," he said, from the doorway of the office, where he was leaning in the way he leaned on everything, as if the world were a wall he was holding up out of courtesy.

"You don't have to."

"I know." He pushed off the doorframe. "I'm coming anyway."

That was Tam. That had always been Tam, from the Thornfell passes to the fen camp where she had walked into the marsh alone and he had sat at its edge all night, not following, not leaving, a lamp lit at the boundary of her grief like a boundary stone. She had chosen him with her eyes open and had never once regretted it, though she had regretted plenty of other things, and she suspected that was what love actually was: not the absence of regret but the presence of one thing you would not trade for any of it.

* * *

The road to Odara's grave went east, out of the city, through country that was still learning its own name.

That was the strangest thing about the Restoration, the thing Mirabel had never managed to put into the chronicle without sounding like a madwoman: the land had come back *remembering*. When she spoke Caerlum's name on the pass, the kingdom had not appeared so much as *resurfaced*—roads rising out of the ground like bones out of a riverbank, walls where walls had been, orchards already in blossom because the trees had never stopped being trees, only stopped being *recorded*, and the difference, she now knew, was smaller than anyone had guessed and larger than anyone could bear.

The villages had filled slowly. Diaspora folk came back in ones and twos at first—old people who had always hummed a lullaby in a language they couldn't name, young people who had inherited boundary stones they thought were just rocks. They arrived with no memory of Caerlum, because there was nothing to remember; it had been taken before their grandparents were born. But they arrived *oriented*. They came up the eastern road and turned left at the millstone without being told, and stood in the ruins of houses that had never been their houses and said, in puzzled voices, *my room would have been the one with the window*, and been right. Mirabel recorded every arrival. She had a page for each. She was building, entry by entry, the thing the Chancellery had destroyed: not a history, which is a story a country tells about itself, but a *record*, which is just the truth, kept faithfully, without a story attached yet. The story would come. Stories always come. But they would grow out of the record like a plant out of seed, instead of being imposed on it like a stamp on wax.

The hawthorn was blooming along the road when they passed it—Tam walking beside her, the satchel with the book between them, carried by turns without either of them discussing the arrangement. A year ago this road had been a sheep track that no map admitted existed. Now it was on every map. Mirabel had checked. She had gone to the new cartographer's office in the spring—Ilexa Quill, sober eleven months and insufferable about it, drawing Caerlum's roads with the fierce joy of a woman avenging a personal insult—and found the Thornfell passes rendered in loving detail, every switchback, every spring, the boundary stone at the summit marked with a small star and a note in Ilexa's hand: *the door, now a door, formerly a door, always a door.*

"You're smiling," Tam said.

"Ilexa's maps."

"Ah." He nodded gravely. "The margin notes?"

"The margin notes."

They walked on. The day was mild, the barley high and green in the fields, and somewhere behind them a reaper had started up the work song—the one with the forgotten verse that was no longer forgotten, that Mirabel had carried out of a harvest village at the cost of a good man's life, a guilt she had stopped trying to set down and had instead, on Odara's advice given long ago in another life, *carried properly*, the way you carry a heavy thing you have decided is yours. The reeve's name was in the chronicle. Brannoc, reeve of Harrow Field, who withheld nothing and was hanged for it. Every year on the anniversary she went to Harrow Field and read his entry aloud in the square, and every year the villagers stood with their hats off, and every year it did not feel like enough, and she had finally accepted that it was not supposed to feel like enough. Enough was a Chancellery word. Nothing she did would ever be *enough*. It would only ever be what she did, recorded honestly, signed.

The cottage appeared around the midafternoon bend, and Mirabel stopped, as she always stopped, at the point where the path left the road.

Odara's cottage had been a ruin for years before the Restoration—burned in one of the Chancellery's quiet purges, the ones that never made any record because the Chancellery did not record its deletions, it simply performed them. But the kingdom's return had touched even here. The stone walls stood to shoulder height now, steady, as if the land had decided that ruins within its borders would at least be *honest* ruins, and roses—roses that did not exist in any herbal, flowers the color of late sun—had climbed the eastern wall and bloomed without soil's permission. Sorrel's garden plants, escaped from the hollow valley, or perhaps the valley's flowers had always been Caerlum's, waiting. Mirabel had stopped trying to sort out which impossible things belonged to which impossible source. The kingdom was one country. Its miracles shared a root.

The grave was behind the cottage, under the largest rose, marked with a stone Ilexa had cut: *ODARA. Hedge-witch. Teacher. Keeper of a hard secret, kept as well as she could.*

*As well as she could.* Mirabel had chosen those words herself, after a long winter of anger. Because the betrayal had been real—Odara had known what Mirabel was, had known about the book, had hidden a letter in a wall and died before she could find the courage to hand her apprentice her inheritance face to face. Mirabel had raged about it. She had raged in the marsh, in the hollow valley, in the linen closet on nights when the ink froze in the well. And then, slowly, she had begun to understand the shape of what Odara had actually done: she had kept Mirabel *small and safe* for as long as safety was possible, and she had left the letter where only a woman clever enough and desperate enough would ever find it. It was not courage Odara had lacked. It was the ability to look at the child she had raised and hand her a burden. Mirabel, who now signed her name to a kingdom's truth every day and knew exactly what each signature cost, had finally—somewhere around the barley moon, in this same yard—forgiven her.

"You go on," Tam said. "I'll see to the roses. They've gone wild again."

"They're supposed to go wild. They're Sorrel's."

"Everything's Sorrel's." He was already kneeling at the wall, sleeves rolled, hands in the tangle of canes, and he said the dead queen's name without bitterness now, which Mirabel noted and did not comment on. Tam had his own reckoning with Sorrel—the curated memory, the first editor, the woman who had taught the Chancellery its cruelest trick without meaning to—and he kept it the way he kept everything, behind the eyes, surfacing only in the occasional flat remark that Mirabel had learned to hear as a whole conversation.

She knelt at the grave and unwrapped the book.

"I brought it home," she said aloud. Her voice sounded strange in the open air; she spent her days in rooms, reading, writing, speaking carefully. Out here the words went where they liked. "I know you'd have said to burn it. You'd have said a book that tells the future makes liars of everyone who lives inside it." She turned the cracked leather cover in her hands. "But I've been thinking about what you actually taught me, and it wasn't *burn the dangerous things*. It was *know what you're holding*. So I'm holding it. And I'm leaving it here, under your rose, open to the last page, and the last page is blank, and I've decided that's not an ending. It's an invitation. Whoever finds it can write in it. That's the rule now. That's rule six."

The wind moved through the roses, and it sounded—she would record it honestly, she would write *it sounded*, not *it was*—it sounded like a dry laugh, the particular laugh Odara had made when Mirabel had been clever and knew it.

"I forgive you," she said. "I should have said it before. I'm saying it now, and I'm putting it in the record, because you taught me that anything worth saying is worth writing down." She paused. "And I'm angry at you still, a little, on bad days. That's in the record too. Rule two. No amending."

She opened the book to the final blank page and laid it on the grave, on the turned earth where the rose's roots drank, and weighted it with the boundary stone's small twin that Ilexa had given her—a stone that had once marked the edge of an unwritten country and now marked the corner of a written one. The pages stirred in the wind and settled. The blank page lay open to the sky, to the rain, to the roses, to whatever hand came next.

She knelt a while longer. She did not pray—she had never learned how, and the recorded-out dead did not seem to require it—but she told Odara about the mill at Alderbrook, and about the child born in the city in the spring who had been named Sorrel by parents who said they liked the sound of it, and about the lullaby, which was now taught in the village school with its meaning written on the slate beside it, so that no child would ever again sing a country's name without knowing they were singing.

When she rose, her knees ached, and she was thirty-one years old and felt every one of them, and it was the best she had ever felt in her life.

* * *

They walked home in the long gold light, and the road was on the map.

Mirabel thought about that the whole way. *The road was on the map.* Three years ago she had made her living knowing roads that no map admitted existed—trading on the gaps, on the silences, on the places where the record had simply failed to look. Her whole self had been a gap like that. She had been a road that existed only to those willing to walk her in the dark.

Now the map was honest, and so was she, and the road home was drawn in Ilexa's sure hand from the city gates to the linen-closet office with its eastern window, and anyone at all could find her. That was the cost of the Restoration, the cost she had paid with her eyes open on the thirteenth moon: she was *findable*. Corvane was dust and the Chancellery was a collapsed archive being catalogued by volunteers, but there would be others—there were always others, people who wanted custody of what was remembered—and now they knew exactly where the Keeper of Records sat, at her desk, by her window, with her unlocked door.

She found that she did not mind.

That was the thing she could never quite explain to the council, or to Tam, or to herself on the bad nights: that visibility was not the wound she had always assumed it was. She had spent three years believing that to be seen was to be written by others—by ledgers, by lists, by men like Corvane with their annotated files. But she had stood in Caerlum's echo among the recorded-out dead, and she had learned the other truth, the one the Scribe had died holding: that to be *unseen* was to be written by no one, and a person written by no one is not free. A person written by no one is a blank page in a closed book.

She had chosen. She had spoken the name. She was bound to this country until she died, anchored to it like a rose to a wall, and every map admitted her road, and her name was in her own hand at the bottom of a hundred honest pages, and when she closed her eyes she could still hear the Scribe's voice saying the only thing that had ever needed saying: *someone must remember, and it might as well be someone who knows what remembering costs.*

"Entry for today?" Tam asked, as the city walls came into view, lit up like the inside of a lantern.

"Mill at Alderbrook. Sef's song. The child named Sorrel." She glanced at him. "And Odara. The book, the blank page, rule six. All of it."

"All of it," he agreed. "I'll witness."

They came through the gates as the lamps were lit, two figures on a road that existed, in a kingdom that existed, with a name that was spoken aloud every day by children who did not know what it had cost, and Mirabel Yarrow climbed the stairs to her office, and sat at her desk by the eastern window, and dipped her pen, and wrote.
