## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

There were three rules in the Cartographers' Guild of Caerlum, and Wendell Nakamura had spent thirty-one years breaking none of them.

Rule the first: never map what you have not measured. Rule the second: never measure what you have not been commissioned to measure. Rule the third — the one inked into her oath at thirteen, when her hands were still too small to hold a ruling pen properly — *never map yourself.*

She had never found the third rule difficult. You could not draw what no one looked at long enough to see. Half-bloods made excellent cartographers for exactly this reason: people talked over you, around you, occasionally directly through you, and none of it required your presence. Wendell had spent her whole life as the margin note in other people's stories — the quiet one with the ink-stained cuffs who knew where every border, vault, and body was buried in the guild's ledgers — and she had come, mostly, to prefer it that way.

*Mostly* was doing a great deal of work in that sentence, but she was a professional, and professionals did not audit their own feelings after midnight.

It was well past midnight now, and she was in the map-vault three levels beneath the guildhall, correcting an error in a surveyor's chart of the Thornmere salt flats that had been wrong for forty years because the surveyor, Master Odo, had refused on principle to believe that salt could move. Salt moved. Everything moved. That was rather the point of maps: you drew the world still so that you could bear its motion.

The lamp above her desk burned low and honey-colored. The vault around her breathed its usual smell of vellum, cedar, and cold stone. Somewhere above, the night watch's boots creaked across the floorboards in their slow patrol.

Wendell dipped her pen, touched it to the flat's western edge —

— and felt the first ward snap.

She would struggle, later, to describe the sensation to anyone who hadn't spent their life listening to borders. It was not a sound. It was the absence of a sound she had never realized she'd been hearing all along, like a river going quiet outside your window, or a heart skipping a beat you only notice once it's missed. A thread that had run through the whole world, taut since before her grandmother's grandmother, went suddenly, horribly slack.

Her pen dragged a long ink scar across the salt flats. Master Odo would have called it an improvement.

"Right," she said aloud, to the empty vault, because speaking made things ordinary and ordinary was safe. "That was nothing. That was the wind. Wind does not, in fact, snap wards, but we're going to pretend."

The second thread snapped before she finished the sentence.

This time she stood. Her chair scraped back against stone, absurdly loud in the hush. She pressed her palm flat against the vault wall — old habit; the guildhall had been warded since the founding, and a warded wall hummed faintly against skin, a vibration so constant that most members forgot it entirely, the way you forget your own pulse.

The wall was silent.

Not cool-silent, not stone-silent. Silent the way a struck bell is silent a half-second after the ringing stops, still holding the shape of its own voice. Something had passed through it. Something had *unmade* it.

Wendell Nakamura, who had once sat perfectly still for six hours while two guildmasters screamed at each other about tidal charts, discovered that her hands were shaking.

"No," she told them firmly. "Absolutely not. We are a grown woman with a commission deadline."

Then the stars began to go out.

She saw it through the vault's high slit window, the one that faced north over the sleeping city, the one she wasn't technically supposed to be able to see the sky through from her desk but had angled her chair toward anyway for eleven years. Caerlum's winter sky was usually thick with stars — the guild called the northern constellation the Ledger-Keeper, nine bright stars arranged, if you squinted, like columns of figures — and Wendell had checked it every night of her working life the way other people checked the weather.

The Ledger-Keeper's brightest star winked out. Not dimmed. Not clouded. *Extinguished*, clean as a pinched candle.

Then the next one. Then the next.

Down the whole constellation, column by column, like figures being struck from a ledger by some vast and unhurried hand.

Wendell watched with her knuckles pressed to her mouth, and the strangest thing happened inside her, the thing she would carry like a splinter for all the chapters of her life that came after: beneath the dread — beneath the ice-water certainty that the world had just been robbed of something irreplaceable — there was relief.

Small. Foul. Unmistakable.

Because if the wards were falling, then everything hidden was becoming visible, and everything visible was becoming possible, and somewhere in her chest a girl who had taken an oath at thirteen to erase herself whispered: *finally, finally, finally.*

She hated that whisper. She hated how warm it was.

* * *

By the third hour past midnight, half the sky was dark. Not overcast-dark — there were no clouds; the moon hung pale and alone like the last coin in an emptied purse — but *absent*-dark, a blackness with texture, with appetite. And the wards kept snapping. Wendell had climbed to the vault's reading gallery to feel it better, one thread, then another, each one a little slackening in her teeth, in the roots of her hair. The northern border. Then the eastern. Then the sea-wards, which had held against three wars and a kraken (the kraken was another ledger entry entirely, and one she treasured).

The guild's great wards were anchored to starlight. Everyone knew this the way everyone knew water was wet — it was the first lesson taught to apprentices, along with *salt moves* and *never trust a surveyor who says he measured it twice.* The ward-smiths of the old bloodlines had hung the borders of the realm on the sky itself, because the sky, they said, could not be stolen.

Apparently, someone had disagreed.

And here was the thought that finally drove Wendell down from the gallery, the thought that made her fetch her coat and her satchel and her father's brass compass with hands that had stopped shaking and started being very, very precise instead:

Someone had disagreed *recently*. Wards did not simply fail. They were maintained, renewed, fed. Which meant that whatever had extinguished the stars tonight had been *let in*, or *let out*, or *sold passage* — and there was exactly one institution in Caerlum that kept records of every trade of impossible things, and she was standing in its basement holding its keys.

The ledgers.

* * *

The Guild Archive occupied the level just above the vault, a long room lined floor-to-ceiling with ledgers bound in green leather, each spine dated in gold leaf, four hundred years of transactions conducted under the guild's seal. Most guilds traded in grain rights and shipping insurance. The Cartographers' Guild of Caerlum had always traded in stranger currency: distances, boundaries, names of places that did not exist yet, oaths drawn to scale.

Wendell lit two lamps, pulled down the current-year ledger, and began at the beginning, because she was her mother's daughter and her mother had taught her that panic is just arithmetic done badly.

The entries marched past under her pen-light: a boundary dispute settled in Ellsmere, paid in timber rights. A commission for a map of the drowned quarter, paid in silence. A renewal of the western sea-ward, paid in — she paused, read it again — *paid in song, one verse, unrecorded.* Fine. Normal. Perfectly normal Tuesday.

She worked backward from midnight. Eleven o'clock: nothing. Ten: nothing. Nine: a routine entry, Corvel's initials, the quarterly tithe to the Crown for maintenance of the palace wards. Eight: nothing.

Seven.

The entry at seven o'clock in the evening was written in a hand she knew better than her own. Neat, upright, patient — the hand of the man who had found her starving in a dockside alley at twelve, fed her stew, tested her on coastlines over dinner, and put the oath-pen in her fingers at thirteen. The hand of Master Corvel, First Keeper of Ledgers, who signed his entries with a small careful star instead of his name, because he said names were for gravestones and ledgers alike.

The entry read:

*Received: sky, whole. Northern hemisphere, all fixed lights, plus anchorage.*
*Paid: neutrality, extended. One hundred years, binding.*
*Witnessed: —*

The witness line was blank. In four hundred years of guild record-keeping, the witness line had never once been blank. An unwitnessed trade wasn't merely illegal. Under the guild's oldest law, it wasn't real — except that it was happening, right now, overhead, star by extinguished star.

*Sky, whole.*

Wendell sat very still for a long time. Above her, through the archive's round window, the last star in the southern sky went out, and the moon seemed to flinch.

She thought about Master Corvel's study, two floors up, where a kettle was probably still warm and a chair was definitely still pulled out for her, because it always was. She thought about the way he'd been watching her lately — not the fond absent way, but a newer way, a checking way, like a man counting spoons.

She thought about rule the third. *Never map yourself.*

And she understood, with the terrible clarity that comes only at the bottom of an archive at the end of the world, that she had never once wondered why the guild had needed a rule against mapping herself. Rules existed because someone, sometime, had tried.

Her invisibility — the half-blood erasure she had worn like a comfortable coat for thirty-one years — had not been an accident. It had been *policy.*

The relief curdled in her chest and came out the far side as something harder. Not dread, exactly. Dread was for things you couldn't face.

This was more like a commission.

"Right," Wendell said to the empty archive, and her voice didn't shake at all, which surprised her. "Ledger open. Pen charged. Let's find out who sold the sky."

Behind her, in the doorway she had not heard open, a floorboard creaked — softly, deliberately, the way a man creaks when he wants to be heard coming.

"Wendell," said Master Corvel's voice, gentle and terribly sad. "You should have stayed invisible, my dear. For everyone's sake — especially yours."
