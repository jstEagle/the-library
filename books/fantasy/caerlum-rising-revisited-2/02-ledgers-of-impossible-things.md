## 2. Ledgers of Impossible Things

The Cartographers' Guild of Caerlum kept its ledgers the way other institutions kept their dead: carefully, formally, and with a great deal of incense.

Wendell Nakamura had been auditing them since she was fourteen, when Master Corvel first put a ledger in her hands and said, *You have the two things this work requires, Wendell: patience, and no one will ever look at you twice.* She had been too young to hear the insult inside the compliment. Now, at twenty-nine, she heard it clearly, and had long since decided to be flattered anyway. Invisibility was a tool. A good cartographer was like a good map — present everywhere, noticed nowhere.

So it was that on the third morning after the stars went out, while the rest of the guild milled about the great hall in various states of theological crisis — Brother Hesk insisting it was an eclipse, Sister Amelie insisting it was a punishment, both of them wrong — Wendell took a lamp, a pot of tea, and herself down into the Ledger Vault, and began to look for the impossible thing she already knew was there.

The vault smelled of vellum and cedar oil and the particular dust that accumulates only around old truths. Ten thousand ledgers, arranged by year, each one a record of the guild's stranger business. Most people believed the Cartographers' Guild drew maps. The maps were true, as far as they went. But borders are not lines; they are agreements. And agreements, in Caerlum, were tradeable.

Hence the ledgers. *Trades of Impossible Things*, the spines read, in Corvel's own careful hand for the early volumes and in a succession of clerks' hands after. Rivers rerouted by treaty. A mountain sold to a neighboring duchy and physically persuaded to comply. One memorable entry from 1187 concerning the trade of "one Tuesday, lightly used" to the Weavers' Conclave, with no record of what came back in exchange, which had haunted Wendell for years.

She started with the current year's ledger, because grief makes you efficient.

* * *

The night the stars went out, Wendell had been working late in the map-vault above, correcting the coastline survey of Lake Verren for the fourth time, because the lake kept moving and nobody could tell her why. She remembered looking up at the high slit window, meaning to check how far the moon had climbed, and finding no moon. No stars. Just a sky like wet slate, smooth and total.

And then the wards. Every cartographer felt them; that was the joke of the profession, that you spent your life touching the world's edges until you developed a feel for them, the way a laundress develops calluses. The border wards of Caerlum ran along the kingdom's boundaries like hem-stitching, old and strong and humming faintly at all times, so constant most people forgot they existed, the way you forget your own heartbeat.

Wendell had felt all of them snap at once. Not fade — *snap*. Twelve threads cut clean, in twelve places, simultaneously. That was the detail that had kept her awake since. Wards frayed. Wards eroded. Wards did not fail in twelve places at once unless someone had unclipped them from their anchors, deliberately, in the manner of a person taking down laundry.

Someone had taken down the sky.

So now she sat in the cold vault with the year's ledger open on her knees, running her thumb down entries in the clerk's tidy script. *Trade of: the flood-memory of the River Sorrel. Received: three miles of uncontested border, southern reach.* *Trade of: the name of the village of Ashford (village consented, having grown tired of it). Received: a standing fog, useful for smuggling.*

And then, dated the night of the fall itself, an entry written not in the clerk's hand but in a hand she knew better than her own:

*Trade of: sky, whole. All stars thereof. Received: continuation of guild charter, one hundred years, terms binding.*

There was no counterparty named. There never was, in the worst entries. And beneath it, in smaller script, the notation reserved for trades sealed under oath rather than contract: *Witnessed and willed. M.C.*

M.C. Master Corvel.

The lamp flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft. Or perhaps there was, and it came from inside Wendell, from the long slow exhalation of forty years of certainty leaving her body all at once.

"No," she said, to the ledger, conversationally. "No, that's not— he wouldn't."

But he would, whispered the part of her brain that had done the ledgers longest. He would if he believed it was the only way to save us. He would if he thought the price was small enough. He has always been very good at deciding what the price is, and who pays it.

Because here was the thing about Master Corvel that Wendell had understood even as a child and never said aloud: he was kind the way a dam is kind. Everything he did was to hold something back. He had taken in a half-blood orphan with no family name worth speaking and made her indispensable and unseen, and Wendell had always assumed the kindness was for her sake. Now, staring at his initials beside the death of every star over Caerlum, she found herself wondering, with a cold new clarity, whether she had ever once been anything more than a well-placed piece of furniture.

Furniture doesn't ask questions. Furniture audits ledgers and finds nothing.

Except she had found something. Which meant either Corvel was slipping in his old age, or — worse — he had left it where she would find it. Both possibilities were terrible. She drank her tea, which had gone cold enough to qualify as soup, and turned to the front of the ledger to check the charter clause, because if you cannot have comfort, you may at least have thoroughness.

* * *

She worked through the morning and into the afternoon, cross-referencing. The guild charter renewal appeared eleven times across four centuries — always renewed, always by trade, and always in the same pattern: something vast given up, something small gained, terms binding. In 1402 the guild traded away "the color blue, as experienced within guild walls" for fifty years of neutrality. Wendell had always wondered why the oldest tapestries in the hall were grey. In 1611 they traded "the guild's collective memory of one specific Thursday." Nobody knew which Thursday. That was rather the point.

Eleven renewals. Eleven prices paid. And every single one witnessed and willed by a Master Corvel — or rather, by *a* Corvel. She checked. She counted backward through the years, doing arithmetic she did not want to do, and arrived at a number that made her set the ledger down and walk twice around the vault.

Corvel had signed the 1402 renewal himself. Corvel was currently sixty-one years old.

Either the guild had a tradition of recycling names, which it did not, or Master Corvel had been Master Corvel for at least five hundred years, and the man who taught her to hold a pen properly was older than the kingdom whose borders she mapped.

"Well," Wendell said to the empty vault, because saying things aloud helped, and there was no one left to think less of her. "Well, that explains the handwriting."

It was the same hand. Of course it was the same hand. Five centuries of ledgers, one unchanging script, neat and upright and patient as a fence line. She had spent her whole professional life admiring that hand. It had never once occurred to her to wonder why it matched the hand on documents from before the printing press.

She sat back down. She made more tea from the flask, out of pure habit, and discovered that ritual comforts still function even when everything else has broken, which is possibly why we invented them.

Here was the shape of it, then, laid out in her mind like a coastline taking form under her pen: the guild's precious neutrality, its famous century-spanning independence, was not independence at all. It was rent. They were tenants. Someone — something — held the charter, and every hundred years Corvel came crawling back to renew the lease, paying in whatever the landlord wanted. Rivers. Colors. Thursdays.

This year, the landlord had wanted the sky.

And the wards hadn't failed. They'd been *repossessed*.

Wendell closed the ledger with a snap that echoed in the vault, and found she was shaking — not with fear, exactly, though fear was in attendance, but with something rawer. Betrayal does not feel the way the ballads describe. It feels like discovering the floor of your house was painted on, and you have been walking on air your whole life, held up only by not looking down.

He raised me, she thought. He fed me and taught me and gave me a place where being half-blood didn't matter, because nothing about me mattered, because he'd made sure of it. And I loved him for it. I loved him the way you love a wall that keeps the weather off. And walls don't love you back, Wendell. Walls just decide which side of themselves you're allowed to stand on.

She was still sitting there, in the cedar-smelling dark, composing the argument she would never make aloud, when the lamp guttered again — and this time there *was* a draft, because the vault door had opened without her hearing it, which should have been impossible, because Wendell Nakamura heard everything, hearing was half her job.

Master Corvel stood in the doorway, silhouetted against the hall light, and even at this distance she could see he was holding two cups of tea.

"Wendell," he said, in the voice he used for students who had just made an error they couldn't yet see the size of. "You've been down here all day."

"I've been working," she said. Her voice came out steady. She was almost proud of it.

"Mm." He came forward slowly and set one cup beside her ledger, and the steam rose between them like a small gray flag of truce that neither of them meant. His eyes went to the open page before she could close it — she saw him see it, saw something move behind his face, some vast weariness surfacing for a moment like a whale under ice. "And what did the ledgers teach you today?"

"That our charter is rented," Wendell said. "That the rent came due three nights ago. And that the landlord took the stars." She looked up at him. "That you sold the sky, Master Corvel. *Sky, whole. All stars thereof.*"

For a long moment he said nothing. When he finally spoke, his voice was gentle, and that gentleness frightened her more than shouting would have.

"You audited five hundred years in one day. You always were the best of them." He lowered himself onto the stool across from her, joints cracking, an old man's sounds from a body that had no business being old. "Now stop looking, Wendell. Please. Some trades can't be unwound, only survived. The guild endures. That has always been the whole of my purpose."

"The guild endures," Wendell repeated. "Into what?"

Corvel looked at her then — really looked, the way he almost never did, and she realized with a fresh small horror that this, too, had been deliberate, all those years of not-seeing her, because his seeing apparently had weight to it, consequence, like a searchlight.

"Into winter," he said quietly. "Endless winter, child. The party we traded with does not negotiate, and it does not forget, and it has begun to notice things it had lost track of. So burn nothing, sign nothing, and ask me nothing further. Go home. Sleep. Be invisible, the way I made you." He rose, and paused, and added, almost tenderly, "It's the safest thing you'll ever be."

He left the second cup of tea behind, untouched, steaming gently in the dark.

Wendell sat alone for a long time, listening to the vault breathe. Then she pulled the year's ledger back into her lap, tore the corner from a survey sheet, and wrote on it in her smallest, sharpest hand:

*Sky, whole. Buyer unnamed. But charters bind both parties — and every lease names its landlord somewhere.*

She had, after all, audited five hundred years in one day. And buried in the oldest volume, behind the founding oaths, there had been one entry she had skimmed past and could not now stop thinking about. A founding trade. Dated the year the kingdom was crowned.

*Received: one crown, royal. Terms: perpetual.*

The summons had come that morning, before dawn — wax sealed with the royal arms, ordering the guild to send its finest ward-surveyor to the capital, to assess the failing border wards. Corvel had signed her name to it in his neat, patient hand, without asking her.

He wanted her far away from the ledgers. Which meant, Wendell thought, folding the note into her coat, that whatever waited at court wearing the king's face was exactly where her answers lived.

She blew out the lamp and went upstairs to pack.
