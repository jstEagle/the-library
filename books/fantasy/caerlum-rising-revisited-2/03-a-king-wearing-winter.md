## 3. A King Wearing Winter

The summons arrived at dawn, which was rude enough on its own. What made it worse was the messenger: a royal courier in silver-and-blue livery who stood in the guild's front hall dripping snowmelt onto Master Corvel's favorite rug and announcing, in a voice trained to carry across battlefields, that *the Crown required the services of the Cartographer's Guild, specifically one Wendell Nakamura, journeyman of the second rank, to attend His Majesty at Caerlum Palace without delay.*

"Without delay," Corvel repeated, reading the letter over my shoulder. He smelled of ink and last night's brandy. "They never say please, do they."

"They said 'required.'"

"That's worse than please." He handed the letter back. "Pack your good pens. And Wendell—" he caught my sleeve as I turned, "—map what they ask you to map. Nothing more."

I should have heard it then, the warning folded inside the warning. But I was twenty-six years old and a king had asked for me by name, and no invisible half-blood journeyman in the history of impossible trades had ever been asked for by name. I packed my good pens.

* * *

Caerlum Palace had been built by people who believed that if you piled enough white stone high enough, winter would take it personally. It rose out of the frozen capital like a jaw of teeth, and every window above the third floor had been bricked over or shuttered against a cold that had, by all accounts, gotten worse since the stars went out. Servants moved through the halls in furs that swallowed them whole. Braziers burned in rooms where braziers had no business burning. And everywhere—everywhere—the frost had grown inward, feathering across the interior walls in patterns I recognized despite myself.

I'd spent my life mapping things. I knew a ward-line when I saw one.

These were wards. Failing ones. Drawn across the palace itself in frost instead of ink, and unraveling from the center outward like a dropped stitch.

"The throne room," said my escort, a chamberlain whose name I never learned because he introduced himself three times and each time the wind stole it differently. He paused outside doors tall enough for a giant's grief and lowered his voice. "A word of advice, mistress cartographer. Don't stare at the crown."

"I beg your pardon?"

"Don't stare at the crown." He said it the way sailors say *don't whistle below decks*—not superstition, exactly, but close kin. Then he knocked, announced me, and pushed the doors open before I could decide whether I'd agreed to anything.

The throne room was cold the way deep water is cold. The court had thinned to perhaps two dozen souls, bundled and miserable, arranged at a respectful distance from the dais. And there, on the throne of white stone beneath a canopy of black velvet—

There was King Aldric.

I had seen his likeness on coinage, of course. Everyone had. The coins showed a broad-faced man with a strong jaw and an expression of mild, permanent patience, like a father waiting out a tantrum. The man on the throne had those same features, but the coin had lied about everything else. He sat very straight, very still, hands resting on the armrests with deliberate care, the way you sit when moving hurts and you have decided no one will know. His skin had gone pale as the palace stone. His breath fogged even though the room was warm enough elsewhere that courtiers were sweating under their furs.

And above his brow sat the crown.

I want to be precise here, because precision is the only prayer I've ever known how to say. The crown of Caerlum was old iron worked with seven stones, and every schoolchild could recite that much. What the schoolchildren could not recite—and what no ledger I had ever audited recorded—was that the crown was not sitting on the king's head. It was *rooted* in it. Thin filaments of light, white and cold as the light off snow at midnight, ran from the crown's circlet down into his temples, his neck, the hollow of his throat. They pulsed faintly, slowly, with a rhythm that was almost but not quite a heartbeat. Feeding rhythm. A tick feeding is patient. A tick feeding does not hurry.

Cold starlight. Stolen sky, worn like a glove.

My stomach did something complicated and traitorous. Because I knew this light. I had spent two sleepless weeks tracing its purchase through ledgers written in six dead hands, and here it was, wearing the kingdom's king, drinking him by increments so small that the courtiers had simply adjusted—shorter audiences, colder rooms, a monarch who no longer walked the walls or laughed in the gardens. They thought it was grief. Grief for his father, gone two years past. Grief keeps kings quiet; nobody questions a quiet king.

He looked up, and his eyes found mine across forty feet of frozen air, and I forgot every single thing I had ever sworn.

Not the crown's eyes. That's the horror I need you to understand. The crown's light lived behind his face like frost behind a window, but the window was still glass, and someone was still standing at it. His eyes were brown. Ordinary, warm, exhausted brown, the color of river water in summer, and they were looking at me the way a drowning man looks at a rope—not hope, exactly. Recognition.

"You're the mapmaker," he said. His voice was rough, like a door long unopened.

"Wendell Nakamura, Your Majesty. Journeyman, second rank." I bowed. My knees behaved. I was proud of my knees.

"Nakamura." He tried the name like tasting something. And then he did the thing I will remember on my deathbed, whatever deathbed I get: he smiled. Small, crooked, startled out of him. "I know your name. Isn't that strange? I've dreamed it. There's a dream I have where someone is drawing lines in the dark and saying *this is where you end and I begin*, and—" He stopped himself, and something behind his face flared white, and his whole body went rigid as the light took its toll for the words. When it passed he was paler, and the smile was gone, and he finished flatly, "Forgive me. I'm told I ramble. Tell me what you see."

It was not a casual question. The chamberlain's advice rang in my ears—*don't stare at the crown*—and I understood suddenly that the king was asking whether I could see what everyone else had learned not to see, and that answering honestly might be the most dangerous act of my carefully erased life.

I looked at the man beneath the glove. He looked back.

"Your Majesty," I said, "the wards of this room are drawn in frost and failing at the keystone. I can mend them. But whoever drew them drew them around *you*, not for you, and I'll need to work close, and I'll need the truth, and I warn you now—I'm a very honest mapper. It's my worst quality."

Somewhere behind us, a courtier gasped. Somewhere farther behind us, a door opened that no one had ordered opened. And Aldric of Caerlum, king of a darkening realm, host to a parasite of stolen stars, laughed—a real laugh, brief and rusty and human, quickly strangled but unmistakable.

"Honest," he repeated, like the word was a gift someone had left on his doorstep. "Gods. Yes. Work close."

* * *

I worked close for eleven days.

The official version, recorded in the guild's copy-book, reads: *Journeyman Nakamura retained at court for the restoration of the palace ward-lattice.* The true version involves considerably more whispering in corridors, one incident involving a hidden stair, two pots of tea gone tragically cold, and a growing collection of notes passed between a king and a cartographer whenever the crown's attention dimmed—which it did, I discovered, in regular cycles, like a lamp running low. During the low hours, Aldric was Aldric: dry-witted, self-deprecating, ferociously intelligent about the small mechanics of ruling, and quietly furious at what was being done to his kingdom from inside his own skull. During the high hours, he went distant and cold, and I learned to fill silence with humming until he surfaced again.

On the fourth day he told me the dream properly. In it, he stood at the edge of a country that wasn't anywhere, and someone was drawing its borders around him—carefully, kindly, in ink the color of lamplight—saying *this is where the winter stops, this is where you are safe, this line is mine to hold*. He had never seen the dreamer's face. "But she had ink on her fingers," he said, looking directly at mine. "To the knuckle. Both hands. Like she'd been born mid-sentence in somebody's ledger."

I didn't tell him I'd had the dream too. Not yet. Some maps you draw slowly.

On the ninth day, working the keystone above the throne with my sleeves rolled and my hair falling in my face, I felt the crown notice me. There's no polite way to put it. The light behind Aldric's eyes turned, the way a head turns, and for one full second I was seen by something older than the guild, older than the border, older than the sky it had eaten—and it looked at my ink-stained fingers and my half-blood face and it *recognized the lineage*. I felt it the way you feel a draft under a door. Something in the vaults of my blood had a lock, and this thing had just found the key it had been searching for.

That night Aldric caught my wrist as I gathered my pens, and his grip was shaking, and his eyes were entirely, fiercely his own.

"It saw you," he whispered. "Don't tell me it didn't, because I felt it turn. Wendell. Whatever you came here thinking you'd find—leave. Take the north road tonight. I'll cover for you, I'll write any lie you need—"

"And leave you alone with that?" I said. "Your Majesty, I audit ledgers of impossible trades for a living. Do you know what I do with a debt I find?"

His brow creased. "...Collect?"

"*Unmake* it," I said. "I'm going to need more ink. And possibly a miracle. Do you keep miracles in the treasury, or is that more of a guild thing?"

He laughed again—that rusty, startled sound—and outside the frost-feathered windows, where no star had shone for twenty-three days, something flickered. Just once. Just briefly. Like a candle remembering itself.

Neither of us saw it. We were busy arguing about whose fault the miracle would be.

But the frost on the windows saw. And far away, in a vault under the guildhall, Master Corvel woke from a dream of drawing lines in the dark, checked a ledger dated the night the stars went out, and began writing a letter he would spend the rest of his life regretting.
