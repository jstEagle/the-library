## 4. The Cartographer's Oath

The guild took Wendell in the spring she turned nine, on a day so gray that even the gulls looked embarrassed by it.

She remembered it in pieces afterward, the way cartographers remember everything—in fragments that could later be stitched into a whole. A woman's hand around hers, callused and cold. A gate of black iron shaped like a compass rose. The smell of the map-vault, which was old paper and lamp oil and something sweeter underneath, like ink that had learned to be honey.

"You'll want to memorize that smell," the woman said. She was Master Corvel's under-clerk then, a narrow person named Ives who spoke as though every sentence had been measured twice and cut once. "In twenty years it will be the smell of home."

Wendell, nine years old and freshly orphaned for the second time—her mother first, then the fever that took the aunt who'd grudgingly inherited her—had said nothing. She had already learned that saying nothing made people underestimate how much you noticed.

She noticed a great deal.

* * *

The Guild of Cartographers and Improbable Trades did not advertise itself, which was fortunate, because no one would have believed the sign. It sat behind the tanners' quarter in Caerlum's lower city, disguised as a dealer in nautical instruments, and it was neither entirely a guild nor entirely a bank nor entirely a church, though it performed the duties of all three for people whose business could not be spoken aloud.

They dealt in trades, the guild. Impossible ones. A fisherman might trade his luck at dice for three years of full nets. A widow might trade her grief for the memory of a better marriage than she'd actually had. And once, according to a ledger Wendell was not supposed to have read until much later, someone had traded their shadow for a second chance at a Tuesday.

Every trade required collateral. Every collateral required a record. And every record required a keeper who could be trusted absolutely—which meant, in practice, someone with nothing to lose and no one looking.

That was where the half-bloods came in.

"Half-bloods see both sides of every border," Master Corvel told her on her first night in the dormitory, crouching to her eye level with the patience of a man who had done this many times and never quite gotten used to it. He was not yet the white-bearded monument he would become; he was only fifty then, gray at the temples, with ink permanently staining the first knuckle of his right hand. "Human on one side, one of the elder folk on the other. You belong to both countries and neither. Which means, little one, that the wards don't argue about you. They let you pass."

"Is that good?"

Corvel smiled. Even then, Wendell noticed, his smile had a careful quality, like a man setting down something fragile. "It is *useful*. And in this guild, useful is the highest compliment there is."

She hadn't understood yet what it cost, being useful. She would learn.

* * *

Her induction came at the end of her first year, when she had proven she could copy a coastal survey without error and keep silent through an entire negotiation between a river-spirit and a miller who owed it forty years of flour.

The ceremony was held in the vault's antechamber, before the First Map—the guild's founding chart, drawn on skin so old no one agreed anymore whose skin it had been. Eleven masters stood in a crescent. Corvel stood at its center, holding the Oath Book open.

"Kneel, child."

She knelt on cold stone while Ives bound her eyes with a strip of gray silk.

"You cannot see," Corvel said, "because a cartographer's first lesson is that seeing and knowing are different trades. Now repeat after me. *I swear my hand to the true lines.*"

"I swear my hand to the true lines."

"*I swear my tongue to the sealed ledgers.*"

"I swear my tongue to the sealed ledgers." Her voice echoed oddly, as if the room were larger than it looked. Later she would learn that it was; the antechamber folded space the way an experienced hand folds a letter.

"*And I swear never to draw the one map forbidden to me.*"

There was a pause. Nine-year-old Wendell frowned behind the blindfold. "Which map is that?"

A rustle went through the masters—not laughter exactly, but its dry cousin. Corvel's voice, when it came, was gentle. "Yours. Yourself. Your own borders, your own bloodline, your own shape. A cartographer who maps herself becomes known, child, and what is known can be found, and what can be found can be taken. The oath keeps you safe. Do you understand?"

She didn't, fully. But she understood the shape of it, the way she understood coastlines before she ever saw the sea. "I swear never to draw myself."

"Then rise, Wendell Nakamura, Keeper-in-Training, Third Ledger. You belong to us now."

*Belong.* Even at nine, some stubborn pilot-light part of her noted that belonging to a place was not the same as being *of* it. But the vault smelled like honey-ink, and the dormitory bed had a wool blanket that didn't itch, and nobody hit her, and for a girl who had been passed along like an unclaimed parcel since her mother died, that was wealth beyond counting.

She signed the oath register with her left hand, because the guild taught that signatures should be slightly wrong—a forgery of oneself, another small wall against being found.

* * *

The years that followed were, by any honest accounting, good years.

Corvel taught her the deep craft: how to read a border in the lie of the grass, how to hear wards hum (they sang, very faintly, in a key only half-bloods could catch), how to hold a pen so steady that a line became true simply by being drawn well. "A map doesn't describe the world," he liked to say, tapping her nose with the handle of a divider. "It *argues* with it. Make your arguments elegant, and the world concedes."

He also taught her the ledgers, and here Wendell discovered her true calling. There was a pleasure in a perfectly balanced impossible account that she suspected normal people got from music or love. Debts of luck carried forward. Interest paid in dreams. A column of traded names, each one struck through when redeemed—and beside it, in Corvel's neat hand, the note *never redeem*, which she copied without comment and thought about for years.

She made friends, in the sideways way of someone trained not to be noticed. Ives, who by then ran the day-ledgers and pretended not to slip extra honey-cakes onto Wendell's tray. Old Sarn in the bindery, who let her sleep in the paper-warmth of his workshop during winter audits. And Corvel most of all—Corvel, who called her *little compass* because, he said, she always knew which way was north even in the dark, especially in the dark.

Once, when she was fifteen, she asked him why the guild wanted invisible keepers at all. Why not just lock the ledgers?

"Locks tell thieves where to look," he said. "But a secret kept by no one—by a girl who officially does not exist in three censuses, whose name appears in no registry, whose blood lets her walk through wards that would flay a full mage—that secret is kept by the world itself forgetting to guard it. You are not hidden from danger, Wendell. You are hidden *as* the danger's blind spot."

"And when I'm older? When I've mapped everything there is to map?"

He was quiet a moment, trimming his lamp wick. "You'll still be unmapped yourself. That's the arrangement."

"The arrangement," she repeated.

"The arrangement," he agreed, and changed the subject to tidal arithmetic, which he knew she hated, which was how she knew he wanted the conversation over.

* * *

Here is what she did not understand at fifteen, and barely understood at nineteen, and only truly understood—standing in the frozen dark of a court that smelled of wrong starlight, watching a king flicker inside his own crown—now:

She had chosen it.

Not the oath—she'd been nine, and the alternative had been the street. But every year after, when the guild offered her the option—the rare, quiet, once-a-decade option—of petitioning for a mapped identity, a name in the civil rolls, a self that could be seen—she had declined.

Because being unknown was power. Because being unknown was *safe*. Because she had built a life out of other people's borders precisely so she would never have to survey her own.

And because—she admitted it now, in the frost-lit corridor of a palace gone strange—there had been a smaller, meaner comfort in it too. If no one could find her, no one could leave her. Her mother had been taken by the sea; her aunt by fever; the world had a habit of repossessing whatever it could locate. Wendell Nakamura, Third Ledger, Keeper of Impossible Things, had solved grief the way she solved all problems: she had removed herself from the map.

The cost had arrived quietly, the way interest always does. She had no childhood friend who could vouch for her. No lover who could say *she was here, she mattered, I saw her.* When she laughed, it echoed in rooms that didn't know her name. She was the most thorough record of an entire hidden world, and there was not one page anywhere that recorded her.

She had traded her visibility for safety the way the guild's clients traded everything else—one thing of value for another, terms favorable to the house, no refunds.

* * *

On her last night in the capital before everything changed—though of course she didn't know it was her last night, no one ever knows—Corvel found her in the vault, correcting a survey of the northern passes.

"You're summoned to court," he said.

She looked up. "The crown never summons us. We're *neutral*."

"The wards along the northern border are failing. His Majesty requests a cartographer of particular... discretion." Corvel set a folded commission on her table, and his hand lingered on it a moment too long. In lamplight, Wendell—who had spent twenty years learning to read what people didn't say—noticed that his fingers were trembling.

"Master. What aren't you telling me?"

For one heartbeat, she saw him weigh it—whatever it was, the whole of it, the sky-sized secret sitting in his chest like swallowed ice. Twenty years of trust leaned toward confession.

"Nothing, little compass," he said. "Only that I am proud of you. Go carefully. And remember—" he tapped the edge of her desk, where her oath register lay, "—some maps exist to be drawn. Some exist to stay shut. Know which is which."

She rode north at dawn with the commission in her satchel and a feeling like a hairline crack running through the foundation of everything she believed.

Behind her, the stars were still burning. All of them. Every single one.

They had six days left.
