## 5. What the Frost Remembers

I had been king of Caerlum for nine years, and in all that time I had never once run from anything.

It is a strange thing to learn about yourself—that your courage was never yours. That when the council praised your steadiness in crisis, when the poets wrote of your unshakeable calm, they were praising a parasite's patience. The crown-thing does not know fear, and so neither did I. It does not know doubt, or longing, or the particular ache of watching a woman walk out of your throne room and wanting, with every piece of you that is still your own, to follow.

But on the third night after Wendell Nakamura mapped the truth onto my skin in a single unflinching glance, I learned fear. It came back to me like a language I had spoken as a boy and forgotten, and it tasted of cold iron and her name.

*Wendell,* I thought, as we ran through the servants' corridors beneath the palace, her hand gripping mine with the desperate strength of someone who had never been allowed to hold on to anything. *Her name is Wendell.*

The crown knew it too. I felt it stir against my skull like a sleeping wasp, cold and curious, and I understood that it had begun to hunt her—not with armies yet, but with the quiet, patient attention of something that had waited three hundred years and could wait three hundred more.

"They'll seal the river gate," Wendell whispered. We had stopped in the dark beneath the wine cellars, and even in that darkness I could see her eyes moving, reading the palace the way she read everything—as territory, as lines and boundaries and ways through. "There's a postern behind the old icehouse. Do you know it?"

"I know every stone of this palace," I said, and then, because the man beneath the crown apparently had a sense of humor that had survived nine years of occupation, "though until three days ago I couldn't have told you that. It knew. I just lived in it."

She made a sound that was half laugh, half sob. "That's either very funny or very horrible."

"Both," I said. "I'm finding that's most things now."

* * *

We came out of the postern into falling snow that was wrong in a way I felt in my teeth. It fell too slowly, each flake hanging in the air a half-beat too long, as though the sky itself had forgotten what falling was for. Beyond the palace walls, the capital burned with fever-lanterns—the crown's agents, the Warden's Men, moving through the streets with their grey cloaks and their grey faces, asking questions in a voice that was not quite a voice.

Wendell had a plan. Of course she had a plan; she had three of them, drawn in a small waterproof notebook she kept against her ribs, each one annotated in a hand so small and precise it looked like the writing of a very careful insect. The plan we chose took us north, through the winter fairs, past the shrine road, toward the border country where the wards had first failed.

"We need somewhere the frost has already been," she explained, as we rode stolen horses through the dead orchards outside the city. "The crown-thing reads warmth. Heartbeats, breath, anger, hope—it hunts by heat. But where the frost has settled deep, everything's already cold. We'll be invisible."

"You've done this before," I said. "Fled from something that hunts by warmth."

"No," she said, and her jaw did a thing I was beginning to catalog—the small stubborn set of it that meant she was deciding how much truth she could afford. "But I've spent my whole life being invisible. I know how it works. You make yourself small. You make yourself nothing. You become a line on someone else's map."

"And what happens," I asked, "when you want to be a line on your own?"

She didn't answer. She rode ahead, and I watched her shoulders, and I thought: *I will find out. Whatever it costs, I will find out who you are when no one is erasing you.*

* * *

The village of Hollowmere had been a living place once—I knew it from the tax rolls, from the petitions about the mill, from a hundred small concerns that had passed across my desk in the years when I still believed the hands signing my name were mine. Now it stood in the frozen heart of the border country like a fly in amber.

The frost had come on the night the stars went out, and it had not finished falling since. It had simply *stopped*, mid-fall, mid-moment, and everything in Hollowmere had stopped with it.

We walked our horses up the high street at a walk, because anything faster felt like blasphemy. A woman stood at a well with her bucket half-raised, her mouth open around a word she would never finish. Two boys were frozen in the middle of a snowball fight, one mid-dodge, the other's throw hanging in the air like a small white moon. In the bakery, bread sat risen and unbaked in its tins. A dog slept curled on a doorstep, and I wanted desperately for it to be sleeping still, and not something worse.

"It's not dead," Wendell said quietly, reading my face. "It's *kept*. The frost here isn't cold the way we think of cold. It's a preserving. Like wax on a letter." She dismounted and pressed her bare palm flat against the doorframe of the bakery, and I saw her flinch. "It remembers everything that was happening when it fell. The ice holds it. Like—" she searched for the word, and I watched her find it, watched her face go strange, "—like a ledger."

"Can you read it?"

"I'm a cartographer," she said, with the ghost of a smile. "I read everything."

* * *

We sheltered in the tavern, because it had a hearth that had gone cold before the frost took it, and because Wendell said the thick stone would muffle whatever warmth we made. We could not light a fire. We could not even hold each other too closely, though I confess I thought about it more than was seemly, sitting across from her in a frozen tavern with a dead hearth between us like a chaperone.

She ate hard bread and dried apples from her pack and talked with her mouth full, which was the first unguarded thing I had ever seen her do. "The wards didn't fail," she said. "That's the thing I keep coming back to. Wards don't fail. They're not machines. They're more like—promises. Someone unmade the promise. Someone inside."

"The guild," I said.

"The guild." She looked at the bread in her hand as though it had personally betrayed her. "I was raised by them. Taken in at seven years old, half-blood, no family, no name anyone wanted. They taught me that being nothing was a *gift*. That a cartographer who maps everything and is mapped by no one can go anywhere, learn anything, keep any secret." Her laugh had no warmth in it, and the frost around us seemed to lean in and listen. "I thought it was mercy. I'm starting to think it was *husbandry*. You don't erase a tool, Aldric. You maintain it."

"Aldric," I repeated.

She went still. "I—forgive me. I shouldn't—"

"No one has said my name in nine years," I said. "Not my name. They say *Your Majesty*, or *the King*, or *sire*, and every time, the thing in the crown turns toward the sound like a dog hearing its master. But you said *Aldric*." I found that my eyes were burning, which was absurd, and that I did not care. "Do you know what it's like to be a house that something lives in? To feel your own hands sign orders you never wrote? And then to have one person look at you—actually *look*—and say your name like it's a door they're knocking on?"

"No," she said softly. "But I know what it's like to be a ledger no one reads."

"Then we are both," I said, "very well acquainted with being furniture."

That startled a real laugh out of her—short, rusty, surprised out of her like a bird from cover—and I thought: *I would burn down the whole of winter to hear that again.*

* * *

It was near midnight, if midnight still meant anything in a place where the stars were gone, that I found her in the tavern's back room, standing over a table where the frost had laid itself down in a thick, glassy sheet. Her hands were above it, palms down, not quite touching, and her lips were moving.

"Wendell?"

"It's a memory," she said, not looking up. "A strong one. The night it fell, someone in this room felt something so hard that the ice took it whole. I can almost—there's a way to read it, if you press warmth into it, just a little, just—"

"Wendell." I caught her wrist. "If you put your warmth into it, the frost will know where we are."

"I know."

"And you're going to do it anyway."

"I'm going to do it anyway," she agreed, "because whatever this person knew on the night the sky was sold, it's the only testimony in the world that isn't in a guild ledger. And guild ledgers, I've recently learned, lie."

So we did it together. She taught me how—warmth, not heat, a slow breath of the self rather than a flare—and we pressed our palms side by side against the ice, and the memory rose up around us like breath on a window.

A tavern room. A fire. A guild-cartographer, robed in the grey and silver of the Cartographers' Guild, sitting across from a man in a miller's apron, and the miller's voice ringing with a terror that the frost had kept fresh as the moment it was born:

*"You're certain? Certain it's the guild itself? Not some rogue member—"*

*"The Master signed it himself," the cartographer said, and his face was grey with shame. "Corvel. Master Corvel. The transaction is dated the night of the fall. 'Sky, whole. One payment, deferred. Buyer: the Crown.'" He looked up, and his eyes found us across three hundred years of ice, or across one frozen night, I could not tell. "God forgive us. We sold the stars to buy ourselves a century."*

The memory collapsed. The frost went dark and silent, and Wendell and I stood with our palms still pressed flat, our fingers touching, neither of us moving.

"Corvel," she said at last. Her voice was very small. "My mentor. The man who taught me to hold a compass." A breath. "He raised me, Aldric. He *chose* me. And all this time I thought—" She stopped. Started again, steadier, the way she steadied everything, by mapping it. "He traded the sky. And the buyer was the crown. Which means the crown-thing didn't conquer Caerlum. It was *invited in*. It was *purchased*."

"Wendell—"

"And there's more." She lifted her hand from the ice, and beneath our palms, where our shared warmth had bloomed and faded, the frost was clearing—clearing in a shape, a pattern, lines crossing lines in a design I recognized the way you recognize your own name shouted in a crowd.

It was a map. Faint, half-formed, but unmistakable: a map of the border country, and at its heart, marked with a symbol I knew from the oldest regalia of my line, a shrine.

"The border shrine," I breathed. "The shrine of the ward-smiths. Where the crown was first bound."

"Yes," Wendell said softly. And when I looked at her face in the starless dark, I saw that she was no longer only afraid. She was beginning, at last, to be *certain*—and certainty, on her, was the most dangerous and beautiful thing I had ever seen. "The frost remembers, Aldric. And I think it's been waiting for someone to ask."
