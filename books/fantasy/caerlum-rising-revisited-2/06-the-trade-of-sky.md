## 6. The Trade of Sky

The ledger trail ended where all the guild's roads ended: at Master Corvel's door.

Wendell had known it would, in the way you know a map is wrong before you've found the error—some small wrongness in the margins, a river bending against its nature, a coastline that repeated itself like a nervous tic. Three days out of the capital, riding north through snow that fell without ceasing and without landing, she had stopped pretending otherwise. Aldric had stopped too. He rode beside her in a borrowed coat two sizes too big, hood up, crownless for the first time since his coronation, and he looked less like a king than like a man who had been swimming for years and had only just been allowed to surface.

"You could still turn back," he said, when the guild's towers rose out of the white like the ribs of some drowned leviathan. "Say the word and I'll say it with you."

"I know," Wendell said. "That's what makes it hard."

The Cartographers' Guild at Caerlum-on-the-Wold had raised her from the age of nine, when her mother died and left nothing but a daughter with ink-stained fingers and a father nobody spoke about. The guild fed her, taught her, gave her a bed in the dormitory that smelled of cedar shavings and old vellum, and asked in return only one thing: that she remain unmapped. Unrecorded. A half-blood with no lineage worth tracing was the perfect keeper of secrets, Master Corvel had told her once, gently, the way you tell a child something true and terrible at the same time. *No one will ever look for you, Wendell. That is a gift.*

She had believed him for twenty years.

Now she stood before his door with a stolen ledger page in her satchel and a fugitive king shivering at her shoulder, and the gift felt very much like a cage.

* * *

Corvel received them in the map-vault itself, which Wendell understood at once to be a message. Not the warm study upstairs where he took his tea and bullied his apprentices. The vault—the deep room, the cold room, the place where the guild kept its ledgers of impossible trades behind three locks and an oath. He wanted them to know he knew why they'd come. He wanted to have the conversation on his own ground, among the shelves where every secret the guild had ever bought or sold sat bound in calfskin, patient as gravestones.

He was smaller than Wendell remembered. Or perhaps she had simply grown, and he had not, and the difference between them had quietly inverted itself sometime in the last decade without either of them noticing. His hair had gone fully white now, fine as frost-fern. His hands, which had once been the steadiest in the guild—hands that could draw a border so fine a moth couldn't cross it—trembled slightly when he poured the tea.

Three cups. He had set out three cups.

"Master Corvel," Wendell said carefully, "this is King Aldric."

"I know who he is." Corvel did not look up. "I also know what follows him around like a smell. Sit down, both of you. The tea is getting cold, and I did not survive sixty years in this trade by serving lukewarm tea."

Aldric glanced at Wendell, and something passed between them—not quite a plan, but an agreement to let the old man have his ritual first. They sat. The tea was black and bitter and perfect, and drinking it felt like a small betrayal of everything Wendell had come to do, which was probably exactly how Corvel intended it.

"The ledger entry," Corvel said finally. "Dated the night the stars went out. 'Sky, whole. One lot. Payment: continuance.'" He set his cup down with a click. "You found it."

"I found it."

"I taught you to find things. I suppose I can't complain when you find the wrong thing in the right direction." He folded his trembling hands on the table. "Ask your question, Wendell. You came a long way through my winter to ask it. Don't leave here with it still in your mouth; those weigh more going home than coming."

"Why?" It came out rougher than she meant. "You sold the sky. You sold the wards—you knew the wards were anchored to the stars, you *taught me* they were anchored to the stars. The whole border froze. Villages are entombed in ice, Master Corvel. People are—" She thought of the village they'd sheltered in, the family frozen mid-laughter at their own table, preserved like flowers in glass. "People are *stuck*. And you traded it away for what? Continuance?"

"Neutrality," Corvel said. "Continuance of neutrality. There is a difference, though I grant it is a fine one."

"A century of it," Aldric said quietly. "That was the price offered?"

"That was the price *demanded*." For the first time, Corvel looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious and ancient. "You think I walked into that negotiation, boy? You think there was a table, and chairs, and terms? There was a thing made of old starlight wearing your father's crown like a glove fits a hand, and it came to the guild in the autumn, and it said: *the stars, or the guild.* Not both. Never both. It had already eaten the Skywardens' archive in Vell. It had already unmade the Observatory at Harrowmere so thoroughly that the mountain forgot it had ever held one. I checked. I sent people. The mountain has a smooth face now, like an egg."

The vault was silent. Somewhere above them, snow ticked against a high window like fingernails.

"It said the guild could keep its neutrality another hundred years," Corvel went on, softer now. "Its maps. Its ledgers. Its little kingdom of paper, safe while every other kingdom burned. All it wanted was the sky. And I want you to understand, Wendell—I want you to sit there and drink my bitter tea and understand—that I did not hesitate because I valued the guild over the world. I hesitated because I valued *you* over the world."

Wendell's cup stopped halfway to her lips.

"The wards are keyed to blood," Corvel said. "Old blood. Ward-smiths' blood. And there is exactly one person left alive carrying that blood, and she has spent her whole life being careful never to be found, and I have spent twenty years making sure of it. If the thing in the crown ever learned her name—if it ever traced the line—it wouldn't need to buy the sky. It would take the key instead. So I sold the sky, and I buried the transaction in a ledger no one audits, and I prayed to every god I don't believe in that you would stay invisible long enough to die of old age in a warm room full of maps, having never once been interesting."

"And instead," Wendell said, "you made me interesting."

"No," said Corvel. "*It* made you interesting. The moment the wards snapped, the moment the border fell, the thing in the crown started looking for its jailer's heir whether I liked it or not. My trade didn't cause that. My trade just delayed it. Bought time. Time for you to be somewhere else when it started looking." He laughed, and it was a horrible sound, dry as old paper catching fire. "And where did you go, Wendell? Where did you ride, the moment the stars went out? Straight to the crown. Straight into its gloved hand. Twenty years of my work, undone in one night, because you are your mother's daughter after all, and your mother also never once did the safe thing."

"I never met my mother," Wendell said.

"No," Corvel said. "You did worse. You became her."

* * *

They talked until the candles burned down to puddles. Corvel showed them the original contract—a single sheet of starlight-grey vellum that hurt to look at directly, the way snowfields hurt at noon—and Wendell read the terms twice and understood, with a cartographer's cold clarity, that the document was itself a map. A map of the sky, drawn in negative. Every star it named was a star that had been handed over, and the thing in the crown had not destroyed them. It had *filed* them. Stored them. Folded the whole night sky up small and put it away, the way the guild folded maps of territories it no longer wished to see.

"They're not gone," she said aloud, and heard Aldric's breath catch beside her. "Corvel. The stars aren't gone. They're *archived*."

"Of course they are," Corvel said wearily. "It learned hoarding from us. We taught it, in a sense—every impossible trade, every secret bought and shelved. It is what we made it, or near enough. Why do you think it wanted the guild's neutrality rather than our destruction? You don't burn a library you intend to use."

Aldric leaned forward, and in the candlelight Wendell saw the man beneath the winter again—the flicker, the warmth, the person the crown wore like a glove wears a hand. "Then it can be unmade," he said. "If the stars are stored, they can be returned."

"If," Corvel said, "someone can find where it keeps them. And if someone can open whatever vault a thing like that uses. And if someone survives the opening." He looked at Wendell for a long moment, and then he did the thing she would remember for the rest of her life, the thing that hurt worse than any betrayal: he reached across the table and covered her ink-stained hand with his trembling one.

"There is a map," he said. "In the deep vault. Behind the third lock. I drew it myself, thirty years ago, from records I should never have touched, and I swore an oath that it would never be shown to anyone, least of all you. It is a map of a bloodline. Your bloodline. And if you are going to do what I think you are going to do, you cannot do it without it." His voice cracked on the last word. "I sold the sky to keep that map locked. Don't make me have sold it for nothing."

Wendell looked at his hand on hers—at the tremor in it, the ink ghosts under the skin, sixty years of drawing borders that kept other people safe—and felt the betrayal in her chest sharpen into something else entirely. Pity, maybe. Or its close cousin, love. He had not sold the world. He had sold the world trying to buy her a quiet life, the way you buy a child a lock for a door that was never going to hold.

"Show me," she said.

Corvel closed his eyes. Then he rose, took the ring of keys from around his neck—heavier than any crown, Wendell thought, watching Aldric watch it—and led them down past the third lock into the dark.

At the bottom of the stairs, in a case of black glass, lay a single sheet of vellum older than the guild itself. And across its top, in a hand Wendell recognized as her mother's, ran a title she had been forbidden her entire life to read:

*The True Borders of the House of Nakamura.*
