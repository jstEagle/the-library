## 8. The Long Winter Finishes Falling

Aldric had learned, in the months since the crown settled over his brow like a hand closing around a candle flame, to measure his life in small victories. Today's victory: he had blinked. Both eyes. At the same time, when he chose.

It had taken him the better part of an hour.

*Good,* he thought at himself, in the dark cellar of his own skull where the crown-thing kept him. *Next we'll attempt swallowing.*

The thing that wore him — it called itself the Regent now, which Aldric found both grandiose and insulting, like a cat naming itself Lord of the House — sat at the writing desk in the map-room of Caerlum Keep and did not blink for hours on end. It was drafting something. Letters. Orders. And beneath them, hidden in a folio it thought no one would ever see because no one could see anything it did not wish seen, a contract.

Aldric could not see the folio either. But he had heard Wendell say its name once, weeks ago, in a voice gone thin and careful, and he had spent every hour since turning that name over like a stone in his pocket: *vessel.*

She knew. She had known since the frost-village, since the ice had shown them the guild's name written in a dead woman's memory, since Corvel's confession. She knew what the crown wanted, and she had kissed him anyway — quickly, fiercely, on the frozen road out of Harrowmere, as though she were sealing a letter she meant him to open later.

He opened it constantly.

"Aldric," said the Regent aloud, to the empty room, because it enjoyed doing this. "Do you know what I admire about your cartographer?"

*I am not listening,* Aldric thought, with the dignity available to a man who had recently celebrated blinking.

"She lies beautifully. Most people lie with their mouths. She lies with her whole body — her hands especially. Did you know that? When she showed me the survey charts of the northern wards, her fingers never trembled. Perfectly steady. That is how I knew she was lying."

*Then you are a fool,* Aldric thought, *because her hands were steady because she is brave, not because she is false—*

But the cold laughter slid through him like water through a cracked cup, and he understood, with a sick lurch, that the Regent wanted him to hear this. Wanted him to understand what was coming. The crown fed on despair the way other things fed on bread.

"Tomorrow," the Regent said pleasantly. "At the border shrine. She has agreed to come alone."

---

They let him surface that night.

This was the cruelest mercy of the arrangement: for an hour or two, when the crown's hunger was sated and the winter's work ran itself, the Regent withdrew to some cold interior room of itself, and Aldric got his body back. His hands shook. His voice came out rusted. He wept, usually, briefly and without shame, because shame was a luxury the drowned could not afford.

And then, three nights ago, there had been a knock at the door of the map-room where they kept him — a soft, particular knock, two quick and one slow, the knock of someone who had spent years signaling colleagues across the guild vaults without waking Master Corvel.

Wendell.

She had bribed a chambermaid with a hand-drawn map of the castle's hot-water pipes, and she came to him in the dark, and she held his face in her ink-stained hands and said, "You're still in there. Good. I have questions, and only you can answer them honestly, which makes you, statistically, the most useful man in this castle."

"I love you too," Aldric croaked.

"That as well." But her thumb brushed under his eye, gathering what was there, and her voice gentled. "Listen. We don't have long. When it lets you up next, I need to know everything you can feel about the binding. Where it anchors. What hurts when you fight it. Every seam."

"Wendell." He caught her wrists. Her pulse hammered against his palms like a bird against glass. "Whatever you're planning—"

"I'm planning nothing. I'm cartographing. There's a difference, and the difference is that one of them has footnotes."

"Wendell." He made himself say it plainly, because plainness was all he had left. "It offered me a trade. Weeks ago. Before Harrowmere. My life for—" He stopped. He could not make his mouth form the rest.

"For a new vessel," she said. "Yes. I read the contract. I'm very good at reading things people hide in folios; it's most of my job." She smiled, and it was the worst smile he had ever seen, bright and brittle as river ice. "I'm going to accept it."

"No."

"Yes."

"No." He stood, and his knees nearly folded, and he stood anyway, because there were exactly two things left in him that still worked and one of them was refusal. "You will not walk into that thing wearing my face and my crown and call it salvation. I would rather burn out entirely. I would rather be a candle in a gale than a lamp you light by putting yourself in the fire."

"That's a lovely image and also completely backwards," Wendell said. "You don't get to choose how you're saved. That's not how being saved works. Believe me, I've been trying to refuse rescue my entire life and it's remarkably ineffective."

"This isn't rescue, it's exchange. You'd be trading yourself for me. That's arithmetic, not love."

"It's both. I've always been good at keeping two ledgers."

"Wendell—"

"And here is the entry I want you to read twice." She stepped close, and her forehead came to rest against his chest, right over the place where his heart still beat, stubbornly, on his own time. "You told me once — that night in the frost village, when we read the dead woman's memory together — you said the worst thing the crown ever did wasn't taking your kingdom. It was making you doubt you'd ever been real. Making you wonder if there'd ever been an Aldric underneath, or just the shape of one." Her voice wavered and steadied. "Nobody gets to do that to you. Not even to save me from saving you."

He wanted to argue. He searched himself for arguments the way he had once searched battlefields for survivors, and found none that she had not already mapped and annotated.

So instead he said: "Then tell me what you actually need from me. The real plan. Not the one you'll feed it tomorrow."

And Wendell Nakamura lifted her head, and for the first time since the stars went out, her eyes looked less afraid than hungry.

---

* * *

The next surfacing came at dusk, two days before the appointed meeting at the shrine. Aldric woke in the map-room chair with Wendell cross-legged on the floor beside him, unrolling paper, and a guard outside who had been paid enough to develop sudden deafness.

"The binding," she said, without preamble. "Talk me through it. Slowly. Like I'm an idiot."

"You're not—"

"Humor me. It puts you in a good mood, and I need you in a good mood, because what I'm about to ask of you is going to feel like dying a little."

So he talked. It was strange, describing the inside of his own captivity — like being asked to survey a house he was buried under. The crown anchored at seven points, he told her. Seven old starlights, seven nails. One behind each eye. One at the base of the throat. One over the heart. Two at the temples. And the seventh—

"The seventh is the strangest," he said. "It isn't in my body at all. It's in my *name.* When it wants me obedient, it doesn't grip my mind. It says my name. And I go."

Wendell had stopped drawing. She sat very still, the way she did over a map that had just revealed a coastline where the charts promised open sea.

"Say that again," she whispered.

"When it says my name, I—"

"No — the seventh anchor. In the name." She was already writing, fast, her pen tearing slightly at the page. "Aldric, do you understand what this means? Six anchors are geography. I can't reach those while it wears you. But the seventh — the seventh is *cartography.* Names are borders. A name is the oldest map there is — it draws a line around a self and says *here, this far, this much, this person.*" She looked up at him, lit from inside. "If the crown holds you by your name, then your name is a door. And I have spent my entire life learning to draw doors."

"My name," Aldric said slowly, "is four letters and a family curse."

"All names are small. Kingdoms fit in them. Ask any king." A ghost of her gentle humor surfaced. "Ask you."

He laughed — actually laughed, a rusty, astonished sound, the first in longer than he could count — and the laugh cost him something, because the crown stirred at the edges of him like a sleeper disturbed, and he felt it take note of the warmth.

"Quickly," he said. "What do you need?"

"To write the unbinding, I need the true borders of the thing holding you — and I need yours. Your name, freely given, written in your own intent. Not spoken *to* me. Given *through* me. Can you do that? Push it down the bond, along the anchor, the way it pushes commands down into you?"

"I don't know."

"Try. Small at first. Think your name at me like you're angry at it."

He closed his eyes. He reached for *Aldric* — not the crown's Aldric, not the Regent's puppet, but the boy who had once fallen out of a pear tree and blamed the pear tree; the young king who had signed his first treaty and misspelled his own chancellor's title and been laughed at, kindly, forever after. He gathered all of it, the whole small ridiculous beloved territory of himself, and he pushed.

Wendell gasped. Her pen flew.

"Oh," she breathed. "Oh, it's beautiful. You're — hold on, hold on, don't stop—" Pages turned. Ink flew. "There's so *much* of you. They told me kings were mostly ceremony with a man attached somewhere near the bottom—"

"Flatterer."

"Cartographer. It's different." But her voice cracked on the word, and when he opened his eyes she was crying without appearing to notice, drawing as she wept, line after sure line.

The crown rose in him like floodwater. He felt the six anchors tighten, felt the Regent turn its attention inward with the mild curiosity of a landlord hearing noise upstairs.

"Time?" he managed.

"Almost. I have the frame. I have your borders." She was rolling the pages with shaking hands, hiding them in the false spine of a road atlas. "One more thing, and then you must go under and pretend, pretend, pretend until the shrine. Tomorrow, when it offers the trade — I will say yes. Whatever it does to your face while I say it, whatever words it puts in your mouth, you remember: *it's a map.* All of this is a map, and maps can be redrawn. Trust the lines."

"And if the lines fail?"

She kissed him then, fierce and brief, sealing another letter.

"Then I'll redraw them," she said, "and charge the crown for the ink."

The flood took him. The last thing he saw, from the bottom of the drowning dark, was Wendell slipping out the door with an atlas full of treason under her arm — and the last thing he heard was the Regent, deep in its cold interior, murmuring with satisfaction:

*"Yes. Come to the shrine, little heir. Bring your blood. Bring everything."*

Aldric sank, and held his name like a stone, and waited for morning.
