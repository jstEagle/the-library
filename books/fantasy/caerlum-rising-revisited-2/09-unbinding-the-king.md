## 9. Unbinding the King

The shrine had been a border once. Wendell could still read its bones: the foundation stones laid in a perfect line, the waystations where ward-smiths had once stamped their signatures into the world, the great arch that had let travelers pass from one country of weather into another. Now frost had eaten it to lacework, and the snow fell upward in places, confused about which way the sky had gone.

She spread her tools on the altar stone with the care of long habit. Compass. Ink. The blank map she had carried for three hundred miles inside her coat, next to her heart, where it had grown warm as a second pulse.

Aldric stood at the edge of the shrine, and he was not wearing the crown.

That was the thing that kept catching her breath. He'd pried it off at the last waystation—she'd watched him do it, watched his hands shake and his face go white as the thing fought him, watched him win anyway—and now it sat wrapped in ward-cloth at the center of the altar, humming like a hive of cold bees. Without it he looked younger. He looked *hungry*, in the way of a man who had been fed on for years and had only just remembered what appetite was.

"You're stalling," he said.

"Cartographers don't stall. We *survey*."

"You've surveyed that altar four times."

"It's a complicated altar." She smoothed the blank map flat. Her hands were steady. She'd made them steady through sheer professional stubbornness, and she was quietly proud of it. "Aldric. If I do this wrong—"

"You won't."

"—if I do this wrong, it takes me instead of you, and you spend the rest of a very long reign wearing a glove made of my hands, and I would rather be flayed."

"Then don't do it wrong." He crossed the snow and took her face in his hands. His palms were warm now. She kept noticing that, kept being astonished by it, the way a person who has lived in a cold house keeps being surprised by sunlight. "Wendell. You drew a map of a city you'd never seen from a merchant's drunk description and it was *accurate*. You are the best mapmaker I have ever met, and I have met the ones in the histories."

"The ones in the histories had better ink."

"The ones in the histories never mapped themselves." His thumb traced her cheekbone, and she felt it like a line being drawn. "No one ever has. That's why it will work. It's also why you can't practice."

"I'm aware of the drafting problem, thank you."

He laughed—short, helpless, the laugh of a man holding terror at arm's length. "There she is. My cartographer."

*His*. She let the word sit in her chest a moment, warm as the map against her heart. Then she took out her knife.

* * *

The first cut was the hardest, and not because of the pain.

She'd cut herself before—every cartographer had, pricking a thumb for a signature, a drop of blood to seal a charter. But this was different. This was a *line*, drawn from the inside of her left wrist down across her palm, and where the blood welled it did not fall. It rose. It hung in the air in a thin bright thread, and it waited for her.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, that's what the ward-smiths were."

The blood remembered. Of course it did. Blood was the oldest ink there was, and hers had been signing borders since before borders had names.

She began to draw.

She drew the shape of her hands first—the hands that had held every impossible ledger, that had ink-stained themselves honest over twenty years of other people's secrets. She drew the road of her spine. She drew the harbor of her heart, and did not flinch from what was anchored in it. She drew the half of her that was guild and the half that was something older, the two coastlines that had never touched, and where they met she drew not a border but a *shore*—a place where two bodies of water agree to be one sea.

The map took shape above the altar, glowing, and it was not a map of a woman. It was a map of a *country*, small and fierce and entirely hers, and the crown-thing felt it before she finished the first coastline.

The cold came down like a verdict.

* * *

Aldric felt it leave the wrapped cloth and rise, and for one heartbeat—one long, terrible heartbeat—he understood exactly what Wendell had been carrying for weeks: the sensation of something vast looking through your eyes.

*There you are,* the thing said, and its voice was starlight stretched thin, beautiful and starving. *Jailer's heir. Little map of a little country. I wore your ancestors' work for a thousand years. I will wear yours longer.*

"You'll try," Wendell said through her teeth. Her blood-map blazed above her. "But I've read your contract, your majesty. Corvel's ledger, entry one. *Sky, whole, traded for a century's neutrality.* You took payment. You're *bound* by the trade. And I am here to collect."

She opened her arms to it.

Aldric shouted her name. She heard it from very far away, because the thing was entering her now, and it was like being read—every line of her surveyed, appraised, *claimed*. It poured into the map of herself and found, to its ancient fury, that the map had no empty spaces. She had left none. She had mapped even her fear, even her guilt, even the strange relief she'd felt the night the stars went out, and there was nowhere for it to wear her.

So it began to *tear* her.

* * *

He should have run. He knew that. He was free—the crown was off him, the thing was busy, the ward was collapsing around the shrine in sheets of falling light—and a king's duty was to live, to reign, to be the country's anchor from the far side of the danger.

Aldric looked at the collapsing ward, and at Wendell inside it, burning like a drawn line, and made the decision his whole reign had been training him for.

He stepped into the light.

It took his breath and gave it back colder. The collapsing ward was a country coming apart at its borders, and he walked into it as if it were a hall he owned, because in every way that mattered, he did. He reached Wendell and took her bleeding hand, the one that had drawn the map, and he did the one thing no one had ever done for her.

He named her.

"Wendell Nakamura," he said, and the ward caught the words and made them true. "Cartographer. Keeper of impossible ledgers. Daughter of two coastlines. *Mine.*" His voice cracked and he let it. "I know you. I have known you since a dream I never had. You are not invisible. You have never been invisible. You are the only map I ever wanted to read to the end."

Inside her, the thing screamed.

Because that was the flaw in its ancient hunger, the flaw it had never understood: it fed on the *unmapped*. It wore the unnamed and the hidden and the erased. And Wendell Nakamura, half-blood, invisible keeper of secrets, was being named—line by line, love by love, word by word—into a country too solid to wear.

"Wendell," Aldric said, and pulled her forehead to his. "Come home. I'm holding the border. Come home."

She came home.

She felt it happen—felt herself gather along her own coastlines like a tide returning, felt the thing squeezed between the shores of her until it had nowhere left that was not *her*. It shrieked in a voice like a star going dark, and then it did the only thing left to a creature of the unmapped when it is thoroughly mapped.

It *condensed*. It poured out of her, out of the ward, out of the world's edges, and it fell—coiling, shrinking, cursing in a language older than winter—onto the blank parchment at the center of the altar, the one map she had never drawn on.

The map that was waiting to be filled.

Aldric's hands found hers. Together, bleeding, laughing, crying, they wrote two names across the parchment's border, sealing it: *Wendell Nakamura* and *Aldric, son of Caerlum*—and the thing inside went still, and the stars—

The stars *lit*.

One, then ten, then a sky's worth, slamming back into the heavens like lamps relighting in a house long dark. The snow stopped falling upward. Somewhere far away, a border thawed with a sound like a great held breath finally released.

Wendell collapsed against Aldric in the ruins of the shrine, both of them steaming in the sudden, astonishing cold-that-was-only-cold. Above them the stars wheeled, whole and indifferent and beautiful.

"Your majesty," she managed, "I believe your sky is back."

"Keep it," he said into her hair. "I'll trade you. Sky for a mapmaker."

She was still laughing when the light of the stars caught the sealed map on the altar—and showed her, faint but unmistakable, a new line appearing at its edge. A line she had not drawn.

Someone else was writing on the map of the crown-thing's prison.

And the handwriting looked terribly, heartbreakingly familiar.
