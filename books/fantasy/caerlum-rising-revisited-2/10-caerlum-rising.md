## 10. Caerlum Rising

The handwriting on the sealed map was Master Corvel's.

Wendell knew it the way she knew the grain of every desk in the guild vault—by its habits. The way his letters leaned eastward like travelers hurrying home before dark. The way his *r*s carried little flags, as though even his alphabet saluted something. She had spent twenty years reading that hand in the margins of ledgers: *verified*, *noted*, *do not pursue*. And now it was writing on the prison of a god.

"Aldric," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "We have a problem shaped exactly like my mentor."

He was already on his feet, one hand pressed to his bare chest where the crown had sat for three years, as if checking that the cold hadn't crawled back in. "The broker you told me about? The one who sold the sky?"

"The same." She lifted the map from the altar. The new line at its edge was small—a single word, half-formed, as if written by someone with very little strength left and very much to say. It read: *if*.

"Just 'if'?" Aldric asked.

"That's how Corvel begins everything. Contracts, apologies, arguments. 'If I may. If you'll permit me. If there were another way.'" Wendell traced the letter with a fingertip, careful not to add ink of her own. "It means he's still alive. And he's still bargaining."

The stars wheeled overhead, fat and golden and shamelessly bright after their long absence, and Wendell felt them on her skin like sunlight through a window she'd forgotten the house had. For three years—or had it been longer?—the night had been a black lid over the world. Now it was a ceiling again. A roof meant to keep weather out, not hope.

"Then we'd better find out what he's offering," Aldric said, "before he finds someone worse than us to sell it to."

* * *

They rode north through a kingdom remembering itself.

The thaw was not gentle—that was the first thing Wendell put in her field notes, because accuracy mattered even in joy—but it was *thorough*. Ice groaned off rooftops in slabs and shattered in courtyards where children screamed with delight and immediately began throwing the pieces at each other. Rivers shrugged off their winter skins all at once, flooding low pastures, and farmers stood knee-deep in their own drowned wheat laughing like madmen, because drowned wheat could be replanted, and frozen wheat could not.

In the village of Harrowmere, which they had fled through in Chapter Five of this misadventure when it had been frozen mid-moment—a woman mid-laugh, a dog mid-leap, a dropped loaf suspended an inch above the cobbles—the ice had let go all at once. The loaf had fallen. The dog had landed. The woman had finished her laugh three years late and then sat down hard in the snow and cried until her neighbors gathered round.

Aldric dismounted to speak with her. Wendell watched him do it—the king of Caerlum, mud to the knees, holding a weeping baker's wife by both hands—and understood something about crowns she hadn't known before. That the good ones were just people who remembered they were people. That Aldric had spent three years being worn like a glove and had come out of it still willing to kneel in thaw-water for a stranger.

She wrote that down too. Some things belonged in atlases.

"You're smiling," Aldric said, catching up with her at the village well. "You only smile like that when you've found something."

"I'm making notes for a new atlas," she said. "Working title: *Caerlum, Actual Size*."

"And what does the old atlas say?"

"The old atlas says nothing about me." She said it lightly, and found—to her genuine surprise—that it was true. Lightly, I mean. "That's rather the point of the new one."

* * *

The Guild of Cartographers and Improbable Trades occupied a building that looked, from the street, like three buildings that had lost an argument and agreed to share a coat. Wendell had loved it her whole life the way you love a house with drafts: knowing its flaws, keeping its secrets.

Now she walked up its steps with a king beside her, and the doors opened before she could knock, and every guildsman in the entry hall went silent in a way that made her skin prickle. Not fear of Aldric, she realized. Fear of *her*.

Of course. She had drawn herself in blood. Every ward-smith's descendant within a hundred miles had probably felt it like a bell rung in their teeth.

"Master Nakamura," said the junior archivist Pellam, who had once hidden sweets in the ledger shelves for her and now looked as if he might faint or curtsy or both. "We—we weren't sure you'd—"

"I'm not a master," Wendell said. "I'm not anything the guild has a title for. Which is, historically, the position I've always held." She held up the sealed map. "Where is Corvel?"

Pellam's eyes went to the map and stayed there, wide as coins. "Vault nine," he whispered. "He's been in vault nine since the night the wards fell. He won't eat. He won't sleep. He just... writes."

* * *

Corvel had aged a decade in a season, and it had nothing to do with time.

He sat at the great drafting table in the deepest vault, surrounded by ledgers stacked into fortifications, his pen moving across parchment with the desperate steadiness of a man bailing out a boat. He did not look up when they entered. His hand kept writing. *If. If. If.*

"You should have stopped at the shrine," he said. "I taught you better operational security."

"You taught me to hide maps inside other maps," Wendell said. "You never taught me to stop drawing."

"No." Something in his shoulders sagged, and the pen slowed. "That was the flaw in the curriculum."

He turned at last, and Wendell saw what the starlight-thing had left in him—not possession, nothing so dramatic. Just cost. The trade of sky had been drawn from him the way blood is drawn: legally, cleanly, and leaving a mark that would never quite close. His eyes were the gray of old paper.

"I bought the guild a century," he said. "Neutrality guaranteed, borders unmolested, our people safe behind whatever king the thing decided to wear. One sky against ten thousand lives. I ran the numbers, Nakamura. I ran them for six days without sleeping, and the numbers said trade."

"The numbers didn't count me," Wendell said. "Or Aldric. Or Harrowmere's bread. Numbers never do. That's why I draw maps instead."

"Yes." And here Corvel did the thing that broke her heart, which was to nod, slowly, as though she had confirmed a finding he'd suspected for years and lacked the courage to publish. "Yes, that's the whole of it. You were the error term I couldn't account for. The jailer's heir, hiding in my own vault, shelving my own ledgers, invisible even to me." He laughed—a dry, terrible sound. "I thought I was saving you. Do you know how insulting it is, Nakamura, to be saved badly?"

"Frequently," said Aldric.

Corvel looked at the king for a long moment. Then he pushed back his chair, stood—stiffly, like a man whose joints had been replaced with contract clauses—and bowed. Not deep. Honest.

"Your majesty. I am prepared for judgment. I ask only that the ledgers burn in front of me, so I can watch the arithmetic go."

"They'll burn," Aldric said. "But judgment isn't mine alone to give. There are villages under that ice who'll want a word. You'll face them. All of them. That will take longer than any execution, and hurt considerably more."

Corvel nodded again, and Wendell realized with a start that he was relieved. Punishment, for a man like him, was simply a ledger balancing at last.

"One thing more," Corvel said, and his gaze dropped to the map under Wendell's arm. "The word I wrote. On your seal."

"*If*," Wendell said.

"It wasn't a bargain. It was a question. *If* the seal holds. *If* the thing can be kept rather than killed. Because—" and here his pen-fingers twitched toward the ledgers, the habit of a lifetime, "—a prison is a trade too, Nakamura. Someone has to keep the books on it. And I would very much like to spend what's left of my life doing honest accounting."

Wendell looked at Aldric. Aldric looked at her. Somewhere above them, through nine floors of vault and stone, the newly returned stars were coming up over Caerlum like a city lighting its lamps.

"We'll need a cartographer's oversight," she said. "Someone to check the prison-map for wear. Redraw the seals as they fade. It's tedious work."

"I excel at tedious work."

"It doesn't pay."

"I have embezzled nothing," Corvel said, "which by guild standards makes me unusually wealthy."

She almost smiled. She didn't quite forgive. But she unrolled the map on the great table between them, and the two of them bent over it together, mentor and student, jailer's heir and sky-seller, checking the seals line by line while a king kept watch at the door.

* * *

The burning of the ledgers took place in the guild courtyard on the first properly warm evening anyone could remember, and it was, Wendell reflected, the strangest funeral she had ever attended—for no one died, and everyone was reborn.

Ledger after ledger went into the fire: the trades of impossible things, the prices paid in memory and moonlight and other people's names. Pellam wept openly. Half the guild cheered. An elderly surveyor threw in her own personal copy of the *Index of Forbidden Coordinates* and then immediately tried to retrieve it and had to be restrained.

Aldric stood at Wendell's shoulder in plain clothes—plain clothes! she kept wanting to write in the margin; *the king owns shirts without embroidery, more research needed*—and when the last ledger caught, he spoke just loudly enough for the courtyard to hear.

"This guild sold the sky. This guild also raised the woman who stole it back. Both things are true, and neither cancels the other, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something." A ripple of uncomfortable laughter. "So the guild stands. Rebuilt. Under new rules, the first of which is that nothing in this building may ever be traded that belongs to someone else—including themselves."

His eyes found Wendell's across the firelight, and she understood that the last sentence had been aimed like an arrow loosed gently, so it could be caught.

Later—much later, when the embers were pink and the crowd had gone home and the stars were fully, gloriously out—they sat on the guildhouse roof with a bottle of plum wine and the first blank pages of the new atlas spread between them.

"So," Aldric said. "Caerlum, actual size. Where does it begin?"

Wendell dipped her pen. She thought about borders—about the ones she'd drawn all her life and the one she'd finally drawn in blood, the map of herself that had unmade a god. She thought about the fact that her name was now in two royal documents, three guild registers, and one prison-seal, and that none of those versions canceled the others either.

"Here," she said, and drew a single point on the page, and labeled it not with coordinates but with two names: hers and his. "Everything else radiates outward from this."

"That's not how cartography works."

"It's how *my* cartography works. I'm setting the standard."

He laughed, and leaned his head against hers, and the stars came up over Caerlum like a promise kept at last.

And far below them, in the deepest vault, on a sealed map guarded by a reformed traitor, the imprisoned crown-thing stirred against its borders—and found, to its ancient fury, that the lines holding it had been redrawn overnight. Not by Wendell's hand. Not by Corvel's.

By a third hand entirely, in handwriting neither of them had ever seen—

and every stroke of it was signed, in tiny patient letters, *the jailer's heir, and the heir after her.*
