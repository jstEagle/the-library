## 1. The Godless Quarter

The bells of Greyhallow rang the hour of dawn, and Tamsin Thornbury counted them the way other people counted breaths—without thinking, without wanting to, because the counting had become a part of them the way a scar becomes part of skin.

Seven bells. Seven for the Vigilant Wake, seven for the Inquisition's morning rounds through the Godless Quarter. Tamsin knew the rhythm by heart: two tolls from the Spire of Law, then one from each of the ward chapels, staggered so that no silence ever quite settled over the city. Greyhallow did not like silence. Silence was where magic hid. Silence was where gods had once spoken, before the Edicts of Ash had strangled every altar and drowned every hymn in the harbor.

Tamsin pulled the shutters open on their little shop—the width of a coffin, the depth of a grave—and let the grey light of morning spill across the counter. The sign above the door read *Thornbury's Charms & Curios* in paint that had faded to the color of old milk. Beneath it, in smaller letters someone else had painted as a joke or an insult: *Harmless Since the War.*

It was not wrong.

Tamsin arranged the day's wares with hands that remembered finer work: knotted cords to ward off bad dreams, iron nails wrapped in red thread against spite and ill-wishing, river-stones drilled and hung on leather thongs for safe travel. A bowl of salt. A bundle of dried rue. Little glass vials of what the label swore was *bottled luck*, which was really rainwater collected from the eaves and a pinch of glittering mica ground fine.

None of it worked. That was the point.

In the years after the war, when the Edicts came down like an axe, the Inquisition had made its distinctions very clear. True magic—prayer that answered, sigils that burned, names of power spoken into the marrow of things—was death. But the small superstitions, the charms and knots and lucky stones, those were permitted. The common people needed their comforts, and the Vigilant Wake understood that comfort, drained of power, was harmless as a toothless dog. So the charm sellers of the Godless Quarter were licensed, taxed, inspected, and left alone, selling hollow things to hollow-hearted folk who needed to believe *something* was watching over them.

Tamsin had been licensed for four years. Before that, there were five blank years the tax rolls did not ask about, and before *that*—

Before that, there had been Caerlum.

Tamsin's hands went still over the bowl of salt. Outside, the market was waking: the groan of cart wheels on cobbles still wet with fog, fishwives crying the night's catch, the clatter of the tinker setting up his stall three doors down. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a city that had decided, collectively and with great relief, that the age of wonders was over and good riddance.

"Charms today, charm seller?" called Marta Ossel from her pie stall across the lane, where she was stoking her oven with the first coals of the morning. She was sixty if she was a day, with forearms like ship rope and a laugh that could be heard from the fish market. "My knees say rain. Got anything for knees?"

"I've got a knot for aching joints," Tamsin called back. "And a stone for patience."

"Ha! I'd need a boulder for patience, living in this quarter." Marta waved a floury hand. "Two knots, then. And don't cheat me with the small ones."

"No cheating," Tamsin said. "The Wake inspects."

Marta laughed again and turned back to her oven, and Tamsin tied the knots—overhand, underhand, the pattern worn into their fingers from ten thousand repetitions—and tried not to think about the fact that once, these same fingers had drawn sigils in blood and ash that had split the sky over the Vaelmark like rotten silk. Once, these hands had held a sworn-blade that drank light. Once, they had knelt in a shrine full of singing and felt the presence of something vast and warm and *attentive* settle over them like a mantle, like a hand on the crown of their head, saying without words: *You are mine, and I am yours, and you will never walk alone.*

Once.

Now the knots were just knots. The salt was just salt. And the god—

The god was quiet.

Five years of quiet. Five years of prayers falling upward like stones dropped into a well that had no bottom, no water, no echo coming back. Tamsin had prayed in shrines and in gutters, in chains and in freedom, at dawn and at midnight, with words and without them, until the praying wore itself out like a shoe worn through at the heel. The god did not answer. The god did not even *not*-answer in a way that meant anything. There was simply nothing where something had been—a wound in the shape of a voice, aching on certain nights when the moon was thin and the wind came down off the mountains smelling of snow.

The city said the gods were dead. The Inquisition said the gods had been *removed*, like tumors, like teeth, for the good of all. The priests who still dared to gather in cellars said the gods slept, or fled, or waited. Tamsin had stopped listening to any of them. Tamsin sold knots and stones and bottled nothing, and paid the license fee on the first of every month, and kept their head down and their past locked behind their ribs where no Inquisitor's key could reach it.

Because here was the truth that would burn Tamsin Thornbury alive if it ever saw daylight: they had not merely believed in Caerlum. They had been *sworn*. Sworn blade of the Order of the Unbroken Vigil, marked with the god's own sigil in ink mixed with ash and their own blood, in a place on the body that clothes could hide but the Wake's bone-readers could find in half a heartbeat if they ever got a hand on bare skin. There were perhaps a dozen sworn blades left alive in all the nine kingdoms, and the Inquisition had spent five patient years hunting them down one by one—not because they were dangerous anymore, not since the god went silent and their blades drank no more light—but because of what they *represented*. Because a living sworn blade was proof that the old world had been real. That the miracles had happened. That the Edicts were built on the graves of things that had genuinely existed and might, some terrible morning, exist again.

So Tamsin sold charms that didn't work, and kept the mark hidden under three layers of cloth and a leather bracer they never removed, and was nobody, harmless since the war.

It was almost enough. It had to be enough. It was certainly all there was.

* * *

Midmorning brought the customers.

They came in the usual trickle: a young wife wanting a cord against nightmares for her husband, who had come back from the border levies with eyes that wouldn't close; a fisherman's boy with a copper bit and a solemn request for luck with eels; a widow in grey who bought the same mourning-knot every month and never said for whom, though everyone in the quarter knew her son had been taken by the Wake three winters past for humming an old hymn in his sleep.

Tamsin treated them all with the same careful warmth—warm enough to keep them coming, distant enough that no one ever thought about Tamsin Thornbury twice. That was the craft of it, the true craft beneath the knot-tying: invisibility. You learned it the way you'd once learned swordwork, drilling it daily until it lived in the sinew. You kept your answers short and your smile modest. You let the neighbors think of you as slightly dull, slightly poor, slightly sad—the charm seller whose wares never quite seemed to do anything but who charged fair prices anyway. You gave the Wake's inspectors nothing to look at: a clean shop, a current license, ledgers written in a deliberately mediocre hand. When the bone-reader had come last spring, running his cold fingers along the arms of every licensed vendor in the quarter, Tamsin had stood in line with heart hammering slow and deep like a drum in another room, bracer snug beneath the sleeve, and had watched the man pass them by with eyes like a butcher appressing carcasses, finding nothing worth the knife.

Nobody. Harmless. Invisible.

It should have been peace. Some days it even was—or close enough that Tamsin could pretend.

Today, the widow in grey lingered after buying her knot, turning it over in her spotted hands.

"They say there was a god once," she said, not looking up. "Out east, past the marshes. A god of watchfires and keeping. My grandmother prayed to him. Said he always answered. Even the smallest prayer, even a child's."

Tamsin's hands, wiping the counter, did not stop moving. This was also craft: never stillness, never a flinch. Stillness was a door and grief walked through open doors.

"Old stories," Tamsin said mildly. "Half the gods people remember, they invented after the fact. Easier than admitting we were alone all along."

"Mm." The widow tucked the knot into her sleeve. Her eyes, when they lifted, were pale and shrewd and terribly tired. "Maybe. Or maybe he's still out there. Maybe he's just—" she searched for the word, and found it, and said it gently, the way you'd set down something fragile, "—quiet."

She paid her coin and shuffled out into the noise of the market, leaving Tamsin standing in the little shop with the smell of dried rue and the ghost of a word hanging in the air like smoke.

*Quiet.* Not dead. Not gone. Quiet—as if the god had only stepped into the next room. As if any moment now, the silence might end and the voice come flooding back, and everything Tamsin had lost would return like a tide, and they would be somebody again, sworn and seen and never alone—

Tamsin pressed both palms flat against the counter until the wood bit into skin, until the ache of it crowded out the other ache, the deeper one, the one that had no name they were willing to speak.

Stop it. Five years, and you still do this. He is not in the next room. There is no next room. There is this shop, and this counter, and these knots, and tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow.

The bell over the door chimed. A customer. Tamsin straightened, smoothed their face into its shopkeeper's mildness, and turned to sell another piece of harmless nothing to another believer in small comforts.

* * *

The trouble started, as trouble in Greyhallow always did, with the sound of boots.

Not the ordinary boots of the market—soft shoes, wooden clogs, the scuff of the poor. These were the hard-heeled cadence of the Vigilant Wake on patrol, four sets of feet moving in step down the lane, unhurried, inevitable. Every head in the market turned and then, carefully, turned away. Stallholders became fascinated by their goods. Conversations died mid-word. The whole street performed the same practiced pantomime of indifference, the way a field of grass lies flat under wind.

Tamsin's hands went still on the counter. Then, with deliberate slowness, resumed.

Four was a large patrol for the Godless Quarter. Two was routine—a pair of inspectors walking their circuit, checking licenses, reminding everyone they were watched. Four meant purpose. Four meant they were looking for something. Or someone.

Through the window, Tamsin watched them come: four figures in the slate-grey coats of the Wake, visored helms hiding their faces, the leader carrying not the usual ledger-slate but a long rod of black iron banded with silver—the seeker-rod, which hummed near active sigils, near hidden relics, near the residue of true magic. It hung silent at his side. For now.

The patrol stopped three doors down, at the tinker's stall. The tinker—a round, cheerful man named Pem, who had sold Tamsin the glass vials for years—stood up from his awning, smiling his salesman's smile, and began to answer questions Tamsin couldn't hear. The leader raised the seeker-rod. Passed it slowly over the tinker's trays of spoons and kettles and cheap tin whistles.

Nothing hummed. Of course nothing hummed. Pem was exactly what he appeared to be, which was the safest thing anyone in the Godless Quarter could possibly be.

But the patrol did not move on. They kept Pem there in the grey afternoon light, asking their questions, while the market bent around them like water around a stone, and Tamsin stood in the coffin-width of the shop and understood, with the cold clarity that had once saved their life a hundred times on battlefields that no longer officially existed, that the shape of the day had changed.

This was how it had begun in the other quarters, the ones that weren't as quiet as this one. First a patrol with a seeker-rod, stopping too long. Then questions at odd hours. Then a name read aloud from a list. Then shutters nailed closed and chalk marks on doors and families standing in the street with their belongings in bundles, watching soldiers carry out whatever the rod had hummed for.

Tamsin looked down at their own hands—clean, calloused, unremarkable hands—and at the counter with its rows of dead charms, and at the bracer under the sleeve that covered the one thing in this entire city that would make the seeker-rod sing.

*You are nobody,* Tamsin told themselves, the old litany, the survival-prayer that had replaced all others. *You sell knots. Your license is current. Your ledgers are dull. Nobody.*

The patrol moved on at last, boots ringing away toward the fish market, taking their silence with them. The market exhaled. Marta Ossel laughed too loudly at something. Pem sat back down at his stall and wiped his forehead with a rag, smiling his ruined smile.

Tamsin let out a breath and reached up to turn the shop's sign to *closed*, because the light had gone the amber of early evening and their hands wanted something useful to do, and because—though they would never have admitted it, even to the empty room—they wanted the door between themselves and the street, wanted wood and iron and the smell of rue between their body and the wide watching world.

The sun was setting behind the spires of the Vigilant Wake when Tamsin heard, above the sounds of the market closing—the carts, the cries, the scrape of awnings folding—a different sound underneath.

Footsteps. Uneven. Dragging. Coming fast.

And then the door burst open, and the evening filled with blood.
