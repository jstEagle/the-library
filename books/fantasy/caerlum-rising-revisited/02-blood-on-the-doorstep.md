## 2. Blood on the Doorstep

The bell above the door of Thornbury's Charms & Curios had not rung all day, which suited Tamsin fine. Silence was currency in Greyhallow, and Tamsin had grown rich in it.

They spent the last hour of daylight counting stock that did not need counting: bundles of dried rowan tied with red thread, wax seals stamped with the sigil of Saint Verren's vigil, little knotted cords sold as "traveler's blessings" for a copper apiece. All of it harmless. All of it dead. Once—five years and a lifetime ago—Tamsin could have whispered into those knots and felt something answer, warm as breath against the palm. Now the charms were only string and superstition, and superstition was the one trade Greyhallow permitted.

The city had made its peace with magic the way a man makes peace with a scarred face in the mirror: by never looking directly at it. The Edict of Ash had outlawed the working of spells in Tamsin's grandfather's time, but laws were the least of it. It was the watching that kept folk honest. The Inquisition's gray cloaks moved through the markets like herons through reeds, patient and still, and every citizen had learned to be nothing at all. No prayers spoken aloud. No symbols worn where they might catch light. The old shrines stood gutted across the city like pulled teeth, and people stepped around them without looking, the way you step around a grave you'd rather forget.

Tamsin had gotten very good at being forgotten.

*One more hour,* they thought, turning the sign to CLOSED as the lamplighter's pole came tapping down Lantern Row, *and then bread, and then bed, and then tomorrow exactly like today.* It was a small life. Small lives survived.

The knock came just as the last light died.

Three raps, uneven, weak—the kind of knocking that comes from someone leaning on the door for support. Tamsin's hands went still over the ledger. After dark, no one came to buy charms.

"Closed," they called, keeping their voice flat, bored, the voice of a shopkeeper with nothing worth stealing.

Another knock. Then a sound that raised every hair on Tamsin's neck—a wet, dragging scrape, like a boot failing to find purchase on stone—and a voice, cracked and thin:

"Please. *Please.* I was told—I was told you'd be open."

*Told.* The word landed wrong. Nobody was told anything about Thornbury's Charms & Curios. The shop existed in the city's blind spot precisely because no one spoke of it. Tamsin crossed the floor quietly, lifted the shutter an inch, and looked out into the blue dusk of Lantern Row.

A man stood swaying against the doorframe. He was young—younger than Tamsin—and dressed in the dust-colored traveling clothes of a courier, though the satchel at his side hung open and empty, its strap cut clean through. Clean cut. Blade work. His left hand was pressed flat against his ribs, and even in the poor light Tamsin could see the black shine of blood between his fingers, welling up and spilling over with each breath.

"Saints," Tamsin breathed, and then hated themself for it, because saints were exactly the sort of thing one did not invoke aloud in Greyhallow.

"You're the charm seller." The man's eyes found theirs through the gap, fever-bright and terribly focused. "They said—down by the tanneries—they said if a man needed a charm that *worked*, there was a shop on Lantern Row."

Tamsin's stomach dropped through the floorboards. Someone down by the tanneries had been talking. Someone had been talking about charms that worked, which meant someone remembered, which meant everything Tamsin had built these five careful years was already cracking like river ice.

"I sell knots," Tamsin said. "Blessings for safe roads. Nothing more. You want a physicker, there's one two streets over—"

"They'll find me at the physicker's." The man laughed, and the laugh broke into a cough, and the cough brought fresh blood to his lips. "They're already looking. Please. *Inside.*"

Every sensible instinct Tamsin owned screamed the same word: *no*. Take him in and you take his death in with him. Take his death in and you take the Inquisition's attention, and attention in Greyhallow was a wound that never closed. Five years of invisibility weighed against the life of one bleeding stranger who was, by the look of him, already past saving.

But he had said *please* twice. And when he shifted his weight, his coat fell open, and Tamsin saw beneath it—not a badge, not a sigil of the Conclave or any of the merchant guilds' private guards, but a plain iron pendant on a leather cord. Unadorned. Round as a coin.

The kind of pendant sworn blades wore when they didn't dare wear anything else.

Tamsin opened the door.

The man came through it like a sack of grain, catching himself on the counter, and Tamsin got an arm under him and steered him behind the shop, into the back room where the candle stubs and packing straw lived. He collapsed onto the floor among the straw, and Tamsin knelt beside him and peeled back his hand from his ribs.

The wound was bad. An Inquisitor's blade was never merely sharp; it carried a blessing of finding, a consecration that made flesh slow to knit and blood reluctant to stop. Tamsin had seen such wounds before—in another life, another war—and knew there was nothing here but hours at best.

"Hold still. I'll pack it."

"No time." He caught Tamsin's wrist with shocking strength. His eyes were going glassy, but his grip was iron, and underneath the fear in his face was something harder: purpose. "Listen to me. Listen. My name is Corwin Haleth. I carried dispatches for the Order of the Quiet Vigil, before—" He stopped, swallowed. "You were one of us. I can see it. The way you looked at the pendant. Nobody else looks at it like that anymore."

"I don't know what you mean." The lie came automatically, smooth as oiled leather. Tamsin had told it so many times it had worn grooves in their tongue.

"Then you'll have no trouble forgetting what I give you." He fumbled at his breast with his free hand and drew out a scroll case—oilskin-wrapped, sealed with plain wax, small enough to vanish in a pocket. He pressed it into Tamsin's palm and folded their fingers over it, both of his bloody hands wrapped around theirs like a benediction. "The Vault. They've found the Vault. Do you understand me? The heart is *there*, it was always *there*, and the Conclave means to wake it, and if they wake it—"

"Stop." Tamsin's voice shook. "I don't know what you're talking about. I sell *knots*—"

"He went silent for a reason," Corwin said, talking over them now, faster, the words tumbling as if he could outrun the dark spreading through him. "Everyone thinks the god abandoned us. Everyone thinks Caerlum just... stopped answering, and the world shrugged and moved on. But gods don't go silent, charm seller. Gods are *silenced*." Blood flecked his lips. "And there are men who would tear the silence open and let the war come back through the wound."

Somewhere outside, far off toward the river, a whistle sounded—two notes, then one. Not a lamplighter's call. Lamplighters whistled three.

Corwin heard it too. His grip tightened until Tamsin's bones ached.

"The map shows the way. Only a sworn blade can open the gates—the blood-sigils won't answer anyone else. That's why I ran. That's why I bled." He smiled, and it was the worst thing Tamsin had seen in five years, because it was peaceful. "I couldn't carry it far enough. But you can. You were at Redford Ford. I saw your name on the rolls, years ago. *Thornbury.*"

"How—" Tamsin's mouth had gone dry as ash. "Who else knows?"

"No one living who matters." His eyes drifted, refocusing somewhere past the ceiling beams. "Deliver it. Or burn it. Either way, don't let them—" The whistle sounded again, closer now. Down the street. A lantern's glow swung pale across the shuttered window. "Don't let them have it. Swear it. Swear it on whatever's left of him."

Tamsin should have said no. Should have thrown the scroll case into the stove and dragged this dying fool out onto the street to die where he fell, among strangers, unconnected. Every survival instinct honed across five years of careful nothing demanded it.

Instead they heard themself say: "I swear it."

Whatever was left of him. As if anything was left of him. As if the silence hadn't eaten its fill long ago.

Corwin exhaled, long and shuddering, and his grip finally loosened. "Good," he whispered. "That's... good. He always liked you, you know. Even when you doubted. *Especially* when you doubted." His gaze fixed on Tamsin one last time, and his lips shaped the name—not the god's formal title, not the way the liturgies had named him, *Caerlum-of-the-Long-Watch*, but simply, intimately, the way soldiers said it around night fires:

"*Caerlum.*"

And then he was gone, the word still warm in the air between them, his eyes open on a ceiling he no longer saw.

The lantern glow passed the window. The footsteps paused—paused!—just outside the door, and Tamsin froze among the candle stubs with a corpse cooling against their knee, hardly daring to breathe. A shadow blocked the gap beneath the front shutters. Held. Held. Moved on, unhurried, toward the corner and away.

Tamsin counted to three hundred before moving.

* * *

Afterward, they did not remember deciding. They remembered the motions only, performed in a strange calm, the body executing what the mind refused to think about.

There was a cellar beneath the shop, dug by some previous tenant, reached through a trapdoor hidden under the flour chest. Tamsin had used it once or twice for smuggled goods in the early days, back when Doss's runners still trusted them. Now they half-dragged, half-carried Corwin Haleth down the narrow stairs and laid him on the cold earthen floor, and stood over him in the dark with a hand pressed over their own mouth as if to hold something in.

He had known their name. From the rolls. Which meant the rolls still existed, somewhere, and someone had read them, and someone had sent a boy with cut straps and a mortal wound to the one shop in Greyhallow that shouldn't exist. And the Inquisition was walking Lantern Row tonight, pausing at doors.

Invisibility was over. The thought arrived with terrible clarity, the way ice announces itself underfoot. Whatever happened next—whether they burned the map or followed it, whether they ran tonight or waited to be found—the quiet life was finished. Corwin Haleth had broken it with his dying breath, and Tamsin had let him, had *sworn* him an oath on a god who hadn't spoken in five years, and some traitorous chamber of their heart had thrilled at the words even as terror flooded the rest.

They climbed back up, barred the door, and sat at the counter with the oilskin scroll case in their lap, turning it over and over. Plain wax seal. No sigil. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

Burn it. That was the sane choice. Feed it to the stove and watch the wax bubble, and in the morning open the shop and sell knots and lie low until the gray cloaks lost interest. Let the Conclave and the Inquisition and whoever else wanted a dead god's secrets kill each other over nothing.

But *sworn blade*, the boy had said. *Only a sworn blade can open the gates.*

And beneath the dread, unwelcome as a splinter working toward the bone, was the other feeling. The one Tamsin had spent five years strangling. The one that woke at the sound of their god's name in a dying man's mouth.

*Hope.*

Outside, Greyhallow settled into its nightly hush. Somewhere a dog barked and was silenced. The lamp guttered. And on Lantern Row, in a shop full of charms that no longer worked, Tamsin Thornbury sat alone with a dead man's errand in their hands, knowing—with the particular certainty of someone watching the first snowfall of a long, hard winter—that nothing would ever be quiet again.

They picked up a knife and broke the seal.
