## 3. What the Map Promises

The candle had burned down to a stub by the time Tamsin dared unroll the map.

They had waited, first. Waited while the last light drained from the window over the workbench, while the bells of the Vigilant rang the hour of closing across Greyhallow's grey rooftops, while footsteps in the street below slowed outside the shop door and passed on. Twice. Three times. The dead courier lay beneath the floorboards of the back room, wrapped in his own cloak, and every creak of the settling house sounded like a knock.

Now, at last, they set the candle stub in its dish of tallow and let the flame stand up straight, and they unrolled what the dying man had pressed into their hands.

It was not parchment. That was the first thing. It was vellum so fine it seemed woven from cloud, and it was old—old in a way that made Tamsin's fingers ache just to hold it, the way a scar aches before rain. The edges were soft as cloth. Someone had carried this thing against their skin for years, maybe decades, until the map itself had learned the shape of a body.

And someone had drawn it in blood.

Not ink. Tamsin knew ink; they sold little bottles of it, harmless and honest, beside the charms that no longer worked. This was darker than ink, redder, and where the candlelight caught it at an angle the lines *moved*—a slow shimmer, like something breathing under ice. They had seen that shimmer once before, long ago, in a place they had spent five years trying not to remember.

Tamsin sat down slowly on the stool behind the counter, because their legs had decided the matter for them.

*Blood-sigils.* The old order's script. The script of sworn blades.

Their hands were shaking. They watched them shake with a strange detachment, as if the hands belonged to someone else—some other charm seller in some other city, some other life entirely. Then they flattened one palm against the counter's edge until the trembling stopped, and they made themselves look.

The map showed mountains. A range drawn in quick, confident strokes, each peak named in the blood-script in letters small enough to require leaning close: *Karrowen. The Sister's Tooth. Vael's Crown.* Names out of the old kingdoms, names from before the Reckoning, names that had been scraped off signposts and burned out of records when magic was outlawed and the past became contraband. South of the range, a valley. In the valley, a circle.

Not a building. Not a keep or a temple. Just a circle, drawn twice—the outer ring fine and precise, the inner ring ragged, almost frantic, as if the hand that drew it had been shaking too.

Around the circle, in a careful spiral, ran words. Tamsin read them once and felt the floor tilt.

*Hollow Vault.*

They said it aloud, barely a breath, and the candle flame leaned toward them as if listening.

"No," Tamsin told it, told themselves, told the empty shop with its shelves of useless protections. "No, that's not—that can't be."

But they knew the sigils ringing the name. Knew them the way you know your own handwriting, your mother's voice, the weight of a sword hilt fitted to your grip. The *vane*-mark, the crescent crossed by a blade. The triple knot that meant *oath*. And there, at the very center of the inner ring, half-erased, deliberately defaced by whoever had made this map—a symbol Tamsin had not seen since the day everything ended.

A heart. Drawn anatomically true, veined and chambered, but crowned with fire.

Caerlum's mark.

* * *

The memory came the way it always came—not summoned, never summoned, but rising like water through stone, finding every crack.

They had been twenty-four. Five years ago, though some days it felt like fifty and other days it felt like yesterday, which Tamsin suspected was simply how grief worked: it kept its own calendar.

Spring in the high camp below Vael's Crown. Mud everywhere, mud the color of old iron, and the war going badly in the way wars go badly—not in battles lost but in prayers unanswered. For three years Caerlum had answered. Tamsin could still remember the feeling if they tried hard enough, and most nights they tried not to: the warmth behind the sternum when a prayer left your lips, the sense of being *held*, of ten thousand sworn blades moving as fingers on one hand. The god of hearth-fires and held lines. The god who stood between.

Then the answers had grown thin. Thin, then faint, then—

That morning, Tamsin had knelt at the war-shrine with thirty others and spoken the dawn litany, and the silence afterward had been so complete that a bird landing on the shrine's broken roof had sounded like thunder. Veyra had been beside them, close enough that their shoulders touched. She'd laughed—short, sharp, wrong.

"Say it again," she'd said. "Maybe he overslept."

No one laughed with her. High Marshal Corren had stood at the shrine's head with his fists clenched at his sides, and Tamsin had watched him understand something terrible before any of them did, because commanders always know first. His face hadn't shown fear. It had shown *shame*, which was worse.

"Sworn blades." His voice had carried across the whole camp without effort. "The god is silent. We do not know why. We do not speculate." A pause, and then, quieter, in the voice he used for orders he hated giving: "We do not desert."

They hadn't deserted. They'd done worse. They'd stayed, and fought on, and prayed into a silence so vast it developed texture, weight, weather—and within a season the war had eaten itself. Armies that had moved like one body fell apart into frightened men. The kingdoms signed their exhausted peace, outlawed what they could not explain, and the Inquisition was born to make sure no one ever explained it.

Tamsin remembered the last night of the order. Remembered the fires burning low in the rain, and Veyra sitting across from them, whittling nothing, her knife making small vicious strokes at a stick of wood.

"Corren took the senior blades east," she said. "Before dawn. I saw them go."

"Where?"

"I don't know. But he was crying, Tam. Corren doesn't cry."

She'd looked at Tamsin then across the fire, and her eyes had held something Tamsin hadn't understood until much later—something between an offer and an accusation. *We could go looking. You and me.* But Tamsin had been hollowed out by then, faith bleeding from them like heat from a corpse, and they had only shaken their head and said the coward's line: *There's nothing to find.*

Veyra had smiled. It had not reached her eyes. "No," she'd agreed. "Nothing to find."

Two weeks later she was gone too, and Tamsin had sold their sword in a river town and begun the long apprenticeship in being no one.

* * *

The candle guttered. Tamsin fed it another inch of wick from the spool and realized they had been staring at the same square inch of map for the better part of an hour.

Fine. Fine. So the map was real. So the sigils were real. So somewhere south of the Karrowen peaks there might be a place called the Hollow Vault, marked in the blood of a sworn blade, defaced at its center with the image of Caerlum's burning heart.

None of that meant anything. Dead men carried all kinds of cargo. The courier had been fleeing Inquisitors, and men fleeing Inquisitors would press anything into any hand if it bought them a moment less of pain. Maybe he'd mistaken Tamsin for someone else. Maybe the map was bait. Maybe—

*Why you?*

The thought arrived fully formed, cold as a blade laid flat against the nape of the neck, and Tamsin went very still.

Because that was the question, wasn't it. Of all the doors in all the streets of Greyhallow, the man had chosen theirs. He'd come in at closing, bleeding out, and he'd known—he must have known—to ask for the charm seller on Candle Lane. And when Tamsin caught him, held his head off the boards, he hadn't said *help me* or *she's coming* or even *run*. He'd said:

*"Thornbury."*

His name. Their name—the name they'd buried with the sword, the name no one in Greyhallow had ever spoken, because in this city Tamsin was simply *the charm woman*, or sometimes not even that. And then, with the last air in his ruined chest, the word that had undone five careful years:

*"Caerlum."*

He'd known who they were. He'd sought them out. Which meant someone had told him, which meant someone *knew*, which meant the invisibility Tamsin had spent half a decade weaving around themselves had holes in it—had perhaps always had holes—and somewhere out in the wet dark of the city, other people were reading those holes the way Tamsin was now reading this map.

Hope arrived first. That was the humiliating truth of it. Even now, even knowing better, hope came flooding in ahead of sense like water into a breached hull: *the Vault is real, the god isn't gone, the silence has a reason, there's something to find, you were right not to stop believing—*

Tamsin gripped the edge of the counter until the wood bit.

*Don't.* They knew this trick. Had fallen for it a hundred times in the early years—every rumor of a working miracle, every mad prophet on a street corner claiming to hear the old gods, every night they'd lain awake inventing reasons. Each time the hope had cost more than the disappointment. Faith, it turned out, was a muscle, and it tore more easily the second time.

And underneath the hope, colder and more patient, suspicion unfolded like a hand opening.

*Why you?* Because you were a sworn blade? There had been thousands. Because you survived? Most had. No—if someone wanted a sworn blade of Caerlum found, there was exactly one kind worth choosing: one who had buried the past so deep they'd believe it dead. One whose guilt still ran, quiet and constant, under everything, like groundwater. Someone who would take the map not out of ambition but out of *need*—who would follow it, because following it was the only act of penance left.

Whoever sent that courier had known precisely what Tamsin was. Not their cover. Their wound.

And people who knew your wound could aim at it.

Outside, rain had begun again, ticking softly against the shutters. Down in the back room, beneath the floorboards, a dead man lay wrapped in his cloak with Caerlum's name spent from his lips, and above him the city slept, and somewhere in that sleeping city—inquisitors with patient faces, or worse, *friends* with patient faces—someone was already counting the hours since a courier failed to report.

Tamsin looked at the map a long time. The blood-lines shivered in the candlelight, breathing their slow breath, and the defaced heart at the valley's center seemed to look back.

"You'd better be worth it," they told it quietly. "Because either you're the answer to five years of silence—" their voice cracked, and they hated it, hated the naked want in it— "or you're the hook, and I'm already swallowed."

They rolled the map closed, careful as handling a sleeping snake, and slid it into the false bottom of the charcoal box where they kept the one relic they'd never managed to sell.

Then they blew out the candle and sat in the dark and listened to the rain, and did not sleep at all.

Near dawn, boots in the street below stopped outside the shop.

They did not pass on.
