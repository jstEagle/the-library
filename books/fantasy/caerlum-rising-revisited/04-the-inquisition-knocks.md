## 4. The Inquisition Knocks

The knock came at dawn, and it was not a knock so much as a sentence being read aloud.

Three strikes, measured and unhurried, the way a magistrate taps a gavel—not the frantic hammering of a neighbor or the casual rap of a customer wanting a luck-charm before market opened. Tamsin lay in the narrow bed above the shop, staring at the water-stained ceiling, and knew before their bare feet touched the cold floorboards that the day they had dreaded for five years had finally arrived.

Below, through the floor, the shop waited: its shelves of knotted thread and river-stone amulets, its counter scarred by decades of honest trade, and beneath the loose flagstone near the hearth—the map, wrapped in oilcloth, buried under a false brick that had never once been false until two nights ago. And beneath the floor of the coal cellar, deeper still, the courier himself, folded into earth that smelled of ash and old rain, his blood soaked into Tamsin's own shovel.

Tamsin dressed quickly and badly. The laces of their left boot refused to sit right, and they could not spare the time to fix them. In the grey light seeping through the shutters, they caught their reflection in the cracked mirror by the door—a thin face, sleepless, the eyes of an animal that has heard the hounds but not yet seen them. They looked like what they were pretending to be: a charm seller, tired, unremarkable, harmless. Almost.

*Almost* was the word that would kill them. Tamsin felt it settle in their chest like a swallowed stone.

They banked the fire, set the kettle on, and arranged three cups on the counter—one for themselves, one for any guest, and one that had sat untouched for five years, glazed in Caerlum-blue, kept clean out of a habit that had long since stopped being devotion and become something closer to grief. This morning, they almost hid it. Their hand hovered over it, hesitated—and then left it where it was. Hiding things was how guilt looked. Leaving it out was how innocence looked. They were learning, too late and too fast, the grammar of fear.

Then the door.

* * *

Inquisitor Hale did not look like the stories.

Tamsin had imagined the Inquisition as a wall of black robes and burning eyes, torchlight and chains, voices like iron dragged over stone. What stood in their doorway instead was a woman of perhaps forty, dressed plainly in charcoal wool, her hair pinned back with severe practicality, a leather folio tucked under one arm as though she'd come to discuss a lease. She was not tall. She was not armed—or if she was, the weapon was hidden so well that even Tamsin's old eye for such things found nothing. Her face was unremarkable in the way that well-made locks are unremarkable: you noticed nothing about it until it was too late to matter.

Behind her, on the street, two men in grey livery stood with the patient stillness of furniture. They did not speak. They did not shift their weight. They simply *were*, and their being there said everything their mouths did not.

"Good morning," Hale said. Her voice was dry and even, a page turning. "I'm looking for the keeper of this establishment."

"That would be me." Tamsin stepped back to let her in, and hated themselves for how smoothly they did it—hated the instinct toward courtesy that five years of survival had ground into them like mortar into brick. "Tamsin Thornbury. I sell charms. Charms, mostly. Nothing that—" They caught themselves before the lie finished leaving their mouth, because the first rule of lying to the Inquisition was to never explain unprompted, and here they were, explaining. "Nothing anyone's complained about."

Hale raised an eyebrow. It was a small movement, precise as a scalpel. "No one's complained about anything," she said. "Yet."

She entered the shop the way water enters a hull: quietly, everywhere at once. She did not go straight to the counter. She stood in the middle of the room and let her gaze travel—along the shelves, across the hanging bundles of dried rowan and mugwort, down to the worn threshold stone, up to the smoke-darkened beams. She read the room the way Tamsin had once read battlefields, looking for the thing out of place, the ground that had been disturbed, the silence where sound should be.

And then she saw the third cup.

Tamsin watched her see it. Watched her pause, half a heartbeat, her attention snagging on the blue-glazed cup sitting clean and empty among the clutter of the counter like a flower growing through a crack in pavement. Watched her file it away somewhere behind those flat grey eyes, unremarked upon, unremarkable, *kept*.

"You take tea?" Hale asked mildly.

"When I can afford it." Which was true, and therefore useless, and Tamsin heard the uselessness of it even as they spoke.

"Three cups," she said. "For a shop this size."

"My mother's. I keep her things." Also true. Also useless. Every truth Tamsin offered seemed to make the air in the room heavier, as though honesty itself had weight here, and Hale was weighing all of it.

She poured herself tea without asking—another small transgression, deliberate, a reminder of who held the keys—and settled onto the stool by the counter as though she had sat there a hundred mornings. She held the cup in both hands. She did not drink.

"I'll be direct, Master Thornbury, because I find indirection wastes both our time." She set the folio on the counter but did not open it. "Two nights ago, a man died in this district. A courier. He was found—or rather, he wasn't found; he was reported missing, and the finding came later, and the finding is the part that interests me. He carried something. Whatever he carried is no longer with him."

Tamsin's hands went still around their own cup. They made themselves breathe. In through the nose, slow, the way they'd once steadied their aim before loosing an arrow into a man's throat at the siege of Vael's Crossing—though that memory belonged to another life, another name written in a god's book, and thinking it now felt like touching a stove to check if it was hot.

"I don't know anything about a courier," Tamsin said.

"No?"

"No."

"Mm." Hale turned the cup a quarter-turn on the counter, a small rotation, like the hand of a clock moving toward an hour Tamsin didn't want to face. "Then perhaps you can help me understand something else. Your shop closes at sundown. The bell above your door rings when it opens. The night watch passed this street at the second hour and again at the fourth, and neither patrol reports disturbance. And yet there is blood."

She let the word sit in the room.

"There is no blood," Tamsin said. "I swept."

"You swept." Hale nodded slowly, as if this confirmed something rather than denied it. "Yes. You swept. Do you know what interests me about that, Master Thornbury? Not that you swept. That you knew to sweep. Most honest people, finding blood on their doorstep, send for the watch. They're frightened. They want authority to come and make the frightening thing official, someone else's problem. You did not send for the watch. You took the problem into your own hands. Quietly. Thoroughly. At night."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. The fire had burned down to a red eye in the grate. Somewhere outside, Greyhallow was waking—cart wheels on cobbles, a fishmonger crying his wares two streets over, the bells of the Cathedral of the Unblinking Eye tolling the morning hours, each note falling on the Godless Quarter like a stone dropped down a well. Tamsin listened to those bells the way a condemned man listens to the reading of charges.

"People panic," Tamsin said. "People do strange things when they're scared."

"Do you frighten easily, Master Thornbury?"

"I frighten like anyone."

"No," Hale said, and her voice did not rise, did not sharpen; it only grew quieter, which was worse. "You don't. I've been watching you since I walked through that door. Your hands are steady. Your breathing is even. When I mentioned a dead man, your pulse didn't jump—I can see it in your throat, Master Thornbury, right there, where the skin is thin. You have the stillness of someone who has stood next to death often enough to know its manners." She drank her tea at last, one measured sip. "Charm sellers startle. Soldiers don't."

The word landed like a blade laid flat against bare skin—not cutting yet, but *there*, its edge turned toward Tamsin's throat, waiting only for the smallest motion to draw it home.

"I've never been a soldier," Tamsin said.

"No," Hale agreed. "You've never been a soldier. But you hold your cup like a sword arm rests after drill—loose, ready, angled away from a strike. You scan my escorts through your window before answering the door—you checked the street twice while I stood knocking, I counted. And when I said the word 'courier,' your eyes went, just for a moment, just briefly, to the floor. To the left of the hearth." She smiled, and it was the coldest thing in the room. "To the flagstone."

Tamsin's mouth had gone dry as paper. They reached for the tea and found their hand trembling after all—not visibly, not enough for the watching escorts, but enough. Enough for them. The stillness Hale had praised was a performance, and performances fail, and beneath it the old self was bleeding through like ink through wet cloth: the sworn blade, the killer, the servant of a dead god, rising to the surface of the charm seller's face no matter how hard Tamsin pressed it down.

"The flagstone is uneven," Tamsin said. "It always has been. Ask any mason."

"I intend to." Hale closed her folio. She stood, unhurried, smoothing her coat. "But here is what I actually came to say, since we've established that I waste no one's time. I cannot arrest you today. There is no body. There is no weapon. There is a swept doorstep and a nervous shopkeeper, and neither of those survives contact with a magistrate." She paused by the door, and the light from the street fell across her face, carving it into planes of shadow. "So instead I leave you a warning. Consider it professional courtesy between people who both understand how these things are done."

"A warning."

"Inquisitors are patient, Master Thornbury. We are paid in patience; it is the coin of our order. I will be back. Perhaps tomorrow. Perhaps next week. Perhaps I'll send someone less courteous than myself, because I am, whatever you may think, one of the gentler instruments in the case. And when I return, I will ask my questions again, and you will answer them again, and every time, the lies will cost you more, because lies are debts, and debts compound." She looked at Tamsin the way a falconer looks at a hawk that has not yet decided whether to fly. "Whatever you're carrying, whoever died to bring it to you—it will come out. Things always do. The only question is whether it comes out in a cell, or in a grave, or in a way that I never have to write down at all."

Tamsin said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't a rope to hang by.

"One more thing." Hale leaned close, and for the first time Tasmam saw something flicker behind the flat grey eyes—not warmth, exactly, but a kind of weary recognition, the look of one hunter acknowledging another across an impossible distance. "Whoever that man was, he chose your door. Out of every door in Greyhallow, he bled his way to yours. That is not chance. Chance doesn't crawl across a city to die on a specific threshold. So either you are more than you appear, Master Thornbury—or you were always going to be part of this, and no amount of sweeping will sweep that away." She straightened. "Think on which of those frightens you more."

Then she was gone, and the grey men with her, and the street swallowed them like water closing over stones.

* * *

Tamsin did not open the shop that day.

They sat behind the shuttered window with the lamp unlit, watching the street through the gap in the slats, and the street watched them back—or might have been; that was the poison of it, that was the claustrophobia pressing in like floodwater. Every passerby became a spy. The baker's boy lingering at the corner. The woman in the green shawl who paused to adjust her boot directly across the lane. The cart that rolled past too slowly, its driver's face turned forward, his eyes—Tamsin was nearly certain—turned elsewhere.

By midday they had catalogued forty faces and trusted none of them. By evening they had memorized the rhythm of footsteps on their stretch of cobbles so thoroughly that a change in tempo made their heart slam like a fist on a door. This was what Hale had left them, more lasting than any warning: the inability to feel safe inside their own skin. The Inquisitor hadn't needed chains. She had installed them, invisible, weightless, and walked out, and they were rattling still.

When full dark came, Tamsin lifted the flagstone.

The map was there. Of course it was there. Nothing had touched it. But they unwrapped the oilcloth anyway, kneeling on the cold floor with their heart hammering, and spread the brittle vellum across their knees, and looked at it again by the light of a single candle shielded behind a clay screen—the sigils of the old order marching along its borders like a procession of ghosts, the winding route through hills that had been battlefields when Tamsin last walked them, and at its heart, circled in faded ink gone brown as dried blood, a mark they had known all their life:

The Hollow Vault.

*Here lies what was taken,* the courier had gasped, dying on Tamsin's floor. *Here lies what was taken.*

What was taken. The god's heart. The reason for the silence. The reason the prayers fell upward into nothing, the reason the shrines stood cold, the reason Tamsin had spent five years selling river-stones to frightened people and calling it a life.

And now the Inquisition knew a courier had died carrying something. Hale didn't know *what*. Not yet. But she would come back—she had promised, and Inquisitors were paid in patience—and every visit would tighten the noose by another turn, and Tamsin could not stay, could not run with the whole city watching, could not destroy the map because destroying it meant destroying the only answer they had ever been given to the question that had hollowed them out for five years.

*Why did you leave me?*

The candle guttered. Outside, far off across the rooftops, the Cathedral bells rang the midnight hour, and Tamsin thought of the third cup on the counter, the blue-glazed one, the one Hale had seen and filed away, and understood with sudden terrible clarity that the game had already begun and they were losing.

There was a smuggler named Doss who worked the river gate. Tamsin had heard the name whispered in the market, twice, carefully, the way people handle coins they suspect are cursed. Passage out of Greyhallow. No questions. No names.

Tomorrow, then. Tomorrow they would find him.

Because tonight, lying awake with the map beneath the floor and the Inquisitor's words beneath their ribs, Tamsin finally admitted what they had known since the stranger's blood soaked into their boards: invisibility was over. The only choice left was what shape the ending would take—and whether they would walk into it, or run, or burn the whole careful life down and follow a dead god's stolen heart into the dark.

The candle died. In the dark, the sigils on the vellum seemed almost to move.

Someone else was asking about the Vault. Tamsin didn't know that yet. But the city was already whispering it, and whispers travel faster than soldiers, faster than fear—and sooner than Tamsin could imagine, the whisper would be standing in their doorway wearing a familiar face.
