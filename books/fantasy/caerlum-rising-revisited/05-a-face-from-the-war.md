## 5. A Face from the War

The road into Greyhallow smelled of fish and fear, and Veyra Sarn had always found the combination honest. Cities that pretended to be clean were the ones rotting underneath. Greyhallow at least had the decency to stink of what it was: a port town gone gray with piety, its walls furred with watch-towers, its streets swept daily by patrols whose boots made a sound like slow applause for a god who no longer took the stage.

She came through the Salt Gate in the guise of a wool merchant's factor, which required only a forged seal, a bored expression, and a bribe pressed into the gate-captain's palm with the smooth discretion of long practice. The guards barely looked at her face. That was good. Her face was known in certain circles—not widely, not yet, but enough. In the borderlands she'd left behind, men still crossed themselves with the old signs when they spoke her name, and not out of affection.

Greyhallow didn't know her yet. She intended to keep it that way.

* * *

She spent the first day doing nothing but walking and listening.

That was the discipline Tamsin had never learned—Veyra thought it now without bitterness, or told herself it was without bitterness—and it was the discipline that had kept Veyra alive through the war's last year when braver, better soldiers were dying in ditches with Caerlum's sigil burned into their shields. You walked. You listened. You let the city tell you its shape before you asked anything of it.

And Greyhallow told her plenty.

It told her in the way the fishwives went quiet when a gray-cloaked Inquisitor passed, then resumed their shouting one octave higher, as if volume could prove innocence. It told her in the chalk marks on doorways—warding signs, clumsy and half-remembered, the kind frightened people drew when they half-believed and wholly feared. It told her in the shrine on Cordwainer's Row where someone had left fresh bread before a statue of Caerlum whose face had been chiseled blank by order of the Magistracy, so that the god's mercy now came to them featureless, anonymous, like rain through a broken roof.

Someone still fed a faceless god. Veyra stood in front of that shrine longer than she meant to.

*He isn't listening,* she reminded herself. *He hasn't listened in five years.*

But the bread was fresh. That was the thing about faith; it didn't die when the god did. It just got hungry.

* * *

She found the charm seller's shop on the second day, and finding it cost her almost nothing, which was itself instructive.

A tanner two streets over remembered "the quiet one who sells luck-charms"—a knot of braided cord here, a hagstone there, trinkets blessed by nobody, sold to people desperate enough to pay for placebo. The Inquisition tolerated the shop the way a cat tolerates a spider in the corner: beneath notice. Magic was outlawed in Greyhallow, but a woman selling knotted thread wasn't magic. She was commerce. And commerce was the only sacrament the Magistracy still honored.

Veyra bought a meat pie from a stall across the lane and settled in to wait, because patience was the other thing she'd learned while Tamsin was learning swordwork. They'd been young together—younger than soldiers should be—and even then the difference between them had been plain as a scar. Tamsin fought like a prayer, all forward motion and open heart, believing that skill plus devotion equaled protection. Veyra fought like arithmetic. She counted everything: exits, debts, the exact weight of a promise against the exact weight of a knife.

She counted them now, watching the shop.

*There you are.*

Tamsin Thornbury looked smaller than memory. That was the first cruelty of it. In Veyra's recollection—the recollection she permitted herself, in the hour after midnight, in the dark, alone, which was the only time she opened that particular chest—Tamsin had been all edges and motion, quicksilver in a mail shirt, laughing in the teeth of a charge. The person behind the charm-seller's table moved slowly, carefully, like someone carrying water across ice. Their hands arranged trinkets with the tenderness of a priest arranging relics. Their eyes, when they lifted to scan the street, were the same gray-green as ever, and they swept the crowd the same way they used to sweep a battlefield—looking for threats, looking for friends, finding neither.

Five years, and the war was still written all over them. Just in a different hand.

Veyra bit into her pie and chewed and watched, and felt something in her chest do a slow, treacherous turn that she recognized and despised.

*Don't,* she told herself. *You know what they are now. You've known since the letter.*

The letter had come eight days ago, sealed in gray wax, unsigned, smelling faintly of ash. *The courier is dead. The map survived. Find the bearer.* Fourteen words, and every one of them a hook. The Ashen Conclave did not send letters to ask favors. They sent letters to start clocks.

She had come to Greyhallow to retrieve a map and, if necessary, remove a witness. Those were her instructions, dressed up in the Conclave's velvet language: *recover the asset, ensure the discretion of any secondary parties.* She had translated it the moment she read it. Kill Tamsin Thornbury if they won't give up the map.

She had said yes. Of course she had said yes. Saying yes to the Conclave was the whole architecture of her life these five years—they had pulled her out of the ruin of the war, given her coin, purpose, direction, a new set of vows to replace the ones that had curdled. The Conclave wanted Caerlum's heart, wanted to crown a god they could hold by the throat, and Veyra had convinced herself this was wisdom rather than ambition. Someone would find the heart eventually. Better hands that understood power than hands that merely worshipped it.

Better hers.

But watching Tamsin arrange worthless charms in a window while the afternoon light went amber, Veyra found the arithmetic refusing to balance. On one side: duty, coin, the future the Conclave promised, the new world she'd be helping to build. On the other side: a memory of Tamsin standing back-to-back with her in the mud at Redford Crossing, bleeding from the ear, screaming laughter, saying *I've got you, Vey, I've always got you*—

She threw the rest of the pie to the gulls and stood very still until the feeling passed.

It didn't pass. It never fully passed. It just went back into its chest, and she locked it, and she put the key somewhere she pretended not to know.

* * *

On the third day she followed them home.

This, too, was discipline. You learned a target's rhythms before you touched their life: where they slept, what they feared, which windows stuck. Tamsin lived above the shop, which was either sentiment or poverty, and in Greyhallow those were often the same thing. Veyra watched the candle in the upper window burn past midnight two nights running, and on the third night it guttered early, and she marked the hour.

They weren't sleeping well. Good. Guilt and fear kept strange hours, and if the map had come to Tamsin the way the Conclave believed—if a dying courier had pressed it into those careful hands—then Tamsin was already living inside a tightening circle, already hearing boots in every stairwell. Fear made people predictable. Fear made them run, and running things could be caught, or cornered, or guided.

She preferred guided. Cornered, Tamsin Thornbury became something else entirely. She'd seen it once, at the siege of Marrowgate, and the memory still visited her at inconvenient moments: Tamsin with their blade broken, surrounded, smiling—that awful bright smile—and turning the fight anyway. Not winning it. *Turning* it, the way you turn a river, with patience and terrible leverage.

The Conclave's dossier called Tamsin a minor threat. The Conclave's dossier had been written by clerks.

* * *

The fourth day, she almost spoke to them.

It happened stupidly, as these things do. A cart lost a wheel on Chandler's Lane, spilling cabbages, and in the ensuing chaos—a bawling driver, scattering vegetables, a constable's whistle—Tamsin stumbled off the shop's threshold directly into Veyra's path. For one heartbeat they were close enough to touch. Close enough that Veyra could see the fine lines around their eyes that hadn't been there five years ago, could smell woodsmoke and cheap tallow and, beneath it, something faintly herbal—sage, maybe, or the ghost of consecrated oil that no amount of market-life had scrubbed away.

Their eyes met.

Something moved behind Tamsin's face—recognition trying to surface through five years of deliberate forgetting, like a body rising in dark water. Veyra saw it happen and did the only thing she could: she stepped past, murmured the universal apology of strangers—"beg pardon"—and kept walking, her heart slamming against her ribs like a prisoner against a cell door.

At the end of the lane she stopped, pressed her hand flat against a wall, and breathed.

*They almost knew me.* And beneath that, quieter, more dangerous: *and I couldn't bear to let them, and I couldn't bear that I couldn't bear it.*

This was the trouble with old comrades. You could kill a stranger cleanly. But Tamsin wasn't a stranger. Tamsin was the last living proof that Veyra Sarn had once been somebody worth loving, and killing that proof felt less like strategy and more like burning a letter you'd reread too many times.

She straightened. Smoothed her merchant's coat. Did the arithmetic again, forcing the numbers to sit still.

The Conclave wanted the heart. The heart required the map. The map sat in Tamsin's keeping. There was a third option she hadn't been ordered to consider: not theft, not murder. *Recruitment.* Walk through that door wearing an old name instead of a new face. Offer what the Conclave offered her—purpose, protection, a share of the prize. Tamsin was alone, hunted, drowning in a city full of Inquisitors. Alone people said yes to almost anyone.

And if they refused?

Then she would do what she'd been sent to do, and she would carry it the way she carried everything: locked, keyed, and never opened at noon.

* * *

That evening she climbed to the roof of the dye-works opposite the shop and watched the light in Tamsin's window. Below, Greyhallow went about its small dishonest business—drunkards singing, watchmen yawning, somewhere a woman praying aloud to a faceless statue. The harbor bells rang the dusk-watch, and fog came off the water in slow gray tongues, swallowing the rooftops one by one.

Veyra thought about the last time she'd seen Tamsin. The war's final day, the field at Vael's Ford, the moment the sky went silent—not quiet, *silent*, the way a room goes silent when someone stops breathing. Every sworn blade had felt it. Caerlum's presence, that warm pressure at the back of the skull that soldiers had marched under for centuries, simply... ended. And in that terrible stillness, Tamsin had turned to look for her, and Veyra had already been walking away. Toward the Conclave's agents. Toward the offer she'd accepted weeks before, in secret, in shame.

She had never decided whether that made her a traitor or merely a survivor. The question had a way of rephrasing itself depending on how much she'd had to drink.

Tomorrow, she told herself. Tomorrow she would knock on the charm-seller's door, and say the old greeting—the one sworn blades used, the one that began *I know your shield-hand*—and watch Tamsin's face do whatever it was going to do. Grief, joy, fury; she'd take any of them. What she couldn't take was another night on this roof, counting the reasons not to.

Below her, in the shadowed mouth of Cordwainer's Row, three figures in gray cloaks paused outside the charm-seller's shop. One knocked. The others spread out, casual as death, covering the alley and the far end of the lane.

Inquisitors. Moving at night. Asking questions.

Veyra rose silently, every instinct sharpening to a point. Whatever game the Conclave was playing, the Inquisition had just entered the board—and they were moving faster than her letter had promised.

*Tamsin,* she thought, already descending into the fog, *you were always better at fighting than hiding. Let's hope you've learned.*
