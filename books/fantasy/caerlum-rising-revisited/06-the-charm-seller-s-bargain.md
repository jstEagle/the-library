## 6. The Charm Seller's Bargain

The rain had been falling on Greyhallow for three days, and by the fourth the city smelled like a wet dog left too long by a fire—smoke and tallow and the sour breath of ten thousand gutters all running at once. Tamsin watched it from behind the shop's grimy window, watching the water bead on glass that hadn't been properly cleaned since before the Edict, and thought about doors.

Doors were what you lived between, in Greyhallow. The door of your shop, bolted at dusk. The door of your mouth, kept shut around dangerous words. The door of your past, which you never opened even for yourself if you could help it. And somewhere out in that drowning grey city, a dead courier's map sat folded inside the false bottom of Tamsin's charm-box like a coal that wouldn't cool, burning a hole through every layer of oak and deception they'd wrapped around it.

*The Hollow Vault.*

Tamsin had not slept. Sleep required a mind that would stop turning over, and theirs turned like a millwheel with something caught in the gears: sigils half-remembered from another life, the courier's blood going tacky on the floorboards, the way he'd said *Caerlum* like a man saying the name of a lover or a wound.

Someone had to know where the roads out of this city still ran clean. Someone who didn't ask questions, because questions cost extra.

Which meant Doss.

* * *

Doss kept his counting-house—or his drinking-house, depending on the hour and how honest he was feeling—in the drowned cellar of a tannery off Ropewalk Lane, down a staircase so narrow two people couldn't pass without one of them turning sideways, which Tamsin suspected was deliberate. The air below was thick with the smell of hides and cheap brandy and the particular staleness of rooms that had given up on ever seeing the sun again. A single lamp burned behind the bar, its flame bent sideways by some draft no one had bothered to hunt down.

Doss was already there, of course. Doss was always already there, as though he'd been poured into his chair along with the foundation stones. He was a squat man built like a rain barrel, bald as an egg and polished nearly as bright, with small quick hands that were never entirely empty. Tonight one of them held a knife he was cleaning, slowly, the way other men cleaned their teeth.

"Charm seller," he said, without looking up. "Didn't send for you."

"Good," Tamsin said, taking the stool across the bar. "Then nobody knows I'm here."

"That's the spirit." He set the knife down, finally deigned to look up. His eyes were pale, almost colorless, the eyes of a fish that had lived its whole life in dark water. "You look like death's own bookkeeper. Sit. Drink?"

"Do I have a choice?"

"You always have a choice." He poured two fingers of something amber into a cup anyway and pushed it across. "That's what everything costs."

Tamsin drank. It burned like a broken promise, which was about what they'd expected. Around them the cellar was empty except for a pair of dockhands in the corner playing dice with the solemn concentration of men losing money they couldn't afford, and a woman asleep face-down at the far end of the bar whom Tamsin chose to believe was simply tired rather than something worse.

"I need passage out of Greyhallow," Tamsin said quietly. "South road, or whatever passes for it this season. Quietly. No manifests, no names, no Inquisitorial checkpoints."

Doss's eyebrows climbed a fraction. On most men it would have been surprise; on Doss it was arithmetic. "That's a dear thing to ask. Roads are watched these days, charm seller. Watched like a rich widow at a wedding. You know what the Edict's done to travel. Every gate, every toll bridge, every ferry crossing—there's a gray cloak standing on it asking pilgrims to recite the Articles of Severance." He smiled, showing teeth stained the color of old bone. "You know the Articles?"

"By heart," Tamsin said. *There is no god above, no power beside; what divinity was is divinity forbidden.* Five years ago they'd have spat reciting it. Now it came out flat as coin.

"Mm." Doss picked his knife back up and began cleaning it again, which Tamsin understood was the sound of him thinking. "So either you're fleeing something, or you're carrying something. And either way—" the knife paused— "you're asking me to risk my neck and my wagons and my very fine reputation for a person whose entire visible trade is selling bits of knotted string to nervous grandmothers."

"The charms work," Tamsin said, out of pure habit.

"They don't, actually," Doss said pleasantly. "I've had three tested. My niece bought a fever-charm off you last spring. It did nothing. She got better anyway, but she'd have gotten better anyway if she'd swallowed a pebble." He leaned forward, and the lamplight made his scalp shine like a saint's halo, which was the funniest thing in the room and neither of them laughed. "Which has always interested me, you understand. A man—or woman, or whatever polite word we're using for you—selling charms that don't work, year after year, never once cheating a customer outright, never once trying to make them work. That's not a merchant's habit, friend. That's penance."

The cellar seemed to get colder. Or perhaps it was only Tamsin's blood remembering how to move.

"You've been asking about me."

"I ask about everyone. It's cheaper than insurance." Doss set the knife down flat on the bar, within his own reach and pointedly outside anyone else's. "Here's what I know about you, charm seller, free of charge, because knowing things is how I stay alive. You came to Greyhallow five years back, right when the god went quiet and the purges started. You had soldier's calluses in odd places—inside the palm, not the fingers, which is sword-work, not rope-work—and you flinched the first time someone said the name Caerlum in your hearing, which was also the last time, because after that you stopped coming to the well on holy days. You paid your first six months' rent in mint-condition silver from mints that haven't struck coin since the war." He spread his small quick hands. "I don't know what you were. But I know what you weren't, and you weren't a charm seller then any more than you are now."

Tamsin's hand wanted to drift toward the knife at their belt—an old instinct, five years dormant, waking up like a dog hearing its name. They kept it flat on the bar instead. "And yet I'm still breathing."

"Because I don't sell information to the Inquisition," Doss said. "Bad for repeat business. The gray cloaks pay in warrants, and warrants have a way of getting everyone who ever touched them hanged." He refilled Tamsin's cup unasked. "But it does mean your passage just got more expensive."

"How much more?"

"Show me what you brought."

Tamsin had known this moment was coming since they'd left the shop, and had spent the whole walk rehearsing the argument against it. *Don't show him. Never show anyone. Carry it alone, trade money, trade labor, trade anything but—*

But money was short, and labor took time, and time was the one thing the dead courier hadn't sold them. So Tamsin reached into their coat and set the relic on the bar between them.

It was a prayer-bead. Just that—a single bead of veined white stone, small enough to vanish in a closed fist, strung on a fraying cord. To any Inquisitor it would read as contraband, nothing more: a fragment of pre-Edict devotion, worth a fine and maybe a night in the cells. But Tamsin had carried it through the war and out the other side, had worn it against the skin for fifteen years until the cord was black with sweat, and knew it for what it was. Cut from the cornerstone of the first shrine at Caerlum's Well. One of nine hundred made the day the order was sworn, each one humming faintly with the god's attention, warm as a held hand.

It wasn't warm anymore. It hadn't been warm in five years. But it was the last object in the world that Tamsin owned which had ever been touched by divinity, and putting it on the bar felt like setting down a limb.

Doss looked at it for a long time. His fish-pale eyes had gone very still.

"That's not a charm," he said softly.

"No."

"That's sworn-stone. My grandmother wore one of those, gods rot her sentimental heart, before the Edict men came through the village." He didn't touch it. Tamsin noticed that—he wanted to, his fingers twitched toward it twice—but he kept his hands flat on the bar like a man keeping them away from a fire. "You know what happens to me if the wrong person learns I held this? Not sold. *Held.*"

"Nothing happens to you," Tamsin said. "Because you're going to sell it south, to a collector in Vellamarre or the Free Ports, where the Edict is a rumor rich people find amusing. And you're going to charge enough that it buys my silence on the road and a berth in your caravan, and you're going to forget my face the moment I'm gone."

Doss laughed. It was a shocking sound in that cellar, big and wheezing, and the sleeping woman at the end of the bar didn't stir, which settled the question of her state. "Forget your face. Oh, you *are* a charmer." He wiped his eyes with a knuckle. "All right. All right. Here's how it stands, and listen close, because I'll only say it once and I hate repeating myself almost as much as I hate the Inquisition, which is considerably less than I hate the Conclave, but we'll come to that."

The word landed in Tamsin's chest like a dropped stone. "Conclave."

"Ashen Conclave." Doss said it the way sailors say a storm's name—not with fear exactly, but with respect, the way you'd respect a thing that had already eaten better men than you. "You know them?"

"I know the name." Everyone knew the name. Ash-cloaked reformers, the street preachers called them—godless men and women who claimed the Edict hadn't gone far enough, that divinity itself was the disease and humanity needed to burn out the last of it. Officially the Inquisition hated them. Unofficially, in the way unofficial things worked in Greyhallow, nobody seemed able to find them when it mattered.

"Well," Doss said, "they've been in my taproom. Twice this week. Asking after roads south. Asking after the old battlefields, and the passes into the Thornmark, and—" his voice dropped, and he glanced at the dice-players, who weren't listening, and lowered it further still— "asking after a place called the Hollow Vault."

The cellar tilted, very slightly, the way a room does when the floor turns out to be a deck. Tamsin kept their hands still through sheer mercenary discipline and heard their own pulse in their ears like a drum in another room.

"Vault," they repeated, flat as a ledger line.

"Mm. Which means nothing to me, naturally, being a simple smuggler with no interest in old holes in the ground. But it clearly means something to *them*, because they're paying for guides, and guides are expensive, and guides who go asking around after gray-cloak business tend to stop needing money." Doss finally, deliberately, slid the prayer-bead across the bar and closed his fist around it, and Tamsin felt the loss of it go through them like a hook drawn out of flesh. "And here's the part you're really going to love, charm seller. Because they're not just asking about places. They're asking about people. And one of the descriptions they passed around—one of the ones they're paying silver for, good silver, the kind that gets neighbors talking—was a soldier of Caerlum's old order. Sworn blade. Scar through the left eyebrow. Came to Greyhallow five years ago and sells trinkets near Tanner's Row."

The scar through Tamsin's left eyebrow itched, as it always did when someone mentioned it, as though the wound itself were trying to get a word in.

"So," Doss went on, cheerfully, horribly, pocketing the bead, "either the Conclave has developed an interest in mediocre jewelry, or somebody out there already knows what you are. And whoever they learned it from—the courier, I'm guessing, since gray cloaks found him dead in your doorway yesterday and have been knocking on doors along Tanner's Row since dawn—you want to be several miles south of this conversation before they finish their rounds."

*Hale,* Tamsin thought. The Inquisitor with the patient eyes and the notebook. Already at the door, already asking. And behind Hale, closer than breath, the Conclave, holding a description like a lantern.

"When does the caravan leave?" Tamsin asked.

"Dawn. Before the gates get their morning shift change, there's a window—twenty minutes, maybe, when the watch is changing and nobody wants to count heads. We go through the tannery yard, under the wall, through a culvert my grandfather dug during the last war and which I will deny to my grave." Doss stood, joints cracking like green wood, and looked down at Tamsin with those pale fish-eyes. "Terms are this. You ride in the wool cart. You don't talk to my drivers. If gray cloaks stop us, you're my deaf-mute cousin from Vellamarre, and if they test that story you'd better be a very good actor. And if the Conclave finds us on the road—"

"What do I do?"

"You do whatever it is soldiers do," Doss said. "I'm told it comes back to you." He jerked his chin toward the stairs. "Go home. Burn anything you can't carry. Be back here before the third bell of morning, and if you're late—"

"I won't be late."

"Everyone's late once," Doss said. "That's how most stories end." 

* * *

The rain had stopped sometime during the bargaining, and Greyhallow emerged dripping and steaming into a thin, grudging dusk. Tamsin walked home the long way, twice doubling back through alleys that stank of tallow and wet ash, checking reflections in dark windows, listening to their own footsteps the way you listen to a heartbeat you've decided not to trust. Old habits surfacing like drowned things. Five years of careful invisibility, undone by one dying man's last choice.

At the mouth of Tanner's Row, Tamsin stopped.

Across the street, leaning in a doorway with the studied idleness of someone being paid by the hour, stood a young man in a rain-dark coat, picking his teeth, watching the charm shop with the fixed, unblinking patience of a cat at a mousehole. Not a gray cloak. No sash, no warrant-badge. But he stood like a soldier stands, weight balanced, hand loose near his belt, and when a lamplighter passed he nodded to him—nodded, familiar, easy—and Tamsin's stomach dropped through the wet cobblestones.

The watch was bought. Or the watch was them. With the Conclave, these days, it amounted to the same thing.

Tamsin turned down the alley beside the chandler's, heart hammering a war-drum rhythm they hadn't heard in years, and let themselves in through the shop's back door with hands that were almost steady. Inside, the candle was still burning where they'd left it, and the map lay hidden in its false bottom, and there was no time—no time at all—to decide what to take and what to burn.

They were reaching for the charm-box when they saw it.

A square of heavy paper on the counter, laid dead center, squared neatly with the counter's edge. White as a shroud in the candlelight. Tamsin hadn't left it. No one had been in. The door had been locked, the windows shuttered—

The note bore a single line, in a fine, slanted hand:

*You should have taken my offer, little blade. We leave at dawn too. Try to keep up.*

Beneath the words, pressed flat and left like a signature, like a kiss, like a knife between the ribs of the past: a sworn-stone prayer-bead, twin to the one now sitting in Doss's pocket.

Warm. Still warm. Warm as a held hand.

Veyra was in Greyhallow.
