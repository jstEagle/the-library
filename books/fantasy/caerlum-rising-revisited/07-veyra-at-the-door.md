## 7. Veyra at the Door

The knock came at the third hour past midnight, and Tamsin knew before opening it that everything was about to change.

Not because of the sound itself. Greyhallow was a city of late noises—the drunk singing in the Godless Quarter, the watchmen's boots on wet cobbles, cats fighting in the temple ruins that no one had bothered to demolish after the Purges. A knock at any hour could be explained away. But this knock had a rhythm to it, three sharp raps followed by a pause, then two more, and Tamsin's body remembered it before their mind did. A field signal, old as the war itself. *All clear, but speak softly.* The kind of knock you used when enemy ears might be listening.

Tamsin lay in the narrow bed above the shop and stared at the ceiling beams, counting heartbeats. One. Two. Ten. The knock did not come again, but neither did footsteps retreat down the stairs. Whoever stood below was patient. Patience, in Tamsin's experience, belonged to two kinds of people: Inquisitors, who enjoyed letting fear ripen like fruit before they picked you, and soldiers, who had learned that rushing a door got you killed.

*It's Hale,* they told themselves. *She came back for the arrest she didn't make this afternoon.*

But Hale would have brought men, and men meant boots, and boots meant noise. The stair below was silent as a held breath.

Tamsin rose without lighting a candle. Five years of careful living had made their body into an instrument of quiet: they knew which floorboards groaned, which steps to skip, how to move through their own home like a stranger in a museum. They took the old knife from beneath the loose stone by the hearth—not the sworn blade, never that, that was wrapped in oilcloth under the floor of the back room—but a plain mercenary's knife with a good edge and no history worth grieving.

Then they went down to meet the night.

* * *

The shop smelled of dried herbs and candle wax and the faint copper ghost of blood that no amount of scrubbing had fully banished. The courier's blood had soaked between the floorboards; Tamsin had pried up three of them two nights ago and buried the stained wood in the midden heap behind the tannery, and still the smell lingered, or perhaps Tamsin only imagined it, the way you imagine a wound itching long after it has healed.

A shape waited beyond the shuttered window, standing in the alley's darkness where the moonlight couldn't reach. Tall. Still. Arms crossed in a way that made the shadow look almost relaxed, if shadows could relax.

"I know you're awake," said a voice like smoke over coals. "You were always awake at this hour. You'd sit up watching the fires burn down and tell me it was the watchman in me." A pause. "I used to think it was just habit. Now I think you never really slept after Caerlum went quiet. None of us did. We just forgot to admit it."

Tamsin's hand tightened on the knife until the grip bit into their palm. That voice. Five years, and it hadn't changed—still low and unhurried, still carrying that thread of wry warmth underneath, as if the whole world were a joke Veyra Sarn had agreed to be in on. It was a voice that had once sung marching songs across the Ashvale flats, that had laughed while pulling arrows from Tamsin's shoulder, that had sworn—at dusk, at a shrine, with wine on both their breaths—that nothing would ever come between shield-sisters. Not death. Not the god's silence. Not anything.

And then the war had ended, and something had come between them after all.

"Open the door, Tam." No one else had ever shortened the name that way. "Or don't, and I'll stand here talking until the neighbors complain. Either way, we should probably not have this conversation through wood."

Every sensible instinct said *leave by the back window, run, run now*. But running was what the guilty did, and besides—some traitor part of Tamsin's chest, the part that still ached at old songs, wanted to see her face.

They unbarred the door.

* * *

Moonlight followed her in, silvering the grey in her close-cropped hair and the scar that ran from her left ear down along her jaw—a new one, Tamsin noted, clean and surgical, the kind of mark left by a Conclave blade. She wore traveling leathers dyed the color of wet slate, practical and expensive, and beneath the smell of road dust Tamsin caught another scent: ash and hot iron. The smell of workings. Of real magic, worn close like perfume.

Veyra closed the door herself, slid the bolt, and turned. Her eyes—dark, quick, always moving, cataloguing exits the way other people noticed faces—swept the shop: the charm stall, the bundles of dried rowan, the little tin amulets Tamsin sold to frightened people for coppers.

"So it's true," she said softly. "The great Tamsin Thornbury, Shield of the Ninth Banner, Bearer of the Dawnmark, sells luck charms to merchants' wives." She picked up one of the tin pieces, turned it over in fingers that had once snapped a Conclave agent's wrist like kindling. "Does it work?"

"It keeps the rain off," Tamsin said. "If you hold it over your head."

Veyra laughed—a short, startled bark of genuine amusement—and set the charm down. For one suspended moment they simply looked at each other across the counter, five years wide, and Tamsin felt the old grief rise up like floodwater.

"You look well," Veyra said. "Older. Careful. But well."

"You look rich."

"I look *employed*." She drifted along the shelves, touching things without picking them up, the way she always inspected a room before trusting it. "Ashen robes, actually, under the leathers. Don't make that face. I know what you've heard about the Conclave, and half of it's true, and the half that's true is uglier than the half that isn't. But I'm not here on their errand tonight. I'm here because I heard a courier died in this shop with Caerlum's name in his mouth, and I needed to know if it was really you he found."

"And if it was?"

Veyra stopped drifting. She looked at Tamsin the way she used to look across a battlefield—measuring, fond, and utterly without illusion. "Then the world just got very small for both of us, sister, and you can either hate me in company or hate me alone. Your choice."

"Don't call me that."

The words came out harder than intended, and Tamsin saw them land—saw the flicker in Veyra's eyes, there and gone, like a fish under ice. Old wounds. Both of them carried them. Only one of them had dealt the blow.

* * *

The last day of the war had been bright, obscenely bright, the kind of autumn morning that made the slaughter feel like sacrilege. The Conclave's lines had broken at Redford Bridge, and the Ninth Banner had held the crossing for six hours against three times their number, and when it was done, Tamsin had knelt in the shallows washing blood off the Dawnmark sigil burned into their vambrace, and Veyra had sat beside them and said, quietly:

"The god's gone, Tam. Whatever's left of Caerlum is in that vault with the heart, and the people who took it aren't going to give it back out of mercy. Somebody has to decide what happens next. Us, or them."

"We swore—"

"We swore to a god who stopped answering." Veyra had taken Tamsin's face in her hands, gentle as a nurse, firm as a manacle. "Listen to me. I've been approached. There are people—survivors, believers, people who want to bring Him back the right way, controlled, contained, so the madness never happens again. Come with me. We do this together, or we spend our lives watching lesser people do it badly."

And Tamsin had said no. And Veyra had smiled that smile—the one that meant the argument wasn't over, merely postponed—and three days later the Ninth Banner's muster roll had been handed to the Inquisition's agents in exchange for safe passage and funding, and forty-one names had become forty-one pyres, and Tamsin had survived only because a dying sergeant shoved them into the river with the words *she sold us, Thornbury, she sold us all.*

Forty-one. Tamsin had counted them every night for a year. Some nights, even now.

"You're doing the arithmetic," Veyra said quietly. Tamsin realized they had spoken none of this aloud; their face had simply shown it, as it always had. "I know. I've done mine too. Forty-one, plus Doss's boy who got whipped for asking questions about me, plus the courier bleeding out on your floor. You think I don't count? I count better than you. I count in coin."

"Is that supposed to be an apology?"

"It's supposed to be honesty, which is rarer." Veyra pulled a chair from the corner, turned it, straddled it backward with the ease of a woman making herself at home in a war camp. "Here's the truth, since we're trading in it. The Conclave knows about the courier. They know the map exists. Within days—they'll have this quarter flooded with their own agents wearing Inquisition colors, and then it won't matter whether Hale believes you or not, because the people who sign her orders will sign different ones. You cannot outrun this alone. You cannot buy passage deep enough. And you certainly—" her eyes flicked toward the back room, toward the floorboards, toward the wrapped thing sleeping there, "—cannot fight what's coming with a kitchen knife and a guilty conscience."

Tamsin's blood went cold. "You don't know what's under my floor."

"I know what's under every sworn blade's floor, Tam. It's the one possession we were never allowed to bury." Veyra leaned forward, elbows on the chair-back, and her voice dropped to the register she'd used before charges, before bridges, before the end of everything. "The Vault opens to sworn blood. Yours. Mine. Maybe three others alive in the world, and two of those are Conclave property now. So here is my offer, and I'll only make it once. Partnership. Equal shares. We reach the Hollow Vault before either the Inquisition or the Conclave's inner circle does, and we decide together what happens to what's inside. Not them. Us. Like it should have been from the start."

"And if what's inside says otherwise?"

Veyra's smile thinned. "Gods don't get a vote. That's rather the point of the exercise."

* * *

Tamsin moved to the window and eased the shutter open a finger's width. The street lay empty, silvered and silent. Somewhere distant, a dog barked twice and quit. Above the rooftops, the moon was a pared fingernail, thin enough to see through.

Five years of invisibility. Five years of small charms and smaller lies, of keeping the head down and the name forgotten, and it had all come apart in a single week—one dying courier, one suspicious Inquisitor, one map inked in a dead language pressed into unwilling hands. And now this: the person Tamsin had loved best in the world, offering the one thing no one else could. A way through. An ally who knew the old roads, the old signs, the weight of the vows.

It would be so easy to say yes. That was the trap of it. Veyra had always known exactly which doors to knock on.

"No," Tamsin said.

Veyra didn't move. "Tam—"

"You gave forty-one of ours to the Inquisition to fund your ambition. You sat at that bridge and washed your hands in the same water I did, and then you sold us." Tamsin turned, and let themself finally say what five years of silence had kept corked. "I buried the Ninth Banner twice. Once when Caerlum went quiet, and once when you signed the muster roll. I'm not giving you a third chance to dig."

Something moved behind Veyra's eyes—hurt, maybe, or its expensive counterfeit. "They were dead anyway. You knew that. A banner without a god is just meat waiting for the butcher's queue. I traded meat for meaning—"

"*Get out.*"

"—and you'd have understood that if you'd ever been able to think past your own grief. You mourn a silence, Tam. I'm trying to end it. There's a difference, and history will—"

Tamsin crossed the room in four strides and put the kitchen knife against Veyra's throat, and the shock of it froze them both. Tamsin hadn't drawn steel against a living person in five years. Their hand shook. Veyra's didn't; she went very still, the way she'd gone still under arrow-fire, trusting her read of the person holding the weapon.

"You want to know what I remember?" Tamsin said, low. "I remember you teaching me to sleep sitting up. I remember you carrying me three miles with an arrow in my lung. And I remember the river, and Sergeant Mael's face when he said your name like a curse. All of it is true at once. That's what you did to me, Veyra. You made it impossible to hate you cleanly."

For a moment, the mask slipped—truly slipped—and what looked out from behind it was tired. Old. Lonely in a way that no amount of Conclave gold could gild.

"That," Veyra said quietly, "is the only honest thing anyone's said to me in years." She reached up, two fingers, and pushed the blade gently aside. "Keep the map. Keep the knife under your floor. When they come—and they will come, both of them, within the week—remember that I offered. Remember that I knocked first."

She stepped back, straightened her leathers, and paused at the door with her hand on the bolt. Without turning, she said:

"For what it's worth—I never sent the muster roll myself. I gave it to a runner and told him to lose it. He didn't." A beat. "I've hated him for five years. It doesn't help, but it's true."

The door opened on cold air and empty street. Then it shut, and the bolt fell, and Tamsin stood alone in the dark shop with a shaking hand and a heart full of knives.

* * *

Sleep did not come. Tamsin lit a single candle and sat at the workbench, turning the courier's map over and over, watching candlelight pool in the wax seal's crevices. *Partnership. Equal shares.* The offer replayed like a song stuck behind the teeth, and the terrible thing—the truly terrible thing—was how much of Tamsin wanted it. Not Veyra. Never Veyra again. But an ally. A shield at their back on the road ahead. Someone who knew the words to the old songs.

*You cannot outrun this alone.* She was right about that. Damn her, she was right about most things; that had always been the worst part of her.

Outside, the sky had begun the slow bruise toward grey. Tamsin snuffed the candle, went to the back room, and knelt on the cold boards. The loose plank came up. Oilcloth, unwrapped. The sworn blade caught the pre-dawn light along its edge, and the Dawnmark sigil in the hilt seemed almost to warm under Tamsin's thumb—faintly, faintly, like a coal remembering fire.

Somewhere beyond the city walls, past the marsh and the ash-roads, the Hollow Vault waited with its stolen heart. And somewhere closer, much closer, boots were already moving.

Tamsin wrapped the blade, tucked it under their arm, and began packing for a journey they had sworn never to take.

By full light, the shop would be dark, the door barred from outside, and the Godless Quarter's charm seller would be nothing but a rumor and a scrubbed patch of floor.

Whatever came next, it would not find them waiting.
