## 8. Flight from Greyhallow

The knock came at the hour when the lamps in the Godless Quarter burned lowest, and Tamsin knew before opening their eyes that it was not a customer's knock.

Customers knocked twice, apologetic, as if asking permission to want something. This was three strikes, hard and even, spaced like the beats of a drum before an execution. Tamsin lay in the narrow cot above the shop and listened to the sound repeat, and in the silence after it, they heard boots—many boots, moving with the particular discipline of people who had never once in their lives been told *no*.

The map was under the floorboard. The map was always under the floorboard, wrapped in oilcloth, three fingers' width from Tamsin's heart if they lay down flat in the right spot. Tamsin had taken to sleeping there, curled around the spot like a dog around a bone, and some animal part of them had known, had been counting down, had woken them tonight not from the knock but from the shape of the silence underneath it.

They dressed in the dark. They did not light the candle. They took the old knife from beneath the loose brick in the hearth—the one they had sworn never to touch again, the one with Caerlum's sunburst worked into the pommel, worn nearly smooth by a hand that had gripped it through a war—and they slid it into their belt where it sat like a swallowed coal.

Below, the front door splintered.

Tamsin did not go down the stairs. There was a hatch in the pantry floor, behind the sacks of dried lentils Tamsin never ate because lentils were cheap and a charm seller was supposed to look like a poor, harmless creature. The hatch opened onto the cellar, and the cellar had a second mouth: a low, brick-throated drain that the city's builders had made in a more honest age, before Greyhallow decided its own filth was a secret worth keeping.

But first—Tamsin stood in the dark of the bedroom with their hand on the hatch ring and made themselves look. The shop below. Five years of it. The counter worn pale where a thousand palms had rested. The jars of river-stones and knotted thread, the dried lavender, the little tin charms stamped with the sigil of the Harrowed Saint that everyone knew meant nothing and everyone bought anyway, because a thing that meant nothing could not be taken from you. The stool by the window where old Marta sat every fourth-day and told Tamsin about her son in the coastal garrisons. The smell of dust and lavender and candle-tallow that had soaked so deep into the wood it had become the wood.

They heard the raiders moving through it now. Glass jarred and shattered. A shelf tipped, deliberately, the way soldiers tip things—not searching, *unmaking*. A voice, flat and bored: "Nothing here. Charm-seller's rubbish."

And then another voice, and Tamsin's blood went to ice water.

"Look again." Inquisitor Hale. Tamsin had heard her only once, across the width of a shop, but a voice like hers did not need repetition. It was the voice of a woman who had already decided how a thing would end and was merely waiting for the world to catch up. "Pull the floorboards. Check the hearth. A courier dies in this quarter with Caerlum's name in his mouth, and four days later I'm meant to believe the nearest charm seller heard *nothing*?"

The floorboards. Tamsin's stomach turned to lead. They had re-laid the boards over the hiding place themselves, but a good searcher reads a floor like a face, and Hale—Hale would read it like a confession.

There was no more time. Tamsin dropped through the hatch, dragged it shut, and heaved the lentil sacks over it, and in the black of the cellar they pressed their ear to the stone and listened to their own life being taken apart above them, board by board, jar by jar, with the patient thoroughness of people who had all night and no soul to spend it on.

They found the floorboard. Of course they found it.

The sound that came down through the stone was small—a scrape, a hollow knock, the particular *clack* of wood lifted from a cavity that should have been solid—and it went through Tamsin like a blade between the ribs. Because the cavity was empty. The map was not there. The map was in the oilcloth wrap against Tamsin's spine, where it had been since the third night, since the dream in which hands they did not recognize had lifted the boards while they slept.

But Hale did not know the cavity was empty. And Hale had just found a hidden space beneath the floor of a charm seller's shop in the week a dying courier had named a dead god, and Tamsin heard her voice change—heard the boredom fall away like a mask slipping.

"Bring a lamp."

*Go*, said every surviving instinct Tamsin owned. *Go now, while they're looking down and not listening for a drain.*

Tamsin went. But at the mouth of the drain they stopped, one hand on the cold brick, and did the thing they would spend the rest of the night not forgiving themselves for: they struck a match from the tin in their pocket and touched it to the tallow shelf above the cellar stairs, and to the oilcloth bundle of dried lavender, and to the curtain of the little storeroom where they had slept for five years believing that smallness was safety.

Fire was the one charm Tamsin had never sold. It was the one thing that had always worked.

The flames took the shop the way rumor takes a name—fast, greedy, delighted. By the time Tamsin dropped into the drain and pulled the grate shut behind them, the smoke was already climbing the pantry shaft, and above it they could hear the first shouts, the confusion, Hale's voice cutting sharp through it: *out, everyone out, the building's going—* and Tamsin turned their back on all of it and crawled into the dark, and did not let themselves think about Marta's stool, or the counter worn pale, or the fact that by morning every watch-post in Greyhallow would know exactly which shop had burned and exactly who had not been found in the ashes.

* * *

The sewers of Greyhallow were older than the city's shame and better built than its churches, and Tamsin knew them the way a soldier knows the trenches they nearly died in—by touch, by smell, by the memory of a night five years past when a different life had ended and this one had begun.

They moved by count. Forty paces along the main channel to the junction where the brickwork changed color. Left at the junction, wading the shallows where the water ran clear over old flagstone, up to the ankle, then the knee when the channel dipped. Count the arches—three, four, five—and take the low passage on the right, the one that smelled of coal-dust because it ran beneath the smiths' row. The dark down there was total and honest. It asked nothing of Tamsin except forward.

The map, wrapped in oilcloth, rode against their spine. The knife rode at their hip. And the fire above them painted the sewer ceilings in slow, breathing shades of red, so that Tamsin fled through the bowels of the city beneath a sky the color of the thing they'd done.

They let themselves think of it only once, in a stretch where the water deepened and they had to swim, one-handed, holding the map-wrapped knife above the surface. They thought: *I had a stool by the window. I had a fourth-day customer. I had a name that meant a small shop that meant nothing.* And then: *I had a god, once, and that meant less.* And then they stopped thinking, because the junction to Doss's door was ahead, and the water there was black and still, and stillness in the sewers of Greyhallow meant someone had stopped moving in it recently, and recently was a word with teeth.

Tamsin went the last fifty paces with the knife drawn.

The smuggler's door was a false wall in the junction's north face, brick painted to look like brick, and the sign of it—three notches cut at shoulder height, old and half-filled with grime—was exactly where Tamsin's memory said it would be. They knocked the pattern Doss had given them: two, pause, three, pause, one. Nothing. They knocked again.

A slot slid open at eye height. One eye, brown and bloodshot, regarded them with the enthusiasm of a tax collector.

"Shop's shut."

"Doss sent me to say the shop's shut."

"The shop's been shut for years." But the eye narrowed, and the voice lost its boredom. "Wait. You're the one he told me about. The one who pays in relics." The slot did not close. "He said you'd come either desperate or dead. Which is it?"

"Both. Open the door, Brann."

The wall ground open. Inside was a smuggler's idea of a chapel: crates stacked to a vaulted ceiling, a lamp burning low, and Brann—a squat man with a face like a closed fist—already reaching for a crowbar as if the world were a crate he intended to open. "Doss said you'd bring trouble. Doss says a lot of things. Doss is a liar with a good heart, which is the worst kind." He looked Tamsin up and down—soaked, soot-streaked, knife bare in hand—and something in his expression shifted from annoyance to a kind of grim respect. "Trouble's already here, isn't it. I can smell the smoke. Whose shop is burning?"

"Mine," Tamsin said, and sheathed the knife, and found that their hand shook. "I need the caravan. Tonight. Doss said—"

"Doss said you'd say that too." Brann jerked his chin toward a back passage. "Caravan's staging at the tanner's yard, moving at the change of watch. He's holding a cart for you. He didn't say why." The squat man's eyes were shrewd and unkind and, in their way, honest. "He also didn't say what you're carrying. And I don't ask. But I'll tell you what I told him: whatever it is, the roads east of the city are watched. Watched by the Inquisition, which is bad, and watched by *other* people, which is worse, because the Inquisition at least wears its face. There've been men in grey asking after the eastern roads. No badges. No names. Paying in old coin." He spat. "Old coin, in this city. You know what that means."

Tamsin knew. Old coin meant people who had been waiting a long time. People for whom the war was not over, only paused, the way a held breath is not the absence of breath.

"Then I'll be gone before they find me," Tamsin said.

Brann laughed, short and without humor. "That's what they all say." He thrust a bundle at Tamsin—a dry cloak, a heel of black bread, a skin of water. "Tanner's yard. Change of watch. Don't be late, because Doss doesn't wait, and if you miss the caravan, I'm not opening this door twice."

* * *

Tamsin reached the tanner's yard as the watch bells rang the change, and the smell of the place—acrid, chemical, the reek of lime and rendered hide—was so violent it scoured the sewer stench from their skin and, for a moment, even the smoke from their memory. Carts stood in the yard in a loose, armed column: six wagons, mules, a dozen figures in road-clothes with the particular stillness of people who had been paid to be calm. And at the head of it, checking a wheel with one hand and a knife's edge with the other, was Doss.

The smuggler looked up, and his face did something complicated. Relief, mostly, and under it the flat calculation of a man totaling a bill.

"You're on fire," Doss said. "I mean your quarter of the city. I can see the glow from here." He straightened, and his voice dropped. "Hale's people pulled three of my street contacts tonight. Not arrested—*pulled*. Off the street, no charges. That's not an Inquisition looking for a courier's killer anymore. That's an Inquisition closing a net." His eyes went to the oilcloth shape against Tamsin's back, then away, deliberately. "I don't want to know. I've told you I don't want to know. Get in the second cart, under the hides. We move in a hundred count, and we don't stop before the ash fields, and if the gods are kind—which they aren't, or one of them would have said so by now—we're over the river by dawn."

Tamsin climbed into the cart. The hides came down over them, heavy and reeking, and the world shrank to leather and dark and the drum of their own heart. Through the gaps they watched the yard gate open, watched Greyhallow's walls slide past for the last time—watched, far off across the rooftops, the glow of their own burning shop, a smear of orange against the low clouds like a wound in the sky's belly.

*Goodbye*, they thought, and did not know if they meant the shop, or the name, or the five years, or the small harmless person they had built out of all three. The cart lurched. The walls fell behind. The road took them.

They were a mile out, maybe two, when Tamsin heard it—hooves behind them, many, and a horn sounding the long note that meant *road closed by order of the Inquisition*. The caravan did not stop. Doss's voice came low along the column: *whips out, we're past the point where stopping helps.* And Tamsin, crushed under the hides with a god's knife at their hip and a dead man's map against their spine, closed their eyes and listened to the pursuit fall behind and not away, and understood with perfect clarity that this was not an escape.

It was a race.

* * *

Inquisitor Hale stood in the ashes of the charm shop and let the snow of it settle on her shoulders, and did not move for a long time.

Her raiders had pulled the shop apart in an hour and the fire had finished the work in twenty minutes, and now the place was a black tooth in the row, dripping char, steaming where the morning damp found the embers. Her people were sifting. They were good at sifting. They had already brought her the things the fire had spared, laid out on a sheet of canvas with the neatness she demanded: a melted tin of worthless charms. A child's clay whistle. A strongbox, empty, its lock sprung by heat.

And a knife.

One of her men had found it in the cellar, in the rubble of a stair that had burned from the top down—someone had set the fire from below, deliberately, a professional's fire, an *escape's* fire. The knife was long, plain, soldier's work. But the pommel stopped Hale's breath.

A sunburst. Worn nearly smooth, but a sunburst. And around the guard, in the old script that the Inquisition made its novices learn precisely so they could recognize and destroy it, a single word.

*Caerlum.*

Hale turned the knife over twice. She thought of the charm seller's face—wary, tired, forgettable, the face of a person who had practiced being forgettable until it became a discipline. She thought of the empty cavity beneath the floorboards, hidden with care and emptied with care, days before her raid. She thought of a dying courier four streets away, bleeding out with a dead god's name in his mouth.

"Well," she said quietly, to the ash, to no one. "You weren't hiding from me at all, were you. You were hiding from everyone *else*."

She wrapped the knife in her own cloak. Around her, her raiders waited for orders, and the burned shop breathed its slow smoke, and Hale looked east, down the road that led out of Greyhallow, toward the ash fields and the old war country beyond.

"Get me the road ledgers," she said. "Every cart out of the east gate since midnight. Every name, every seal, every toll paid in old coin." She was already walking. "Our charm seller didn't burn. Our charm seller *ran*. And people only run toward something."

Behind her, the shop's last wall groaned and came down, and the dust of five years of smallness rose up and drifted east on the wind, following the road.
