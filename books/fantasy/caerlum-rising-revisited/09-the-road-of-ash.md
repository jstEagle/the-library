## 9. The Road of Ash

The road out of Greyhallow was called the Pilgrim's Way once, back when pilgrims had somewhere to go and gods had something to say. Now the milestones were broken, the wayshrines gutted, and the only souls who traveled it were smugglers, deserters, and the occasional fool with a map that burned a hole through their pack like a coal that refused to die.

Tamsin rode in the third wagon of Doss's caravan, wedged between bolts of salted hide and a crate that clinked when the wheels hit ruts, and tried not to think about the map. Tried, and failed, the way they failed at most things these days.

Three days out of the city, the land changed.

It happened gradually, the way rot does. First the hedgerows thinned, then the farms, then even the weeds gave up, until the road ran through a country of gray dust and black soil that would not take seed. The wind carried a smell Tamsin knew from the inside of their own worst memories—old iron, old smoke, the sweetness of things that had rotted where they fell. The other travelers pulled their scarves up over their faces. Tamsin didn't bother. You couldn't filter out a memory.

"Emberfield," Doss said, spitting over the wagon's rail. The smuggler had a face like a dropped plate, all cracks and bad repairs, and a voice that came out of him like gravel poured slow. "Used to be a town. Then the war came through. Then the Inquisition came through after, burning what was left and salting what wouldn't burn." He glanced at Tamsin sidelong, one eye pale as fish skin. "You've been here before."

It wasn't a question. Tamsin kept their gaze on the horizon, where the land rose into low hills stitched with what might have been trenches, might have been graves. "No."

"Liar," Doss said comfortably. "Everybody who crosses Emberfield's been here before. That's what makes it Emberfield." He flicked the reins and the wagon groaned forward. "Keep your hood up past the shrines. The Inquisition's got watchers on the ridge, and they count faces going through. Count them twice if the face looks like it's remembering something."

* * *

The shrines began at midday.

Tamsin had known they would. Every road in the old kingdoms had been lined with them once—Caerlum's wayshrines, knee-high cairns of fieldstone crowned with an iron lantern, each one lit by whoever passed, because a god of hearths and hedges and the small stubborn lights people keep against the dark deserved small stubborn offerings. Tamsin had lit a hundred of them in the war years. Had knelt at them with their sword across their knees and their oath fresh in their mouth, and had felt—*felt*, the way you feel sun through a window—something answer.

The first shrine they passed was a stump of stones, the lantern torn away, the cairn kicked apart. The second had been defaced more thoroughly: the stones painted over with the Inquisition's gray sigil, the circle-and-bar that meant *nothing answers here, and nothing may*. Someone had tried to scratch through the paint. The scratches were old and shallow and had given up.

The third still had its lantern.

Tamsin saw it from thirty paces and their heart did something complicated and painful, a muscle cramping around a hope they'd thought long dead. The lantern hung crooked on its hook, rusted, its glass clouded—but intact. Unbroken. As if the iconoclasts had missed it, or as if some part of them, too, had flinched.

The caravan rolled past. Tamsin let it. "Water the mules at the ford," they said to Doss, and dropped from the wagon, and stood in the road dust watching the wagons shrink, and told themselves it was only to stretch their legs.

The lantern was empty, of course. Whatever oil had fed it had gone to crust and dust years ago. But the wick was still there, curled like a dead man's finger, and when Tamsin touched it, the char came away soft as ash.

They had flint in their pocket. They always had flint; a charm seller needs fire for sealing wax. They crouched at the ruined cairn with the flint in their hand for a long time, long enough for the caravan to disappear around the hills, long enough for the wind to fill their hood with grit.

There was no one to see. There was never anyone to see. That was the whole architecture of their life now—invisibility, smallness, the careful dull surface of a person who sold charms that didn't work and wanted nothing and remembered less.

Tamsin struck the flint.

The spark caught on the second try, caught the dry wick, and the lantern bloomed with a small yellow flame that leaned in the wind and held. Tamsin knelt there with their hands cupped around it as if around a bird, and the heat on their palms was nothing, was only fire, was the same fire that came from any flint in any hand.

But their lips moved. That was the thing they couldn't stop, the thing five years of silence hadn't taught them. The old words came up out of them like water from a cracked well, half-swallowed, barely voiced:

*Light for the road, heart for the hearth, keep what I cannot carry.*

And nothing answered.

Nothing had answered for five years, and Tamsin had built a whole self out of that silence, out of the weary arithmetic of it: *a god who does not answer is a god who does not care, and a god who does not care cannot be betrayed by your disappointment.* They had made the silence into a wall to lean against. They had made it into a home.

Kneeling there, with the little flame trembling and the gray sigil of the Inquisition staring down from the shrine's stones, Tamsin felt the wall crack.

Because it wasn't only silence. It had never been only silence. There had been that night at the ford at Vael's Crossing, the last night of the war, when Tamsin had prayed with blood in their mouth and felt something turn away—not absent, not dead, but *turning away*, deliberately, like a man closing a door on a room where something terrible was happening. And there had been the morning after, when every sworn blade in the order had woken with the same word carved into the inside of their skulls, a word with no sound and no letters, a word that meant, as nearly as mortal tongues could carry it:

*Enough.*

Tamsin blew out the lantern. The wick smoked. They stood, knees cracking, and looked down the road at the hills full of graves, and thought: *I am walking toward the thing that was taken out of you. And I don't know if I'm going to give it back or burn it. And you won't tell me. And maybe you can't.*

The wind came down off the ridge and put out the smoke.

* * *

They caught the caravan at dusk, at a ford where the water ran thin and red-brown over stones the color of old scars. Doss said nothing about the delay, which was its own kind of commentary. Tamsin ate hard bread and salt fish and drank a cup of wine that tasted like the inside of a barrel, and lay back against the hide bales, and the map pressed against their spine through the canvas of their pack like a hand.

They dreamed, and the dream was not kind.

* * *

In the dream it was raining, and Tamsin knew the rain, knew it the way you know a face in a crowd that you've been avoiding for years. It was the rain at Vael's Crossing, the last night of the war, when the mud had been so deep it swallowed the dead standing up. They were in the war camp. The tents leaned under the water. The fires hissed. And everyone was walking in the same direction, all of them, the whole order, hundreds of sworn blades with their hoods down and their swords bare, moving through the rain without a word, and Tamsin's feet were moving too, and they could not stop them.

They walked up the hill to the god's field-tent, the great pavilion of gray wool where the relic of Caerlum was kept—the *heartstone*, the old men called it, though Tamsin had never seen it and had never asked, because some things you serve better blind. The tent flaps were open. The rain ran in.

Inside there was no rain. Inside there was a light like the light through honey, gold and slow, and in the middle of it, on a table of black stone, lay the heart.

It was not what Tamsin had imagined. They had imagined stone, or iron, some dead emblem. It was not dead. It was the size of a child's fist and it moved—contracted, released, contracted—with the patient rhythm of a thing that had been beating since before the mountains were named. The light came from inside it. The light was it.

And around the table stood the high blades of the order, the ones Tamsin had followed into fire and out again, and their faces in the dream were wrong—smooth, calm, the faces of people doing arithmetic. The First Sword held a knife that was not a knife, a thing of gray metal that drank the gold light and gave nothing back.

*No,* Tamsin said in the dream. Their voice made no sound. *No, we swore—we swore to the hearth and the hedge, we swore—*

The First Sword reached down, and took hold of the heart the way you take hold of a coal you intend to carry, and pulled.

Tamsin felt it. That was the cruelty of the dream, that it made them feel it: a tearing, deep in the chest, behind the sternum, as if the heart on the table were threaded through every sworn blade in the order by cords of light, and as it came free the cords snapped one by one, and each snap was a name. Tamsin knew some of the names. Bren of the Low Fells. The twins, Ash and Ila. Serah, who had taught them the oath. Each snap a name, and each name a small death, and Tamsin screaming without sound as the heart came out of the light and the light went out of the world.

The god's voice came last. Not angry. That was the worst of it. Not angry, not betrayed—*tired*, tired the way only the very old and the very kind can be tired, and it said the word again, the word from the morning after, the word with no letters:

*Enough. Let it end with me. Let them have their gray world. Let them sleep.*

And the heart, in the First Sword's bleeding hands, went dark. Not dead. Tamsin knew, in the dream, with the certainty dreams give: not dead. *Caged.* Folded up small and hidden in the earth, in a vault with blood on its gates, beating in the dark for five years, beating and beating, waiting for someone to come and either let it out or let it rest.

Tamsin woke with the taste of gold in their mouth and their hand closed around the hilt of the old sword they'd sworn never to carry again, the one buried at the bottom of their pack under the charm-boxes.

The caravan was stopped. Gray light, not quite dawn, pressed against the wagon's canvas. Somewhere ahead, Doss was swearing at a wheel.

Tamsin lay still and listened to their own heart and hated it a little, that small stubborn muscle, beating in the dark for no one, asking nothing, keeping time for a god who had asked to be forgotten.

*You didn't abandon us,* they thought, and the thought was a wound opening. *You were taken. You were taken and we let them take you, and I spent five years cursing a prisoner for not writing.*

They sat up. Their eyes were wet and they let them be, briefly, in the dark of the wagon, where no one could count it against them.

Then they pulled the map from their pack and unrolled it across their knees, and by the thin gray light read it again, the sigils of the old order marching down its margins, the way to the Hollow Vault drawn in a hand Tamsin almost recognized.

And that was when they saw it—the thing they had missed in a dozen readings, hidden in the border's knotwork, small as a flea: a second mark, inked over the first, in a hand that was not the courier's.

A shield-split-by-a-sword. The mark Veyra had cut into her own pauldron the day she'd sworn, the mark Tamsin had last seen pointing at them across the burning field at the war's end.

The map had been marked. Not for Tamsin.

*For whoever came next.*

Tamsin sat in the cold wagon with the map trembling in their hands, and behind them, far back down the road of ash, they heard—or dreamed they heard, or feared they heard—the creak of wheels that were not Doss's, following.
