## 10. Veyra's Bargain

The Ashen Conclave kept its meeting hall where no honest person would look for it: beneath a tannery on the far side of the river, down stairs that smelled of lime and rotting hide, into a chamber that had been a crypt before it was anything else. Veyra felt the change in the air as she descended—the way she always did, the way she'd once felt it crossing into temple grounds in another life, another war. Old stone held old prayers the way riverbeds held water, long after the river moved on.

Two hooded figures flanked the door. They did not ask her name. They never asked her name; that was the point of her, of what she'd made herself into. A woman with no allegiance was worth ten with one, provided you could afford her, and the Conclave could afford her. She'd seen their ledgers once, by accident or by design—she'd never decided which—and the sums had made her laugh out loud in a room where laughing was forbidden. Gold enough to buy a kingdom. Gold enough to buy a god.

The chamber itself was round, and cold, and lit by a ring of grey candles whose flames burned without flickering, as if even fire here had been taught discipline. Nine chairs stood against the walls. Only five were occupied tonight, and Veyra noted who was absent the way a soldier notes which officers have stayed behind the lines: the ones missing were the ones who feared what was coming. Fear traveled upward through an organization like damp through plaster. She could read this room the way she'd once read battlefields—by what was not there.

"Sit," said the speaker, a thin man called Magister Corvel, whose voice sounded like paper being folded. He occupied the center chair, though he was not the eldest among them. Ambition, too, traveled upward through organizations. "You've come from Greyhallow."

"I've come from watching Greyhallow," Veyra corrected, taking the seat left for her at the ring's edge—an outsider's seat, deliberately placed, deliberately accepted. Let them think she didn't notice these things. People told you everything when they believed you weren't paying attention. "Watching is what you pay me for."

"And what did you see?"

"A charm seller." She let the words sit in the candlelight a moment. "Small shop off Tanner's Row. Sells love charms and luck knots to people who don't believe in either. Harmless. Careful. The kind of careful that comes from having something to hide." She leaned forward. "And carrying a map they don't yet understand, drawn in sigils I last saw burned into a standard at Redford Field."

Corvel's fingers stilled on the armrests. Around the ring, the other four magisters exchanged looks that were meant to be invisible and were not. Veyra catalogued each one. The woman with the silver chain—Doubt. The fat man picking at his cuff—Relief, because he'd wanted someone else to say it first. The two brothers who finished each other's silences—Hunger, both of them, plain as scars.

"You're certain it's them," Corvel said. It wasn't quite a question. It was the question beneath all his questions, the one he'd been asking since the day the god went silent and the Conclave's founding members had realized that silence was not death. Silence was opportunity. A dead god was gone. A silent god was *waiting*.

"The courier died in their shop with Caerlum's name on his lips," Veyra said. "They hid the body themselves rather than call the watch. Guilty people run. Innocent people panic. This one *buried* something." She smiled, and it cost her nothing, because smiling at these people was like smiling at weather. "They'll go to the Vault. Not because they want to. Because they can't help it. I've known Tamsin Thornbury for eleven years, and every choice they've ever made has been made by that god through them. The god went quiet five years ago. Tamsin hasn't noticed the difference."

The magister with the silver chain spoke then—a woman named Ilsette, dry as old vellum. "You speak of your former shield-sister with great certainty. And great contempt."

"Certainty, yes." Veyra met her eyes. "Contempt is yours. I'm just carrying it for you."

Someone at the ring exhaled through their nose—not quite laughter, but its cousin. Even here, even now, Veyra could make rooms do that. It was a skill like any other, honed like any other, and she had sharpened it on whetstones harder than these.

"The terms," Corvel said. "You will follow Thornbury to the Hollow Vault. You will not interfere unless they falter. If the Inquisition takes them, you recover the map from whatever remains. If they reach the Vault and open it—" his voice thinned further, folding itself smaller, "—you will be inside when the ritual begins. That is why we chose you. The blood-locks answer to sworn blades. Yours answered once. Ours never would."

"They still answer," Veyra said quietly. "I tested them. At the boundary stones, on the road east. The old marks drank my blood and warmed." She had not told anyone that. She had knelt alone in the frost with her palm pressed to a weathered sigil and felt it pulse against her skin like something alive beneath the stone, and she had stayed there longer than she meant to, kneeling in the dark like a penitent, hating herself for the comfort of it. "Five years of silence and the locks still know us. Whatever sleeps in that Vault isn't dead. It's *held*."

"Held," Corvel repeated, savoring the word. "Yes. Held, and waiting to be crowned."

That was the phrase. Veyra had heard it three times now, in three different mouths, always with the same reverent hunger: *crowned*. Not freed. Not restored. Crowned. A god on a throne, and hands on the throne's levers. She understood the architecture of the ambition better than most of them did—she'd spent five years understanding it, ever since the first Conclave agent found her drowning her memories in a border tavern and offered her work instead of judgment. A new god, bound at the moment of its waking, shaped before it could shape itself. A deity raised like a weapon and aimed like one. No more silence. No more abandonment. No more gods who loved you and left you bleeding in a field with your oaths still warm in your mouth.

She thought about that word—*abandonment*—as Corvel laid out the payment schedule. Three hundred crowns now. Seven hundred on delivery of the map. The rest upon completion, paid in a city of her choosing, in a currency of her choosing, after which she would be owed nothing by anyone, which was the only wealth she had ever truly wanted. To owe nothing. To be owned by nothing. To walk away from the whole rotten architecture of faith and fealty and stand somewhere in the sun owing the world exactly nothing.

That was the lie she told herself, anyway. She was good at telling it. Eleven years of practice.

"There is one matter remaining," Corvel said, and paused, and Veyra knew immediately—from the pause, from the way the others' breathing changed—that this was the thing they had saved for last, the thing they discussed only when the outsider was already committed. "The ritual demands preparation. The heart cannot simply be crowned. It must first be *willing*."

"Willing," Veyra said flatly.

"Hearts are not thrones," said Ilsette, leaning forward, her voice gaining a strange tenderness, the tenderness of people describing atrocities they've made peace with. "A caged heart beats against its bars. Ours has beaten against its cage for five years, and the beating has grown... erratic. Desperate. A desperate heart cannot be trusted with divinity. It must be calmed. Persuaded. Made to *consent*."

"How do you calm a heart?"

Ilsette's eyes were pale and patient. "With grief," she said. "Grief freely given. The ritual requires a mourner. Someone who loved the god truly, and who will grieve over the heart—not for its loss, but for its suffering. Genuine sorrow, offered willingly, opens what force cannot. The heart hears true grief and stills. And in its stillness, we bind it."

The chamber was very quiet. The grey flames did not flicker. Somewhere above them, faintly, the tannery's machinery began its day, a slow rhythmic pounding like a distant heartbeat, and Veyra thought: *even the building knows.*

"A mourner," she repeated.

"A sworn blade," Corvel corrected gently. "The grief must carry the weight of the old vows. A stranger's tears are water. Yours—or Thornbury's—are blood." He spread his hands, reasonable, almost kind. "We assumed you would nominate Thornbury. Their grief is famous, in the sense that it is legible. They pray at defaced shrines. They kept the vows after the vows stopped meaning anything. The heart would hear them and still itself in an instant."

"And if I'm the mourner?"

The pause this time was longer. Ilsette looked at Corvel. Corvel looked at his own folded hands.

"Then the grief must be genuine," Ilsette said at last. "Which presents difficulties. Your reputation—"

"My reputation is that I survived," Veyra said. "There's a difference between not grieving and grieving well-hidden. Ask anyone who's watched me drink."

But she heard her own voice as she said it, and she knew how it sounded, and she hated how it sounded, and she said it anyway, because the alternative was to admit what had turned over in her chest when Ilsette described the ritual. Because here was the truth she had not planned to find in this cold room under a tannery: some part of her *wanted* to kneel at that heart. Some part of her had wanted it for five years, since Redford Field, since the retreat, since the night she'd walked away from a burning standard and told herself it was strategy. Grief was the one debt she'd never managed to stop paying. She'd sold everything else—her oaths, her loyalty, her name, her memory of loving any of it—but grief had refused every buyer. It sat in her like a tenant who wouldn't leave, and now these grey-robed strangers were offering to make it *useful*. To give her mourning a purpose. To turn the worst thing she carried into the key that opened the cage.

It should have been a relief. Instead it felt like being handed a shovel at her own funeral.

"And afterward?" she asked. "When the heart is bound and crowned. What happens to the mourner?"

"The mourner is honored," Corvel said.

Veyra almost laughed. "Honored. Meaning what? A title? A pension? A statue in a city that doesn't exist yet?"

"Meaning the god remembers," Ilsette said softly. "Whoever grieves it free will be the first hand it knows in its new life. The first name. The favored soul." Her pale eyes gleamed. "You would not be its servant, Veyra. You would be its *first friend*."

*First friend.* Veyra turned the phrase over and found it rotten at the core, and couldn't entirely say why, and that frightened her more than the ritual did. Perhaps because she'd heard language like it before. Perhaps because every cage she'd ever seen had been built by someone who called himself a friend first. Perhaps because she remembered—she still remembered, God help her, she remembered everything, that was her curse, she remembered *everything*—what it was to be someone's favored blade, and how it ended, and where she'd been standing when it ended, and whose blood had been on her gauntlets when it ended.

Favored souls got used up. Favored souls got *aimed*.

"And if the heart refuses?" she asked instead. "If the grief doesn't take?"

Ilsette's tenderness fell away like a mask slipping its cord. "Then the ritual proceeds regardless," she said, "and the heart is bound by force, and the binding of a struggling heart is..." She searched for the word with the delicacy of a woman choosing wine. "...loud. There will be signs. There may be deaths. The city nearest the Vault—there is a town called Marrow's Ford, some six miles east—may need to be evacuated. Or not. Casualties of awakening are acceptable. The Conclave has always been clear on this. A god's birth pangs are not murder."

*A god's birth pangs are not murder.* Veyra wrote the sentence in her head and underlined it twice, because sentences like that were how you knew. That was how you always knew. Five years among the faithless and the faithful alike had taught her the grammar of atrocity, and it never changed: first the passive voice, then the arithmetic, then the noun doing the work so no hand has to. *Casualties occur. Losses are expected. Birth pangs are not murder.* Nobody says *we will kill them*. Somebody says *the ritual demands*, and everyone nods, and six miles away a town full of people who have never prayed to anything goes into the ground.

She looked around the ring of grey candles at the five faces, and she saw them clearly for perhaps the first time: not conspirators, not visionaries, not even fanatics, really. Just people who had found a sentence that let them sleep. She'd been looking for that sentence herself for five years. She'd nearly bought theirs.

"What does the binding require," she said, "beyond grief."

Corvel hesitated. Ilsette did not.

"Anchors," she said. "A bound god needs weight. Living anchors, sworn to the binding, sustained by it, consumed by it—slowly, over years, the way a lamp consumes oil. Nine anchors for nine points of the binding. The Conclave will provide six." Another pause, delicate, terrible. "Three must come from outside. Sworn blades, ideally. Blades whose names the heart already knows."

The tannery pounded above them. The candles burned their disciplined, flickerless burn. Veyra sat very still and did the arithmetic she'd been refusing to do since the moment she'd walked down those stairs: three anchors. Two sworn blades within a hundred miles whom the heart knew by name. Herself. And Tamsin.

And a third.

She thought of the third and her stomach turned over slowly, like a boat shipping water. Because there had been others, once—there had been twelve of them at the last count, sworn blades of Caerlum, and five years of plague and Inquisition and despair had thinned them, but not to nothing. She could name the survivors. She could name them the way you name the dead, because in every way that mattered they were dead already, walking around inside their own silences. Bren of the fords. Little Maera, who'd been sixteen at Redford Field. And—

And the arithmetic did not care what she thought of it. Three anchors. The Conclave would take whoever she gave them, and if she gave them nothing they would take everyone, because that was what organizations like this did with reluctance: they treated it as inventory.

"You mean to burn us," Veyra said. "Slowly. Over years. As fuel."

"To sustain a god," Ilsette said, and her voice had the serene cadence of a lullaby, "is to be sustained by one. The anchors will not suffer. They will be *part of something*. Most faiths ask more and explain less."

Veyra stood up. She hadn't decided to stand; her body simply did it, the way it had once moved on battlefields ahead of her conscious mind, and for a moment she was eleven years younger and standing in a trench at Redford Field listening to an order she should have refused. The grey light caught her face. The five magisters watched her with the patience of people who had watched many soldiers stand up angry and sit back down bought.

"The price is agreed," she said. "Map recovered. Vault reached. My blade where you need it." She took the purse of three hundred crowns from the table without looking at it. "The rest—we'll discuss when I've seen the heart. I don't bind what I haven't weighed."

"Veyra." Corvel's voice, mild, folding itself smaller. "The Conclave prefers its instruments committed."

"The Conclave can prefer whatever it likes." She was already moving toward the door, past the hooded figures, past the smell of lime and hides. "I've been committed before. Ask the god how that went."

The stairs took her up into grey daylight and the reek of the tannery yards, and she breathed it in like a woman surfacing, and it tasted foul and it tasted like honesty, and she stood in the yard among the hanging skins for a long moment with the purse heavy in her hand and the arithmetic sitting in her chest like swallowed stone.

Three anchors. Six miles to Marrow's Ford. A town of people who had never prayed to anything. A heart that beat against its cage, waiting to hear a voice it loved, and two voices it loved within reach, and one of them belonged to a charm seller on Tanner's Row who still prayed at defaced shrines to a god that had never once answered them, and the other belonged to her, and she could not decide which of them the Conclave would find easier to spend.

She knew which one *she* would find easier to spend. That was the problem. That had always been the problem. She could spend Tamsin the way she'd spent everything else—cleanly, efficiently, with a good story attached—and the story was even ready-made: *it's mercy, it's rest, the god wants to sleep, I'm giving them both what they've wanted for five years.* All true, probably. All beside the point, certainly. The best lies were true things arranged badly, and hers was a masterpiece: she could dress a sacrifice up as a kindness and never once have to look at it directly.

Unless she looked at it directly.

She started walking, because walking was what she did with things she couldn't look at. Through the tannery district, across the bridge, into the streets where the morning crowds thickened and jostled and none of them knew that under a building nearby, nine grey candles burned without flickering over plans to crown a god on a bed of borrowed grief. She walked and she counted, the way she always counted: contacts, exits, coin, time. Time most of all. Tamsin would move soon—she'd read it in the shop, in the packed bag hidden behind the counter, in the way they'd started selling off stock. Days, not weeks. And Hale's Inquisition was circling tighter than the Conclave knew, and the roads east were already watched, and somewhere ahead of all of them lay a sealed gate that only sworn blood would open, and behind that gate a heart that had been listening for five years to nothing but its own beating.

Her palm itched. She looked down at it without meaning to—at the old scar across the lifeline, the vow-scar, cut into her the same night it was cut into Tamsin and eleven others, in a shrine that no longer existed, by a priestess who was certainly dead. The scar had ached for five years, on and off, the way old wounds ache before rain. It was aching now. It had ached every day since she'd pressed it to the boundary stone on the road east and felt the stone drink.

*The heart knows our names,* she thought. *It's been saying them for five years. We just stopped listening.*

She crossed into the market quarter as the bells rang noon, and the crowd closed around her, and she let it, because invisibility was also a skill, and she had never stopped practicing. Somewhere ahead, on Tanner's Row, a shop with a crooked sign was selling charms that didn't work to customers who didn't believe, and behind its counter a person she had once loved enough to bleed for was packing a bag and lying to an Inquisitor and preparing to walk into the dark for a god that had abandoned them—or hadn't, she reminded herself, *hadn't*, the murmur of the Vault's truth already working at her like water at mortar—abandoned them, or been taken, or been silenced by the very people who now wanted to wake it wearing a crown.

Ambition said: move first. Move tonight. Get ahead of Thornbury, secure the route, present the Conclave with a fait accompli and negotiate from strength. Ambition had gotten her everything she had. Ambition had gotten her out of Redford Field alive when loyalty got eleven others killed or scattered. Ambition was the only god that had never gone silent on her.

Conscience—which was not a voice, conscience was never a voice, it was a weight, a stone sitting low in the belly where you keep the things you've swallowed—conscience said: *three anchors, Veyra. Say their names. Say all three.*

She said two.

She stood in the market crowd with the bells dying away and the purse of crowns pressing its shape into her palm next to the vow-scar, and she said two names, and the third name stuck in her throat like a fishbone, and she knew—with the terrible clarity that came to her only in moments like this, moments she could not monetize or strategize or survive on—that the third name was the whole bargain. Everything else was negotiable. The third name was the price.

She began to walk toward Tanner's Row.

* * *

Tanner's Row smelled of tallow and wet wool, and the shop's crooked sign swung in a wind that had teeth in it. Veyra did not approach. She had learned patience from professionals, and besides, the scene she needed required darkness. Instead she took up her post across the row, in the doorway of a shuttered chandlery, where the shadow was deep enough to hold her and the angle was right, and she waited, and while she waited she did what she always did in doorways: she rehearsed.

Not the offer. The offer was simple; she'd built simpler cons with more moving parts. *Partnership. Split the risk. Reach the Vault before the Inquisition, before the Conclave, before anyone. Just the two of us, like the old days.* No—the rehearsal was for the moment after the offer, the moment she was actually afraid of, the moment when Tamsin looked at her the way they'd looked at her at the war's end, across the burning standard, and asked her the question she had never answered honestly because she had never once said it aloud to anyone:

*Why?*

She had answers prepared. She had eleven years of answers prepared, ranked and polished, each one load-bearing, each one false in the way that useful things are false. *Survival. Strategy. The order was finished anyway. You'd have done the same.* And underneath them, in the place where she kept the things she'd swallowed, the true answer sat like a stone: *because staying meant dying for something that had already stopped speaking, and I could not tell whether that was faith or madness, and I chose, and I have been choosing the same wrong thing every day since, and I am so tired, Tamsin, I am so tired of being the one who left.*

The afternoon thinned. Lamplighters worked the row. Customers came and went from the charm shop—a girl buying a luck knot, an old man arguing about prices, a nervous clerk who glanced over his shoulder twice. Ordinary commerce. Ordinary lives. Veyra watched them the way she watched everything now, as material, as leverage, as risk, and then—briefly, unwillingly—as what they were: people buying small hopes from someone selling them honestly, which was more religion than the Conclave had ever offered anyone.

Near dusk, the shop's light changed. Tamsin appeared in the doorway, turning the sign, closing for the evening, and Veyra studied them with the naked attention she allowed herself only when observation could pass for surveillance. Thinner than at Redford Field. Careful hands. Eyes that went to the rooftops without appearing to, a habit she recognized because she'd taught it to them, years ago, on a wall at Cael Morath, back when teaching them things had been the point of her days.

*They still check the roofs,* she thought, and something in her chest did the thing it did, the thing she had no name for and refused to acquire one for. *Eleven years, and they still check the roofs.*

The sign turned. The door shut. Light bloomed warm in the window and then dimmed to a single candle's glow, and Tanner's Row emptied itself street by street, and Veyra stood in the chandlery's shadow with the purse of crowns and the vow-scar and the swallowed stone, and she made the decision she had been walking toward all day, the decision she would unmake and remake forty times before dawn, the decision that was not really a decision at all but a surrender to the only gravity she had never escaped.

She would offer partnership. She would split the risk. She would get Tamsin to the Vault—get them both to the Vault—before the Conclave, before the Inquisition, before the ritual could be assembled and the anchors named and the arithmetic completed. And once inside, once the gates were open and the heart was before them, she would decide. That was the bargain she struck with herself, in the chandlery doorway, in the dark: *not now. Inside. When I've seen it. When I've weighed it. When the choice is no longer a sentence in a cold room but a heart beating in front of me.*

It was not a plan. Plans had contingencies. This was a postponement wearing a plan's clothes, and she knew it, and she went forward anyway, because going forward was the only direction she had ever been able to travel, and because somewhere beneath the ambition and the arithmetic and the eleven years of polished lies, there was a girl from the fords who had once sworn an oath in a dead priestess's shrine, and that girl had never signed anything the Conclave put in front of her, and she was still, despite everything—despite Redford Field, despite the standard, despite the purse in her pocket and the stone in her belly—she was still, technically, technically, on duty.

The candle in the shop window guttered once, twice, and steadied.

Veyra stepped out of the shadow, crossed the empty row, and knocked.
