## 12. Hale's Pursuit

The dead smuggler's caravan smelled of pitch, blood, and burnt myrrh—the last of it clinging to everything like a confession that would not wash away.

Inquisitor Maren Hale crouched beside the third body and did not touch it. She had learned long ago that the dead told their stories best to those who kept their hands clean. This one had been young. A knife-man for Doss's outfit, by the leather bracers and the short blade still sheathed at his hip—he had died without drawing it, which meant the killing had been fast, or he had never seen it coming. The wound was a single thrust beneath the ribs, angled upward with a professional's economy. Conclave work. The Ashen Order had always favored the quiet blade; they had never needed to be loud, not when half the old kingdoms owed them favors and the other half owed them fear.

She rose, knees cracking, and looked down the length of the ravine where the ambush had happened. Ash drifted from the scorched wagons in slow grey spirals, settling on her shoulders like a blessing no one had asked for. Somewhere up-slope, one of her wardens was vomiting into the brush. Young. They were all so young, and they all thought the Inquisition was a hammer, and hammers did not need to understand what they struck.

"Two more in the trees, Inquisitor," said Warden Pellik, coming down the trail with his lantern held high. He was breathing hard. "Both cut down running toward the road. And—" he hesitated, and Hale had learned to wait out hesitations, because they always carried the true report, "—the wagon in the middle wasn't carrying goods. It was carrying papers. Orders, maybe. Someone burned them, but not well."

Hale walked to the middle wagon herself.

The fire had taken most of it, but fire was an honest servant and a careless master. What survived was enough: a wax seal, half-melted but recognizable—the ash-tree sigil of the Conclave—and beneath it, a fragment of vellum bearing four words in a clerk's careful hand: *the vessel must be secured alive.*

*Alive.* Not the charm seller who had fled Greyhallow three days ahead of her warrant. Not the courier whose corpse she had viewed in the mortuary cellar with its throat opened by an Inquisitor's own steel—her order's steel, though no warden would admit whose hand. *Alive*, and called a *vessel*, as though a person were a jug to be filled.

Hale folded the scrap into the lead-lined case at her belt, the case she used for things she did not yet want her superiors to know she had found. It was becoming crowded in there. A page torn from a Vault survey, dated forty years past. A list of names—sworn blades of Caerlum, compiled after the god went silent, most of them struck through, two left unmarked. And now this.

Five years she had served the Inquisition of Greyhallow. Five years of burning what burned, of questioning what could still speak, of filing reports that climbed the hierarchy like smoke and came back down as orders she no longer read twice because reading them twice meant understanding them, and understanding them meant choosing, and choosing was the one thing the white cloak made impossible. You wore the color of ash and you did not ask what the fire had been.

But lately the orders had changed flavor. When she had requested reinforcements to pursue the fugitive south along the pilgrim road, the reply from the Chapterhouse had come back within hours—hours, when routine requests took weeks—with a single instruction: *secure the site before any other party*. No mention of the fugitive. No mention of justice for a murdered courier. *Site.* As if the whole affair were not about a woman selling useless charms in the Godless Quarter at all.

Inquisitors were taught that doubt was the first symptom of corruption. Hale had repeated that catechism to a hundred trembling prisoners. Standing in the ash of Doss's caravan, she felt the symptom take root and found she could not bring herself to pray it away.

"Inquisitor?" Pellik again, from the edge of the ravine. His lantern light shook. "There's someone in the rocks above. Alive. Conclave colors under a merchant's coat."

Hale looked up at the dark jumble of stone against the stars.

"Bring them down gently," she said. "I have questions, and I'd rather they be answered by a living tongue."

* * *

They put the prisoner in the waystation chapel because it was the only building with walls thick enough to muffle sound and a floor that scrubbed clean. The village had emptied at the first sight of white cloaks; the priest had left the keys and fled with his family toward Lowmarsh, and so the chapel belonged to the Inquisition now, as everything eventually did.

The agent was a woman of perhaps fifty, grey-streaked hair cropped practical-short, hands bound behind her with consecrated cord. They had found a broken ankle on her—she had come down the rocks badly when taken—and Hale ordered it splinted before anything else. Mercy, or tactics; after fifteen years she no longer troubled herself to know the difference.

"You're Ashen Order," Hale said, taking the chair across from her. "I've seen your work tonight. Clean cuts. Professional."

"I've seen yours too." The woman's voice was dry as old paper. "The courier in Greyhallow. Throat opened by an Inquisitor's blade, left in the Godless Quarter like refuse. Was that professional?"

Hale did not flinch, though the words landed exactly where they were aimed. "That was not sanctioned."

"No," the agent agreed softly. "Very little is, anymore. That's rather the point."

"Name."

"Aldith Crane. Senior factor, Ashen Conclave, twenty-six years' service." She recited it like a woman giving directions to her own house. "You may as well write it down. I'm past caring who knows."

"Why does the Conclave hunt a charm seller through the borderlands? Why kill a smuggler's crew over one fugitive?"

Crane studied her for a long moment. The splinted ankle rested on a stool between them, absurdly domestic.

"You've read the reports from the Vault surveys," Crane said. "Don't look surprised—I know the seal I saw on your satchel, and I know which archives feed which desks. Forty years ago the Inquisition helped bury something in the Hollow Vault. Do you know what it was, Inquisitor? Truly? Not the catechism answer. The catechism says Caerlum fell silent because the world grew too faithless to hold him. A gentle lie. Gods don't die of neglect."

"What do they die of?"

"Knives," said Aldith Crane. "Their own faithful's knives. Torn out of their own chest and buried in the earth so the war would end." She let that sit in the candle-smoke between them. "Your masters know this. Mine certainly do. The difference is that mine intend to do something about it, and yours intend to profit from it quietly."

Hale kept her face still. It was the discipline of a career, and it held, mostly. "Profit how?"

"The heart is not dead. Buried, caged, starving—but not dead. Divinity doesn't rot, Inquisitor, it waits. And there are those in the Chapterhouse—above you, far above you—who have known this since before you took your vows. Why do you think pursuit orders moved at the speed of desperation? Why do you think the courier was silenced by our blade rather than theirs—they wanted him taken, questioned, and then they wanted him gone, and the man who did it will be promoted within the year." Crane's eyes were steady, almost kind. "The Conclave wants to crown a new god. Your masters want to keep the old one in the ground until they can dig it up themselves, on their terms, with their leash ready. Neither side gives a fig for the woman with the map except as the key that opens the door."

"And you're telling me this because?"

"Because I was ordered to secure the vessel alive, and I looked into what 'alive' meant for the ritual, and I broke my ankle coming down off a rock rather than carry it out." For the first time, something moved behind the dry composure—a tremor, quickly mastered. "Twenty-six years, Inquisitor. I have burned villages for lesser purposes than what's written in those orders. Ask me what the ritual demands."

Hale did not want to ask. She asked anyway, because that was the job, because the catechism said doubt was corruption but curiosity was diligence, and the line between them had never been drawn anywhere she could stand.

"What does the ritual demand?"

Crane told her.

It did not take long. That was the horror of it—it fit into a few sentences, delivered flatly, while the candles guttered and the splinted ankle sat on its stool like an offering. Hale listened, and somewhere in the listening, the white cloak stopped feeling like cloth and started feeling like a skin she had grown into without noticing, one that would not come off without taking more than she had planned to give.

When Crane finished, the silence in the chapel was total. Even the wind outside seemed to be holding its breath.

"That's not a ritual," Hale said finally. Her voice sounded strange to her, distant, like someone else speaking through a wall. "That's a harvest."

"Now you understand why the roads are watched from both directions," said Crane. "And why neither of us can go home. The question, Inquisitor—the only question that matters tonight—is what a white cloak does when she learns the ash she wears was never holy. There are three parties converging on the Hollow Vault. The Conclave. Your Chapterhouse. And one woman who, if my people's reports hold true, has already bled on the Vault's door." Crane leaned forward as far as her bonds allowed. "Which side of that door will you be standing on when it opens?"

Hale stood. She took the chair back to its place against the wall, precisely, the way she did everything, and found her hands were not quite steady.

"Keep her alive," she told Pellik at the chapel door. "Feed her. Splint stays clean. If anyone from the Chapterhouse arrives asking about a prisoner, tell them she died of her wounds."

Pellik blinked. "And if they ask us, Inquisitor?"

"Then we'll have a different conversation than the one you're dreading." She pulled her hood up against the cold. Outside, the ash from the burned caravan still hung in the air, fine as flour, tasting of myrrh and endings. "Get the men mounted. We ride for the marsh country within the hour."

"The Vault, ma'am? The Chapterhouse orders say—"

"I know what the orders say." Hale looked east, toward the black line of the hills, beyond which lay the marshes and the shrine country and, somewhere beneath old stone, a god's stolen heart waiting in the dark like a coal that refused to cool. Five years of catechism, five years of clean hands and unread reports, and every hour of it had led here—to a fork with no loyal road.

"Pellik," she said quietly. "How would you feel about learning what we actually serve?"

The warden looked at her the way soldiers look at officers who have just begun to burn. Then, slowly, he nodded once.

Hale turned her horse east, and the night swallowed the road, and somewhere ahead of her—she could feel it now, the way one feels weather coming—a silence older than the city was waiting to be broken.
