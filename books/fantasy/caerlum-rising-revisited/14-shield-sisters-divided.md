## 14. Shield-Sisters Divided

The marsh breathed at dusk the way a dying man breathes—shallow, uneven, each exhale carrying something away that would not come back. Veyra crouched at the edge of the black water where the reeds grew tall as spears, and she watched the shrine's small light gutter in the distance like a candle cupped against wind. Someone was inside it. She had tracked them for three days through the ash country, and every sign said the same thing: Tamsin Thornbury moved slower now, prayed now, walked like a person carrying something fragile in both hands.

Veyra knew that walk. She had seen it once before, on the last morning of the war, when Tamsin had stood in the ruins of the chapel at Karst Hollow and refused to burn it.

She had thought about killing them there. That was the truth of it, the truth she kept folded up tight beneath her breastplate where even the Conclave couldn't reach. On the road out of Karst Hollow, with the smoke still rising and their oaths still warm as coals in their mouths, Veyra had drawn her knife and considered opening her shield-sister's throat—not from hatred, but because she had already begun to understand what Tamsin was, what they had always been: an obstacle dressed as a friend.

She hadn't done it. Five years later she still wasn't sure whether that was mercy or cowardice, and the not-knowing sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

Now the marsh light flickered, and Veyra made her choice again, the same choice, over and over, every night since Greyhallow. Not yet. Not here. Not while the Conclave's agents were still two days behind her and Hale's riders were somewhere to the east, closing like a hand around a fist.

She rose from the reeds and walked toward the shrine, and the water took her boots to the ankle, cold as confession.

* * *

Tamsin was sitting cross-legged before the little altar when she filled the doorway, and for a moment—one long, suspended moment—neither of them moved. The shrine was no bigger than a granary, its walls furred with moss, its single window a slit that framed a stripe of dying sky. A stone figure of Caerlum stood on the altar, headless, hands broken off at the wrist, and someone long ago had set a bowl of river-water at its feet. In the water floated a single white flower that had no business blooming this far north, this late in the year.

Veyra stood in the doorway with her sword still sheathed and her knives where they always were, and she watched Tamsin look up at her.

There was a moment, in the first year after the silence, when Veyra had believed she could describe Tamsin Thornbury in a sentence. *A sworn blade who lost their god.* It had seemed simple then, clean, the kind of grief you could carry in one hand. But grief was never one-handed. Grief was a door that opened onto other doors, and behind each one was another version of the person you'd loved, and another, and another, until you understood that you had never known them at all—you had only ever known the shape they made against your own life.

"Tamsin," she said, and her voice came out rougher than she meant it to, scraped raw by three days of ash roads and bad water. "You look like hell."

"You look like money," Tamsin said. "New armor. New blades. Someone's paying."

The words landed between them like a dropped torch. Veyra felt her jaw tighten. Of course Tamsin knew. Tamsin had always been able to read her the way soldiers read weather—not the details, never the details, but the pressure, the direction of the coming storm. And Veyra had always let them believe they were wrong about the worst of it.

"I'm not going to apologize for surviving," she said. "We both sold ourselves after Karst Hollow. Yours just happened to be charms that don't work."

"Mine doesn't cost anyone anything." Tamsin's hand drifted toward the old shortsword lying across their knees—the sworn blade, the one they'd carried since before the war, the one Veyra had watched them sharpen a thousand times in a thousand camps. "Yours does. I've heard what the Conclave wants. I've heard what they're willing to do to get it."

"And what have you heard, exactly?" Veyra stepped inside, and the doorway framed her like a painting of everything Tamsin had lost. "Rumors? Doss's gossip? Or did one of my patrons' little birds find you on the road?"

"Doss is dead." Tamsin said it flatly, without looking up, and Veyra felt something twist in her gut that was not quite guilt and not quite relief. "Conclave agents hit the caravan two days east of here. They killed him for harboring me. So yes—I know what your masters do to people who get in their way. I know what they'll do to me when they catch me at the Vault. And I know you're the one they sent to make sure I don't get there first."

For a long moment the only sound was the marsh outside, the small wet machinery of frogs and wind, and the flower turning slowly in its bowl of water as if it were trying to face them both.

Veyra unbuckled her sword belt. She set it against the wall, deliberately, where Tamsin could see her doing it, and then she sat down across the altar from them, close enough that their knees nearly touched in the narrow space.

"If I wanted you dead," she said quietly, "you'd be dead. You know that. You've always known that."

"I know you're good enough to make it look like an accident." But Tamsin didn't move away either. Their eyes, in the shrine's dim light, were the same eyes Veyra had memorized across ten years of campfires—grey-green, watchful, always half a conversation ahead of the one actually happening. "So talk, Vey. Talk fast, and talk true, because I'm done being managed by people who love me."

*That* word. Veyra flinched from it the way a hand flinches from a stove, and Tamsin saw her flinch, and filed it away, because Tamsin always filed things away.

* * *

They talked until the sky through the slit window went from copper to violet to a black so complete it looked solid. They talked the way they used to talk on watch, in the years when watch-talk was the only honest language either of them spoke—low, unhurried, circling the big things the way wolves circle fire.

Veyra told them about the Ashen Conclave. All of it, or nearly all. Nine magistrates, old families, old gold, survivors of the war's losing side who had spent five years buying relics and bribing archivists and digging through the buried records of what had happened at the Hollow Vault. They believed Caerlum's heart still beat in the dark down there. They believed a god could be regrown from it—slowly, carefully, fed on ritual and obedience until it woke strong enough to matter. And they believed that if they were the ones holding the leash when it woke, the old kingdoms would kneel, and the Inquisition would burn, and the world would be remade in the image of the people who had suffered most under the old order.

"They call it restoration," Veyra said. "They dress it in mourning-clothes. *We lost our god, we lost our dead, we deserve what was taken.* And maybe some of them even believe it. Magistrate Corvane believes it—he weeps when he says it, actual tears, and I've watched men follow weeping men into worse things than this."

"And you?" Tamsin asked. "Do you believe it?"

Veyra turned the question over the way she'd turn a blade, checking the edge.

"I believe the world broke something when it broke Caerlum," she said finally. "I believe the Inquisition's peace is a lie built on a graveyard, and everyone in Greyhallow knows it and pretends they don't. I believe power doesn't rot unless it's held by people who never had any reason to fear it—and the Conclave has spent five years afraid. Fear keeps you honest longer than virtue does." She paused. "And I believe that if I don't stand next to the heart when it wakes, someone worse will. That's all. That's the whole of my faith. It isn't much, but it holds weight."

Tamsin was quiet for so long that Veyra began to count their breathing, an old habit from the field, checking whether the person beside you was asleep or composing themselves for violence.

Then Tamsin said: "They tore it out themselves. Did you know that? The followers. The sworn blades. Our own order." Their voice cracked on *our*, and Veyra heard five years fall out of it at once. "The murals in the Vault—or what I could read of them, from the map's marginalia, from the courier's notes before the blood got them—Caerlum wanted the war ended, and the only way to end a war fought in a god's name was to take the god's name away. So they took the heart. They unmade their own divinity to starve the fighting. The silence wasn't abandonment, Vey. It was surgery."

"How do you know that's true?"

"I don't. Not yet. That's why I'm going." Tamsin looked at her across the broken altar, and their expression was one Veyra had only seen once before, on the last morning of the war, and it frightened her more than any threat had. "But if it's true—if our order chose mercy over miracle, if they cut out the part of the god that makes wars—then the Conclave isn't restoring anything. They're reopening the wound. They're pulling the blade back out of the body to see if it still bleeds."

"And you want to stop them." Veyra leaned forward. "Or you want to get there first."

Silence. The flower turned in its bowl.

"I want the god back," Tamsin said at last, and saying it aloud seemed to cost them something physical; Veyra watched their shoulders drop as though a weight had been set down and immediately regretted. "There. I said it. No doctrine, no strategy, no noble reason. I want to pray and be answered. I want the silence to end. I have spent five years selling charms that don't work to people who need them to, and every single day I have wondered if the god I bled for ever heard me at all, or if I was talking into a grave. If the heart can wake—even caged, even leashed, even wrong—I want to stand in front of it and ask it one question. Just one. *Why?*" They laughed, short and bitter. "That's my whole ambition, Vey. One answer. The Conclave wants a kingdom. I want a conversation."

It should have sounded small. It didn't. It sounded like the most dangerous thing Veyra had heard in five years, because kingdoms could be bought and leashes could be forged, but a conversation with a god—unscripted, unwitnessed, uncontrolled—was the one outcome the Conclave's entire architecture existed to prevent.

And it was the one outcome Veyra, in the secret room behind her own ribs, wanted too.

* * *

Later, when the marsh had gone fully dark and the frogs had handed the night over to the owls, they lay on opposite sides of the shrine with their backs against the walls, weapons within reach, the way soldiers sleep when they don't trust the ground. Neither slept. Veyra could tell by the breathing.

"Do you remember the ford at Ellowmere?" Tamsin asked the ceiling.

"I remember you carrying me out of it." Veyra closed her eyes. The memory came whole, unbidden, the way the oldest ones did: the river running red, the arrows coming down like sleet, Tamsin wading hip-deep through bodies with Veyra's arm across their shoulders, Veyra's leg opened to the bone, both of them screaming at each other not because they were angry but because screaming was the only prayer left that anyone answered. "You were supposed to leave me. The retreat order was clear."

"The retreat order was stupid."

"You disobeyed a direct command to save me, and they gave you a commendation for it, which taught us both exactly how much oaths are worth."

Tamsin laughed—a real laugh, rusty, startled out of them, and Veyra treasured it the way you treasure anything you suspect you're hearing for the last time. "And then two years later you stood in front of the tribunal at Karst Hollow and testified that I'd been compromised by grief. That my judgment couldn't be trusted. That's when the order fell apart, Vey. Not when the god went silent. When you went silent."

The words went in deep, where she kept nothing armored, and Veyra let them sit there without flinching, because flinching would have been a lie and she had decided—walking through the reeds, hours ago—that tonight she would not lie.

"I testified what was true," she said. "Your judgment *couldn't* be trusted. You wanted to march on the Vault with forty exhausted blades and dig out whatever the silence was hiding, and it would have gotten every one of them killed, and you knew it, and you wanted it anyway. That's not compromise. That's just grief wearing armor." She opened her eyes and found Tamsin's across the dark shrine. "I betrayed you to save you, and it destroyed us both, and I have spent five years telling myself that makes it different from betrayal. It doesn't. I know it doesn't. I did it anyway, because I am the kind of person who does the arithmetic first and the grieving after, and you are the kind of person who grieves first and does the arithmetic never, and that is the entire history of us in one sentence."

Tamsin was quiet. Then: "That's the truest thing anyone's ever said to me."

"It doesn't fix anything."

"No. Nothing fixes anything anymore." Tamsin shifted against the wall, and Veyra heard the old sword scrape stone. "But it means we can walk together without pretending we're walking together for good reasons. You're using me to reach the heart for your masters. I'm using you because you're the best fighter I've ever served beside and the roads ahead are full of Conclave agents and Inquisitors. We both know it. We say it out loud, and then nobody gets to feel surprised."

Veyra almost smiled. Almost. Her mouth made the shape of it and stopped halfway, the way her mouth had done for five years.

"There's one more thing," she said, and her voice dropped, and the shrine seemed to lean in. "Corvane showed me the rite. The binding ritual. What it takes to cage a waking god." She waited until Tamsin looked at her. "It takes a sworn blade. Living. Willing. The heart needs an anchor—someone whose vows are still written into it, someone the god remembers. The Conclave bought my oath years ago, Tamsin. Before I ever came to Greyhallow. My orders aren't to bring you the map's destination. My orders are to bring *you*."

The silence afterward was total. Even the marsh seemed to hold its breath.

"So this is the trap," Tamsin said finally, and their voice was terribly calm, the calm of a person standing in a burning house counting the exits. "You tell me this now, in a shrine, at night, with your sword against the wall. Why?"

"Because I'm not going to do it." Veyra said it plainly, flatly, without drama, and somehow that made it heavier than any oath she'd ever sworn. "I read the rite four times. Do you know what 'willing' means, in the old liturgy? It doesn't mean you consent once. It means you consent forever. The anchor goes into the heart and stays there—alive, aware, dreaming the god's dreams, for as long as the god endures. Centuries. They showed me the accounts of the last anchors, from before the war. They don't die, Tamsin. They *persist*. And I looked at those accounts and I looked at Corvane's weeping face and I understood that I was never going to be his blade. I was going to be his material."

"So you warn me instead. You burn your patron, your payment, your plan. For what?"

"For the same reason you pulled me out of Ellowmere." Veyra's throat tightened around it, and she let it. "Because the arithmetic finally came out wrong. Because I've done the sums every way they can be done, and every sum ends with me kneeling in the dark inside a god's heart, forever, watching the Conclave wear the world like a coat—and I would rather die on a marsh road tomorrow than persist in that dark for a thousand years." She reached across the dark floor and touched Tamsin's boot, lightly, the way soldiers touch each other when words are spent. "So: no trust. No forgiveness. No lies. Two people who owe each other everything and hate each other for it, walking the same road because the alternative is walking it alone. That's the offer. It's the only one I have."

Tamsin looked at her for a long time. Long enough that Veyra began, involuntarily, to count their breaths again.

Then Tamsin picked up the old sworn blade from the floor, weighed it in their hand, and laid it down between them—halfway, equidistant, touching neither.

"We leave before dawn," they said. "East road, past the drowned chapel. And Vey—if you're lying, if any part of this is the Conclave's script, I will put this sword through you and I won't grieve first. I'll do the arithmetic first. I've learned."

"I'd expect nothing less," Veyra said. "It's why I'm here."

Outside, far off across the black water, a light appeared—small, moving, deliberate. A lantern, traveling the eastern road in the dead hours, where no honest traveler traveled. Then a second light behind it. Then a third.

Tamsin saw it at the same moment Veyra did. Their eyes met across the broken altar, across five years and one shared sword, and neither of them said a word, because neither of them needed to.

Dawn was coming early, and it was bringing company.
