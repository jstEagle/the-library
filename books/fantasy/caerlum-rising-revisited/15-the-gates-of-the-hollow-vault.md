## 15. The Gates of the Hollow Vault

They walked the last league in silence, because the marsh had taken their voices.

Not literally—Tamsin had learned long ago that the world rarely bothered with literal cruelty—but the fog that rose off the black water at first light swallowed sound the way it swallowed everything else. Footsteps went in and did not come back out. Breath went in and did not come back out. Even the reeds, when the wind moved through them, made no whisper, only a slow silent bending, like a crowd bowing before a procession that never arrived.

Tamsin had not spoken in an hour. Veyra had not spoken in two. It was, Tamsin thought, the most honest conversation they'd had since the war.

The ground firmed beneath their boots as the marsh shallowed, peat giving way to old stone buried just under the skin of the world. Tamsin felt it through the soles of their feet before they saw it—a vibration, faint and rhythmic, like a heartbeat heard through a wall. They stopped. Veyra stopped a half-pace later, close enough that Tamsin could hear her breathing, which was the only sound left in the world.

"You feel it," she said. Not a question.

"I feel it."

"Five years, and it's still beating." Veyra's voice was strange—stripped of its calculation, its charm, all the armor she'd worn since Greyhallow. What was left underneath sounded almost young. "I used to think it was the mountain. Some river under the rock. Every blade in the order felt it on watch, and every one of us pretended it was the mountain."

"And now?"

"Now I think we were lying to ourselves the way soldiers lie. Comfortably. Together."

The fog thinned as they climbed, peeling back in ragged veils, and the Hollow Vault revealed itself the way a wound reveals itself when the bandage comes away—suddenly, and all at once, and worse than remembered.

It was set into the face of a valley that had no name on any map Tamsin had ever seen, a bowl of gray stone ringed by hills like cupped hands. The Vault itself was not a building. Buildings had doors and windows, roofs and gutters, the modest architecture of human life. This was a *seal*. A single wall of black basalt, fifty feet high and twice as wide, set into the mountain's flank as if the mountain had grown around it, had swallowed it, had spent centuries trying to digest it and failed. No windows. No battlements. Only the wall, and set into the wall's center—

The gates.

They had been bronze once. Tamsin remembered them bronze, remembered the green-gold shimmer of them in torchlight, remembered pressing a palm to their cold surface at nineteen years old and swearing an oath that had seemed, at the time, like the simplest thing in the world. Now they were black. Not tarnished—*blacked*, as if fire had passed over them and taken the color as its toll. And across their surface, in lines that caught the gray dawn light and refused to let it go, ran the sigils.

Tamsin knew every one of them. That was the cruelty of it. Five years of silence, five years of selling dead charms to frightened people, and the sigils still lived in Tamsin's hands the way a language lives in the mouth of someone forbidden to speak it. *Veth-kar. The binding of the gate. Ilum-drae. The sealing of the blood. The third line, the fourth, the ninth*—each one a word in a sentence Tamsin's body could read even as Tamsin's mind screamed at it to forget.

"They're still lit," Veyra breathed.

They were. Faint as embers under ash, a dull red glow moved through the sigils' channels, slow as syrup, patient as anything that has waited five years and expects to wait five hundred more.

"The order sealed them with a death-oath," Tamsin said. Their own voice sounded foreign in the soundless valley. "I told you that."

"You told me a lot of things on that road. Half of them were to see if I'd flinch." Veyra crouched, studying the base of the wall, where the stone met the buried foundations. "The Conclave's scholars think the seal can be broken with enough blood. Any blood. A hundred head of cattle, if it came to it."

"It can't."

"I know." She looked up, and the fog-light made her face hollow, older. "I've known since the shrine. It has to be *sworn* blood. It has to be yours."

Tamsin walked forward alone.

There was no decision in it—that was the thing Tamsin would remember afterward, in the fragments of memory that would survive the coming hours, the ones that would surface later like drowned things and be examined with no understanding of what they were. There was no moment of choosing. There was only the valley, and the wall, and the gates, and the pull in Tamsin's chest that had been there since the courier died on the shop floor with a god's name in his mouth. Five years of silence, and the silence had never actually been empty. It had been a held breath. And held breaths end.

Up close, the gates were taller than memory had made them. The sigils rose past Tamsin's head, past the reach of any ladder, curling into patterns that the eye could not hold all at once—the old problem, the old beauty, the script of a god written by hands that had loved him. Tamsin raised their hand and stopped it an inch from the bronze-black surface.

"If I open this," Tamsin said, without turning, "there's no closing it. You understand that. The Inquisition will come. The Conclave will come. Whatever's in there—" Tamsin's throat tightened around the words. "Whatever's in there comes out, one way or another. There's no walking back to the shop after this. No more charms. No more Greyhallow. No more *nobody*."

"I know what it costs," Veyra said. "I've been counting since the war."

"That's the problem with you, Vey. You've always counted." Tamsin almost smiled. "I never learned how. It's why I was the shield and you were the blade."

"And look which one of us the god kept."

The words landed like a knife between ribs, and Tamsin knew—knew with the sick certainty of old wounds—that she hadn't meant them as cruelty. That was the worst of Veyra. She only ever told the truth when it could do the most damage.

Tamsin drew the knife.

Not the shop knife, not the little bone-handled thing that trimmed wicks and opened crates. The other one. It had ridden at the small of Tamsin's back for five years, wrapped in oilcloth, carried from city to city like a guilt no pawnbroker would touch. The sworn blade. Caerlum's mark still ran down the fuller—a single line, like a river, like a scar. Tamsin had told themselves a hundred times that they kept it for its value, for the day the Inquisition's heat faded and a buyer could be found. They had never once believed it.

The blade came free with a sound like a sigh.

Tamsin opened their palm along the edge—one clean stroke, the old rite, the first lesson every sworn blade learned at fourteen kneeling in a cold temple while an elder intoned the words. *The god does not take. The god receives.* Blood welled bright and shocking against the gray morning, and Tamsin pressed the palm flat against the gate.

For a moment, nothing. The valley held its silence. Veyra's breath stopped behind them. Somewhere far off, Tamsin thought they heard the marsh exhale.

Then the sigils *woke*.

Light poured through the channels like water through a broken dam—not the ember-dull red of the seal's long patience but a white-gold blaze, fierce and alive, and Tamsin felt it answer the blood, felt it *recognize* the blood, felt the gate know them the way a dog knows its master's step after years at a stranger's door. Heat bloomed under Tamsin's palm. Not burning. *Welcoming.* And beneath the heat, beneath the light, beneath everything—

A heartbeat.

Not the buried vibration of the valley. A real one, vast and slow, shaking the basalt, shaking Tamsin's teeth, shaking five years of careful numbness apart like wet paper. *There you are,* said something that was not a voice, that came up through the bones rather than the ears. *There you are, little blade. I felt every step.*

Tamsin staggered back. The gates were moving.

They parted along a seam Tamsin had never known was there, grinding inward with a sound like a mountain clearing its throat, and the light that came out was not dawn-light, was not torchlight, was not any light that belonged to the world above. It was the color of the oath Tamsin had sworn at nineteen. It was the color of the last morning of the war. It rolled down the valley floor and touched the hills and made them glow like coals, and Tamsin stood in it with blood running off their fingertips and felt, for the first time in five years, *seen*.

"No," Veyra said, very quietly. "Oh, no. Tamsin—listen."

Tamsin listened.

The valley was no longer silent. The sound that filled it came from everywhere at once, from the hills, from the marsh, from the buried stone underfoot: a low brazen tone, rising and falling, rising and falling. Not an echo of the gates. A signal. The old alarm of the order, the one drilled into every sworn blade's memory—*when the gate opens unbidden, the valley sings, and every ear sworn to Caerlum hears it, and every enemy too.*

"They'll have heard it in Doss's crossing," Veyra said. She had her blades out; Tamsin hadn't seen her draw them. "They'll have heard it in Greyhallow."

"Hale."

"Hale. And the Conclave. And God knows what else is camped on those roads waiting for exactly this." She grabbed Tamsin's shoulder, spun them around, and her eyes were the eyes Tamsin remembered from the war's worst hour—wide, fierce, already fighting. "How long do we have?"

Tamsin did the arithmetic the way they'd once done it on battlefields, with blood on their hands and no time to be afraid. The alarm would carry twenty miles in this valley, thirty in the right wind. Hale had been a day behind them at the marsh. The Conclave's agents, less. Riders would already be moving.

"Hours," Tamsin said. "Perhaps less."

"Then we don't stand in the doorway admiring the view."

The gates had opened to the width of a coffin, and beyond them the light went down and down into the mountain, down stairs cut from living rock, down toward the heartbeat that shook the dark. Tamsin looked at it. Tamsin looked at their own bleeding hand, at the sworn blade in the other, at the five years of silence that had led here like a rope.

Somewhere behind them, faint but unmistakable, carried up the eastern road on the wind that had finally remembered how to blow—a sound like hooves.

Many hooves.

Veyra heard it too. She didn't waste breath on curses. She simply stepped through the gates into the god-light, blades low, and waited, and Tamsin—blood still dripping, heart hammering in time with something vast and caged and *awake*—followed her in.

Behind them, the gates began to grind shut.

And in the valley above, the riders came on.
