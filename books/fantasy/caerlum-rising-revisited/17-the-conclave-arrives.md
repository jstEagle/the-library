## 17. The Conclave Arrives

Veyra heard the horns before she saw the valley.

They came down from the northern ridgeline in a long, braided wail—three notes, then a fourth, then silence, the way the Ashen Conclave always sounded their arrival. Not a warning. A summons. *We are here,* the horns said. *Kneel or burn.*

She stood at the lip of the hollow where the Vault had slept for five years, and she watched them come down the switchback trails like a dark tide finding its level. Forty of them in ash-grey robes over mail the color of old iron, faces masked with lacquered half-moons that caught the dying light and gave nothing back. Behind them came the burden-bearers with the ritual cage on their shoulders—a thing of blackened silver and chained glass, built to hold something vast in something small—and behind *them*, walking apart as hermits walk apart from plague carts, came Magister Corvane.

He was smaller than she remembered. That was the first cruelty of it. Five years of hearing his voice through conclave doors, five years of imagining the architect of the new god as some colossus of purpose—and here he was, a narrow man in a scholar's robe, hands folded into his sleeves, stepping carefully over the bones of an old battlefield as though afraid of staining his hem.

*You are afraid of him,* Veyra told herself, and made herself believe it was fear and not disappointment.

"Shield-Veyra." His voice carried without effort, dry as paper in wind. "You have done well."

She did not kneel. None of the others noticed; they were too busy fanning out across the hollow, driving iron stakes into the earth at the points marked on her maps, muttering the binding-canticles under their breaths. The ground here remembered war. She could feel it through her boots—the old ache of it, like a scar that ached before rain.

"The gates are open," she said. "The sworn blade went inside an hour past. Alone."

Corvane paused at the edge of the stairwell cut into the hillside, where torchlight bled upward out of the dark. He looked down into it the way a man looks down a well, checking for his reflection.

"Alone," he repeated softly. "Good. A lone blade can be bound. A blade with friends is a complication." He turned those pale, patient eyes on her. "You will go down and bring them to me. Alive. Unspoiled. The rite requires blood freely given by a sworn hand, and there are no other sworn hands left in the world but yours and theirs."

The words landed in her stomach like swallowed stones. She had known this was coming. She had written the plan herself, in her own neat courier's hand, and delivered it to men whose faces she had never seen. But knowing a thing and standing in front of it were different countries, and she had just crossed the border.

"And if they refuse?"

"They will not refuse." Corvane smiled, and it was the worst smile she had ever seen, because it held no malice at all. Only certainty. "You were shield-sisters. You shared a tent, a table, a god. When you offer them a place at your side in what comes next, they will take it. People want to believe their suffering meant something, Shield-Veyra. You above all should understand that."

She understood. Gods, she understood. That was the whole rotting heart of it—that she *did* understand, that every step of the last five years had been walked by a woman who knew exactly what she was doing and called it survival, then strategy, then destiny, until the word fit so snugly she could no longer see the seams.

"I'll need my blades," she said. "In case."

"You will need nothing," Corvane said, already turning away, already ascending toward the stake-marked perimeter where his canticle-chanters were setting up their work. "You are not going down as a soldier. You are going down as a memory. Memories don't carry weapons."

* * *

The stairs were older than the war, older than the silence, maybe older than the kingdom that had buried this place. Veyra counted them out of habit—two hundred and six steps, each one worn into a shallow bowl by feet that had climbed them when the god still answered—and by the hundredth step the air had changed. Warmer. Thicker. It tasted faintly of copper and candle-smoke and something underneath both, something like the moment after lightning.

Her torch found the murals first.

She had read Corvane's reports. She had studied the fragments the Conclave's agents had smuggled out over the years, sketches and secondhand descriptions, enough to know what waited on these walls. Knowing did not prepare her.

Caerlum's people had painted their own hands red. That was the first thing. Row upon row of sworn blades and shrine-keepers and humble market-priests, all of them reaching together toward the radiant figure at the center of the wall—not in worship. In *taking*. And the figure—she had seen that face a thousand times across a thousand shrines, stern and bright and beloved—was looking back at them not with rage but with something worse. With trust. With the terrible, unguarded trust of a parent who cannot imagine what the child holds behind its back.

Below it, in the oldest paint, the artists had written the only words on any wall: *NOT ABANDONED. UNMADE. FORGIVE US THE MERCY.*

Veyra stood in front of that sentence until her torch guttered, and she thought about the night the god went silent, and how she had knelt in a shrine in the ruins of Carrowmere with Tamsin's shoulder against hers, and how they had both wept, believing themselves orphaned. Believing themselves *left*. Five years of anger built on a foundation of paint and lies, and the liars were dead, and the lie had raised two soldiers who now stood on opposite sides of a stolen heart.

*Tamsin doesn't know yet.* The thought arrived cold and clear. *They went in blind. They're reading these walls right now.*

And Corvane wanted her to bring that person up in chains, still believing, so that the last thing a sworn blade ever felt was the certainty of abandonment—because a blade that believed itself abandoned would give its blood to anyone who offered it a new purpose. That was the arithmetic of the rite. That was why they needed her. Not her swords. Her *face*. Her history. The shape of an old love, hollowed out and used as a door.

Veyra looked at her own reflection in the wet stone, torchlight swimming across it, and saw a woman she had been careful never to look at directly for five years.

Then she put out the torch and kept climbing down into the warm dark, and began, quietly, to plan her betrayal.

* * *

She found Tamsin in the heart-chamber.

Not approaching it—no one approached it; the space around the caged radiance kept its own distance, a ring of scorched floor where even dust would not settle—but standing at the chamber's far side, before the final mural, one hand pressed flat against the stone. Their sword was still sheathed. Their shoulders were shaking.

"Tamsin."

They didn't turn. Of course they didn't turn. They had felt her coming since the stairs; sworn blades always knew each other's footsteps, even after five years, even across the width of a world.

"How many?" Tamsin asked. Their voice was raw gravel. "How many came down behind you?"

"Forty on the rim. Corvane among them. Chanters, stake-irons, a cage." She let the numbers sit between them like drawn knives. "I'm supposed to bind you and walk you up the stairs with a smile on my face. Your blood, freely given. That's the whole ritual, in the end. That's all it ever needed."

Now they turned. Their eyes were red-rimmed and lit from within by the heart's glow, and Veyra saw that they had been crying, and that they were past being ashamed of it, and that some part of them had already moved miles beyond grief into a country she couldn't follow yet.

"You've known," Tamsin said. "All along. You knew what the silence was."

"I learned three years ago." She said it plainly, because plainness was the only coin she had left. "I sat where you're sitting. I read the walls. And I made a choice, and I told myself it was for us—for the survivors—and then I stopped telling myself anything at all, because telling myself things was how I survived, and I was tired of lying even to me." She drew a slow breath. "I sold you to them, Tam. On paper. Before I ever came to Greyhallow. I want you to hear that from my mouth and not theirs."

Something moved behind Tamsin's eyes—hurt, fury, the old battlefield reflex that sorted every blow into *blockable* and *not*. Then, unbelievably, exhaustion won, and they almost smiled.

"I know," they said. "I figured it out on the road. Doss died for it." A breath. "Why tell me now? Why come down here at all? You could have let them take me sleeping."

"Because I've spent five years being the knife someone else aims," Veyra said, "and I am so very tired of other people's hands on my hilt."

She unbuckled her sword belt and let it fall.

The sound of it rang through the chamber like a struck bell, and Tamsin stared at the fallen weapon as though it might be another mural-trick, another mercy-lie. Above them both, faintly, through two hundred feet of stone, the Conclave's canticles had begun—the low, rhythmic drone of the binding-rite assembling itself, patient as tide.

"Corvane expects you in chains," Veyra said. "So here's what he gets instead. You run for the stairs while I hold the chamber. I bought us maybe four minutes before they send someone down after me—they think I'm talking you around. Four minutes is nothing, and it's everything, and it's all I've got."

"No." Tamsin said it flatly. "Together. We fight up together."

"Together we die halfway, and the heart gets its freely-given blood anyway, just spilled instead of poured." She stepped closer, close enough to see the pulse jumping in their throat. "Tam. Listen to me. Whatever you saw in the murals—whatever the walls told you about the day the god went quiet—you have to be the one to decide what happens next. Not Corvane. Not the Conclave. Not the god, if the god's still in there whispering. *You.* That's the only freedom anyone ever actually gets. I finally understood it about ten minutes ago, standing in a hallway, and I'd like at least one of us to live long enough to use it."

Tamsin's jaw worked. Five years ago Veyra would have read every muscle of that face like a dispatch; now she could barely find the person she'd known beneath the scar tissue of the silence. But the eyes were the same eyes that had dragged her out of the burning shrine at Carrowmere, and they made the same calculation now they'd made then: *the living mission over the dead pride.*

"If you die down here," Tamsin said quietly, "I'll never forgive you."

"Good." Veyra smiled, and it was a real one, crooked and sharp, the first honest thing she'd owned in years. "Hold onto that. Hate travels farther than grief. Now *go*."

* * *

They came down the stairs ninety seconds later—four of them, chanters with iron rods and a stake-master with a face like a folded map—and Veyra met them on the landing with her twin blades drawn and her back to the heart-chamber door.

"Stand aside, Shield-Veyra," said the stake-master, whose name she had never bothered learning. "The magister commands—"

"The magister," Veyra said, "is about to learn what a shield-sister is for."

She killed the first one before his rod cleared its loop. The second lasted longer—he was good, Conclave-trained, and for six seconds the landing rang like a smithy—but she had fought beside better and against worse, and she had spent five years sharpening herself against the day she would finally stop aiming at targets and start aiming at *reasons*. When the last of them fell, breathing wet and shallow against the wall, she stood over him in the glow of the heart-light spilling through the doorway, and she listened to the canticle overhead falter, stumble, resume.

They knew, now. Somewhere above, Corvane was recalculating, and forty sets of ash-grey boots were reorganizing around a new variable.

Veyra set her back against the doorframe, blades loose, and settled in to sell every second as dearly as the market would bear.

*Come on, Tam,* she thought, to a person already climbing toward the grey world and whatever choice waited there. *Run. Run and don't look back, and when you get to the top, do the one thing none of us ever had the courage to do.*

*Make it end.*

* * *

The second wave came with fire-pots and discipline, and Veyra learned the true price of seconds.

Each one cost her something. A parry that opened her forearm to the bone. A foot of ground she could never reclaim, given up grudgingly, bought back with blood that wasn't hers. She fought the way she had fought at Carrowmere, the way she had fought through the whole long dying of the old kingdoms—with a clarity that felt almost holy, every movement stripped down to its irreducible truth—and somewhere in the middle of it she started laughing, quietly, because it was funny, it was *finally* funny, that it had taken treason to make her feel faithful again.

Through the doorway at her back, the heart pulsed. She could feel it against her spine like a second heartbeat, vast and caged and watching, and once—only once—it spoke to her, in that language older than words.

*Little blade. You could still have it. All of it. Say the word.*

"Not from you," Veyra gasped, cutting down a chanter mid-canticle. "I'm done... taking gifts... from gods..."

The sixth wave broke against her and washed past her—one man through, sprinting for the stairs—and she let him go, because the stairs were the point, the delay was the point, and because her left arm had stopped answering questions some minutes ago.

That was when the horns sounded again. Close. Wrong key.

Not the Conclave's braided triple-call. Something harsher, flatter, official. The Inquisition's signal, played by men who believed in neither gods nor bargains, descending the switchback trails with lanterns and warrants and thirty years of accumulated certainty.

Through the stairwell's mouth, torchlight flickered against stone—and this time it was not Corvane's people coming down. Grey cloaks. Iron badges. And at their head, tall and grim, her face carved from the same stone as the Vault itself, Inquisitor Hale.

Her eyes swept the landing: the bodies, the blood, the exhausted traitor leaning on her sword in a doorway full of divine light. Everything Hale needed to make a dozen easy arrests.

Veyra lifted her chin, and grinned through the blood in her mouth.

"Well," she said. "Inquisitor. You're late. The god's in the next room, and everyone up there wants a piece of it—and I don't mean a piece of its *grace*." She pushed off the doorframe, straightened as much as her ruined arm allowed, and leveled her blade at the darkness above, toward the sound of Corvane's chanters gathering for a seventh wave. "So. Whose law are you here to serve tonight—the one on your badge, or the one your masters answer to?"

Hale looked at her for a long, long moment. Down in the chamber behind them both, the heart pulsed like a held breath.

Then the Inquisitor drew her sword, turned to face the stairs, and said, "Form up. Nothing passes this line."
