## 18. The Heart of Caerlum

The Vault swallowed sound.

Tamsin felt it in their teeth as they descended the last stair—a silence so complete it pressed against the eardrums like deep water. Behind them, far above, the gates they had opened with their own blood still rang with the echoes of alarm, but down here none of that reached. Down here there was only the dark, and the cold, and the thing at the bottom of the world that had been waiting five years for someone to come.

Their boots found stone slick with condensation. The murals were behind them now—the truth of them still burning behind Tamsin's eyes like a brand. *We tore it out ourselves.* The followers. The faithful. Their own hands. Not abandonment. Mercy. Or what the old order had convinced itself was mercy, before mercy curdled into theft and theft into silence.

Veyra's footsteps sounded somewhere above, distant and deliberate. She had said she would hold the stair. She had said many things. Tamsin had stopped trying to sort which of them to believe and simply walked on, because the alternative was standing still while the Conclave's horns sounded across the valley, and standing still had never once saved anyone.

The passage opened.

Tamsin knew it was open even with eyes closed, because the darkness changed quality—thinned, as though something vast had exhaled into the space. They opened their eyes anyway.

The chamber was a hollowed mountain, ribbed with pillars carved in the likeness of upraised hands, hundreds of them, a forest of petrified supplication. And at the center, suspended in a cage of interlocking sigils that turned slowly, patiently, like the mechanism of some celestial clock—

The heart.

It did not beat. That was the first horror of it. A heart the size of an ox should have thundered; this one hung motionless in its cage of light, a mass of veined gold and shadow, beautiful the way a frozen waterfall is beautiful, the way a held breath is beautiful. It radiated no warmth. And yet every hair on Tamsin's body stood toward it, the way iron filings stand toward a lodestone, the way a starving man's body turns toward bread.

"Caerlum," Tamsin said.

Their voice came out small. The chamber ate it.

And then the god answered—not with sound, not with words at first, but with *remembering*. It poured into Tamsin like water into cracked earth: the smell of the training yard at dawn, frost on the practice blades; Veyra laughing with her helmet under her arm, her braid singed from a spell gone wrong; the taste of oath-wine, bitter honey on the tongue; the weight of a god's attention resting on your shoulders like a hand, warm and enormous, saying *you are mine and I am yours*.

Five years of silence, and it all came back at once, and Tamsin staggered against one of the pillar-hands and wept without deciding to.

*Little blade.*

The voice was inside them. Not in the ears—in the marrow, in the spaces between thoughts, familiar as their own heartbeat and ten thousand times older.

"You left," Tamsin whispered. "Five years. I prayed until my knees broke. I sold *charms*, Caerlum. I sold trinkets to people who needed miracles, and you let me."

*I did not leave. I was taken. You know this now—you have seen the walls. Your own kindred caged me so that kings could sleep.*

"I know." Tamsin wiped their face with the back of a hand. "I know what they did. But you're asking me—" They stopped. Realized the god hadn't asked anything yet. Realized, with a chill that had nothing to do with the Vault's cold, that the remembering itself had been the opening move.

*What am I asking?* Amusement moved through Tamsin's bones like a current through wire. *Nothing yet, little blade. Only look. Only remember what we were together.*

The vision came whether Tamsin willed it or not.

They stood—some part of them stood, some part of them hung in a cage of golden light—on the walls of Carrowmere at dusk, and the enemy host below was a sea of torches, and Tamsin raised their sworn-blade and spoke the word of unmaking, and the god came through them like a sun coming through a keyhole. The river boiled. The siege towers folded like paper. Forty thousand men threw down their arms and fled, and the war ended that night, and Tamsin woke three days later in a temple infirmary with white streaks in their hair that never faded, and everyone knelt when they walked past.

*I gave you that,* the god murmured. *I will give it again. More than that. I will give you everything you buried.*

"No." But the word had no spine. Tamsin heard it fail.

The vision shifted. Greyhallow now—but not the Greyhallow of fear and informers and Inquisitors' lanterns. A Greyhallow where the shrines stood open and candlelit, where the Godless Quarter had been renamed by acclamation, where the market crowd parted for Tamsin Thornbury, Sworn Blade, First of the Restored Order, and children ran alongside asking for blessings. Hale's masters knelt—or burned; the vision offered both options with equal serenity. The Conclave's ash-robed hierophants knelt too, their stolen ambitions ground to powder beneath a heel the size of a cathedral. Veyra stood at Tamsin's right hand, whole and laughing, the betrayal at Ashford Bridge erased as if it had never happened, as if forgiveness could be issued retroactively by divine decree.

And the dead were there. Doss, grinning his gap-toothed grin, alive behind his stall. The courier who had died on Tamsin's doorstep, breathing, upright, his blood unwritten from the world.

"That's not how it works," Tamsin said hoarsely. "You can't give back the dead."

*I am a god,* Caerlum said, gently, the way one corrects a child. *I can do whatever I am permitted to do. And I am permitted everything, if only you open the cage.*

The heart pulsed.

Once. Slowly. Like an eye opening after long sleep. The sigil-cage groaned, and one rune along its rim guttered and dimmed, and Tamsin felt the change in their own chest—a pressure, a leaning, the whole chamber tilting imperceptibly toward them, or perhaps toward the door, toward the stair, toward any way out.

Above, faintly, steel met steel. Shouting. Veyra's voice, sharp and furious, and other voices answering, and the particular wet percussion of combat on stone. The Conclave had come down into the Vault. Tamsin should run to help her. Tamsin could not make their feet obey.

*They will use me,* the god said. *You know this. The Conclave wants a god on a leash—a thing to crown and command. Your Inquisitors want me melted down and sold. Even your friend above, even Veyra—she wants me for the same reasons she wanted everything, little blade. Because having is safer than being.*

"And you?" Tamsin's voice shook. "What do you want?"

The silence that followed had texture. Weight. When the answer came, it was soft as snowfall, and it was the most honest thing the god had said:

*Out.*

Tamsin laughed—a broken, ugly sound. "That's it? Five years, and all the prayers, and the war you'd start again—and all you want is *out*?"

*Is that so small a thing?* Something moved behind the voice, vast and wounded. *You know what the cage is like. You built your own, these five years. A shop. Charms that don't work. A name that isn't yours. You know exactly what it is to be shut away from yourself and call it living.*

Tamsin's breath caught. Because it was true, and the god knew it was true, and cruelty dressed in truth was still the sharpest blade there was.

"If I open it," Tamsin said slowly, "what happens to the world? The murals said—"

*The murals were written by cowards.* Fury now, tectonic, banked but present, and the temperature of the chamber seemed to drop around it. *Your precious old order started that war and could not finish it, and rather than pay the price themselves they paid mine. They took my heart out through my ribs and called it peace. And the peace you inherited—the peace you've been starving in—is built on my body. Every quiet year, every law, every little charmless life in every grey little city: purchased with me.*

"They were afraid of you."

*Of course they were afraid of me. I am the end of arguments.* The heart pulsed again, and another rune guttered, and this time Tamsin saw the light of it reach the far wall and crawl there like a living thing. *But hear me, Tamsin Thornbury, and hear me well: I am not offering you vengeance. Vengeance is a human hunger and beneath us both. I am offering you wholeness. Yours and mine. You were cut in half the day they caged me—you simply didn't bleed enough to notice.*

And gods help them, Tamsin *felt* it. Felt the half-shaped hole where the oaths used to live, the place where meaning had been scooped out and filled with candle-smoke and cheap pewter charms. Five years of waking and reaching for something that wasn't there. Five years of praying into a well and hearing nothing come back. If the god offered a hand across that distance—if wholeness was truly on the table, if the ache could simply *end*—

Their feet were moving. They realized it distantly, the way one realizes rain has started. Three steps toward the cage. Four.

*THERE,* the god breathed, and the tenderness in it was the worst thing yet. *There is my blade. Come. One drop of sworn blood on the lowest sigil—that is all. It was always meant to be you. The map chose you. The blood-sigils knew your hand. Do you imagine that was chance?*

Tamsin stopped.

Cold walked up their spine, slower than fear, colder. "...What did you say?"

*The courier. You think a dying man found you by accident?* A ripple of amusement, warm as bathwater, terrible as drowning. *I have been whispering through the cracks of my cage for five years. Through dreams. Through shrines. Through dying men. I have spent five years threading every needle, and every thread led here, to you, because you are the last sworn blade and my blood remembers yours. I chose you before you knew there was a choice.*

"So I'm not—" Tamsin's mouth had gone dry. "This isn't my choice at all. You've been *herding* me."

*I have been calling you home.*

"It's the same thing!" The words tore out of them, echoing off the pillar-hands, off the upraised carved fingers of a hundred dead faithful. "That's what they must have felt—the old order. That's why they did it. Not mercy. Not cowardice. *Fear.* Because you don't ask, Caerlum. You never asked. You reached into us and rearranged the furniture and called it love, and when we finally noticed we couldn't get you *out* fast enough—"

The chamber went very still.

Even the heart seemed to pause mid-pulse. And in that pause, Tamsin heard, from far above, the sounds of battle intensify—a scream that might have been Veyra, the crash of something heavy falling, and beneath it a new voice, clear and commanding and unmistakably Hale's, ringing down the stairwell like a bell thrown down a well:

"HOLD! By authority of the Inquisition of Greyhallow, this site is seized! Conclave agents—drop your blades or be written down as heretics of the second rank!"

Steel again. Chaos. Boots on stairs. None of it mattered. None of it touched the small frozen space where Tamsin stood with their hand drifting toward the hilt of the sworn-blade they had carried five years without knowing why they couldn't sell it.

*Little blade,* the god said, and now the tenderness had thinned, and beneath it something else showed through, something with teeth. *Consider carefully. The Conclave comes to bind me. The Inquisition comes to bury me. If either reaches me first, you lose everything—your purpose, your quest, your reason for walking out of that grey city. I am the only door back to the self you mourn. Open me, and be whole. Refuse me—*

The pause stretched.

*—and remain a charm seller.*

The words landed like a knife between ribs. Tamsin saw it all in a single flaying instant: the shop, the stall, the decades of harmless pewter and polite lies, growing old over a counter while the world forgot their name, and no god, and no war, and no meaning, only the long grey afternoon of a life that had peaked in someone else's story.

The sworn-blade was in their hand. Tamsin didn't remember drawing it.

One step. Two. The lowest sigil glowed faintly at their boots, waiting for blood, patient as its maker. The heart turned slowly in its cage, and for the first time Tamsin could see that its surface was not smooth—it was *moving*, veins crawling like roots seeking soil, shadows swimming beneath gold like fish under ice, and within it, dim and vast and hungry, an eye the size of a lake beginning to focus on them with five years of accumulated longing.

"Tamsin!" Veyra's voice, ragged, from the top of the stair. "Whatever it's saying to you—it's *lying!* It told me the same things, do you hear me? Back at Ashford, before the bridge—it promised me *everything*—"

The rest was drowned under a clash of arms.

The heart's eye fixed on Tamsin fully now, and the voice came one final time, and it was no longer tender at all. It was the voice from the vision of Carrowmere, the voice that had spoken the word of unmaking through a mortal throat, and it said:

*Choose.*

Tamsin looked down at the blade in their hand—at the old oath-sigil etched in the fuller, worn nearly smooth by a thumb that had worried it for twenty years—and understood, with a calm that felt like falling, that whatever they did next, they would do it forever.

Above, the doors of the inner vault burst inward, and Hale's voice cried out a warning, and Veyra screamed a name that might have been Tamsin's, and the Conclave's hierophants began the first syllables of the binding rite.

And the heart of Caerlum waited, beating at last, slow and enormous, counting down.
