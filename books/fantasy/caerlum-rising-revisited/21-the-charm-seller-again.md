## 21. The Charm Seller, Again

The shop smelled of dust and old cedar when Tamsin unlocked the door for the first time in—how long? Weeks. Perhaps a season. The memory would not come. It sat just beyond the reach of thought like a coin dropped down a well: they could hear it strike water somewhere far below, but they could not say how deep.

Tamsin stood in the doorway and let the morning in.

Greyhallow's Godless Quarter woke slowly, as it always did, the way old animals wake—stiffly, suspiciously, one eye open for hawks. Smoke from breakfast fires braided through the narrow lane outside. A cart rattled past, its driver wrapped against the damp. Somewhere a child was being scolded for stepping in a puddle, and somewhere else a bell tolled the hour from the Inquisitors' tower, that flat grey note that marked time here the way a scar marks skin.

Tamsin's hands were steady on the doorframe. That surprised them. They had woken three days ago in a hired room near the marsh road with mud to the knee and no money and no memory of how they had come there, and they had expected—what? Terror? Grief? Instead there was only this strange, swept-clean quiet, as if someone had taken a broom to the inside of their skull and left nothing but smooth floor.

They had walked back to Greyhallow along roads they seemed to know without knowing why. Their feet had chosen the turns. Their body had known which ditches to skirt, which hedgerows hid dogs, where the watch changed shifts. It was like wearing a coat tailored for someone very like them but not quite them—the seams pulled wrong in places, the pockets full of things their fingers found by habit.

*You've been away*, their body said. *You've been a long way away.*

But away from what? From where?

Tamsin stepped inside and began, without thinking about it, to set the shop to rights.

* * *

The charms hung from their pegs along the back wall, rows of small woven things: knotted cords and river-stones wrapped in wire, sprigs of dried rowan bound with red thread, little tin discs stamped with spirals. Harmless things. Pretty things. Things the Quarter's frightened folk bought because a charm that did nothing could still do this much—it let you hold something in your hand and believe, for a moment, that the world was bigger than the Inquisition's shadow.

Tamsin took each one down, blew off the dust, and hung it again. And here was the strangeness, the strangeness they had stopped fighting on the second day: their hands knew things.

When they picked up a knot-cord, their fingers moved through its loops in an order they had never been taught—or rather, an order they could not remember being taught—and the cord came out of their hands tighter, cleaner, more *true* than it had gone in. When they turned over the tin discs, they knew which spiral was the waking-spiral and which was the sleeping one, though if you asked them how they knew, they would have to look at you blankly. When they reached for the red thread, they caught themselves reaching instead for a spool that wasn't there—a heavier thread, a grey one—and stood for a moment with their hand outstretched over empty air, heart beating strangely, before withdrawing it.

There was a word that kept almost arriving, too. It hovered at the edge of every quiet moment like a bird at a window. *Caer—* something. It began with a sound like a door opening. But whenever Tamsin turned toward it, it lifted and was gone, leaving behind only a faint ache, the way a dream leaves an ache in the chest after the dream itself has dissolved.

Once—they had been sorting stones by the window, the grey light coming up silver off the river beyond the roofs—once, they had looked down and found they had arranged all the river-stones in a circle. Not scattered, not stacked: laid in a ring, each stone touching the next, and in the center, nothing. An absence shaped exactly like something that should have been there.

Tamsin had stared at it for a long time. Then they had swept the stones into a jar and put the jar on the highest shelf, facing the wall.

Some things, they decided, a person could be better off not understanding. Some absences were load-bearing. Knock them out and who knew what would come down.

* * *

By midmorning the shop was ready, and Tamsin flipped the sign in the window—the little wooden card that said CHARMS, WARDINGS, SMALL KINDNESSES—and took their place behind the counter, and waited for the Quarter to remember they existed.

It remembered quickly. That was the nature of Greyhallow; grief and gossip both traveled fast there, and a shop shuttered for weeks was nearly as interesting as a death.

The first customer was Old Marta from the tannery row, who came shuffling in with her shawl pinned at her throat and her eyes already watering from the dust. "Thought you'd died," she announced, by way of greeting. "Or been taken. Both, maybe. One then the other."

"Not yet," Tamsin said. "I went away. I'm back now."

"Where'd you go?"

And Tamsin opened their mouth and found nothing there—not a lie, even, just emptiness, clean as new snow. "I don't know," they said, and heard how strange it sounded, and watched Marta's face fold up with a kind of fierce pity.

"Ay," she said. "Happens, in this city. People go away and come back less." She patted the counter with a spotted hand. "Give us a knot-cord. Blue one. My knees are speaking to me again, and my granddaughter swears your knots settle them."

Tamsin took down a blue cord and, without deciding to, worked the loose loops true between finger and thumb, the way their hands insisted, and felt something pass through the wool under their fingers—something faint, something like warmth, something like attention. For half a heartbeat the shop smelled of rain on high mountains, of cold clean stone, of—

Nothing. The smell was gone before it finished arriving. Tamsin handed over the cord and Marta pressed a copper into their palm and shuffled out, and Tamsin stood looking at their own hands as if they belonged to a stranger who had briefly borrowed them.

* * *

The day wore on. The Quarter came and went.

A bricklayer bought a warding-charm for his young wife, who was pregnant and afraid of the fever that had gone through the weavers' street in the spring. Two children pressed their faces to the glass until Tamsin waved them off, laughing. A woman in a grey cloak asked, very quietly, whether Tamsin had any charms against dreams, and paid too much for one, and left without meeting their eyes, and Tamsin watched her go and felt a sorrow rise in them that had no name attached to it—grief with the name torn off, like a letter with the address burned away.

That was the shape of the whole day, really. Shapes without names. Sorrow without a reason. Skill without a teacher. Once, when a drunk stumbled past the window singing, Tamsin's hand went to their hip—to their hip, where no sword had hung in living memory, where no sword hung now—and closed on empty cloth, and stayed there a moment, gripping nothing, before falling away.

In the afternoon a man came in whom Tamsin didn't know, tall and plain-dressed, with a watchman's walk and a soldier's stillness underneath it, and he browsed the pegs for a long time without buying anything, and finally said, "You've been away."

"I've been told."

"The Quarter talks." He turned a tin disc over in his fingers. "People wondered. There was trouble on the roads east, they say. Trouble at the marsh shrines, some say." His eyes were mild and missed nothing. "You hear anything, selling out here?"

"No," Tamsin said, and it was true, and the truth of it sat in them like a stone. "I don't hear anything anymore."

The man nodded slowly, set the disc down, and bought a warding-charm he clearly didn't need, paying with exact change, and left. Through the window Tamsin watched him pause across the lane, look back at the shop once, and move on—and understood, without knowing how they understood, that they had just been measured and found harmless. Or found *worth watching*. With men like that, the two were often the same thing.

When he was gone Tamsin noticed that the hair on their arms had been standing since he entered, and would not lie down again for some time.

* * *

Near dusk, when the light in the lane had gone the color of weak tea, a girl of perhaps twelve slipped inside, quick as a mouse avoiding a cat, and stood breathing hard in the middle of the floor.

"You're open," she said, accusingly, as though Tamsin had committed some crime by it.

"I'm open."

"Mam says the charm shops are all trickery and the Inquisition should burn them all." She said it in the flat reciting voice of a child repeating doctrine she did not believe. Then, in her own voice, much smaller: "She's sick, though. Since the winter. Coughing." Her chin came up, defiant, daring Tamsin to quote her mother back at her. "Have you got something?"

Tamsin looked at the child for a long moment. Outside, the tower bell tolled the closing hour. Inside, dust motes turned slowly in the last of the light, and the jar of river-stones sat on its high shelf facing the wall, and somewhere below the floor of Tamsin's mind, in the deep well where memories used to be, something stirred that might have been a voice or might have been only water moving.

What do you say to a child, when you don't know yourself whether anything you sell is anything but string and tin?

Tamsin went to the wall and chose a cord—plain brown wool, nothing special—and worked it true the way their hands demanded, loop over loop, and as they worked they spoke, quietly, not quite to the girl.

"There was—" They stopped. Started again. The words came from somewhere they couldn't see, rising up the well like bubbles. "There was a place I think I went once. High up. Cold. You could see the whole world from it, all the little lights of it going about their business, and it was so small from up there, all of it, every fear, every war—small enough to cup in two hands. Like a spark. Like something you could carry." Their fingers tightened on the cord. "I don't remember it. I want to be clear about that. I don't remember anything. But I know—I know *that* the same way I know how to tie these knots. Somebody taught me. Something taught me. And what it taught me was this:"

They held the finished cord out to the girl.

"It doesn't matter if it works. That's the secret nobody wants told. What matters is that when your mam holds it, she remembers somebody loves her enough to have walked into a charm shop for her. That's not trickery. That's the oldest magic there is, and no law ever outlawed it, because no law can. It keeps what matters. Everything else falls away anyway."

The girl took the cord with both hands, the way you take something holy, and stared at it, and then dug in her pocket and produced a button, a bent nail, and half a biscuit, and pushed the biscuit across the counter with great ceremony, and fled.

Tamsin ate the biscuit standing in the empty shop while the dark came down outside, and lit the lamp, and laughed at nothing, and wiped their eyes with the heel of their hand, and did not ask themselves why they were crying, because they suspected they would not get an answer, and suspected also that the answer was a name, and that the name was a door they had locked from the other side for reasons that had probably been very good ones.

* * *

Closing took less time than opening. Sweep the floor. Bank the lamp low. Turn the sign. Count the coppers—enough, barely, for bread and oil and the rent that would come due soon with its patient, inevitable face.

Last of all, as always now, Tamsin checked the jar of river-stones on its high shelf. They didn't know why they did this. It had become ritual without their consent: end of day, eyes up, confirm the jar sits facing the wall, confirm the circle stays broken. On the fourth night they had tried, experimentally, to leave the stones in their ring overnight, and had lain awake until dawn with their heart hammering and tears running sideways into their pillow, weeping over—what? Nothing. No one. A jar of rocks. After that, the jar faced the wall, and that was that.

Tonight, though, when they reached up to check it, their fingers found the jar already turned.

Facing outward.

Tamsin went very still.

The shop was silent. The lamp hissed softly. Out in the lane, footsteps passed and faded—just a neighbor, surely, just anyone—and the bell tower counted the ninth hour into the dark.

Slowly, Tamsin brought the jar down and held it in both hands. Through the glass, in the lamplight, they could see the stones inside: no longer a heap, no longer scattered. Arranged. A perfect ring around the center of the jar, each stone touching the next, and in the center of the ring, where there had been nothing for weeks, lay a single small pebble they had never seen before—pale, sea-smooth, faintly warm.

Warm. Warm as a hand. Warm as a cheek. Warm as—

The ache rose in them like floodwater, vast and sourceless, grief and joy fused into one unbearable alloy, and Tamsin sank onto the stool behind the counter with the jar cradled against their chest, rocking slightly, crying without sound, without knowledge, without a single name to give any of it.

Outside, far off toward the marshes, so faint it might have been wind in the reeds or might have been a wish, something sang—one clear note, rising, like a door beginning to open.

Tamsin lifted their head.

Listened.

And did not lock the shop door that night.
