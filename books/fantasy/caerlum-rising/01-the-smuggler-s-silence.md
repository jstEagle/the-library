## 1. The Smuggler's Silence

The mule stopped three switchbacks below the high pass, and Dominic Okafor learned everything they needed to know about the day from the set of her ears.

Bramble stood with her left ear swiveled back toward the treeline and her right cocked forward, which was the mule's particular grammar for *something moved in the pines, but it was small, and it has gone*. A lesser traveler would have missed it entirely. A careless one would have kept walking and met the small thing's larger cousins around the next bend. Dominic simply stopped, shifted the pack straps on their shoulders, and waited, one hand resting on the warm grey slope of Bramble's neck.

"I know," they murmured, too quiet for anything but the mule to hear. "I see it too."

The pines of Thornfell grew thick and black on the northern face of the ridge, and in the blue hour before full light they held shadows the way a cupped hand holds water. Dominic had been crossing these mountains for eleven years, and in eleven years they had never once been surprised. Not because they were the fastest or the strongest or the best armed—though they could be quick when it mattered—but because the mountain itself had never once been silent to them. Every runt fox, every nesting corvid, every half-wild goat with an opinion about strangers had told them where the dangers slept.

That was the gift. That was the crime.

Dominic had learned early that the word for it was not a word you used. Beast-speaking. The crown had made it a hanging offense the year Dominic turned nine, the same year the first binding-yards went up outside the capital, the same year the king's criers had come through the lowland villages reading a proclamation in a voice like a struck bell: *All commerce with the minds of beasts is hereby declared treason against the realm.* Dominic's mother had pulled them close that night and said, very softly, "You never heard that word before today. Do you understand me? You have never heard it." And Dominic, who had been talking to the neighbor's dog since they could form sentences, had nodded and buried the word so deep it only surfaced in the dark, in the hollow hours, where it ached like an old break.

They had never been caught. That was the whole architecture of their life: a life built like a smuggler's road, winding, concealed, never straight, never seen.

Bramble snorted and flicked one ear forward. *Clear now,* the gesture said. *The small thing went home.*

"Good girl." Dominic clicked their tongue and the mule resumed the climb, hooves finding the shelf of stone with the unerring certainty of an animal who had walked this road more times than her rider could count. She carried salt-dried fish and iron nails and a bolt of southern dye the color of wine, all of it bound for the freehold at Caerlum's foot, and none of it worth the rope it was tied with if a crown patrol found it. Smuggled goods were a fine. Smugglers themselves were a fine and a flogging. And a smuggler caught whispering to the wildlife—

Dominic didn't finish the thought. They never let themselves finish that thought on the mountain. The mountain deserved better than fear.

* * *

The pass at the top was called the Shepherd's Tooth by the lowlanders and nothing at all by Dominic, who thought of it simply as the place where the wind lived. It was a saddle of bare grey stone between two shoulders of the range, and when you came up onto it the whole southern slope of Thornfell opened below you like a held breath released—miles of pine and scree and the thin silver stitching of streams, and far off, hazy with distance, the first grey suggestion of the valleys where people lived and did not ask questions.

Dominic stood there a moment, as they always did, and let the wind shove at them. It was a hard wind this morning, colder than the season promised, and it carried a smell that made the back of their neck prickle. Not snow. Not smoke. Something older and stranger, like the air inside a cellar that had been shut for a hundred years.

Bramble felt it too. Her skin shivered in a ripple from shoulder to haunch, though the wind had not touched her yet.

"Easy," Dominic said, and meant it as much for themselves as for her.

They had a rule about the mountain: never linger at the top. High places were where you could be seen from, and being seen from was the same as being seen. So Dominic shouldered the lead rope and started down the southern face, and the wind followed them, worrying at their collar, carrying that cellar-smell, and by the third switchback down Dominic had catalogued three separate wrongnesses and was trying very hard to treat them as coincidence.

The first: the jays had gone quiet. Thornfell jays were loud, rude, relentless creatures, and a morning without their arguments was a morning with a hole in it. Dominic reached out—carefully, the way they always did, a feather-touch of attention rather than a voice, because a voice left traces in a way a touch did not—and found the jays still in their trees, still alert, but silent. Watching the northern sky.

The second: the stream they followed was running low. It was the wrong month for it, and the wrong weather, and when Dominic crouched at its edge and cupped a handful they found the water cold in a way that had nothing to do with altitude. Cold like something had drunk the warmth out of it.

The third wrongness they didn't find at all. It found them.

A fox came out of the bracken at the bend below, a vixen with a winter coat still patchy from the molt, and she did not flee. That alone was strange. What she did instead was worse: she sat down on the trail in front of Bramble, deliberate as a toll-keeper, and looked up at Dominic with eyes like two chips of amber, and she *pushed*. Dominic felt it arrive in their mind like a stone dropped into a still pool—no words, foxes didn't have words, only shapes and hungers and a fierce bright attention—and the shape she gave them was this: *the sky is wrong, the sky is wrong, the sky is wrong,* and beneath it, fainter, a feeling Dominic had never received from a wild thing in eleven years of listening.

Fear of something above.

Dominic went very still. "Above what?" they asked, and their voice came out rough, and they hated how loud it sounded, how human. "Above the ridge? Above the—"

The vixen looked up. Not at the peaks. At the sky itself, at the pale washed-out blue of it, and then she turned and flowed back into the bracken and was gone, and Bramble stood trembling under Dominic's hand, and the wind came down off the pass carrying that deep cellar-cold, and Dominic Okafor, who had never in their life been surprised by the mountain, stood on a road they knew better than their own heartbeat and felt, for the first time, lost.

* * *

They made the freehold by late afternoon, because routine was a kind of armor and Dominic wore theirs even when the mountain was acting like something out of an old wife's tale. The freehold of Dunmarrow's Rest sat in the crook of a valley where two streams met, a scatter of stone houses and drying-racks and one tavern with a sign depicting a boar that had died of optimism. Dominic had been coming here for years. They knew which fence posts leaned, which dogs would bark at strangers (all of them, but only two would mean it), and which of the tavern's corners had sightlines to both the door and the back window.

The corner by the hearth, as it happened. Dominic took it, as they always did, with their back to the wall and Bramble stabled and paid for and, Dominic suspected, currently lecturing the stable cat on the subject of personal boundaries.

"Okafor." The tavern keeper, a broad woman named Hesta with forearms like ship rope, set down a bowl of stew without being asked and a cup of cider without being charged. "You look like a man who's seen a ghost."

"I look like a man who walked over a mountain," Dominic said. "That's the same face every time."

"Uh-huh." Hesta wiped her hands on her apron and didn't leave. That was the trouble with Hesta; she had spent twenty years reading smugglers the way priests read scripture, and she was very good at it. "Roads quiet?"

"Quiet enough."

"Nothing strange?"

Dominic lifted the cider and drank, and considered, over the rim of the cup, exactly how much of the truth they could afford. This was the arithmetic they performed a hundred times a day, in a hundred small ways: how much to say, how much to soften, how much to bury. It was the tax on their kind of life. Every conversation was a customs house.

"Fox acted strange," they said at last. "Jays gone quiet. Stream running low." They kept their voice bored. "Probably a hard winter coming."

"Probably," Hesta said, in a tone that said she'd noticed the cider cup trembling slightly and had decided, for reasons of her own, to let it go. "There's a man asking after you, by the way."

The cold that went through Dominic then had nothing to do with mountain streams.

"Asking after me," they repeated.

"Lowlander. Says he's a wool factor out of Merrowgate. Says he heard a smuggler works Thornfell who never gets lost and never gets caught, and he's got cargo needs moving quiet." Hesta shrugged. "Could be honest. Could be a trap. Could be both. He's in the corner booth looking like a man who's never carried wool in his life."

Dominic turned their head, slowly, casually, the way you turn your head when you're looking at the fire. The corner booth. A man in a good coat gone slightly shabby, sitting with an untouched cup in front of him and his hands folded on the table like a man at a funeral. He was watching the room the way Dominic watched rooms, which was to say: not watching it, and missing nothing.

"Did he give a name?"

"Vane," Hesta said. "Serah Vane."

The name landed somewhere in the back of Dominic's memory and rang, faintly, like a bell in a buried church. Vane. They knew that name. Every smuggler in Thornfell knew that name, the way sailors know the name of a storm: *Serah Vane, the ward-keeper who cried wolf, the one who swore the border wards were failing years before they did, the one they stripped of rank and exiled for saying out loud what the crown wanted never said.* Dominic had filed her away years ago as a cautionary tale about the price of speaking. Apparently she had filed Dominic away as something else entirely.

"I don't know her," Dominic said.

"Didn't say you did." Hesta gathered the empty bowl. "But she's been sitting there for two days, and she only ordered food once, and last night she paid the stable boy silver to make sure your mule got extra hay." She paused at the edge of the table. "Whatever you're carrying, Okafor, I'd say she's not after the cargo."

She left. Dominic sat with their cider and their heartbeat and the firelight, and did the arithmetic again, and came up with a number they didn't like. Because a stranger asking after a smuggler was trouble, but a stranger who had *bribed the stable boy to feed the mule* was something else. That was not the move of someone hunting a beast-speaker. That was the move of someone who knew exactly what a beast-speaker was worth, and what they needed, and had decided to be kind to the animal first.

The man in the booth—no, Dominic looked again, and the coat was a man's coat but the way the stranger sat was wrong for it, shoulders too square, hands too still—rose, crossed the room, and stood at Dominic's table. Close up, the disguise was worse and better at once: the jaw shadowed with something that wasn't quite beard-stubble, the throat a half-finger too smooth. A woman in a bad wig, doing a very committed impression of a wool factor.

"Master Okafor." The voice was pitched low and rough. "I'm told you know the Thornfell roads better than any living soul."

"I know some roads," Dominic said. "Most of them I don't. Depends what cargo you're moving."

"Stars," the stranger said.

The word dropped into the tavern noise and vanished, but for Dominic it rang like the vixen's fear, like the cold stream, like the silent jays. The stranger leaned in, and up close her eyes were the color of wet slate and rimmed red with sleeplessness, and she spoke fast and quiet, a woman spending words she could not take back.

"Three nights ago the ward-stones at the border went dark. All of them, at once. And last night—" her voice caught, steadied, "—last night the stars went out. Every one. Not clouds. *Out.* The garrison's calling it weather. It isn't weather. I spent six years telling them the wards were dying and they exiled me for it, and now the sky is empty, and you are the only person in these mountains who might believe me, because I have watched you for two days, Master Okafor, and I have seen the way the wild things come to you."

The tavern roared on around them—dice, laughter, Hesta's bellows—and Dominic sat in the middle of it, perfectly still, feeling eleven years of careful, winding, concealed life come to the edge of a cliff.

"I think," Dominic said at last, very quietly, "you have me confused with someone else."

"I think," said the stranger who was not a wool factor, whose name was Serah Vane and whose eyes held the particular terror of a person who has been right too early and is running out of time, "that your mule told the stable cat to leave her alone, and the cat *did*."

Outside, the light through the tavern windows began, very gently, to fail—and it was not yet evening, and there were no clouds, and somewhere above Thornfell the first stars were going out like snuffed candles, one by one, in silence.
