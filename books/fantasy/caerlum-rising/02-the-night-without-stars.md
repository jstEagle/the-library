## 2. The Night Without Stars

Serah Vane had spent three years learning to read the sky the way other people read scripture, and so she was the first to know when the words began to disappear.

She stood on the balcony of a rented room above the Gilded Anchor in Marrow's Rest, the last town before the Thornfell passes, with a cup of cold tea going bitter in her hand. Below her, the harbor lamps burned their ordinary gold, and the fishing boats knocked against the pilings in their ordinary rhythm, and nothing—nothing at all—suggested to anyone else that the world was ending.

But Serah had kept ward-logs for eleven years. She had memorized the constellations not as sailors did, for navigation, but as a warden did: each star a node, each constellation a lattice, the whole vault of heaven one vast and patient engine that held the border wards charged from dusk to dawn. The Caerlum sky wasn't decoration. It was the kingdom's oldest fortification, laid down by the first wardens when the binding-stones were young, and every star had a name and a duty.

The first to go out was Veshel, the Shepherd's Eye, low on the southern horizon. It didn't flicker. It didn't dim. One moment it burned; the next it was simply absent, like a word struck from a page by a careful hand.

Serah set down her tea very slowly, the way you move when you don't want to wake something.

"Come back," she whispered. "Come back, come back, come back."

Veshel had guarded the southern approaches for nine hundred years. Wardens sang to it at solstice. Children were named for it. And it was gone, and where it had been there was only a patch of black so complete it looked less like absence than like hunger—a hole in the fabric of the night with edges.

Then the Hollow Sisters went. Then the Wainwright's Wheel, star by star, as if someone were picking jewels off a crown. Then whole constellations at once, north and east and west, the sky emptying itself in silence, no sound from the heavens at all, which was somehow worse than thunder would have been. Thunder would have meant a battle. This meant a meal.

By midnight, Marrow's Rest lay under a sky with nothing in it. Not clouds. Not haze. Nothing. A black ceiling, seamless and total, pressing down on the town like a lid.

Below, the dogs began to howl.

* * *

They howled in relays, from the harbor yards up through the terraced streets, one pack taking up the cry as another fell exhausted silent, and Serah listened from her balcony with her arms wrapped around herself and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the animals knew before any human in the town did. They always knew. That was the first lesson of ward-craft: the land keeps its own watch, and the beasts are its sentries, and this was precisely why the crown had spent forty years chaining them into its war-engines—because a bound beast could be made to lie about what it sensed.

A free animal cannot lie. That was the second lesson.

Serah had learned both lessons at her father's knee in a ward-station so far east the maps called it the Edge of the World, and she had carried them into the Warden Corps at sixteen, and she had carried them, eleven years later, into the great hall at Caerlum itself, where she had stood before the assembled court with her charts shaking in her hands and told King Aldric—the king with the kind eyes and the tired mouth—that the border wards were failing. That the draw on them had tripled in two years. That something on the other side of the wards was *drinking*, drinking starlight, drinking ward-light, drinking whatever it could reach, and that if the crown continued diverting ward-power into the binding-yards to fuel its new war-engines, there would be nothing left to hold the borders when—

"When what?" the Master of Coin had asked, smiling the smile of a man humoring a hysterical girl. "Finish your sentence, Warden Vane. When *what*?"

And she had said, because she was twenty-seven and certain and stupid with certainty: "When it comes through."

They had laughed. Not all of them—she remembered that now with an ache, remembered the few faces that hadn't laughed, the old marshal who had studied her charts too long, the queen who had gone pale and said nothing—but enough of them had laughed, and King Aldric had looked at her with those kind eyes full of something that wasn't laughter but also wasn't belief, and he had said, gently, *Warden, the wards have held for nine centuries. They will hold for nine more.*

Six months later she had been stripped of her rank, her badge melted down in front of her, and marched out of Caerlum under guard for "sedition and false alarm," her family name struck from the rolls. Exile. The word they used was *disgrace*, but exile was the honest word, and Serah had learned to love honest words, because she had so few of them left.

Now the stars were dying overhead exactly as her charts had predicted, on the schedule she had drawn, along the gradient she had mapped—and vindication should have tasted like anything at all. Instead it tasted like ash and salt water, like the harbor air on a night when the world ends quietly while fishermen sleep.

She heard boots on the stairs and did not reach for the knife under her mattress. There was only one person who knocked on her door at this hour, and he always announced himself by walking loudly, because he believed loudness was honesty.

"Serah." Doss Hallow's voice through the wood, pitched low and urgent. "You need to come see the square."

"I've seen it," she said. "I've seen all of it."

"The sky. Serah, the *sky*—"

"I told you it would happen. I told you the month. I told you the week." Her voice came out flat and strange, and she hated it, because she wanted it to shake. Shaking would have been human. "Open the door, Doss."

He did. Doss Hallow was a former quartermaster built like a rain barrel, with a beard he trimmed to look respectable and hands that were never still, and behind him in the lamplit stairwell his face wore the expression of a man watching his house burn and realizing he'd left the ledger inside.

"You weren't wrong," he said simply.

"No."

"They'll come for you now. You understand that? When this gets to the capital—and it will get there by morning—you're either a prophet or a traitor, and the crown doesn't keep prophets."

"I know." She moved past him into the room and began, with hands that finally, finally shook, to pack. Charts. The stolen copy of the First Warden's Concordance. Her father's tuning fork, the only thing she'd taken from the eastern stations when they exiled her. "So we won't be here in the morning."

"Where will we be?"

"That depends," Serah said, "on whether the mountains are still standing."

* * *

The soldiers came through Marrow's Rest at dawn—or what should have been dawn, though no light came to mark it. The town woke to a gray half-dark that never brightened, lamps burning at noon-hour, shadows lying wrong in the streets because there was no sun to organize them, only a diffuse, sourceless pallor like light filtering through deep water. Serah stood in the crowd at the market square and watched the Third Legion march down the coast road toward the capital, and counted, and felt sick.

Two thousand men, maybe more, marching in good order under banners that showed the sunburst of House Aldric—and every banner hung dead still, because there was no wind. No weather at all. The air had the held-breath quality of a room before lightning, and it had held that quality since midnight, and the sea beyond the breakwater lay flat as poured pewter.

"Where are they going?" muttered the fishwife beside her. "There's no war."

"There's about to be one," said her neighbor, a cooper with sawdust in his hair, and spat. "Word from the passes is the border's broken. Whole garrisons pulling back. My cousin says things are coming through the Thornfell that—" He stopped, glanced at Serah, at her travel-worn clothes and her foreign-looking satchel, and shut his mouth with the instinct of small towns everywhere.

Things coming through. Yes. Serah had tried to warn them about that too, in the pamphlets she'd printed in exile, the ones that got her run out of two more towns. *The wards do not merely keep things out,* she had written. *They keep the shape of the world intact. What waits outside the borders is not an army. Armies can be fought.*

She watched the legion pass and looked at their faces, and that was when dread stopped being abstract and became a stone in her chest that she would carry for the rest of her life, however long that turned out to be.

The soldiers' faces were wrong.

Not wounded, not sick, not afraid—wrong in a subtler way, the way a portrait is wrong when the painter has captured everything except the soul. They marched in perfect step, perfect silence, two thousand men without a single cough or curse or bawdy joke, and their eyes were fixed on the middle distance with an attention that never wavered, never blinked in sequence, and when the officer at the column's head raised his fist, all two thousand stopped at once, as one body, without a single man stumbling.

Bound. Every one of them bound—not the beasts, the *men*, bound the way the war-engines were bound, their wills strung on lines of power that should have been impossible, that should have required more energy than existed in the world.

Unless, said the warden in her head, cold and precise beneath the fear, unless you had a source of power no one had ever tapped. Unless you had, say, an entire sky to eat.

"They're not marching to defend the capital," Serah said aloud, and didn't realize she'd spoken until people around her turned to stare. She lowered her voice. "They're marching to *hold* it. Something has taken the palace, and it wants the capital sealed before anyone understands what's happened."

Doss appeared at her elbow, sweating in the windless cold. "You can't know that."

"I know the difference between an army and a puppet show." She watched the column start forward again, those thousand unblinking faces, and made herself keep watching, made herself memorize it, because someone had to remember what happened today and witnesses were scarce. "Doss, I need horses, coin, and the name of a smuggler who knows Thornfell better than the crown does. In that order."

"A smuggler." He said it the way he said most things, flat, but his eyes flicked to the soldiers, then to the starless sky, then back. Three years of renting her the room above the Anchor had taught him that when Serah Vane stated a fact about wards or hunger or the end of the world, arguing cost more than compliance. "There's a name. Okafor. Runs the high passes nobody else will touch. Costs double and asks no questions, which around you is probably worth triple."

"Find them."

"And after Thornfell?" Doss asked. "After the passes—where?"

Serah looked south, toward Merrowgate and the free cities, where councils of merchants and scholars would be arguing right now about whether the darkness over Caerlum was even real, whether it was some trick of weather, whether it warranted interrupting trade. Nine hundred years of walls had made them soft. She had begged them once, in writing, and been ignored.

"After Thornfell," she said, "we find whoever is still free enough to listen. And then we take back a kingdom most people don't yet know has been stolen."

Above them, the seamless black pressed down. Somewhere in it, where Veshel the Shepherd's Eye had burned for nine hundred years, something was digesting, and Serah Vane—who had been laughed at, stripped, branded traitor, and erased—stood in the wrong-colored dawn and finally let herself feel the truth she'd been holding at arm's length since midnight:

Being right was the loneliest thing she had ever been, and it was about to get much, much worse.

She turned away from the road and went to find a smuggler.
