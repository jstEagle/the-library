## 3. What Bramble Refused

The mule woke Dominic an hour before dawn, and she was screaming.

Not braying—*screaming*, a sound Bramble had never made in the nine years Dominic had owned her, a sound like a saw going through living wood. It ripped down the narrow defile where they'd made camp and came back off the stone walls doubled, and somewhere above, a night bird went up out of the pines in a clatter of wings.

Dominic was on their feet before they were fully awake, knife in hand, scanning the dark for wolves or worse. The fire had burned down to a red eye of coals. Bramble stood at the end of her picket line with her whole body strung tight as bridge cable, ears flat, eyes rolled white, hauling against the rope hard enough that the iron stake in the ground hummed.

"Bramble." Dominic kept their voice low, the way they'd learned to keep everything about themselves low. "Hey. Hey, girl. What is it?"

She couldn't answer. That was the cruelty of the law, Dominic thought—not that beast-speaking was forbidden, but that it had never been a conversation anyway. It was more like pressing your ear to a door and hearing muffled voices on the other side. Dominic could feel the shape of Bramble's terror the way you could feel a storm coming in your teeth, but the words themselves stayed behind the door, and the door had a king's seal on it now, and men had been hanged for turning the handle.

Dominic turned the handle anyway. They always had. They'd simply never let anyone see.

*What is it, what is it, what is it* — they pushed the thought at her, soft as breath, the way you'd speak to something skittish.

What came back was not a thought. It was a feeling, huge and formless, and it knocked Dominic back a step: a sensation like the ground being pulled out from under the world. Like a rope going slack that had always been taut. Like something *eating*.

Bramble didn't have words for wards. She didn't know the word *ward* any more than she knew the word *king* or the word *law*. But she knew the border stones. Every beast on the Thornfell roads knew them—the way a dog knows the fence line of its yard, the way a horse knows where the safe pasture ends. The wards that ran along the crown's border were a hum in the world, low and constant, and every animal Dominic had ever spoken to had leaned against that hum the way you lean against a warm wall without thinking about it.

The hum was gone.

Dominic stood in the cold with their hand on Bramble's heaving neck and listened, really listened, the way their mother had taught them before the teaching became a crime. The mountain was silent. Not quiet—*silent*, the difference between a held breath and no breath at all. No foxes moving in the scree. No owls. The night birds had not come back down after their fright. Even the wind through the pines sounded wrong, like it was passing through a dead thing.

"Something's happened at the border," Dominic said aloud, because saying it made it a fact instead of a fear, and facts could be managed. Fears couldn't.

Bramble screamed again, and this time Dominic felt the shape under it, and the shape was a single wordless certainty: *run south, run south, run south.*

*No.* Dominic pushed the thought at her, firm, the way you'd press a hand to a panicking animal's shoulder. *Not yet. Not in the dark on the scree. You'll break a leg, and then we're both dead.*

She fought them. She was nine years of trust and she fought them, hauling at the picket line, and Dominic had to put their whole body against her neck and breathe at her, slow, in and out, until the terrible machine of her panic wound down by degrees into a trembling that at least had room in it for reason. Dominic talked to her the whole time—not with the gift, just with their voice, the ordinary human voice, telling her about the morning, about the oats waiting in the feed bag, about the pass below Coldwater and the soft going on the far side. It didn't matter what the words were. It mattered that they were steady.

By the time the sky in the east had gone from black to a bruised grey, Bramble was standing with her head hanging, sweat-dark along her shoulders, and Dominic was crouched at the dead fire feeding it twigs, and both of them were listening to the same silence.

The stars had not gone out, exactly. Dominic had looked. The sky above Thornfell was simply *empty*—not clouded, not fogged, but vacant, the way a room is vacant after the furniture's been carried out. Dominic had spent their whole life learning to read the sky for weather, for season, for the hour of the night, and looking up at it now was like opening a book and finding the pages blank.

Beasts felt the wards dying. That was the old smugglers' wisdom, back before the wisdom itself became contraband. His mother had said it: *when the stones sing wrong, the animals know first. Trust the animals, little fox, because the crown will tell you nothing true.*

The stones weren't singing wrong. The stones weren't singing.

Dominic fed Bramble, checked her hooves by touch, and did not strike camp. Every instinct that had kept them alive through nine years of smuggling said *move, move now, get off this road, get under stone*. But Bramble was spent, and the high passes in the dark with a panicked animal was a way to die stupid, and besides—

Besides, there was a sound coming up the defile. Faint. Rhythmic. Dominic froze with a currycomb in their hand and identified it the way they identified everything: by what it would cost them.

Hoofbeats. More than one. Coming fast from the north, from the border, moving with the particular urgent rhythm of riders who were chasing something or fleeing something, and in Dominic's experience the two sounded nearly the same until you saw the colors.

They saw the colors. Dominic, flattened behind a granite shoulder with Bramble's trembling nose pressed against their neck, watched three riders come up the defile below in the first grey light, and the grey light caught the black and silver of crown livery, and Dominic's mouth went dry.

Not fleeing, then. Chasing. Crown riders, out at first light, hard off the border road—and the border road was a day's ride north of here, which meant they'd ridden through the night under an empty sky, which meant whatever they were chasing mattered.

The riders passed. The hoofbeats faded south down the switchbacks. Dominic counted to three hundred, the way their mother had taught them—*count to three hundred, little fox, and then count to three hundred again*—and was about to breathe when Bramble went rigid against them.

Not fear this time. Something else. Her head came up, ears swiveling toward the scree slope above the camp, toward a tumble of boulders and dead bracken where there should have been nothing, and Dominic felt her attention like a finger pointing.

Dominic listened with the other sense, the secret one, the one that lived under their tongue like a coin under a floorboard.

And heard, faint and ragged, a human heartbeat. And beneath it—this was the strange part, the part that made the hairs on Dominic's arms stand up—beneath it, a thread of the same wrongness Bramble had been screaming about all night. Not in the heartbeat. *Around* it. Clinging to it like smoke clings to a coat.

Someone was up there. Someone who'd been near the border stones when they died.

* * *

The sensible thing was to break camp and go. Dominic knew this. The sensible thing had been Dominic's religion for nine years: move light, move quiet, be nobody, help no one, because the crown had made compassion a felony and every kindness was a rope you handed a man to hang you with. Dominic's brother had been kind once, loudly, in a marketplace, and the rope had gone around his neck instead. Dominic had learned. Dominic had gotten very good at learning.

Dominic climbed the scree slope anyway.

They hated themselves a little for it, in the familiar way, the way you hate an old scar. *Just to see*, they told themselves. *Just to know what the riders were after. Knowledge is cargo too.*

The figure was crammed into a cleft between two boulders, and it took Dominic a moment to even recognize it as human. Grey cloak, grey with dust and worse. A horse's leg-boot, cracked. One hand pressed flat against the stone, and the hand was the worst thing—knuckles split, nails torn, as if its owner had climbed the slope on it. As if its owner had climbed anything at all on legs that, when Dominic got close enough to see the rest, were shaking like a foal's.

The stranger heard them. Dominic watched it happen—the head coming up, the whole body coiling to fight even though there was clearly nothing left in it to fight with—and Dominic saw the moment the stranger's eyes found them and the fight drained out and something worse replaced it, something like a person watching the last door close.

"Please," the stranger said. The voice was a woman's, scraped raw. "I'm not— I need—" She stopped, swallowed, and Dominic watched her try again with the grim discipline of someone who had been trained to be precise and was fighting her own body to manage it. "Water. Please. And then I'll be gone, and you never saw me."

Dominic should have said yes to that. *You never saw me* was the only contract Dominic Okafor had honored without exception for nine years.

"Turn around," Dominic said instead.

The woman's mouth twisted—not quite a laugh. "If I could turn around, smuggler, I'd have turned around an hour ago and kept walking." She shifted, and the shift cost her a hiss through her teeth, and Dominic saw then the dark wet bloom on her side, low, under the ribs, soaking through the grey cloak. A blade wound. Not fresh—the blood had gone brown at the edges—but not old either.

"Off my mountain," Dominic said, "there's no such thing as *gone*. There's *found by the riders* and *found by me*, and you've managed the second, which means the first is close behind."

Something moved in the woman's face. Not hope—Dominic knew hope when they saw it, and this was its poorer cousin, the thing hope sent ahead of itself to negotiate. "You're the one they call the fox of Thornfell," she said. "The one who knows the passes."

"I'm the one nobody calls anything, because nobody knows me." Dominic crouched, out of grabbing range, and studied her the way they'd study a bridge before crossing: weight-bearing, or not. She was younger than the voice suggested, thirty at most, with a ward-keeper's close-cropped hair growing out unevenly, the way hair grows when the person wearing it stopped caring about a month ago. There was a brand of office on her belt-buckle—a silver ward-wheel, scratched nearly smooth. Deliberately scratched, Dominic realized. She'd tried to destroy it and hadn't had the heart or the knife to finish.

"You were ward-keeper," Dominic said. It wasn't a question. The cloak was wrong for a soldier, wrong for a courier, and only one kind of person wore a ward-wheel and rode the border alone.

The woman's jaw tightened. "Was."

"They say you warned them. Before the war-engine bill. That you stood up in the assembly at Caerlum and said the wards would fail if they kept feeding them bound beasts, and they stripped your office for it." Dominic had heard the story in three border taverns and one smuggler's campfire, and in every version the teller's voice had dropped, the way voices drop around dangerous truths. "Serah Vane."

The woman closed her eyes. "You have the advantage of me, and I'm in no state to enjoy it."

*Leave her.* The sensible thing sat on Dominic's shoulder like a carrion bird, patient, certain. *She's crown-marked, she's hunted, she's bleeding, and every hour she's on your mountain the riders come closer to finding the routes that keep you alive. Leave her. You buried your brother. You know the arithmetic.*

Bramble's voice came up the slope, faint, not words, never words, but a feeling like a hand on Dominic's wrist: the same wrongness clinging to this woman, the same *eating* emptiness—and threaded through it, unmistakably, pain. The woman's pain, and beneath it something else, something that felt the way the border stones used to hum. Faint. Flickering. But *there*.

The wards had died and this woman was still carrying a spark of them, and the beasts could feel it, and Dominic could feel it, and it felt like the first true thing Dominic had touched in nine years.

"Here's how it will be," Dominic said, and heard their own voice go flat and hard, the smuggler's voice, the one that made terms instead of friends. "I carry you down to the camp. I stop the bleeding. You rest until dark, no longer. Then I put you on the south road and we never saw each other. That's the whole contract. You don't learn my name, you don't learn my routes, and if the riders take you, you never saw me. Agreed?"

The woman—Serah Vane, disgraced, bleeding, three crown riders behind her and an empty sky above her—looked up at Dominic for a long moment. Dominic had the uncomfortable sense of being weighed by someone who was very good at weighing things, and had found something on the scale that she hadn't expected.

"Agreed," she said.

Getting her down the scree took the better part of an hour and cost Dominic a new understanding of the word *heavy*. She didn't complain. That was the remarkable thing—she made the sounds a body makes when it's torn, sharp breaths, one bitten-off cry when her boot skidded on loose stone—but she never once asked to stop, and she kept her split hand flat against the rock the whole way down, taking her share of the weight even when she had no share to give. Dominic had carried drunk smugglers and wounded smugglers and one dying horse, and they had never carried anyone who *refused to be cargo*.

Bramble stood at the bottom with her ears forward, which was its own miracle, and let Serah Vane put a bloodied hand on her nose without so much as a flicker.

"She likes you," Dominic said. "That's a first. She doesn't like anyone."

"Animals always—" Serah stopped. Dominic watched her stop, watched the sentence die behind her teeth, watched her look at Bramble with an expression that was suddenly, sharply careful. "Animals are better judges than people," she finished.

*Mm,* Dominic thought. *She noticed.* Of course she'd noticed; a ward-keeper lived her whole professional life reading the behavior of beasts, and Bramble had spent the last hour behaving toward Dominic like a soldier toward a beloved sergeant. Dominic filed the look away, a debt marked in an invisible ledger, and got the woman down onto the bedroll by the rekindled fire.

The wound was ugly but clean, a slash rather than a stab, and it had stopped bleeding on its own, which meant she'd bound it on the ride and bound it well. Dominic cleaned it with boiled water and the good spirits they kept for emergencies, and stitched it with the steady hands their mother had called surgeon's hands and their father had called wasted on a smuggler, and Serah Vane took all of it without a sound except at the very end, when Dominic tied off the last knot and she let out a breath that sounded almost like a word in a language Dominic didn't know.

"Who cut you?" Dominic asked, because they couldn't not.

"Riders." Her eyes were on the fire. "The same three, if your ears are as good as your hands. I killed their horses at the ford and took a blade doing it. They'll have remounted at Coldwater by now. They'll pick up my trail by midmorning."

"Then you rest until dark and you're gone by—"

"They're not following *me*." She turned her head, and up close, in the firelight, her eyes were the grey of the border stones before the stones went silent. "They're following what I'm carrying. I was at the third ward-station when it failed, smuggler. I saw it fail. It didn't break. It was *eaten*. And I pulled the core-stone out of the station before the riders came, and I have been running south with it for two days, and it is the only piece of the border wards left in the world." She lifted the hem of her cloak, and there, wrapped in rag and bound to her belt, was a lump of dull grey crystal no bigger than a fist, and it was humming.

Dominic heard it without the gift and with it at once—heard it in their ears and in their teeth and in the coin under their floorboard, a thread of the old warm hum, thin as the last note of a struck bell.

"The sky," Dominic said. Their voice came out strange. "The stars. Last night—"

"Eaten," Serah Vane said again. "Every star. Every ward. The same hunger, all at once, along the whole border." She looked up at the blank eastern sky, and Dominic saw in her face the thing their mother had once described to them as the look of a person watching a prophecy come true: no satisfaction in it at all. "I told them for six years this would happen. I told them the wards were alive and we were starving them. And now it's happened, and I'm the only witness, and the crown wants me dead before I can say *I told you so* to anyone who matters."

The fire cracked. Bramble shifted her weight, and the picket stake hummed.

Dominic sat back on their heels and did the arithmetic they'd spent nine years refusing to do: the riders at Coldwater, two hours' hard ride; the woman who couldn't walk; the stone that hummed like the world used to; and the empty sky pressing down on all of it like a lid.

"Change of contract," Dominic heard themselves say, from very far away, in a voice that didn't sound like a smuggler's at all. "You're not going anywhere until I move you myself. And I know roads south that the riders don't."

Serah Vane looked at them for a long time. Then, from the north, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of hoofbeats—many more than three—coming down out of the high pass like a landslide with a rhythm.

"They found the ford," she said quietly. "That's not a pursuit. That's a sweep." Her grey eyes came back to Dominic, and there was no hope in them, only arithmetic, the cold kind, the kind that counted other people's lives. "They're clearing the mountain, smuggler. Every living thing on it. Starting with the ones who helped me."

Above them, under the lid of the starless sky, Bramble began, very softly, to scream.
