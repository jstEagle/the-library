## 4. A Debt Paid in Secrets

The stranger slept like the dead, which Serah Vane considered deeply unfair.

Her own body had spent three days running on fear and pine tea, and every hour of rest she'd stolen had been paid back in dreams—wards unraveling like cut thread, a sky with no stars in it, her own name spoken in the King's voice. The smuggler, meanwhile, had dragged her half-conscious up a goat track no wider than a hand's span, fed her broth from a dented tin cup, and then curled against the mule for warmth as if this were any other night.

She watched them now across the small fire. Dominic. That was all she'd gotten—a first name, offered flatly, like a coin tossed to a beggar to make them go away. Short dark hair cropped close for practicality, hands scarred in the pattern of someone who handled rope and cargo and animal more than blade. Eyes that never quite settled on anything for longer than a breath, always tracking the treeline, the wind, the sound of water below.

*Running,* Serah thought. *But careful about it.*

The kind of careful that took years to build. She knew because she'd built it once herself, before the Order stripped her sash and her name and put her out of Caerlum's gates with nothing but the clothes she wore and the truth nobody wanted.

The fire popped. Dominic didn't stir—but the mule did.

Bramble lifted its head from its doze, ears swiveling toward the darkness beyond the camp, and made a sound Serah had never heard a mule make: a low, deliberate huffing, rhythmic almost, like speech without words. And Dominic's hand moved in their sleep—not reaching, not flinching, but *answering*, two fingers curling twice against the ground.

The huffing stopped. Bramble settled.

Serah sat very still.

She had spent eleven years keeping the border wards of the eastern reach, and before that four years in the ward-halls learning the theory of binding—the mathematics of it, the cruelty dressed up as necessity. She had stood in the Binding Yards at Caerlum once, only once, and watched a hawk have its wild mind folded down into something obedient, and she had thrown up behind the wall afterward, and she had been told by her superiors that this was what service cost.

So she knew what binding looked like from the outside. She also knew what the *opposite* looked like—what it looked like when a creature was not bound but *addressed*. The old texts called it beast-speech. The law called it sedition. The last public speaker of beasts had been burned in Merrowgate square nine years ago, and Serah remembered the crowd's silence, the way even the children had understood not to cheer.

Two fingers, curling twice. A mule answering in the dark.

Serah looked at the sleeping smuggler and felt the cold arithmetic begin.

*Report them.* One word to any crown rider, one description, and she bought back everything she'd lost—pardon, standing, maybe even reinstatement if the right captain heard. They'd take Dominic in chains to Caerlum, and there would be a trial, and there would be a pyre, and Serah would sleep warm in a barracks bed knowing she'd done it with a single sentence.

That was the bargain the crown had left her. That was the shape of loyalty now: informing on strangers who pulled you out of a river and fed you broth.

Bramble snorted softly. Dominic's fingers twitched again, an idle little motion, the way a person might pat a dog without waking.

Or maybe, said a colder voice in her head, you're wrong. Maybe they just talk to animals the way lonely people do. Maybe the mountain has made you see treason in every shadow.

Maybe. But the wards hadn't failed because she was paranoid either. And she'd learned, at the cost of everything, to trust the evidence over the comfortable explanation.

She fed another branch to the fire and waited for morning.

* * *

Dominic woke the way they always woke: all at once, silently, one hand already on the knife under their bedroll.

The woman was sitting up, watching them. Not pretending otherwise, which was worse. In the grey pre-light her eyes were sharp and level, the eyes of someone doing sums.

"Your mule woke me twice in the night," she said.

"Bramble's talkative."

"Mm." She turned a stick in the fire. "Third time I've crossed Thornfell. First time I've seen a mule hold a conversation."

There it was. Dominic kept their face still and their breathing easy and reached for the blandest answer available. "Mules are honest creatures. More than people."

"That wasn't a question."

"No," Dominic agreed. "It wasn't."

They studied each other across the coals. Dominic catalogued the details they'd avoided examining yesterday while hauling a half-drowned stranger uphill: the faded line across her forearm where a ward-keeper's oath-band used to sit, the military posture she couldn't quite slouch out of, the fine burn scars on her fingertips that came from ward-work, not cooking fires. This was not a bandit or a refugee farmer. This was a soldier of some flavor, recently disgraced, currently very dangerous.

"You pulled me out of the Whitewash ford," she said. "Carried me two miles uphill. Fed me. Asked nothing." She tilted her head. "People don't do that. So either you're a saint, or you wanted something and hadn't decided what yet."

"Maybe I'm just bad at business."

"Maybe." Her gaze flicked—deliberately, Dominic thought—to Bramble, grazing at the edge of camp, and back. "Or maybe you know exactly what I am, and you're waiting to find out if I'm going to be a problem."

The wind moved through the pines. Somewhere downslope, a jay started up its harsh alarm call, and Dominic felt the familiar reflexive listening, the way the sound unpacked itself in their head—*stranger-stranger-below-two-legs-two-legs*—before they could stop it.

And the woman watched them hear it.

Not guessed. *Watched.* Saw the half-second where Dominic's attention went somewhere no ordinary person's attention went, saw the way they caught themselves.

"I was a ward-keeper for fifteen years," she said quietly. "I studied binding. Which means I studied the thing binding was outlawed to replace. Do you know how few people alive have ever seen true beast-speech?" She let that sit. "One. Before today, I'd only read accounts."

Dominic's mouth had gone dry. Every instinct screamed *deny*, the old litany—deny and deflect and be nobody, be a smuggler, be a mule-driver, be nothing worth a second look. Their whole life was built on the next lie being small enough to believe.

But she'd seen. There was no unseeing it. And she hadn't run, hadn't shouted, hadn't reached for a weapon.

"The ford," Dominic said at last. "You were fleeing crown riders. Wounded. You could have died in that water."

"Yes."

"Anyone who reports me gets a pardon." Dominic kept their voice flat. "I've seen it happen. A shepherd near Highpass, three years ago. His own brother sold him."

"I know what the reward is." She didn't look away. "I'm telling you I'm not taking it."

"Why?"

"Because I spent fifteen years holding the wards that keep things like what's coming out of the north, and when I told the crown those wards were failing, they took my rank and my name and threw me out." Her jaw tightened. "Then the wards fell anyway, and the sky went black, and the army marched north under an empty heaven. So forgive me if I've stopped believing the crown's arithmetic. It doesn't count people like you. It doesn't count much of anything anymore."

Dominic stared at her. Something in their chest had gone tight and strange—the feeling of a locked door being addressed from the other side.

"What's coming out of the north?" they asked.

"I don't know yet. That's why I need to reach Merrowgate. The free cities still have scholars, old records, ward-masters who remember things the capital burned." She paused. "And I can't get there alone. My shoulder's torn, my supplies are gone, and every road south of here will have riders on it within the week. But there's one person in these mountains who knows paths the crown's maps don't show." She met Dominic's eyes. "I'm asking for your help, smuggler. Not your secret. Just the roads."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I'll limp south alone and probably die at the second checkpoint, and you'll go back to being nobody, and in a year none of this will matter because whatever ate the stars will be eating us too." She said it without drama, like a weather report. "But I think you won't say no. I think you've been watching the sky too."

Dominic had. Three nights now, the stars had simply been gone—not clouded, not dimmed, but absent, as if the whole ceiling of the world had been scraped clean. Bramble had refused to graze under the open sky after dark. Even the jays had gone quiet at dusk, a silence that pressed on the ear like deep water.

"The free cities are six weeks' travel if you know the ways," Dominic said slowly. "Eight if I have to carry you part of it."

"I heal fast."

"That's not a yes."

"It's not," she agreed. "But it's a start."

Dominic looked at Bramble, who looked back with one liquid eye, inscrutable as ever. Then at the woman with the burned fingers and the missing oath-band and the terrible level honesty.

"Dominic," they said. "You have my name now. If you spend it, spend it well."

"Serah Vane." She extended her good hand. "And I don't sell people. I've been on the wrong end of that trade too long."

They shook. Her grip was strong despite the wound. Neither of them smiled.

* * *

They broke camp within the hour, moving along a ridge route Dominic had used since childhood—a path that existed on no map because Dominic's mother had carved it out of goat trails and memory, and her mother before her. The mountains fell away to the east in grey-green folds; below, the Whitewash ran thin and bright, and beyond it, faint as smoke, the lowland roads where riders would soon be hunting a disgraced ward-keeper.

Serah walked well for a wounded woman, though Dominic noted how she favored the left side, how she never let Bramble get behind her. Old habits. Soldiers counted threats.

By midday she had begun asking questions—not about the gift, which surprised Dominic, but about everything else. The state of the passes. Which villages still traded openly. Whether the high shepherds would shelter travelers. Each answer she filed away with visible precision, and Dominic realized with growing unease that they weren't guiding a fugitive so much as briefing a general.

At the noon halt, while Bramble drank from a snowmelt stream, Serah crouched at the water's edge and finally asked the question that mattered.

"Does it work on everything? Birds, wolves—all of it?"

Dominic weighed lying one more time. It would be so easy. *Just mules. Just livestock.* A small lie, survivable.

But the stars were gone. And this woman had looked at the empty sky and believed her own eyes when the whole crown had looked away.

"Wolves are hardest," Dominic said. "Too much pride. Ravens are easiest—they'll tell you anything for the right scrap. Deer lie constantly." They hesitated, then added, because some stubborn honesty had taken root overnight and wouldn't stop: "My sister could do it better than me. She could calm anything. A bull elk once, with a broken leg, singing to it until it let her splint the limb."

"Where is she now?"

"Caerlum. There's a stone." Dominic picked up their pack, ending the subject. "We should move. Storm coming off the western peaks—I can smell it."

Serah stood too. For a moment her hand hovered near Dominic's arm—an instinct of comfort, aborted halfway—and then she simply nodded and shouldered her borrowed pack.

"I'm sorry," she said. "For your sister."

Dominic didn't answer. But they walked a little closer to her after that, close enough that their shadows touched on the trail, and neither of them remarked on it.

* * *

The storm caught them at dusk in a hollow beneath a granite shelf, and they made camp in its dry throat while rain hammered the mountainside like a fist demanding entry. Bramble dozed standing; the fire was small and smokeless; and Serah, wrapped in the single blanket, finally let exhaustion show in her face.

"They'll have found my horse by now," she murmured. "Or my body washed downstream. Either way, by tomorrow they'll know I survived the ford. Riders will start sweeping the high trails."

"Then we move at first light and don't stop till the far side of the Kettle."

"The Kettle?"

"A valley between two ridges. One way in, one way out, both mine." Dominic fed a twig to the flames. "Crown maps show a dead end there. Smugglers use it twice a season. By the time they figure out we're not in it, we'll be three days gone."

Serah studied them across the fire—that same assessing look, but softer now at its edges, worn down by gratitude she clearly didn't enjoy owing. "Why are you doing this? Truly. You owe me nothing. Your whole life is built on staying invisible, and I'm the least invisible thing that's happened to you in years."

Dominic was quiet a long moment. Rain drummed the stone above. When they spoke, their voice was low, almost lost under the storm.

"Because when I was twelve, my sister hid me in a hollow like this one while riders took her away, and I stayed quiet, and I've been staying quiet for twenty years, and the sky just went black anyway." They looked up at the shelf of stone as if they could see through it to the murdered heavens. "Hiding didn't save her. Hiding isn't saving anyone. Maybe it's time somebody with my particular skills stopped being nobody."

Serah Vane looked at the smuggler for a long moment, and something passed across her face that might have been the beginning of trust—or the beginning of grief for a person who'd had to become this hard, this watchful, this alone.

"Then we have a bargain," she said. "Your roads, my knowledge. Your secrets stay yours unless you choose to give them. And when we reach Merrowgate—" she hesitated, then finished, quieter, "—when we reach Merrowgate, maybe you'll let me introduce you to people who won't ask you to be nobody."

Outside, the storm raged on. But inside the hollow, two fugitives sat close to a small fire, having traded the only currencies that mattered: a name given, a promise made, a debt acknowledged that neither intended to forget.

Neither of them slept much. Near midnight, Bramble suddenly raised its head and blew a sharp note through its nostrils, staring into the rain-dark below the hollow—and Dominic, listening past the hearing of any human, went rigid.

"Serah." Their voice was barely a whisper. "Riders. On the lower trail. Four of them, maybe five. And they're not passing through."
