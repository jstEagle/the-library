## 5. The Hollow King

He is in the garden. He knows this because the roses are blooming, and the roses only bloom in the month of Emberfall, and the month of Emberfall is when Liria was born, and he had planted the first bush the morning after her birth with dirt still under his fingernails from— 

From what?

The thought slides away like water through cupped hands. He reaches for it again, the way he has reached a thousand times, ten thousand times, for the memory that will tell him where he is, who he is, why his mouth tastes of iron and old starlight.

*You are King Aldric Caerlum,* he tells himself. *Third of that name. You were crowned at nineteen. Your wife's name was Maren. Your daughter's name is Liria. You are in the palace at Caerlum, and you are king.*

The words feel true. That is the worst part. They feel true the way a painted door feels like an exit.

His hand rises — his hand rises without him asking it to — and touches one of the roses. The petals are wrong. Too pale. The color of bone left out in rain. When his fingers close around the stem, he feels the thorns bite, and he feels the blood well up, and he feels—

Nothing.

That is the trick of it. That is always the trick of it. The pain belongs to someone else. It happens in his body, in his skin, but it is reported to some distant office he cannot find, filed by clerks he has never met. He watches his own blood bead on his own thumb with the detachment of a man watching weather.

*Wake up*, he thinks. *Wake up wake up wake—*

The garden shudders.

No — not the garden. Everything shudders. The sky above the palace walls, which should be deepening toward evening and studded with stars, is instead a flat black expanse, smooth as the inside of a closed eye. There are no stars. There have been no stars for — how long? A day? A year? Time here moves like syrup, like something drowning slowly in its own thickness.

And beneath his feet, far below the flagstones, below the foundations, below even the oldest cellars where the first kings of Caerlum were laid to rest, something *humms*. He has felt it every day since the night the sky went dark. A vibration in the bones of the world. The binding-stone, the old histories called it. The First Anchor. He used to think it was a myth, a story the ward-keepers told to make their order seem older than it was.

Now he can feel it breathing.

*Wake up.*

For one glorious, terrible moment, he does.

* * *

He is standing on the balcony overlooking the great hall, and there are hundreds of people below him, courtiers and soldiers and servants all kneeling, all facing him, and their faces are wrong. Not monstrous. Worse than monstrous: *smooth*. Faces like eggs, like unfinished sculpture, like people waiting for someone to tell them what expression to wear.

"Rise," says his voice.

He did not say it. He felt the shape of the word form in his throat the way a puppet feels the string — no. He doesn't know what a puppet feels. Why does he know that word? Where did he learn it? His memories come to him now in categories he never made, labeled in a hand that isn't his.

The courtiers rise as one body. One hundred and forty of them — he counts without wanting to, counts the way he now counts everything, compulsively, because counting is one of the few things that still feels like *his*. One hundred and forty faces turned upward, and not one pair of eyes holds anything he recognizes. Not loyalty. Not fear. Not love.

Hunger.

They are hungry the way he is hungry. That is the realization that comes to him in this lucid moment, arriving cold and complete as a blade between the ribs: whatever took him did not take him alone. It has been drinking from the whole city, the whole country, sip by sip, and everyone it drinks from grows smoother, quieter, more obedient. The courtiers first, then the guards, then the servants, then the farmers in their fields, all of them being sipped down to nothing while they smile and bow and call him *Your Majesty* in voices growing flatter by the week.

Except —

There. At the edge of the hall, near the doors, a serving girl with flour on her apron. She is looking at him, and her face is not smooth. Her face is *terrified*, and terror is the most beautiful thing Aldric Caerlum has seen in longer than he can measure, because terror means she is still in there. Terror means she remembers what a king should be and knows he is not it.

She sees him see her. Her eyes widen. And Aldric does the only thing left to him, the small rebellious act of a prisoner scratching marks on a dungeon wall:

He weeps.

Or — his body tries to. His eyes sting; something in his chest clenches; a sound builds in his throat. But before the tear can fall, before the sound can escape, the other thing in him catches it, examines it with something like curiosity, and sets it aside the way a man might set aside a curious stone found on the road.

Interesting, it seems to say. The vessel still produces salt water when distressed. Note it.

Then his mouth opens and his voice says, "Bring me the reports from the northern wards," and the girl scurries away, and Aldric screams inside the prison of his own skull until the scream wears itself down to a whisper, and the whisper wears itself down to nothing.

* * *

Here is what he remembers of the last true night, the night before the stars went out. He clings to it. He polishes it every day like a coin he fears will wear thin.

He had been awake past midnight in his study, reading the dispatches Serah Vane had sent before her exile — the ones he'd been told were hysterical, paranoid, the ravings of a disgraced ward-keeper trying to claw back relevance. *The border wards are failing. Not weakening — failing. Something is drawing on them from the inside. I beg you, Majesty, let me show you the binding-stone records. I beg you to look at what your grandfather sealed.*

He had almost summoned her. That is his confession, the one he makes to himself in the dark places between lucidities: he had *almost*. He had held her letter over a candle flame and then, at the last moment, set it down unburned, because some animal instinct in him — the same instinct that had once told him not to trust a smiling chancellor, not to drink the wine at his uncle's feast, the instinct that had kept a boy-king alive in a nest of vipers — that instinct had whispered *keep this, keep her, keep this thread unpulled until you understand it.*

He fell asleep at his desk with her letter under his hand.

And he dreamed of a doorway.

Not a door. A doorway with no wall around it, standing alone in a darkness deeper than any cellar, and the doorway was open, and beyond it there was a vastness that was not space and not time but *appetite*, pure and patient and older than the mountains, older than the stars themselves, and it looked at him across that impossible threshold and it said — not in words, because words were beneath it, words were a courtesy it extended the way a butcher extends a clean knife —

It said: *I have been locked out for so long. And you have been locked in for so long. Shall we trade?*

"No," Aldric had said, in the dream, in the study, in the last hour of his reign over his own body. "No. I am the king. This is my house."

*Yes,* it agreed, gentle as snowfall. *Yours. For now.*

And then it came through him like winter coming through a cracked window, and the last thing the true King Aldric saw from behind his own eyes was his own hand rising to snuff the candle, steady and unhurried, and the last thing he heard was his own voice saying, in a tone of perfect calm that he had never once been able to achieve in forty years of kingship:

"Send word to the ward-masters. The borders will need reinforcing."

The lie had been so easy. That is what haunts him most. The thing wore his voice better than he ever had.

* * *

Time passes. He thinks it passes. In the hollow places between lucidities there is no clock, only the hum of the binding-stone and the slow, rhythmic sensation of being *drunk from*. That is the closest word he has found for it. Every day the other thing draws on him — on his memories first, the sweetest vintage, then his habits, his preferences, the small textures of a self — and every day there is less of him to draw on, and he has begun to notice the gaps the way a man notices missing teeth: with his tongue, constantly, helplessly.

He no longer remembers his mother's face.

He noticed this three days ago — or three years; time is syrup — when he tried to summon her image and found only a shape in a dress, a warm blur where a person should be. He had raged against the loss, thrown himself against the walls of his own mind until the effort broke him open into another lucidity, and he had spent that entire precious window of clarity reciting what remained: *Maren. Maren's laugh sounded like water over stones. Liria's hair was the color of autumn oak. She is in the south, at her aunt's estate in Merrowgate, she is safe, she is safe, she is—*

Is she safe? Is anyone? Can the thing reach that far, drink that wide? He does not know. He knows only what he can see from the balcony of his own stolen eyes: the black sky, the smooth-faced court, the armies drilling in the outer yards with a precision no human army has ever managed, moving in patterns too perfect, too identical, like ants, like cells in a body, like things that share one hungering mind.

And he knows the letters. Every evening, the other thing sits at his desk — *their* desk — and reads dispatches by candlelight, and Aldric, caged in the back of his own skull, reads over its shoulder because reading is all he has left. Reports from the north: wards collapsing in sequence, the Thornfell passes quiet, the free cities debating. Reports from the hunting parties: *the Vane woman eluded riders at the mountain's edge. Local guide suspected. Possibly gifted.* 

Gifted.

The word lands in him like a spark landing in dry grass.

Beast-speakers. Outlawed in his grandfather's time, hunted in his father's, tolerated nowhere, and Aldric — the real Aldric — had signed the edicts himself, young and frightened and advised by men who assured him it was necessary, and he had told himself for thirty years that necessity was a kind of innocence. Now, watching the thing in his body read about a smuggler in the Thornfells who could speak to beasts, Aldric feels the old shame rise up hot and alive, and he clings to the heat of it, because shame is proof of self, and proof of self is all the kingdom he has left.

*If I ever get out,* he vows, in the dark behind his own eyes, *if some miracle pries this creature off my soul, the first edict I burn will be the Binding Law. The first thing I restore will be the ward-keepers. The first person I find will be that guide in the mountains, whoever they are, and I will kneel if I have to—*

The other thing pauses over the dispatch.

Aldric goes utterly still, the way a mouse goes still under an owl's attention. Has it heard him? Can it hear him? In all this time — however long this time is — he has never known whether his thoughts leak. Whether his rage, his grief, his small nightly rebellions are private or whether they are entertainment, whether the ancient hunger keeps him conscious for the same reason men keep songbirds: for the pleasure of listening.

The candleflame bends. Somewhere far below, the binding-stone hums a half-tone lower.

Then his hand — their hand — turns the page, and the moment passes, and Aldric understands with sick certainty that it had paused for no reason at all, that he is so small now, so diminished, that he registers to the thing wearing him as barely a flicker, a candle seen from a mountaintop, a fly's heartbeat against glass.

He is going to disappear. Not die — disappear. Be poured out drop by drop until the vessel called Aldric Caerlum holds nothing but the shape of a man, a glove waiting for a hand, and already the glove is stretching, softening, learning to hold something else entirely.

Unless.

Unless somewhere out there, in the mountains where the stars used to be visible, a smuggler with a forbidden gift is running from crown riders. Unless the spark he felt land in the dry grass of him can catch. Unless the thing that ate a kingdom can be made to choke.

Aldric has nothing left to pray with. No gods he trusts, no saints he remembers, no church whose rites survive in him intact. So he prays the only way a hollowed king can: he takes the one memory he has defended every single day, the memory he has polished until it shines, and he offers it up into the dark like a coin tossed into a well.

*Maren laughing. Water over stones. Liria's hair like autumn oak. Remember me. Someone remember me. Someone come looking.*

Far below the palace, the binding-stone hums.

And in the mountains of Thornfell, though he cannot know it, a wounded woman is sheltering from crown riders in a smuggler's cave, and a mule named Bramble is refusing, very quietly and very firmly, to let the darkness win.

* * *

That night — if it is night; the sky gives no answer — the other thing stands at the window of the royal bedchamber and looks up at the starless black, and Aldric, watching from the depths, feels it do something it has never done before.

It smiles with his mouth. And it speaks aloud, softly, to no one, in a voice that is Aldric's voice and yet flows like something poured:

"Soon."

The word echoes in the empty chamber, and beneath the floor, beneath the foundations, beneath the bones of the first kings, the binding-stone answers — a low, grinding note, like a door being forced.

Aldric presses himself against the innermost wall of his own mind and begins, again, the only prayer he has left:

*Someone come looking. Someone come looking. Someone—*
