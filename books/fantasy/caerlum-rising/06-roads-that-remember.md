## 6. Roads That Remember

Three days south of the pass where I'd pulled Serah Vane out of the snow, the road remembered things I wished it didn't.

I'd walked this stretch a hundred times—the old drover's track that wound down from Thornfell's shoulder through country the maps called empty. It wasn't empty. It was full of everything people had stopped looking at: cairns raised by shepherds four generations dead, a shrine to some river-god whose name had worn off the stone, the foundations of a tollhouse burned in a war nobody sang about anymore. The land kept its own ledger. That was one of the first things my mother taught me about smuggling, though she never used the word. *The roads remember, Dom. You just have to be quiet enough to hear them.*

I hadn't been quiet enough lately. Serah Vane rode behind me on Bramble—she'd insisted on walking the first day and I'd insisted harder that a wounded woman with a cracked rib wasn't going to outpace crown riders on her own feet—and every mile she asked questions I couldn't afford to answer honestly.

"How far to the next settlement?"

"Half a day."

"Is it loyalist?"

"It's a place where people eat." I kept my eyes on the track. "Loyalty is a menu item. Costs extra."

She made a sound that might have been a laugh if it hadn't been so tired. "You talk like a man who's never trusted anyone."

"I talk like a man who's still alive."

That shut us both up for a while, which was fine by me. The truth was worse than what I'd said: I talked like someone whose brother had trusted the wrong person once, and paid for it with his neck and my whole family's name. Trusting got you killed. Being known got everyone around you killed too. So I'd built myself into a shape that fit through cracks—no name worth remembering, no trade worth taxing, no gift worth burning.

Except now there was a woman behind me who'd watched me murmur a frightened fox across a flooded ford like it was an old friend, and who hadn't reported me, and who wanted something from me that required me to keep existing in her company. The cracks were getting crowded.

Bramble's ears swiveled back before I heard anything. Then I heard it: the smell of woodsmoke, wrong for midmorning, and under it the particular scorched-onion reek of a cookfire left too long unattended.

"There," Serah said, pointing ahead with the hand not pressed to her ribs.

A waystation huddled against the hillside—one of the crown's rest houses, built back when the border wards were new and travelers still believed the roads were safe. Its door stood open. Its chimney bled smoke. And tied to its post outside stood a mule wearing a saddle blanket stamped with the crest of the Fourth Legion.

"Army remount," Serah murmured. "Deserter?"

"Or thief." I slid off Bramble's back and put a hand on her neck. She was trembling—not at the smoke, not at the strange mule. At nothing I could find. *Easy, girl. What is it?* Her answer came up through my palms as it always did, wordless and enormous: a feeling like standing too close to a cliff edge in fog. Something ahead that smelled wrong. Not dangerous-wrong. *Hungry*-wrong.

"You feel it too?" Serah said quietly, watching my face. I realized my hand was still on Bramble's neck and my expression had gone somewhere honest. I dropped both.

"Horses spook easy."

"They do." She let it go, which scared me more than an argument would have. Every hour she spent not saying *beast-speaker* out loud was an hour the word grew heavier between us.

We approached the door the way I approached everything: low, quiet, from the side. Inside, the waystation's common room was a disaster of flour and blood—flour everywhere, blood only a little, and a man standing over the wreckage holding a carving knife like a sword he'd only read about.

He was big, soft in the middle, maybe forty, wearing the grey-and-red of a legion cook with the insignia ripped off. His hands shook so badly the knife chattered against itself.

"Don't," he said. "Don't come in. I'm armed. I'm—I've killed men."

"You've killed onions," I said. "There's an onion right there that lost the fight."

His eyes flicked down, came back up. The knife lowered an inch. "They'll hang me," he said. "I ran. Third night of the march, when they started feeding the horses the way they were feeding them, I just—I couldn't—" He swallowed. "Tamsin. Tamsin Ferrow. Cook, Fourth Legion, eleven years."

"Feeding them how?" Serah asked, and her voice had changed, gone flat and ward-keeper sharp.

Tamsin looked at her uniform fragments—at the faded silver thread of the ward-keeper's knot on her collar, half torn away—and something in him broke open like a dam. The words poured out. The army marching without stars. The supply wagons that went north heavy and came back light. The horses bound for the front lines that came back from the binding yards with eyes like wet stones, that didn't eat, didn't sleep, didn't *blink*, just stood in their traces until they dropped and were replaced. And the soldiers. The ones sent to the yards to be fitted with the new discipline-collars. How they came back obedient and punctual and hollow, how they laughed at nothing, all together, all at once, at 3 bells exactly, every night.

"The officers say it makes the army efficient," Tamsin whispered. "Efficient. I cooked for those boys. I know what a boy looks like when there's no one home behind his face." The knife finally clattered onto the table. "So I took the mule and I ran, and then I couldn't stop running because every direction felt the same, and I ended up here, and I've been cooking for two days because I don't know how to do anything else. You can turn me in if you want. I won't run again. My legs are done."

I looked at Serah. Serah looked at me. And here's the thing I'll tell true, even now: I meant to walk out of that room. I meant to take the road alone the way I always had, because every extra body was weight, was risk, was a name the crown could put on a list next to mine. That was the arithmetic I'd lived by since I was fourteen years old and learned what happened to people who were known.

But Tamsin had set out bowls. Three bowls. He'd seen two strangers come through the door and his first instinct, after the knife failed, was to feed us. Eleven years a cook, and the war had taken his army and his courage and his legs' willingness to carry him, and it had not managed to take that.

"Sit down," I heard myself say. "Before you burn something else."

* * *

We ate stew that was, genuinely, the best thing I'd tasted in two years—smoked pork and wild garlic and some trick with dried plums Tamsin refused to explain—and somewhere between the second bowl and the third, the arithmetic changed. Not all at once. Arithmetic doesn't work that way. But a number shifted somewhere, and I found myself telling him the road south, the safe crossings, the wells that weren't poisoned. Found myself saying *we*.

Serah laid out our situation plainly, because she was the sort of woman who thought secrets were ammunition and allies deserved to know what war they'd joined. The wards were failing. The stars were being eaten—she said *eaten*, not *hidden*, and the certainty in her voice made the fire seem colder. The king was dead or worse than dead. The free cities were the only power in Caerlum that might still act, if they could be convinced to believe it, and convincing them required getting past the crown's riders, which required a smuggler, which required—

"A guide," Tamsin said. He was staring into his bowl. "You need a guide, and you need food, and you need someone who can walk into any garrison kitchen in the country and get fed without questions, because cooks don't get interrogated, cooks get *complained at*." He looked up, and his eyes were red-rimmed and terrified and resolved. "Take me. Please. I can't go home and I can't hide in a kitchen forever. Give me something to carry besides this knife."

I should have said no. I want that on the record of whatever ledger the roads keep: I knew better, and I said yes anyway.

"Can you ride?" I asked.

"Badly."

"Good. Bramble hates competence."

* * *

Two days further south, the road brought us to Harrowmere—or what the maps called Harrowmere, a market town strung along a river crossing, famous for one thing: paper. Half the scribes in Caerlum bought their vellum from Harrowmere's tanneries, and where the paper trade goes, the mapmakers follow. Which is why I'd come here before, and why I knew the shop at the end of Tanner's Row where an old man sold charts of places that officially didn't exist.

Old Wren was blind. Everyone said so; Wren most insistently of all. He sat behind a counter stacked with rolled charts he could not see, in a room lit by one window he did not face, and he drew coastlines and mountain passes and smuggler roads from memory with a steadiness of hand that made my skin crawl. They said he'd been a royal cartographer once, before the binding laws, before he'd walked out of the capital one night leaving his sight behind him like an unpaid debt. Nobody knew the true story. Wren charged triple for it and nobody paid.

The bell above his door rang when we entered—Tamsin had to duck—and Wren's head turned toward us with the unnerving precision of a compass finding north.

"Four sets of feet," he said. "One limping. One trying not to limp. One heavy enough to be nervous about floorboards." A pause, nostrils flaring. "And a mule. You're either smugglers or pilgrims, and pilgrims smell better."

"Wren." I kept my voice level. "It's Okafor."

"Okafor." The old mouth folded around the name like it was tasting something. "Haven't heard that surname in this shop in years. Last time, it came attached to a boy who bought a chart of the capital sewers and paid in stolen candlesticks." His blind eyes fixed on me, milky and terrible. "You've grown into your trouble, I see. Who's your lady soldier?"

"That's what I came to ask about," Serah said, stepping forward. "Master Wren, I need—"

"You need a route to Merrowgate that avoids the king's road, the ferry taxes, and the new checkpoint at Stonebridge, which was built last month and isn't on any chart but mine." He smiled, dry as dust. "Yes. Everyone running south needs that. Business is booming. War is wonderful for cartographers."

"How did you know about the checkpoint?" Serah asked. "If it's not on any chart—"

"Because the birds complain about it, girl. Constantly. Crows have opinions about checkpoints the way priests have opinions about sin." He tapped his temple. "I pay attention to what the birds say. It's cheaper than paying for spies, and more reliable than paying for friends."

Behind me, Tamsin made a small choking noise. I turned. In the doorway, half-hidden by Tamsin's bulk, stood a child—a girl, nine or ten maybe, skinny as a heron, with a crow perched on each shoulder and a third on her fist, and a look on her face like she'd been caught somewhere she absolutely intended to keep being.

"That would be my fault," the child announced. "Mostly."

"Kess," Wren sighed. "How many times have I told you not to follow customers home?"

"You said *don't follow customers home*. These aren't your customers. They're *interesting*." The crow on her fist croaked agreement, and I swear on Bramble's good hoof, it sounded like it understood the conversation. Kess pointed at me with her free hand. "Corax says the tall one smells like the mountain animals. Says they *talk to him*. Corax says—" and here she tilted her head, listening to a bird the way other children listened to music, "—Corax says you told a fox about the flood. Two weeks ago. Up past the black pines."

The room went very quiet. Serah's eyes moved to me. Tamsin's eyes moved to Serah. Somewhere outside, the ordinary sounds of Harrowmere went on—cart wheels, gulls, a tanner shouting—but inside that shop the air stopped moving.

I had spent my entire adult life building the silence around my gift brick by brick. One child and her crow had kicked a hole in it in eleven seconds.

"Kess talks to birds," Wren said mildly, into the silence. "Openly. Loudly. In markets. On docks. To anyone within earshot, including, on one memorable occasion, a crown tax collector. She's alive only because everyone in Harrowmere agrees she's touched by the river god and touched children are bad luck to harm." He turned his head toward me. "You understand why I mention it."

Because it was a warning. Because it was a mirror. Because a child doing openly what I did in secret was either proof that hiding was pointless or proof that hiding was the only reason I still breathed, and I didn't know which.

"She shouldn't do that," I said. My voice came out rougher than I intended. "Talking to them where people can hear. It's—it's outlawed for a reason."

"The law's a reason, is it?" Kess frowned at me, genuinely puzzled, the way only children and saints can be puzzled. "Corax says the crows think the law is silly. The crows say lots of things are silly. The crows are very wise." She leaned toward me, sudden and close, and her eyes were quick brown and utterly unafraid. "Do you really talk to foxes? What do they talk about? Is it true they're all liars? Corax says foxes exaggerate."

"Kess," Wren said again, gentler.

But something had already happened in me by then, something I couldn't point to precisely—some seam giving way. Maybe it was the stew. Maybe it was Tamsin's shaking hands setting out three bowls. Maybe it was the fact that I'd spent three days with Serah Vane watching me not-talk about the biggest thing in my life, and here was a child who'd never learned to not-talk about anything, still breathing, still laughing, protected by a whole town's willing blindness. Maybe the roads had decided I'd carried the silence long enough and were handing me pieces of a different life, one stitch at a time, whether I wanted them or not.

"We're going to Merrowgate," I said. "To the council of the free cities. It's dangerous. There are crown riders on the roads, and worse things than riders, and anyone caught with us will be tried for treason." I looked at the old man behind his invisible charts, at the cook with the carving knife, at the bird-girl with her impossible crows. "Nobody has to come."

Wren laughed. It was a real laugh, rusty but real, and it transformed his whole ruined face. "Boy, I've been waiting fifteen years for someone to walk into this shop and say *nobody has to come*. Do you know how long a man can draw the same coastline before he starts wanting to *walk* it?" He was already reaching for his staff, already rolling charts with those impossibly steady hands. "Merrowgate. I know nine ways in and the council chamber's worst acoustics. I'm coming. Kess—"

"I'm coming," Kess said instantly.

"—will stay with her aunt—"

"I'm COMING, Wren. Corax already asked the other crows. The crows vote yes. Crows always vote yes on interesting things."

"The crows," Wren said gravely, "are not a council."

"They're better than a council," Tamsin muttered, and then looked horrified at himself, and then—slowly, like ice cracking—grinned.

* * *

We left Harrowmere at dawn, six of us and three crows and one opinionated mule, taking the river path south where the ground stayed soft enough to swallow hoofprints. Wren rode double with Tamsin and narrated the landscape from memory, correcting himself only twice. Kess ran ahead and came back and ran ahead, reporting traffic on the king's road in the crows' own vocabulary: *feathers, feathers, iron feathers, many iron feathers, going north.*

North. Everything was going north. Toward the border. Toward the dark sky.

It was near dusk on the second day when Kess came back at a run instead of a saunter, her crows wheeling above her in agitated spirals, and the smile was gone off her face entirely.

"Dominic." She caught her breath. "Corax says—you have to see. Down by the ford. There's a column coming up the valley, and Corax says it's not soldiers." Her voice dropped, and I saw that she was frightened in a way that had nothing childish left in it. "Corax says it's *chains*. Miles of chains. And the chains are full of animals."

Serah closed her eyes. Tamsin went grey. And Bramble, beside me, began to tremble.

Down in the valley, faint on the wind, I could already hear it: the groan of wagon wheels, the jingle of harness, and underneath it all, thin and rising, the sound of hundreds of voices screaming in languages no human court had ever bothered to learn.

Every instinct I owned told me to take the high path, cut east, put the valley between us and that sound, and never look back.

Instead I found myself turning to Serah, and hearing myself say: "Show me."
