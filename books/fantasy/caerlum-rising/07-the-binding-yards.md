## 7. The Binding Yards

Serah counted the wagons twice because the first count made her sick.

Eleven. Eleven iron-barred carts grinding up the pass road below their hiding place, each one drawn by a pair of oxen whose eyes had been sewn shut with silver thread. Not blindfolded—sewn. She had seen the practice once before, years ago, from the ward-tower at Caerlum's edge, and she had told herself then that distance had made it look worse than it was.

It did not look worse from thirty feet away. It looked exactly as bad as it was.

"Down," she breathed, though none of them needed telling. The ridge above the pass was a spine of grey granite furred with pine, and they lay along it like stones themselves, Tamsin flat on her belly with flour still ghosting her knuckles, Old Wren with his sightless face tilted toward the sound of wheels, Kess pressed between Dominic and a boulder, small hands white-knuckled around a crow's legs before the bird went still in her grip.

The convoy moved slow. That was the worst of it—the patience of it, the unhurried certainty of a machine that knew nothing on this road could stop it. Crown banners snapped above the drivers, gold thread on black, and beneath each banner rode a handler in the lacquered half-mask of the Binding Corps, its blank porcelain face turned toward the cargo it governed.

And the cargo—

Serah made herself look. She owed them that much. Every soul who had ever called her mad in a council chamber, every door closed against her, every year of exile in the free cities where she'd drunk cheap wine and repeated her warnings to people who smiled politely and changed the subject—all of it had bought her the right to look, now, without flinching.

The beasts were chained standing, packed so tight in the wagons that their flanks touched. Stags with antlers sawn to stumps. A bear the size of a granary, its muzzle caged in riveted steel. Wolves—and there were more wolves than anything else, grey bodies stacked in the last three wagons like cordwood, too listless even to growl. And threaded through fur and hide, sunk into the flesh at the shoulder and the haunch and the base of the skull: binding-rings. Black iron circles etched with ward-script she recognized because she had helped design half of it, back when she had believed the wards were only for keeping magic *in*, not for reaching into a living mind and hollowing out everything except obedience.

She could see the script moving. That was new. The characters crawled along the rings like insects, feeding, and as she watched, the nearest stag lifted its head—not in struggle, not in fear, but in the empty, waiting way of a thing that had forgotten what wanting felt like—and turned toward the ridge as if summoned.

Its eyes found nothing. There was nothing left behind them to find.

"They're taking them north," Tamsin whispered. "Toward the front." Her voice had the flat carefulness of someone working very hard not to feel something. "I cooked for garrisons six years. I know what supply trains look like. That's not supplies."

"It's an army," Serah said quietly.

Old Wren's head turned toward her. "An army of what?"

"Of stolen minds." She kept her voice low and level, the voice she'd used in briefing rooms, the one that had never once saved anyone. "A bound beast doesn't just obey. It gives. Whatever it was—its cunning, its strength, its instinct—that all gets siphoned through the ring into whatever the handlers point it at. A war-engine isn't a weapon you aim. It's a mind you spend."

"How many minds does a war cost?" Kess asked. She was eleven years old and she asked questions like knives, and no one ever had a good answer for her, and today was not going to be the first day.

"As many as it takes," Serah said, and hated herself for it.

Below, the lead handler raised one gauntleted hand and the convoy halted. A wheel had jammed—or been made to jam—in the rutted ice of the pass, and while drivers swore and pried at it, the handlers walked the line of wagons with the bored efficiency of men checking inventory. One of them stopped at the bear's cage, produced a slender rod tipped with a sliver of binding-iron, and pressed it through the bars against the animal's skull.

Serah expected bellowing. What came instead was worse: silence. The bear shuddered once, all the way down its enormous frame, and then went still as furniture. The handler moved on to the next cage.

Beside her, she heard Dominic's breathing change.

She had learned, over the weeks on the road, to read Dominic the way she'd once read wards—by pressure, by absence, by the shape of what wasn't being said. Dominic hid everything, and so everything showed, if you knew where the seams ran. And right now every seam Serah knew was straining.

"Dominic." She said it barely above breath.

Nothing. Dominic's eyes were fixed on the wolf wagon, on a she-wolf at the front bar with frost crusted in her fur and a binding-ring crawling with light at her throat, and Serah watched the exact moment when looking became unbearable.

Then Dominic moved.

Not far. Six inches, maybe—a shift of weight off the elbows, muscles gathering under that ragged smuggler's coat, the whole body becoming a drawn bow aimed down the slope at eleven wagons and forty armed men. Serah had seen soldiers make that same transition, and it always looked the same: the moment arithmetic died and something older took over.

She caught Dominic's wrist.

Her fingers closed around bone and sinew and the fine tremor running through both, and Dominic turned on her with a look Serah had never seen on that carefully blank face—raw, furious, wet-eyed, young. For one heartbeat she thought she would be shoved aside. She tightened her grip instead and held on.

"There are forty of them," she mouthed.

Dominic's lips shaped a word back. At first Serah thought it was *so*, and then she understood: *so*. So what. So there are forty. As if numbers were a language spoken by cowards.

"You'd get us killed," she breathed, "and you'd get *them* killed—" a tilt of her chin toward the wagons— "for nothing. They don't even know enough to run. The rings take that first. The knowing-how-to-run."

Something in Dominic's face cracked along a fault line Serah hadn't known was there. And then, so quietly that the wind nearly stole it, Dominic spoke—not to her, she realized, or not only to her. The words were shaped for the slope below, for the frozen air between ridge and road, in a low murmur that didn't sound like any language Serah knew and yet landed in her chest with the weight of meaning anyway.

The she-wolf at the front bar lifted her head.

It was a small motion. A few inches. But it was the first voluntary movement Serah had seen from any animal in that convoy, and it carried more defiance than a regiment—the wolf raising her muzzle toward the ridge, toward the voice calling to her from cover, ears straining forward under the frost. Listening. Remembering, for one held breath, that she had ever been a creature who listened to anything but the ring.

The handler noticed.

He turned, rod rising, and Serah's heart slammed once against her ribs as the sliver of binding-iron came up—but the wolf had already dropped her head, gone dull again, a puppet hanging slack on its strings. The handler studied her a moment, tapped the rod against the cage bars twice, and moved along.

On the ridge, Dominic exhaled like a man surfacing from deep water. The fury drained out of that coiled body by degrees, leaving something worse behind: shame. Dominic pulled the wrist gently from Serah's grasp and turned away, pressing a forearm against the granite, forehead down, shoulders shaking with breaths that were carefully, deliberately silent.

* * *

They let the convoy pass. There was nothing else to do, and doing nothing carved a trench in all of them that Serah suspected would be there for the rest of their lives.

They waited until the last wheel-sound faded northward and the crows Kess had posted reported the road clear, and then they descended to the pass itself, because Wren wanted to stand where the wagons had stood and read the ground. He did that sometimes—walked a road with his boots and his stick and his strange, total memory, and came away knowing things no scout with two good eyes could have told him.

"Ice's been cut," he announced, crouching to drag his fingertips through the ruts. "Wheel-jam back there? Not an accident. They cut the ruts ahead of time and fill them with water overnight. Stops the train on schedule." He straightened. "Regular stops mean regular feedings. You can't siphon a mind continuously or it burns out. So they drain them in doses, at halts." His jaw worked. "Like milking."

"That's enough, Wren," Tamsin said.

"It's precisely enough," Wren said, mild and terrible. "You asked me to map this country, girl, and I'm mapping it. This is what's on the road."

Kess had wandered to the spot where the wolf wagon had halted. Crows had come down around her—six of them, seven, dropping out of the pines at her low whistle—and they stood in a loose dark semicircle, feathers fluffed against the cold, and Serah realized they weren't begging food. They were reporting. Kess stood among them with her head cocked, lips moving, and her face was doing something complicated and terrible: the face of a child assembling an understanding of the world that no child should have to assemble.

"The crows followed them," Kess said, without turning. "From the valley. From the yards." She pronounced the word carefully, tasting it. "There's a place. Fences, and pens, and fires that burn green. That's where they put the rings on. The crows say the screaming starts at dusk and stops by morning, and after morning the animals are quiet forever, and the crows don't like to land there because the ground tastes wrong." She finally turned, and her eyes were too old. "The crows say the ground tastes wrong, Serah."

Serah knelt to her level. It took effort; her knees had opinions about the cold these days. "Thank you for telling me."

"Is that where they're going? The north place?"

"Yes."

"Are we going to follow them?"

Serah opened her mouth to say something prudent, something about the mission, about Merrowgate and the council and the slim thread of hope they were carrying south—and then she caught sight of Dominic, standing apart at the road's edge, looking north along the empty pass.

"No," Serah said. "Not today. Today we go to Merrowgate and we make the council listen, because words are also weapons, Kess, and right now they're the ones we have." She held the girl's gaze. "But I want you to understand something. What we saw today—every person in this company saw it, and none of us will unsee it. When the time comes, and it comes for everyone eventually, you'll know why we fight. Some people need that taught to them. You got it in one morning, on a mountain road. I'm sorry for that."

Kess considered this with the grave attention she gave everything. Then one of the crows hopped onto her shoulder and said something in crow, and she almost smiled, and the almost was enough.

* * *

They camped that night in a hollow below the tree line, a smuggler's cache Dominic had marked on the way south—a stone overhang deep enough to kill the wind, with dry wood already stacked under it like a gift left by some past version of themselves. Tamsin built a fire small enough to hide and large enough to matter, and put oats and dried plums and the last of the salt pork into one pot, because she held the theory, stated often and never argued with, that people who ate together stayed together.

Serah took first watch at the lip of the overhang, and it was there, an hour into the dark, that Dominic came and sat beside her.

Neither of them spoke for a long time. Below the hollow the snowfields glowed faintly blue under a sky that was still wrong—still starless, still a smooth black lid over the world—and Serah found herself doing what she always did on watch: reading the darkness for what wasn't visible in it.

"I had a dog," Dominic said at last.

Serah didn't turn her head. You didn't turn your head when a wild thing finally approached you; everyone knew that, even people who couldn't speak to beasts.

"Her name was Sorrel. Before I understood what I was—before I knew it had a name, or a law—I used to talk to her. Just talk. Tell her things." A pause. The firelight behind them painted half of Dominic's face orange and left the other half in darkness, which felt accurate. "She was the only one who ever heard my actual voice. Everyone else got the other one. The one I built."

"What happened to her?"

"She got old. She died in a warm corner of a barn I was robbing, actually, which is a better death than most people get." Dominic's laugh had no humor in it, only shape. "But here's the thing I keep thinking about today. Near the end, when she was stiff and slow, I talked to her less. Not because I stopped loving her. Because I started being afraid someone would hear me. Even then. Even with her, I rationed it. I made my own dog into another thing I hid." Dominic's hand closed slowly around a pebble, lifted it, set it down. "Today I watched forty men drive eleven wagons full of chained voices north to be spent like coin, and the law says that's order, and my silence says that's safety, and I lay on that ridge and did the arithmetic and chose the arithmetic. Again. Like I've done every day of my life."

"You'd have died," Serah said quietly. "All of us. And the wolves too."

"I know." The pebble skittered off the ledge into the dark. "Knowing is the trap, isn't it? They build a world where doing the right thing is suicide, and then they call you wise for choosing to live." Dominic finally looked at her, and the firelight caught something raw in it. "You caught my wrist today. Why? You could've let me go. My corpse would've been very educational for the others."

Because your gift is the only weapon I've seen in ten years that frightens me with hope. Because I've spent my whole life watching institutions fail and I cannot watch people fail too. Because—

"Because you weren't throwing your life away," Serah said. "You were throwing it away *badly*. If we do this—if we ever do this—we do it in a way that costs them more than it costs us. Otherwise it's just grief wearing a flag."

Dominic stared at her. Something shifted behind those guarded eyes, tectonic and slow, the way a mountain moves.

"When you say *we*," Dominic said carefully, "you say it like it's already decided."

"Is it?"

Another long silence. Down in the hollow, Tamsin murmured in her sleep; one of Kess's crows muttered back from the rafters of stone, and the two sounds braided together oddly, domestic and absurd in the middle of a starving wilderness.

"I keep hearing that wolf," Dominic said finally. "The one at the front bar. When I called to her, she remembered. One breath, and she remembered what she was. They can't erase it, Serah. The rings can sit on a mind for years and one true word pulls it back up to the surface." Dominic's voice dropped. "Which means every beast in every wagon, in every yard, in every garrison in this kingdom, is sitting right under the surface. Waiting for one true word."

Serah felt the idea settle over her like a mantle—cold, heavy, and unmistakably shaped like a crown. Or a burden.

"And you're the only person alive who speaks it," she said.

"For now." Dominic's mouth twisted. "The law calls it witchcraft. The Corps calls it sedition. My mother called it a curse the day they took my brother for it." A breath. "But yes. Me."

"Then here's what I know," Serah said, and she heard her own voice go hard and clear, the ward-keeper's voice, the one that had once held a border against the dark and been exiled for the trouble. "Tomorrow we walk into Merrowgate, and I am going to stand in front of the fattest, most cowardly collection of merchants and magistrates the free cities have ever produced, and I am going to tell them their king is dead and his crown is a mouth, and they are going to call me mad again. And when they do, I want to be able to tell them about the binding yards. About the wagons. About the she-wolf who lifted her head when someone spoke her name in the old tongue." She met Dominic's eyes. "Not because it will convince them. Because it's true, and truth is the only thing I've got left, and I intend to spend it loudly."

Above them, the starless sky pressed down like a held breath.

And far to the north, so distant it might have been imagination, the horizon flickered—a thin pulse of greenish light, there and gone, like something vast swallowing.

Kess's voice drifted up sleepily from the fire. "My crows say the sky blinked."

Serah watched the horizon a long time after the light faded, and beside her Dominic watched it too, and neither of them said what they were both thinking:

Whatever was eating the stars had just finished a course.
