## 8. Crows Bring Bad News

The crows came back at dawn, and they came back wrong.

Kess knew every bird in her murder by the sound of their wings—Bramblewing flew with a hitch in his left stroke from the hawk that had caught him as a fledgling and let him go; Old Marrow glided like a scrap of black cloth thrown on the wind; the young ones, Pebble and Soot and the twins, still flapped too much, all enthusiasm and no economy. She could have picked them out of any sky with her eyes shut. That was how she'd first known she was different, back in the fen villages where nobody talked to birds except to shoo them: she didn't just recognize them. She *heard* them.

So when six shapes dropped out of the grey morning over the ruined watchtower where the company had made camp, Kess heard them before she saw them, and what she heard made her sit up so fast her neck cracked.

They were tired. Not road-tired, not we-flown-far tired. Tired the way Bramble the mule had been tired the night the wards fell, trembling and refusing to eat. Tired the way you got when you'd seen something your mind couldn't fold up small enough to carry.

"Kess." Soot landed on her knee first, because Soot always did, pressing his warm feathered weight against her shin like he wanted to be a cat instead of a crow. His voice in her head wasn't words exactly—it was pictures and hungers and cold—but this morning the pictures came sharp enough to ache.

She fed them first. That was the rule, hers alone, because birds who worked for nothing learned to distrust you, and because her mother had said you could tell everything about a person by whether they fed strays. Dried fish from Tamsin's stores, torn into strips. The crows ate fast and silent, which was itself an answer. Crows chattered while they ate. Crows complained about portion sizes. These crows ate like soldiers.

Around her, the camp woke in its small careful ways. Tamsin already had the cookfire going low and smokeless, muttering to a pot of oats like the oats were a difficult child. Old Wren sat propped against the watchtower's fallen stone, his milky eyes turned toward the dawn he couldn't see, hands moving over the map he was redrawing from memory. Dominic was at the treeline doing their morning listening—that was what Kess called it, though she suspected it was more than listening—and Serah stood on the tower's broken stair with a spyglass she never used anymore, because there hadn't been stars to navigate by for days and no sun bright enough to matter.

Nobody noticed the crows at first. Nobody but Kess.

That was the arrangement. Serah had laid it out the night after Merrowgate, in the flat voice she used for things she'd already decided: *You're the youngest, so you're invisible. People look through children. Your birds can go where we can't.* Kess had agreed because agreeing meant belonging, and belonging meant Tamsin saved her the crisped edges of the flatbread and Wren told her stories about cities she'd drawn maps of and Dominic—Dominic looked at her sometimes with an understanding Kess couldn't name yet, like looking into a mirror held up across ten years.

But standing here now, with Soot's pictures unspooling behind her eyes, she understood something else about the arrangement: it meant she heard the bad news first. Alone.

*Show me again,* she told Soot. *Slow.*

He showed her roads. The north road out of Thornfell, the one that ran along the river toward the crown city. It had been empty for years—Kess's crows had told her that long ago, told her which villages still lit lamps and which had gone dark, told her where the tax riders patrolled and where the smugglers' cut-throughs began. Now the road was a river of men. Not marching. *Flowing*, the way water flows downhill, without banners, without drums, without the ragged human rhythm of an army that eats and sleeps and grumbles. They moved in the dark. They moved through the rain. They moved without stopping, and their faces—

Soot flinched away from his own memory, and Kess felt him do it, felt the picture tear.

*Their faces,* she said. *Soot. Their faces.*

He gave it to her because he trusted her, and she would remember that trust later, when she understood what it had cost him: faces lit faintly from within, pale as fish bellies, eyes open and fixed on nothing, mouths slack. Men who weren't asleep and weren't awake. Men being *poured* down a road like grain into a mill.

And above them, riding the wind like smoke given purpose, threads of light. Thin silver threads, streaming up off the land itself—from the fields, the rivers, the sleeping towns—and flowing south and east toward the crown city, converging like veins feeding a heart.

Or a mouth.

"Kess."

She came back to herself with Tamsin's hand on her shoulder, warm and flour-dusted. "You've gone white as porridge, little one. Breathe."

"I'm fine," Kess said, which was the first lie she could remember telling anyone in this family, and it tasted awful, like chewing foil.

Tamsin didn't believe her—Tamsin never believed her, it was one of the best things about her—but the camp was stirring and Serah was coming down the stairs with questions already forming, so Kess folded Soot's pictures up small and put them somewhere deep, the way she'd learned to fold up the memory of her mother's face, and said, "The crows are back. From the north. You'll want to hear this."

* *

Serah heard it standing up, arms crossed, jaw set, the way she heard everything—as a problem with a shape she intended to break. Kess reported it the way she'd been taught, plain and ordered: the army on the north road, its size, its speed, its direction.

"How fast?" Serah asked.

Kess did the arithmetic she hated, the kind that turned birds into numbers. "A hundred miles since two nights ago. Maybe more."

The silence that followed had weight. Wren stopped tracing his map. Even Tamsin's pot went quiet.

"That's impossible," Serah said finally. "No infantry does thirty miles a day. Not crown regulars, not deserters, not anyone. Men need sleep. They need food. They *break down*."

"They're not walking like men," Kess said, and then, because the picture wouldn't stay folded anymore, because it was pushing up through her like a splinter working toward skin: "Serah, they're not walking like *alive*. Their eyes are open the whole time. They don't blink. The crows tried landing near them to listen and the men didn't react, didn't swat, didn't even—" Her voice did something embarrassing and young, and she hated it. "One of the men had mud in his mouth. Just standing there with mud in his mouth. And the light—the land is *draining*, there are these threads, like the wards but backwards, like someone pulled the wards out and is drinking through them—"

"Kess." Dominic's voice, quiet, from the treeline. They'd come back without her noticing, the way they always did. "Slow down. Come sit. Tell it in order."

She sat on the cold stone next to Wren because Wren was the safest thing in the world, blind and ancient and smelling of ink and pipe smoke, and she took a breath the way Tamsin had taught her when the nightmares came, four counts in, seven counts hold, eight counts out, and she told it in order.

When she finished, nobody spoke. Down in the valley below the watchtower, morning fog lay over the fields like something drowned.

"The land," Wren said at last. He said it slowly, tasting the words. "You say the light comes *out of* the land."

"Yes."

"And flows toward the capital."

"Yes. Like it's being drunk."

Wren's hands found each other in his lap and gripped. Kess had never seen him frightened. Frightened Wren was a new country, and she didn't want the map.

"When I was a young man," he said, "before the binding laws, there were ward-keepers who claimed the old wards didn't only guard borders. They claimed the first ward-masons bound something into the very bones of Caerlum—a covenant, land and crown, each sustaining the other. The king keeps the land. The land keeps the king." His sightless eyes turned toward where Serah stood. "If something is drawing the land's strength southward, into the seat of the crown—"

"It's feeding," Serah said flatly. "It's not just wearing Aldric. It's eating us. The country itself. Star by star, field by field, man by man." She laughed, one hard note with no humor in it. "We thought we were running from an army. We're standing in a larder."

Kess watched the grown-ups do what grown-ups did, which was turn fear into plans: Serah calculating distances and days, Dominic quiet and watchful, offering once, softly, "I could ask the wolves along that road. They'd see closer than the crows," and Serah nodding like that was a reasonable sentence, which Kess still found miraculous every time. Plans were good. Plans kept hands busy.

But underneath the plans, Kess sat with Soot on her knee and understood something the grown-ups hadn't said out loud yet, because they were kind, or maybe because they hadn't done the arithmetic she'd done:

Her crows had flown over that army. Had flown close enough to count faces. And the thing in the crown city drank light out of the whole land—so what happened, someday soon, when it noticed six small lights moving south? What happened when it noticed *her birds*?

Soot pressed against her leg, and she pressed back, and for the first time in her life she wished she couldn't hear them. Because if she could hear them, she could hear them be afraid. And if she could hear them be afraid, then some part of her would always, forever, be standing on that north road among the open-eyed men, watching the light get pulled out of the world.

* *

They broke camp at midmorning, heading south along the ridge, and Kess flew her birds in relays the way Serah had taught her—ahead, behind, ahead—scouting the road to Merrowgate's southern gates. It was work. Work was good. Work kept the pictures folded.

It was Old Marrow, coming back from the south at noon, who brought the last piece.

He landed on her shoulder heavier than usual, and what he brought wasn't a picture. It was a feeling, and feelings from crows were rare and enormous, the way earthquakes were rare and enormous. Marrow was old. Marrow had survived hawks and winters and boys with slingshots and one memorable encounter with a fox den, and nothing scared him. But what he'd seen south of Merrowgate had reached into his old clever mind and left claw-marks.

Roads of soldiers. Not one column—*many*, converging on the free cities from three directions, moving at that same terrible poured-water pace. And between the columns, the fields of the lowlands, and in the fields—

Kess made him show her twice. She wished she hadn't. The farmers were still in the fields. Still bent over the late harvest, still swinging scythes, still living their small lives—and the light was coming out of them too, thin silver threads rising off their bent backs like heat-shimmer, drifting south. They didn't fall down. They didn't even stop working. They just grew fainter, somehow, the way a coal grows fainter, the way a voice grows fainter when you've been talking all day. Working and fading, fading and working, and none of them looking up.

They didn't know. That was the worst of it, the piece Kess carried afterward everywhere she went like a stone in her shoe: *they didn't know*. The kingdom was being eaten alive and the people in it were still harvesting dinner.

She walked into the middle of the afternoon halt and said, "Serah. I have to tell you something and I don't want to."

Serah looked at her—at her, really looked, the way she almost never did, the way she reserved for battlefields—and knelt so they were eye to eye. "Tell me anyway."

So Kess did, all of it, the converging columns and the farms and the fading people, her voice staying level right up until the end, when it betrayed her: "They don't know, Serah. Nobody knows except us and the birds. Everyone's just—they're just *harvesting*, and it's eating them, and they don't even know—"

"Hey." Serah's hand settled on her head, awkward and heavy, a hand that clearly didn't know how to be gentle but was trying with everything it had. "Listen to me. That's why it matters that you know. That's why your birds matter. The whole kingdom gets to not-know because you knew first." A pause, and then, quieter, rougher: "You did good, Kess. You did better than good."

Kess cried anyway, briefly and furiously, wiping her face on her sleeve and glaring at everyone daring them to mention it, and nobody did, and Tamsin appeared with a honey-cake from nowhere, because Tamsin always appeared with food from nowhere at exactly the right moment, and that helped more than anything.

But that night, lying under the watchtower stars-that-weren't, wrapped in her blanket with Soot tucked against her chin, Kess did the last arithmetic, the one she'd been avoiding.

Merrowgate was two days south. The armies were three days out, maybe less, moving faster than anything alive should move. Which meant the council that wouldn't listen, the squabbling lords and merchants who'd spent a whole week refusing to believe the sky, were going to learn the truth very soon, in the worst classroom there was.

Unless somebody made them listen first.

She rolled over and found Serah sitting awake on the last watch, silhouetted against a horizon where, far to the south, above the distant smudge of Merrowgate, there was a glow that shouldn't have been there. Not firelight. Not sunrise. Something paler, and hungrier.

"Serah," Kess whispered across the dark. "My crows can be in Merrowgate by tomorrow morning. Before the envoy. Before anyone. If you write down what we know—all of it, the roads, the speed, the draining—I can make them *see* it. Every lord at that council table, all at once, through a hundred pairs of crow's eyes."

Serah was quiet a long moment. When she answered, her voice had the edge Kess had learned to love, the blade-edge of a decision already made. "Can you do that? Show a hundred people the same thing at once?"

Kess looked at Soot. Soot looked back with one bright eye, and around the dark camp she felt the others waking, listening—Marrow, Pebble, the twins, her strange loud family of feathers and hunger.

"We're crows," she said. "Showing people things they don't want to see is what we're *for*."
