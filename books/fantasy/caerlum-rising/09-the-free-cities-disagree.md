## 9. The Free Cities Disagree

Old Wren had drawn the coast of Merrowgate four times in his life, and each time the harbor had changed its shape like a cat settling into a new position. The first time, he'd been a young man with ink-stained fingers and a patron who paid by the league. The last time, he'd been old enough that the harbormaster had walked him along the seawall personally, describing every crane and warehouse so he could render them true. *Here is where the fish-tithes are weighed. Here is where the ward-keepers' barges moor. Here is where the sea-wall's oldest stones remember the drowning of the first king.*

He could not see any of it now. But he could hear it—the gulls wheeling and complaining in their flat coastal voices, the slap of hulls against pilings, the groan of the great chain that closed the harbor mouth at dusk. And beneath it all, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, he could feel it: the hum of the wards.

Or what was left of them.

"They're fraying," Serah said beside him. She said it quietly, the way you name a sickness in front of a patient who hasn't been told. "Can you feel that? The harmonic's wrong. It used to ring clean as a bell. Now it's—" She searched for the word.

"Like a bell with a crack in it," Wren said. "Rings anyway. Pretends it doesn't."

Behind them, Tamsin's cart creaked to a halt. Kess slid off the back before it fully stopped, and Wren heard the crows go up from her shoulders in a scatter of wings—six of them now, or seven; they multiplied when he wasn't counting—and settle onto the customs-house roof like a delegation arriving early for an unpleasant meeting.

"Everyone remembers the plan?" Serah asked.

"We're refugees," Dominic said. Their voice was flat and even, the voice of someone reciting a border-crossing story they'd told at a hundred checkpoints. "Fleeing the fighting upcountry. We have trade goods"—Tamsin's dried fish and salt pork, packed under honest canvas—"and no business with anyone. Kess doesn't talk to strangers. Nobody mentions the king."

"Nobody mentions the sky," Kess said solemnly, from somewhere around hip height.

"That too."

Wren kept his silence, because the plan had a hole in it the size of the harbor, and everyone knew it, and saying so would only make it wider. The plan assumed Merrowgate was still a city with a gate. But he had spent three days listening on the road—listening the way blind men listen, with the whole surface of the self—and what he'd heard coming out of the city was not the sound of a place preparing for war. It was the sound of a place preparing to argue.

* * *

The argument was audible from the customs yard.

They were waved through the gates with barely a glance—the guards were watching the sea, not the land, which Wren filed away as either wisdom or a very expensive mistake—and funneled into the lower city, where the refugee quarter already smelled of woodsmoke, wet wool, and fear. And over all of it, rolling down from the council heights above the harbor like weather, came voices.

Not one voice. A dozen. Raised, overlapping, breaking apart and reassembling into new shapes of disagreement.

"The Council of Free Cities," Serah murmured, "in full session."

"You can tell?" Tamsin asked.

"I sat in those chambers for eleven years." There was something raw in her voice, something she smoothed over quickly. "I know what a knife-fight sounds like even through three walls."

Wren heard more than knives. He heard the particular music of institutions failing—a sound he'd catalogued across forty years and nine kingdoms: the scrape of chairs, the gavel that no longer commanded silence, the rising pitch of men who had decided that volume was the same thing as truth. He'd drawn maps of battlefields that sounded friendlier than this.

They found lodging in the house of a woman named Maren, a former quartermaster of the ward-service whom Serah trusted and everyone else pretended not to notice trusting. Maren took their coin, asked no questions about the child with crows perched on her shutters, and fed them barley soup with the grim generosity of someone who had fed a hundred strangers this month and expected to feed a thousand more.

"How bad?" Serah asked her, low.

Maren glanced at Kess, then at Wren, and seemed to decide blindness counted as discretion. "The southern cities want to bar the harbor and wait it out. Corvane wants to treat with the crown—send envoys, open negotiations. Half the merchants believe the whole thing is a smugglers' panic cooked up to corner the grain routes." She set down the ladle. "And the other half believe it, but think saying so out loud will make the crown come for them next. So they say nothing, loudly."

"And the wards?"

Maren's silence lasted long enough for Wren to map the room: the table, the stove, the crack in the plaster above the door, the way Serah's breathing went shallow. "You'd know better than me. I just carry the lamp oil up to the relay stations. But the keepers stopped singing the evening rounds two weeks ago. Said there was no point harmonizing against—" She stopped.

"Against nothing," Wren said softly. "Against an empty sky."

Maren turned toward his voice, startled. Then, carefully: "The council convenes again tomorrow at midbell. Petitioners are heard at the start. If you're going to do what I think you're going to do, Serah Vane, you should know that Speaker Halvard has already declared the border-collapse a matter for the crown's own inquiry. Which is to say, a matter for no one."

"I sat across from Halvard for eleven years," Serah said. "I know exactly how much no one weighs."

* * *

The council chamber smelled of tallow, wet stone, and old authority—the smell of every seat of power Wren had ever mapped. He counted his way in by ear: the double doors, each tall enough to admit a man on horseback; the floor flags worn into a shallow bowl by two centuries of feet; the horseshoe of benches rising in three tiers; the speaker's dais dead center, positioned so that no petitioner ever stood level with a councilor's eyes.

He had drawn this room once, years ago, from another man's description. His memory-map had placed the windows high and east-facing. Now he felt the cold light falling on the back of his neck and revised the drawing without regret. Some things you only learned by standing inside them.

Serah walked ahead of him, upright, in her old ward-keeper's coat with the sigil torn off but the shape of its absence still legible. Tamsin carried their petition—written by candlelight, signed by Serah, witnessed by Maren. Dominic hung back near the doors, hood up, a shadow among shadows. Wren heard Kess breathing beside him, quick and controlled, and felt the small pressure of her hand finding his sleeve. The crows had been left on Maren's roof. It was the hardest bargain of the morning.

"—will not be lectured on the wards by a woman stripped of her office!" That voice was round, well-fed, carrying beautifully. Halvard, presumably. "The crown's own wardens report the boundary lattice intact. Intact! We are asked to believe that every star in heaven was stolen overnight by—by what? By rumor? By a disgraced functionary and her caravan of—"

"Of witnesses," Serah said.

Her voice didn't rise. That was the thing Wren remembered about her from the old days, the thing that had made half the council hate her: she never competed on volume. She simply spoke as though the room would be quiet, and the room, resentfully, mostly was.

"Speaker Halvard cites the crown's wardens," she continued. "I would remind this council that the crown's wardens serve the crown's king. And I am here to tell you that the crown's king is dead—or worse than dead. Something wears his body. Something ate the stars on the night the borders failed, because it eats light the way we eat bread, and it is moving south faster than any army has ever moved, and when it arrives at these walls, your intact lattice will fold like wet paper, because I built half that lattice and I am telling you it was never made to hold against *that*."

A storm of noise broke. Wren stood in it and listened past it, the way he'd learned to listen past surf for the sound of a reef underneath. Under the shouting he caught fragments: *unverifiable, hysterical, the grain contracts, my constituents, treasonous slander, the girl's testimony, what girl, the deserter's girl, order, order—*

Then a new voice, dry and precise as a ledger line. "The petition claims corroboration. It lists a cartographer." Papers shuffled. "One 'Old Wren.' No surname. No guild registration. No—" a pause, delicately disgusted, "—eyes. The council is invited to weigh the credibility of a witness who cannot witness."

Wren smiled. He'd been waiting for this since the doors opened. He stepped forward until Serah's shoulder brushed his, and he pitched his voice to fill the chamber the way a tuning fork fills a room—not loud, but total.

"Councilors. Forty-one years ago I surveyed the Merrowgate seawall for the Harbor Trust. Your eastern pier has settled nine inches since then—you can feel it in the third flagstone if you walk it sober. The great chain-gate sticks at the fourth link from the top; it has stuck since the year of the twin floods, and your harbormaster has been paying a diver to chip it free every spring rather than ask the trust for repairs, because asking costs more than chipping. In the vault beneath this chamber there are seventeen sealed charter-cases. Twelve contain what your seals claim. Five do not. I drew the inventory of those five myself, and I will describe their contents to any councilor who dares come down and look."

Silence. The good kind—the kind that meant he'd hit bone somewhere.

"A blind man," Wren went on, "learns to verify what he cannot see. It is the whole of my trade. I cannot see your faces, councilors, and so I will tell you honestly: I do not know which of you are cowards and which are fools. But I know the difference between a wall that holds and a wall that has already fallen, and your wards, sirs and madams, have already fallen. You are arguing about the paint."

"Enough." Halvard again, and now the roundness had gone out of his voice, leaving something thinner. "This chamber does not take instruction from vagabonds. The petition is denied. The matter of the border failure remains referred to the crown's inquiry. This session—" 

The doors behind them boomed open.

Wren felt the draft before he heard anything else—cold harbor air rolling up the aisle, carrying salt and, beneath the salt, something else. Something wrong. A smell like the inside of a bell after it's been struck too hard, like burnt sugar and ozone. Beside him, Kess's breath hitched. Dominic's hood turned. And every crow on Maren's roof, a quarter mile away, began to scream.

At the head of the chamber, a herald's staff cracked twice against stone.

"The envoy of His Majesty King Aldric the Second," the herald announced, "arrives under seal and safe-conduct, bearing terms for the Free Cities' continued loyalty."

Terms. Not greetings. Terms.

Wren turned his face toward the doorway and listened to the envoy's footsteps cross the floor—heavy, measured, unhurried, the tread of a body that did not need to hurry—and heard beneath them, faint and rhythmic, a sound he had last heard on the night the stars went out.

A hum. Like a bell with a crack in it.

Like a bell pretending it hadn't broken.

Beside him, Serah's hand closed on his arm hard enough to leave marks, and Dominic said, very quietly, in a voice meant for no one: "We're leaving. Now."
