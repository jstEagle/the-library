## 10. An Audience of Cowards

The Council Hall of Merrowgate had been built to make petitioners feel small, and it succeeded.

Serah stood on the mosaic floor at the base of the dais steps, and the ceiling arched away above her like the underside of a wave frozen mid-break, painted with constellations that hadn't been in the sky for three months. Someone had commissioned the work a century ago, when Merrowgate's shipwrights still trusted their own charts more than the crown's. The stars overhead were gilded, arrogant, beautiful—and every one of them a lie now. She kept her eyes on the floor.

Eleven seats curved along the dais in a shallow crescent, one for each of the free cities' delegates, though only nine were occupied today. The delegate from Vess had died in autumn—old age, they said—and the seat from Callowmere had been empty for years, ever since the crown annexed its harbor under terms nobody liked to say aloud. Nine delegates, then, to hear the case that the King of Caerlum was dead and something else was wearing him.

Nine faces, and not one of them looked at her directly.

"The petition has been heard," said Delegate Marrow of Saltreach, who presided by seniority and wore his authority the way other men wore furs, as insulation against the weather of other people's opinions. He was a broad man gone soft, with rings on fingers that hadn't hauled a rope or struck a coin in thirty years. "You may step down, Keeper Vane."

"Keeper," Serah repeated. "That's generous. Your last letter to the Wardens' Conclave called me *disgraced*."

Murmurs moved through the crescent like wind through wheat. A woman two seats down—the delegate from Harrow Fen, sharp-faced, silver-streaked hair bound in merchant's knots—leaned toward her neighbor. Serah caught fragments: *the Vane girl*, *her father's reputation*, *three months we've heard nothing*.

"I said what was diplomatic," Marrow said. "Diplomacy is my office."

"Diplomacy." Serah tasted the word and found it rotten. "Let me tell you what diplomacy has purchased. On the night the wards failed, I stood on the Thornfell overlook and watched the border lights gutter out like candles pinched between wet fingers. I had warned the Conclave for two years that the binding rites were being pushed past any safe measure—that you cannot chain a living mind without leaving a door open, and doors open both ways. For that warning, I was stripped of rank and exiled. And when the sky went black over half the continent—" she let the silence carry it a moment, let them all glance upward despite themselves, at the painted stars that mocked them— "your response was to raise harbor tariffs."

Delegate Ossler of Greywater, a thin man with ink-stained fingers, cleared his throat. "The tariff question is separate from—"

"It is not separate. Nothing is separate. That is the entire point I have crossed four hundred miles of hostile country to make." Her voice rang up into the painted dark, and she saw flinches she pretended not to see. "The thing that ate our stars is feeding. You've had reports—I know you have; your own harbormasters file them. Crops failing in valleys the drought never touched. Rivers running cold in summer. Villages on the crown roads where every dog went silent in a single night. The usurper's armies move faster than horses can carry them because they are not carrying men anymore—they're carrying puppets strung on stolen light. Every day this council spends weighing trade consequences is a day it grows stronger, and it does not negotiate, because hunger does not negotiate. It only waits until you are worth eating."

Silence. Somewhere high above, a pigeon shifted on a rafter, and the small sound fell into the hall like a stone into a well.

It was the Harrow Fen delegate who spoke next, and Serah thought, absurdly, *finally*—until the woman opened her mouth.

"You speak compellingly, Keeper. But consider what you ask." She folded her hands atop the rail. "You ask nine sovereign cities to declare war on the crown of Caerlum. Not a raid, not a embargo—a war, on the strength of the testimony of a disgraced ward-keeper, a deserter cook, a blind cartographer, a smuggler of no registered name, and—" she consulted a paper before her with delicate distaste— "a child who claims to converse with crows."

"They don't claim it," Kess said.

She'd been told to stay behind the barrier with Tamsin and Wren. Of course she hadn't. She stood there in her too-big coat, chin up, crow feather tucked behind one ear, and the entire council stared down at her as if something had crawled onto the dais.

"The crows talk to *me*," Kess said. "And they've seen the army. Ask them yourself if you don't believe me."

"That will be all," Marrow said sharply.

"She's a child," the Harrow Fen delegate said, "and children say things. What you are asking us to believe, Keeper Vane, is that King Aldric—the man whose fleets broke the Corsair League, whose ward-treaties your own Conclave helped draft—is dead or possessed, and that no one in the palace has noticed. No chancellor. No guard captain. No queen's kin. You are asking us to believe that the most observed man in the world has been replaced, and the only evidence is rumor, omen, and the word of people with every reason to lie."

"The evidence is the sky," Serah said.

"The sky is an anomaly. Anomalies get investigated, not declared wars over." The woman spread her hands, reasonable, terrible. "If half of what you say is true, then yes, it is grave. But if we act on it and are wrong—if we march on Caerlum and unseat an anointed king on the testimony of exiles—we do the usurper's work for him. We shatter the one institution strong enough to resist whatever this is. Can you guarantee us, Keeper, that Aldric lives? That he could be restored? Can you guarantee anything?"

*Yes*, she almost said. *I can guarantee that if we wait, there will be no cities left to argue about it.*

But that wasn't true either, and they all knew it, and lies before a council die fast. So instead she said, quietly, "I can guarantee you that courage deferred is still courage. It keeps. Fear doesn't. Fear rots while you hold it, and right now every one of you is holding it and calling it prudence."

Ossler made a sound that might have been a laugh bitten in half. Marrow's face went the color of tallow.

"Your petition is denied," Marrow said. "Pending independent verification—which this council will pursue through proper channels—you and your companions are instructed to remain in Merrowgate, where you can be... available for questioning."

*Available.* Like cargo. Like beasts in a yard.

Serah bowed, because the form demanded it, and hated how easily her body remembered obedience even after everything.

---

They regrouped in the colonnade outside, where the sea-wind came through the arches smelling of salt and tar and the fish-market below. Wren sat on a bench with his white eyes turned toward the harbor, listening to the gulls the way other men read documents. Tamsin pressed a honey-bun into Kess's hands without comment, which was how Tamsin said *it's going to be all right* when she didn't believe it herself. Dominic leaned against a pillar, arms folded, watchful, saying nothing—but their eyes found Serah's, and held, and that was its own kind of counsel.

"Well," Tamsin said. "That went swimmingly."

"They need time," Ossler's voice said behind them, and the thin delegate from Greywater came hurrying along the colonnade, cloak pulled up against the wind, looking like a man committing treason at speed. "Don't look at me like that. I voted against you, but I'm not blind. There are four of us—maybe five—who believe you, privately. But belief isn't leverage. The harbors are mortgaged to crown lenders. Half the delegates have family in Caerlum City. Marrow's grandson serves in the king's own guard." He grimaced. "Give me a month. Let me build a coalition quietly. If you parade through the city shouting apocalypse, you'll spook them into denial forever."

"A month," Serah said.

"A month. Stay low. The Gullwrights' Quarter has rooms. Avoid the—"

He never finished.

The bells started first—all of them, every tower in Merrowgate at once, which happened only for royal arrivals or fires. Then came the sound underneath the bells: hooves. Many hooves, iron-shod, coming up the Processional Way at a pace no courteous visitor would use.

From the colonnade they could see it: a column of riders in crown colors, black and gold, moving through the city gates in perfect, terrible order. At their center rode a palanquin-bearer's litter bearing a single figure in envoy's robes, and above the column floated banners that made Wren go rigid on his bench.

"What is it?" Kess whispered.

"The king's seal," Wren said quietly, "but the stitching's wrong. I mapped that banner twenty years ago, thread for thread. Whoever sewed these never learned why the old seamstress left the fly-end loose." His hands found his walking stick. "These weren't made by the king's tailors. They were made by something that studied the king's tailors."

The column halted before the Council Hall steps. The litter descended. And the envoy who emerged was a woman Serah had hoped never to see again: Lady Corvane, the Conclave's chief inquisitor, who had signed the order stripping Serah's rank—with a personal note thanking her for identifying the weakness in her own argument.

Corvane climbed the steps unhurried, and the doors of the hall opened before she reached them, and Serah understood with sick clarity that the council had known. Had known and scheduled it, had let Serah stand up there and plead her case with the wolf already walking up the road, so that the decision would be made for them by someone with real power. Cowards didn't just fail to act. They arranged to have their failure performed for them.

"We should go," Dominic said, low. "Now, out the market side—"

"No." Serah's heart was hammering, but her feet wouldn't move. Some stubborn, ruined part of her needed to hear it said aloud. Needed to know exactly what her country thought of her now.

They heard it within minutes, because Corvane did not bother with closed doors. The herald's voice carried across the whole colonnade, amplified by some ward-trick that made it arrive inside the skull rather than the ear:

"By decree of the Crown Regency of Caerlum: the fugitive Serah Vane, formerly of the Wardens' Conclave, stands accused of high treason, consorting with beast-speakers, sedition against the anointed king, and conspiracy in the sabotage of the border wards. Evidence has come into the Regency's hands. The fugitive is to be taken alive. Rewards are offered for information regarding her associates: an unnamed smuggler; a deserter of the Third Mess Corps; the cartographer Wren of Callowmere, wanted since the annexation; and an unidentified minor child."

Kess made a small sound. Tamsin's arm came around her shoulders like a gate closing.

"*Consorting with beast-speakers*," Serah breathed. They knew. Somehow they knew about Dominic—or suspected enough to cast the net wide. Which meant somewhere, in the palace, something hungry had tasted the edges of what Dominic was.

Down on the steps, Lady Corvane paused, turned her head, and looked directly at the colonnade—directly at Serah—as if the distance were nothing, as if the crowd between them were glass. She smiled. It was a kind smile. It was the worst thing Serah had seen in months.

Then she gestured, and six riders detached from the column and walked their horses toward the colonnade arches, unhurried, certain, the way hunters move when the quarry is already dead and merely hasn't been informed.

"Now," Dominic said. "Market side. *Move.*"

---

They ran.

Through the fish-market, where Tamsin flipped a cart of eels across the lane behind them and the screams of the vendors covered their footfalls. Through the dye-works, where the air burned purple and blue and hid them from sight for eleven precious seconds. Down stair-streets cut into the cliffside, Wren's hand on Dominic's shoulder, Kess carried on Tamsin's back, the child's crows shrieking overhead in a scatter-pattern that Serah realized, with a jolt, was deliberate—drawing eyes upward, away from them.

Dominic led. Dominic always led when the map ended and instinct began, cutting through gaps Serah would have sworn weren't there, whispering to a dock cat that yowled and tripped a pursuing rider's horse at exactly the right moment—so smoothly that only Serah, watching, understood what she'd seen.

At the water-gate, an old smuggler took one look at Dominic, spat over the side of his boat, and said, "Didn't see you. Never do." And then they were out on the gray water, the city shrinking behind them, and Merrowgate's towers catching the afternoon light like gilding on a lie.

No one spoke for a long time. Kess cried silently into Tamsin's coat, and Tamsin held her and stared back at the city with an expression Serah had last seen on soldiers the night before a battle they expected to lose. Wren murmured to himself, re-mapping the coast from memory. And Dominic sat at the tiller, guiding the boat through channels only they could read, face turned into the wind so no one could see it clearly.

Serah sat at the stern and looked at the city that was supposed to be the free world's conscience, and felt something in her chest finish breaking—not loudly, not dramatically, just quietly, the way a load-bearing wall gives way.

Her whole life she had believed in institutions. The Conclave. The wards. The councils and treaties and the slow machinery of lawful civilization. She had given that machine her career, her name, her exile, and today it had sold her to the wolf at the door rather than admit the wolf existed.

"Serah." Dominic's voice, quiet under the wind. They didn't look at her, but they passed her something wrapped in cloth—a honey-bun Tamsin must have handed them, saved. "Eat. Whatever comes next, you don't face it hollow."

She took it. Her hands shook.

"Where do we go?" Tamsin asked finally. "Every city on this coast will have the decree by week's end. We're fugitives with no country."

"Not no country," Dominic said. Their voice changed—something underneath it Serah had never heard there before, hard and bright as ore. "There are roads the decrees don't reach. Under them, maybe. Old roads." They glanced at Serah, and for the first time since she'd met them, Dominic looked less like someone hiding than like someone deciding. "I know caves beneath Thornfell where no starlight ever reaches. If your wards still hum there, the way you once said..."

Serah went still. "I told you about the deep wards? When?"

"You talked in your sleep, the second night. Fever, after the river crossing." Dominic's mouth twitched, almost a smile. "You talk a great deal in your sleep. Most of it's apologies to a door."

Despite everything—despite the bells still tolling faintly across the water—Serah laughed, one broken breath of it, and found that it hurt and helped in equal measure.

"The deep wards," she said slowly, testing the shape of the thought. The old ward-lattice, pre-crown, threaded through the roots of the mountains themselves—the lattice she'd spent her career proving still lived, and been exiled for it. "If the stars are being eaten, the deep wards were built before anyone thought to eat them. They might still hold. They might even show us..." She stopped. Show them what the hunger was. How to starve it.

"Then it's decided," Tamsin said. She pulled her coat tighter around Kess. "Back into the mountains. Wonderful. I'd just gotten used to beds."

Wren chuckled, low and dry. "Every good map ends off the edge of itself," he said. "Onward."

The boat ran south along the gray coast, and behind them Merrowgate's painted stars gleamed on in the Council Hall, beautiful and useless, while ahead the sun sank toward Thornfell's black teeth—and somewhere far north, under a lightless sky, a crowned hunger waited, patient as winter, for the world to finish denying it.

Serah ate the honey-bun. It tasted of nothing and everything. And when Dominic's hand brushed hers on the tiller—briefly, deliberately, an anchor thrown—she did not pull away.

Whatever came next, they would walk into it together. Fugitives. Traitors. Family.

The stars, she swore to the darkening east, would hear about this.
