## 11. Underground, Under Stars

The cave mouth swallowed us at dusk, and for the first time in nine days I was grateful for the dark.

We had been running since Merrowgate—since the crown envoy's voice cracked like a whip through the council hall and Serah's name became a death sentence shouted in three cities at once. Fugitives now, all of us, with a price on our heads that would buy a farm in the lowlands and a comfortable old age besides. Kess had laughed when she heard the number. "I'm worth two chickens," she'd said. "That's insulting." Tamsin had laughed too, and then gone quiet the way she did when laughing was the only alternative to screaming.

So we ran. South out of Merrowgate before the gates closed, east along the river until the river turned, and then north, because Serah knew a place. *"The Underhalls,"* she'd said, not meeting anyone's eyes. *"I kept them once. Before."* Before the disgrace. Before the exile. Before the night the sky went black and proved her right in the worst possible way.

Now I stood just inside the entrance, letting my eyes adjust, and listened to the mountain the way I'd listened to it my whole life.

Thornfell was different underground. Up on the ridges, the mountain spoke in wind and stone-scrape, in the chitter of pikas and the far-off complaint of goats. Down here it spoke in water—slow, patient water, finding its way through limestone the way a rumor finds its way through a market town. And beneath the water, beneath the stone, there was something else. A hum. Low and steady, felt in the teeth more than the ears, like the mountain was a struck bell that no one had ever allowed to finish ringing.

I knew that hum. I'd felt it faintly in deep places all my life and never had a word for it, because there was no word for it, because the word had been made a crime before I was born.

*Ward-magic.* Serah's magic. The old wards of Caerlum, the ones she'd tended and the crown had let rot, and here, in the dark under the world, they were still singing.

"Dom." Serah's voice, low, behind me. She had a lantern lit—she'd stopped being careful about light the moment we passed the last farm, as if being hunted had finally freed her from the habit of hiding. "Stay close. The first galleries are safe, but there are shafts. Old Wren would know them, but—"

She stopped. We all stopped, the way we did now, a small flinch we'd all learned in the same week.

Old Wren was three days behind us. Or ahead of us. Or in a crown wagon with his hands bound, being driven back toward the capital as bait in a trap we were supposed to walk into. He'd kissed Kess's forehead at dusk outside Merrowgate, handed me his map roll like he was handing me a basket of laundry, and said, *"Mind the third turning. It lies."* Then he'd walked back toward the road with his white cane tapping, whistling an old cartographer's marching song, and the last I saw of him was his silhouette against the burning west, straight-backed as a surveyor's plumb line.

Kess hadn't spoken above a whisper since.

"I know," I said. "The third turning. It lies."

Serah's hand found my shoulder in the dark. She didn't say anything else. That was the thing about Serah Vane—she had learned, somewhere in the years of her disgrace, that silence could be a gift rather than a wound, and she gave it at exactly the right moments. It was one of perhaps four hundred reasons I was in love with her, a number I kept revising upward and telling no one, least of all her.

We went down.

* * *

The Underhalls were nothing like the smuggler caves I knew.

My caves were practical things—dry chambers, hidden entrances, the occasional cache of contraband brandy wrapped in oilcloth. These halls had been *built*, or at least *finished*, by people who believed the dark was worth adorning. The lantern found carvings in the walls: spirals and star-patterns, lines of script in a hand so old the letters had worn round as river stones. Niches held the shadows of things long removed. And everywhere, under everything, the hum.

Bramble felt it first. My mule had come down with us—there had been no question of leaving her, and no question of her allowing it—and in the third gallery she stopped, planted her feet, and turned her head in that slow deliberate way she had, ears swiveling, drinking in something I could only half-hear.

*What is it?* I asked her, silently, the way I asked everything. The words weren't words, not really; they were shapes of meaning I pushed out from behind my ribs, careful as a man passing contraband through a checkpoint.

*Old,* Bramble said back. *Old and kind. Like the stable before the stable was the stable.* Which was Bramble's way of saying something profound while also being a mule about it.

"Serah," I said aloud. "What is this place?"

She'd gone ahead with the lantern, and the light caught her face turned up toward the ceiling, toward carvings I couldn't read but she clearly could. When she answered, her voice had a strange texture, like someone speaking a language they'd sworn off years ago and finding their mouth still remembered it.

"Ward-keeper halls. There were nine of them once, one under each of the great ward-stones. This is the one beneath the Thornfell anchor. We—" her voice caught, steadied. "I trained here. Before they moved the keeping to the capital. Before the crown decided wards were better *bound into things* than *held by people*." She touched the wall, and I swear the hum deepened, just slightly, the way a cat's purr deepens under a hand. "I used to think the mountain was singing. The old keeper told me that was foolish."

"The old keeper," Tamsin said from behind us, "sounds like she never listened to a mountain in her life." She had Kess by the shoulders, steering her gently, the way she steered everyone—toward food, toward rest, toward the next small necessary thing. Tamsin had been a cook in a garrison before she'd been a deserter, and she had never once stopped being a cook; it was her way of fighting. Feed people. Feed them until they remember they're people. "Kess, love, sit down. Dom, there's a stream through that arch, I can hear it. Water first, moping later."

"I'm not moping," Kess said, in the smallest voice I'd ever heard her use.

"I know," Tamsin said. "I am. Sit with me while I do it."

Kess sat. And that—more than the carvings, more than the hum—was the moment I understood we were going to survive this. Not because we were strong or clever or right, but because Tamsin would not allow us to come apart while there was still a kettle to put on.

* * *

I went for water because I needed to be alone, and because Bramble needed it, and because the stream-arch pulled at something in me I didn't have a name for yet.

The gallery beyond the arch opened like the inside of a swallowed cathedral. The lantern-light didn't reach its ceiling. The stream ran shallow and quick over pale stone, and the walls on either side were carved floor to unseen height with stars.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. Stars by the fistful, stars in constellations I half-recognized from the few clear nights I'd ever allowed myself, stars in patterns no sky had ever held. And around each carved star, worn into the stone by centuries of touch, were small cupped hollows—handprints, I realized. Handprints. Generations of ward-keepers, pressing their palms to the dark and holding the sky in place.

I crouched by the stream and filled the skins, and that was when I felt it properly.

The hum wasn't just in the walls. It was in the *water*. Faint, but there—a thread of the same old music running through the stream like light through a fiber of glass, coming from somewhere deeper. And the thread wasn't steady. It *pulsed*. Slowly. Rhythmically.

Like breathing. Like chewing.

I put the skins down and pressed my bare hand flat against the wet stone, and let myself listen the way I listened to beasts—not with my ears, which were mediocre, but with the thing under my ribs that I'd spent thirty years pretending wasn't there.

The mountain spoke to me.

Not in words. Mountains don't have words. It spoke in *condition*, the way Bramble spoke to me about her feet or the weather or the exact quality of her grievances. And what it told me was this: *something above is drinking.*

I sat back on my heels, water soaking through my trousers, and let the understanding arrive the way weather arrives—all at once, from every direction.

The stars hadn't gone out.

I'd assumed it, everyone had assumed it—some catastrophe, some great snuffing, the heavens a burned field. Serah had assumed the wards had failed and the stars had been lost with them, and she carried that failure like a stone in her chest. But the mountain was telling me something else. The mountain, which felt everything that happened on it and in it and above it through the roots of its stone, was telling me that on the night the sky went black, it had felt not an ending but a *feeding*. A vast, slow, patient drawing-upward, like sap through a tree, like blood through a wound. Something had put its mouth to the sky and begun to drink.

The stars weren't dead. They were being *eaten*.

And if they were being eaten, they could be—my heart stumbled on the thought and refused to finish it, so I finished it anyway, alone in the dark with wet hands—*they could be given back.*

I stayed there a long time. Long enough for Bramble to come find me and stand with her warm flank against my shoulder, which was her way of saying *I know, and I'm not leaving, and also I would like some of that water.* Long enough for the pulse in the stone to slow and steady, as if the mountain had said its piece and was content to be heard.

Then I carried the water back, and I told them.

* * *

They didn't believe me at first. I don't blame them. I was a smuggler telling a disgraced ward-keeper that the apocalypse was reversible, in a cave, while we were all fugitives with a dead man's map and a captured friend. Hope was a dangerous cargo. I'd have checked it for knives too.

"Say it again," Serah said. "Slowly."

"The stars aren't destroyed." We were in the third gallery now, around Tamsin's small cookfire, which she'd built in a carved firepit that had clearly been waiting for her for two hundred years, as if the hall had known a cook was coming. "They're being consumed. Drawn up. The mountain felt it happen—it's stone, it doesn't sleep, it felt the pull the way you feel a tooth being pulled. Slow. Continuous. It's still happening." I looked at the fire, because looking at Serah while I said the next part was more than I could manage. "The thing wearing the king isn't just using the starlight. It's *eating* it. That's why the army moves the way it does. That's why the soldiers—" I stopped. I'd scouted them once, through a raven's eyes, and I still dreamed about the way they moved, all of them slightly out of rhythm, like puppets whose puppeteer was chewing. "It's not hoarding power. It's *hungry*. And hunger can be interrupted."

Silence. Kess had her knees pulled up to her chin, her crow—her crows had followed us as far as the foothills and one of them, the ragged one she called Bishop, had actually come underground, which should have been impossible and wasn't—perched on her knee with his head cocked at me.

"Dom," Serah said carefully, "how do you know what the mountain felt?"

And there it was. The question I'd been circling for eleven days, the one she'd been not-asking since the night she'd watched me murmur a wounded hawk out of panic on a mountainside and then looked at me with an expression I still couldn't fully read. She knew. I knew she knew. We had built a whole careful architecture of not-saying around it, and now, in the singing dark, the architecture groaned.

I could have deflected. I was good at deflecting; I'd had a lifetime of practice. *The stone told me. Smugglers read stone.* But Wren had given up his freedom without ceremony, and Tamsin was feeding grief to a child one bowl at a time, and Serah had held the truth of her own failure for years rather than let it be twisted into someone else's lie, and I was so tired, down in the dark under the world, of being nobody.

"The mountain didn't tell me," I said. "The mountain doesn't talk. But everything else does, if you listen. The stream. The birds. Bramble." Bramble, dozing by the fire, opened one eye at the sound of her name with the expression of a creature being slandered. "The wolves on the high passes. The ravens over the crown roads. I hear them. I speak to them. I have since I was six years old, and I have hidden it since I was nine, because my sister had the same gift and didn't hide it, and they bound her into a siege-beast when she was fourteen and she lasted three years before her mind—" My voice did something I hadn't authorized. I let it. "They called it treason. The gift. *Compassion for beasts*, written down as a crime. So I made myself small. I made myself a smuggler, a nobody, a man who is beneath notice. And then the sky went out, and a woman fell bleeding into my camp, and I have been unmaking thirty years of hiding ever since, one day at a time, and I am apparently not finished, because I just told you all of that in front of a fire in a hole in the ground."

The fire popped. Bishop the crow made a sound that was almost a laugh.

Kess spoke first. "Your sister's name," she said. "You never said her name."

"Ada," I said. It had been years since I'd said it aloud. It tasted like a star coming back.

"Ada," Kess repeated, gravely, the way children consecrate things. "When the king's back, we'll say it where everyone hears it. That's what you do with names. Bishop told me."

"Did he," I managed.

"He says you're the loudest quiet person he's ever met. All the wild things say that. They've known about you for years, Dom. You're famous. In the trees." She said it with the absolute sincerity of a nine-year-old delivering terrible news, and Tamsin laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her—and the room broke open, just slightly, just enough to breathe in.

Serah hadn't laughed. She was looking at me across the fire with the expression from the mountainside, the one I couldn't read, and now, in the steadier light, I could. It wasn't pity. It wasn't the careful face people put on around a dangerous gift. It was the look of someone watching a locked door open from the inside.

"The wards," she said quietly. "You felt the ward-hum in the water. Without training. Without the sight." She shook her head slowly. "Dom, the old keepers used to say beast-speakers were the first ward-keepers. That the gift and the keeping were one thing, before the crown split them and criminalized half of what it needed. I thought that was legend."

"Most of your order's legends are probably just things the crown made illegal," Tamsin said, ladling out stew. "Eat. All of you. Revelation keeps better on a full stomach."

We ate. And as we ate, the plan assembled itself out of the firelight, the way plans do when everyone in the room has been thinking the same forbidden thing and one person finally says it.

"The binding-stone under the palace," Serah said. "Wren's map. If the thing in the king is feeding on the stars, the stone is its mouth—the first binding-stone, the oldest channel, the thing it used to reach the sky in the first place. We don't need to kill it. You can't kill a hunger by stabbing it. We need to *sever the feeding*. Close its mouth. And the only people who ever knew how to work a binding-stone were—" she stopped, and looked at me, and finished it with a small, wondering, terrified smile, "—ward-keepers. And the first ward-keepers."

"Me," I said.

"You," she said. "With me holding the wards open. If we can reach the stone. If we can get into the capital at all. If Wren's route—"

"Wren's route is good," I said. "Wren's route is always good. The third turning lies, and he told us so, and we'll mind it." I looked around the fire: Tamsin with her ladle like a scepter, Kess with a crow on her knee and war in her eyes, Serah lit orange and gold with the old carvings at her back, and Bramble, asleep, trusting, her breath keeping time with the mountain's hum. A smuggler, a disgraced keeper, a deserter, a child, a mule, and a crow. The free cities had nine councils and three armies and hadn't managed a single plan. We had a stew pot and a dead man's map.

It would have to do. It was, in fact, exactly enough.

"One thing first," I said, and got up, and crossed to the wall of carved stars, and put my hand into one of the old cupped hollows, where some keeper's palm had rested a thousand years ago.

The hum rose to meet me. Not the feeding-pulse from the stream—this was the other music, the *kind* music, the wards themselves, still alive down here in the dark where the crown's long arm had never reached. It ran up my arm and settled behind my ribs, next to the place where the beast-speech lived, and the two things recognized each other like siblings at a border crossing.

And far overhead, through a mile of stone, through the hungry dark where the stars used to be, I felt the faint, vast, patient pull of the thing that was drinking the sky—and felt, beneath it, the stars themselves. Dimmed. Drained. *Held.*

Not gone. Held. The way a prisoner is held. The way a voice is held. The way I had held myself for thirty years.

"We can get them back," I said to the fire, to the carvings, to her. "Serah. Whatever it costs. We can get them back."

Serah crossed the room and put her hand in the hollow beside mine, and the mountain sang.

* * *

Later, when the others slept, I lay awake in the ward-keeper hall under a ceiling of carved stars and listened to the two musics—the mountain's kind hum, and the far-off rhythm of the feeding—and made a promise to both of them, and to a girl named Ada, and to a woman asleep across the fire whose hand had fit that hollow like it had been carved for her.

Then Bishop dropped down through some impossible crack in the stone, landed on my chest, and looked at me with one eye, and said, in the flat gray grammar of crows, the words I'd been dreading and waiting for since Merrowgate:

*Road. Wagons. Many. The old one walks. The old one sings. They take him to the stone.*

Wren was being brought to the capital.

Which meant, if the crown was pulling its prisoner toward the binding-stone, they were guarding it more heavily than ever—or they were setting the trap they'd always meant to set, with a blind old man as the cheese.

Either way, we had our answer, and no time at all.

I woke the others. Tamsin put the kettle on.
