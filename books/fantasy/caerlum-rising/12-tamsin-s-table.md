## 12. Tamsin's Table

The safehouse was a smuggler's barn three days' walk south of the ward-caves, all sagging beams and hay that smelled of mouse and old rain, and Tamsin loved it the way you love a thing that might not last.

She'd been in kitchens her whole life—first her mother's, then the garrison mess at Caerlum Ford, then the crown camp at Thornfell's edge, where she'd fed the men who chained the beasts and finally couldn't do it anymore. She knew what food was for. It wasn't just for the belly. It was for the hour after the belly, when people sat back and their shoulders came down from around their ears and they started saying true things. Kitchens were where armies fell apart and families got built, if you did it right.

And these people needed building. Badly.

She'd watched them come into the barn that evening like survivors of a shipwreck: Dominic first, moving quiet as spilled water, checking the corners before letting anyone else in; then Serah with her ward-keeper's staff worn down to a walking stick, scanning the roofline out of habit even though the stars were still gone and there was nothing up there to see her; then Old Wren tapping his cane along the wall until he found the doorframe and pronounced it "adequate" in the tone of a man reviewing a palace; and last Kess, nine years old and filthy to the elbows, carrying a crow on each shoulder like epaulettes.

Three days underground in caves that hummed with dead magic. Three days of hardtack and creek water and Serah's increasingly unhinged theories about binding-stones. Tamsin had counted the meals they owed themselves and found the debt enormous.

So while the others argued about watch rotations, she went through the barn stores with a professional's eye. The smuggler who owned this cache had taste, or had once robbed someone who did: a crock of rendered goose fat gone slightly off at the edges but salvageable, a sack of barley, dried apples hard as roof slates, a heel of salt pork, onions sprouting green hope from their tops, and—a miracle, an actual gift from whatever saints watched over deserters—a jar of honey sealed in wax, unspoiled.

Tamsin stood in the lantern light holding the honey and felt something in her chest go loose and warm. You could feed people gruel and keep them alive. This was different. Tonight she was going to feed them like they deserved to exist.

* * *

Cooking in a fugitive's barn was its own kind of craft. No smoke worth seeing, so she banked the fire low in the old stone hearth and let the barley take its time. She fried the salt pork first because fat is memory—it filled the whole barn with a smell so honest it made Kess stop mid-sentence and drift toward the hearth like a compass needle finding north.

"What is that," the girl said. Not a question. A religious experience.

"Pork," Tamsin said. "Sit down before you fall into my fire."

"It smells like—" Kess frowned, hunting for the word. Her crows shifted on the fence rail outside, muttering. "One of my crows says *feast*. He's never used that word before. He mostly says *bread*, *shiny*, and a bad word I taught him by accident."

"That bird and I are going to get along."

She cooked the way her mother had taught her, which was the way soldiers' mothers teach: everything stretched, nothing wasted, flavor fought for like ground. Barley cooked in the pork fat with onions browned dark at the edges. Dried apples simmered soft with a spoonful of the honey and a pinch of whatever passed for cinnamon in this forgotten corner of the world—which was actually dried yarrow, but nobody would know unless they were rude. She toasted the last of a flat loaf on the hearthstone and rubbed it with garlic she'd found growing wild along the creek, half-wild and fierce.

By the time she called them, the barn smelled like a home that somebody had bothered to make.

Dominic came first—of course they did, they always came first when there was work or danger or food—and stopped just inside the door and breathed in, slow, and something crossed their face too quick to name. Serah came second, drawn despite herself, and actually blinked at the table Tamsin had dragged to the middle of the floor and scrubbed with creek water and sand.

"You made this," Serah said. "From what?"

"From what was here. That's the entire job description." Tamsin ladled barley into tin bowls, and didn't skimp, and watched every one of them flinch slightly at being given more than their share. Deserters, exiles, smugglers—all of them trained to eat fast and expect the bowl to be taken away. Well. Not tonight.

Wren found his place by touch and sat with the straight-backed dignity of a man at high table. "I haven't smelled garlic since Merrowgate burned the market quarter," he said. "Or rather, I haven't smelled anything since, but I remember it vividly, and this is a creditable impersonation."

"That's the nicest thing anyone's said about my cooking in six years."

"The bar was low?"

"The last person who ate my cooking was a crown sergeant who said it reminded him of his mother and then cried into his bowl and told me which officers were taking bribes. So yes."

Kess had already eaten half of hers, scalding-hot, without pausing for breath. Tamsin put a hand gently on top of the girl's bowl. "It'll still be there in a minute. It's not running anywhere."

"Everything runs somewhere," Kess said, mouth full. "That's what the crows say. Everything runs, except crows."

"Eat slower anyway."

* * *

The meal went down and the hour after the meal arrived, exactly as Tamsin knew it would. The lantern burned low. Nobody wanted to move. And the true things started coming up out of people like ground water finding its level.

It started with Wren, because Wren was old enough to have stopped guarding himself. He held his bowl in both hands as if warming them, though the barn was mild, and said, "My daughter liked honey."

Nobody spoke. They'd all learned that Wren's silences had doors in them, and if you waited, sometimes he opened one.

"She kept bees at our house outside Vaelmoor, before the crown requisitioned the valley for the war-engines. Requisitioned—that's the word they used. Like the bees were furniture. She wouldn't leave them. Said a hive can't be marched anywhere, it dies of grief within the season." His thumb moved over the rim of the bowl. "They took her for obstructing a military operation. I don't know where they took her. I've spent eleven years mapping roads I'll never walk, looking for the one that leads to wherever they keep people like her."

Tamsin set the honey jar down in front of him without a word. Wren found it by smell, smiled the smallest smile she'd ever seen on a human face, and dipped two fingers in like a boy stealing from a pantry.

"I tell you this," he said, "because tonight it seems important that you all know we are not here by accident. Every one of us lost something specific. The crown keeps excellent records of what it takes. We should keep ours too."

Serah was staring at the fire. Tamsin had learned to read the ward-keeper's face by now—the officer's mask, the exile's bitterness, and underneath both, the young woman who had warned them all and been stripped of rank for it. Tonight the mask was off.

"When the wards failed," Serah said quietly, "the first thing I thought wasn't *I told them so.* It was—I thought of my training master, Master Ilvane. Dead four years now. She taught me the wards weren't walls. She said they were promises. Every keeper who ever laid stone into the border was making a promise to everyone behind it, and the promise only holds if somebody keeps tending it." Her jaw tightened. "They stopped tending. They pulled keepers off the borders to run the binding yards, because a bound beast needs a handler more than a village needs a promise. And when the sky went black I stood on a hill and thought: somewhere, Ilvane's ghost is watching me fail to fix the thing I tried to warn them about, and I could not decide if she'd be proud of me or ashamed."

"Proud," Dominic said.

Everyone looked at them. Dominic almost never spoke first, and almost never spoke plainly, and the two at once landed in the silence like a dropped plate.

"They'd be proud," Dominic repeated, quieter, and studied their bowl. "You were right. Being right costs. Most people never pay it. Doesn't mean they don't know who did."

Serah looked at Dominic for a long moment, and Tamsin saw something pass between them—not softness exactly, more like two blades being shown to each other hilt-first—and then Serah nodded once and looked back at the fire, and that was that.

Which left, Tamsin realized with a lurch, Kess, who had gone very quiet, and quiet was wrong on that child the way silence is wrong on a river.

"Kess," Tamsin said. "You don't have to share anything. But you're allowed to."

The girl pushed apple slices around her bowl. One of the crows had come inside at some point—it perched on the beam above her head, and she glanced up at it as if listening.

"Mama didn't die," Kess said finally. "Everybody thinks she died when the riders took me. But the crows say she's alive. Crows don't lie about people, only about cheese." She looked up, chin jutting, braced for laughter or pity. "They say she walks past the place they took me from every market day. She looks at the road. So I'm going back there. When this is done. Whatever 'this' is."

No one laughed. No one pitied, either. Dominic reached across the table and turned Kess's bowl so the best apple slice faced her, which was such a small thing and such a precisely right thing that Tamsin had to look at the fire for a moment to get her eyes under control.

"Then that's settled," Serah said. "When this is done, we deliver one child to one mother. Add it to the ledger."

"And I want it noted," Wren said, "that I intend to collect my own entry. Vaelmoor, east gate, the house with the bee-yard. Someone draw it for me someday and I will walk it."

"We'll walk it together," Tamsin heard herself say. "All of us. I'll cook."

* * *

Later—bowls scraped, Kess asleep against Serah's shoulder in the manner of children everywhere who insist they aren't tired and then drop like cut wheat—Tamsin stayed at the hearth banking the coals, and Dominic stayed too, sitting on the far side of the fire with Bramble's saddle blanket across their knees, mending a strap.

"You didn't eat much," Tamsin said. "Don't think I didn't notice. Cooks notice."

"I ate."

"You ate like a spy eats. Watching the exits." Tamsin poked the coals. "In the camps, the ones who ate like that were the ones about to do something stupid and brave. Usually alone."

Dominic's needle paused. Firelight caught the planes of their face, the scar through one eyebrow Tamsin had never asked about. For a long moment she thought they wouldn't answer at all.

"The army moves faster than it should," Dominic said at last. "Kess's birds have been saying it for days. Armies don't move like that unless every soldier is... less than a soldier. More than a soldier. Something else." They tied off the stitch and bit the thread. "Someone should know how far it is. How fast it's really coming. What's driving it."

"Someone meaning you."

"Someone meaning me."

Tamsin looked at them—at the smuggler who'd survived Thornfell by being no one, whose whole life was a held breath—and felt the kitchen-knowledge rise in her, the oldest thing she knew: that you cannot force a person to sit at a table, you can only make sure the seat is warm when they choose it.

"Well," she said. "Before you do anything stupid and brave, you'll eat a proper breakfast. That's not a suggestion, that's a threat. And if you're going to talk to the wolves about it—" she kept her voice level, easy, the way you speak around something shy, "—you won't be doing it alone either. Whatever you decide. That's how tables work."

Dominic stared at her. Then, slowly, they almost smiled—an expression clearly out of practice, handled like borrowed tools.

"Tamsin," they said, "what did you put in that honey?"

"Nothing. Love and yarrow." She stood, joints cracking, and jerked her chin toward the dark mouth of the road beyond the barn. "Go sleep. All of you. Because tomorrow, I'm thinking, we stop hiding in barns—and whatever comes next, nobody fights it hungry."

Outside, far off in the black starless hills, a wolf began to howl—and then, impossibly, a second voice answered it from the direction of the barn itself: low, threaded, almost like words.

Dominic froze with the strap half-mended.

Tamsin pretended not to hear, and started counting spoons for morning.
