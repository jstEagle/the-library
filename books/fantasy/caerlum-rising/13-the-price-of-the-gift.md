## 13. The Price of the Gift

The fire had burned down to a red memory by the time Serah came to sit beside him.

Dominic heard her before he saw her—of course he did; the mountain had taught him to read footsteps the way other men read faces—but he didn't turn. He kept his hands busy with the whetstone, dragging it along the skinning knife in long, even strokes, though the blade had been sharp enough to shave with since sundown. It wasn't about the knife. It was about having somewhere to put his eyes.

Behind them, the others slept. Tamsin snored with the contentment of a woman who'd fed nine people until they begged for mercy. Old Wren sat propped against a boulder, sightless eyes closed, but Dominic knew better than to believe the old man slept—he listened the way roots drink, slow and constant and from everywhere. Kess was a small shape curled against Bramble's flank, her breath rising and falling in time with the mule's, one hand fisted in his mane even now. The crows roosted above in a dead pine, three dark commas against the faint gray of cloud.

No stars. Still no stars. Eleven nights now, and Dominic still caught himself looking up, still flinched at the nakedness of that sky. It felt like standing in a room where the walls had been taken away.

"You're sharpening that knife wrong," Serah said quietly.

"It's sharp."

"I know. That's what makes it wrong." She settled onto the stone beside him, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, not quite. She'd learned that distance too. They'd been learning each other's distances for days now, two people circling a thing neither would name. "You only do that when your head's loud."

"My head's fine."

"Your head's been fine for eleven nights, and every night you sharpen that knife like it insulted you." She looked out at the valley below—their camp perched high on a shoulder of Thornfell's southern reach, the land dropping away into black forest, and beyond it, far beyond it, a smudge of wrongness on the horizon where the usurper's host lay camped. Even from here, even in the dark, you could feel it. Like a toothache in the world. "Dominic."

He set down the whetstone. That was the thing about Serah Vane—she used your name like a key, and she knew exactly which doors it opened.

"Ask me," he said.

She turned. In the ember-light her face was all planes and shadows, the scar through her left eyebrow catching orange. The crown had carved that scar when they stripped her rank; the story of it had come out over Tamsin's cooking, told flat and fast, the way you tell things you've decided not to bleed over. *I said the wards were failing. I said it for six years. They gave me a scar and a road instead of thanks.*

"Scout them," Serah said. "The host. Tomorrow we hit the river crossing, and after that there's no going around—we go through the edge of their picket lines or we don't reach the capital at all. Wren's map says the old shepherd tunnels come up past the eastern farms, but the map is forty years old and Wren will tell you himself that maps lie about the present tense. What I need is the present tense. What I need—" and here her voice did something rare, it hesitated, "—is birds. Wolves. Anything with eyes out there that isn't wearing a crown's collar."

"You're asking me to speak for them."

"I'm asking you to ask them. There's a difference, and I think you know it better than anyone alive." Her hands were folded in her lap, deliberate, controlled—a ward-keeper's hands, hands trained to hold power carefully. "And I'm asking knowing what it costs you to be asked. So if the answer is no, it's no, and I'll walk us through those lines blind and we'll take our chances. But I wanted you to hear it from me straight, because everything else between us has been sideways, and I'm tired of it."

Dominic looked at the knife. Looked at the sky. Somewhere down in the valley a fox screamed, thin and brief, and his chest tightened with the old reflex—the urge to answer, to call back, *what's wrong, little sister, what's hunting you*—and he strangled it the way he'd strangled it ten thousand times before.

"You want to know why I hide it," he said.

"I've guessed some of it."

"Guessing isn't the same as hearing it." He picked up the whetstone again just to have the weight in his hand, then made himself set it down. If he was going to do this, he'd do it empty-handed. "All right. Straight, then. Since you asked straight."

* * *

"My sister's name was Adaeze."

The name came out rusty. He hadn't said it aloud in years—not to anyone, not even alone, not even in prayer, if what he did under his breath at night counted as prayer. Saying it now felt like opening a door onto a cold room.

"She was four years younger than me and twice as brave, which is a bad combination in a child with our gift. I learned early to keep my mouth shut around animals where anyone could see. Adaeze never learned. Wouldn't learn. Said it was like being ashamed of laughing." He almost smiled. "She talked to everything. Sparrows. Goats. A half-blind dog in our village that followed her like a shadow. When the binding writs came to the Thornfell villages—the first ones, when the crown was still pretending it was conscription and not slavery—the ward-officers did the testing. Blood-prick and command-word, all children tested. She answered a sparrow in front of the wrong officer's daughter."

Serah had gone very still beside him.

"They took her to Caerlum," Dominic went on, and his voice stayed level because he'd spent twenty years making it stay level, and that levelness was its own kind of wound. "Public trial. They made it a spectacle—*the beast-witch of Thornfell*, that's what the criers called her, a girl of thirteen. My parents were made to watch. I was made to watch. That was the point, you understand. The execution wasn't punishment. Punishment is for what someone's done. This was instruction. *This is what your compassion buys.*"

The fire shifted. A knot popped and collapsed into glow.

"They bound her first," he said. "Did you know that? They don't tell you that part. They bound her—put a war-collar on a child, wired her mind into a draft-beast harness so her gift could be *used*, one last time, one final service to the crown—and then they executed her while she was still in it. While the animal she was linked to was still feeling through her senses. They killed them together. And the officers wrote in the ledger afterward that the subject demonstrated unusual resonance with the stock and recommended further study of bloodlines." He heard his own voice crack on the last word and hated it, hated that twenty years hadn't been enough, hated that some rooms in a person never close no matter how many doors you build over them. "Further study of bloodlines. Meaning me. Meaning any child like us. My parents sent me south within the month. I changed my name. I became nobody, and nobody has kept me alive ever since."

Silence held the camp. Above them, the crows murmured in their sleep, soft throat-sounds, dreaming.

"Dominic," Serah said finally, and her voice was rough in a way he'd never heard it. "Adaeze—"

"Don't." He held up a hand, not unkindly. "If you say you're sorry, I'll have to say thank you, and then this becomes a thing we've done politely to each other, and I can't—I can't do polite about her. Polite is how she got erased. Everyone was polite."

"Then what do you want me to say?"

He thought about it honestly, turning the question over, and found that the answer surprised him.

"Say what you said before. Ask me again. Ask me properly."

Serah turned to face him fully. She took a breath, and he watched her assemble the words with a care she gave to nothing else—not tactics, not survival, not even the wards she'd once sworn her life to keeping.

"Dominic Okafor," she said, "will you speak to the beasts for us? Not for me. Not for the rebellion, whatever we are. For Kess, who talks to crows in the open because nobody's taught her yet to be afraid. For Tamsin, who deserted rather than cook for men who guard the binding yards. For Wren, who drew roads from memory for people he'll probably never see again. For the king who might still be screaming inside that thing wearing him. And for yourself—for the part of you that's been holding its breath for twenty years." Her jaw tightened. "I know what it costs. I watched them take my rank and mark my face for saying true things, and that was a paper cut next to what they did to you. So I won't pretend it's small. But I am asking. On my knees inside, if that helps, which it doesn't, because I'm bad at kneeling."

Despite everything—despite the cold room he'd opened and couldn't shut—a laugh escaped him, short and cracked and real.

"You're terrible at kneeling."

"The worst. It was noted in my evaluations."

He looked at her a long moment. Then he stood, and offered her his hand, and pulled her up, and walked with her to the edge of the overlook where the whole black valley spread below them and the wrongness of the enemy camp pulsed on the horizon like an infected wound.

"If I do this," he said, "it changes things. Not just the danger—you understand the danger. But between us. Once I speak out loud, once these people see, I stop being nobody. Nobody survives. Somebody gets hunted."

"Good," Serah said.

He glanced at her. "Good?"

"Somebody should be worth hunting. We're building a rebellion out of people the crown threw away—deserters, exiles, smugglers, children, blind old cartographers. Every single one of us is somebody the world said shouldn't exist. You've been hiding among the dead your whole life, Dominic. Time you joined the living." She said it plainly, without poetry, which somehow made it worse. Better. Worse-better. He was losing the ability to tell the difference around this woman.

He closed his eyes. Reached out the way he hadn't reached out in daylight, in company, in twenty years.

Thornfell answered immediately. It always did; the mountains were thick with listening things. Ravens on the night wind. Foxes denned in the scree. A wolf-pack ranging the far ridge, lean winter-hungry, moving south. And closer than anything, warm and drowsing and loyal as gravity: Bramble, half-asleep on his feet behind him, one ear swiveled toward his human.

*I'm here,* Dominic said, and it was like unlocking a door sealed so long the hinges screamed. *All of me. No more hiding.*

Bramble's answer came slow and deep as bedrock: *Knew you'd get there eventually. Stubborn.*

A raven wheeled overhead, drawn by the open door of him, and he sent it south with a question shaped like a whisper: *What do you see in the hungry camp?*

The raven's mind touched his—and recoiled, shrieking static and terror, images tumbling through broken and bleeding: soldiers standing too still, too straight, ranks of them motionless in the dark like cut stones, and threaded through the camp a pale light with no source, starlight, stolen starlight, strung between poles and through tents and *into the soldiers themselves*, silver threads sewn through flesh—

The raven fled back to him shaking. Dominic staggered, caught himself on Serah's arm, and found her gripping him hard, her face white in the dark.

"What did you see?"

"Not tonight." His voice came out hoarse. "Tomorrow, with everyone. Because if I'm done being nobody—" He looked at her, at this woman who'd asked him properly, and felt the terrifying vertigo of a man stepping off a cliff he'd been standing on the edge of his entire life. "Then everyone hears it at once. Including the ugly parts."

Serah nodded slowly. "Tomorrow, then."

But as they turned back toward the fire, Dominic paused. One more thing, and it wouldn't keep.

"There's something else you should know," he said. "About why I'm telling you all this, why it's you. Twenty years I've told no one. Not the free cities' priests, not smuggler confidants, no one. Tonight I opened every door I have." He met her eyes, and let her see all the way down. "It's not just trust, Serah. Trust I could give to Wren, to Tamsin. With you it's—it's more than the secret. It's you. It's been you since the river crossing, maybe since the wounded stranger bleeding in my shelter, and I needed you to know that before tomorrow puts a price on both our heads. Whatever happens next. However this ends."

For once, Serah Vane—who could argue councils into retreat and stare down crown envoys without blinking—had nothing to say. She just looked at him, ember-lit and wide-eyed, her hand still gripping his arm where he'd staggered.

Above them, the starless sky pressed down like a lid.

And far to the south, unseen, unheard, the raven's terror still echoed in Dominic's bones—because in the last fractured image before it fled, threaded among all that stolen silver light, it had seen something that made no sense at all.

It had seen a face.

Young. Thirteen years old. Bound in a war-collar, eyes open and screaming without sound.

His sister's face, standing in the heart of the usurper's camp.
