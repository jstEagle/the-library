## 14. Scouting the Hungry Host

The wolves found Dominic before dawn, which was either a very good sign or a very bad one.

They came down out of the high pines in a loose crescent, five of them, moving the way wolves do when they've decided not to hunt — heads low, tails level, all that coiled violence folded politely away. The leader stopped at the edge of the firelight and sat, and looked at Dominic with yellow eyes that held no fear at all.

"They know what you are," Serah had said, three nights ago, after the confession that had cost Dominic more than any border crossing ever had. "The beasts always know. It's why they come to you."

Dominic had spent twenty years making sure they didn't come to them. Now here were five wolves sitting in the snow like petitioners, and Kess watching from her bedroll with her mouth open, and Tamsin pretending very hard to still be asleep, and Old Wren facing the darkness with his blind eyes turned toward the trees as if he could see everything.

"Go on," Kess whispered. "They want to talk to you."

*They don't talk,* Dominic wanted to say. *It's not words. It's—* But there was no word for it in any tongue, human or otherwise. It was pressure and scent and image, the grammar of hunger and territory and the long memory of winter. Speaking it back was less like talking and more like opening a door in your own chest and letting the world walk through.

Serah stood at the fire's edge, arms crossed against the cold, waiting. She had asked. That was the whole weight of it. She had asked directly, and Dominic had given her the truth about their brother, about the executioner's block, about why a gift became a grave — and then, because she had asked, because she had not flinched, Dominic had said yes.

So now: yes.

Dominic knelt in the snow and opened the door.

* * *

It was like falling into cold water that breathed.

The lead wolf — she was old, gray-muzzled, a hunter with more seasons behind her than ahead — took Dominic's reaching mind the way she'd take a scent: carefully, completely. And then the world tilted sideways and became the forest floor, all smell and sound, the snow a map of every living thing that had crossed it since moonset.

*South,* Dominic shaped, in the wordless way. *South, where the roads run. There is a thing that walks like men.*

The wolf's answer was a ripple of unease so strong it made Dominic's actual body shiver by the fire. She knew. Her pack had been avoiding the south for a turning of the moon. Something down there ate the wildness out of the air. Something down there made even the wind feel leashed.

*Show me,* Dominic said.

And she did.

The vision poured in — not sight exactly, but wolf-sense translated into something a human mind could hold. They ran the ridge lines at speed, five bodies moving as one thought, Dominic riding inside the old she-wolf's skull like a passenger in their own flesh. The trees thinned. The land dropped toward the river valley where the crown road bent east toward Caerlum, and there—

Dominic would remember this for the rest of their life: the moment they saw the host.

It filled the valley. Not an army the way armies were drawn on Wren's maps, neat blocks and supply lines. This was a dark flood, ten thousand men at least, camped in ranks so precise they might have been planted. And above them, above the whole valley—

The sky was wrong.

Everyone knew the sky was wrong; the stars had been gone for weeks. But from inside a wolf's senses, from a mind that read the heavens the way humans read faces, Dominic could feel *how* wrong. The light that should have fallen from above was falling anyway — thin threads of silver, fine as spider silk, descending over the valley like rain frozen mid-fall. And each thread ended in a man.

Ten thousand threads. Ten thousand soldiers standing slack-jawed in the dark, staring up at nothing, while stolen starlight ran into them through the crown of the head like wine into a cup.

*Oh,* Dominic thought, with the wolf's calm and their own rising horror braided together. *Oh, gods. They're not just led. They're strung.*

* * *

Coming back was worse than going under. Dominic surfaced gasping, hands in the snow, the fire roaring too loud and too bright, Kess gripping their sleeve, saying their name, saying it again.

"I'm here." Their voice came out scraped. "I'm here."

"You were gone," Kess said. "Your eyes went all — all silvery, like the thread-stuff, and you weren't breathing right, and Bramble kept pulling at your coat—"

Bramble stood over them, ears flat, tugging at their collar with his teeth, gentle as he'd ever been with a lamb. Dominic put a hand on his neck and felt their own heartbeat slow to match his.

"Tell us," Serah said quietly.

Dominic told them. The valley. The numbers — Wren exhaled sharply at that, murmuring calculations. The precision, the silence, the camps without cook-fires or latrine trenches or dice or song. An army with no humanity left in its rhythm.

"And the threads," Dominic said. "Starlight. It comes down out of the empty sky and goes into them. Through the head. Like — like puppet strings, but made of the stars themselves. That's where the stars went. He didn't blot them out. He's *using* them."

Silence around the fire. Tamsin had gone pale in a way that had nothing to do with cold; she'd walked away from the crown army eight months ago, and whatever she was remembering, it was written on her face in old scars.

"That explains the marching," she said at last, hollow. "I told you they moved too fast. I told you they didn't stop to eat. I thought — I told myself they had some new supply wizardry. You don't need supply," she added bitterly, "if your men don't eat. If they don't sleep. If they're just... meat on a string."

"The binding-stone," Serah said slowly. "Wren said the palace sits on the first binding-stone. If the stone can bind a beast's mind, and the king is wearing the thing that hungers—" She stopped. Started again. "It's eating starlight and pouring it into men. Binding them the way the crown binds hawks and horses. Except a man bound doesn't pull a cart. A man bound holds a spear. Marches forever. Dies on command."

"It's scaling up the law," Dominic said, and heard their own voice shake with fury. "That's all the binding law ever was, wasn't it? A small version of this. Practice. They outlawed the gift because free-speaking beasts couldn't be strung, and they chained the ones they caught, and now the thing in the king has decided ten thousand chains aren't enough. It wants everyone. It wants the whole kingdom on strings."

Kess had gone very quiet, hugging her knees. One of her crows — Marrow, the bold one with the torn wing — sat on her shoulder, feathers fluffed, and for once even the crows had nothing clever to say.

* * *

But Dominic hadn't finished scouting.

Because the wolves had shown them the host, but the host was only half the truth, and Dominic needed the other half before they could look Serah in the eye and say *yes, I'll do it, I'll go into that throne room.* So at dusk the next day, they climbed the tallest pine on the ridge and spoke — really spoke, wide-open, door off its hinges — to the ravens.

Ravens were harder than wolves. Wolves thought in packs; ravens thought in *selves*, sharp and suspicious, each one a merchant driving a bargain. But a raven's price was simple: shine and secrets. Dominic paid in both.

Three birds went south at last light, and Dominic rode their eyes from the ridge, cold to the bone, teeth chattering in a body they barely inhabited.

The ravens flew low over the vanguard of the hungry host, and this close, the threads resolved into horror. Each soldier's string of light ran upward and *southward*, converging, gathering, streaming toward Caerlum like rivers running backward into a sea. Above the capital, where the stars used to be densest — the Crown constellation, the Seven Sisters, all the old bright names — there was a darkness. Not emptiness. A darkness with *appetite* in it, a hole in the heaven that pulled.

And the ravens, bless their black hearts, dove closer than Dominic asked, because curiosity was also their price, and one of them skimmed the column near enough to taste the air above a soldier's head.

What came back nearly broke Dominic's grip on the connection entirely.

Inside the man — inside the nearest one, the one the raven tasted — there was still a person. Dim, distant, screaming without sound, pressed down under the starlight like a flower under glass. Dominic felt him the way they'd once felt a bound hawk in a crown convoy: the flicker beneath the binding, the self still alive underneath, *still alive*, dreaming of a farm, a sister, bread, spring.

He was aware. All of them were aware. Ten thousand people locked inside their own skulls, forced to watch their hands build siege engines and sharpen blades.

Dominic wrenched back into their body and fell out of the pine tree.

* * *

They caught themselves badly, slid the last few feet, landed in the snow at the base of the trunk. Serah reached them first — she was always the fastest, when it mattered — and hauled them upright by both arms.

"What did you see?"

"Let him breathe," Tamsin said, but Dominic shook their head, shook off the help, stood on their own legs because they needed to stand on their own legs for what came next.

"The soldiers," they said. "The bound ones. They're awake inside. All of them. Trapped behind their own eyes, watching themselves march. I felt one — I felt *him*, Serah. He misses his sister. He dreams about bread."

Nobody spoke. The fire crackled. Somewhere downslope, Bramble stamped and blew, uneasy.

"If we fight that army," Dominic went on, and their voice steadied as they spoke, terror cooling into something harder, something usable, "we don't win. We kill ten thousand prisoners to reach the jailer. And the jailer wins either way, because it feeds on — I don't know, on the fighting, on the fear, on the *binding*. Every battle is a meal."

"So we don't fight the army," Serah said. Her eyes had gone bright and fierce, ward-keeper's eyes, a woman recalculating a failing equation and finding, at last, a variable she could turn. "We cut the strings at the spool. The binding-stone. Under the palace."

"Wren's route," Tamsin said.

"Wren's route," Serah agreed, and then she looked at Dominic, and the question in her face was the same one she'd asked by the fire two nights ago, but heavier now, weighted with everything the ravens had shown. "You felt Aldric in the wolf-run?"

Dominic hesitated. They had — a flicker, far off, deep under the valley's hunger, a mind like theirs, a speaker's mind, drowning. But saying it aloud made it real.

"Yes," they said. "He's still in there. Same as the soldiers. Same as the hawk in the convoy." They met her eyes. "Same as my brother was, at the end. Nobody bound is ever really gone. That's what they never understood. That's what the law was built on top of — a lie. That binding erases. It doesn't erase. It just buries you alive and makes you watch."

Serah's hand found their shoulder. Held it. Said nothing, because nothing needed saying, and everything that needed saying had already been said by the fact that her hand stayed.

"So we go to Caerlum," Dominic said. "All of us. And I go into that throne room, and I find the king buried under the thing wearing him, and I speak to him the way I spoke to the wolves. Free speech. The kind that can't be bound." They looked around the fire, at each of them — Tamsin with her scarred hands, Wren with his blank seeing eyes, Kess with her crows, Serah with her broken wards and unbroken will. "The thing eats voices. Fine. It's going to choke on mine."

Old Wren laughed softly in the dark. "Spoken like a smuggler."

"No," Dominic said, feeling the truth of it settle into their bones like returned starlight, feeling, for the first time in twenty years, no urge whatsoever to hide. "Spoken like somebody who's done being nobody."

Above them, the empty sky pressed down, threaded with stolen silver. Below them, southward, the hungry host slept on its strings.

And in Dominic's chest, where the gift lived, something that had been a wound for twenty years closed itself into a blade.

They broke camp before dawn, heading for the capital — and it was Kess, climbing onto the cart, who froze with one hand raised to the gray morning and said, in a very small voice:

"Dominic. My crows say someone's been following us since the ridge. And they say he smells like the palace."
