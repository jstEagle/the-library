## 15. Wren's Last Map

The map was already in my hands before anyone asked for it.

That is the trouble with a life spent drawing the world: you come to know it too well, and knowing too much makes a man generous in ways that cost him. I had been feeling the shape of this moment for three days—since the caves, since Dominic came back from the wolves gray-faced and whispering about soldiers strung together like beads on stolen light—and I knew what the shape required. Some things a blind man sees earlier than the sighted. We have less to distract us.

I sat by the fire in the hollow beneath the old watchtower where we'd made camp, and I turned the map over in my fingers. It was not paper. Paper forgets. It was vellum, cured goat, worked soft as a cheek from thirty years of my thumbs finding their way across it. The palace district of Caerlum sat at its heart, drawn from memory so many times that my fingertips had worn faint valleys into the ink—the keep, the wardens' college, the processional way Serah had once marched down in her white-and-silver, the river gate where barges unloaded grain for a king who no longer ate grain, if he ever had.

"You've gone quiet," Tamsin said from the pot. She always said it like an accusation. "Quieter," she amended, because quiet was my native country and everyone knew it.

"I'm working."

"You're fondling that map like it owes you money."

"Everything I own owes me money. I lent myself all of it." I heard her snort, heard the ladle knock the pot rim, heard Kess laughing low near the crows, and under all of it, the sound I was actually listening for: Dominic and Serah, apart from the fire, speaking in the clipped half-voices of people planning something they hadn't yet agreed to say aloud.

Good, I thought. Let them plan. It would spare me the ceremony.

* * *

Ceremony is what the sighted do when they cannot bear a thing plainly. I had watched enough farewells in Merrowgate—watched the council wring its forty hands while crown envoys sharpened their smiles—to know that the world prefers its losses dressed up. Banners. Speeches. Someone singing. But a sacrifice offered without ceremony is just work done early, and I had been doing work early my whole life. When your eyes go out at nine years old, you learn to pack the night before.

So when Serah finally came to me—I felt her shadow cross the firelight, felt the heat shift on my left cheek—I had the vellum already smoothed across my knees and my stylus warm in my fist.

"Old Wren." Her voice had that careful flatness she used when she thought she was delivering bad news gently. "We need to talk about the capital."

"We do." I tapped the map. "Sit. Bring your knee against mine so you can see over my shoulder. That joke never gets old for me."

She sat. She did not laugh, which told me how frightened she was. Serah Vane laughed at funerals; I'd been told so, and I believed it, because I'd drawn the faces of people who laugh at funerals and they all share a jaw set like a door barred from inside.

"Dominic scouted the host," she said. "Half those soldiers are puppets. Strung on starlight. And the thing wearing Aldric—it's feeding on the sky itself, Wren. The stars aren't gone. They're being eaten."

"I know. I've known since the caves." I let that sit. Blind men are allowed a little theatre; it's how we collect our wages. "You want to know how we get into the palace. Not past the army. Into it."

"How—" She stopped. Of course she stopped. Everyone always stopped, the first time, at the discovery that the blind cartographer had already built the road they were only now imagining. "Yes."

"Then listen, and put your finger where I tell you, because you're going to need to be able to read this without me."

* * *

Here is what I told them, gathered around the fire with the vellum passed hand to hand like a relic—which, I suppose, it was.

"The free cities teach that binding began two hundred years ago, with the Wardens' Accords. That is a lie the council tells itself so it can sleep. Binding began with the founding of Caerlum itself. Under the palace—under the throne room, under the king's own floor—sits the first binding-stone. Before there were wards on borders, before there were laws about beast-speakers, there was a stone that could hold a will inside another thing. The first kings used it sparingly. The last kings forgot it existed. And whatever wears Aldric's crown now has remembered."

Serah's breath went out slow. "The wards. When they failed—"

"They didn't fail, girl. They were eaten. Every ward ever laid is a child of that stone, spun off it like thread off a distaff. The thing under the throne simply drew the thread home." I turned the stylus in my fingers. "Which means it can't be fought with armies. Armies are made of wills, and wills are what it eats. It has to be unmade where it sits. On the stone. In the throne room."

"And getting to the throne room," Tamsin said darkly, from the pot, "means walking through a city full of puppet soldiers and then up through a palace full of worse."

"It means walking in the way nobody walks anymore," I said, "because nobody remembers it exists. Which is fortunate, because I am nobody who remembers everything."

I found Serah's hand and set it on the lower edge of the map. "Feel here. The river gate—closed these twenty years, silted, forgotten. Above it, the dyers' quarter, burned in the bread riots and never rebuilt. The crown maps show it as rubble. Rubble is a kind of door, if you know which stones were walls." My thumb traveled north. "There's a smuggler's cut through the foundations—an old drainage gallery from before the Accords, when kings still feared their cities and built themselves escape routes. It runs beneath the dyers' quarter and comes up inside the outer ward, behind the barracks cistern. Dominic will know the style of it. Thornfell runs with these."

Dominic's voice came quiet from the dark beyond the fire. "I've never taken one into a palace."

"There's a first hiding for every smuggler," I said, and heard Kess giggle, and felt the small warmth of it and banked it like a coal, the way you do with things you may not get to keep.

"Then the inner keep." My thumb climbed again, through the corridors I had walked as a young man with ink-stained cuffs and a commission from a king who still had his own eyes. "Servants' stair behind the tapestry of the Ninefold Hunt. Two turns, then the gallery above the throne room. From the gallery there's a maintenance ladder down behind the dais—the masons built it for repairing the vaulting. Nobody uses it. Nobody but a cartographer who once measured every inch of that room because he wanted to draw the ceiling stars true."

"The ceiling stars," Serah repeated softly.

"A map of the heavens, gilded, made a hundred years before the Accords. Back when the sky belonged to everyone." I smiled, though there was no one to see it, which is the best time to smile. "It's still there. Whatever sits on the throne now doesn't look up."

Silence around the fire. The good kind, the kind where a plan settles into people like rain into dry ground.

Then Dominic, carefully: "Wren. This route—you're giving it to us from memory. Every turn. Every measurement."

"Every inch," I said. "I told you. I measured."

"And you'll be—" Dominic hesitated, and I loved them for hating to say it.

"I'll be elsewhere," I said pleasantly. "Now, Kess, come here. You'll carry a copy. Fold it in quarters and learn it with your feet, the way I taught you. A map in the head survives a map in the pocket getting wet."

Kess came scrambling, all elbows and crow-feather smell, and pressed close against my side, and I put the stylus in her hand and guided her through the lines, naming each one twice. She was quick. She was quicker than I had been at nine, and no one had taken her eyes yet, and I held onto that thought the way you hold a rope over a drop.

* * *

The arranging took me most of that night, and it was the easiest work I had done in years, precisely because it was not mine alone.

A decoy needs three things: a reason to be seen, a direction to be seen moving, and someone to be seen by. The crown would be hunting us—Serah's face was on warrants in every garrison town, and after Merrowgate, so were descriptions of all of us. Descriptions, I noted, that mentioned an old blind man with a cartographer's case. Good. A description is a leash; you may as well decide where it pulls.

I woke Tamsin near dawn because she slept lightest and lied least.

"I need grease for the cart axle," I said. "And I need you to help me move my chest to the front of the wagon."

"What's wrong with where it is?"

"Nothing. That's why I'm moving it."

Tamsin looked at me for a long moment. I felt it the way you feel sun through a window. Then she said, "Wren."

"Tamsin."

"Don't." Just that. Don't. As if one word could hold a man the way a stone holds a will. She tried it anyway. "Don't you dare do this quietly. If you're going to be a damned fool, do it loudly, at breakfast, in front of everyone, so we can all shout at you properly."

"And spoil the surprise?" I patted her arm. "Grease. Chest. Go."

She went, and I heard her crying while she pumped the grease, quietly, in rhythm with the pump handle, which was very like Tamsin: grief with a chore attached.

Serah I did not wake. Serah I left a note for, in the block letters I print because cursive lies to the fingers—no, that isn't true, I leave notes in block letters because Serah reads with her eyes and I wanted her to have to look at every word straight on, no beauty to soften it:

*The host must believe the fugitives are fleeing west toward the coast. An old blind cartographer traveling alone with his maps, asking after safe roads, is exactly the sort of quarry they will chase instead of thinking. While they chase him, the others walk east. By the time they realize the bait had no hook, the fish will be in the kitchen. — W.*

*I have been decoyed before, in my line of work. Every false border I ever drew on a crown map was a decoy. I am good at being looked at in the wrong place. Do not waste my last good trick by arguing about it.*

*And Serah: the ceiling stars. When it's done, when the sky comes back—look up for me. Describe them to Kess. She'll pass them along.*

* * *

They found out at breakfast, of course. Tamsin cracked within the hour, bless her treacherous heart, and the camp erupted around me like a kicked anthill.

"You're not staying," Dominic said, and their voice had the flat hard note of a person holding a door shut against a flood. "Absolutely not."

"No," I agreed cheerfully. "I'm going. West, alone, loudly. There's a difference."

"Wren—"

"Dominic." I turned my face toward their voice and gave them the whole weight of my attention, which is the only gift a blind man owns outright. "You speak to beasts. You have spent your whole life making yourself small so the world wouldn't notice what you are. I have spent mine making myself large so it would. Let me spend it the way it was minted."

"That's not—"

"Someone has to hold the leash while you slip it. You said it yourself, around the fire: the host moves faster than any human army. Puppets don't tire, but they follow what catches their eye. Give them an old man and a wagon of maps worth more than a county, and they'll follow him at a walk, all day, away from the road you take." I spread my hands. "I'm not sacrificing anything. I'm doing my job. Cartographers exist so other people don't get lost."

Serah had not spoken. I could feel her standing at the edge of the firelight, jaw barred like that door again, and when she finally spoke it was low and rough. "There has to be another way. We could—"

"You could," I said, "and you'd lose a day arguing, and the day costs more than I do. I'm eighty-one years old, Serah. My knees gave notice years ago; my eyes left when I was nine; my heart keeps regular hours like a clerk. What exactly do you imagine I'm keeping it for?" I softened, because cruelty is just honesty wearing armor, and she deserved honesty unhelmeted. "Girl. Warden. I mapped borders for the crown for thirty years. Borders are lines men draw to decide whom they're allowed to abandon. This is the first line I've ever drawn that abandons no one. Let me have it."

Kess was crying. I could hear it, that ragged child breathing, and it hurt more than everything else combined, so I held out my arms and let her crash into me and I held her and said the only true thing I had left.

"Little wren," I said—her name for me, my name for her, we'd traded it somewhere on the road and neither had asked for it back. "Birds don't grieve the branch when they fly. Listen. You'll carry the map. That means I go everywhere you go. Every corridor, every stair, right down behind the dais. You can't lose me. No one's ever been closer to a place than you'll be to my map. So I'm not staying behind at all. I'm going ahead. The way maps always do."

She laughed wetly into my coat. "That's a lie."

"It's a map," I said. "Maps are truer than the ground. Ask any traveler in a storm."

* * *

We left at dusk, because dusk is when the sighted world goes half-blind and the balance tips toward my kind.

The plan ran simple, the way good plans do. I would take the wagon west on the coast road with the cart horse, openly, stopping at the toll house to ask loudly after safe passage and the price of rooms in Merrowgate. Tamsin's grease made the axle sing; I'd have them hearing me for a mile. The others would cut east on foot through the gorse with Kess's folded map and the crows overhead, and by moonrise the puppet host's outriders would be strung out along the coast chasing the sound of my wheels.

At the edge of camp, Serah took my hand. Her fingers were cold. Warden's fingers, ward-keeper's fingers, fingers that had held failing wards open against the dark.

"Look up for me," I reminded her. "When the stars come back. All of them. Take your time. Be greedy about it."

"I'll describe every single one," she said, and her voice broke on *single*, and I squeezed her hand once, hard, the way you stamp a seal, and let go.

I climbed onto the wagon. The reins were where my hands expected them; the road was where my memory kept it; the evening air smelled of gorse and coming cold and, faintly, of woodsmoke from the camp I was leaving, which I did not look back toward, having nothing to look with and everything to remember.

Somewhere behind me, hooves. Outriders, drawn to the sound of a singing axle and an old man humming a mapping-song older than the Accords. Right on schedule. I tipped my hat to the darkness in general.

"Well," I said to the horse, and to the night, and to whatever hungry thing was already turning its borrowed eyes toward the noise I was making. "Shall we give them a map worth following?"

The wheel sang. The road ran west. And in Kess's pocket, folded in quarters, worn soft as a cheek, I rode east with them—all the way down the servants' stair, all the way to the gallery, all the way to the ladder behind the throne, where the first stone waited under a king's floor like a heart waiting to be restarted.

Hold the leash, I thought, as the first torchlight bloomed on the road ahead. Hold it long.

They only need you to look at me until dawn.
