## 16. The Decoy at Dusk

Kess had named forty-one crows in her life, and every single one of them was smarter than the soldiers in the valley below.

She lay on her belly at the lip of the ridge, chin propped on her folded arms, watching the crown convoy wind along the river road like a black thread pulled through grey cloth. Wagons, outriders, the tall banners with the sun-and-spear stitched in gold thread that always made her think of a knife wearing a smile. Sixty men, maybe more. Her crows had counted them twice, once at dawn and once an hour ago, and crows did not miscount. Crows had never once miscounted anything in the whole history of the world, as far as Kess was concerned, which was more than she could say for people.

*Forty-two wagons,* said Marrow, perched on a stone beside her head, his voice arriving not in her ears but somewhere behind her eyes, the way crow-speech always did. *Sixty-one soldiers. One fat officer with a purple sash who keeps stopping to complain about his boots.*

"Everybody be quiet now," Kess whispered, though she wasn't sure if she was talking to the crows or to herself. "It's starting."

Below them, at the bend of the river where the old watchtower leaned like a drunk uncle against the sky, Old Wren sat in his cart with his maps spread out around him in the gathering dusk, looking for all the world like a mad old cartographer who had gotten lost, which was exactly what he was supposed to look like, and which was also, Kess thought with a small hot twist in her chest, not acting at all.

Because Wren wasn't acting. Wren had decided. That was the thing about Wren that Kess was still learning, the thing that sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone: Wren had decided three nights ago, over the cold remains of Tamsin's stew, and nobody had been able to talk him out of it, and now here they all were on a ridge at dusk, watching it happen.

*He can't see any of this,* Kess said to Marrow. *He can't even see the tower.*

*He doesn't need to see it,* Marrow said. *He drew it.*

That was Wren all over. He had drawn the river bend from memory, the leaning tower, the ford, the exact spot where the road narrowed between the rocks and the water, and when Dominic had said *are you sure* for the eleventh time, Wren had tapped the map with one blunt finger and said, *I drew this road before your grandmother drew breath, child. Roads remember. So do I.*

The convoy's lead riders reached the bend just as the sun went behind the western crags and the whole valley filled up with blue shadow. Kess saw the officer with the purple sash rein in, saw him squint up at the leaning tower, saw the cart.

Saw the old blind man sitting in the cart, sorting papers.

*Here it comes,* said Marrow.

*Here it comes,* agreed Soot, from the juniper bush.

*Here it comes,* said all forty-one of Kess's crows at once, and she had to press her face into her arm to keep from laughing, because if she laughed she might do the other thing instead, and the other thing was not allowed. Not today. Today she was a scout, and scouts were quiet, and quiet was the whole entire job.

The soldiers fanned out around the cart. Kess counted them doing it — eight, ten, twelve — peeling off the column like drops off a feather. The officer with the purple sash rode forward and shouted something the wind ate before it reached the ridge, and Wren's voice came back, thin and crackly and perfectly, beautifully annoyed, and even from this distance Kess could hear the shape of it: *Can't you see I'm working, you great galloping idiots?*

Tamsin made a sound beside her that was half sob and half laugh, and Kess reached over without looking and found Tamsin's hand and squeezed it, and Tamsin squeezed back hard enough to hurt, and that was how they held on to each other while the plan worked exactly the way Wren had drawn it.

*Twelve soldiers stopped at the cart,* Marrow reported. *The column is halting. The officer is dismounting. He is picking up one of the maps.*

"He'd better be careful with that," Kess whispered. "That's the good one. That's the one with the ford marked."

*That's the one Wren wants them to find,* said Marrow.

Right. That was the whole point. Kess knew the whole point. She just kept forgetting it on purpose, because remembering the whole point meant remembering what the whole point cost, and she wasn't ready to do that yet. Maybe by morning. Maybe by the time they reached the capital. Maybe never, if she was lucky, though Serah said luck wasn't a plan, and Serah was usually right about grim things.

The officer with the purple sash held the map up to the last of the light. Kess imagined what he was seeing: the river, the ford, the road south — and, inked in Wren's careful old hand, a route toward the capital that did not exist, a smuggler's path that Wren had invented out of whole cloth two nights ago, complete with fords and waystations and a pass through the hills, a road that would lead sixty-one soldiers and forty-two wagons on a three-day goose chase into country with no roads in it at all.

And while the whole column stopped to admire it, the six of them — Kess, Dominic, Serah, Tamsin, Bramble, and the map that mattered, the *real* map, folded flat against Dominic's ribs — would slip down the far side of the ridge and be gone.

It was a good plan. It was a Wren plan: no blood in it, if it went right. Just paper and patience and an old man's certainty that soldiers were too stupid to check whether a blind man could really see the maps he was selling them.

Kess watched the officer roll up the false map and tuck it into his saddlebag, and her heart did a little leap. *He took it,* she breathed. *Marrow, he took it, he—*

Then the officer said something to his men, and four of them moved toward the cart, and one of them took the bridle of Wren's mule, and the officer grabbed Wren by the collar of his coat and hauled him up out of the cart's seat, and Kess stopped breathing.

* * *

"They're taking him," Tamsin said. Her hand had gone rigid in Kess's grip. "They're — no. No, that's not — the plan was they'd search the cart and wave him on, the plan was—"

"The plan was always a gamble," Serah said. Her voice was flat and hard, the voice she used when she was holding something down. Kess had learned to hear the thing underneath it, the thing being held. "Wren knew that. He told us the odds himself."

"He said *likely*," Tamsin said. "He said *likely they'll just take the map and move on.* Likely. That's not the same as—"

"Everyone down," Dominic said quietly.

They all went down. Below them the officer was shouting now, and soldiers were swarming over the cart, pulling the maps out and throwing them on the ground, stamping through Wren's whole life in paper, and Wren stood in the middle of it with his arms out from his sides, that particular stiff dignity he had, like a heron standing in shallow water, and he was saying something over and over that Kess couldn't hear, and she hated that, she hated it so much her teeth ached. She wanted to hear it. Whatever it was — cursing them, or lying to them, or telling them exactly what he thought of their boots and their sashes and their king — she wanted to hear it, because if she could hear it then it wasn't over yet, and if she couldn't hear it then it was already becoming the kind of thing that happened to other people in stories, and Wren was not other people, Wren was *theirs*.

*They are binding his hands,* Marrow said. His voice had gone very flat, the way crow voices went when they were being careful. *Rope. They are putting him on a wagon. The officer has all the maps now, the false one and the real-looking ones, the ones Wren made to be found.*

"The decoys," Kess whispered. "The whole cart was decoys. He made a whole cart of decoys."

*He made a whole cart of himself,* said Marrow, and Kess pressed her face into her arm again, hard, and this time it wasn't to keep from laughing.

Because that was it, that was the thing she'd been carrying around like a hot coal for three days and couldn't put down: Wren hadn't just built a decoy route. He'd built a decoy *Wren*. A blind old man, alone, on a road the crown controlled, carrying papers that made him worth capturing — he had built himself into the plan as the part that could be lost. He'd done the arithmetic in his head, the same arithmetic he did for fords and passes and distances, and he'd put himself on the losing side of it without telling anyone, without ceremony, without even letting Tamsin make him a proper last meal, which Tamsin was taking *extremely* personally and would probably take personally for the rest of her life.

"Can't we—" Kess started. "Dominic. Can't we do something? You could talk to the mules, the wagon mules, you could make them bolt, you could—"

"And then what?" Serah said. Not cruelly. That was the worst part; she said it gently, like she was setting down something heavy. "Six of us against sixty. Kess. You know what's in those wagons. You've seen what's in those wagons."

Kess had. Three days ago, the binding yards, the convoy of chained beasts with the fog in their eyes, the great shaggy hill-ox whose mind Dominic said was *still screaming, they can't even scream anymore, they just scream inward*. She'd had nightmares about it every night since, the kind where she was the ox, and woke up with her jaw aching from clenching it shut around a bellow that wasn't hers. If they attacked the convoy and lost, Wren would end up in a wagon like that. Or worse. Wren, who drew the world for people who couldn't see it, folded up into a crown wagon with fog where his eyes should be.

"We go," Dominic said. Just that. Kess looked at them — at the sharp quiet profile, the eyes that never quite matched their voice — and Dominic was watching the wagon where Wren sat with bound hands, and something moved across their face like weather. "He bought us the road. We take it. That's how we pay him."

"That's not paying him," Kess said, and her voice cracked in the middle and she hated that too. "That's just leaving."

"No," Dominic said. "Leaving is what he's doing. We're carrying."

* * *

They went down the far side of the ridge in the last light, single file, Bramble's hooves wrapped in sacking so the rocks wouldn't sing about them. Kess rode in the middle, which she protested and nobody listened, because apparently once you were the youngest you were always cargo no matter how many crows answered you. Soot rode on her shoulder and Marrow flew above, and behind them, small with distance, the crown convoy was turning around — all forty-two wagons, all sixty-one soldiers, hauling off down the false road into the hills, chasing a road that wasn't there, following a map drawn by a man who couldn't see it.

*He'd have liked that,* Kess said to Marrow. *If he could see it. Them all going the wrong way because of him.*

*He can see it,* Marrow said. *He drew it.*

She held on to that. She turned it over in her chest like a stone from a river, smooth and cold and solid, and it was almost enough. It was almost enough for the first hour.

They traveled through the blue dark and then the black dark, no stars, never any stars, the sky above the Thornfell passes a low dead ceiling that Kess had stopped noticing and now noticed constantly, the way you noticed a tooth that ached. Tamsin drove the cart and didn't talk, which from Tamsin was louder than screaming. Serah walked at the front with Dominic, and their voices came back to Kess in low pieces — *the capital road, the ferry at Marle, four days if the ferry's running* — planning, always planning, the machine of them grinding forward because the alternative was stopping and stopping was unthinkable.

Kess sat in the back of the cart among Tamsin's pots and let her crows settle on her like a feather coat, and she did the thing she'd been not-doing all day, which was open the little packet Wren had pressed into her hands at dawn, when everyone else had been checking straps and arguing about the ridge.

*For the road,* he'd said, folding her fingers over it. *And because if I gave it to one of the others they'd argue with me about it, and you're the only one in this company with any sense.*

Inside the packet, wrapped in oilcloth, was a single sheet of paper. Not a map. Kess had unfolded it by moonlight-that-wasn't, by feel, and read it three times, and now, in the dark of the cart, she read it again, moving her lips, though she mostly knew it by heart:

*Girl — if you're reading this, the plan went the way plans go, and you're cross with me, and you have every right. So listen. I have been blind since before your mother was born, and I will tell you the only thing blindness ever taught me: the world does not end where your eyes stop. It keeps going. It is all still there. You just have to trust the drawing.*

*I have drawn you all a road to the capital, and it is a good road, and it will hold. The ferryman at Marle owes me his life and hates me for it, which is the most reliable kind of debt. Tell Tamsin the stew needed more salt, which is a lie, the stew was perfect, I only want her arguing with me in her head for a while yet. Tell Serah the wards she thinks are dead are only sleeping, and sleeping things can be woken. Tell Dominic—*

Kess stopped. Her throat had gone tight.

*Tell Dominic that a voice given away is not a voice lost, if it was given for love. I know a thing or two about giving things away. I gave my eyes to the road, in a manner of speaking, and I have never once regretted the view.*

*You are the smallest one, so they will all think they are protecting you. Let them. It does them good. But you and I know the truth, don't we, girl? You're the one with forty-one eyes in the sky. You're the one who sees the whole map.*

*Keep them on the road. That's your job now. Not the crows. Them.*

*— W.*

Kess folded the paper along its worn creases, very carefully, and put it away, and then she did cry, quietly, the way she'd learned to do it, face turned to the dead black sky where the stars should have been, while Soot pressed his warm head under her chin and Marrow landed on the cart's rail and said nothing at all, because there was nothing to say, and crows always knew the difference.

* * *

They made the ferry road by midnight, and camped in a hollow off the track with no fire, because Serah said no fires until Marle. Tamsin handed out cold bread and harder cheese and everyone ate in the dark like the fugitives they were.

It was Dominic who broke the silence, which surprised Kess; Dominic usually spoke the way water dripped, one word at a time, hours apart.

"She should have the letter," Dominic said. "Wren's letter. If there's a part for me, I want to hear it from her."

Kess's stomach dropped. She hadn't told anyone. She'd been carrying it like a stolen thing.

"It's private," she said. "He gave it to *me*."

"He gave you all of us," Serah said softly, from across the hollow. Kess couldn't see her face, only the shape of her against the starless sky. "That's what the letter is, Kess. It's all of us, in his handwriting."

Kess sat with that for a long moment. Then she took out the paper, and crawled across the cold ground to where Serah sat, and put it in her hands, and crawled back, and buried her face in Soot's feathers while Serah read it by touch and memory, because Serah couldn't read in the dark either, and had Dominic read it to her, word by word, in their low careful voice, and when Dominic got to *a voice given away is not a voice lost, if it was given for love*, the whole hollow went so quiet Kess could hear the river a mile off.

Nobody said anything. Nobody had to. That was the thing about them, Kess thought — the thing Wren had known when he wrote it down. They were a map now. Wren had drawn them, every line and ford and pass, and he'd folded himself into the packet and given it to the smallest one to carry, and the road went on.

*The road goes on,* Marrow said, from the dark above her. *And girl — the others are asking. The crows from the city. The ones who watch the palace road.*

Kess lifted her head. "What about them?"

*They say the roads to the capital are filling up,* Marrow said. *Wagons and wagons and wagons, all coming from the east, all moving at night, and the soldiers with them don't sleep and don't eat and don't speak. And they say the sky over the palace isn't black like ours.*

Kess waited. Soot had gone rigid under her hand.

*It's darker,* said Marrow. *And it's moving.*
