## 17. Into the Crown City

The bound-army could be smelled before it could be seen.

Serah had told herself she was prepared. She had spent three days on the ridgelines with Kess's crows wheeling overhead, counting campfires by their reports, mapping the muster in her head the way she'd once mapped wards—lines and nodes and pressure points. But no report had carried the smell: a thousand hearths burning green-tinged fuel that stank of old magic, of starlight being burned like peat, and beneath it something sweeter and far worse, like flowers rotting in milk.

"They're all down there," Kess whispered beside her at the ridge's lip. Her voice was steady, which frightened Serah more than weeping would have. Children who had learned to keep their voices steady at thirteen were children who had already lost too much. "All of them. The crows say they don't sleep right. They stand up in the night and face east, toward the city, all together."

"Like puppets waiting for a hand," Serah said.

Kess nodded once and said nothing else. Below them, spread across the floodplain of the Caerlum river like a scab over green land, the host of King Aldric—the thing wearing King Aldric—lay mustered. Ten thousand tents, maybe more. Banners Serah had sworn an oath under twenty years ago hung limp in the windless dusk. And along the army's edges, pacing the picket lines on chains, walked the war-beasts: bears with iron collars fused to their necks, great elk whose antlers had been drilled through and threaded with binding-wire, a hill-troll so vast it moved with the patience of a felled tree, its eyes two dull coins.

She made herself look at each one. She owed them that much. Every ward-keeper's oath ended with the same line—*the keeper serves the kept*—and she had broken that oath the day she let herself be driven from her tower instead of dying on its stairs. Looking at the chained beasts was a small penance. It changed nothing. She looked anyway.

"Dominic says there's a way in."

Serah turned. Dominic stood behind them with Bramble's lead rope looped over one shoulder, silhouetted against the last grey light, and even now—even after everything between them, after the confession in the dark of Tamsin's wagon and the wolves' eyes they had borrowed on the scout—there was something guarded in how they held themselves. A person built out of concealment didn't unbuilt quickly. Serah knew that better than anyone; she had spent two years in exile perfecting the art of being unremarkable.

"The aqueduct?" Serah asked.

"The aqueduct." Dominic crouched and scratched a line in the dirt with a stick. "Everyone thinks Thornfell is the smuggler's road. It isn't. The smuggler's road is *water*. The old Caerlum aqueduct runs off the mountains into the crown city's upper cisterns—built three hundred years ago when the kings still trusted the mountain clans. It's been sealed since Aldric's grandfather. The seals are wax and prayer, not stone. Nobody guards a door everyone believes is buried."

"And you know this because?"

A ghost of a smile. "Because I've been moving contraband through it for six years, and if you tell anyone, I'll deny every word."

Tamsin, hauling the cook-pot down off the mule, snorted. Old Wren sat against a boulder with his hands folded over his carved map-stick and his blind eyes aimed at the plain below, and he said quietly, "It's true. I mapped that tunnel's mouth forty years ago. There's a shrine to some forgotten saint halfway along where the smugglers used to leave offerings. Candles and coin. The saints of thieves have the best-maintained altars in any country."

"You've been holding this back?" Serah asked Wren.

"I've been holding back half of everything since Merrowgate, dear. You only need what you need when you need it." He patted the map-stick. "What you need now is a route in past ten thousand soldiers, and this is it."

Below them, across the whole breadth of the floodplain, the first stars should have been coming out. There were none. There had been none for weeks, and Serah still caught herself looking up at the wrong moments, still felt the old reflexive comfort curdle into vertigo. An empty sky was a room with the walls removed. She had spent her whole life reading the heavens as a text—wards were calibrated to starlight, keyed to constellations the way locks are keyed to teeth—and the black above them now was not absence but *appetite*. Somewhere under that darkness, in the palace at Caerlum's heart, something was eating.

"All right," she said. "Walk me through it."

Dominic drew while they talked: the aqueduct's high arches crossing the river gorge north of the city, the maintenance hatches, the point where the channel passed within a spear-cast of the bound-army's northern flank. "We go at the change of watch, third hour. The sentries on the aqueduct itself are human—crown regulars, bored, cold, badly fed. We take the first hatch quiet. After that we're inside the mountain's own bones and nobody looks for water where there hasn't been water for a century."

"And the beasts?"

Dominic's jaw tightened. "The beasts will smell us. Wolves on the flank lines, and whatever they've got collared in the ravines." A pause, long enough that Serah understood the shape of what was coming before it came. "I can talk them past us. Quietly. A suggestion here, a drowsy mind nudged toward sleep there. No chains breaking, no heroics. Just—" they glanced at Kess, then away, "—just enough."

"You'll be doing it under the nose of that thing in the palace," Serah said. "If it feels you touching minds—"

"It won't. On the scout, when I wore the wolf, I was a grain of sand in a river. It's loud, Serah. Loud things don't hear whispers." But Dominic's hand had found the strap of Bramble's harness and gripped it, and Serah saw the tremor they were hiding, and she did not say *you don't have to*. That would have been cruelty dressed as kindness. They all had to do things they didn't have to do.

"Then we whisper," she said.

* * *

They came down off the ridge in the dead hours, single file, wrapped in wool dyed the grey-brown of winter scrub. Serah took point because she insisted on it and nobody wasted breath arguing; Dominic walked second with Kess's hand tucked in theirs, and behind them came Tamsin bearing the pack with Wren's map and their remaining food, and Wren last, moving with the eerie sureness of a man who navigated by ground-sense and memory and had never once in all their weeks together stumbled.

The smell thickened as they descended. By the time they reached the river gorge, it coated the back of the throat like tallow. The aqueduct's arches rose out of the dark ahead, colossal, older than the dynasty, their stones furred with moss and starred with lichen that had never seen a sky worth reaching for. Water still trickled somewhere in the channel bed—a thread of it, snowmelt finding the old path—and Serah found the sound steadying. Water remembered its roads. So did she.

The sentry post clung to the third arch like a swallow's nest: a lean-to of planks, a brazier burning low, two men in crown grey. Serah watched them for a quarter hour from the shadow of the abutment. One slept sitting up. The other paced, stopped, rubbed his hands, paced again—a man whose body wanted to desert and whose terror wouldn't let him. Half-bound, Dominic had called the soldiers they'd seen through raven-eyes. Strung on stolen starlight. This one, though—this one just looked like a farm boy a long way from home, guarding an aqueduct nobody used, under a sky with nothing in it.

"We could go around," Kess whispered. "There's a gap in the rocks—"

"There isn't, love. Not with the ravine flooded." Serah flexed her fingers. Her knife felt suddenly heavy and stupid in its sheath. "Wait here. All of you."

She crossed the open ground low and quick, smuggler-fashion—she'd learned more useful things in exile than she'd ever learned in the warders' towers—and came up beneath the lean-to's edge. The awake sentry heard her at the last moment, spun, and found a knife resting flat against his belt-line and a woman's eyes level with his own.

"Don't," she said, before he could inhale. "You shout, my friends come out of the dark and it gets ugly, and you die for an aqueduct. Or you listen, and in five minutes you're standing your watch having seen nothing, and you get to keep being alive. Choose fast."

His breath came ragged. Up close she could see he was younger than the farm-boy guess—younger than Kess would be in four years, maybe—and that his hands were cracked with cold. "Who—who are you? Ward-grey," he stammered, staring at the faded badge on her shoulder. "Warders were disbanded. Warders were—"

"Arrested. Exiled. Executed, some of us. Yes." She eased the knife back a finger's width. "Here's what I am tonight: someone who needs that hatch open for a few minutes and needs you to forget it forever. In exchange, a question answered honestly gets you a coin honestly given."

He licked his lips. "What question?"

"Why does a boy guard an empty aqueduct under a starless sky, and still wear the king's grey?"

Something went out of him then—not fear; something sadder. "Because the alternative is the pits," he whispered. "Because they say the king eats light now, and if you say it too loud they put a collar on you and walk you down among the beasts, and my brother's already down there. I saw him at the muster. On a chain. He looked at me and he didn't know me, and his eyes—" His voice broke and reassembled itself into soldier-flatness, badly. "Open the hatch. Take your coin. I never saw you."

Serah gave him the coin and pressed her second knife into his hand besides, handle-first. "For your brother's chain, when the day comes," she said. "And when it comes—it's coming sooner than they think—cut it."

She was back at the rocks before her heart slowed. Dominic looked at her, one eyebrow raised, and she shook her head: *later*. Some stories weren't for the road.

* * *

Inside, the aqueduct was a cathedral of dripping dark.

Their single shuttered lantern showed walls of fitted granite going up beyond the light's reach, and the channel bed running narrow and dry except for that faithful thread of meltwater, and everywhere the slow percussion of drops falling from heights unseen. The air tasted of mineral and deep time. When Serah laid her palm against the wall, she felt it faintly—a hum, a resonance, like a struck bell two rooms away.

"You feel that?" she murmured.

Dominic knelt and pressed their ear to the stone. "Old wards. Dead ones, mostly." A pause. "Not all dead."

"No." Serah traced the wall until her fingers found it: a hairline seam in the granite, and within the seam, so worn it was nearly smooth, a ward-sign she knew intimately—the keeper's mark, the interlaced ring. "These walls were warded by my order, centuries ago. Before the binding laws. Before the crown decided living minds were ammunition." She laughed, short and bitter, and the sound ran strangely down the tunnel. "Do you understand what that means? The road we're sneaking into the capital through was built by people who believed exactly what we believe. It's been carrying our cause under the usurper's feet for three hundred years, and nobody ever noticed."

"Water remembers its roads," Dominic said quietly, echoing her thought without having heard it, and Serah looked at them in the lantern-glow and felt the thing she had been refusing to name turn over in her chest like a fish.

They made nine miles that night, halting in the side-chambers where smugglers' caches still lingered—rusty lantern-hangers, a rotted rope, one alcove where Wren's fingers found the shrine he'd promised: a niche with a weathered stone figure, its features worn to anonymity, its base littered with ancient candle-stubs and, incredibly, one coin not yet green with age. Somebody had paid respects recently. Smugglers were still using the road.

"She has no name anymore," Wren said, turning the coin in his fingers and giving it back to the altar. "Which means she can be anybody. Patron saint of everyone who was ever erased." He set it down gently. "Good enough for me."

They slept in shifts in the shrine chamber, packed close for warmth, and Serah lay listening to the breathing around her—Tamsin's rumble, Kess's small sighs, Wren's measured rasp, Dominic's near silence—and understood that she had done the unthinkable: she had assembled a family out of wreckage, and now she was leading it into the mouth of the thing that ate light. Dread sat in her stomach like swallowed stone. Determination sat beside it, equally heavy, equally permanent. They had been walking together so long she could no longer tell which weight was which.

In the darkest hour, Dominic's voice came softly across the chamber. "Can't sleep either?"

"I'm making lists," Serah said. "It's what I do instead of sleeping. Wards to hold, doors to count, ways this goes wrong."

"How many ways?"

"Forty-one so far."

"Mm." A pause. "I'm at fifty-six, but mine include Bramble opinions."

Above them, in the dark, the mule shifted and blew air through her lips in a sound that was unmistakably commentary. Kess giggled in her sleep. And for one breath, down in the bones of the mountain, under a sky with no stars, Serah Vane laughed too—quietly, helplessly, with tears she didn't examine—because dread and determination might walk hand in hand, but apparently so did terror and love, and she was done pretending otherwise.

* * *

They reached the aqueduct's end at dusk the next day.

The final stretch ran beneath the city itself, and the hum in the walls changed character here—stronger, stranger, threaded with something that made Serah's teeth ache. She recognized it the way you recognize a voice through a door: the binding-stone. Wren had drawn it for them on the map-stick, the great engine beneath the palace, the source from which the crown drew power and into which, if Serah's guesses held and Dominic's wolf-sight held and the gods had one mercy left, the hunger could be lured and sealed. It sang through sixty feet of granite like a furnace behind a wall.

And through the last grate, down through the channels of the old cistern, they saw the crown city for the first time.

Caerlum at dusk should have been gold—windows catching the westering sun, river bright as hammered tin. Instead the whole city glowed faintly, sickly, lit from below, as if the streets were veins and something pumped light through them instead of blood. The palace stood on its rock above the river, and the light concentrated there, pouring upward from the foundations in a column that simply *stopped* against the underside of the black sky, like smoke against glass.

"That's where the stars are going," Kess whispered. Her crows clustered on the grating above her, feathers tight, saying nothing, which for crows was a scream. "It's drinking them and lighting itself."

"Then it's full," Dominic said. Serah heard the tremor beneath the steadiness. "Full things can be tipped."

They gathered at the hatch that opened, according to Wren's memory and Dominic's smuggling, into the undercrofts of a tannery street on the city's north rim—one door between them and the enemy's capital, between them and the throne room, between them and whatever remained of a king crying out inside his own stolen body.

Serah checked each of them with a look and a touch: Tamsin, knives in her apron strings and grief converted to purpose; Wren with his map-stick, staying behind at the hatch as agreed, because someone had to hold the door and he had already decided, with terrible gentleness, that it would be him; Kess, chin up, three crows hidden in the basket on her back; Dominic, pale, silent, Bramble's muzzle pressed to their shoulder as if the mule knew what was coming and was saying what she could.

"Once we're through," Serah said softly, "we're ghosts until the throne room. No names, no noise. We move when the bells move." She put her hand on the hatch bar. Under her palm, through wood and stone and sixty feet of earth, the binding-stone pulsed like a second heartbeat, faster than her own.

"Whatever happens," she said, "we go together."

She lifted the bar. The hatch swung inward onto darkness, breathing cold cellar air, and from somewhere far above—muffled, immense, rhythmic as a heart that was not hers—came the sound of ten thousand soldiers stamping their feet in perfect, terrible unison.
