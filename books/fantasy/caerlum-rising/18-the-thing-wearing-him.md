## 18. The Thing Wearing Him

The crown city smelled of ash and lilies.

That was the first thing Dominic noticed as they came up through the smuggler's tunnel beneath the tanner's row—two scents that had no business sharing the same air. Ash from the mustering yards outside the walls, where ten thousand bound soldiers drilled in perfect, terrible silence. Lilies from the palace gardens above, blooming fat and white in a season when nothing should have bloomed at all.

Everything here grew too well, or not at all. The land around Caerlum's heart was drunk on stolen starlight, and it didn't know what to do with the feast.

"Stay close," Serah murmured. In the tunnel's dark her face was all edges and shadow, ward-marks inked down her forearms faintly luminous, like embers refusing to die. "Once we're inside, we don't stop. We don't look back. We go where the map says."

"The map says," Kess whispered from behind her, clutching Wren's rolled vellum against her chest like a shield. Her crows were outside now, scattered across the rooftops of the palace district, and every few heartbeats one of them sent back a flicker of feeling through the strange thread she held them by—*stone, stone, guard, stone*—"the guards are wrong. My birds say they're *empty*. Like the yard soldiers."

"They are the yard soldiers," Tamsin said grimly. He'd traded his cook's apron for a dead man's mail, and he wore it like an apology. "Half-bound, all of them. You walk past them the way you'd walk past furniture."

Dominic said nothing. They hadn't said much since the caverns under the hills, since the night they'd knelt with their palms flat against cold stone and let Serah feel the old wards still humming in the deep places—and made the promise neither of them had spoken aloud but both of them had heard. *If there's anything left of Aldric in there, I'll find it.*

Bramble shifted at their side, the mule's warm flank pressing close. They'd brought her into the city itself, bribed and hooded and led down routes only Thornfell smugglers knew, because Dominic could not do what came next without her heartbeat beside theirs. Beasts felt the truth of things. Bramble had been trembling since sundown.

They came up out of the tunnel into a gardener's shed, then a hedge maze gone wild, then—the stars' absence pressing down like a hand on the back of the neck—the open colonnade before the great doors of Aldric's hall.

No one stopped them. That was the worst part. Two servants glided past carrying wine, faces smooth as eggs, eyes fixed on nothing. A pair of guards flanked the doors and did not turn their heads. Dominic brushed close enough to one to hear what lived behind its breathing, and heard exactly what Kess's crows had told them: a hollow room where a man used to be, wind moving through it, someone else's will keeping the lungs working.

*Don't look at them,* Dominic reminded themself. *You've walked past worse things than this. You've been invisible your whole life.*

But invisibility had always been a choice. This was different. This was a whole kingdom choosing not to see.

Serah pressed her palm flat against the door, and for one breath her lips moved—ward-keeper's cant, old words, words she'd been exiled for speaking. The doors swung inward without a sound.

* * *

The throne room of Caerlum had been built to make visitors feel small.

Dominic had never seen it before tonight, but they knew its architecture the way they knew every trap-laid road in the mountains: by instinct, by the way sound moved through it. Vaulted ceiling lost in gloom. Columns marching away into dark. A floor of pale marble worn by two hundred years of knees and processions, and at the far end, raised on seven steps, the throne itself—ironwood and silver, plain as a judge's chair, because the first kings of Caerlum had wanted to be remembered as servants of the land rather than masters of it.

Something was sitting in it that understood none of that.

It had King Aldric's shape. It sat the way Aldric must have sat for thirty years, one hand on the armrest, the other loose against his knee. It wore his face—or nearly. The proportions were right, the grey at the temples, the scar through the left brow that ballads sang about. But the skin was too smooth over the bone, like a glove that had been worn long enough to learn the shape of the hand inside it and forgotten there had ever been a difference. And the eyes—

The eyes were lit.

Not glowing, exactly. Not the theatrical hellfire of village scare-stories. Just lit, softly, from within, the way a lamp shines through a curtain: the color of starlight strained through skin, and just as hungry.

Behind Dominic, Tamsin's breath went ragged. Kess made a small sound and Serah's hand found her shoulder without either of them seeming to decide it.

And beneath all of it—beneath the lit eyes and the borrowed face—Dominic heard it. Not with ears. With the sense they had spent thirty years strangling in the dark, the gift that had gotten their brother killed, rising unbidden the way water rises through cracked ground.

A voice, screaming.

It was very far away. It was very tired. It came from somewhere underneath the thing on the throne, down through layers of silence packed around it like grave-dirt, and it was saying a name. Saying it over and over, the way a drowning sailor says the name of a shore.

*Aldric. I am Aldric. I am Aldric. I am—*

Dominic's knees nearly buckled.

They covered it by shifting their weight, by letting Bramble lean into them, by doing all the small dishonest things a body does to stay upright. But the thing on the throne saw. Of course it saw. Its head tilted—a fraction too fluid, a motion no living neck would choose—and those curtained-starlight eyes found Dominic across forty feet of marble as though the whole hall were empty except for the two of them.

"You brought me a gift," it said.

Its voice was Aldric's voice. That was the cruelest thing. It was a good voice, a king's voice, worn smooth by decades of council chambers and harvest festivals and the naming of ships, and the thing wearing him used it the way a musician uses a stolen instrument—with perfect skill and no love whatsoever.

"Serah Vane." The eyes moved to her. "The ward-keeper who cried wolf. Do you know, I kept expecting you? Every ward that failed, every star I swallowed, I thought: *now she comes.* And instead you hid in the mud with smugglers and children." Something moved at the corner of its mouth. If Aldric's face had ever made that expression, it had meant something kinder. "Sentiment. It makes you so slow."

"You're not him," Serah said. Her voice held. Dominic loved her for it and feared for her because of it, both at once, inextricably. "Whatever you are—you're wearing a dead man's chair."

"I'm wearing a *living* man's body," it corrected gently. "There's a difference. Dead men don't scream."

Kess flinched. Tamsin put a hand on her hair.

"And you," the thing said, and the eyes came back to Dominic, and this time they stayed. "The beast-speaker. Did you think I wouldn't know? I have eaten every star in your sky one by one, child. Do you imagine there is anything in this kingdom I have not tasted?" It leaned forward on the throne, slow as syrup. "The wolves of Thornfell speak of you. The ravens remember your name in their croak-songs. There has not been a true speaker in Caerlum since the purges, and here one walks into my hall wearing a mule's company and another person's courage."

"Don't listen," Serah breathed.

"I'm going to offer you something," the thing continued, as if she hadn't spoken, as if she were furniture, as if everyone in the world were furniture except the one mind it wanted. "I want you to understand that I am not your enemy, Dominic Okafor."

*It knows my name.* Cold walked down Dominic's spine. *It knows my name and it said it like it was handing me a coin.*

"Your king outlawed your gift. Your king burned your brother."

The words landed like a blade between ribs. Beside them, Serah went very still—she knew this story now, knew it from the caverns, from the night Dominic had finally said *Elian* aloud for the first time in fifteen years—but knowing it and hearing it in the mouth of the thing that ruled them were two different wounds.

"Your brother spoke to a dying horse in a public road, and the crown cut out his tongue for it while he still lived." The thing recited it fluently, tenderly, the way a connoisseur describes a vintage. "I have read the records. I keep all the records. And now your new king—your restored Aldric, if you manage to dig him out of me—will do what? Abolish the laws? Laws are paper. Hearts are slower. You would spend the rest of your life being *tolerated*, Dominic. Pardoned. Pitied. The smuggler who did a brave thing once."

It rose from the throne.

The motion was wrong—all of its motions were wrong, too smooth, too continuous, like water pouring uphill—and it descended the seven steps without appearing to touch them. Up close it was worse. Up close, the lit eyes had depth, and the depth had movement, and the movement was *hunger*, patient and enormous, the hunger of something that had watched the first binding-stone be set in the earth and had waited, all this time, to be fed.

"Or," it said, "you kneel."

The word hung in the vaulted dark.

"Kneel, and I give you legitimacy no restored king could grant. Not tolerance. *Office.* First Speaker of Caerlum. Your gift written into law—not permitted, *required*. Every stable-hand and shepherd and soldier trained to do what you were born doing. No more Elians. No more tongues cut out in public roads. No more hiding." It smiled with Aldric's mouth. "I know about hiding. I have hidden for a very long time. I am offering you the end of it."

And here was the horror, the thing Dominic would carry even if they survived the night: some part of them *wanted it*.

Not the kneeling. Not the hunger. But the picture it painted—bright and clean as morning—of walking through a market with Bramble and speaking to her openly, of children learning the old words in schoolrooms, of a world where their whole self wasn't contraband. Fifteen years of silence reached up in Dominic's chest and said *yes, yes, take it, take anything but this hiding, this weight, this life spent being nobody—*

Then they felt Serah's hand.

She hadn't grabbed them. She'd only let her fingers rest against the back of their wrist, light as a bird landing, the way she'd learned to touch them in the months since the caverns—asking, never taking. And through that touch came everything she wasn't saying: *I know what he's offering. I know it's real. I also know what's underneath it, screaming. So do you. That's why we're here.*

Love and duty. Everyone always said it like a choice. Standing on the marble with a god-shaped hunger smiling at them, Dominic finally understood it wasn't. Duty was just love with its sleeves rolled up.

"No," Dominic said.

Their voice came out rough, quiet, steady. Bramble's ear flicked toward them; through the bond, warmth.

The thing's smile didn't waver. "Because of her?"

"Because of him." Dominic lifted their chin toward the throne. "The man you're wearing. He's still in there. I can hear him."

For the first time, the lit eyes flickered.

It was small—the smallest thing, a stutter in the light, a curtain swaying—and Dominic seized it the way they'd seize a handhold on a cliff-face. *He's in there. He heard me. He heard his own name in my refusal.*

"He is *tired*," the thing said, and something in its voice slipped its leash for half a syllable—something vast and irritated, something that had been patient for centuries and hated being made to wait longer. "He has been screaming for three years and no one came. Not his knights. Not his priests. Not her." A gesture at Serah, dismissive, careless. "Why should you succeed where their loyalty failed?"

"Because I'm not loyal," Dominic said. "I'm stubborn. There's a difference."

They stepped forward.

Tamsin hissed something behind them. Kess's crows, somewhere high above the palace roof, went up in a ragged black burst of alarm that Kess caught and wrestled down with a child's fierce concentration—*not yet, not yet, wait for my call*. Serah's fingers tightened on Dominic's wrist and then, deliberately, let go.

Dominic walked toward the throne alone.

Seven steps. Pale marble. The lily-smell drifting in from the gardens, sweet as rot. And all the way, growing louder with each stride, the voice beneath the silence:

*Aldric. I am Aldric. Whoever you are—I am Aldric—*

"I can hear him," Dominic said again, stopping at the foot of the steps, close enough to see the fine seams where the glove of flesh met the glove of face. Close enough to smell it—starlight had a smell, they realized, ozone and cold iron and something older, like caves before men. "Every word. And I know how to reach what's left of him."

The thing looked down at them from the top step, hunger wearing a king's patience.

"You can try," it said. "I have eaten voices older than your language."

"Yes," Dominic said. "So I've been told."

They drew a breath. Beneath their feet, so deep in the earth that only a beast-speaker's bones could catch it, the first binding-stone began—very faintly—to hum.

And Serah Vane stepped forward into the starless dark, hands already moving, and began to open the wards.
