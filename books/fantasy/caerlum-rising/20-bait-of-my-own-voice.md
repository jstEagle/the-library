## 20. Bait of My Own Voice

The hunger had my voice in its teeth.

I felt it pulling—down through the ruined throne room, down past the shattered binding-stone's roots, into a dark that had no bottom. It drank at me the way Bramble drank from a mountain stream after a hard crossing: greedy, unthinking, certain there would always be more.

And that was the thought that saved me.

Because I had spent thirty years learning what beasts did when they were certain there would always be more. They overreached. They followed the lure past the snare-line and into the pit. My mother had taught me that much before the crown took everything else—the first lesson of any smuggler, any herder, anyone who lived by reading the minds of animals:

*Nothing that hungers thinks clearly while it is feeding.*

So I stopped fighting it.

I let go of the walls I had built around myself since I was six years old, since the night soldiers came for my brother and his voice went silent in the yard behind our house. I opened every door inside me at once.

The hunger surged in like floodwater.

* * *

For a moment—one terrible, endless moment—I was nothing but a throat. A voice being sung through by something vast and old and cold as the space between stars. It poured itself along my gift like water filling a channel, tasting it, mapping it, learning the shape of every whisper I had ever given to a mule or a wolf or a crow on a wire fence. It found Bramble in me. It found the wolves of Thornfell, the ravens Kess had lent us, the small secret conversations of a lifetime spent talking to things the crown said could not talk back.

It liked what it found.

*More,* it said, in Aldric's mouth, in my mouth, in a hundred mouths it had worn before either of us. *More.*

"Dom!" Serah's voice, far away, ragged. She was on her knees at the base of the binding-stone with both hands pressed flat against its cracked face, holding open the last thread of ward-light she had coaxed out of twenty years of exile and shame. Blood ran from her nose in a thin line. "Whatever you're doing—I can't hold this much longer!"

*I know,* I tried to say, and discovered I had no mouth left to say it with.

That was all right. That was the whole point.

Instead I did the hardest thing I have ever done, harder than the mountains, harder than hiding, harder than watching my brother die for the crime of kindness to a lame horse. I turned my voice into a road—a long bright road running away from Aldric's body, away from the throne, away from the thing crouched inside the king like a hand inside a glove—and I made the road end at the binding-stone Serah was holding open with nothing but her hands and her guilt and her love for a kingdom that had cast her out.

Then I called the hunger home to supper.

*Here,* my gift sang, in the oldest tongue, the one older than law, older than binding, the one that says *brother* and means it. *All of it. Everything I am. Come and take it.*

The hunger hesitated.

It had Aldric's body still—most of him. It wore the king like a coat it was reluctant to shed, because a king's body was useful, a king's voice commanded armies, a king's face opened gates. Leaving meant giving up the throne, the host, the half-bound soldiers strung outside the walls on stolen starlight like lanterns on a wire.

But it was feeding. And nothing that hungers thinks clearly while it is feeding.

It let go of Aldric's left hand first. I felt it—a shudder through the throne room, a sound like a great bell cracking—and the king's fingers, gray and trembling, curled inward on the arm of the throne. His own fingers. His own will, moving them.

*Come,* I sang again, and poured more of myself onto the road. I gave it the memory of Bramble's breath warm against my palm. I gave it the wolves' dawn-song in the high passes. I gave it every word of comfort I had ever whispered to a frightened animal in the dark, and I made each one taste like the last meal it would ever be offered.

Aldric's head turned toward me. His eyes were wrong—black all the way through, starless, two holes punched in a face that had once, if the old portraits told truth, been kind. But beneath the black, deep down where even the hunger's grip went thin, something looked out at me.

Something that was drowning.

He couldn't speak. The hunger owned his tongue. But his mind touched mine across the gap the way a beast touches a beast-speaker—wordless, desperate, trusting in a way that broke my heart, because he had been alone in there for so long and here, finally, was someone who could hear him.

*Please,* said what remained of King Aldric. Not in words. In the shape of words. The way Bramble said *home*.

"I'm coming," I said aloud, with the mouth I had left. "Hold on."

* * *

Serah saw it happen. Later she would tell it differently—she always told it bigger, with more fire, the way ward-keepers are taught—but what I remember is this:

The hunger began to leave the king.

It came out of him the way smoke comes out of a burning house: reluctantly, in tendrils, in long gray ropes that unwound from his spine and his skull and the marrow of him, streaming across the air toward me, toward the feast I had laid out of my own self. Where it passed, the torches guttered. Where it passed, the stone itself seemed to lean away. Kess's crows—she had loosed them all, every bird she'd spoken to since Merrowgate—swirled above the throne room screaming in voices that weren't quite crow-voices anymore, harried and frantic, buying seconds wherever they could, dive-bombing the bound guards who were already collapsing as the strings that held them frayed.

Aldric slid from the throne like a coat sliding off a hook.

He hit the floor on hands and knees, and he made a sound I will hear until I die—not a scream, not a word, just the raw noise of a man feeling his own weight for the first time in years. He crawled. Toward me, actually, which I didn't expect. Toward the light of the wards, maybe. Toward anything.

The hunger reached me then—or reached the shape of me I had built as bait—and it took hold, and it pulled itself along my voice like a thing climbing a rope hand over hand over hand—

—and Serah Vane, disgraced, exiled, bleeding from the nose, laughing or crying or both, threw every scrap of the old magic she had left into the binding-stone and *slammed the door shut behind it.*

* * *

I have stood at the top of the Thornfell passes in an avalanche and heard the mountain come apart around me. It sounded like a lullaby compared to this.

The binding-stone—the first one, the source, the thing Wren had mapped in his blind man's memory and given his freedom to show us—took the hunger the way a wound takes a knife. The chamber floor split in a spiderweb from wall to wall. Ward-light that hadn't burned in centuries flared up through the cracks, white and gold and furious, and the hunger went into it screaming.

Not in Aldric's voice anymore. Not in mine. In its own voice—which was no voice at all, only a pressure, a cold, a vast and ancient *want* folding smaller and smaller as the stone closed over it like a fist closing over a fly.

It grabbed for me on the way down.

Of course it did. I was the bait; bait gets swallowed. I felt it take my gift the way a current takes a swimmer—my beast-speech, my secret lifetime of whispers, the one true thing about me, torn loose and dragged down into the dark with it. I heard Bramble somewhere very far away calling my name and I could not answer. I heard the wolves, the ravens, the whole listening world going quiet around me like doors closing one by one down a long corridor.

I thought: *so this is what my brother felt.*

I thought: *at least I chose it.*

And then the stone sealed, and the silence where my gift used to be was total, and I fell.

* * *

Someone caught me. Arms around my shoulders, my head against a shoulder plated in old guard-leather gone soft at the seams—Serah, kneeling in the wreckage, holding me the way you hold something you're afraid will break.

"Dom." Her voice cracked on the second syllable. "Dom, stay with me. Look at me."

I looked. Her face was filthy and streaked with blood and she was the most beautiful thing I had ever seen, and I wanted to tell her so, except when I opened my mouth what came out was not words. It was a thin, wordless sound, the kind a hurt animal makes.

The silence in me where the gift had been was enormous. All my life it had been there—under everything, under the smuggling and the hiding and the careful blank face I showed the world—a constant low murmur, the mule and the mountain and the crows and the wind all talking, talking, talking. Now there was nothing. Just me. Just one small human mind rattling around inside itself, alone, the way everyone else had always been.

I understood, suddenly, why they outlawed it. Not because they feared the power. Because they feared the loneliness of losing it, and hated anyone who carried what they could never have.

"Dominic." Serah took my face in both hands. "Listen to me. You don't need it to talk to me. You never did."

I shook my head. Tried again. This time a word came: "Stars."

She frowned. "What?"

I lifted a shaking arm and pointed at the ceiling of the throne room—at the great shattered dome where the roof had half-collapsed during the fighting, opening a jagged window onto the sky.

Kess got there first. Of course she did. She scrambled up over a slab of fallen masonry, crow perched on her shoulder like a general's aide, and stood silhouetted against the broken roof with her mouth hanging open.

"It's back," she whispered. Then louder, in the voice of a child waking the whole house: "IT'S BACK! THE STARS ARE COMING BACK!"

* * *

They came the way dawn comes—not all at once, but in a slow tide. First one, low over the eastern wall, trembling as though unsure it was welcome. Then three. Then a scatter of them, then a spill, then a flood, stars pouring back into the black sky like water returning to a dry riverbed, and with each one that kindled I felt something loosen in the air itself—the land exhaling, the stolen light flowing home, the half-bound soldiers outside the walls dropping their weapons and falling to their knees as the strings went slack and they woke up men again instead of puppets.

Tamsin appeared at my other side—when had she come up from the tunnels?—and put my arm over her shoulders, and together with Serah they got me upright. Old Wren wasn't there to steady me. Wren was miles north in a cell, having bought us this night with himself, and I filed that grief away in the place where I kept my brother, promising it we'd come back for it later.

Aldric was sitting up.

He looked smaller than a king should look. Gray-faced, hollowed, hands shaking as he pressed them flat against the stones as if checking that they were real. He stared at the stars coming home through the broken roof and he wept without any dignity at all, and it was the most kingly thing I ever saw a man do.

His eyes found mine across the ruin of his throne room. He knew. Whatever else the hunger had eaten, it had left him the memory of the last moments—of the bait, of the voice, of someone choosing to be devoured so he could live.

He started to bow. Actually bow, from his knees, the King of Caerlum bowing to a smuggler in a mud-caked coat.

"Don't," I rasped. My voice was wrecked but it worked. Words worked. They'd have to be enough. "Save it. There's a blind man in your dungeons who deserves it more than I do. And a child who talks to crows. And a deserter who cooks better than any royal chef you've ever employed. Get them home safe, and we'll call it even."

The king laughed—a cracked, rusted sound, like a gate opening for the first time in years—and nodded, and it was a promise.

Serah's hand found mine. I turned my palm up and laced our fingers together, right there, in front of the king and Tamsin and Kess and the returning stars and whatever gods had watched this whole miserable business from the beginning. I was done hiding things. Done with secrets, done with the dark, done with being nobody.

"You lost it," she said quietly. Searching my face. "Your gift."

"Maybe," I said.

Her brow furrowed. "Maybe?"

Because here is the thing I hadn't told her yet. Couldn't have, until now. When the hunger dragged my voice down into the stone, I had felt it go—all of it, clean away, the corridor of doors closing one by one. But in the last instant, as the seal closed, something had brushed back against me from the other side. Faint. Far. Familiar.

Not the hunger.

*A question.* Small and stubborn and smelling of mountain hay, asked in the oldest tongue, the one that says *brother* and means it.

Bramble's voice, reaching for me through a foot of enchanted stone and half a kingdom of distance.

*Home?*

And though I had no gift left to answer with—though every law of ward-magic and binding-stone and common sense said the connection was severed forever—I lifted my ruined voice to the returning stars, and I answered anyway.

"Yes," I said, out loud, in plain human words, with Serah's hand in mine and my family around me and the whole sky burning back to life overhead. "Yes. Home."

Far away, in a stable in the mountains, I swear I heard a mule snort.

But that—what came next, the ride south, Wren, the laws, the life we built after—is another chapter.
