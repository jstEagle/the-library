## 21. The King Who Came Back

The first thing Aldric learned about being free was that freedom had a weight.

He had imagined, in the screaming dark beneath the hunger, that liberation would feel like lightness—like surfacing from deep water into air. Instead it felt like armor he had to learn to carry again. His hands shook when he lifted a cup. His voice cracked on the second word of every sentence. When the healers bathed him, he flinched at their touch, because for two years no one had touched *him*; they had touched the thing wearing him, and the thing had worn his skin so well that even now he could not always tell which memories were his and which had been planted in him like seeds in a grave.

He stood before the tall window of his own bedchamber—the one they said he had never left—and looked out over Caerlum under a sky that was remembering itself.

Stars. Thin ones, hesitant, like candles relit one by one in an enormous dark cathedral. The court astronomers swore the sky was mending from the east, where the binding-stone had collapsed into silence. Aldric did not need astronomers to tell him that. He had felt the stars go out from the inside. He had felt each one swallowed like a lamp snuffed between greedy fingers, and he knew, with a certainty no scholar could give him, exactly how many were still missing.

He counted them every night. It was a penance he had assigned himself, though he told no one. Two hundred and six, when the hunger fell. Tonight he counted two hundred and eleven, and something in his chest loosened by a degree too small to measure and too large to ignore.

Behind him, the door opened. He knew the tread—Serah Vane walked like a woman who expected floors to betray her.

"Your council is waiting," she said. "Again."

"Let them wait." He watched a star brighten as if testing whether it was safe. "They've been waiting two years for a king. What's another hour?"

"They're not waiting for a king." Serah came to stand beside him, arms folded, her ward-keeper's sigil long since torn from her coat and replaced with nothing at all. "They're waiting to see what you'll do. There's a difference, and they know it, and you know it."

Aldric smiled, and felt the smile sit strangely on a face that had spent two years smiling for something else. "You don't bow anymore."

"You abolished kneeling in the throne room three days ago. I'm simply ahead of fashion."

"I abolished it because I couldn't bear—" His voice failed. He tried again. "I remember everything it did while wearing me. Every decree signed with my hand. Every beast led into the binding yards with my seal on the warrant. I remember *watching*, Vane. From underneath. Screaming into my own skull while my mouth gave the orders." He turned from the window. "Do you know what that is? To be present for your own tyranny?"

Serah was quiet a moment. Outside, far below, the city murmured—fearful, watchful, waiting for its king to show them what kind of king had come back.

"It's worse than not knowing," she said finally. "But it's also the only reason anyone might believe you. A man who claims innocence is cheap. A man who claims guilt has to be reckoned with." She met his eyes. "So reckon with it. Publicly. Before they make up their minds without you."

* * *

The Great Hall of Caerlum had been built for acclamation. Its vaulted ceiling had once been painted with constellations, and Aldric remembered—he *chose* to remember, fiercely, against the hunger's counterfeit memories—being crowned beneath them at nineteen, his father's hand heavy on his shoulder, the whole hall roaring his name.

Now the paint was peeling, and the hall was full of people who had every right to hate him.

They had come at his summons: lords in silk who had profited handsomely from the binding trade and now wore expressions of pious shock; guildmasters whose foundries had forged the binding-collars; soldiers hollow-eyed from a war they'd begun to understand they'd lost before it started. But he had asked for others too, and those were the faces he searched for as he descended the dais steps alone, unguarded, unarmed.

Deserters, brought in chains and freed at the door. Smugglers with rope-burned hands and insolent eyes. A girl with crows on her shoulders who sat down in the middle of the aisle because standing was clearly optional where she came from. A cook—a broad, scarred woman named Tamsin whom the records called a deserter but whom the soldiers' letters, which Aldric had demanded be gathered, called *the only good thing left in the camp*. And near the front, blinking at the light, an old blind man with a cartographer's ink-stained fingers, escorted gently by a healer, his wrists still ringed with the marks of manacles.

Old Wren. Aldric had read the report four times. Captured as a decoy so that others could escape. Held for interrogation. Had given up nothing—not even his name, at first; he'd given them a false name and a false map, and three crown patrols were still wandering the Thornfell passes looking for roads that existed only in a blind man's memory.

Aldric stopped on the floor of the hall, below the dais, below the throne, and the herald's voice died in his throat when the king raised one trembling hand.

"No," Aldric said, and his voice carried badly, cracked, and he did not care. "I will not speak from up there. That throne belonged to the thing that wore me, and I am not going to stand above any of you until I've earned the ground we share."

Silence, of the dangerous kind.

"My name is Aldric Caerlum," he said, "and for two years and four months, I was not the man speaking to you. Something ancient came into me through the binding-stones—something that eats, that hungers, that wears kings because kings command armies. Everything done in my name in that time was done by it. The binding laws. The war-engines. The vanished stars." He let the words hang. "I remember all of it. I watched all of it. And I am here to tell you that *I signed those orders*—my hand, my seal, my voice—and if you require a villain, you may have me, because the thing itself is ash and cannot be hated properly."

Someone in the ranks of lords shifted uncomfortably. In the aisle, the crow-girl whispered something to her birds, and both crows croaked at the same instant, like laughter.

"But hatred is not justice," Aldric went on, "and I did not crawl back from the inside of that hunger to preside over more cruelty. So hear the first act of the returned king, and hear it plainly."

He turned to face the whole hall.

"The Binding Laws are abolished. Not reformed. Not suspended pending review. Abolished—every statute, every warrant, every collar. The binding-yards are to be emptied within the month. Every bound creature that still lives is to be unbound by whatever means our ward-keepers can devise, freely, at the crown's expense, and any soul who profited from the trade will pay reparations into a fund for their care." He drew breath. "And beast-speaking is no longer a crime. It was never a crime. It was a gift, and we caged it, and the cages are coming down."

The hall erupted—not into cheers, not at first, but into noise: the lords' outrage, the soldiers' stunned murmur, and from the smugglers and deserters, a sound lower and rougher, like a fire catching in wet wood.

Aldric waited for quiet. It took a long time.

"There is a second matter," he said. "Harder than laws. Laws can be written in an afternoon. Debts take longer." He walked, slowly, along the front rank of the common folk, past the crow-girl, past Tamsin the cook, and stopped before Old Wren.

"Blind Wren of Merrowgate," he said. "You gave up your freedom so that others could reach me. You endured the crown's dungeons holding nothing but a lie, and you held the truth so well they never found it." He knelt. The hall gasped; the lords on the dais steps looked scandalized; Aldric found, to his surprise, that his knees did not shake this time. "I am told you draw maps of places you have never seen, from the way the wind sounds against stone. Then let me tell you of a place you have never seen, so you can add it to your atlas." He bowed his head. "This is the country of a king who was eaten, and the road back out of it was paid for by smugglers, deserters, cooks, children, and one disgraced ward-keeper who warned us all and was exiled for her trouble."

He rose and faced the hall again.

"So hear their names. Write them down. Carve them somewhere permanent, because I intend to be dead a long time before these debts are forgiven, and I do not trust my successors to remember." His voice steadied as he spoke them, name by name, the names he had asked for and memorized in the small hours when sleep would not have him:

"Dominic Okafor, smuggler of the Thornfell passes, beast-speaker, without whose voice the hunger could not have been lured from its throne. Serah Vane, ward-keeper, who held the wards open with her bare hands while the binding-stone tried to kill her. Tamsin Oduya, cook and deserter, who fed an army of the unwanted and taught them they were a family. Kess Ilvara of the crow-road, who is twelve years old and braver than any lord in this room. Old Wren, cartographer, who mapped the way to save a king he had every reason to abandon. And Bramble," he added, and a ripple of confusion crossed the hall, "who is a mule, and who carried better people than most of us deserve."

He looked directly at the assembled nobility then, and let them see everything—the guilt, the grief, the iron underneath.

"These people broke my kingdom to save it. I built the kingdom that required breaking. Weigh that however you like—but know that from this day, the crown serves *them*, and all like them, or it serves nothing at all."

* * *

Afterward, in the smaller council chamber, with the windows open to a night that held eleven more stars than yesterday, Aldric poured wine with his own hands—another small penance, another muscle relearned—for the five people seated around his table who looked profoundly uncomfortable being there.

"This is absurd," Tamsin said, turning her cup. "I'm a cook. Kings don't pour for cooks."

"This king does," Aldric said. "This king pours for everyone until his hands stop shaking, which the physicians assure me may take years, so settle in."

Kess had claimed the windowsill, crows at her shoulders, and was watching the sky with fierce attention. "It's doing it again," she announced. "The stars. They're coming back faster near the mountains. My birds say the sky smells like it used to. Like before." She glanced at Aldric with the terrible frankness of children. "Did it hurt? When it ate you?"

"Kess," Tamsin began.

"No, let her ask." Aldric set down the decanter. "Yes. It hurt. It hurt the whole time. And knowing that someone was coming—that *you* were coming—is the only reason there was anything left of me to save."

Kess nodded gravely, satisfied, and returned to star-counting.

Wren sat with his face tilted toward the window, drinking in the evening air as though it were vintage. "There's a constellation rising over Thornfell," he said. "I've never heard it. Describe it to me, someone—I want to put it on the map. Every map should have one thing on it no one's ever drawn before."

Serah described it, haltingly, and Wren's ink-stained fingers moved on the table as though tracing lines only he could see.

Only Dominic was silent. They sat at the corner of the table, hood back for perhaps the first time since Aldric had known of them, and there was something strange in their stillness—the stillness of a person listening to a conversation no one else could hear.

Then Dominic looked up, straight at the king, and their eyes were wet.

"What is it?" Aldric asked quietly.

Dominic hesitated. Glanced at Serah, who gave the smallest nod. Then, in a voice barely louder than the candleflame:

"Bramble says hello."

The table went still. Serah reached over and took Dominic's hand without ceremony, without explanation, as though she'd been waiting days to do exactly that.

"He—" Dominic laughed, wiped their eyes, laughed again. "The gift isn't gone. It changed. It's quieter—it comes slower, like a letter instead of a shout—but Bramble is in the palace stables, complaining about the quality of the hay, and asking—" Their voice broke clean through. "Asking if I'm happy. And I get to say yes. Out loud, to a mule, in front of a king. I never thought—"

"Say it anyway," Aldric said. "All of it. This court has been silent too long."

So Dominic closed their eyes, and spoke—to the stable across the courtyard, to a mule named Bramble, to whatever wild things were stirring in the darkening hills beyond the walls—and though Aldric could not hear the half of that conversation, he saw its effect: the way the last tension went out of Dominic's shoulders, the way Kess's crows ruffled and settled, the way Serah watched the person beside her as though watching dawn arrive somewhere she'd stopped believing in.

Aldric raised his cup.

"To the smuggler's silence," he said softly, "finally broken. May it never close again."

Outside, the stars went on returning—one, then another, then another—like a city lighting its windows after a long, long night.

And far to the north, past the mountains, past the ruined binding-yard, in the deep places where the first binding-stone had screamed itself to silence, something old stirred in the rubble—and opened what might, in time, come to be called an eye.
