## 22. Caerlum Rising

The ridge above Thornfell had a name once. The old maps called it the Shepherd's Crown, back when shepherds still climbed it to count their flocks against the stars. Nobody counted sheep anymore. Tonight, on the first clear night since the sky went black and came back, Dominic Okafor sat on the Shepherd's Crown and counted stars instead.

They were all there. Every one the hunger had swallowed, poured back into heaven like water returning to a dry riverbed. The constellations Serah had named in her ward-keeper's cadence — the Plough, the Sisters, the long slow arc of the Wanderer — burned steady and unafraid, as if they had never been eaten at all. As if they had simply closed their eyes in the dark and waited for someone to sing them awake.

Dominic's throat still ached. It had ached for three weeks, ever since the binding-stone collapsed in on itself with the hunger screaming inside it and Dominic's voice caught somewhere between their chest and the world. For three weeks they had spoken to no beast, felt nothing on the other side of the silence but the ordinary animal minds of ordinary animals — warm, close, and utterly locked away, the way a house with shutters drawn is locked away. They had told themselves it was the price. They had told themselves they would learn to live with it.

They had almost believed it.

Below the ridge, the camp was banked down to embers. Tamsin's cookfire, always the last light to die, threw a low orange glow against the rocks. Kess was asleep in the cart with three crows roosting on the canvas above her like a mobile of small dark gods. Old Wren sat propped against a wheel with his sightless face tipped up to the sky he couldn't see, listening to it the way other men listened to music. He had come back to them from the crown city thin as a winter branch and missing two fingers, and he had refused, absolutely refused, to let anyone apologize for leaving him behind as a decoy. "I've been a map my whole life," he'd said. "Maps are meant to be used up."

And Serah Vane was climbing the ridge path toward Dominic, her boots quiet on the frost-stiff grass, her ward-keeper's coat — the one she'd finally taken out of hiding, the one with the silver thread at the cuffs that marked her rank restored by royal decree — pale in the starlight.

Dominic watched her come and felt the old reflex rise: the flinch, the calculation, the quick mental inventory of everything that must not be seen. Twenty years of being nobody. Twenty years of watching their own hands for signs of the gift, of swallowing words meant for beasts, of walking through the world like a draft through a house — present everywhere, noticed nowhere.

The reflex rose. And, for the first time Dominic could remember, found nothing to grip.

"Can't sleep either?" Serah said, settling onto the rock beside them. Close enough to share warmth. Not so close as to presume. She had a soldier's instincts about distance, and she had never once, in all these months, used them against Dominic.

"Didn't try." Dominic pulled their cloak tighter. "I keep thinking I'll look up and they'll be gone again. Like the whole sky is a door someone left open and any minute the wind will take it."

"It's not a door." Serah leaned back on her palms. "It's a promise. Wards are promises, that's all they ever were. The old ones. The first binding-stone made one too, and the hunger broke it, and we made another one with its own teeth." She smiled, thin and tired and real. "The stars are just the oldest ward there is. They held."

"They didn't hold on their own."

"No," Serah agreed quietly. "They didn't."

* * *

Bramble came up the ridge path at midnight, because Bramble had never in her life respected a bedtime.

Dominic heard the mule's hooves on the frost before they saw her — that deliberate, opinionated walk, each step a small argument with the ground. She crested the rise, regarded the two of them on the rock with the flat judgment only mules can manage, and stood there. Waiting.

"Go back to sleep, you great gray tyrant," Dominic said.

Bramble did not go back to sleep. She walked closer, put her nose nearly against Dominic's knee, and breathed. And in that breath — in the warm hay-and-lichen smell of her, in the slow engine of her lungs — Dominic felt something they had not felt in three weeks.

Not the old gift. Not exactly. The old gift had been a door flung open, a flood of other minds, the world shouting at them in a hundred tongues they'd spent a lifetime pretending not to understand. This was smaller. Quieter. This was a single thread, fine as spider silk, running from Dominic's chest to the gray head bowed over their knee.

And along that thread, faint as a star seen through smoke, came a feeling that was unmistakably, irrefutably Bramble.

*Cold,* the feeling said. *And you are warm. And I have decided to stand here.*

Dominic's breath went out of them in a long, shaking rush.

It wasn't gone. It wasn't the same — the hunger had chewed something out of them, some great roaring channel of speech, and what grew back in its place was this: not a shout but a touch. Not a hundred voices but one at a time, chosen, earned. Where once they had heard everything whether they wished to or not, now they would have to listen. Now it would be a conversation, not a conquest.

They had spent their whole life hiding a gift that had never asked their permission. This one was asking.

"Bramble," Dominic whispered, and their voice cracked on the second syllable.

The mule's ear flicked toward them. Along the thread: *Yes. Obviously. You're being strange again. I've decided to allow it.*

A sound came out of Dominic that was half laugh, half sob, and they buried their face in the rough gray neck and held on. Bramble tolerated this with the magnanimity of the truly superior, and beneath the silk-thread of her presence Dominic felt her heart beating, steady and unimpressed and alive, and thought: *this. This is what I was for. Not the shouting. This.*

Serah had gone very still beside them. Dominic felt her watching — felt it the way you feel sunlight through a window, without needing to look.

"You heard it," Dominic said into Bramble's neck. Not a question.

"I felt it." Serah's voice was careful, the way it was careful when she was handling something that might break — not Dominic, Dominic realized, but the moment. "The wards hummed. All of them, every ward-thread I can still feel, they all just... leaned toward you. Like grass toward sun." A pause. "Dominic. What happened on that throne room floor — when you offered it your voice — I thought it took everything."

"It took the loud part." Dominic lifted their head. Bramble's breath fogged in the cold. "The part that never asked. The part I had to hide, because it was a door that couldn't close." They turned and looked at Serah fully, starlight and firelight on her face, and made the choice the way you make the last step of a long climb — not because it's easy, but because you are done with the mountain. "What's left is quiet. It has to be invited. And I don't—" Their voice caught. They made themself finish. "I don't want to hide it. Not from you. Not anymore. Not ever again."

Serah looked at them for a long moment. Then she did the thing that undid Dominic more completely than the hunger ever had: she reached out and took their hand, deliberately, in full view of the open sky, and laced her fingers through theirs like it was the simplest thing in the world.

"Good," she said. "Because the king's first decree is already causing riots in Merrowgate, and the binding yards are being torn down by the very handlers who ran them, and someone is going to have to teach a whole kingdom how to listen. And I am not doing it alone."

* * *

They came up the ridge one by one, because of course they did. Nobody in this family had ever slept when it mattered.

Kess arrived first, barefoot and wrapped in a blanket, with a crow on each shoulder and a third riding her head like a hat. She had felt the wards hum in her sleep, she announced, and the crows had told her — told her properly, not the half-heard crow-gossip she'd always had to squint at — that something had changed on the ridge. She took one look at Bramble standing over Dominic like a gray monument and her eyes went wide as coins.

"You *talked* to her. I felt it. She said something back." Kess dropped to her knees in the frost, heedless, grinning so wide it looked like it might hurt. "What did she say? What did she say?"

"That you're loud," Dominic said, "and that she allows it."

"I *do* allow it," Kess told the mule solemnly, and the crows on her shoulders bobbed in agreement, and Dominic felt them too now — faint, bright, quicksilver minds like sparks off a flint — and had to close their eyes for a moment, because it was almost too much. Not the flood. The *welcome.*

Tamsin came up with a lantern she didn't need and a pot of something that steamed. She was a big woman built like her own ovens, and she had wept exactly once in all the months Dominic had known her — at Wren's capture, into her apron, where she thought no one saw — and she surveyed the ridge now with the expression of a general assessing a battlefield.

"Right," she said. "If we're all awake, we're all eating. Nobody argues with the cook. That's the law now. I heard it from the king himself."

"You did not," said Old Wren, feeling his way up the path on Kess's arm.

"He'd have said it if I'd asked him."

Wren settled onto the rock beside Serah and turned his blind face toward Dominic, and there was something in it — that terrible, gentle accuracy the man had, seeing everything except with his eyes. "The sky's different tonight," he said. "I can hear it. It used to sound like a held breath. Now it sounds like a room after everyone's stopped arguing." He smiled his dry, folded-paper smile. "And it sounds like you're at the center of it, Dominic Okafor. It sounds like you finally stopped whispering."

"I'm still whispering," Dominic said.

"Yes," Wren agreed. "But out loud."

* * *

They ate Tamsin's stew on the Shepherd's Crown under the whole returned heaven, and it was, objectively, the best thing Dominic had ever tasted, and they told her so, and Tamsin said "obviously" and ladled out more.

Somewhere below, far to the south, the crown city was lit like a fallen constellation — bonfires on the walls, bells ringing in the towers, the whole capital awake on the first starred night in a season. King Aldric had stood on his balcony two days ago, hollow-eyed and upright, and told the truth to ten thousand people: about the hunger, about the binding-stone, about the smuggler with a mule and the disgraced ward-keeper and the deserter and the blind man and the child who had done what armies could not. He had torn the binding laws in half on the palace steps, actually torn them, parchment ripping like a scream ending. And then he had looked directly at the crowd — at the beast-speakers who had spent their lives flinching, at the families of the executed, at the ones who still hid their hands — and said, in a voice that carried to the walls: *Come out. You are wanted. You are welcome. The kingdom was never whole without you.*

Serah had wept at that. Dominic had pretended not to notice, and Serah had pretended not to notice them noticing, and that was its own kind of tenderness.

Now, on the ridge, Kess fell asleep mid-sentence with her head in Tamsin's lap, and the crows folded themselves down around her like a dark feathered blanket. Wren began, quietly, to draw a new map in the air with his ruined hand — not a map of roads, he said, but a map of listeners: every beast-speaker his informants had whispered of, every hidden gift from Thornfell to the coast, a cartography of the silenced. Serah was already planning, because Serah was always already planning — schools, she said, ward-schools that taught listening instead of binding, and someone would have to build them, and someone would have to convince the free cities to fund them, and someone would have to stand up in front of the Merrowgate council, the same council of cowards, and—

"You're volunteering me," Dominic said.

"I'm volunteering *us*," Serah said. "There's a difference. The difference is that I'll be standing next to you."

Bramble stood at Dominic's shoulder, warm as a hearth, and along the fine silk thread between them came her steady, unhurried assent: *Where you go, I go. Obviously. I've decided.*

Dominic looked around the fire — the cook, the map, the child, the mule, the woman whose hand was still laced through theirs — and understood, at last, with a certainty that needed no gift at all, that the thing they had spent a lifetime hiding had never once been the danger. The hiding was the danger. The hiding was the hunger's oldest trick, the small cold voice that said *be nobody, want nothing, stay small and the dark will pass you by.* The dark had not passed them by. The dark had eaten the sky. And the sky came back because a smuggler, a ward-keeper, a cook, a map, a child, and a mule had refused, all together, in their various stubborn ways, to stay small.

Above them, the Wanderer climbed the heavens, bright and unhurried. Dominic tipped their head back and let the starlight fall on their face like rain on ground that had forgotten rain.

"Serah," they said.

"Hm?"

"When we get to Merrowgate. When I stand in front of that council." Dominic squeezed her hand. "I'm going to tell them what I am. Out loud. First thing. Before the arguments start."

Serah turned to look at them, and the firelight and the starlight met in her eyes, and she smiled the smile she'd worn on the throne room floor, the one that meant *yes, whatever it costs, yes.*

"Caerlum," she said softly, using the old word, the smuggler's word, the word for the moment a hidden thing comes up into the open air and finds it can breathe. "Rising."

And on the ridge, in the open, under every star there was, Dominic Okafor — beast-speaker, smuggler, nobody, somebody, theirs — whispered to the mule beside them, out loud, in front of everyone:

"Ready, old girl?"

Bramble's ear flicked. Along the thread, clear as a bell, warm as bread:

*I was born ready. You were the slow one. Walk on.*
