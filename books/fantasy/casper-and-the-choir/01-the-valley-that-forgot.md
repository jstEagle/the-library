## 1. The Valley That Forgot

The mist came down off the ridge before dawn the way it always did, slow and deliberate, like something that had been making this same journey for a hundred years and saw no reason to hurry. Freya Pemberton watched it from the doorway of the gatehouse with a cup of barley tea going cold in her hands, and she thought—not for the first time—that the valley below her was the most beautiful lie she had ever seen.

Vell lay spread out beneath her in the early light: forty farms stitched together by dirt roads, a mill on the river, a chapel with a leaning bell tower, orchards heavy with apples nobody bothered to count. It looked like any valley in the world. That was the point. That had always been the point.

Her grandmother used to say that Vell's greatest achievement wasn't anything it built. It was everything it agreed not to see.

Freya climbed the path to the Hollow Gate while the mist was still deciding what to become. The path was old—older than the chapel, older than the mill, older than any name anyone in the valley still used—and it wound up through birch trees whose bark had gone silver as candle wax. She walked it every morning of her life, or close enough to it that the stones knew her feet. There were three in particular, halfway up, that had worn hollow under generations of Pembrotons, and she stepped into them without looking, the way you step into your own footprints.

The gate stood at the top of the world, or what passed for it here: a great arch of grey stone rising out of the hillside like the rib of something buried. Moss covered it in green velvet, thick enough to swallow light. Ivy had been working at it patiently for decades, threading through cracks, prying at joints with the slow criminal fingers of a plant that had all the time in creation. Wild honeysuckle grew up one side and flowered white in June, and when the wind came off the high country it carried the smell of it down into the valley, where nobody ever asked what it was growing on.

Nobody asked about the gate at all, really. That was the arrangement.

It hadn't opened in a hundred years. It couldn't open. Behind it—if there was a behind it, and Freya had spent twenty-six years being carefully told there wasn't—lay the kingdom of Casper, which had ended, everyone said, the way kingdoms end: war, plague, forgetting. A century ago the crown's maps had shown Casper as a country. Now they showed nothing at all, just blank parchment where the mountains folded over on themselves, and the cartographers' guild called it *unsurveyed* in small apologetic letters, the way you'd label a swamp.

In Vell, they didn't even call it unsurveyed. They called it nothing. They had gotten very good at calling things nothing.

Freya set down her basket at the base of the arch and began the morning work.

The rituals were her grandmother's rituals, and her grandmother's mother's before that, handed down like a recipe with half the ingredients scratched out. First she cleared the moss from the keystone—not all of it, never all of it, just enough to bare the stone beneath—and pressed her palm flat against it until the cold of it climbed her arm. Then she took the brush of horsehair and swept the threshold clean, left to right, always left to right, because her grandmother had once snapped at her for doing it backward and never explained why, and Freya had learned young that in her family the questions you weren't supposed to ask outnumbered the ones you were.

Then the water. Spring water, drawn at dawn, poured along the arch's foundations in a thin silver thread while she spoke the words.

She spoke them now, quietly, her breath fogging in the cold. *What sleeps is not dead. What is closed is not gone. What is forgotten keeps its own counsel.* Nine words, three phrases, three times each. She'd asked her grandmother once what language they were in, because they weren't Vellish and they weren't crown-speech either—they sat somewhere between, vowels rounded wrong, consonants soft as bread. Her grandmother had looked at her for a long moment with an expression Freya had never learned to read, and said only: *They're ours.*

That was all. Ours. As if the words belonged to the family rather than to any country, the way grief belongs to a family, the way secrets do.

Freya finished the pouring and stood back, and the gate did what the gate always did, which was nothing. Stone and moss and ivy and silence. A wren worked the honeysuckle for insects, indifferent to her. Somewhere below, the first smoke rose from the mill chimney, a single grey thread against the pines.

She knelt and pressed her ear to the stone, the last part of the ritual, the part she'd done so long it had stopped feeling strange. Listening to a door. Waiting for a wall to say something.

Nothing said anything. Nothing ever said anything. And every morning, in the space between one heartbeat and the next, Freya felt the same small vertigo she'd felt since she was a child: the vertigo of a woman who spends her life guarding a door that everyone insists is not a door, performing rites for a kingdom everyone insists is not a kingdom, in service of a magic everyone insists does not exist.

She had asked her grandmother about it exactly once, directly, when she was nineteen. Sat across the hearth from her, tea steaming, and said: *If there's nothing behind the gate, why are we here? Why me? Why the words? Why every morning for a hundred years?*

Her grandmother had stirred the fire and taken her time answering, and when she finally did, her voice had been gentle in the way that made Freya's stomach drop, because gentleness in her grandmother was always load-bearing.

*Because the valley forgets, love,* she said. *Someone has to remember for it.*

*Remember what?*

And the old woman had smiled—the saddest smile Freya had ever seen on a face that smiled often—and said, *Ask me again when I'm dying. I promise I'll answer.*

Which was, Freya would think later, the most honest thing anyone in Vell had said aloud in a hundred years.

* * *

By midmorning the mist had burned off and the valley was going about its business of not knowing things.

Freya went down for market day, as she did every seventh day, carrying eggs and a wheel of last summer's cheese wrapped in cloth. The road into the village ran beside the river, and the river ran loud and clear over stones the color of iron, and everything about it was ordinary, deliberately, expensively ordinary.

That was the thing about Vell that outsiders never understood, if outsiders ever came, which they almost never did. The ordinariness wasn't natural. It was maintained. It cost something. Freya could feel the cost every time she climbed to the gate—a faint drag on the air, a heaviness in the birches, a sense that the whole valley was holding its breath and had been holding it for so long that no one remembered breath was meant to move.

Magic didn't work in Vell. Everyone knew that the way everyone knows the sun rises. Candles burned straight, dowsing rods spun uselessly, charms sold at the autumn fair were tin painted to look like silver, and the one time a peddler had come through claiming he could find water with a forked stick, old Marta Hollis had laughed him off the green and the whole village had laughed with her, warm and easy and united, and Freya had watched them laugh and felt cold all the way down her spine.

Because she knew what the laughter was for. It was a wall. They had all built it together, brick by brick, generation by generation, without ever once saying so out loud. You didn't talk about the gate. You didn't wonder about the blank place on the maps. You didn't hum certain old songs, the ones with the strange round vowels, the ones Grandmother sang sometimes when she thought Freya was asleep—songs that had no names and no verses anyone would admit to knowing. You farmed. You married. You buried your dead. You kept the valley's silence the way you kept a roof: constantly, without comment, because the alternative was rain coming in.

The strange thing—the thing Freya had understood only slowly, the way you understand water is wet—was that the silence wasn't imposed on the valley. It was chosen. Chosen by people who had long since forgotten they'd chosen it, which was the safest kind of choosing there was. Her neighbors were not liars. They were something sadder and more durable than liars: they were believers in their own forgetting. Ask Tam Weaver why his grandfather's field had a ring of standing stones in the corner and he'd say, cheerfully, *old boundary markers, probably*, and believe it completely, and sleep well, and never once feel the itch of the question underneath.

Freya felt the itch. She'd felt it all her life. It was the family curse, maybe—the Pemberton inheritance wasn't the gate itself but the inability to stop looking at it.

At the market she traded her eggs to the Hendersons and her cheese to the widow Crane, and heard the news, which was no news: a good apple year, the mill race running low, someone's cousin married in the next valley over. Talk moved through the square like water through a channel, smooth and shallow, never pooling anywhere deep. Nobody mentioned the crown. Nobody ever mentioned the crown. The king's name came up at funerals and tax days and otherwise lived in the same drawer as the gate: closed, unexamined, fine.

But there was a new herald expected soon, the widow Crane said, folding Freya's cheese into her basket. Crown business. A proclamation. Something about edicts.

*Edicts?* Freya asked, and the word sat strangely in her mouth, formal and cold, a word from the other side of the mountains, a word that had never had any business in Vell.

*Oh, who knows,* the widow said comfortably. *Taxes, probably. Or roads. Something dull. These things always are.*

Everyone within earshot nodded. Of course. Dull. Everything from outside was dull; that was another brick in the wall. Freya nodded too, because nodding was survival, and carried her empty basket home up the hill with the itch under her skin louder than it had been in years.

Something was coming. She didn't have a word for how she knew. It wasn't magic—she'd never been sure she had any, whatever *any* meant in a valley where none was allowed. It was older than magic, maybe. It was the animal knowledge of a woman who has spent her whole life listening to a wall, and has just heard, in the deep quiet of it, a shift of weight.

* * *

Evening came down blue and cold. Freya banked the gatehouse fire, fed the hens, ate bread and honey standing up the way she always did when her grandmother wasn't there to disapprove. Her grandmother was three weeks gone now, buried under the birches on the path, and the gatehouse had the particular silence of a house that has lost its oldest voice. Freya kept hearing echoes of it anyway—in the creak of the third stair, in the way the kettle needed lifting with a cloth on the left handle only—and she had decided, without quite deciding, to let the echoes stay as long as they wanted.

After dark she went up to the gate one more time. This wasn't ritual. Ritual was morning. This was the other thing, the thing she'd done since childhood and never named: the night visit, the checking, the laying of her palm against the keystone in the dark just to confirm the world was still shaped the way she'd been told it was.

Frost was forming on the moss. The stars were hard and close. The valley below was a scatter of embers—two, three lights, the mill, the chapel, nothing else—and beyond the valley the crown's lands stretched away into darkness toward a capital she had never seen and had been taught, gently, thoroughly, not to think about.

She put her hand on the stone. Cold, damp, solid. A wall.

And then, from somewhere down the valley—a long way down, near the river, near the mill—someone sang.

One note. That was all. A single note, rising out of the dark, unhurried, unhoused, the kind of note a person gives voice to without thinking, washing a dish or walking a fence line or standing at a window unable to sleep. It might have been nothing. In any other valley in the world it would have been nothing.

But it was one of the notes. One of the round-voweled, nameless notes, the ones from the songs with no names, the ones her grandmother sang past midnight like a woman whispering to something in her sleep. Freya knew it instantly, the way you know your own name spoken by a stranger in a crowd. It climbed the hill through the frost-dark and broke against the arch—

—and the gate hummed.

Not loudly. Not dramatically. If she had been two steps further away she'd have missed it entirely. But under her palm, deep in the stone, in the bones of the hill, something answered the note. A vibration, low and vast and patient, like a bell struck so gently you feel it rather than hear it, like the purr of something enormous sleeping in a room you were told was empty. It moved up through her hand, through her wrist, into her chest, and settled there, resonant, unmistakable, real.

For the first time in her twenty-six years—for the first time, she suspected, in longer than that—the Hollow Gate had spoken back.

Freya stood absolutely still, afraid that moving would end it, the way waking ends a dream. The note from the valley faded. Whoever had sung it went on with their evening, unaware, and the hum thinned, and softened, and sank back down into the stone until it was gone, or seemed gone, or was waiting.

She stayed until her fingers went numb against the keystone. Then she walked back down the path in the dark without a lantern, faster than was wise, stepping into her grandmother's hollowed stones and her great-grandmother's before those, and inside the gatehouse she lit every candle she owned and sat at the table with her hands flat on the wood, breathing.

A wall doesn't hum. She knew that the way she knew the sun rose. Whatever slept behind the arch was not dead, whatever was closed was not gone, whatever was forgotten kept its own counsel—and tonight, for the first time, it had kept its counsel a little too loudly, the way a sleeper turns over, the way a house settles, the way something old and tired shifts its weight after a hundred years of stillness because it has begun, at last, to fail.

*Fail.* The word arrived in her mind uninvited and refused to leave. She turned it over and found it fit. All her life the gate had felt less like a fortress than like an old woman standing in a doorway—arms braced, back aching, holding shut something enormous through sheer habit and stubbornness. Her grandmother had held it. Her mother, briefly, before the fever. And now her, alone, with a horsehair brush and nine words in a language no one would claim and spring water and a palm pressed flat to the cold.

Old things don't hold forever. Old songs don't stay forgotten. And doors that have been shut for a hundred years, Freya thought, staring into the candles, do not hum unless something on one side of them—or both—has started to change.

Outside, the frost thickened on the honeysuckle. Down in the valley, the widow Crane's proclamation was four days out, though no one knew it yet, and somewhere beyond the mountains a man was walking toward Vell with a country hidden in his throat, and an Inquisitor was reading a list of names by lamplight, and one of the names on that list, though the ink had dried years ago and the trail had gone cold as the gate itself, was *Pemberton*.

Freya blew out the candles one by one and lay awake in the dark, her hand still tingling, listening to the silence of the hill.

The gate did not hum again.

But it did not, quite, go back to sleep.
