## 2. Proclamation at the Mill

The herald came up the valley road on a gray horse with a crown stamped on its saddlecloth, and the whole of Vell turned out to watch him as if he were a traveling fair.

That was the first wrongness, though Freya didn't know it yet. Crowns did not send heralds to Vell. The king's men had come twice in her lifetime — once to count chimneys for a new tax, once when a shepherd's boy went missing in the high passes and was found three days later asleep in his own bed with no memory of where he'd been. Both times the riders had looked at the valley the way men look at a field that isn't worth plowing: politely, briefly, and already turning away.

This time they had sent a herald in livery the color of wet slate, with two soldiers behind him and a clerk who carried a scroll case bound in black leather, and they did not turn away at all.

* * *

It was market day, or near enough. The mill stood at the valley's narrow waist where the river ran fast and shallow over white stones, and its yard filled every week with carts and trestle tables and the smell of fresh-cut grain and horse dung and frying onions. Freya had walked down from the gatehouse that morning with her grandmother's list in her pocket — salt, lamp oil, a new whetstone, thread the color of moss — and she had been standing at the dry-goods stall arguing the price of the oil down by a copper when the bell above the mill door began to ring.

Not the mill's bell. A handbell, small and bright and utterly out of place, and the sound of it went through the market like a knife through cloth. Conversations stopped mid-word. A child laughing by the river went silent so completely that her mother grabbed her and pulled her close as if the laugh itself might be held against them later.

The herald climbed onto the loading step of the mill, unrolled his scroll, and waited. He was young, Freya saw. Younger than her. He had the kind of face that hadn't decided yet what it believed, and hands that were too clean for the road, and he read in a voice trained to carry across courtyards:

"By decree of the Crown, in the fourth year of the Regency, and by authority vested in the Office of Inquiry—" He paused there, and something crossed his face, quickly, like a bird past a window. "—the Edict of Cleansing is proclaimed throughout the realm."

The word *Cleansing* landed on the market yard and sat there. Nobody picked it up.

"Let all persons gifted beyond nature present themselves to the officers of the Inquisition within thirty days of this reading. Let all persons harboring such persons make known their names. Let no village, hold, or household conceal what belongs to the Crown." He swallowed. "Those who surrender freely shall be treated with mercy. Those who are taken shall not."

He rolled the scroll with a snap. The silence afterward was enormous. Somewhere downstream a heron cried, absurdly ordinary, and everyone flinched.

Then the herald said, quieter, off-script, almost human: "There will be a second reading at dusk. And — officers will remain in the valley through the month." He stepped down, and his boots hit the packed earth of the yard, and that sound broke whatever spell the silence had cast, because the market came back to life all at once, too loud, too fast, everyone talking at once about anything except what they'd just heard.

Freya stood very still at the dry-goods stall with the bottle of lamp oil in her hand and did not buy it after all.

* * *

What struck her first was not fear. It was watching.

She had grown up invisible, and she had thought invisibility was a fact, the way weather is a fact. The Pembrotons kept the gate; nobody much asked why, or how the gatekeeping family ate in a valley where nobody traded with them above courtesy. It had always been like that. She had assumed it was like that everywhere, that every village had its quiet house on the hill, its family set slightly apart, and that the apartness was simply the shape of things.

But now she watched the widow Crane look at her across the yard — really look, for the first time in years, eyes narrowing, mouth doing a small arithmetic — and she understood that the invisibility had never been hers. It had been *given*. Maintained. An agreement signed by every soul in Vell without any of them ever saying so aloud: we do not see the Pembertons, and in exchange we are not made to see them, and nothing in our valley burns.

And the agreement had just been torn up by a boy on a loading step with a handbell.

Old Tamsin caught her elbow at the edge of the yard. Tamsin was eighty and sold eggs and had buried three husbands and feared nothing except deep water, but her grip shook. "Go home, girl," she murmured, not looking at her, smiling at someone over her shoulder as if they were chatting about onions. "Go home now, and don't run. Walk."

"I haven't done anything," Freya said. Her voice sounded very young.

"No," Tamsin said. "That's not how it works, though, is it. Go on."

Freya walked. She counted her steps without meaning to. Behind her the market noise rose and fell and rose again, and beneath it she could feel — not hear, *feel*, the way you feel a change in pressure before a storm — the attention of the valley rearranging itself around her back. Not hatred. Nothing so simple. Something worse: consideration. Forty people weighing a thing they'd never had to weigh before, the way a man weighs whether to sell a field he never expected anyone to want.

At the bend below the gatehouse path she looked back once. The herald's gray horse stood tethered by the mill. Two soldiers were going door to door with the clerk, and the clerk was writing names, and people were opening their doors, because what else could they do?

* * *

Her grandmother had the letters out of the floor before dark.

Freya came through the gatehouse door and knew instantly that something had changed — the house smelled of smoke though no fire was lit upstairs, and Merrin Pemberton was kneeling at the big hearth in the keeping room with the flagstone beside the chimney levered up, and spread around her on the floor was a lifetime of paper.

Letters. Bundles of letters, tied with string gone brittle, wrapped in oilcloth, stacked in a cavity under the hearthstone that Freya had walked over ten thousand times and never once suspected. Some were old enough that the ink had faded to the color of weak tea. Some were recent — she could see her own name on one, and her mother's on others, and a seal she didn't recognize, broken wax stamped with an arch and a star.

"You're home early," her grandmother said, without turning around. "Did you get the oil?"

"The herald came."

"I know." Merrin fed the first bundle into the cold hearth, then another, methodically, a woman laying a fire in reverse. "Help me or don't, but shut the door first."

Freya shut the door. Then she knelt down in the paper. "Grandmother. What is all this?"

"Correspondence." Merrin struck flint to steel and touched the flame to the oldest bundle, and the paper caught with a soft rush, and light jumped up the chimney. "Forty years of it. More. Letters from the capital, letters from other gate-keepers — yes, there were others, don't look at me like that, you knew there must have been — letters between your mother and a woman in Casper's shadow, God rest her, letters that say things which, read aloud in front of a soldier, would have us both on a cart inside a week."

"You never told me any of this existed."

"There was never a reason to." Her grandmother's face was lit orange from below, and in that light Freya could see how old she actually was, which she never let anyone see. "A secret you tell becomes a burden you share. I wanted you light, girl. Light travels faster."

"That's not fair."

"No," Merrin agreed placidly. "Very little of it is." She passed another stack into the flames. "Hand me the ones in the blue oilcloth. No — not those. Those burn last."

"Why last?"

"Because those are the only ones worth memorizing before they go." She pulled the blue-wrapped bundle onto her lap and untied it with fingers that trembled only slightly, and Freya saw that her grandmother's eyes were wet, and that she was burning these papers the way some women pray: steadily, and without hope of being understood.

"Tell me what's happening," Freya said. "Truly happening. You've known the edict was coming. I watched your face today — well, I didn't, I wasn't home, but you're not surprised, and Tamsin gripped my arm like the world was ending, and you're *burning letters* instead of panicking. You've been waiting for this."

Merrin was quiet a long moment. The fire crackled. Outside, the wind moved through the honeysuckle, and far off, at the top of the hill behind the house, Freya felt the gate shift in its sleep — a sensation like a floor settling in an old house, familiar and suddenly ominous.

"When your mother died," Merrin said at last, "she made me promise two things. That I would teach you the gate-keeping, because it must be taught and cannot be written. And that I would never teach you the rest until the rest arrived on our doorstep." She looked up. "Well. It's on the doorstep."

"What rest?"

"The truth about the ward. About why Vell has no magic in it, and never has, and why that's the only reason any of us are still alive." She fed the blue bundle toward the fire, then stopped, holding it half over the coals. "Thirty days, the boy said. We may not have thirty days. Officers stay through the month, he said, which means they'll be walking that hill inside a fortnight, and when they walk it they'll find exactly what the edict says they should find: nothing. A valley empty of the gift. Because that is what this valley is, Freya. Empty. On purpose. Every soul born here since before your great-grandmother was born has been born *blank*, warded blank, and the ward is older than the kingdom that built it, and it is the single greatest work of hiding ever done in this country, and it is failing."

The words went into Freya like cold water. "Failing how? Failing since when? Why didn't you—"

"Yesterday," Merrin said. "Or the day before. When the gate hummed."

The room went very still. The fire popped.

"You heard it," Freya whispered.

"I've heard it hum four times in my life. Three of them meant nothing — wind in the arch, a stag rubbing the stone. The fourth was the night your mother died." Her grandmother drew the blue bundle back from the fire and held it against her chest like a child. "This one means the ward is thinning. Which means the gate is waking. Which means somewhere, something on the other side wants *through* — or something on this side has stopped being able to hold it shut." She met Freya's eyes. "Which means, my love, that everything I promised your mother I'd keep from you has just arrived on our doorstep, exactly as she predicted, wearing a crown's colors and ringing a little bell."

Freya sat back on her heels among the ashes and the burning paper and felt the careful architecture of her life — gate-tending and market lists and a valley that politely did not see her — settle and slide, like snow off a roof.

"And the letters?" she managed. "Why burn them at all? If the ward hides us—"

"The ward hides the gift. It does not hide paper." Merrin smiled, and it was a terrible smile, tired and fond at once. "Inquisitors can't find what isn't in a person. They can read anything left lying in a house. Your mother's handwriting is in these letters, and yours is in some of the replies, and a name repeated often enough becomes a trail even through a perfect ward." She tipped the blue bundle toward the flames at last, then paused. "Last chance to ask me anything, girl. Once these burn, you learn it all the slow way, from scratch, and the slow way is longer than thirty days."

Freya looked at the papers. At the arch-and-star seals. At her own name in her own younger handwriting.

"Who was the woman in Casper's shadow?" she asked. "The one Mama wrote to?"

Her grandmother's hand stopped over the fire.

"That," she said quietly, "is the right question. And it's the one I hoped you wouldn't ask, because the answer takes all night and we may not have one." She fed the bundle into the flames anyway, and the blue oilcloth went up in a bloom of green-white fire that was not, Freya noticed, entirely natural, and the letters inside curled and blackened, and the name of the woman in Casper's shadow was gone before Merrin could decide whether to speak it.

Down in the valley, faint on the wind, the herald's handbell rang the second reading.

Merrin closed her eyes. "They'll do the rounds tomorrow," she said. "House to house, taking names of anyone who's acted strange, anyone with old fevers, anyone whose grandmother burned papers in her hearth on proclamation day." She opened her eyes and looked at her granddaughter across the dying fire, and for the first time in Freya's memory, Merrin Pemberton looked afraid.

"So tomorrow," she said, "we are going to be the most boring women in Vell."
