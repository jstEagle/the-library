## 3. What Grandmother Buried

The letters burned for three days.

Freya knew because she was the one who fed them to the hearth, one folded packet at a time, while her grandmother sat in the chair by the window and watched the smoke with an expression Freya had never learned to read. Not grief, exactly. Something more like accounting. As if each letter were a coin being counted out of a purse that had never been full enough.

"Read them first," Maren Pemberton had said on the first evening, after the herald's voice had faded from the mill square and the valley had gone quiet in that particular way—everyone indoors, everyone pretending not to look at their neighbors' windows. "You should know what I'm asking you to burn."

So Freya read them.

They were old. The paper had gone soft and yellow at the folds, and the ink had faded to the color of weak tea. Most were written in a hand Freya recognized from the ledgers downstairs—the same slanted, careful script her grandmother still used when she tallied what the gate-tending cost against what the orchard earned. Letters about ward-stones and boundary-lines. Letters about a woman named Ilse who had died before Freya was born, and who signed herself *Keeper of the Hollow Gate* in a way that made it sound like a title passed down rather than a job performed. Letters about a child—a girl—with a birthdate that made Freya's stomach drop when she did the arithmetic.

The girl had been born inside the ward. The girl had been born *to* the ward. And one letter, older than all the others, written in a different hand entirely—sharper, faster, the ink pressed hard enough to tear the paper in places—said only:

*The valley forgets because we make it forget. The valley is safe because we make it safe. But the making costs, Maren, and the cost is not paid once. It is paid every year of the child's life, whether you spend it or no.*

Freya had read that one three times. Then she had fed it to the fire, because her grandmother asked her to, and because some part of her understood already that keeping things was how this family got hurt.

By the third evening, the hearth held nothing but ash and the smell of scorched paper, and her grandmother had stopped getting up from the chair.

* * *

She died the way she had lived: inconveniently, in the middle of something else.

It was raining. Freya remembered that afterward, with the strange precision of memory under shock—that the rain came down in grey sheets past the window, that the gutters gurgled like something drowning, that the fire had been laid but not lit because her grandmother had waved off matches all week as if warmth were another thing she'd decided to refuse. The house smelled of cold ash and dried lavender and the particular staleness of a room where someone has been dying for days and everyone has agreed not to say so.

Maren Pemberton had not left the chair since the last letter burned. She had eaten almost nothing. She had slept in snatches, waking with her jaw set as if she'd been arguing with someone in her dreams and losing. And now, on the fourth night, she said, in a voice scraped thin but perfectly clear:

"Sit down, Freya."

"I'll light the fire—"

"Sit *down.*"

Freya sat. The chair across from her grandmother was hard and straight-backed, and it had always been the listening chair, the confessing chair, the chair where you sat when you'd broken something or lied about something or come home with blood on your hands. Freya had spent her whole childhood learning to sit in it without fidgeting. She sat in it now and felt six years old and forty years old at the same time.

Her grandmother looked at her for a long moment. Rain ticked against the glass. Somewhere down the lane, a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"You burned them," Maren said. "All of them?"

"All of them."

"Did you read them?"

"...Yes."

Her grandmother nodded slowly, as if ticking something off a list. "Then you know there was a girl born to the ward."

"I know there are letters about a girl." Freya kept her voice level. It took effort. "I know the handwriting changes. I know someone named Ilse was keeper before you. I know—" and here her voice did crack, just slightly, "—I know whatever you've been doing my whole life, it wasn't tending moss on an arch."

"No," her grandmother said. "It wasn't."

The fire stayed unlit. Her grandmother's hands lay folded in her lap, and Freya saw—for the first time, though she would wonder forever why she'd never seen it before—that they were marked. Old scars, silver-white, running across both palms in lines too straight and deliberate to be accidents. Lines that looked almost like writing.

"The valley doesn't forget," Maren said. "The valley is *made* to forget. Do you understand the difference?"

"No."

"Neither did I, at your age." Her grandmother turned her head toward the window. Rain crawled down the panes. "There's a seam in the world, Freya. You've swept its doorstep your whole life. On one side is Vell—our valley, our mud, our mill, our people who can't so much as light a candle with a wish because they haven't a wish in them. On the other side is Casper."

"Casper's gone."

"Casper is *sealed.*" The word came out with force, and then her grandmother coughed, long and wet, and Freya was halfway out of her chair before the thin hand caught her wrist. "Sealed. A century ago, when the crown started burning the gifted out of the world, the kingdom closed its own door. Sealed itself away behind the gate you sweep. Every gifted soul in this country went through that arch, and the arch shut behind them, and the price of the sealing was—" her grip tightened, "—was forgetting. Everyone on this side forgot magic was ever real. Forgot Casper was ever real. The ward makes the valley dreamless. Makes it safe. The crown can't hunt what nobody remembers existing."

"And the cost?" Freya said. Her mouth was dry. "You said the cost is paid every year."

Her grandmother looked at her then, really looked, with those pale eyes that had seemed merely sharp for twenty years and now seemed ancient.

"The ward needs an anchor," she said. "Something living, tied into the boundary-line, holding the forgetting in place the way a keystone holds an arch. Ilse anchored it first. When she died, I anchored it. And when I die—"

"No." Freya heard herself say it flatly, stupidly, the word falling out of her like something dropped. "No. I'm not—I don't have anything, I'm not gifted, I've never done anything, I sweep moss—"

"You're a Pemberton." Her grandmother's voice gentled, which was worse. "You were born inside the ward, to the keeper's line, on the night of the turning of the year. The ward chose its anchor before you could walk. Why do you think nothing has ever happened to you? No fevers, no accidents, no strange dreams. Why do you think you've never wanted anything badly enough to leave?" The pale eyes didn't waver. "It isn't contentment, child. It's the ward. It holds you the way it holds everything else. Flat. Quiet. Safe."

Freya stood up. She didn't remember deciding to. Her legs simply moved, and she found herself at the window with her palms flat against the cold glass, watching rain drown the lane, and her reflection looked back at her like a stranger wearing her face.

"That's not true," she said to the glass.

"It's the truest thing anyone will ever tell you."

"I have wanted things." Her breath fogged the pane. "I want—"

"What?" her grandmother asked softly. "Name one thing. One thing you've ever wanted past the edge of this valley. One road you've ever itched to walk. One song you've ever hummed that wasn't one of ours."

And Freya opened her mouth, and nothing came out.

Twenty-two years of life, and the sum of her wanting fit inside the valley walls. She had never once dreamed of the capital. She had never wondered, with any real hunger, what lay over the eastern ridge. She had been *content*, the way a stone is content, the way a fencepost is content, and she had called it a temperament. Called it being practical. Called it being like her grandmother.

Like her grandmother. Who had anchored a ward with her own life for fifty years and called that a temperament too.

Freya turned around. "How long do I have? If you die tonight—if the anchor passes to me—how long before I'm what you are?"

"You already are what I am." Maren's voice was very quiet now. "You have been since the night you were born. The only difference between us is that I knew, and you didn't. That was Ilse's advice. She said a keeper who knows she's a key spends her whole life grieving. She said let the girl be happy for as long as the world allows." A pause. The rain filled it. "Ilse was wrong about most things, but I think she might have been wrong about that worst of all."

"Why are you telling me now?"

"Because the ward is failing."

The words landed in the room like a stone dropped down a well. Freya waited for the splash and heard none.

"Failing how?" she managed.

"A century is a long time for anything to hold. The seal was built by desperate people in a hurry, and it's been fraying since before I took the post. Forgetting isn't perfect anymore—you've seen it. Odd travelers who remember too much. Songs in the village that nobody knows where they came from." Freya thought of the note that had made the gate hum, three weeks ago, and said nothing. "And now the crown has remembered. Whatever broke loose in the capital, whatever made them write their Edict—it broke loose because the forgetting is leaking. And when Inquisitors start hunting the gifted, they follow leaks. They'll find the valley, Freya. Maybe not this year. But soon. And they'll find a ward held together by one dying woman and one girl who doesn't know the songs."

"I don't know the songs," Freya repeated. It came out like an accusation.

"No." Her grandmother reached into the folds of her shawl. "But you will."

What she brought out was small enough to close in a fist, and it caught the firelight—even unlit, even in a dark room, it seemed to hold a light of its own—and it was white. Not silver, not bone: white, the white of deep snow at dusk, of moonlight through ice. A tuning fork. Two prongs, worn smooth, mounted on a short handle wrapped in leather gone black with a century of handling.

Freya took it because her hand simply extended and took it, the way hands do, before the mind catches up.

It hummed the moment her skin touched it.

Not loudly. Not even audibly, really—she felt it more than heard it, a fine trembling in her palm bones, up the bones of her wrist, settling somewhere beneath her breastbone like a second heartbeat finding its rhythm. The room seemed to lean toward it. The rain on the glass fell, for one impossible moment, in silence.

"White iron," her grandmother said. "Smelted before the sealing, from ore that grew in Casper's mountains. There were twelve forks once, one for each gate-song. This is the last. It answers blood. It won't sing for an Inquisitor, no matter how they threaten it. It sings for us." Her mouth twisted—not quite a smile. "It sang for Ilse. It sang for me. Now it will sing for you, and when you hold it to the arch and strike it, you'll hear the first of the songs, and you'll understand everything I've failed to teach you in one unbearable moment, and I'm sorry for that, I truly am, but there's no time left to be gentle."

"Grandmother—"

"Maren." Her grandmother's voice cracked, and for the first time in Freya's entire life, Maren Pemberton looked small. Old beyond her years, small in her chair, a woman at the end of a long arithmetic that had never once come out fair. "Call me Maren. If we're going to be honest with each other at last, let's be honest all the way down. I watched you your whole life and loved you your whole life and used you your whole life, and I never told you, because telling you meant taking your happiness away, and I was a coward, and I was tired, and I told myself it was mercy. It wasn't mercy. It was theft. I stole your knowing from you, the same way the ward steals the valley's remembering, and I am sorry, and sorry is worth nothing, and I am saying it anyway."

The rain fell. The dog down the lane had given up entirely. Freya stood in her own kitchen holding a piece of a dead country that trembled against her heart like a living thing, and she found that her face was wet, and she had no memory of starting to cry.

"I hate you," she said. It came out broken. "I hate you and you're dying and I hate you."

"I know." Maren closed her eyes. "Good. Hate means you finally want something. Hold onto that. Wanting is the one thing the ward can't flatten, if you feed it."

"Feed it what?"

"Anger. Grief. Love, if you're brave enough." Her breathing was shallower now, and Freya saw that they were not going to have long. "Listen to me. Listen. The Inquisitors are coming—I've felt them in the ward-line for a fortnight, like rats in a wall. When they come, the gate will open for you and no one else. It will open *wrong*—it hasn't been opened in a hundred years, and it's sick, and crossing it will be the worst thing that's ever happened to you. Cross anyway. Find the choir on the other side. There are singers in Casper who never stopped singing, and they know what the fork is, and they know what you are. Trust the ones who sing. Trust no one who carries a sword, and trust least of all anyone from the crown, even if she weeps, even if she was your mother's friend—" her breath hitched, "—even if she says she's sorry. Sorry is worth nothing. I taught you that tonight myself."

"Mother's friend," Freya whispered. "What does that—"

"And Freya." Her grandmother's eyes opened, and they were clear, terribly clear, fixed on Freya's face as if memorizing it. "When the time comes—and it will come, sooner than either of us wants—the door will ask you for something. Doors always ask. It will offer you choices, and every choice it offers will be a lie dressed as arithmetic. Remember this: the ward was never fed by deaths. It was fed by names. Ilse knew. I forgot, near the end. Don't you forget."

"Fed by names," Freya said. "I don't understand."

"You will." Maren Pemberton smiled then, small and crooked and exactly the smile Freya had known all her life, the smile that meant *we'll manage, we always manage,* and it broke Freya's heart more cleanly than anything else that night. "Strike the fork at dawn. At the arch. Sing what comes back to you. And live, girl. Live past the edge of this valley. It's the one thing I never managed, and the one thing I always—"

She didn't finish.

She didn't need to. The sentence completed itself in the silence after, in the rain and the cold hearth and the humming weight in Freya's fist, and Freya sat down on the floor beside the chair and held her grandmother's scarred hand and did not move for a long time, while outside the storm worked itself out over the valley that had forgotten how to remember anything at all.

* * *

Dawn came grey and rinsed-clean, the way mornings do after storms, and Freya walked up the hill to the gate with the tuning fork in her pocket and mud on her boots and her grandmother's body laid out decently on the bed behind locked doors.

She had washed. She had dressed. She had done all the ordinary things that prove a person is still a person, and she had cried exactly once, briefly, over the washbasin, and then stopped, because her grandmother had told her to feed the wanting and not the weeping, and she intended to obey for once in her life.

The arch stood where it had always stood, half-swallowed by moss, older than the village, older than the name Vell. Freya put her hand against the stone. It was warm. It had never been warm before.

"Well," she said aloud, to the arch, to the valley, to the dead woman back at the house. "Let's find out what I am."

She drew out the fork. Held it up. Struck it against the archstone the way you'd strike a bell—

—and the world rang.

Sound poured out of the stone, out of the fork, out of Freya's own chest, a chord vast and low and mournful, a chord like a cathedral exhaling, and the moss on the arch began to glow faintly silver, and far below in the valley dogs began to howl and lamps flickered in windows and somewhere a bell tower woke and tolled once, confusedly, as if it had forgotten why. And threaded through the great chord, rising like a thread of gold through grey wool, came a melody. A song. The first of the songs.

Freya knew it.

That was the terrible part. She had never heard it in her life, and she knew it—knew where it went, knew how the second phrase answered the first, knew it the way you know your own heartbeat, and she understood, standing in the ringing dawn with tears freezing on her cheeks, that this knowledge had been sitting in her blood her entire life, waiting, patient as a seed, for someone to finally strike the iron.

The gate hummed. Louder than before. Louder than it had ever hummed.

And from somewhere on the far side of the arch—from the other side of a door that had not opened in a hundred years—something answered.
