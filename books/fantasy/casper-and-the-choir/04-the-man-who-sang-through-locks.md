## 4. The Man Who Sang Through Locks

The lock on the granary door was a good one—crown-forged, triple-bitted, blessed twice by a licensed priest—and it opened for Aldric like a child unclenching its fist.

He hadn't touched it. He never touched them anymore. He simply stood in the cold before the door with his collar up and the sleet needling his neck, and he sang three notes under his breath, low, almost conversational, the way a man might hum while thinking of something else. The notes were old. Older than the crown that had outlawed them. They were shaped like a key that no longer existed, cut for a lock no smith had made in a hundred years.

The bolt slid back with a sound like a sigh.

Aldric slipped inside and let the door swing shut behind him, and only then did he allow himself to lean against it and breathe. His breath came out ragged. It always did now. Three years of running had taught his lungs to be economical, but there was no economy left in him tonight; he'd sung through four locks and one ward-stone since dusk, and each time it cost more than the last, as if the songs themselves were wearing thin, as if he were drawing on some account that was nearly empty.

*You are carrying a country in your throat,* his teacher had told him once, back when the conservatory still stood and the word *choir-master* still meant something other than *traitor*. *Every voice is a vessel, boy, but yours was built to hold more than itself.*

His teacher had been burned in the square outside that same conservatory eleven months later, along with forty-one other singers, and Aldric had learned what else a throat could hold: ash. A great deal of ash.

He crossed the granary floor by feel. Farmers here in the Marchlands stacked their sacks against the north wall and hung their tools from pegs they could find blind drunk, so he trusted his hands and kept his eyes shut, saving them. In the corner behind the seed barrels there was a gap—a hollow where the wall's stones had settled away from the timber frame—and into that gap, three nights ago, a boy named Tam had stuffed a bundle wrapped in oilcloth.

Tam was twelve. Tam was gifted. Tam's mother had heard the heralds reading the Edict of Cleansing in the market square at Duncarrie and had done the arithmetic every gifted family in Vell was doing now: *surrender my child to the Inquisition, or find someone who can make him disappear.* And there was exactly one person left in the world who could make a gifted child disappear, because he had spent three years becoming very, very good at it.

Aldric knelt. The bundle was still there. Inside it, when he unwrapped the oilcloth with hands that shook from cold or fear—he'd stopped telling the difference—the boy slept on, breathing slow and deep, drugged with valerian and poppy syrup. Small face. Chapped lips. A smudge of soot on one cheekbone that his mother hadn't managed to scrub away before handing him over to a stranger in the dark.

"There you are," Aldric whispered. "There you are, then."

He gathered the boy up. Forty pounds of sleeping child, and Aldric felt every ounce of it in his knees. He was thirty-four years old and he moved like an old man, and he knew precisely why: because for three years he had been walking out of the capital carrying something heavier than any child. He'd been carrying Casper.

Not the valley—that valley down south with its gate and its gatekeeper, the place the smugglers' whispers pointed to like a compass needle. Not the kingdom either, not really, because kingdoms were borders and taxes and thrones, and Casper's throne had been a chair in a burning room a century ago. He meant the other thing. The thing that lived in the songs.

Casper had been a country once, everyone agreed on that. Then the first purge came, the Purge of Ashes, and by every official history Casper had simply—ceased. Not fallen. Not conquered. *Ceased*, the way a note ceases when a hand is laid flat across the strings. The crown's historians wrote that the kingdom's people had scattered and assimilated, that its towers had crumbled from neglect, that its famous choirs had dispersed into ordinary villages and forgotten themselves within a generation. Tidy. Final. The kind of ending that closes a book.

Aldric knew better, because Aldric had been trained in the last school that still taught the true version, and the true version was this: Casper hadn't died. Casper had been *put away*. Folded up behind a seam in the world, sealed by its own magicians at the moment of greatest grief, hidden the way a mother hides a child under the floorboards while soldiers kick through the house. Hidden so well that even the hiding was forgotten. Hidden so completely that only one door remained, and one family of keepers, and one bloodline of keys.

And now the crown had started hunting the gifted again, which meant the crown remembered—or worse, the crown had *never forgotten*, and everything Aldric had believed about the Edict of Cleansing being mere fanaticism was wrong in a way that turned his stomach.

That was the thought he couldn't put down. It followed him through the dark like a second set of footsteps. *They're not purging. They're searching.* Every gifted child dragged from a village, every singer burned in a square—it wasn't fear driving the Inquisition. It was excavation. They were digging through the population of Vell the way you dig through a drawer, looking for a key.

And somewhere south, behind a moss-eaten arch in a valley full of people who had agreed not to notice magic, the last key was living a small quiet life and probably didn't know it.

Aldric shifted the boy higher on his shoulder and began to move.

* * *

He walked all night. That was normal. Sleep was a currency he'd stopped spending except in installments of twenty minutes, sitting upright, back to a wall, knife loose in his belt. The Marchlands blurred past in the sleet—bare hedgerows, frozen ruts, the occasional farmhouse with its windows shuttered tight against more than weather. Everyone had heard the heralds by now. Everyone had learned the new posture: head down, eyes anywhere but on strangers, doors barred at dusk. A whole country learning to flinch in unison.

Toward dawn the sleet turned to proper snow, fat and silent, and Aldric allowed himself a fire in a hollow beneath a leaning beech—small, screened, the kind of fire a careful man permits himself because a freezing man makes mistakes. He fed the boy broth from the skin in his pack, tipping it between chapped lips until those eyelids fluttered.

"Where—" Tam's voice was a croak.

"Somewhere safe."

"Are you taking me to the valley?" The boy's eyes went wide in the firelight. Even drugged, even half-frozen, children knew the stories. *South past the fells, there's a door that doesn't open, and a keeper who doesn't speak, and if you're marked the keeper will hide you where the king's men can't follow.* Every gifted child grew up on that story the way other children grew up on saints.

"I'm taking you to someone who'll take you the rest of the way," Aldric said. Which was true, though the route had gotten longer lately. The usual smuggler lines through the Marchlands had gone quiet one by one—safehouses burned, contacts arrested, two of his fellow couriers hanging from the bridge at Corven Mill with placards around their necks reading GIFTED ABETTOR. What remained was the long way. The hard way. South along the high ground, then west through the passes, then down into the valley that forgot.

"Why do you do it?" Tam asked suddenly, the way children ask things, with no warning and no mercy. "Why do they want you dead?"

Aldric looked at him for a long moment. There were answers that would keep a child asleep at night and answers that were true, and after three years he had lost the talent for telling them apart.

"Because I sing," he said finally. "Because I know songs they've forbidden. Because I can walk through any door in Vell without touching it, and that frightens powerful men." He smiled, and knew the smile didn't reach anything. "Because I remember things they've spent a hundred years making everyone forget."

"What things?"

"Casper," Aldric said.

Tam frowned. "Casper's just a story. My da says it's a story."

"Yes," Aldric said gently. "That's rather the point."

The boy drifted off again, and Aldric sat watching the fire eat down, and let himself do the thing he tried not to do on the road, the thing that was dangerous not because anyone could see him but because grief slowed a man's feet. He let himself remember the choir.

The Royal Conservatory, before. Two hundred voices in the great hall under windows of colored glass, and him at the center of it, twenty-two years old, conducting with his whole body because the music deserved a whole body. And beside him in the alto line—always beside him, because she'd chosen her place and refused every promotion that would move her—Serane Voss.

She had been the best musician he'd ever known. Better than him, though neither of them said so. She heard harmonies the way other people heard thunder, instantly and bodily, and when she sang the descant in the Vespers of the Founding, grown men in the audience wept and pretended they had dust in their eyes. She was serious and exact and funny in a dry, sideways way that caught you a half-second late, and she had laughed at his jokes before anyone else did, and once—once, in the empty hall after midnight rehearsal, standing among the scattered chairs with candle-smoke curling toward the glass—she had taken his hand during the silence after a cadence and held it, and neither of them had said a word, and he had thought: *this is the shape my whole life will take.*

Then the Edicts came. Then the tests. Then the lists.

Serane had passed the loyalty examinations with the highest marks in the conservatory's history, and Aldric had understood, in the way you understand a chord turning minor, exactly what that meant—not that she had no gift, but that she had decided her gift belonged to the crown, that duty was a harmony too and she would sing her line in it whatever the melody cost. She had stood in the hall on the last morning with the other examiners' clerks, in grey instead of white, holding the register of names, and when they brought out the students who'd failed—children, mostly, terrified, some of them weeping—she had not looked away. That was the worst part. She had *not looked away*. She had written their names in her beautiful precise hand, and she had looked at Aldric across the hall with an expression he had spent three years failing to decipher, and he had run, and she had let him run, and he had never decided whether that was mercy or arithmetic.

Now she hunted him. He'd seen the warrants. INQUISITOR SERANE VOSS, FIRST CIRCLE, and beneath the seal a description that was cruelly accurate down to the scar on his left forearm. They had reports of him in six provinces. They called him *the Singer* in the broadsheets, as if he were a ghost story, and perhaps he was; ghosts were useful. But Serane wouldn't chase a ghost story. Serane chased patterns. And the pattern of his flight over three years—south, always angling south, always toward one particular valley—was legible to anyone who could read music.

Which meant she would read it soon. Weeks, maybe days.

He banked the fire and woke the boy and walked on.

* * *

On the fourth day the land changed. The fells rose around him, grey shoulders shouldering the sky, and the roads emptied entirely, and the snow lay unbroken except for fox-tracks and the tracks of one other traveler that Aldric studied for a long, cold quarter hour before deciding they weren't military. On the fifth day he crossed the stone bridge at the edge of the map he carried in his head, the one the smugglers called the Last Bridge because beyond it the informers thinned out and the wards thickened and the crown's writ went strange and soft at the edges.

It was there, on the Last Bridge, that the ward found him.

He felt it before he saw it—a pressure in the ears, a flattening in the air, the sensation of walking into a room where a vast chord was being held. Crown work, and recent: the ward-stone stood at the bridge's far end, a pillar of grey granite carved with the triple-barred sigil of the Inquisition, humming with iron hymns. Someone had raised a net across the pass. Anyone gifted who crossed it would feel it grip them, name them, ring like a struck bell that could be heard for miles.

Aldric stood at the near end of the bridge with a sleeping child on his back and considered his options, of which there was one.

The hymns they used were iron hymns—crown-made, machine-true, built to be unsingable-against, harmonies designed to close rather than open. But every harmony has a root, and every root has a shadow, and Aldric had spent three years learning the shadows of the crown's own music, note by stolen note. He set the boy down behind the bridge's parapet, out of the wind. He rolled his shoulders. He spat, uselessly, out of habit.

Then he sang.

Not loudly. Never loudly; loudness was for people whose songs worked by force. He sang quietly, intimately, the opening phrase of a song older than the bridge, older than the crown, older than the stone the bridge was made of—an unlocking-song, a door-song, one of the nine great forms the conservatory had taught in its last year before the burning, and his voice took it and bent it around the ward's iron chord like water finding the crack in a wall.

For a moment nothing. The ward hummed on, patient as law.

Then, softly, the granite pillar cracked down the middle, top to bottom, and the pressure lifted, and somewhere far off to the east a bell that Aldric couldn't see began to ring anyway—because the hymn had felt its own death, and hymns report.

He stood very still in the falling snow, listening to that distant bell, doing the arithmetic he already knew the answer to. The Inquisition would come. Not today—they'd have to ride, and the passes were deep—but soon, and behind them, if the pattern held, would come a rider in grey who did not look away from things.

"Well," Aldric said to nobody, to the snow, to the country folded up somewhere south of him behind a mossy door. "That's torn it."

He picked the boy up. His knees complained. Somewhere in his chest, beneath the weariness and the ash, something that was not quite hope and not quite dread lifted its head like a dog hearing its name, because the valley was close now—two days, maybe three—and at the end of it stood the Hollow Gate, and behind the Hollow Gate lay everything the world had agreed to forget.

He walked south, carrying a sleeping child and a kingdom, and behind him the bell rang on, and ahead of him, though he could not have known it, in a valley that had just begun to hum, a young woman was standing in front of an old stone arch, listening to it sing.
