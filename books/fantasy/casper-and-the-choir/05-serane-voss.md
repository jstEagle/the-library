## 5. Serane Voss

The child had been in the holding room for two days, and Serane Voss had not once raised her voice to him.

She brought him bread with honey on it, because he was nine and nine-year-olds liked honey. She sat across the table from him at the same hour each morning, folded her hands, and waited, because children could not outlast patience. Patience was a kind of mercy. She had learned that from her own interrogators, years ago, though she no longer thought of them that way—she thought of them as her first teachers, the ones who had found a frightened orphan girl with too much light behind her eyes and shown her what that light could become when it was given structure, purpose, law.

"Tam," she said now. "Do you know why you're here?"

The boy looked at his bread. He had eaten everything else on the plate but left the honeyed half untouched, which told her something: he was saving it. A child who saved sweetness was a child who understood that sweetness might be taken away.

"Because I whistled," Tam said.

"You whistled," Serane agreed. "Tell me what happened after."

He turned the bread over. Turned it back. "Nothing happened."

"Tam." She kept her voice low, warm, the voice she used for frightened animals and frightened people, which were often the same thing wearing different coats. "I have read the miller's account. I have read the constable's account. Both say that when you whistled, the flour rose off the floor. Not blew around—rose. In columns. Like it was listening to you."

"It didn't listen to me," the boy said, with the fierce precision of a child correcting an adult's error. "It listened to the song. My gran used to sing it. The song remembers things."

Serane felt the small cold click inside her chest that meant *true*. Twenty years of this work had taught her to hear truth the way a tuner hears pitch—not by argument but by resonance. The boy believed every word. He was not lying about the song remembering things.

That was the tragedy of them, always. They never lied. Liars she could forgive; liars were just people trying to survive, like everyone. It was the honest ones who broke your heart, sitting there with their honey-bread and their terrible gifts, certain that magic was a kindness the world had misplaced rather than a knife the world had asked to be rid of.

"The song remembers things," she repeated gently. "And what do you think it remembered, Tam?"

He looked up for the first time. His eyes were brown and ordinary and full of a light that had nothing to do with color. "My gran said it used to remember a whole country."

* * *

Afterward she stood at the narrow window of the holding wing and watched rain come down over the capital in long gray sheets, and she let herself feel the thing she allowed herself to feel for exactly as long as the rain took to fill one gutter: grief.

Then she put it away, because that was the discipline, and went to find Warden Corliss.

Corliss was in the records room, a lamp-lit cavern of shelves where the crown kept its accounting of the gifted the way a bank keeps its accounting of debts. He was a soft man made harder by paperwork, and he greeted her with the particular relief of a bureaucrat who has been waiting to hand someone a problem.

"Voss. Good. There's a name come up."

"There's always a name."

"This one's old." He set a folder on the table between them—a real folder, vellum-edged, the kind they used for matters older than either of them. "You know the Hollow Gate?"

"Everyone knows the Hollow Gate." Everyone did. It appeared in the school primers: *the seam where Casper fell*, the arch of moss-grown stone at the head of a valley so unremarkable the cartographers sometimes forgot to label it. A hundred years ago the kingdom of Casper had ended—burned, dissolved, erased depending on which historian you trusted—and all that remained was the gate, and the family who kept it, and the strange quiet around the valley that scholars called residual warding and farmers called weather. "The Pemberton line. Wardens of the gate. Hereditary office, ceremonial by now."

"Ceremonial," Corliss said, in the tone of a man tasting a word and spitting it out. "That's what we assumed. Then the Edict went out, and every district began its census of the gifted, and one of my clerks—bright boy, too clever for his post—cross-referenced the old choir registries against the valley tax rolls." He opened the folder. Inside was a single sheet, copied in a clerk's careful hand, and beneath it a map of the valley with one house circled. "There's a Pemberton still living at the gate. Young woman. Freya Pemberton. And here's the part that should interest you specifically—" he tapped the registry copy, "—her mother's name appears in the royal choir rolls. Pre-purge. Before the second fire."

Serane read the name twice. *Maren Pemberton.* It did not mean anything to her. Names from before rarely did; the purge had been thorough, and thoroughness was another word for forgetting done properly.

But *choir* meant something. Choir always meant something.

"You want me to ride to a valley," she said, "and interview a gatekeeper."

"I want you to assess a potential key." Corliss closed the folder and slid it toward her, and for a moment his soft face did something almost sharp. "You've read the same reports I have, Voss. The wards are failing everywhere. Every sealed site in the kingdom—the old chantries, the warded houses, the places we locked down after the first purge—they're all going quiet or going *loud*, and nobody down in Artificers can tell me why. Something old is coming loose. And if there is one place in this country where 'something old' has an address, it's the Hollow Gate."

"And if the girl is gifted?"

"Then the Edict applies." He said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "You know how this works. You've always known how this works."

Yes. She had. That was the point of her—that someone had to know how it worked and still be able to look at themselves in the morning. She took the folder.

"One more thing," Corliss said, as she reached the door. "On your way through the eastern districts. There's a rumor of a fugitive. Former master of the royal choir, before the dissolution. Aldric Vael."

The name landed in her like a stone dropped into still water, and she stood very still until the ripples stopped.

"You knew him," Corliss observed. Not a question. He was soft, but he was not stupid.

"I sang under him," Serane said. "When I was a girl. Before the choir was dissolved." She kept her voice level, and was pleased at how level it was. "He was the finest teacher I ever had."

"And now?"

"Now he's a name on a list, like everyone else." She picked up the folder. "I'll bring him in if I find him."

"That's not—" Corliss hesitated. "The order says alive if possible. But Voss. If he sings—if he does what they say he can do—you are authorized to stop him however stopping requires."

*However stopping requires.* She nodded and went out into the rain, and did not let herself think about a rehearsal hall twenty-five years gone, dust motes turning in high windows, and a young man with a scarred throat lifting forty children into harmony with nothing but his hands and his patience.

Patience, she reminded herself, was a kind of mercy. But mercy had a shape, and the shape was law, and anything outside the law was not mercy at all. It was indulgence. Indulgence was how the world had burned the first time.

* * *

They gave her three riders and a warrant and a week of hard roads through country getting steadily poorer the farther it got from the capital. She barely noticed. She had grown up poor herself—in the workhouse annexes of the river districts, in the years when the crown was still deciding what to do with the orphaned children of purged households—and poverty was a landscape she could walk through blindfolded.

What she noticed was the wards.

Or rather, their absence. She carried a detection charm on her wrist, standard issue, a sliver of cold iron that warmed near working magic. For the first two days it stayed cold as a dead man's finger. On the third day, crossing a stone bridge over a brown river, it shivered. By the fifth day, riding through villages where the Edict had already been read from church steps, it was warm enough that she'd moved it under her glove so her riders wouldn't see and start asking questions. Magic was leaking back into the world like water into a cellar, and no one in authority seemed to understand how fast.

On the sixth day she reached the lip of the valley.

She reined in at the ridge road and looked down, and felt—for just a moment, just one breath—the sensation of looking at a room she had dreamed about and never seen. Green terraces. A stream stitched through them. Smoke rising from a scatter of roofs, and above them all, at the valley's head, the dark shoulder of a hill with a moss-covered arch standing in it like a doorway cut by no human hand.

Her wrist-iron went cold. Perfectly, absolutely cold. Colder than inert—it felt *sucked*, as though the valley drank magic the way sand drinks rain.

"Well," said Rider Dannoc beside her, squinting down at the peaceful scene. "Doesn't look like much trouble."

"No," Serane agreed. "It doesn't." Trouble, in her experience, never did. Trouble looked like honey-bread and brown eyes and a boy who saved half his sweetness for later. Trouble looked like a green valley pretending very hard to be ordinary.

They rode down.

* * *

The village was called Millbrook, because there was a mill, because there is always a mill. Word of their coming had run ahead of them the way word always did, faster than horses, and by the time they reached the square most of the village had arranged itself into a tableau of studied indifference: women at doorways with laundry that needed no beating, men mending tools that were not broken, children gathered at the well with the particular stillness of children who have been told to be still.

Serane dismounted and let the silence stretch. She had learned long ago that the first minute of an arrival told you more than the first month of interviews. She watched who would not meet her eye. She watched who watched the hill.

And then she watched a woman in a blue shawl glance—at the house at the valley's head, at the smoke rising from it, and then away again, too quickly, with an expression Serane recognized from a hundred rooms: the look of someone checking whether a dangerous thing is still where they left it.

There. That was her thread. Everything else was weaving.

She read the Edict from the steps of the meetinghouse, because the law required it be read aloud wherever it was enforced, and she read it in her clear carrying alto, the voice trained into her by the finest choirmaster in the kingdom, and she watched the village flinch at the beauty of it, which was the cruelty of it, which was the necessity of it:

*"Let it be known throughout the realm: that the practice of the gift is proscribed; that all persons harboring such practice shall surrender them or be counted with them; that the crown extends mercy to those who come forward, and justice to those who conceal..."*

Mercy and justice, mercy and justice. She had read the words so many times they had worn smooth as river stones. She believed them. That was the thing no one understood about her, the thing her enemies would never grasp even when they learned her name: she believed them completely. The gifted burned the world once. She had seen the ash-charts, the casualty rolls of the First Purge's causes, the cities that had died when unbound power tore loose. The Edict was not cruelty. The Edict was a dam. Someone had to be the dam.

When she finished, the silence had a different texture. Fear had entered it, officially, and fear changes air the way salt changes water.

"Is there anyone," Serane asked gently, "who wishes to speak with me?"

No one spoke. Of course no one spoke. But the woman in the blue shawl had stopped breathing altogether, and Serane marked her face, and marked the direction her fear pointed, uphill.

* * *

She walked up alone. She sent her riders to stable the horses and buy goodwill with coin, because a village that resented soldiers would close like a fist, and a village that pitied them would open like a hand, and she needed hands open.

The path to the gate wound through birches gone gold with autumn. The arch grew larger and stranger as she climbed—black stone furred with moss, carved with letters in no alphabet the crown recognized, and around it the air had a quality she had no word for except *held*, as though the whole valley were a note being sustained by someone with tremendous lungs who was, at last, growing tired.

The keeper's cottage sat below the arch, small and neat, smoke rising straight from its chimney in windless air.

Serane knocked.

The door opened on a young woman—tall, plain-dressed, with ink-stained fingers and her grandmother's jaw, though Serane did not know yet whose jaw it was. Brown hair escaping a braid. Eyes the color of the valley in October. And behind the wariness in them, unmistakable to anyone who had spent a life reading faces: guilt. Fresh guilt, days old, the guilt of someone with secrets recently disturbed.

"Freya Pemberton?" Serane said.

"Yes." The word came out steady. Too steady. Rehearsed steady.

"Inquisitor Serane Voss." She showed the warrant, the seal, the whole liturgy of legitimacy, because ritual reassured the innocent and unsettled the guilty, and she genuinely did not yet know which this woman was. "May I come in? I have questions about the gate."

For one heartbeat—one—something moved through Freya Pemberton's face that was not guilt at all. It was older than guilt. It was grief, arriving early, the way animals know about storms before the sky changes.

"My grandmother's ill," she said. "If this could wait—"

"How ill?"

"Why?"

"Because the crown's records show a Maren Pemberton on the old choir rolls," Serane said, watching her, "and because the wards across this entire kingdom are failing, and because I have ridden six days to ask a handful of questions that will take ten minutes, and because if you make me force them, it will go worse for everyone. Including her."

The lie about the questions taking ten minutes sat comfortably in her mouth. She had long since stopped counting lies of efficiency as sins; they were tools, and tools were innocent.

Freya Pemberton stepped aside and let her in.

The cottage smelled of herbs and woodsmoke and, faintly, underneath, of something metallic Serane's wrist-iron answered with a whisper of warmth before going cold again—the valley drinking it down. An old woman slept in the inner room, breath rattling, a blanket pulled to her chin. On the table lay a tuning fork of white metal, and beside it a burnt-edged scrap of paper, as though something had recently been fed to a fire and not entirely consumed.

Serane looked at the fork. At the scrap. At the girl.

"Sit down, Warden Pemberton," she said, in the gentle voice, the patient voice, the voice of the woman who brought honey-bread to children and never raised it. "Let's talk about what your family has been keeping in this valley for a hundred years."

Outside, the light through the birches was going amber and long. Somewhere below, the mill turned. And somewhere east, on roads she would ride within the week, a man with a ruined throat was carrying a country in his mouth toward this valley, and neither of them knew yet that their stories had already met—here, in this small room that smelled of smoke and endings.

Freya sat. Her hands were shaking. Her eyes were not.

"Ask," said the last Pemberton.

And Serane Voss, believing with her whole immaculate heart that she was the mercy in the room, leaned forward and began.
