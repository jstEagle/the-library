## 6. Soldiers at the Door

The first sign was the birds.

Freya noticed it on her morning walk up to the gate, basket on her arm, the tuning fork of white iron warm in her pocket where it had lived for three days now, ever since Grandmother died with it pressed into her palm. The valley's birds were always noisy at dawn—sparrows arguing over the eaves of the mill, crows strutting the fence lines like landlords. This morning they were silent. All of them. As if someone had drawn a line around the valley and told every living thing with wings to hold its breath.

She stopped on the path and listened to the absence.

The gate hummed.

Not the faint, almost-imagined hum she'd coaxed from it with the fork and the old words—the deep, uneasy vibration she'd felt the day the stray note of song had woken it. This was different. This was the hum of something straining. The moss on the arch had begun to die in patches, curling brown at the edges, and through the bare stone she could see lines she had never noticed before, thin as hair, running through the arch like cracks in ice.

Like it was holding itself together with effort.

"Not now," she told it, the way she'd told it a hundred useless times before. "Whatever you're doing, stop it."

It did not stop.

She did her rounds anyway, because rounds were what kept a person sane when the world tilted: she checked the anchor-stone at the gate's foot, cold and smooth as an egg; she walked the ward-line to the boundary oak, where the air went soft and stupid and the valley's forgetting pressed against her like wool; she stood a long moment looking down at Vell, at the smoke rising from chimneys, at Mrs. Halloway hanging washing that would be dry by noon because the weather in the valley was always, always kind.

The valley had been kind for a hundred years. Freya had grown up inside that kindness without ever once wondering what it cost.

Now she knew. The ward was anchored to her blood. Her grandmother had said so with her last breath, and the ward was failing, and the crown had sent its Inquisitors into the world to hunt the gifted, and Freya Pemberton was the most gifted thing in the valley whether she'd ever asked to be or not.

She was walking back down to the village, rehearsing what she'd say to Tam Halloway about the mill levy, when she saw the soldiers.

They came up the valley road in a column of grey, six of them on horses and two on foot, and at their head rode a woman in a coat the color of wet slate with the crown's sunburst stitched in silver at the collar. Not armor. Inquisitors didn't wear armor; they wore authority, and it weighed more.

Freya stepped off the road into the shade of the tithe-barn before her mind finished deciding to. Her heart was going like a fist on a door.

They weren't hurrying. That was the worst of it. They came at the pace of people who had already sent letters ahead, who already knew the lay of the land, who were not searching for anything because everything had been reported to them in writing. The herald's visit a month ago had not been a warning. It had been an appointment.

At the well in the village square, the slate-coated woman dismounted, and Freya saw her face: young, maybe thirty, with a scar through one eyebrow and eyes that were not cruel. That was the horror of them. The eyes were kind. She spoke to the villagers gathered at the square with the gentle patience of a schoolmistress, and Freya, pressed against the barn wall with her basket digging into her arm, could not hear the words but could see the effect: heads nodding, shoulders easing, the particular relief of people being told that cooperation is safety.

Then the woman turned her head, slowly, and looked directly at the tithe-barn.

Freya did not remember running. She remembered the back lane, the smell of pig muck and wet straw, the gap in Tam Halloway's fence she'd squeezed through since she was six. She remembered thinking, with a clarity that felt like falling: *the gate, the gate, get to the gate.*

She burst through her own front door and stopped.

Grandmother's chair sat empty by the hearth. On the table, arranged with terrible neatness, lay everything Freya owned that mattered: the tuning fork. The Pemberton ledger, three generations of gate-records in her grandmother's cramped hand. And a letter, folded once, her name on it in that same cramped hand.

There was no time to read it. There was a knock at the door—polite, measured, the knock of a neighbor.

"Freya?" Tam Halloway's voice. "Freya, love, there's folk here asking after you. Best come out and speak plain. It'll go easier."

It'll go easier. She stared at the door. Tam had taught her to fish. Tam's wife had mended her winter coat. Tam knew—everyone in Vell had always known, in the wordless way valleys know things—that the Pembertons were the Pembertons, and everyone had agreed, for a hundred years, to know it quietly.

The agreement had ended this morning.

"They're Inquisitors, love," Tam said through the door, and his voice was gentle, and that was the cruelest thing yet. "The lady says if you come out willing, there'll be no trouble for the village. She gave her word."

Freya looked at the letter. At the fork. At the hearth where her grandmother had burned forty years of correspondence in a single night, page by page, feeding the fire like it was a sick animal.

*I'm sorry, love,* Tam said through the door, and she believed him, and it changed nothing. "They know about your gran. They know what she was. They only want—"

She went out the back.

* * *

The lane behind the house was narrow and the wall was low and she went over it with the fork in her fist and the letter crushed against her ribs, and then she was running up the hill path with the valley falling away behind her and the gate's hum rising ahead of her like a struck bell that wouldn't finish ringing.

They saw her. Of course they saw her. A shout went up from the square, thin with distance, and then hoofbeats, and she did not look back because looking back was how people died in every story her grandmother had ever told, and her grandmother had never once told a story that wasn't true.

The path climbed through the birches. Her lungs burned. The hum became a roar she felt in her teeth, in the bones of her feet, and when she broke through the trees into the clearing the sight of it stopped her mid-stride for one wasted, unforgivable second:

The Hollow Gate was open a hand's width.

Not the dead grey shimmer of nothing that had filled its arch her whole life. A crack. A seam of light the color of dusk, deep violet shot through with gold, and through it she could see—she could *see*—towers. Rooftops. A street going down into a city that no map in the valley had carried for a hundred years.

Casper. The word arrived in her mind unbidden, in her grandmother's voice, and it fit.

The hoofbeats broke from the trees behind her.

Freya did the only thing left. She put her hand flat against the stone of the arch—it was warm, it was *alive*, it shuddered under her palm like a horse feeling thunder—and she pressed the tuning fork of white iron into the crack where the stone met the light, and she said the words her grandmother had made her memorize every night of her childhood, the ones she'd always thought were a lullaby:

*"Hollow gate, hold fast. Hold fast. Hold fast."*

The gate did not hold fast.

The gate *opened*.

It came apart along the hairline cracks like ice giving way on a spring pond—not breaking, *unfolding*—and the violet light poured over her, and the last thing she heard from the valley was Tam Halloway shouting her name, actually shouting it, as if he were sorry, as if sorry were worth anything, and then she stepped through because the alternative was the woman with the kind eyes, and the world—

The world turned over.

There was no other way to describe it. One step she was on the hill above Vell in the flat grey light of morning, and the next step she was somewhere that made her knees buckle and her stomach lurch and her eyes water, because the sky was wrong. It was dusk, a permanent, sourceless dusk, violet and gold and going on forever in every direction, and the air tasted of woodsmoke and rain on warm stone and something sweeter underneath, something like the smell of a song.

She turned around.

The gate stood behind her, the arch and its dying moss, but the valley was gone. Where Vell should have been—her hills, her birches, the smoke of her village—there was only the dusk and a road, an actual road, cobbled and worn, running down between hedges of a flower she didn't know, white and heavy-headed, nodding in a wind she couldn't feel.

She had stepped out of her country. And her country had stepped out of the world.

"No," she said aloud. Her voice sounded small and flattened, as if the dusk were pressing on it. "No, no—"

She pressed her hands to the arch. It hummed under her fingers, fainter now, tired. She said the words again, the holding words, the fast words, and the gate gave a shudder and the crack of violet light narrowed to a thread and then, with a sound like a long-held breath finally let go, sealed itself to a shimmer, and the shimmer faded, and the arch was only an arch again, mossy and grey and dead as it had always looked.

Alone. She was alone on the far side of a door that no longer opened, in a country that had been dead for a hundred years, and behind her was everything she had lost and ahead of her was—

Ahead of her was the road, and the road went down, and down at the bottom of it, in the violet half-light, there were lights. Windows. A city.

And someone was singing.

It came up the road to her faint and fractured, many voices, a choir, singing something in a mode she didn't know that nonetheless made the backs of her eyes ache with familiarity, the way a smell can ache. They sang, and the white flowers along the hedges turned toward the sound, and the dusk itself seemed to lean in and listen, and Freya Pemberton stood on the threshold of the impossible with a dead woman's tuning fork in her fist and a dead woman's letter against her heart, and understood, with a terror that was somehow also a kind of homecoming, that she had not escaped.

She had arrived.

The singing stopped. All at once, mid-phrase, the way a room goes quiet when a stranger walks in.

Down the road, in the deepening gold of the endless dusk, a figure detached itself from the city gates and began to walk toward her. Then another. Then a dozen, pale as paper in the half-light, moving with the unhurried certainty of people who had been waiting a very long time for exactly this.

The tuning fork hummed in her hand, though she hadn't touched it to anything at all.
