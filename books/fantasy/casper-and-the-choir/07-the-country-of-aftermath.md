## 7. The Country of Aftermath

The first thing Freya noticed was the light.

It was not dawn and not dusk but something between them that had forgotten how to move on—a gold so old it had gone soft at the edges, like lamplight through honey. It fell across a street that should not have existed: cobbles worn smooth by centuries of feet, shopfronts with their shutters half-lowered, a fountain in the middle distance throwing water upward in a long silver thread that never seemed to come all the way down.

She was standing on a road called—she knew this suddenly, the way you know your own name—without ever having learned it—the Way of Bells.

Behind her, the gate she had come through was gone. Not closed. Gone. Where the Hollow Gate's mossy arch should have been there was only air, and beyond the air, impossibly, the valley: green hills under a grey sky, seen from above and behind, as if she were looking out through the pupil of some enormous eye. She could see the Pemberton cottage. She could see smoke rising from her neighbor Marta's chimney, the same chimney whose owner had sold her to the Inquisitors an hour ago.

*An hour ago.* Time felt unreliable here, like a story told by someone who kept losing his place.

"Hello?" Her voice came out small and was swallowed by the golden light. No echo. Streets echoed. This one drank sound the way sand drank rain.

She took a step, then another, and the cobbles accepted her weight with a faint chime, almost too low to hear, like a bell struck under water. The fountain's suspended water trembled when she passed it. Up close she could see that the droplets were not quite falling—they hung in the air in the shape of falling, each one a small bright pause, a held breath made visible. A copper coin rested on the surface of the water without sinking. Someone had thrown it and never gotten their wish.

Freya walked because standing still felt worse. The street bent downhill toward a square, and the buildings grew taller and older around her, timber-framed houses leaning together like conspirators, laundry lines strung between them bearing shirts and sheets that stirred in no wind she could feel. Everything was immaculate. That was the first wrong thing, though it took her a while to name it. A city lived in by no one should be dusty. This one looked *tended*. Swept. Loved.

The second wrong thing was the people.

She saw the first of them at the mouth of the square: a woman in an apron, shaking out a rug over a second-floor balcony. Freya watched her beat the rug three times, pause, look down the empty street as if waiting for someone, and begin again. Three beats. Pause. The same searching look, identical down to the angle of her head. Three beats. Pause.

Not a woman doing a chore. A memory of a woman, doing a chore, forever.

Freya's stomach turned over slowly, coldly. She pressed herself into the shadow of a doorway—which was absurd, she realized a moment later, because nothing here could see her, or nothing here had eyes left to see with—and watched a man cross the square carrying a ladder. He set it against a wall, climbed two rungs, descended, carried the ladder to another wall, climbed two rungs, descended. His face was pleasant and vacant as a Sunday afternoon. He would do this until the world ended or he did.

*The dead repeat their last days.* Grandmother's voice, from a confession made in a dying whisper: *the kingdom didn't vanish, girl. It was put away. And everything inside it was mid-sentence.*

Mid-sentence. That was the phrase that unlocked it. Every person she saw was caught in the middle of something—a task, a goodbye, a walk home—and had been caught there for a hundred years, repeating the gesture without ever completing it, because completing it meant admitting there was nothing after.

Freya came around a corner and stopped breathing.

Below her, terraced down a hillside, lay the city entire: Casper. Not ruins. Not bones. Casper *whole*, rooftops and spires and bridges and gardens, every window glowing with that impossible honeyed light, the whole kingdom spread out like a painting of itself, preserved under glass. A river ran through it, wide and slow, and the river did not reflect the sky above it but a different sky entirely—one with clouds shaped differently, moving, alive. Somewhere far off, bells were ringing. They rang a pattern she almost recognized, a call to evening service, and they had been ringing it, she understood with a lurch, since before her grandmother was born.

It was beautiful. It was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen.

And it was wrong the way a beautiful corpse is wrong—all the parts present, arranged with care, and nothing home behind the eyes of it.

* * *

She went down into the city because there was nowhere else to go, and because terror, Freya was learning, feels a great deal like curiosity if you don't let yourself stop walking.

The streets filled as she descended. Not with crowds—with repetitions. A baker drew loaves from an oven that held no fire, set them on a windowsill to cool, turned, reached for dough that was never there, frowned at the absence, and began again with the same loaves. Two children played a game with a hoop and stick; the hoop rolled six feet, wobbled, fell, and the boy laughed the same laugh each time, a bright unchanging coin of a laugh, and the girl clapped the same clap, and they began again. Freya stood and watched four full cycles before she had to make herself look away. There was a version of watching this that was unbearable, and a version that was worse: the version where you started to find it peaceful.

A market square opened before her, stalls with fruit arranged in pyramids, apples and plums that gleamed like lacquer and had not rotted in a century. A fishmonger called out prices in a language that was almost hers—words with familiar bones and strange flesh. *Fersk brising, tre mynter pundet.* Fresh herring, three coins the pound. He called it to customers who were not there, who had perhaps stepped away a hundred years ago meaning to come right back.

Freya reached out, very slowly, and touched an apple.

Her finger went through it. Not like mist—like touching the place where a sound used to be. The apple rippled, once, like the surface of the fountain, and settled back into its shape. But the ripple traveled. It moved outward through the pyramid of fruit, up the stall, along the awning, and the fishmonger paused in mid-call—paused, for the first time in a hundred years—and turned his head, just slightly, toward the disturbance, with an expression that was not confusion and not hope but the raw material both of them are made from.

Then the moment passed through him like weather and he resumed: *Fersk brising, tre mynter pundet.*

"I'm sorry," Freya whispered. It was the only prayer she knew.

That was when she heard the singing.

* * *

It came from everywhere and nowhere at first, the way the bell-chime in the cobbles had—underwater, distant, threaded through the stones. Then it resolved, gained direction, and pulled her down three streets and through an archway into a colonnaded courtyard where a cathedral had once stood and still, in a manner of speaking, stood: its walls present, its roof a suggestion, its great rose window hanging in the air with no wall around it, turning slowly, casting colored light that moved across the flagstones like slow fish.

In the middle of the courtyard stood the singers.

There were perhaps thirty of them, arranged in a crescent, robed in vestments gone soft with age, and they were singing a chord that had no bottom note—or rather, the bottom note was the courtyard itself, the stones, the city, the whole suspended kingdom humming in sympathy. Their faces were translucent at the edges, like paper held up to flame. Some were old. Some were children. One was a young man whose mouth moved with the others while his eyes stayed fixed on a point somewhere past the end of the world.

They were holding the city up. Freya understood this without being told, the way you understand falling. The song was the light. The song was the repetition, the loop, the mercy of the unfinished sentence. As long as the choir sang, Casper remained mid-sorrow instead of finished sorrow, and everyone inside it went on shaking rugs and baking bread and laughing the same laugh, safe from the ending they had all been promised.

As long as the choir sang. And voices, even ghost voices, even voices made of stubbornness and grief—even those wore out.

Freya could hear it now that she listened: fraying. The chord had rough places in it, dropped stitches, notes where a singer had simply stopped and the others stretched to cover the gap. One corner of the crescent had gone silent altogether, and where it was silent, the courtyard was *less*—the flagstones grayer, the rose window's light thinner, a hairline crack running up through the air itself like a flaw in old glass.

"You can see us."

One of them had stepped forward. A woman, elderly or ageless, her translucence deepest at her hands, as if she had given herself away piece by piece. Her voice was dry and warm, like pages turned near a fire.

"I—" Freya's voice cracked. "I can see you. I'm sorry. I don't—I came through the gate. I didn't know anything was—"

"No." The woman studied her with an expression Freya couldn't parse until she realized it was hunger—not for food, for *certainty*, for proof. "No, child. You came through the gate because the gate is dying, and the gate is dying because we are dying, and we are dying because the world outside decided a hundred years ago that we had already ended." She said it without bitterness, which somehow made it worse. "What is your name?"

"Freya. Freya Pemberton."

The effect was immediate and physical. Thirty translucent heads turned. The chord wavered, bent, and reformed around a new note that hadn't existed a moment before—something between awe and alarm. An old man at the crescent's end gripped the shoulder of the singer beside him, and the singer beside him was solid enough to grip, briefly, before remembering to fade.

"Pemberton," the woman said. "Gatekeeper."

"My grandmother was. My mother was. I am, I think. Was." Freya's hands wouldn't stay still. "My grandmother died three days ago. She told me things. About the ward. About what the valley really is. She gave me—" Freya pulled the tuning fork from her coat, white iron dull as bone in the honeyed light, and the whole courtyard flinched from it, gently, like plants from frost.

"Put that away," the woman said softly. "Not here. Not yet. It remembers being struck, and we remember being rung."

Freya wrapped it back in its cloth, heart hammering. "I don't understand any of this. My grandmother said the valley's magiclessness is a ward. She said it was anchored to our blood. She said it was failing. She said—" Freya stopped, because saying the rest aloud made it real, and she wasn't ready. *She said you were real. She said the kingdom never vanished. She said someone would come looking for the door.*

"We know what she said," the woman said. "We sang to her, once, through the stone. Did she never tell you? On quiet nights, when the ward thinned. We sang the gate-songs to her grandmother, and her grandmother taught them to her daughter, and her daughter—" the woman's voice gentled, "—her daughter wrote them down and hid them in the floor of a cottage kitchen, under the third flagstone, beneath the kettle hook. In case. Always in case."

Freya sat down. There was a bench of pale stone and her legs simply declined to hold her anymore, and she sat, and the ghost-choir watched her with a century of patience.

"They're killing us," she said finally. "Outside. The crown. There's an edict. They're taking anyone who's gifted, and the Inquisitors came to the valley, and I ran, and I didn't run *to* anywhere, I ran through, and now I'm here, and you're all—" She gestured helplessly at the translucent woman, the cracked air, the city of unfinished afternoons. "How many of you are left? Singing?"

"One hundred and forty," the woman said. "When the seal was new, there were nine thousand."

Nobody spoke. Somewhere in the city a bell rang its eternal call to a service that had ended a hundred years ago, and the sound came into the courtyard and lay down among them like a tired animal.

"The kingdom was sealed to save it," the woman continued, when it was clear Freya needed the words more than silence. "The first purge. You'll have heard of it as history, if you heard of it at all—the crown burned the gifted in the capital squares, and the last choir-master of Casper did the unforgivable arithmetic. He gathered what he could save and he folded the kingdom closed, like a letter, like a hand closing over a coal. Sealed it out of time. Left a door, and a bloodline to keep the door, and a valley on the other side scrubbed clean of magic so the crown would never smell what was hidden there." She smiled, and it was a terrible smile, tender and ruined. "He saved us. That is the truth of it. And he buried us alive, and that is also the truth of it, and both truths have been singing in this courtyard for a hundred years, and we are so tired, Freya Pemberton. We are so tired of being saved."

The chord behind her swelled slightly, unconsciously, the way a sleeping person turns toward warmth. Freya thought of the baker reaching for dough that was never there. Of the boy with the hoop, laughing his one laugh. Of the fishmonger calling his prices to no one.

"If the song stops," she said, "what happens to them?"

The woman was quiet for a long moment.

"Nothing terrible," she said at last. "That is the cruelty of it. They finish. The rug gets shaken one final time and stays shaken. The loaf cools and goes stale like an honest loaf. The boy's hoop rolls to the end of the street and lies still, and the game is over, and the game was always going to be over, and none of them will feel a thing except, perhaps, at the very last instant—an ending. Which is what we have spent a hundred years protecting them from." She looked out past the roofless walls, at the golden city, at the river reflecting another sky. "You may decide for yourself whether that was love."

Freya thought about her valley. About neighbors who had agreed, generation after generation, not to notice. About Marta's face in the doorway yesterday, calculating, apologetic, *already sorry*. Safety built out of not-looking. A whole community holding its breath for a hundred years and calling the breath peace.

"Yes," Freya said quietly. "I may."

The woman laughed—a real laugh, brief and startled out of her, the first unrepeated sound Freya had heard in this country. "You sound like her. Your grandmother. She sat on that same bench, the night before her wedding, and argued with me about wards and mercy until the bells rang morning." Her translucence flickered, steadied. "Listen to me, gatekeeper. The gate is failing because we are failing, and we cannot mend ourselves, and outside your valley the crown has begun again what it began a century ago—except now they know the door exists. They will come for it. Perhaps they are already coming. And there is a way out of this country—we are its keepers, the way out is why we sing—but the way out requires a living key, and the last living key is sitting on a bench in a dead cathedral with iron in her pocket and three days of grief in her chest."

"What does a key have to do?" Freya asked. Her voice sounded very young to her own ears.

"It opens," the woman said. "That is all a key does. It opens. But every opening is also a closing, child, and the arithmetic of this door is written in blood and choice, and I will not lie to you about it tonight. Tonight you are a guest. Tonight you will eat"—and here the woman's smile turned wry—"or you will go through the motions of eating, which is the best we can offer, and you will sleep in the dormitory where the choir-children slept, and tomorrow we will teach you what your grandmother's floorboards have been waiting a hundred years to tell you."

Behind her, the choir took up the chord again, and the rose window turned, and the colored light moved across the flagstones like slow fish, and Freya sat on the bench in the country of aftermath and felt—for the first time since the soldiers came, since Marta's apologetic eyes, since her grandmother's last breath—something that was not fear.

It was almost like being expected.

Then, from far away, from the direction of the gate, from the thin place in the air where her valley hung like a picture in a locket—a sound. Faint. Underwater. A single voice, singing a lock open.

Someone else had found the door.
