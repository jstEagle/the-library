## 8. Aldric in the Ash Choir

The gate was empty.

Aldric Vane had crossed four hundred miles of crown country to reach it—stowed under wool bales, wading the Alderfen at night, singing his way past three ward-stones and one very suspicious toll-keeper—and when he finally crested the last hill above Hollow Vale at dawn, breath ragged, throat raw from a winter of whispering instead of singing, he found the arch standing quiet in its collar of moss and no one home.

No keeper. No fire. The little hut beside it dark, door ajar, a chair tipped over on its side like a man mid-fall.

He stood in the wet grass with his heart going like a hammer on a nail and listened. The valley below was waking—smoke from chimneys, a dog somewhere, the ordinary music of people who believed themselves safe. And the gate itself hummed. That was wrong. He knew gates; he had spent eleven years learning what gates sounded like, ever since the choir schools were closed and the masters were taken and the songs themselves were made contraband. Gates were dead things, mostly, or sleeping ones. This one was *listening*.

And it had been opened recently. Recently enough that the air still smelled of struck flint and something sweeter underneath—rowan berries, maybe, or rain on hot stone.

Someone had gone through.

"Of course," he said to the empty arch. His voice came out cracked and small. "Of course they have."

He should rest. Every sensible part of him said so: eat, sleep, let the voice mend. But sensible men did not survive purges, and Aldric had survived one by learning that the moment you stop moving is the moment they find you. Somewhere behind him, days back along his trail, Inquisitor Serane Voss was doing exactly what she always did—following, patiently, with that terrible gentle face of hers, closing distance like a tide.

So he ate half a heel of bread standing up, drank from the stream, and put his hand flat against the mossy stone of the arch.

It was warm.

* * *

Opening a locked gate is not force. That was the first thing Master Ondrey had beaten into them, back in the choir school before the school stopped existing: *a lock is a question asked badly, boy. Your job is to ask it well.* So Aldric stood before the Hollow Gate in the gray morning light and asked it well.

He started low, in the chest, where the old notes lived—the drone beneath the drone, the sound that was less music than permission. The moss shivered. Water beaded off the stone and hung in the air for a heartbeat too long before falling. He climbed the scale slowly, feeling for the place where the gate's own note waited, the way you'd feel for a handhold on a dark stair.

He found it on the seventh tone—a thin, high, lonely sound, like a glass rimmed by a wet finger. His voice wrapped around it. The gate sighed.

The air inside the arch went soft, then silver, then simply *elsewhere*—a twilight country he could smell before he could see: woodsmoke and lilies and something faintly burnt, like a candle snuffed a long time ago.

Aldric Vane looked at the open door of a kingdom that had been erased from every map and register and memory the crown could burn, and he felt the same thing he had felt at nine years old, hearing Master Ondrey sing the chapel shut behind them as the soldiers came up the hill.

Hope. And right behind hope, close as a shadow, the certainty that hope was how they got you.

He went through anyway.

* * *

Casper was worse than the stories.

Not ruined—that was the thing no story got right. Ruin would have been honest. Casper was *intact*, and that was the horror of it. Streets swept clean under a sky the color of pewter. Lanterns lit in windows where nobody lived. A fountain still playing in a square where the water fell upward for a moment, confused, before remembering which way was down. Everything mid-gesture. Everything waiting.

And everywhere, the ash. It drifted down fine and gray and endless, settling on shoulders and sills, and Aldric understood within a hundred paces what it was: memory wearing out. A kingdom held together by song for a hundred years, fraying at every seam, shedding itself like a moth's wing.

The dead—or whatever the dead were here—walked their last days over and over. A woman leaned out a window shaking a rug that never got clean. Two boys played a game of skittles that ended the same way each time, the pins scattering in slow motion, reset, scattered again. An old man sat on a bench feeding pigeons that were also memories, because there were no living things here except the ones that came through the door.

They ignored him. Mostly. He kept his voice down and walked the way Ondrey had taught him to walk through hostile country: unhurried, harmless, listening.

Because he was listening for her. The keeper's daughter, or granddaughter—the bloodline, the key. He'd known the Pembertons only as names in a smuggled letter, but the letter had said what mattered: *the ward fails, the girl doesn't know what she is.* And now she had come through a gate that took a century of knowing to crack, alone, hours ahead of him, into a country that ate the unprepared.

Her traces were easy to follow, actually. Living feet left prints in the ash that the ash forgot to erase. Here a handprint on a wall where she'd steadied herself. There—his chest tightened—a scuff of running. She had run. Something had made her run.

The traces led uphill, toward the cathedral whose spire he could see needling the pewter sky, and the closer he came, the more the streets changed. Quieter. The repeated lives thinned out. And the ash grew thicker, drifting in slow spirals, and some of the spirals had shapes.

*Wraiths,* the old songs called them. Not ghosts. Ghosts were people who wouldn't leave. Wraiths were memories that had forgotten they'd been anyone—grief with no name attached, hunger without a mouth. They formed where the kingdom was thinnest, where the song sustaining it had worn through, and they did what any starving thing does.

They fed on the living. On warmth. On remembered joy, which was the richest food a wraith knew.

Aldric broke into a run.

* * *

He heard her before he saw her—not screaming, which would have been bad, but breathing in that fast controlled way people breathe when they are deciding whether to scream. He rounded a corner of cloister and there she was: young, mud to the knees, a white iron fork clutched in one fist like a knife, backed against the cathedral wall while the wraiths came down on her out of the ashfall like weather given intent.

There were five of them. Maybe six. Gray, tall, wrong—faces like smudged charcoal, mouths that weren't mouths opening on a silence that pulled.

Aldric didn't think. Eleven years of running had burned the thinking out of moments like this and left only the doing. He planted his feet, filled his lungs past the ache in his ribs, and sang.

Not loudly. Loudness is for armies. He sang the *Stillness*, the oldest piece in the repertoire they'd burned the books to destroy—the song that tells a frightened thing, in its own grammar, *you are remembered; you may rest.* He pitched it under the wraiths' silence, threaded it into the pull of them, gave each shapeless hunger one true note to hold onto instead of the girl.

It worked the way it always worked, and it cost the way it always cost. The wraiths stopped. Turned. The nearest one came apart into ordinary ash, gently, almost gratefully. The others followed—one, two, three—each dissolution taking a thread of warmth out of him, a coin from a purse already light. By the sixth his knees were buckling and his vision had gone white at the edges, and the last wraith paused an arm's length from the girl, considering, and then sighed into dust between one note and the next.

Silence. Ashfall. The girl staring at him with the tuning fork raised, wild-eyed, ready to fight God himself if God came through that cloister.

"Don't," Aldric managed, and sat down hard on the flagstones. "Don't hit me. I've had a very long year."

She lowered the fork slowly. Up close she had her mother's face—he'd seen a miniature once, in another life, in a smuggler's coat pocket—and none of her mother's calm. What she had was fury held together with grief, which he recognized, because it was his own face ten years ago.

"You sang them dead," she said.

"Asleep. I hope." He coughed. "Dead is possible. I've never had the courage to check."

"Who are you?"

"Aldric Vane." He said it plainly, though saying your name aloud in this country felt like leaving a door open. "Choir-master. Formerly of the Royal Choir, formerly of everything, currently of nowhere. And you're Freya Pemberton, and we are both in approximately the worst place in the world, so I'd suggest we postpone the rest."

She didn't lower the fork all the way. Smart girl. "You came through the gate."

"This morning. Found it empty. Found you missing. Followed you." He looked up at the cathedral rising over them both, its rose window dark, its doors standing open on gloom. "Why did you run? What chased you?"

"Nothing chased me." Her jaw worked. "I wasn't running from. I was—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I was looking for my grandmother. Someone told me she might be here. Someone told me a lot of things."

Ah, thought Aldric. Ah, no. He knew what the ghost-choir told keys, and he knew what most of it cost.

"Freya." He used her name deliberately, the way you'd use a rope thrown to someone in deep water. "Before anything else—you should know two things. First: an Inquisitor named Serane Voss is following me, and she'll find the gate, and she will come through. We have time, but not much."

Something moved behind her eyes at the name—recognition, and something older than recognition. He filed that away.

"Second thing," he said, and here he hesitated, because for eleven years he had told no one, because telling people got people killed, and because once he said it aloud it would become real again after all these years of keeping it folded away like a letter he couldn't bear to finish reading.

"I knew your mother."

The tuning fork finally dropped to her side.

"Maren Pemberton," he said. "Before she was Maren Pemberton. Before the valley, before the gate, before any of it. She stood where you're standing. She wore that same expression, like she was deciding whether to hit me." His throat tightened around the next words, and he let them come anyway, because the girl deserved them, because the dead deserved them, because eleven years was long enough. "She was my friend. She sang second alto, and she laughed in the rests, which drove Ondrey mad, and she was the bravest coward I ever knew—which is to say, she was afraid of everything and did the necessary thing anyway, every single time. When the purge came, she was the one who carried the children out. Not me. Her."

Freya had gone very still. "My mother never sang."

"No," Aldric said softly. "I imagine she didn't. Not after." He pushed himself upright, joints protesting, ribs screaming, and stood swaying in the ashfall with his hand braced on the cathedral wall. "She sealed it all away. The valley, the magic, the songs—she helped build the ward that made your whole world forget, so the crown would forget you too. It was the bravest thing I ever watched anyone do, and I think it broke her heart, and I think she spent thirty years not singing about it."

The girl's eyes were bright and furious and wet. "You're lying."

"Yes," Aldric agreed. "I probably am. About half of everything, on any given day. But not about her." He held out his hand, palm up, empty—an offering, or a surrender. "I can't prove any of this, Freya. I can only offer you the alliance of two people who've lost the same war at different ends. You're an orphan of the valley. I'm an orphan of the capital. Different disasters, same fire."

For a long moment she didn't move. The ash drifted between them. Somewhere in the cathedral behind her, faintly, voices began—a chord, low and patient, the ghost-choir holding their endless note.

Then Freya Pemberton took his hand. Her grip was strong and cold and did not let go quickly.

"If you're lying," she said, "I'll feed you to the next wraith myself."

"Fair terms," said Aldric. "I'd expect nothing less from Maren's daughter."

They turned toward the cathedral doors together, and that was when the chord inside changed—frayed, stumbled, caught. And under it, distant but unmistakable, from the direction of the Hollow Gate, a sound that froze them both where they stood:

A single clear voice, singing a lock open.
