## 9. The Choir's Bargain

They led her down through the city by lamplight that wasn't fire.

Freya had stopped trying to name things. The lamps were glass globes of held dusk; the streets were cobbles made of someone's wedding day; the air smelled faintly of rain on stone in a country where, she was beginning to understand, it had not rained in a hundred years. Everything here was memory wearing the shape of matter, and every step she took felt like walking through a room where someone had just been crying.

The choir walked ahead of her—nine figures in robes the color of old lead, their faces flickering like portraits seen through water. They had introduced themselves with a single word each: *Vessel*. *Second*. *Keeper-of-the-Low-Part*. Names like duties. She had given them her own name and watched it land strangely among them, the way a coin dropped into a well takes a long time to be heard.

"You're dead," she said, for the fourth time, because saying it out loud helped. "All of you."

"We are what remained when the dying was done," said Vessel, who seemed to be the one who spoke most. Her voice was a low warm thing, an alto that made Freya's ribs hum. "There is a difference. The dead are finished. We are unfinished."

"That's worse."

Vessel turned, and beneath the hood her face was a woman's face, kind and worn and wrong in some way Freya couldn't fix—like a painting hung a half-inch crooked. "Yes," the ghost said simply. "It is worse. You may as well know it now as later."

They descended a stair cut into the living rock beneath the cathedral district, and the stair itself was singing.

Not metaphorically. Each step gave off a note underfoot, a scale running down into the dark, and Freya realized with a lurch that this was how the city had been built—not on foundations but on music, whole architecture carried on sustained chords. Aldric would have wept at this stair. She thought of him outside the gate, somewhere behind her in the waking world or already following, and pushed the thought away before it could become a hope. Hopes were heavy. She was carrying enough.

At the bottom lay a chamber shaped like the inside of a bell.

It was vast and round and ribbed, and every surface was covered in names. Thousands upon thousands of names, carved so fine they looked from a distance like wood grain, up close like the veins in a leaf. Freya ran her fingers along the wall without thinking and felt warmth under her skin, faint as sun through cloth.

"What is this place?"

"The counting house," said Second—a tall figure whose voice cracked like a boy soprano's, though nothing about him had been a boy for a very long time. "Every soul sealed inside the gate has a line here. Every soul the gate holds."

"How many?"

Neither answered. Vessel gestured instead, one pale hand sweeping the chamber, and Freya understood that the answer was the walls, all of them, floor to invisible ceiling, circling her on every side.

Her hand came off the stone.

* * *

They sat her down on a bench that might once have been a pew, in the center of the bell-chamber, and told her the story the way people tell stories they have told themselves so many times the telling has gone smooth as river stone.

"Casper did not fall," Vessel began. "That is the first lie. Casper was *put away*."

"By the crown."

"By the crown, yes—but at our own asking." The alto voice caught on something. "You have to understand what those years were. The first purge. Before your valley had even heard the word *gifted*, the crown was burning choirs. Not arresting. Burning. They came to the capital with iron wagons and men in grey who could hear magic the way dogs hear whistles, and they took three hundred children out of the royal choir in a single week."

Second spoke from the shadows. "I was one of the ones they didn't get."

"There were mages then," Vessel went on. "Real ones, a hundred strong, the last generation that ever learned the deep craft. And they looked at what was coming and understood there was no winning a war against a kingdom that outnumbered them ten thousand to one. So they made a door." Her hood tilted toward the walls of names. "They made a door, and they put a whole country through it."

"The kingdom everyone says vanished."

"The kingdom that was *folded*, child. Folded like a letter. Sealed inside a pocket of the world where time moves slow and nothing rots and nothing grows. A hundred years out there—" she paused, and the pause had weight, "—is eleven years in here. That is the mercy we chose. That is also the trap we built."

Freya's mouth had gone dry. "Eleven years."

"We counted on rescue," said Second. "We counted on the purge passing, the king dying, sanity returning. Someone was to come back for us. There was a plan. There were keys." His voice did something terrible and small. "Plans rot faster than bodies, it turns out. Even in a place where nothing else does."

"The keymakers died outside," Vessel said. "Or were found. Or forgot. The bloodlines scattered. The knowledge went into hiding and hid so well it hid from itself. And Casper—" the ghost spread her hands, and for a moment the flicker of her face steadied, and Freya saw the woman underneath, the one who had been young once, in a country with a sky, "—Casper waited. Eleven years. Then twelve. Then twenty."

"And now?" Freya asked, though the walls of names had already told her.

"Now we are starving," Vessel said, flat and clean, no self-pity in it at all, which somehow made it worse. "A sealed country runs on its own substance. Memory first—it burns brightest. Then craft. Then song. Then—" she touched the wall beside her, gently, the way you touch a sleeping child, "—then the singers themselves. There were nine thousand of us when the door closed. There are nine of us left who can still hold a note. The rest are lines on these walls. The rest are *fuel*."

Freya looked at the names again and couldn't breathe properly. Nine thousand lines. She had run her fingers across maybe forty of them.

"My grandmother knew," she said. "Didn't she. That's what she wasn't saying. That's why the ward, why the valley, why everything."

"Your family kept the outer lock," Vessel said. "The Pembrotons were warders before they were farmers. Your grandmother's mother signed the sealing. It's in the ledger." A pause, delicate as a held breath. "She hoped, I think, that keeping the door shut was the same as keeping the promise alive. That someday a Pemberton would come down a stair like this one and say *we remember, we've come to open it.*"

"She never said a word. Not one."

"No," Vessel agreed softly. "And neither did we. Everyone in this story was very good at silence. It's the national art form, silence. We perfected it."

* * *

They brought her bread after that.

It should have been absurd—ghosts serving bread—and it was, and it wasn't, because the bread was real in the way everything here was real: baked from remembered wheat by a baker who had died sixty years ago and still rose at four each morning because rising at four was the only part of him left. It tasted almost like bread. It tasted like the idea of bread described by someone who missed it terribly. Freya ate it anyway. Her body insisted, and she had learned on the road that the body knows things the heart argues about.

While she ate, they showed her the rest.

They took her up through the city at its evening hour—the hour that never ended, the golden light frozen mid-fall over the rooftops—and showed her the seams. A street where the cobbles had gone translucent, fading like breath off glass. A fountain pouring water upward because the memory of falling had worn out. A market square where a fruit seller stood at his stall arranging oranges that had been the same seven oranges for thirty years, arranging them, rearranging them, forever, his hands moving through the motions of plenty in a country with no orchards left.

"He doesn't know he's repeating," Second said quietly. "None of the sleepers know. Only the choir stays awake, and only because staying awake is our duty. Someone must keep the count."

Freya watched the fruit seller set an orange just so, and adjust it a hair's width, and begin again. She thought of her own valley below the gate—the mill, the millpond, old Tamsin who swept her step every morning in exactly the same arcs, the whole valley moving through its rounds like a music box. She had always loved that about home. The steadiness of it. Now she saw the same motion everywhere she looked, and it terrified her.

"Is my valley like this?" she asked. "Under the ward? Are we repeating too?"

"Worse," said Vessel, without cruelty. "You're *forgetting*. A ward that size drinks something to stay fed, and it drinks from the people under it. That fog you all live in—that comfortable blindness? That's not innocence, child. That's the bill being paid. Your valley traded its memory of magic for safety, one generation at a time, and the trade gets steeper every year."

Freya stood very still in the frozen gold light.

*Everyone agreed to keep the illusion,* she had thought, back at the mill, watching her neighbors' eyes go calculating during the herald's proclamation. She had blamed them for it. For the willful blindness, the careful not-knowing. And all along they hadn't agreed to anything. They'd been *drunk on it*. Drunk and paying and never told the price.

"I'm the last key," she said. It wasn't a question anymore. It hadn't been since the tuning fork sang in her hand at her grandmother's grave.

"You are," Vessel said. "The bloodline thinned to a single thread, and the thread is you. A living Pemberton, standing at the heart of the inner door, willing it open. That is the whole of the spell. That is what the keymakers wrote into the stone a century ago, praying someone would read it in time."

"Then open it. Take me to the door. What are we waiting for?"

The ghosts exchanged a look. It passed between them like weather.

"Because," Vessel said, "a door opens both ways. And the outer lock—the one your blood anchors—is the ward around your valley. Open the inner door fully, and the seal comes undone top to bottom. The valley's blindness lifts in an hour. Every gifted soul between the mill and the mountains suddenly shines like a beacon to every Inquisitor in Vell." She let that sit. "How many do you think the crown would find?"

Freya thought of the proclamation. Of the herald's voice reading the Edict of Cleansing while the whole village stood in the dust doing arithmetic with their neighbors' lives. Of Widow Marsh, who could coax blight off apple trees and had started locking her doors. Of the ferryman's son, who'd been taken already—taken quietly, in the night, before anyone even admitted he was anything at all.

"Hundreds," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Maybe a thousand. Small gifts, mostly. Nothing that fights."

"Small gifts burn the same," said Second.

"And if I don't open it?"

"Then Casper finishes starving. Years yet, perhaps—we are patient, we have learned patience the way drowning men learn water. But it ends. All of it. Every name on every wall. The last true thing the old world ever made, gone, and no one outside will ever know it existed." Vessel's hood turned toward her. "So. There is your arithmetic, gatekeeper. One kingdom or one valley. Nine thousand souls already spent, or a thousand more put at risk. A door that saves everyone it opens and damns everyone it passes over. We did not choose this bargain. We inherited it, the way you inherited the fork and the arch and the moss. But someone must choose it now, and the only person in two worlds with the right to choose is standing here eating ghost-bread."

Freya looked down at the crust in her hand. Almost-flesh of almost-wheat. She set it down carefully on the bench, as if it might break.

"You knew my grandmother," she said. "When she was young. She came here, didn't she. At least once."

"She came twice," Vessel said. "Once as a girl, with her mother, to learn the outer rites. And once alone, when she was older than you are now, after her daughter ran off with a singer from beyond the hills." The ghost's voice gentled. "She stood where you're standing and asked us the same question you're about to ask."

"What question?"

"*Why does it always have to be a choice.*"

Freya's throat closed. "What did you tell her?"

"The truth. That the keymakers were brilliant and hurried and afraid, and afraid people build locks, not answers. That the design was flawed because fear designs flawed things." Vessel stepped closer, and up close the flicker of her face resolved, briefly, into features Freya half recognized—the set of the jaw, the way of holding the head. Her grandmother's jaw. Some other grandmother entirely, a hundred years upstream. "But I will tell you one thing more, child, one thing we did not tell her, because we did not know it then. The inner door needs a life freely given—or so the inscription says. Those were the keymakers' terms. A life, freely given."

Freya went cold all over. "Whose life?"

"Whoever opens it, we assumed. Why else write it?" The ghost paused, and the pause stretched, and stretched, and somewhere above them the frozen golden hour trembled—actually *trembled*, the light stuttering like a candle in wind—and every ghost in the bell-chamber turned its head toward the stair as one.

"What was that?" Freya whispered.

Vessel's face had gone strange. Not frightened. *Reading* something, the way Aldric read weather in a hymn.

"The terms," she said slowly, "were written by people in a hurry, in a language that has been dead for a century, carved by masons who did not speak it natively. Words shift. Meanings erode. *A life*—" she shook her head, "—in the old tongue, the phrase can be read two ways. *En livr*, a life surrendered. Or *en lyver*—a name, given over. A life. Or a name. The difference between dying at the threshold and merely—" her flickering hands opened, empty, "—ceasing to be *anyone*. Unregistered. Unwritten. Unknown to every book and bond and bloodline in either world."

Above them, the light stuttered again, harder. Dust sifted down through gold. And far off, at the top of the long singing stair, Freya heard—faint, impossible, unmistakable—a human voice, ragged and cracking and absolutely alive, working its way down the scale.

Someone was descending. Someone was singing through the wards.

Vessel turned back to her, and beneath the hood her eyes were bright with something that in a living face would have been tears.

"It seems," said the last voice of Casper, "that the question of sacrifice has arrived ahead of schedule. Come, gatekeeper. Let's see who's knocking."
