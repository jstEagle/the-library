## 10. Lessons in the Drowned Cathedral

The cathedral had drowned a hundred years ago and never finished the business.

That was Aldric's way of explaining it, anyway, as we stood in the flooded nave where Casper's greatest choir had once rehearsed. Water lay shin-deep across the marble floor, so still it held the vaulted ceiling like a mirror, and above us the broken dome let down shafts of grey-gold light that moved with the slow weather of a sky that wasn't really there. The pews had rotted to lace. Candles floated on little wooden rafts, still lit, still burning after a century, because nothing in this country was allowed to finish.

"It didn't drown," I said. "It's being *held* underwater."

"Everything here is being held somewhere," Aldric said. "Between one breath and the next. The whole kingdom is a held breath." He waded forward, and the water rang faintly under his steps — not splashing, but sounding, each footfall a low note like a struck bell. I had noticed that about Casper since I'd arrived: the country answered sound before it answered touch. "Which is why we learn here. A held breath is easier to shape than a free one."

I looked at the water, at my own reflection floating among the candle flames, and thought: *and when the breath finally comes out, everything here ends.* I didn't say it. We had an unspoken agreement, Aldric and I, to spend our mornings pretending the bargain in the choir's crypt wasn't sitting between us like a third person.

*Only a living key can open the door,* the ghost-choir had told me. And living keys burn out. They had said that too, gently, the way you tell someone their house is on fire while they're still inside admiring the curtains.

"All right," I said. "Teach me."

Aldric smiled — that tired, crooked smile that made him look ten years younger and a hundred years sadder at once — and gestured me onto the rotted remains of the choir platform, which held our weight the way everything here held everything: out of habit, out of grief, out of a stubborn refusal to be done.

* * *

He began, as he put it, with the worst news possible.

"You cannot sing."

"I sang in the village choir until I was twelve."

"You *made noise* in time with other people making noise," he said. "That's not singing. Singing is listening with your whole body. The gate-songs aren't music, Freya. They're questions. You don't perform them. You ask them, and the world answers, and you have to hear the answer or you're just shouting at stone."

"Stone has never once answered me."

"Stone has been waiting a hundred years for someone to ask properly." He stepped closer, and up close he smelled of woodsmoke and old paper and something green, like crushed leaves — the smell of the road, of a man who had been running for a long time and hadn't stopped long enough to wash it off. "Give me your hands."

I gave them. He turned them palms-up, studied them like a cartographer studying a coastline, then pressed two fingers into the heel of my left palm.

"What do you feel?"

"Fingers."

"*Freya.*"

I closed my eyes and actually listened, which apparently was the entire lesson and I had already failed it twice. Under his fingertips there was — something. A hum. Not in my hand exactly. In the water around us, in the air, in the cathedral itself, a vibration so constant I had stopped hearing it days ago the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat.

"The hum," I said. "The one that's everywhere."

"That hum is Casper holding its shape. It's the song the ghost-choir has been singing without pause since the sealing." His fingers pressed harder. "And *this*" — the pressure found a point that sparked, sudden and cold, up my wrist and into my teeth — "is where the hum passes through *you*. Because you're a key, and keys are made of the same metal as locks."

I snatched my hand back. The spark kept ringing in my bones like a swallowed bell.

"That hurt."

"Good. Pain means the note is true. Now—" he took my hand again, gentler now, arranging my fingers against his like we were about to dance some formal court measure, "—we start with the First Question. It only has three notes. Children in the royal choir learned it at five."

"And what does the First Question do?"

"Nothing, by itself. It's a greeting. You say hello to a lock before you ask it to open." His voice dropped, and though it was barely louder than speech, the water around us shivered, rings spreading from his boots in perfect circles. "*Hello* is a very powerful word, if you mean it."

He sang three notes.

I will do my best to write them down, and I will fail, because they were not really notes at all. The first was low and warm, a foundation-stone of a note. The second bent upward through the middle of it, questioning. The third resolved — no. The third *refused* to resolve. It hung there, open, unfinished, and the whole cathedral leaned toward it. The candles guttered. The water rose in a fine mist off the surface, hanging in the air like held breath made visible.

Then it faded, and Aldric looked at me with an expression I would come to know very well over the following weeks: patient, encouraging, and braced for disaster.

"Your turn."

I opened my mouth and produced a sound like a cart with a bad axle rolling downhill into a flock of geese.

The mist fell. The candles steadied. Somewhere high in the ruined dome, a pigeon — a memory-pigeon, one of the city's repeating dead birds — woke, screamed its last scream from a hundred years ago, and died again.

Aldric pressed his lips together very hard.

"Don't," I warned.

"I'm not laughing."

"Your shoulders are laughing."

"My shoulders are *sympathizing*." But he couldn't hold it, and the laugh came out of him anyway, rough and real, the first true laugh I'd heard from him since the gate. It echoed strangely in the drowned nave, and — this was the part I didn't tell him — the water rang with it, sweetly, as if the cathedral were glad.

We tried again. And again. My third attempt frightened a shoal of memory-fish into fleeing through a wall. My seventh made the nearest candle raft spin in place like a confused beetle. By the twentieth I had learned to find the first note, the warm foundation-stone one, and hold it — barely, wavering, like a child standing on ice.

"There," Aldric said quietly. "There it is."

"There's *what*? That was terrible."

"That was the first honest sound you've made in your life." He was looking at me with an intensity that made the back of my neck hot. "Do you understand what you've been doing wrong? You've been trying to make the note *correct*. Correct is a cage. The note doesn't want correct. It wants *true*. You grew up in a valley that agreed to forget. You've spent your whole life keeping things quiet — your voice, your blood, all of it. Well." He spread his hands, and the light from the broken dome caught the grey in his dark hair, the lines around his eyes. "This is the one place in two worlds where quiet is a lie. Let it out or go home."

Something in me cracked along an old seam.

I thought of my grandmother's hands burning letters in the hearth. Of twenty-two years of lowering my voice, dulling my eyes, walking small past neighbors who had agreed, all of us, together, to pretend our valley was nothing but mud and sheep and rain. Of the Inquisitors coming, and Mrs. Harrow's face as she watched me run. Of a whole life spent as a held breath.

I sang the first note again, and this time I did not try to make it correct. I made it *mine*. Low, and angry, and grieving, and true.

The cathedral answered.

Every candle flared. The water leapt in a thousand bright beads. High overhead, in the broken dome, something vast and ancient shifted — dust falling in golden sheets, and beneath the dust, faintly, impossibly, the ghost of a response: three notes returned, from somewhere deep in the country's bones, like a sleeper turning toward a voice calling her name.

Then silence. Then dripping.

Aldric stared at me. "That," he said finally, "was the gate answering."

"It was?"

"The First Question is a greeting. Something just greeted you back." He laughed again, shakier this time. "Freya, I have sung at the doors of wards that could crack mountains. I have sung the Second Question in front of the crown's own artificers. Do you know how many times anything has greeted me back?"

"How many?"

"Once." He wouldn't say more, and I knew — I knew with a certainty that settled into my chest like a stone dropping into a well — that the once had been Serane Voss, back when she was a girl in the royal choir and not the crown's knife. Some doors in a man stay shut even after you've learned to sing them open.

* * *

We worked every day, or what passed for day in a country where the light came and went like a tide.

Mornings: the First Question, until my voice stopped shaking. Then the Second, which was harder — a rising line of seven notes that asked a lock *may I*, and required you to genuinely accept the possibility of *no*. "You can't trick a ward," Aldric said. "Wards were built by people who expected liars. You have to stand in front of the door honestly willing to walk away, and mean it, and then ask." I asked the drowned cathedral's great west door if I might pass, meaning it, and the door groaned open six inches and stuck, and Aldric declared it the finest failure he'd ever heard.

Afternoons: listening. He'd send me out into the memory-streets with instructions to sit still for an hour and catalog every sound the country made. This was worse than the singing, because the sounds of Casper were the sounds of its dying. The baker who relit his oven every hour forever. The mother on the corner who called her children home at dusk, every dusk, and no children ever came. The bells of the evening service that rang and rang with no ringers, a hymn with the heart cut out of it.

"They repeat their last day," I told him one evening, back on the choir platform, watching the false dusk settle gold over the flooded nave. "The dead. Is that what waiting does? Is that what a century of *almost* looks like?"

"Yes," Aldric said simply. "I've seen it happen to the living, too."

He sat down beside me at the platform's edge, boots dangling over the water, and for a while neither of us spoke. The candles drifted. Somewhere far off, the mother called her children home.

"My choir in the capital," he said at last. "Forty voices. I built it from nothing — street kids, fishwives' sons, a chimney sweep with a voice like a silver trumpet. The crown called it a gathering of the gifted. It was a gathering of children who liked to harmonize." His jaw tightened. "When the Edict came, I got eleven of them out through the sewers before Voss's people sealed the district. Eleven. Out of forty." He looked at his hands. "I've been running ever since. Not away from them. Toward the only thing left that could matter — this gate. Carrying the rest of them in my throat. Every song I sing, I sing the missing twenty-nine underneath it. That's why my voice is what it is. It isn't talent, Freya. It's weight."

"I know about weight," I said. "I'm the last of mine."

"No," he said. "You're the *first* of yours. There's a difference. Lasts carry graves. Firsts carry doors."

I turned to look at him, and he was already looking at me, and the space between us went quiet in a way that had nothing to do with the valley's enforced silence and everything to do with two people discovering they had been lonely in the same language.

"Aldric." My voice came out lower than I intended. "The choir told me what opening the door costs. A living key burns out. And you've been singing wards apart since before I was born — your voice is half-spent already. If it comes down to who stands at the inner door—"

"Stop."

"—then it should be me, because I'm the key, and you're the singer, and a singer can—"

"*Stop.*" He said it softly, which was somehow worse. "You don't get to spend yourself to save me. I've buried enough people who decided their lives were currency. I won't teach you songs just to watch you pay for a door with your own—"

"Then why are you teaching me?" It came out sharper than I meant, cracking down the middle. "If we both know how this ends, why are we spending our evenings here, on this platform, with the candles and the water and you fixing my posture with your hands like we have *time*—"

"Because we do have time," he said. "Right now. This evening. This hour." He reached over and took my hand — the left one, palm-up, and pressed two fingers to the spot below my thumb where the country's hum ran through me, and this time the spark that jumped wasn't pain. It was warmth, spreading up my arm, and I couldn't tell anymore whether it came from the gate-songs or from him, and I suspected the distinction had quietly stopped existing. "That's all any of us ever get. One note at a time. The dead here learned it too late — they tried to hold the whole song, keep it safe, freeze it perfect, and it killed them. The lesson, Freya — the *real* lesson — is to sing the note you're in and let the next one come."

The water rang softly beneath us. The candles burned. Above, through the broken dome, the first stars of Casper's remembered night appeared — stars that had been dead for longer than the kingdom, whose light was still arriving, still faithful, still crossing the distance.

"One more verse," Aldric said. "The Third Question. It's the hardest one."

"What does it ask?"

He hesitated, and I saw — clearly, the way you see lightning before the thunder — that he knew exactly what it asked, and that it was dangerous, and that he was going to teach it to me anyway.

"It asks a door to become a wall," he said. "Or a wall to become a door. It asks a thing to change what it is. It's the verse the founders used to seal Casper away, and it's the verse that will open it again." He met my eyes. "No choir has sung the Third Question complete in a hundred years. When you learn it, every ward in two worlds will feel it. Including the ones Serane Voss carries."

"Then she'll know where we are."

"She'll know *what we're attempting*. There's a difference." His fingers were still on my pulse-point, and I felt my heartbeat speed under them and pretended I didn't, and felt him notice, and pretend the same. "We'll need it soon. The inner door isn't a lock, Freya. It's a choice. And choices require vocabulary."

So he taught me.

The Third Question had nine notes, and the ninth did not exist anywhere in the scales of the waking world — it lived in the gap between two ordinary notes, the silence inside a harmony, and finding it was like trying to look directly at the dark between stars. I failed at it for what felt like hours. I failed at it with the geese-cart sound. I failed at it so thoroughly that two memory-beggars in the square outside stopped mid-pace to stare through the wall at me.

And then —

I don't know what happened. Perhaps it was the warmth of his hand still lingering on my wrist. Perhaps it was exhaustion stripping away everything in me that was careful. Perhaps it was the country itself leaning close, hungry for the sound of its own name. I stopped trying to find the ninth note and simply sang the eighth and meant, with everything I had, for there to be something after it.

The note came out of the silence instead of out of me.

The cathedral did not shiver this time. It *rang* — every rotted pew, every flooded flagstone, every candle flame standing up straight and tall — and far away, beyond the walls of the drowned nave, beyond the memory-streets and the repeating dead, something enormous turned over in its sleep. I felt it in my teeth. I felt it in the scar-tissue of the Hollow Gate, which I still carried somewhere behind my sternum like a second heartbeat.

And I felt something else. Far off, thin as a wire: a voice I recognized, lifted in answer. Not a greeting. A warning. A single sustained note of iron and certainty, coming from the direction of the valley, getting closer.

Aldric went rigid beside me.

"That's her," he breathed. "That's *Voss*. She's found the outer ward." He was already moving, hauling me up from the platform edge, water streaming from our clothes. "How — the ward should have held another month—"

"It held until tonight," I said, and my mouth had gone dry. "Until I sang. She didn't find the ward, Aldric. She heard me practicing."

Above us, the remembered stars wheeled on, indifferent. Below us, the drowned cathedral slowly settled back into its held breath. And out past the edge of the country that existed only in memory and song, something with a sword and a conscience was stepping through a broken ward, following the sound of my voice home.
