## 11. Serane Crosses the Threshold

The ward around Vell was old work, and old work had a smell.

Serane Voss stood at the valley's rim in the grey hour before dawn and breathed it in: cold iron and river stone and something underneath, something green, like moss grown over a blade. The other Inquisitors smelled nothing. That was the difference between them and her. They had been taught to find the gifted. She had been taught to *listen* for them, and listening was a kind of gift the crown preferred not to name.

"Outer perimeter's quiet, Inquisitor," said Corporal Hesk, coming up the slope with his lantern hooded. "No lights. No movement. The village sleeps like the dead."

"The village sleeps like a lie," Serane said. "That's not the same thing."

Hesk didn't answer. He was a good soldier in the way of men who had learned that questions were a debt that always came due. She appreciated that in him. She had no patience left for curiosity.

Below her, Vell lay folded in its little valley like a child pretending to be asleep — the thatched roofs, the mill with its sagging wheel, the crooked lane where she had once watched a Pemberton woman buy apples and laugh at something the fruit-seller said. Twenty years ago. She had been a different person then, or she had been this person and simply hadn't finished being built yet. It was hard to remember which.

The Edict was clear. The Edict was always clear; that was its whole genius. *All persons of gifted blood shall be surrendered to the crown's officers for assessment.* Assessment. She had run four hundred assessments in eleven years. She knew precisely what the word meant, and she had learned to say it without her mouth changing shape.

And the last Pemberton had run. Through the Hollow Gate, according to the tracker's report, which was itself impossible, because the Hollow Gate was a moss-eaten arch in a hillside that led to a hillside, and had for a hundred years. Except it hadn't led to a hillside last night. Except a woman had stepped through a door that did not exist and vanished like a coin into deep water.

Serane had read the old files. All of them, the ones the crown kept and the ones the crown didn't know it kept, because archivists were people too and people could be gently, gently persuaded. A century ago, the kingdom of Casper had not fallen. It had *closed*. There was a difference, and the difference had a name, and the name had been burned out of every record she'd ever touched, leaving only a hole shaped like a word.

She intended to walk into that hole.

"Inquisitor." Hesk again, cautious. "The ward. If we breach it without the artificers—"

"The artificers are three days behind us with their iron hymns and their wagons," Serane said. "The Pemberton woman has a night and a morning on us. By the time the engineers arrive, whatever door she opened will have learned her name and forgotten ours." She pulled off her gloves, finger by finger. "So we go through now. Before it forgets us is not the problem. Before it *closes* is."

"And the ward itself?"

"I'll handle the ward."

* * *

She had broken wards before. It was not a battle; it was more like the moment in a song when a key changes and every singer has to find their footing again. You found the note the ward was built on, and you sang against it — not louder, *truer*, until the false note had nowhere left to stand.

The valley's ward was anchored to blood. She had known that since the files, since the child in the capital's holding cells had told her, in that terrible small voice, *the gatekeeper's line, the gatekeeper's line, everyone knows it's the gatekeeper's line* — and then had wept, and Serane had given her the honeyed water, and had written in her report *subject cooperative, assessment complete*, and had not slept for two nights afterward, and had slept fine ever since, which was worse.

She stood at the valley's rim and she sang.

Not loudly. The crown had trained its Inquisitors in the old discipline — the *counter-song*, they called it, though the crown called it nothing at all, because naming it would have meant admitting what it was for. Her voice went out over the sleeping valley low and steady, a note chosen to sit against the ward's foundation and lean.

The ward resisted. Of course it did. It had been holding for a hundred years, a patient green thing, and it knew her note for what it was. But a ward anchored to a bloodline is only as strong as the bloodline, and the bloodline had just run through a hole in the world, and a key that has turned is a key that has loosened.

Serane felt it give the way you feel ice give under a boot — not all at once, but in a long, hairline shudder that ran from her feet to her teeth.

The valley exhaled.

She hadn't known a ward could do that. Hadn't known it could feel like anything. But the whole of Vell seemed to sigh — the river flattening, the wind dying, the last of the green smell lifting off the slopes like a sheet pulled from furniture in a house no one lives in — and for one moment Serane stood inside a silence so complete she could hear her own heart, and under it, faint and far away, something that might have been singing.

She shut that door in herself as quickly as she'd learned to. There was a room in her where such sounds were put, and the room had no lamp, and she did not go in.

"Perimeter's down," she said. "Move. Single file, no torches. We follow her trail to the gate."

* * *

The Hollow Gate was worse than the files described, because the files described a ruin and what stood in the hillside was a *wound*.

The moss-covered arch — she had expected the moss, had expected the worn stones, the whole picturesque decay of it — was split down the center like a loaf. Not broken. *Opened*, the way a mouth opens, the way a book opens, with intention. The air inside it was the wrong color: not dark, not light, a grey-violet twilight that belonged to no hour of any day she had ever stood in. And it moved. It breathed, almost. The arch drew air in and let it out, slow as a sleeper.

Hesk stood well back from it, and he was not the only one. Two of the six had drifted toward the treeline without appearing to decide to.

"Inquisitor," Hesk said, and his voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a man standing on a floor he no longer trusts. "That's a doorway to nowhere."

"That's a doorway to somewhere the crown has spent a hundred years pretending isn't there," Serane said. "Which makes it the most important doorway in the kingdom."

"And if it's a trap?"

"Then it's a trap that's been waiting a century for one Pemberton woman to walk through it, and I doubt we've improved the flavor." She drew her sword — not because she expected to need it, but because her hand wanted the weight of a thing that was only a thing. "Wait here. If I'm not back by midday, hold the gate. The engineers will want it intact."

"And if it closes behind you?"

Serane looked at the breathing arch. Somewhere on the other side of it, a woman who had grown up buying apples and laughing was running through a country that had no business existing, and somewhere behind that woman, Serane's own future was closing like a hand.

"Then hold it anyway," she said, and stepped through.

* * *

The crossing was one step long and lasted a season.

She came out onto a road — an actual road, cobbled, crowned for drainage, the work of masons who had expected their work to be walked on for a thousand years — under a sky the color of the inside of a shell. Twilight stood on every side of her and did not move. There was a sun somewhere behind the cloud-veil, or the memory of a sun, a pale coin of light that gave illumination and no warmth at all.

And the city.

The files had said *ruin*. The files had said *empty*. The files, Serane understood now, had been written by people who had come here and seen exactly this and had been unable, or unwilling, to write the truth, which was that Casper was not ruined and not empty. Casper was *paused*. The street before her ran downhill into a town that looked as though a hand had been laid flat upon it mid-morning: shutters half-open, a cart of vegetables standing unattended with one cabbage rolled into the gutter, washing strung between upper windows stirring in no wind at all. Doors stood ajar. Somewhere, distantly, a hammer struck an anvil — once — and did not strike again.

"Hello," Serane said, because she was an Inquisitor and procedure held even at the edge of the impossible, and her voice went out and came back flattened, as if the town were listening and disapproving of the volume.

She followed the road down. The Pemberton woman's trail was easy to read here — the dust of the cobbles held her bootprints with a clarity that felt less like luck than like *attention*, as if the whole country were a page that remembered whatever touched it. Serane noted that, and noted her own noting of it, and put both observations in the room with no lamp.

The town had a name. She read it off a fountain at the crossroads, letters worn but legible: *Marenholt*. And under the name, a date, and under the date a line of smaller text, and Serane knelt and brushed the moss away with her thumb and read:

*In the hundredth year of the Sealing, let the city keep what the world could not.*

The Sealing. Capital S. Not the Fall, not the End. The word choice of someone who had done a thing on purpose and wanted it remembered as mercy.

She stood up. Her hand was still on the fountain's rim, and the water in the basin — there should not have been water, a century of neglect, the pipes should have rotted — the water was clear and still, and it was showing her something.

Not a reflection.

A kitchen. A small stone kitchen with a window over the sink, and a woman standing at a table rolling dough, and a child on a stool beside her, maybe four years old, singing — a nonsense song, a made-up song, the kind children invent and adults pretend not to treasure. The woman was laughing. The light through the window was gold. The child's voice went up and up on the last line, proud and tuneless and utterly happy, and the woman caught the flour-dusted little hands and sang the last line back to her, *true*, and the child gasped with delight, and—

Serane took her hand off the fountain.

The water stilled. The kitchen vanished. Her own face looked back at her from the basin, pale and hard, an Inquisitor's face, a face built for holding.

She knew that kitchen.

She did not know that kitchen. She had never been in Marenholt in her life; she had been born in the capital, in the Foundling Wing of the Crown Chorale, where the records said she'd been left on the steps in winter with no name but the one the chorale gave her. *Serane, for the season of her finding.* She knew that. She had that on paper. She had built eleven years of work on the solid ground of that.

And yet her hand remembered flour. Her throat remembered the shape of the last line of that nonsense song, the *true* version, the one the mother sang, and it sat in her throat now like a stone that had always been there and had only just been noticed.

"No," Serane said aloud, to the fountain, to the town, to whatever was doing this. "I don't buy from ghosts."

The town said nothing. The anvil did not strike again. The washing hung unmoving between its windows, and Serane Voss walked on down the cobbled street with her sword drawn and her jaw set, and behind her the fountain water trembled once, gently, like a thing deciding to wait.

* * *

She found the choir in the cathedral square at dusk-that-was-not-dusk, and she should have kept walking.

She knew that. Later, in the cell of herself where she kept her failures, she would file it there: *I saw them and I stopped.* A dozen figures standing in the square in a half-ring, robed in the faded colors of a court that no longer existed, their faces turned toward the cathedral's broken towers. They were singing. It was the sound from under the ward, the sound from the valley's rim, the sound she had spent a lifetime not-hearing, and it was more beautiful than anything the Crown Chorale had ever trained into her, and it was wrong, wrong the way a portrait's eyes are wrong, because the singers did not breathe. The song went through them like wind through a reed bed, and none of their chests moved.

They were singing the names of streets. She listened despite herself — listened the way you look at an accident — and the song was a census. *Ellow Lane, and who walked it: Bren the cooper, and his sons, and his sons' dog, and the bread cart at dawn.* They were singing the town into staying. Street by street, name by name, a hundred years of a hundred years, a country held together by throat and grief.

And in the middle of the song, without any of them turning, without any of them moving at all, the nearest singer said:

"You were one of ours."

Serane's sword came up. It was absurd and she did it anyway, steel against singers who had no bodies that mattered, and the singer — a woman, or the memory of a woman, grey at the temples and kind at the eyes in a way that made Serane's chest hurt — looked at the blade with mild sorrow.

"You were one of ours," she said again. "Marenholt choir, the children's voices. You had the high line in the winter canticle. You always came in early. Your mother used to say—" and here the ghost paused, and something moved across her face like weather, "—your mother used to say, *let her come in early, the song is better when she leads.*"

"My mother," Serane said, and her voice came out level, which cost her more than the ward had, "was the Crown Chorale's record of a foundling. Winter. No name. I have it on paper."

"Paper," the ghost said, gently, the way you'd repeat a word in a language you were trying to learn. "The crown took the children out before the Sealing. The smallest ones, the ones who'd forget. They thought it was mercy too." Her eyes were terrible and patient. "They left us a list. You're on it, child. Third from the top. We've sung your name every year."

"Stop." The word cracked out of her. "Stop *singing* it."

"We could never stop. That's what a choir is."

Serane backed away, one step, two, sword still up, and the singers did not follow, because they had not been following anything for a hundred years. The census-song went on around her, street by street, name by name, and she put her hands over her ears and it did not matter, because the song was not coming through her ears. It was coming up through the cobbles, through the soles of her boots, through the floor of her, into the room with no lamp, and the door of that room was rattling now, rattling in its frame, and from under the door, faint and terrible and unmistakable, came a child's voice singing the last line of a nonsense song, coming in early, always early, the song better when she leads—

"NO."

She shouted it into the square, and her voice broke the song the way her counter-song had broken the ward — not louder, *truer*, truer to the thing she had built herself to be. The census-song faltered. The grey woman closed her eyes. And in the ringing silence Serane Voss stood in the middle of a dead country that knew her name, and did the only thing left that she knew how to do.

She turned her back on it, and she walked.

The Pemberton woman's trail led uphill, out of Marenholt, toward the high country and the cathedral ruins and, the tracker's report said, a second door — an inner door, the one that mattered. Serane followed it because following it was the duty, and duty was the floor she stood on, and if she stopped standing on it she did not know what was underneath.

Behind her, very softly, the choir took the song up again. And though she walked fast, and farther, and did not look back, she could not stop her ears from catching the newest verse — the one they had added, the one they sang now every year, third from the top of the list:

*Serane, who was taken. Serane, who will come home.*

She walked faster.

She did not come home. She climbed, all the grey night, toward the inner door and the two sets of footprints that led to it — one set light and quick, one set heavier, a man's, a stranger's — and it was not until she crested the last ridge at the hour that was no hour and saw them, small in the distance, two figures standing before a door of white light, that she understood the thing the choir had not said and the fountain had only shown her:

She had not been sent here to catch the Pemberton woman.

She had been sent here because the crown suspected what a fountain in a dead town knew for certain, and the crown did not send Inquisitors to arrest their own past. They sent them to bury it.

Serane drew her sword one more time, and this time her hand was not steady, and this time it was not the ghost-song making it shake — and she started down the slope toward the door, the two figures, and the end of every true thing she had ever been told.
