## 12. The Market of Last Days

The market appeared where no market should be, in the flooded ruin of what had once been Casper's customs house.

They came down to it through streets that grew quieter the deeper they went, past the ghost-choir's territory, past the last of the singing, into a district where even the memories seemed to hold their breath. Freya felt the change before she understood it. The air thickened. The light, which everywhere else in Casper came from nowhere and everywhere at once, gathered itself here into a single source: a lantern hanging above a stall, burning with a flame that gave off no heat and cast no shadows.

The stall was built from the ribs of a ship. Behind it sat an old woman—or something shaped like one—with hands folded on the counter and eyes like two coins dropped into milk.

"You've come to trade," she said. Not a question. Her voice was dry paper, patient as dust.

"We're looking for the Bridge of Nailed Songs," Aldric said. "We were told you know the way."

"I know every way." The broker unfolded her hands. "Ways are cheap. Knowing is dear." She turned her coin-eyes on Freya, and Freya felt them go through her like hooks through cloth. "But you're not here for ways, are you, gatekeeper. You're here for the crossing. And the crossing takes more than knowing. It takes *weight*. The bridge holds nothing that isn't paid for."

"What does it cost?" Freya asked, though some cold part of her already knew. In Casper, everything ran on the same currency. She'd watched it for days now—the dead repeating their last days because those days were all they had left to spend; the choir sustaining the city out of memory worn thin as communion linen; the wraiths that swarmed anything bright and unclaimed. Memory was money here. Memory was bread, and timber, and rope.

"Whatever you can spare," the broker said, "and more than you think you can."

Aldric shifted beside her. "There has to be another way across."

"There's always another way." The broker smiled without moving her mouth. "Through the chasm, over the chasm, around the chasm. All of them end the same way. The bridge asks its toll, and if you arrive empty-handed, it takes from your bones instead. I have seen what walks away from an unpaid crossing." She paused, letting the silence do its work. "It doesn't walk far."

Freya looked at the stall properly then. Behind the old woman, stacked in rows like jars of preserves, were hundreds of small glass vials. Each held a curl of light—a color, a sound, almost a smell. Some glowed warm gold. Some were pale as frost. One near the edge flickered a bruised purple, and when Freya looked at it too long she felt a grief that wasn't hers settle into her chest like rain.

"You sell them," Freya said. "To whom?"

"To anyone who has coin. To anyone who wants to remember something they never lived." The broker lifted a vial—gold, warm—and turned it in the light. "Half this city was bought back piece by piece from me. The dead woman who repeats her marketing because she sold the day she died. The soldier who marches his last morning because his final memory was taken as tax. I am not a monster, girl. I am a *bank*."

"And what do we have," Aldric said quietly, "that a bank would want?"

The broker's coin-eyes found him. "You? A fugitive with a country in your throat." She said it lightly, but Freya saw Aldric flinch. "Your memories are strange ones, choir-master. Songs remember you back. But yes. There are things in you worth buying."

"And her?" Aldric's voice had gone flat and careful, the voice he used when he was frightened and refusing to show it.

"Oh, *her*." For the first time, the broker leaned forward. The lantern flame bent toward Freya like a listener. "You are the most valuable thing to walk into this market in a hundred years. Do you know what you are? A living key. Every memory in you is anchored to iron—to the gate, to the ward, to a valley full of people who don't know they're protected by your grandmother's stubbornness and your blood. Your memories aren't just yours. They're *load-bearing*."

Freya's mouth went dry. "Then selling them would—"

"Would loosen things. Yes." The broker sat back, folding her hands again, and there was something almost gentle in it, which was worse. "Take a stone from an arch and see how long the arch stands. Most people, I could strip bare and they'd walk away lighter, happier, free. You—" she tapped one grey fingernail on the counter, once, twice, "—you could sell me three memories before the cracks start showing. Four, if you're lucky. Sell me five and you'll forget which door you're keeping, and the gate will forget you, and everything your bloodline did for a century will come undone in an afternoon while the Inquisitors sharpen their knives in your valley."

The word *Inquisitors* landed between them like a dropped knife.

"You know about them," Aldric said.

"I know about everyone who owes me or is owed. It amounts to the same city." The broker began drawing vials from under the counter, arranging them in a fan before her, each one glowing faintly with someone else's yesterday. "So. Terms. You'll want the inner door. The bridge will take you to it. The bridge requires payment—paid here, in advance, sealed into you so the stones recognize their owners. Two crossings, two prices. And because you are what you are, gatekeeper, I will tell you the truth no one tells customers: choose carefully. Whatever you sell me, you will not misremember. You will not half-remember. You will *not remember*, and the shape it leaves behind will ache like a pulled tooth for the rest of your life."

* * *

They walked away from the stall to argue, though there was nowhere in Casper that counted as private—the drowned cathedral's bells hummed overhead, and somewhere behind a shuttered window a dead man practiced his scales forever.

"She's right about one thing," Aldric said. "I shouldn't sell much. My songs live in my memories. Strip enough of them and the bridge gets crossed by a man with a beautiful voice and nothing to say." He rubbed his face. His hands were shaking slightly, and Freya pretended not to notice. "I'll give her the years in the capital. The conservatory. Serane, if it comes to that." A pause. "Better I lose them than keep them and fail."

"Serane," Freya repeated.

"She was my friend before she was the crown's. If I have to choose what to lose, I'd rather lose the part of me that still grieves her. It's the part that slows my hand." He said it like a man pricing a limb.

Freya looked out over the water. Somewhere beneath it lay the true Casper, the living one, drowned and dreaming. Above it hung the memory-Casper, sustained by a choir of ghosts and a dying gate and, apparently, a banking system.

"My whole life," she said slowly, "I thought the worst thing that could happen was losing the valley. Losing the people. That was the nightmare I planned around. Keep the rituals. Tend the arch. Don't let anyone see you're different. And now I'm standing here learning that I can lose pieces of myself instead, one at a time, and call it payment." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "My grandmother would say that's exactly what a key is *for*. Being spent."

"Don't." Aldric touched her arm. "Don't do that thing where you make it noble before you've even decided. Decide first. Noble after, if it helps."

She looked at him. In the lantern-light from the distant market he looked older than he had in the valley, younger than he had in the cathedral, and tired in a way that sleep wouldn't fix. He had known her mother. He had sung through locks for strangers until the crown made him a fugitive. He carried a country in his throat and he was offering to cut pieces out of himself to carry it further, and he'd do it without complaint, and that was the most frightening thing about him.

"The happiest day of my life," Freya said, "was the day my grandmother taught me the first verse of the gate-song. I was nine. We were up at the arch at dawn, and there was frost on the moss, and she sang, and the gate hummed back at her, and she let me hum along. Just once. She said the gate would remember it even if I didn't." Her voice caught. "I've thought about that day every time things got bad. Every single time. It's the day I go to when the world is too heavy."

The broker's stall waited in its pool of false light.

"That's the one, isn't it," Aldric said quietly. Not a question either.

"It's load-bearing," Freya said, and hated how steady her voice came out. "She told me the gate remembers. So maybe if I sell the remembering, the gate keeps its copy. Maybe that's the trick of it—I'm not destroying it, just... moving it. Paying with the coin and trusting the vault."

"Maybe," Aldric said. "Or maybe you sell it and spend the rest of your life wondering why dawn makes you sad."

Freya closed her eyes. She thought about her grandmother's hands, cracked and certain, on the tuning fork of white iron that now hung against Freya's sternum. She thought about the Edict read at the mill, and the neighbors' calculating eyes, and the hearth-full of burning letters, and the crack in the world she'd fallen through with soldiers at her heels. She thought about a kingdom full of people who had been locked away from life for a hundred years, waiting for a key who kept hesitating.

"They're starving," she said. "All of them. And I'm rationing my *memories*."

* * *

The transaction took less time than a confession.

The broker had Freya sit on a stool before the counter, and produced a vial of clear glass and a needle-thin tool of white metal that looked uncomfortably like a smaller cousin of the tuning fork.

"Hold this," the broker said, pressing the fork-end gently beneath Freya's jaw, "and think of the day. All of it. Leave nothing out. The memory must be whole or the vial cracks."

Freya held still. Beneath her jaw, the little fork hummed, and the day rose up in her with terrible clarity: the frost on the moss silvering in the first light. Her breath steaming. Grandmother's shawl smelling of woodsmoke and dried lavender. The first note—low, uncertain, hers—and the gate answering, a deep bronze hum she felt in her teeth and her chest and the soles of her feet. Grandmother laughing—not the careful laugh she used in the valley, but the real one, the one Freya had only ever heard up at the arch, where her grandmother got to be the keeper of a wonder instead of the hider of one.

*"Listen, little bird. A hundred years from now, someone will stand where you're standing and thank us both."*

The humming stopped.

Freya opened her eyes. The broker was corking a vial in which a small gold warmth curled and turned like a captured ember. Freya looked at it and felt—

Nothing.

That was the horror of it. Not absence-with-an-edge, not a scar she could probe. Nothing. She reached for the day and found a hole shaped exactly like it, smooth-walled, and around the hole everything else stood intact and accusing: her grandmother's deathbed, the confession, the white iron pressed into her palm. She remembered being nine. She remembered the arch at dawn. She remembered that something important had happened there, once, because the ache said so—but the middle was gone, scooped clean, and the ache poured into it like water into a footprint.

"There," the broker said softly. "Paid in full."

Freya stood. Her legs worked. Her voice, when she tested it, worked. She was still herself—she checked, frantically, item by item, the way you check pockets after a theft. Name: present. Valley: present. Mother: present, dimly, a woman gone too soon. Grandmother: present, but—

But thinner. As if a single thread had been pulled from a tapestry, and now the whole weave sat slightly wrong.

"How much did the bridge need?" Aldric asked the broker. His own turn had not yet come; he stood with his hands flat on the counter, waiting, and Freya realized he was steadying himself.

"For her, one was enough. The bridge weighs keys differently—it likes a large denomination rather than many small ones. Fewer wounds, longer seams." The broker turned her milk-and-coin eyes to Aldric. "For you, choir-master, the bridge wants two. The capital years, and—" she tilted her head, listening to something Freya couldn't hear, "—the first song you ever wrote. The one for the boy who drowned. You've carried it forty years. It's time."

Aldric went very still. "How do you know about that?"

"I'm a bank," the broker said. "Everything is deposited eventually."

He didn't argue. Freya watched him press the fork under his own jaw, watched his eyes go distant and wet and then simply distant, and when he surfaced he looked around the market with the mild confusion of a man who had entered a room and forgotten why. Then he shook it off, and straightened, and something in his posture had changed—heavier somehow, as if he'd set down bags he'd forgotten he was carrying and now missed the weight.

"Done?" he asked.

"Done," said the broker.

She slid the vials into a drawer lined with velvet, among hundreds of others, and Freya thought: *somewhere in that drawer is the happiest day of my life, filed between strangers' sorrows.* Then she thought nothing, because there was nothing left to think with, just the smooth-walled hole and the ache around it.

"One more thing," the broker said as they turned to leave. "Gratis, because you've paid honestly and I respect honesty in a dishonest age." She leaned forward, and the lantern flame bent toward her as if it too were a customer. "The bridge will ask you to sing to cross it. Two voices, matched. When it does—gatekeeper—do not reach for the day you sold. Everyone reaches for it. The hole pulls at you, especially mid-crossing, over the chasm. Reach for the hole and you'll fall into it." Her coin-eyes glittered. "Sing forward. Never backward. Do you understand me?"

"No," Freya said honestly.

"You will." The broker settled back among her shelves of other people's yesterdays. "Now go. The bridge is nailed and waiting, and you've given it cause to expect you. And gatekeeper—" the dry voice followed them into the dark, "—when the crown's wolf comes sniffing after you, and she will, tell her the bank extends her credit. She has more to sell than she knows, and more to buy than she dares."

Freya and Aldric exchanged a look in the dark—the look of two people who have each just paid a price they haven't finished counting—and walked out of the market of last days with their pockets full of holes, toward a chasm that could only be crossed by two voices exactly matched, and neither of them knew yet whether what remained of them would harmonize.

Behind them, in her drawer of velvet and glass, the small gold warmth of a frost-bright dawn curled slowly in its vial, waiting for a buyer who would never come—or, worse, one who would.
