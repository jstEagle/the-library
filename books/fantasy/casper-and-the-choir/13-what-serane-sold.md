## 13. What Serane Sold

The memory-broker's stall sat in a crooked lane that Serane had walked three times before finding it, because in Casper the lanes did not hold still. It was built into the ribs of an overturned market cart, draped with faded silk, and lit by lanterns that burned without flame—just a soft gold glow, like the last hour of a summer evening, trapped in glass.

The broker himself was small and neat and wrong. He had the face of a man who had been handsome once and had traded it away piece by piece, so that what remained was pleasant but generic, a face assembled from leftovers. His hands were clean and quick. When he smiled at her, she saw that his teeth were too even, like piano keys.

"Inquisitor," he said, bowing. "You've been circling."

"I don't circle," Serane said.

"Everyone circles." He gestured to a stool opposite his counter. "Sit. You want something. In my experience, Inquisitors want one of two things: information, or certainty. I can't sell you either." He spread his hands. "But I sell memory. And memory is where both of those things live."

Serane did not sit. She kept her hand near her sword and made herself look around the stall—the jars on the shelves behind him, each one holding a curl of light that moved like smoke underwater. Some were pale as milk. Some burned red. One, high on a shelf, glowed a deep and patient blue, and when she looked at it, something behind her ribs leaned toward it like a dog hearing its name.

She looked away from that one immediately.

"The Pemberton girl came here," she said. "The choir-master too. What did they trade?"

The broker's smile widened by a fraction. "That's not how this works. What they sold is theirs. What they bought is theirs. I keep no ledger anyone else can read."

"Then you're useless to me."

"I'm useful to everyone," he said mildly. "That's the whole trouble with me. You want to know what they took out of this place so you can predict them. But you're standing in Casper, Inquisitor. Nothing here behaves. You could interrogate every jar on those shelves and you'd still be guessing." He tilted his head. "Unless you'd rather stop guessing."

"How?"

He drew a small brass instrument from under the counter—a lens on a chain, like a jeweler's glass, but older, the brass gone green at the rim. "Memories leave residue. Everyone who trades leaves traces in the air of this lane, in the silk, in me. Hold this to your eye and you'll see the shape of what passed through. Not the content. The weight of it."

"And what does that cost?"

"Nothing," he said. "Consider it hospitality." His smile thinned just enough to show the mechanism beneath. "Though I'll note that you came here intending to buy. Everyone who comes here intending only to *look* ends up buying. It's a feature of the merchandise."

Serane held out her hand for the lens.

* * *

Through the glass, the lane caught fire—not fire, but the residue of feeling, hanging in the air like heat-shimmer over a road. She turned slowly, and the traces resolved: a bright thread where Freya Pemberton had stood, frayed at the edges, ragged with grief. Something had been torn out of her here, something large, and the hole it left still bled light.

And there, woven through it, another trace—older, deeper, a bass note under the girl's bright grief. A man's. Aldric's. His residue was strange: not torn but *layered*, as if he had sold the same memories many times, or as if his memories were so dense with music that they left harmonics in the air.

And under both of them, faint as a watermark—

Serane went very still.

There was a third trace in the lane. Old. Very old. Faded almost to nothing, the way a footprint fades on a stair that thousands have climbed. But it was there, pressed into the fabric of Casper itself: the residue of a woman who had stood in this exact spot, decades ago, and traded something enormous, and walked away lighter and emptier and alive.

She knew the stance. She knew the set of the shoulders. She had spent thirty years studying that stance from across interrogation tables.

It was her own.

* * *

"That's not possible," Serane said.

The broker had taken the lens back and was polishing it with a square of grey silk, unhurried. "No?"

"I have never been in Casper before this week."

"Mm," he said.

"This kingdom fell before I was born. It doesn't exist. That's not a metaphor; that's policy. There is no Casper. There is a sealed anomaly behind a ward in a valley of sheep farmers, and my duty is to see it neutralized before it destabilizes the realm."

"Certainly," the broker agreed. "That is the official position. I've read the proclamations. They're very well written." He set the lens down. "Would you like to know why your trace is sixty years deep in my lane?"

"No," Serane said.

"Because you're the first person to ask about it in twenty years, and I find I want to tell someone." He folded his hands. "Sixty years ago, a woman came to this stall. She was young. She was terrified. She carried a bundle wrapped in a blanket, and she could not feed it, and the soldiers were coming up the coast road, and she had heard there was a door out of the world. She sold nearly everything she had to pay a smuggler—her name, most of all. Names are expensive. Hers bought passage for two." He looked up. "Her name was Voss. She gave it to me willingly, because a nameless woman crosses borders more easily than a named one, and because she wanted her daughter to never be findable. She thought being unregistered would keep the child safe from exactly the people who are now hunting through my country with iron hymns."

The stall was very quiet. Somewhere above, in the broken sky of Casper, a bell rang a note that had no bell attached to it.

"You're lying," Serane said, and her voice came out flat and steady, which surprised her. "This is a technique. This whole kingdom is a weapon designed to compromise crown personnel. Memory-wraiths, ghost choirs, now this. I was trained for this. Emotional subversion through fabricated intimacy. It has a number. It's in the manual."

"It does," the broker said sympathetically. "Section nine. I'm familiar; I helped write some of the source material." He reached up to the high shelf and took down the deep blue jar. "But I didn't fabricate anything. I don't deal in fabrication. Fabrication spoils. Everything I sell is genuine, verified, and non-refundable." He set the jar on the counter between them. Inside, the light moved like a sleeping animal breathing. "Do you know what this is?"

"No."

"A memory. Someone's. Someone born in this kingdom, in a house with a blue door, on a street where the gutters ran sweet water in summer. Someone who, at six weeks old, was carried out of the world in a smuggler's arms while her mother paid for it with her own name." He rotated the jar slowly. "I don't sell children's memories, normally. Infants barely have any. But a choir-child's first year isn't stored in the mind, Inquisitor. It's stored in the *ears*. Every lullaby sung over a cradle settles somewhere deeper than forgetting reaches. When the crown took you at seven and scraped your mind clean for service—" he watched her flinch and continued gently, relentlessly, "—they missed the part of you that isn't mind. They always do. So this has been sitting on my shelf for thirty years, waiting. Your mother arranged for its return. Payment deferred. She was thorough that way."

"My mother died in childbirth," Serane said. "In a workhouse in Marrowfield. I checked the records myself. Twice."

"You checked the records the crown wrote about a woman who had sold her name. Records follow names. No name, no history. The workhouse entry is real—but it belongs to a different woman, a nameless pauper the clerks called *the Voss woman* because she wouldn't give them another." He slid the jar toward her. "Buy it back or don't. But if you break it, or arrest me, or burn this stall down, the memory dies with the jar, and you will spend the rest of your life wondering whether section nine applies to the sound of your own mother's voice."

* * *

She should walk out.

She catalogued the reasons with the cold efficiency she used for everything: the offer was a textbook compromise operation; accepting created leverage; a compromised Inquisitor is a failed Inquisitor; the crown's interest required her to turn and go. Thirty years of training lined up behind the decision like soldiers on a parade ground, and every one of them told her the same thing.

But her hand was already reaching for the jar.

Later, she would tell herself she picked it up to destroy it. Later, in the worst hours, she would construct elaborate justifications—that she meant to confiscate it as evidence, that she intended to study it for weaknesses in the enemy's methods. All lies. Her hand reached because a thing behind her ribs, older than her training, older than her name, had been leaning toward that blue light since she first saw it, the way a plant leans toward a window.

"How much?" she asked.

"For you? Nothing." The broker's too-even smile returned. "Your mother already paid."

* * *

She broke the seal in the alley behind the overturned cart, alone, because some part of her still believed she could control what happened next.

The memory poured in through her ears.

Blue door. Sweet water in the gutters. Warmth against her cheek that was a chest rising and falling, a heartbeat not her own, a voice above her doing something impossible: singing a descending line that resolved, again and again, onto a note like a hand closing gently around the whole world. A lullaby, but not a lullaby—a *part*. Harmony. Other voices around it, other cradles, a room full of infants and the women who sang to them, the choir of mothers, and her small body knew the song the way stones know rivers. Cold air suddenly. Arms running. A door that hummed. Darkness. A woman's voice, cracked with exhaustion, saying words into her ear that the infant-Serane couldn't understand but the grown Serane could, because the memory translated itself:

*"Forget the kingdom. Forget the song. Be no one, and live."*

Then white space. Then a stone room. Then a man in grey asking her name over and over until the word *Voss* became just a sound, then a flag, then a job.

Then nothing. Then thirty years of nothing, filled with procedure.

Serane came back to herself sitting in the dirt of the alley, the empty jar in her lap, tears freezing on her face in the cold air of a country that officially did not exist.

* * *

She stayed in the alley a long time.

She ran the drill, because the drill was all she had: assess the material. Verify provenance. Cross-reference. But every check came back the same way. The lullaby was in a harmonic mode the crown's own archives classified as *Casperan modal practice, extinct*. She had heard it once before—hummed, half-remembered, by a dying old woman in an interrogation cell eleven years ago, a woman she had reported as *uncooperative* right up until the moment she died humming it. The melody lived in Serane's body. She could sing it now, note-perfect, having never learned it.

You cannot be trained into knowing a song. You can only be trained out of it. And someone had trained it out of her, and called the training mercy, and she had thanked them with thirty years of flawless service.

Duty curdles slowly, and then all at once. It happened in her like milk turning—one moment she was kneeling in the alley assembling justifications, and the next she understood, with total clarity, that the crown had not saved her from Casper. The crown had stolen her from it. Had scraped out her mother's voice and replaced it with a flag, had taken a refugee child and forged her into a key for locking doors, and had sent her back through the door her mother died escaping—sent her home—to burn it down.

They hadn't sent an Inquisitor into Casper.

They'd sent the kingdom's own lost child to do the crowning cruelty. Because who better? Who would never suspect? Who would hunt hardest?

She thought of Freya Pemberton, ragged with grief, trading her happiest day to a man with piano-key teeth. She thought of Aldric, layered thick with harmonics, carrying a country in his throat. She thought of the gifted child in the capital, months ago, whose gentle surrender she had overseen, whose eyes had followed her to the door—and she felt the first crack run through the edifice of her certainty, deep as a foundation stone splitting.

They weren't anomalies. They were *remnants*. They were what was left of a people her crown had erased and then taught the world to say had never existed.

And she was the erasure, walking.

Serane rose. She cleaned the dirt from her uniform with brisk, mechanical strokes, because the habits of thirty years do not die in an alley, and buckled her sword, and turned toward the heart of the city where the gate-signal pulsed—toward the inner door, toward the Pemberton girl and the choir-master.

Not, she told herself, to help them. To verify. To confirm the provenance of what she had learned. Duty still had a shape; she could still see it; she simply could no longer remember which side of the door it stood on.

Behind her, in the crooked lane, the broker began putting his jars back on their shelves, and paused over one small empty vessel of deep blue glass, which he set aside gently, the way you set aside a letter that has finally been delivered.

Ahead of her, across the rooftops of a dead kingdom, the bells of Casper began to ring—all of them at once, though there were no bell towers anymore, and the sound they made was not alarm.

It was a summons. Somewhere in the city, engineers in crown black were driving the first iron hymn-stakes into holy ground, and the choir of the dead had begun, at last, to scream.
