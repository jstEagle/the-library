## 14. The Bridge of Nailed Songs

Aldric had crossed the Bridge of Nailed Songs once before, when he was nineteen and stupid and the royal choir still had a king to sing for.

He had not enjoyed it then, either.

The chasm opened beneath the last street of Casper like a wound in the memory of the world. There was no bottom that he could see—only a slow drift of pale things falling upward from the dark, fragments of songs that no one remembered anymore, rising like ash from a fire burning in reverse. The air above it tasted of cold iron and old grief.

And across it, nailed into nothing, hung the bridge.

It was not made of wood or stone or rope. It was made of intervals. A third here, suspended in the air like a held breath. A fifth there, humming faintly, its edges fraying where some singer long dead had let it waver. Between them, thin as spider silk and just as honest, stretched lines of pure unbroken harmony—the only substance in all of Casper that could bear weight, because it was the only substance that had never been anything other than true.

The old choir-masters had called the planks *nails* because each one had to be struck, sung into being, driven home by two voices at once. One voice alone made nothing but noise. Two voices, exactly matched—not similar, not close, but *exact*, the way two halves of a single thought are exact—made something solid enough to walk on.

Aldric stood at the edge with his hands in his pockets and looked at the dark and did not look at Freya, because he knew what he would see on her face if he did, and he was not yet ready to be brave about it.

"It doesn't look like a bridge," she said.

"That's because it isn't one. Not yet."

"Then what is it?"

"A question." He finally turned. Her hair was loose; somewhere back in the Market of Last Days she'd lost whatever she used to bind it, and she hadn't mentioned it, and he had spent three days noticing. "The bridge asks: can you mean the same thing at the same moment? Can you hold one sound between you without either of you flinching? Most people can't. Most people would rather fall."

"And if we can't?"

He shrugged. "Then we'll have a great deal of time to think about it on the way down."

Freya looked at him for a long moment. She had gotten thinner inside Casper, as though the country were quietly eating her edges, and her eyes had gotten older. Three weeks ago she had been a gatekeeper who half-believed in her own rituals. Now she carried gate-songs in her chest the way other people carried knives, and she slept badly, and she had sold her happiest day to a broker with ink-stained fingers, and Aldric had watched her do it and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wasn't a lie.

"You're scared," she said.

"I'm terrified."

"Good." Something almost like a smile. "You're less insufferable when you're terrified."

* * *

He taught her the crossing-song in the shadow of the ruined tollhouse at the chasm's lip—a squat stone building that had forgotten it was ever a building and now stood as only the idea of shelter, roofless, its walls humming faintly with the residue of every toll ever paid.

"The song has two parts," he said. "The ground and the flight. I take the ground—it never changes, never moves, it's the floor under your feet. You take the flight. The melody. And here's the cruelty of it—" he held up a finger, "—you don't get to choose your notes."

"What do you mean, I don't get to choose?"

"The flight follows the ground. Wherever my line bends, yours has to bend with it, instantly, perfectly, or the nail doesn't form. If you sing what you planned instead of what I'm singing, we make a gap. Gaps are how people die on this thing."

Freya's jaw set in the particular way it set when someone was about to argue with the architecture of the universe. "So I have to listen so closely to you that I stop hearing myself."

"Yes."

"And trust that wherever you go, you're going somewhere I can follow."

"Yes."

She was quiet for a moment. Below them, an upward-falling fragment of some lullaby drifted past, dissolving as it rose.

"My grandmother used to say," Freya said slowly, "that trusting someone is just deciding, in advance, how much betrayal you can survive."

"She sounds like she was fun at festivals."

"She burned letters in the hearth for forty years. So. Yes." But there was no bitterness left in it—or rather, the bitterness had been refined into something else, something that had stopped cutting her and started carrying her. Aldric recognized it. He had seen the same transmutation in refugees, in smuggled choir-children, in himself. Grief, annealed long enough, became a kind of fuel.

They began.

He gave her the ground first: a low, patient line, four notes cycling, the oldest progression in the Casperite canon, the one they sang over cradles and graves alike because it fit both. She listened with her whole body—he could see it, the way she stopped breathing through her mouth and started listening through her sternum—and when she came in on the flight, her first phrase wandered, corrected, wandered again. A nail flickered into existence over the void, translucent, trembling, and dissolved.

"Again," he said.

They tried again. The nail lasted longer. On the fourth attempt it held—small and grey and ugly, like a plank hewn by a nervous carpenter—but it held.

"There," he said softly. "There's your first step."

She stared at it, floating over a darkness full of rising songs, and laughed—one short, disbelieving note—and the nail shivered at the laughter and nearly died, and they both went very still, and then they were both laughing silently, shoulders shaking, careful as burglars.

* * *

They stepped onto the bridge at dusk, if Casper could be said to have a dusk. Its sky was always the color of the hour before the end, amber going bruise-dark at the edges, and the light never quite finished leaving.

The rule was simple and terrible: you could not stop singing. Not between nails, not to rest, not to speak. The moment the harmony broke, the nail behind you unraveled and the nail ahead of you never formed, and you stood on nothing. Every crossing was sung straight through, from lip to lip, in one unbroken breath of two voices.

Aldric went first onto the opening nail, testing it with his weight the way you test river ice. It bore him. He turned and offered his hand, and Freya took it, and her palm was cold and certain, and they began.

For the first hundred paces it was work. Hard, technical, exhausting work. He bent the ground-line up through a fourth and she followed; he dropped it sudden and low and she dropped with him, a half-beat late the first time—the nail sagged beneath them like wet cloth, and his heart slammed against his ribs, and then she caught the interval and the plank firmed and they moved on. Her breathing came ragged through the melody. His arms ached from holding pitch the way rowers' arms ache.

At the second hundred paces, something changed.

It happened the way it always happens, which is to say it happened without permission. Their two lines stopped being two. He felt it first as a loosening in his throat—as though the effort had been lifted out of him by someone else's hands—and then he understood, with a clarity that felt almost indecent, that Freya was no longer following him. She was *with* him. The flight and the ground had fused into a single moving body of sound, and neither of them was leading, and neither of them was following, and the nails began to form ahead of them before he even sang toward them, gold-edged and clean, as though the chasm itself were clearing its throat to let them pass.

He had sung this bridge with the finest musicians of a kingdom. He had never heard it sound like this.

He risked looking at her.

She was looking at him too. Of course she was—she had to; the song required it; you cannot share a single sound while looking elsewhere. Her eyes were wide and wet at the rims and absolutely steady, and in them he saw the thing he had been refusing to name since the drowned cathedral, since the market, since the night she fell asleep sitting up against his shoulder in the ghost-choir's vestry and he had sat unmoving for six hours rather than wake her.

The song wouldn't let him hide. That was the truth of the Bridge of Nailed Songs that the old masters never wrote down: you could fake technique, you could fake confidence, but you could not fake meaning. The bridge weighed intention. If there was anything withheld between the two singers—if there was fear of the other, or want of the other, unspoken and dammed up—it made the harmony false, and false harmony does not bear weight.

Which meant that somewhere around the middle of the chasm, with a mile of singing void below and the inner door a golden smudge on the far cliff, Aldric understood that the bridge was going to make them say it.

Not in words. Words were the one thing forbidden. But the song demanded everything else—all of it, the whole unguarded inventory of the heart, poured out in pitch and interval where it could not be taken back.

His ground-line trembled. Just once. Just a hair.

Freya caught it—not the wavering, but the feeling underneath it—and her flight answered, bending down to meet him, wrapping around his lower register like a hand closing over his, and she sang, wordless, unmistakable: *I know. I know. Me too.*

He nearly fell.

Not off the bridge. Off the edge of the person he had been before that moment. For eleven years he had carried a country in his throat and told himself there was no room in him for anything smaller and more human—for a woman with moss-green eyes and her grandmother's stubbornness and a laugh she was always halfway to apologizing for. He had made a virtue of emptiness. And here was this gatekeeper's daughter, this impossible, terrible singer, filling him up in four-part harmony over a bottomless dark, and the nails under their feet were blazing now, bright as forge-iron, because nothing false remained between them and the bridge knew it.

They sang it to each other across the second half of the crossing. Not love—that word was too small and too large and belonged to a world with time in it. They sang the rawer thing underneath: *I see you. All of it. The coward parts, the tired parts, the parts you sold. Still here. Still here. Still here.* And each time one of them flagged—and they both flagged, near the far cliff, voices fraying like old rope—the other's line came underneath and bore the weight alone, and that was its own declaration, maybe the truest one: *when you cannot, I will.*

The last nail formed ten feet from solid rock. It was the most beautiful thing Aldric had ever helped make: wide as a door, gold to its core, ringing faintly like a bell that had decided to become a floor.

Freya reached the cliff-edge first and turned and pulled him up, both hands, hard, and they stood on stone, gasping, throats raw, hands still clasped, neither letting go.

Above the inner door, the golden smudge resolved into carved words that Aldric had memorized as a boy and never understood until now:

*TWO VOICES OPEN WHAT ONE VOICE CANNOT.*

"Well," Freya rasped, when she had breath enough. "That was—"

"Don't," he said hoarsely. "Don't try to make it small. Let it stay enormous for one minute longer."

She didn't make it small. She leaned her forehead against his, salt and sweat and song between them, and for one full minute, in the amber failing light of a kingdom that was dying faster every day, they simply breathed together, matched, exact, the way they had learned to be over a mile of nailed songs.

Then the light changed.

Not the sky's light. The door's. The gold along its seams guttered, dimmed, flared again—unevenly, arrhythmically, like a pulse losing its pattern—and from somewhere beyond it, muffled by stone and distance, came a sound that Aldric had hoped never to hear in this place: the flat, grinding insistence of crown machinery, iron hymns being wound on drums, the measured tread of boots.

Freya's head snapped up. "Is that—"

"Engineers." His voice cracked on the word. "They're not coming for us, Freya. They're already here. They're on the *other side* of the inner door."

The gold seam split with a sound like a scream, and through the widening crack spilled light that no part of Casper had ever produced—white, hard, industrial, wrong—and with it, faintly, the smell of hot iron and machine oil drifting into a country made entirely of memory.

Aldric tightened his grip on Freya's hand.

Whatever waited beyond the inner door, it was not salvation, and it was not waiting politely.
