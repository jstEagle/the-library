## 15. The Door Out of a Dead Country

The inner door was not what Freya had imagined, after the drowned cathedral and the bridge of nailed songs and the long grey miles of a country that remembered itself too well.

She had imagined something grand. A gate of white stone, perhaps, or a wall of singing light. What they found instead was a kitchen door.

It stood alone at the top of a hill that had once been a vineyard, in what remained of a farmhouse whose walls had melted into the hillside like candle wax. The door was oak, ordinary, scarred at knee-height where children had kicked it open a thousand times. Someone had painted it blue once. The paint was peeling in long curls, and through the gaps Freya could see that the wood beneath was not wood at all but something pale and dense, like bone, like the white iron of her grandmother's tuning fork.

It did not glow. It did not hum. It simply stood there in the perpetual amber dusk of Casper, patient as a held breath, and Freya understood with a lurch that this was the most important object in two worlds, and it was a kitchen door.

"That's it?" she said. Her voice came out smaller than she meant it to.

Aldric leaned on her shoulder. He had not let go of her since the bridge, and she had not asked him to. His wound had stopped bleeding days ago—or what passed for days in a country without weather—but the flesh around it had gone the grey-white of old candle wax, and when he walked he favored his left side like a man carrying water.

"That's it," he said. "The Choir told me about it when I was a boy. Every child in Casper learned the rhyme. *The way out is the way in, and the way in is small.*" He managed something that was almost a laugh. "I always thought it was a metaphor. Choir-masters love metaphors. It's a kitchen door."

"It's a kitchen door," Freya agreed.

They stood before it and did not touch it. There was a quality to the silence around the door that made them both careful, the way you are careful near a sleeping animal or a deep well. The amber light came down through a sky the color of weak tea, and somewhere behind them, far off across the memory-streets of the city, the ghost-choir was singing its endless, fraying round—and Freya could hear, threaded through it now, notes that were missing. Voices that had gone out since they'd begun the crossing. The kingdom was starving faster.

"Tell me again what the Choir said," she said. "About the opening."

Aldric was quiet for a moment. "They said the door was made to be opened once, from the inside, by a key that would pay its own weight." He touched the doorframe without touching it, his fingers a hair's breadth from the bone-pale wood. "The Hollow Gate was built to keep Casper *in*. This one was built to let Casper *out*. And a door that lets a whole kingdom out into a world that wants it dead needs something to hold it open against the pressure. Against—" he gestured vaguely at everything, the amber sky, the fraying choir, the weight of a century of held sorrow, "—against all of this wanting to stay closed."

"Pay its own weight," Freya said. "In what?"

"You know in what."

She did. She had known since the drowned cathedral, since Aldric first taught her the gate-songs and could not meet her eyes during the verses about price. Every lesson had carried it underneath, the way a river carries a stone: the door wanted a life. Freely given. That was the whole of the arithmetic, and there was no other arithmetic.

Freya walked forward and put her palm flat against the blue paint. It was warm. That was the worst part—it was warm, the way a hand is warm, the way her grandmother's kitchen had been warm on winter mornings, and she felt the door feel her back, a slow attention like something turning over in its sleep.

*Hello,* the door said, without words. *At last. At last.*

She snatched her hand back.

"It knows me," she said. "Aldric. It knows what I am."

"It's been waiting a hundred years for what you are." He came to stand beside her, and she saw him sway, and she took his arm without asking, and he let her. "Freya. Before you decide anything. There's a verse I never taught you. The last one. Because I didn't want it to be true."

"Sing it."

"It's not a song. It's just—" he closed his eyes. "The door needs a life. Freely given. But the Choir's oldest singers used to argue about what *given* meant. Whether it meant given *up* or given *over*. Whether the door takes the life or—" he stopped. "Whether the key has to die, or only stop being who she was."

"That's not a difference," Freya said. "That's the same thing wearing different clothes."

"Maybe. Maybe not." He looked at her then, and his eyes were wet, and she realized with a strange, distant surprise that he had been carrying this longer than she had. "I have watched one Pemberton die for this door already. I will not stand here and watch a second one walk into it because the arithmetic says she must."

Freya went very still. "You knew my mother."

"I sang at her wedding. I taught her the descending third that opens the outer ward, the one she used to put you to sleep with, though I doubt she told you that's what it was." His voice cracked on the last word. "She came to me the year you were born. She'd worked out what the ward was doing to her—how it spent her, a little more every year, the way a candle spends itself. She asked me if there was another way." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound. "I told her no. I told her the door takes what it takes. I was wrong, Freya. I've had a hundred years and a country of ghosts to be wrong in, and I am telling you I was wrong. Do not give it your life. Promise me nothing. Just—do not decide yet."

Freya looked at the door. Warm. Patient. A kitchen door that had once led to a kitchen, to bread and morning light and someone calling children in from the vineyard, and now it led out of a dead country, and it wanted what it wanted.

"I promise nothing," she said. "But I'm not deciding yet."

* * *

They made camp in the farmhouse's ruin, in what had been the kitchen, because it seemed right to wait out the decision in the room the door had come from. There was no fire to build—Casper's air didn't want one—but Aldric found a shelf that still held three clay cups, and Freya held one, and the weight of it, the ordinary weight of a cup, made her throat close.

They sat against the melted wall, shoulder to shoulder, and for a while neither of them spoke.

"When this is over," Aldric said finally, "if there is an over—I'm going to open a choir school. Small. Ugly building. Terrible acoustics."

"Terrible acoustics," Freya repeated.

"The best choirs come out of terrible acoustics. You have to *listen* harder." He turned the clay cup in his grey-white fingers. "You could teach there. The gate-songs. Someone has to, after. The valley will need them. The valley doesn't know it yet, but it's going to need—"

He stopped.

Freya had heard it too.

Footsteps. Not the strange, echoing footsteps of the memory-people, who walked their last days in loops and never varied. Real footsteps. Heavy. Booted. Coming up the vineyard hill with the deliberate, unhurried tread of someone who knew exactly where she was going and had known for a long time.

Aldric was on his feet in an instant, and the motion tore at his wound, and he made a sound he tried to swallow. Freya rose with him, her hand finding the tuning fork in her pocket, white iron cold against her palm.

The footsteps stopped at the edge of the ruin.

Serane Voss stood in the amber dusk with her sword drawn.

She was not what Freya had expected. She had built the Inquisitor out of the villagers' whispers—tall and terrible, a figure out of a cautionary tale. What stood at the edge of the kitchen was a woman of middling height, mud to the knee, her grey coat scorched along one side, her face hollowed by a long road. Her sword was drawn, yes, but it was pointed at the ground, and it was shaking.

Not with fear. With something worse. With the trembling of a person holding something up that they are no longer sure should be held up.

"Aldric," she said. Her voice was rough, as if it had been used for shouting and then for silence and then for shouting again.

"Serane." Aldric did not sing. Freya felt him gathering the gate-song anyway, felt the tension of it in his arm under her hand, and she stepped half in front of him, tuning fork ready, though she did not know what she would do with it.

"You're wounded," Serane said to him. It was not a question, and it was not concern, exactly. It was an observation made by someone who had been trained to observe and could not stop.

"You followed me across a dead kingdom," Aldric said. "I'm flattered you noticed."

"I didn't follow you." Serane's eyes moved to Freya, and Freya made herself meet them. They were pale eyes, the color of river water over stone, and they were full of something Freya had not expected to find in an Inquisitor: a terrible, exhausted attention. "I followed the door. The crown's engineers have been mapping the gate's resonance for a year. The Edict of Cleansing was—" She stopped. Started again. Her jaw worked as if the words were physically difficult. "The Edict was never about the gifted."

The wind moved through the ruined vineyard, and somewhere below them the ghost-choir sang, and a voice went out of it even as they listened, a note dropping away like a lamp guttering.

"The Edict was a net," Serane said. "Every gifted person we took, we tested. Not for power. For *resonance*. For blood that could turn a lock the crown couldn't turn. The crown has known about the Hollow Gate for years. It has known what's behind it. And it has known that a gate like that doesn't just keep a kingdom in—it holds enough of the old magic to—" her voice did something Freya had never heard a voice do, it *bent*, "—to power anything. Forever."

"Harvest," Freya said. The word came out of her flat and cold.

"The engineers are already at the Hollow Gate." Serane said it the way you report a wound's depth: quickly, to get past it. "Iron hymns. Chaining. They breached the outer ward three days ago. Whatever this door needs to open—" she lifted her sword, and for one heartbeat Freya thought *this is it, this is the strike*—but the blade turned in her hand and came down, and she drove it into the earth at her feet and let go of it.

The sound of an Inquisitor's sword hitting Casper's memory-soil was very small and very final.

"Whatever it needs," Serane said, "the crown intends you never to have the chance. They don't want the door opened. They want the *pressure*. A kingdom's worth of sealed magic, tapped like a well. And the people inside—" for the first time her composure moved, cracked along a fault line, and Freya watched an entire conviction die in another person's face and it was like watching a wall come down, "—the people inside are not a consideration. They were never a consideration. They are *contents*."

Aldric made a low sound. Freya realized she was the one holding him up now, both of them, all three of them standing in the ruins of a kitchen at the end of a dead country while a woman who had hunted them across it told them the truth with her sword in the dirt.

"Why," Freya said. "Why are you telling us this."

Serane looked at her for a long moment. And then she did something Freya would think about for the rest of her life, however long that turned out to be. She reached into her coat and took out a small object and held it up between two fingers.

It was a memory-vial. Freya knew the shape now; the broker's stall had been full of them, little glass teardrops, each one a sold day. This one was old, the glass clouded, the seal stamped with a crest Freya didn't recognize—and Serane's hand was shaking around it, and her eyes when they met Freya's were the eyes of someone standing on ground that had begun, very quietly, to move.

"Because three days ago I bought back something the crown took from me," Serane Voss said, "and I am no longer certain which side of this door I was born on."

Behind her, far below in the darkening city, the ghost-choir's song faltered—and did not resume.
