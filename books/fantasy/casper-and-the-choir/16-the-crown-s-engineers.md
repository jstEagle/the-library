## 16. The Crown's Engineers

Serane Voss had spent twenty years learning to read the sounds of a country the way other people read weather. A creak of leather meant a soldier deciding whether to run. A hymn hummed too evenly meant fear dressed as discipline. And silence—silence in a place that had been singing—meant that something had already gone wrong, and was waiting for her to notice.

The ghost-choir's song had stopped.

She stood at the edge of the Bridge of Nailed Songs with Freya Pemberton and Aldric Vey, and she listened to the kingdom of Casper hold its breath, and every trained instinct in her body said: *run toward it*.

Every other instinct said: *you are standing on the wrong side of a door you were ordered to burn.*

"The engineers," she said. "They're through."

Freya looked at her. In three days Serane had learned to read this girl too—the set of her jaw when she was about to argue, the flicker of her eyes when she was about to obey something older than argument. Now there was neither. There was only a question, and it was worse than an accusation: *what did you bring here?*

"I didn't open the outer ward for them," Serane said. "I broke it because I thought I could reach you before they did. I was—" She stopped. Lying was a tool like any other and she had used it well for two decades, but it would not fit this moment; it kept slipping out of her hand. "I was slow. Because I stopped to buy back a memory from a broker in a dead market, and I told myself that was tactics."

Aldric made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't been sanded off him somewhere between the capital and here. "Tactics," he said. "Yes. I sang at the crown's winter festival once and called it service." His voice was wrecked—he had torn it apart on the bridge, holding the harmony while Freya crossed, and now each word came out frayed at the edges, like cloth worn thin by use. "They're coming up the Processional Road. You can hear them."

And she could. Not footsteps—the engineers of the Ninth Office did not walk so much as *proceed*, in cadence, in order—but the sound underneath their steps. Iron hymns. The crown had spent forty years developing them: verses stripped of everything that made singing holy, reduced to load-bearing syllables, stamped into iron plates that artificers wore on their chests like armor. Each plate held one note. Together, worn in ranks, they made a chord that could pin a ward to the ground the way a specimen is pinned to a board.

Serane had escorted the hymn-carts twice in her career. She had never heard them *fired*.

Now, far below, the first chord sounded, and Casper shuddered.

It was not an earthquake. Earthquakes are honest; they shake stone and stone answers. This was different. This was the city itself being told what it was allowed to be. The street beneath their feet—the memory of a street, paved with someone's last afternoon—went briefly translucent, like ice over deep water, and then firmed again, but wrong, the cobbles slightly too regular, the colors a half-tone flatter. Somewhere down the hill a bell tower that had been leaning with beautiful indifference for a hundred years straightened itself with a groan, because the hymn had informed it that towers stand straight or not at all.

"They're not destroying it," Freya whispered. "Why aren't they destroying it?"

"Because destruction isn't efficient," Serane said, and hated how easily the Office's vocabulary still came to her. "You don't burn a field you intend to harvest. They're flattening it. Making it legible. A kingdom that runs on memory can't be taxed until the memory has been standardized." She heard herself and closed her eyes briefly. "That is what the briefing documents say. Word for word."

"And you believed it," Aldric said. Not cruelly. He simply wanted it stated, the way a physician wants a wound named before he cleans it.

"I believed it was better than the alternative," Serane said. "I believed the alternative was a purge. I believed we were the mercy." She opened her eyes. Below them, along the Processional Road, lanterns were moving in formation, and behind the lanterns came the carts. "I need you to understand what I am about to show you, and then I need you to decide whether to shoot me where I stand, because after this I will not be able to go back, whichever way this ends."

Neither of them answered. Freya's hand had gone to the white-iron tuning fork at her belt—the grandmother's fork, the key of keys—and rested there, not drawing it, which Serane chose to read as patience rather than threat.

"Follow me," Serane said. "Quietly. The choir will know we're coming anyway. Everything here knows everything eventually."

* * *

They went down through streets that were coming apart politely.

That was the horror of it, Serane thought later—that was the thing she would carry, whatever she became after this night. The hymns did not smash Casper. They *edited* it. A baker who had stood in his doorway for a century, forever pulling loaves for children who died in the exodus, suddenly turned and walked into his shop and shut the door, because the hymn had no verse for generosity and the street had to conform. A fountain that had flowed upward, laughing at its own impossibility, sagged mid-air and fell, and became only water, only plumbing, only a thing that obeyed.

The people were worse.

They didn't scream. Screaming would have been a relief; screaming would have meant they were still themselves enough to protest. Instead they went quiet in stages, like lamps dimming. A woman watering a window-box looked up as they passed, and Serane saw her face try to remember why she was sad, and fail, and settle into blankness, and the blankness was so much more like death than death itself that Freya made a small sound and pressed her fist to her mouth.

"They're taking the grief out," Aldric rasped. "Grief is the load-bearing wall of this place. You take the grief out of a city built on a last day, and—"

He didn't finish, because ahead of them the Processional Road opened onto the plaza of the gate, and there it all was, laid out under a sky the color of old pewter.

The Hollow Gate from the inside was not an arch. From within Casper it was a wound in the world—a seam of light stitched shut, tall as a cathedral door, humming faintly with the ghost-choir's long maintenance. Around it, the ghost-choir itself: perhaps sixty singers, some translucent, some nearly solid, arranged in crescents facing the light, holding the kingdom together note by note, hour by hour, for a hundred years without applause or rest.

And in front of them, the Ninth Office.

Forty artificers in ranks of eight. Iron plates bolted across their chests, each plate glowing a dull furnace-red with a single stamped syllable. Behind them, the hymn-carts: great iron drums on wheels, wound with chains of smaller plates, tended by clerks with styluses and ledgers. And above it all, on a scaffolding of black timber already half-built against the gate's flank, engineers in wax-stiff coats swarming like respectful insects, measuring the seam with instruments that ticked.

The lead engineer—a small, neat woman with ink-stained cuffs and the serenity of someone doing arithmetic—stood at a lectern of iron, reading aloud.

Not orders. *Verses.* Stripped ones. Serane recognized the source and felt her stomach turn: it was the Coronation Hymnal, the songs of Casper itself, the very songs Aldric had smuggled out in his throat, cut down to their skeletons. Every line that asked anything of the heart had been removed. What remained was pure instruction. *Stand. Hold. Be counted. Be still.*

"She's using their own music," Freya breathed.

"It's the only music that fits the lock," Aldric said. His voice cracked on it. "Of course it is. That's the whole cruelty of the Office. They never invent anything. They take what a people loved and turn it into the instrument of its management. I watched them do it to the harvest songs in Vell's neighbor provinces. I told myself it was different here." He laughed, or tried. "Nothing is different anywhere. That's the discovery of my entire life."

Below, the engineer raised one finger, and forty artificers breathed in together.

The chord fired.

Serane had braced for loudness and it wasn't loud. It was *flat*. A sound with no top and no bottom, no attack, no decay—just pressure, total and gray, like the sky pressing down. The nearest crescent of ghost-singers buckled. One—an old man, solid enough that Serane could see the silver buttons of a coat he must have died in—clutched at the air as though the air had been pulled out from under him. His voice tore free of the choir's harmony and hung alone, a single unsupported thread, and then the hymn folded it in, absorbed it, filed it, and he was silent, and he did not fall, because falling required a decision, and the hymn had taken those too.

Above the gate, the seam of light flickered.

And the city began to come down.

Not all at once. Street by street, in order, alphabetically almost—Serane realized with a lurch that the clerks' ledgers contained a schedule, that the collapse had been *plotted*, that somewhere in those carts was a map of Casper with districts shaded by priority. A row of houses near the river went soft and slid into themselves like sand castles at tide-line, and no dust rose, because dust would have been undignified, and the hymns permitted nothing undignified. A clock tower leaned, considered, and lay down in the road like a tired animal.

"They'll be done by dawn," the little engineer said—not loudly, but the plaza carried it. "Full containment of the asset by dawn. Note for the record: local population non-resistant. Recommend reclassification of site from 'purge remnant' to 'annexed resource.' The Choir will be dissolved into the drum-choruses. Their harmonic output is, frankly, remarkable. Waste not."

One of the ghost-singers stepped forward.

She had been young when Casper fell—young the way Freya was young, Serane thought, young in the specific terrible way of people who are handed a duty instead of a life. Her feet left no prints. Her voice, when she spoke, was the voice of someone who had been rehearsing a single sentence for a century.

"You cannot chain a door that is also a promise."

"We can," the engineer said pleasantly, consulting a ledger. "Page four hundred and eleven."

And that—that page number, that mild bureaucratic certainty—did something to Serane Voss that twenty years of interrogations had never done. It was not rage. Rage she knew how to carry; rage had a shape, a handle. This was colder. This was the sound of her own profession speaking in its natural voice, unhidden, and hearing it for the first time not as order but as obscenity.

Her hand was on her sword. She had no memory of putting it there.

"Voss." Aldric's ruined whisper. "Whatever you're about to do, do it knowing they'll write your name in one of those ledgers too, and it won't be a kind entry."

"Good," Serane said.

She stepped out of the shadow of the colonnade into the open plaza, into the flat gray pressure of the hymns, and drew her sword, and did not point it at the choir.

"Inquisitor First Rank Serane Voss," she said, and her voice carried the way voices do in places built of memory, carrying further than physics should allow. "By authority of the Edict of Cleansing, I am placing this site under protective interdiction. All engineering operations will cease. All personnel will withdraw beyond the outer ward."

The little engineer looked up from her ledger with genuine puzzlement, the puzzlement of a clerk encountering a form filled out incorrectly. "Inquisitor Voss. You're not listed as attached to this operation."

"No," Serane said. "I'm not."

"Then you have no authority here."

"I know," said Serane Voss, who had been authority her whole life, and found that saying otherwise felt like setting down a weight she had mistaken for her own skeleton. "For the first time in my career, I am entirely aware that I have none whatsoever."

She cut the nearest hymn-drum's chain with two strokes. The drum's note died mid-syllable, and the silence it left was so sudden and so clean that the whole plaza flinched—and in the flinch, in that half-second of structural surprise, the ghost-choir breathed in as one body and *sang*.

It came up through the cobblestones, through Serane's boots, through her ribs, through the ledger-pages of her stolen memory: the full unsanitized Coronation Hymnal, the versions with the asking in them, the versions that required a heart to perform. The second drum went as Aldric and Freya broke from cover, the boyish choir-master tearing his scarred voice wide open on a note that unmade a bolt, the Pemberton girl striking the white-iron fork against her own palm and letting its true tone run down the scaffolding like fire down a fuse.

The engineers scattered. The artificers wavered, forty men and women suddenly aware that the plates on their chests were screaming.

And high above the plaza, on the black scaffold, the little engineer calmly turned a page in her ledger, produced a whistle of white iron, and blew a signal that Serane had only ever seen described in sealed annexes—one short note, meaning: *asset unstable; deploy secondary protocol.*

Beyond the hills of Casper, in the twilight country between the kingdom and the waking world, something began to march.

"Run," Serane said, turning, sword still bare, blood running unnoticed from her palm where she'd gripped the blade too hard. "Both of you. Get to the choir, get to the inner door—I'll hold the plaza as long as I can, and then I'm going to do something I was trained never to do."

"What's that?" Freya shouted.

"Lose," said Serane Voss, and went to meet the thing that was marching out of the dark.
