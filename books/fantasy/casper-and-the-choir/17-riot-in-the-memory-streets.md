## 17. Riot in the Memory Streets

The first street died while we were still on it.

I felt it go the way you feel a floorboard give beneath your foot—a lurch, a wrongness, a drop that hadn't happened yet. The cobbles ahead of us turned grey as ash, then translucent, and through them we could see nothing at all, which was worse than seeing the void. A baker's stall unraveled into threads of light. The woman who had been selling bread from it for—what? A hundred years? A century of identical mornings—looked down at her dissolving hands with an expression not of terror but of confusion, as if she'd simply forgotten what hands were for.

Then she was gone, and the stall was gone, and the street where they had stood was a scar of empty dark.

"Keep moving." Serane's voice came flat over my shoulder. She had her sword out, though there was nothing yet to use it on. Behind us, the crown's engineers were advancing up the Processional with their iron hymns chained to the gate like leeches, singing their thin metal notes into the bones of Casper, and every verse they sang unmade another block of a country that had already survived one apocalypse by hiding.

Aldric caught my arm. "Not that way. The Choir keeps to the high streets when the city shakes. They'll have gone up."

"Up to what?"

"To sing louder." His face was grey with exhaustion, and there was blood on his sleeve I didn't want to think about yet—the riot in the market quarter had cost us an hour and nearly cost us him—but his eyes were steady. "If the engineers' hymns are pulling the kingdom apart note by note, the choir has to hold the notes together. It's the oldest war there is, Freya. Counterpoint."

So we went up.

* * *

Casper had never been built for running. Its streets had been designed for processions, for mourning, for the slow ceremonial grief of a kingdom that knew it was dying and wanted to do it beautifully. Now we ran through them anyway, three fugitives hammering along avenues of memory while the memory itself collapsed behind us.

It wasn't orderly destruction. That was the thing no one warned you about. Buildings didn't fall; they *forgot themselves*. A clock tower we passed shed its clock first, then its tower-ness, standing for one horrible moment as a column of raw stone before it remembered it had once been a tree and burst into green, impossible foliage, and then that too faded and it was just a shape in the fog. A fountain ran backward. Somewhere a dog barked in a language that predated dogs.

And the people—the people who were left, the ones whose last days were still anchored enough to repeat—were panicking. A hundred years of the same morning, the same funeral, the same farewell at the same dock, and now the script was tearing. They poured into the streets screaming in voices that hadn't screamed since the Sealing. Some of them mobbed us. Not from malice—from need. An old man grabbed my wrist with fingers like smoke and begged me, in a dialect I half understood through Aldric's murmured translation, to tell him whether his daughter had made it onto the boat.

"I don't know," I said, and hated how it sounded. "I'm sorry. I don't know."

"You're the key," he said. "They said the key would come back."

Then the engineers' hymn swelled, a chord of iron and obedience rolling up through the stones, and the old man's face went slack, and he walked away from me into the fog, obediently, toward whatever dissolution the crown had written for him.

Serane watched him go. Her knuckles were white on her sword hilt. "That's the Ninth Hymn," she said quietly. "Compulsion register. I wrote part of its harmony when I was nineteen. I thought—I was told—it was for calming riots."

"It is," Aldric said, without looking at her. "Look around. No one is fighting anymore."

She flinched as if struck. For a heartbeat I thought she would turn her sword on herself, or on him, or on me—and then she sheathed it and said, "The high streets. Move."

* * *

We found the first singers two terraces up: three ghosts pressed into the doorway of a chapel, holding a single sustained note between them the way you'd hold a door shut against a flood. Their voices were fraying. You could hear it—the note wobbled, split, reknit itself. Every time the engineers' hymn surged below, the note thinned.

"They can't hold alone," Aldric breathed. He was already moving toward them, and something in his posture made my stomach drop, because I'd seen that posture before, in a different life, in a story my grandmother told about brave fools.

"Aldric—"

"Somebody has to give them a foundation." He planted himself beside the chapel door, filled his lungs, and joined them.

I will try to describe what his voice did, knowing I'll fail. It wasn't loud. It was *true*. His note went under theirs like a keel, like bedrock, and the wobbling chord above him steadied, brightened, became a wall instead of a wish. The chapel doorway solidified. The street beyond it firmed like muscle remembering itself. For fifty yards in every direction, Casper stopped dying.

And the engineers' hymn, below us, changed key to hunt him.

"Oh no," I said. "No, no—" Because I understood then, with the terrible speed of things you understand too late, exactly what a counterpoint war meant. To hold the note was to be findable. His voice was a beacon now, and the iron hymn would climb toward it terrace by terrace until it reached him, and iron hymns did not argue. Iron hymns *unmade*. I had seen what the Ninth Hymn had done to the old man's mind. I did not want to learn what the engineers' siege-register did to a living throat.

The first squad came around the bell tower four minutes later.

There were six of them, in grey lacquered armor, carrying instruments that were also weapons—long steel horns fitted with keys and chains, each one humming a fragment of the siege-hymn. Their officer saw Aldric singing and raised one gauntleted hand, and all six horns swung toward him like the mouths of wells.

Everything after that happened very fast and very slowly at once.

I heard myself start to sing.

I hadn't decided to. Later, Aldric would tell me that was the point—that the gate-songs don't wait for permission, they live somewhere below decision, in the marrow, and when the marrow calls they answer. But in the moment it felt less like choosing than like falling. The note came up out of me rough and enormous, a sound I had no idea my body could make, and it was the First Gate-Song, the one Grandmother's tuning fork had hummed against my teeth a lifetime ago in a kitchen that no longer existed: the song of *threshold*, the song that says *you may not pass*.

The lead horn's note shattered.

Not metaphorically. The steel cracked along its length with a sound like ice giving way, and the engineer holding it staggered, dropped it, clapped both hands over his ears. The second horn's hymn bent sideways, sliding off mine the way water slides off wax. My voice rolled down the terrace and met theirs head-on, and where the two sounds touched, the air itself went white.

*You may not pass,* I sang at them, in a language older than the crown, older than Vell, older than the word *crown*, and six trained killers of the Inquisition's finest engineering corps broke and ran.

I stopped. My knees buckled. The taste in my mouth was copper and moss and gate-iron, and my whole skeleton rang like the fork.

"Freya." Aldric had stopped singing too—the other singers could hold their own line now, briefly—and he was staring at me with an expression I couldn't read, awe and fear braided together. "That was the Warding Verse. Nobody sings the Warding Verse on their first day. Nobody sings it in their first *year*—"

"There's no time," I said. Below us, faint but rising, the engineers had regrouped, and their hymn had found a new target: not Aldric now. Me. "They're coming for the key. Come on."

* * *

The choir had gathered in the rotunda of the Drowned Cathedral's bell tower—the highest point left standing in Casper, a ring of stone open to a sky that wasn't a sky, just layered veils of grey-gold memory going up forever. Maybe forty singers remained of the hundreds Aldric had described. They stood in concentric rings, holding the Great Chord, the one that had kept the kingdom breathing through a century of exile, and the strain on them was visible the way heat shimmer is visible: the air around the rings warped and shivered.

Their cantor was a woman who had been old when the kingdom sealed and was somehow younger now, worn smooth by repetition. She saw Aldric and her face cracked open with relief and grief in equal measure.

"Brother. You brought her."

"I brought her," he said. "Cantor, the engineers are chaining the gate with siege-hymns. The city has maybe hours. Less."

"Then we sing louder," she said, simply, the way you'd say *then we eat*, and turned back to her rings.

But there was no time even for that, because the horns came up the tower stairs.

We heard them first—their hymn climbing the spiral like water rising in a well—and Serane moved before any of us, putting herself in the stairway mouth with her sword drawn and her back to everything she'd spent her life serving. "Go," she said. "Hold your chord. I'll hold the stairs."

"You're one woman," I said.

"I'm the reason they taught those hymnals restraint," she said. "Let me be the reason they remember it."

What followed on that staircase, I mostly heard rather than saw, because I was inside the ring of singers by then, and Aldric was pushing me toward the center, toward the cantor, shouting something about the Great Chord needing a living key to anchor its final verse. Steel rang on steel above us. Someone screamed—not Serane. A horn-note cut off mid-phrase with a crunch. Then silence, and Serane's voice, ragged: "More coming. Two minutes, maybe. Whatever you're doing, do it faster."

The cantor took my hands. Hers felt like cool paper.

"The last verse needs a name," she said. "A true one, spoken by a true key, sung into the chord. Can you do that?"

"My name is Freya Pemberton," I said, "and I am the daughter of the Hollow Gate, and I say this kingdom is not finished—"

I got as far as *not finished* when the stairwell exploded.

Not fire. *Forgetting.* The engineers had stopped trying to come up the stairs and started singing the stairs out of existence, and the rotunda lurched like a ship in weather, and through the widening gap where the staircase had been I saw grey armor and grey horns and above them the banner of the Inquisition, white on black, and I heard the officer's voice call up the siege-order: "Take the key alive. Burn the rest."

Aldric moved.

I didn't see him decide either—he was simply elsewhere, between the gap and me, arms spread wide as the first horn-note came through, and he *sang into it*, took the siege-hymn head-on with nothing but his throat and a hundred years of counterpoint, bending it, breaking it, buying seconds the way you'd buy them with coins torn from your own flesh.

He bought us maybe ten.

Then a second horn found the gap in his line—a note he couldn't reach because he was holding two others—and it went through him like a spear of sound.

The noise he made was not a scream. It was worse. It was a chord, breaking. He folded at the waist, and blood came from his mouth, and Serane was suddenly there, hauling him back from the edge, her sword abandoned, her hands red to the wrist.

"*Aldric!*"

His eyes found mine across the shaking rotunda. He tried to speak. What came out was a whisper shaped like a note, and I understood it anyway, because some languages don't need sound: *finish it.*

The cantor gripped my shoulders. Around us the rings of singers were wavering, fraying, forty voices holding a country's soul with their fingernails while the stairs dissolved and the sky-veils dimmed and the man I loved drowned in his own blood ten feet away with an enemy's hands pressed to his wounds.

"Key," the cantor said. "Now. Or there is no kingdom left to save, and no one left in it worth saving him for."

Below, the next wave of horns began to climb.

Above, the sky-veils flickered like failing candles.

And in my chest, the tuning fork my grandmother gave me began—for the first time since the valley—to hum all on its own, pointing, insistent, toward the heart of the chord, toward the last verse I hadn't learned yet, toward the choice I could feel waiting for me at the top of the tower like a blade laid flat on a table.

I opened my mouth, and sang.
