## 18. The Last Verse

The choir gathered around the broken altar of the drowned cathedral like people gathering around a deathbed.

That was the first thing Freya understood, standing in the flooded nave with Aldric's blood cooling on her hands: this was not a council. It was a vigil. The ghost-singers had arranged themselves in their ancient crescent—the old ones in back, barely more than outlines, the children in front, translucent as breath on glass—and they were waiting for her the way the dying wait for a last visitor.

The city was coming apart behind them. She could hear it through the shattered rose window: the groan of streets unremembering themselves, the market of last days collapsing stall by stall into white mist, the bell tower leaning at an angle no honest tower would survive. Somewhere out there, crown engineers were winding iron hymns through the gate's roots, and every verse they chained took another street with it.

"Aldric." She knelt beside him again, though she'd already done everything she knew to do. The wound was a black blossom across his ribs where the warden-engineer's blade had found him—found him because he'd stepped between it and three child-singers who hadn't been able to run. His voice, when he tried to speak, came out frayed at every edge, a tapestry unraveling.

"Still here," he managed. "Annoying, isn't it."

"Don't." Her own voice cracked. "Don't do the thing where you make jokes so I won't notice."

"I have exactly one skill," he said, "and it isn't singing anymore." He lifted his head a fraction from her arm and looked at the choir. "Tell her."

* * *

The eldest of them—she had learned his name was Corvin, though he'd been dead so long that his face had gone soft as wet paper—stepped forward. Or didn't step; the dead don't walk so much as arrive where they intend to be.

"Key-bearer," he said. His voice was all the cathedral's voices at once, stone and water and centuries. "You have seen what is happening. The engineers' hymns are closing the inner door even as we speak. There are two roads left, and neither is long."

Freya stood. Her legs felt borrowed. "Say them."

"You may seal the gate," Corvin said. "Turn the key inward, close the way entirely. Casper ends tonight—but quietly, wholly, before the crown can harvest what remains. And your valley—" he paused, and something moved across his ruined face that might have been pity, "—your valley keeps its ward. Your neighbors live. Your mother's grave stays undisturbed. You go home to a world that never knew what you did."

"And the other road."

"You open it fully. All the way. Every soul in Casper passes through into the waking world—and Casper dissolves, because a kingdom is only its people, and once they're gone there is nothing left to hold the shape." Corvin's hands spread wide, encompassing the failing city, the fading singers, the hundred years of frozen sorrow. "But a door this size does not open on will alone. It opens on payment. A door out of a dead country must be bought with a living one."

The words landed in Freya's chest like stones dropped down a well.

"No," she said.

"The gate was built by desperate people," said a woman further back in the crescent, one of the ones so old she was nearly transparent. "Desperate people build doors that drink. That was the price they accepted—a key that lives in flesh must be spent from flesh. One life freely given, and the way stands open until the last of us has passed."

"One life," Freya repeated. "Mine."

"It cannot be taken," Corvin said quickly. "Understand that. The engineers could carve you apart and the door would stay shut. It must be *given*. Willingly. Completely. That is why we waited a hundred years for a Pemberton rather than seizing one."

She looked at them—the whole pale crescent of them, the children in front who had died so young they still wore the shapes of their last afternoons. A little girl near the front was holding a wooden bird. She'd been holding it since Freya arrived. She would be holding it forever if no one opened the door.

Behind Freya, Aldric made a sound.

It wasn't words. It was lower than words, a note dragged up from somewhere beneath the ruin of his voice, and the whole cathedral heard it—not loudly, but *deeply*, the way you feel thunder in your teeth. The flooded floor shivered into rings. Dust fell from the vaulting in slow gray snow.

"Aldric, don't—"

"Somebody's going to say it," he said, and pushed himself up against the altar stone until he was half-sitting, gray-faced, smiling at her with blood on his teeth. "So it may as well be me, before I'm too far gone to argue properly. Me. Not her."

"You can barely breathe—"

"Which makes me cheap," he said. "Economically speaking. A dying man for a kingdom and a valley full of living people. Name me a better price anywhere in either world." His eyes were bright and terrible and absolutely certain. "I've been carrying a country in my throat my whole life, Freya. Let it finally weigh something."

"No." She said it fast, before the arithmetic could get its hooks into her. Because it was good arithmetic—that was the horror of it. It was *beautiful* arithmetic. One dying singer, already bleeding out, traded for tens of thousands of souls and everyone she'd ever known in Vell. Any reasonable person would sign.

"That's not how songs work," she said instead.

He blinked. "What?"

"You told me. First lesson, in the drowned cathedral, before I could hold a pitch. *A song isn't a coin, girl, it's a conversation.* You made me sing scales until I cried." She was talking too fast and couldn't stop. "You said the gate-songs aren't spells, they're *agreements*. The bridge of nailed songs only held because our voices agreed. So why would a door built by singers—a door built out of *song*—run on murder? Even willing murder?"

The choir had gone very still. All those soft dead faces turned toward her, and in the silence she could hear the city dying outside, street by street, and beneath it something else—something she hadn't noticed before because grief had been filling her ears.

The gate itself. Humming.

Low, patient, persistent. The same note she'd heard the day in the valley when a stray thread of song had made the moss-covered arch tremble. It had been humming the whole time. Under the engineers' iron, under the collapsing streets, under everything—it had been humming like a throat clearing. Like someone about to speak who kept being talked over.

"Aldric," she whispered. "Listen."

His head tilted. Frayed as his voice was, his ear had never been damaged, and she watched understanding move across his face like weather. "...It's not asking," he breathed. "It's been trying to tell us. This entire time."

* * *

They got him upright between them—her on one side, two of the elder ghosts passing through his arms in a way that made him shudder and laugh weakly—and together they followed the note.

It led down. Of course it did; everything in Casper led down eventually, toward the gate's heart, the place the maps of memory called the Threshold Root. The stairways were flooding now, the city's dissolution reaching them at last, knee-deep water full of dissolving memories: a wedding procession drifting past them as pale confetti, a baker's whole lifetime rising off the water in the smell of bread. The little girl with the wooden bird followed at Freya's heel, and Freya didn't have the heart—or perhaps didn't have the right—to tell her to stay back.

At the bottom was the door.

Not the inner door they'd crossed at the Bridge of Nailed Songs. The true one. The original. A plain arch of gray stone, unadorned, older than every ornament the kingdom had piled onto its descendants—and carved into its keystone, so worn it was nearly invisible, a line of text in the old script. The script Aldric had taught her. The script of the gate-songs.

She read it aloud, badly, translating as she went, her voice echoing in the flooded chamber:

"*Not blood. Not breath. Not bone.*"

The hum deepened.

"*A door remembers what a sword forgets: that a person is not their body, nor their years, nor their deeds. A person is their name. The word the world calls them by. Give the door the word, freely spoken, freely surrendered—and walk through changed but walking.*"

The silence afterward lasted three full seconds. Then Aldric started laughing, which turned into coughing, which Freya decided to interpret as laughter.

"It was never a life," he rasped. "All this—all the hundred years of waiting, the starving, the *bargains*—" He gestured at the choir crowded down the stairs behind them, at Corvin's stricken paper face. "They misread their own founders. Grief does that. A hundred years of grief, and nobody thought to reread the keystone."

"They knew," Freya said slowly. Something cold and hot at once was moving through her. "Corvin—they *knew*, didn't they? Or some of the oldest ones did. They offered me the life-price because they believed it. Because believing your suffering means something is easier than believing it was a translation error."

Corvin's expression didn't change, but he didn't deny it either, and somehow that was worse.

"It doesn't matter," Freya said. She surprised herself with how steady her voice came out. "Look—I understand why. You've been trapped in your last afternoon for a century. You wanted the ending to cost something, because if it costs nothing then what was all the waiting *for*?" She stepped toward the door. The hum rose to meet her, warm as a hand. "But I am so tired of doors that eat people. My whole life has been doors that eat people. Grandmother's ward ate her. The crown eats whoever it's pointed at. Serane was eaten by her duty so thoroughly she doesn't know there's anything left of her. Well—not this one. This one gets fed a *word*."

"And whose word?" Aldric asked quietly.

She looked back at him. At the gray of his lips, the blood soaking through her bandages, the stubborn ridiculous hope on his face. If she gave her name, she would walk out of this door a person the registers had never heard of. No Pemberton. No gatekeeper. No daughter, granddaughter, keeper, key. The valley would forget how to find her in its ledgers; the crown would have nothing to burn.

And he—he was dying anyway. If she refused, he would offer himself again, and next time the choir might believe him hard enough to help, and the door might take a dying man's whole remaining hour and call it a life, and it would work, probably, the way a bent nail holds a shelf—until it didn't.

"There's something I have to do first," she said. "Before anyone gives anything."

She put both hands flat against the gray stone of the arch.

And she began to sing the gate-song Aldric had taught her in the drowned cathedral—the first one, the clumsy one, the one she'd been terrible at—with the one correction the keystone had just handed her. Where the song demanded a name given, she sang a name *returned*. Not hers.

His.

"Aldric Vane," she sang, in the old tongue, in the register that made the water stand up in small bright rings. "Choir-master of the royal chapel of Casper. Smuggled out in a grain cart. Carrier of a country in his throat. Teacher, exile, fool, friend. The world gave you this name and the world takes it back—not as payment, but as *witness*. Be named. Be counted. Belong to the record again."

The door flared white.

Aldric made a sound she had never heard from him—something between a sob and a chord—as the name settled back onto him like a coat he'd forgotten he owned. And she felt it happen in her own chest, felt the shape of the lock turn over, felt the mechanism of the gate taste a freely surrendered name and find it—

—almost enough.

Almost.

The light steadied. Waited.

"It needs the key's own name too," Corvin said softly behind her, wonder and dread in equal measure. "One witness, and one... surrender. That is the full verse. That is the *last* verse."

Freya nodded. She had known, somewhere below knowing, since she read the keystone.

She turned to Aldric. "When I give mine," she said, "I won't remember being Freya Pemberton. Whoever walks through that door will be someone new. Someone with no valley, no bloodline, no debt." Her throat tightened. "So I need you to do one last thing for me. The same thing I just did for you."

"Freya—"

"*Sing me back,*" she said. "Whatever I become on the other side—sing me my name. Not the old one. A new one. Yours to choose." She smiled, and it hurt, and she let it. "You always said a song is a conversation. Finish it."

Outside, above them, through the drowning city, the engineers' iron hymns struck a new verse, and the cathedral lurched, and there was no more time for anything—only the arch, blazing, patient, humming her nameless name.

Freya Pemberton filled her lungs.

* * *

She was still breathing in when the first ceiling beam let go.
