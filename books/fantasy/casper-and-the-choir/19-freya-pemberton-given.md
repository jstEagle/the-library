## 19. Freya Pemberton, Given

The heart of the gate was not a room. Freya had expected a chamber, something with walls and a floor and an obvious center, because everything else in her life had come with instructions. What she found instead was a place where architecture gave up pretending: a hollow in the fabric of Casper where the streets simply stopped, and the air thickened until it felt like standing inside a held breath.

Above her—below her—it was impossible to say—the gate turned. Not the mossy arch she had tended all her life on the Vell side, but its true shape: two great rings of stone, one carved with every name the valley had ever forgotten, the other with every name Casper had ever mourned, turning slowly against each other like millstones that ground nothing but time.

Between them hung the door.

It was small. That was the first cruel joke of it. After everything—the drowned cathedral, the bridge of nailed songs, the city coming apart street by street behind them—the door itself was no bigger than the pantry door in her grandmother's house. Plain wood. Iron hinges going to rust. A latch worn smooth by hands that had been dead for a hundred years.

"It's always like that," said the oldest voice of the ghost-choir, a woman named Ilse who had died at thirty and now wore her death the way other people wore a shawl. "Kings build cathedrals. Doors stay doors."

Freya stood before it with Aldric leaning against her shoulder, his weight more than half his own, his blood soaking through the bandage Serane had torn from her own cloak. Behind them, the last singers of Casper formed their broken crescent, voices frayed to threads. Beyond them, through the shuddering streets, came the sound Freya would hear in her dreams for the rest of whatever life she had left: iron hymns, sung by machines that did not tire, chewing the kingdom down to its foundations.

"The engineers have reached the Market of Last Days," Serane reported. She had come back from scouting the eastern avenue with ash on her face and something new in her eyes—not softness, exactly. More like a woman who had finally stopped arguing with herself. "An hour. Perhaps less."

"Then we do it now," Aldric rasped.

"You can barely stand."

"I don't need to stand." He tried to smile and managed half of it. "I need to sing. Sit me down and point me at the door."

"No." Freya heard her own voice come out flat and certain, and was briefly surprised by it. "Nobody is offering anything. We settled this."

They had not settled it. They had screamed about it, on the bridge, with the chasm still humming beneath them, and then they had run out of time to scream. The choir wanted a life. The door demanded a life—or so every song, every inscription, every ghostly warning had insisted for a century. *A life freely given keeps the way.* Aldric had offered his. Freya had refused. The choir had offered Freya the choice between her own death and the sealing of the gate forever, and she had refused that too, standing in the ruins of the cathedral square while the sky of Casper flickered like a bad lamp, refusing to choose either answer to a question she had suddenly, blindingly understood was asked wrong.

Because she had read the ring of names. All night, in the cathedral, while Aldric slept his wounded sleep, she had walked the circle of stone and put her palm against the carvings, and somewhere around the four thousandth name she had stopped seeing words and started hearing them—and the gate-songs Aldric had taught her, the ones she'd been so terrible at and then so startlingly not, had rearranged themselves in her head into a single sentence:

*A life freely given keeps the way.*

Not *a death.* A life. A whole life, given over—not ended, but surrendered. Given to whom? To the door. To the kingdom. To everyone on the other side of it.

And what was a life, in the language of wards and registers and crown rolls, but a name?

*Freya Pemberton.* The name on the valley's oldest ward. The name in the crown's Inquisitorial registers, the one Serane Voss had carried across a country like a warrant. The name her grandmother had guarded with white iron and burned letters and a lifetime of careful silence. The name that made her someone—someone's granddaughter, someone's keeper, someone's quarry.

She didn't need to die. She needed to stop being anyone.

* * *

"I won't allow it," Aldric said, when she told them.

He was sitting on a fallen lintel, gray-faced, one hand pressed to his side as if holding himself together by main force. The choir stood around them in their crescent, and even the ghosts looked stricken, which Freya hadn't known was possible.

"That's convenient," she said, "because I've spent my entire life doing things other people allowed. My grandmother allowed me out of the valley twice. The crown allowed my family to exist. You know what I've learned about allowing?"

"Freya—"

"It doesn't require my permission," she said, "and I'm done waiting for anyone else's."

Ilse drifted closer, and up close her death-shawl shimmered like heat off summer stones. "Child," she said gently, "we have watched keys try this. There was a man, eighty years ago, who tried to give the door his name and keep himself besides. He thought he could hide half of it in his pocket like bread." Her gaze flicked toward the darkening streets. "There is a wraith in the Market of Last Days that used to be him. It repeats the moment of his splitting forever."

"I'm not keeping half," Freya said. "I'm giving all of it. That's the whole point. That's why it works when dying doesn't." She turned to face the whole crescent of them, the dead of Casper, and found that her fear had gone strangely quiet, the way a room goes quiet after a storm has passed through it. "You've been starving for a hundred years because the door only opens for someone who gives up everything they are. And everyone who reached it wanted to survive the giving. I understand. But I've already given up almost everything. My valley. My grandmother. The girl who believed the rituals meant something. There's very little left to surrender except the part that owns the rest."

"And us?" Aldric's voice cracked on it. He got to his feet anyway, swaying, furious, alive. "You cross that door and become no one, and then what? I find you on the other side and there's nothing to find. No name to call. No—" He stopped. The word he wasn't saying sat in the space between them, big as the chasm they had crossed singing each other's harmony.

Freya crossed to him and took his face in her hands. His skin was cold and stubbled and human, and she memorized it the way you memorize a song you expect to lose.

"Aldric. When I was small, my grandmother told me the story of the tuning fork. White iron, struck once, a hundred years ago, and it's been ringing ever since—too high for ears, too true to stop." She pressed the fork into his palm and closed his fingers over it. "That's what a name is. Strike it and it rings forever, somewhere, whether or not anyone's listening. Give mine away and it doesn't vanish. It just stops belonging to me." Her throat tightened. "So when the door is open and the last singer of Casper has walked through into morning—when you're standing in the waking world with your ruined beautiful voice—you sing something. Anything. A scale. And somewhere in it, I'll hear my name."

"That's not how sound works," he whispered.

"It's how everything works here," she said. "You taught me that."

* * *

Serane Voss cleared her throat.

It should have been absurd—a queen's Inquisitor, sword still at her hip, interrupting a farewell at the end of the world—but when Freya turned, Serane was looking at the door with an expression Freya recognized from mirrors: a person watching a thing they had spent their whole life being kept from.

"The registers," Serane said. "When you give your name away—the crown's copy doesn't burn, if that's what you're hoping. I've seen unbinding done once, in the capital, to a traitor's family. The paper stays. The paper always stays." Her jaw worked. "But the name stops answering to it. The writ goes blank. They'll send men to fetch a Pemberton and the Pemberton won't be anywhere in the world for the writ to find." She met Freya's eyes. "Do you understand what you're about to be? Not free. *Unlisted.* There are worse things than hunted, in an empire. There is being outside its sentences entirely."

"There are worse things than hunted," Freya agreed. "I've been hunted since chapter one."

Something moved across Serane's face that might, in a better century, have been a smile. Then she unsheathed her sword—not to threaten, but to lay it flat across her palms, the way soldiers did for oaths in the old songs Aldric sang.

"Casper-born," she said quietly. "Choir-child. Smuggled out in a bread basket before I could walk. I bought that memory back from a broker in a market that shouldn't exist, and it has ruined every certainty I own." She looked at the plain wooden door. "If this works—if they pour through—I will stand on the other side and I will not raise a hand against a single one of them. Whatever that makes me."

"A witness," said Ilse. "Every kingdom needs one. Most kill theirs."

Behind them, through the bones of the dying city, the iron hymns swelled. The ground trembled. Somewhere a street that had repeated its last day for a hundred years simply stopped mid-stride, and its people folded into motes of light like breath fading off glass. The crescent of the choir thinned. Time was not merely short; it was being eaten.

Freya turned to the door.

* * *

Up close, the wood smelled of her grandmother's hearth. Of course it did. Every door was every door; that was the whole secret of doors, and she had been a gatekeeper her entire life without once understanding it.

She put her hand on the latch. It was warm.

"How?" Aldric asked hoarsely. He stood at her shoulder, the tuning fork clutched in his fist like a rosary. "What do you actually *do*?"

"I say it," Freya said. "All of it. Everything the name carries. And then I let go of the handle." She breathed. "Hold the note for me? Just—one note. So there's something on this side to walk toward."

He didn't argue again. He gathered what was left of his voice—the voice that had unmade wards and opened locks and carried a whole smuggled country in its throat—and he began, low and steady, the first note of the crossing-song, the one they had sung together on the bridge of nailed songs with the chasm roaring underneath. His wound bled through the bandage with the effort. He sang anyway.

And the choir, what remained of it, took the third above him. And Serane, to Freya's astonishment, took the fifth, her trained royal voice finding the interval as if a hundred years of silence were nothing. Three voices. A chord. The smallest possible harmony, which was, Aldric had once told her, how every door in every world had ever been opened: never by force, always by agreement.

Freya closed her eyes.

*My name is Freya Pemberton.*

*I am the daughter of a daughter of a daughter of keepers. I am the girl who swept moss off an arch for nineteen years believing it mattered. I am the one who ran through a cracking gate with soldiers behind her and stepped into a country that was supposed to be gone. I am the student who was terrible at the gate-songs and then wasn't. I am the woman who crossed a chasm on another man's breath and loved him in the only language the crossing allowed. I am the granddaughter of Marren Pemberton, who burned our letters and lied beautifully and gave me white iron and never once told me she was sorry.*

*I am afraid. I am so tired. I am ready.*

*Take it. Not my life. My name. Let it ring somewhere I can't follow. Let the registers go blank. Let the ward fall and the valley wake and the crown reach for me and close on empty air. I give it freely, wholly, with nothing hidden in my pockets—*

*I am no one. Open.*

She let go of the handle.

For one endless instant nothing happened, and Freya stood in the silence at the heart of the gate feeling exactly the same—which was, she realized distantly, the terror of it. No lightning. No tearing. Surrender was not dramatic; it was just letting your hand open.

Then the name left her.

She felt it go the way you feel a tooth that has ached for years finally come free—pain and relief braided so tightly they were indistinguishable. Something vast and old turned its attention upon her, weighed her, found the gift complete, and *accepted*. The two stone rings above and below her shuddered, slowed, aligned—for the first time in a century, the names of the forgotten and the names of the mourned faced each other perfectly—and the plain wooden door swung inward onto ordinary golden daylight.

Not twilight. Daylight. Wind. The smell of rain on the Vell hillsides, drifting through from a world that had gone on existing all this time without knowing what it had lost.

"Oh," said Ilse, in a voice like dawn. "Oh, it's *morning.*"

The people of Casper came like water finds a crack.

They poured through—first the ghost-choir, whose deaths fell away from them at the threshold like cloaks dropped at a doorway, so that they stumbled out into the waking world solid and weeping and alive; then the market folk and the millers and the children frozen mid-game, the thousands who had repeated their last days for a century, each one shedding sorrow at the sill and emerging gasping into grass and weather and time. Freya stood beside the open door and could not stop smiling. Names streamed past her—she heard them, every one, called out by the door itself in a voice like the tuning fork's ring: *Bren of the Weavers' Row, Maret Ossel, little Tam, Tam, Tam*—each name ringing once as its owner crossed, and passing on, freed, into the morning.

Aldric sank down beside her, spent, laughing, crying, his ruined voice a rasp of joy. Serane stood rigid at parade rest, watching her stolen country walk home past her, and did not move, and did not look away, and her sword stayed in its sheath.

Freya felt the emptiness where her name had been and found it was not hollow at all. It was spacious. It was a doorway-shaped room inside her, and through it she could feel everything: the valley waking below the hills, the ward dissolving like frost, the crown's registers three hundred miles away going quietly, irrevocably blank.

She was nobody. She was the way.

She turned back once, before following the last of them through—to look at the rings of stone, already dimming, already forgetting—and saw, carved fresh on the inner rim where no carving had been, a single new name, ringing softly in white iron tones too high for ears, too true to stop.

She smiled, and stepped out of the kingdom, and did not look again.

Behind her, far off through the collapsing light, the crown's engineers reached the heart of Casper—and found it empty, and began, in their iron voices, to sing.
