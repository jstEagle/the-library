## 20. The Unmaking of the Kingdom

The door opened the way a wound opens—slowly, then all at once—and Serane Voss stood in the flood of it and understood that she had spent her whole life on the wrong side of history without ever once being told which side was which.

Light came through. Not sunlight; she knew sunlight. This was older, warmer, the color of things remembered rather than seen, and it poured through Freya Pemberton's half-open gate like water through a broken dam, and where it touched the iron hymns the engineers had chained across Casper's heart, the hymns began to scream.

Serane had heard men scream. She had heard wards break and choirs burn and a child in an interrogation cell sing her mother's lullaby in a voice that cracked on every third note. None of it had prepared her for the sound of chained magic coming apart.

It was not loud, at first. That was the horror of it. The white-iron chains the artificers had wound around the gate's keystone simply began to *sing back*—each locked verse finding its original key, each suppression finding the song it had been built to smother. And the engineers, kneeling at their instruments with their styluses and their calibration plates, looked up with the expressions of men who have just realized the animal they are skinning is still alive.

"Voss!" Captain Helder's voice, from somewhere behind her. "Containment is failing! Fall back to the second perimeter!"

She did not move.

She was watching a man named Ostrev—one of the crown's best artificers, a gray-bearded technician who hummed while he worked, who had once shown her his daughter's drawing of a horse—watching him try to cut a chain free of the gate with cold-iron shears. The chain sang under his hands. It sang the first line of something old, something in the pre-purge cadences Aldric Vane had taught her twenty years ago in a rehearsal hall that no longer existed, and Ostrev's shears went soft as wax in his grip, and the chain passed through them like light through glass, and then it passed through *him*.

He did not die screaming. He died singing—the hymn taking his voice and giving it back to the kingdom, one more voice returned to a choir that had been starved for a century. His body came apart the way a chord comes apart: all at once, into notes, into air. Where he had knelt there was nothing but a scatter of gray dust and a daughter's drawing of a horse, lifting gently on a wind that should not exist indoors.

*Oh,* Serane thought, with terrible clarity. *We were never purging magic. We were hoarding it. And now the vault has decided to empty itself.*

* * *

The exodus was already underway.

Through the widening gate they came—not ghosts anymore, not quite, because the door was open halfway and the halfway was enough. The dead of Casper, or the not-dead, or whatever you called people who had been folded out of time to keep them safe from the same crown that now stood in their streets with chains and calipers. They came through in families. In guilds. A baker carrying a tray of bread that steamed in the waking world's air. Two masons arguing about a lintel. Children first, always children, because the ghost-choir had organized the order of departure the way they organized everything—with music, with patience, with a grief so old it had worn smooth as river stone.

And everywhere the people passed, the kingdom behind them dimmed.

That was the arithmetic no one had said aloud. Casper was not being saved by this. Casper was being *spent*. Every soul that walked through the gate took a street with it, a memory, a stone's worth of song. Serane could see it happening in real time: the Market of Last Days going translucent at its edges like breath fading off glass; the drowned cathedral's towers sinking another foot into nothing; a whole district of weavers' houses simply ceasing between one heartbeat and the next, not destroyed but *concluded*, finished the way a finished song is finished.

Freya Pemberton stood at the gate's heart with her hand flat against the stone, holding the door at half, and she looked like a woman bailing a sinking boat with her own two hands and knowing exactly how much sea remained.

Aldric Voss—no. Aldric Vane. She would have to learn to say it right again—lay propped against the archway, bandaged, gray-faced, his ruined throat working as he coached the departing singers in low broken phrases. Even wrecked, even whispering, his voice moved people into lines. Some things did not need volume. Some things only needed to be true.

"Serane." Helder again, closer now, breathing hard. He had blood on his cheek that wasn't his. "Second perimeter is gone. Third is forming up at the breach. The Marshal wants—"

"The Marshal wants what?" She turned and looked at him fully, and she watched the words die in his mouth, because she had been interrogating people for fifteen years and she knew precisely how to make a face that ended sentences.

Helder was forty years old. He had a wife in the capital and a mother in the provinces and a career built on doing what he was told inside walls that were, it turned out, built on theft. He looked at her the way soldiers look at officers when the ground shifts beneath everyone at once and nobody knows whose orders still count.

"He wants the gate held," Helder said finally. "At any cost. He wants the engineers reinforced. He wants—" His eyes flicked past her, to the exodus, to the baker and the masons and the children, and something in his jaw worked sideways. "Inquisitor, they're just... walking. They're not fighting. Why aren't they fighting?"

"Because they're not invaders," Serane said. "They're refugees. From us."

* * *

She found the last of the engineers at the gate's northern anchor point, six of them in crown gray, driving new chains into stone that was actively refusing to hold them. Their foreman was a woman named Ilsette Kraye whom Serane had personally cleared for this posting three months ago, back when clearing people for postings meant checking loyalty ledgers instead of deciding whether they deserved to be unmade.

"Inquisitor Voss." Kraye didn't look up from her work. Sweat ran through the dust on her face in clean channels. "Thank the saints. We need escort. The locals are panicking, the chains are resonating wrong, and I've lost four crews. Four. I need you to clear this plaza and hold it until the Marshal's reinforcement arrives with the heavy anchors."

"The heavy anchors won't arrive," Serane said.

Kraye looked up then. "Excuse me?"

"The Marshal's column broke at the valley road. The ward failed. Half your reinforcements are standing in a wheat field wondering why their instruments read nothing at all, and the other half deserted when they saw what came out of the gate." She kept her voice level, professional, the tone she'd used in a hundred interrogation rooms, and hated herself a little for how well it still served. "You're done, Kraye. Pull your crews out."

"We are *not* done. The edict—"

"The edict says the gifted must be surrendered to the crown." Serane stepped over a fallen chain—a chain that was quietly humming a harvest song, a reaping song, a song about bringing things home—and stopped close enough that Kraye had to tilt her head back to hold her gaze. "I've read the original commission. Have you? Not the herald's version. The sealed one. Because I have, and it doesn't say *surrender*. It says *inventory*."

Kraye's face did something complicated. "I don't know what you're talking about."

"You do. You calibrated the extraction arrays yourself. You know the gate wasn't being studied. It was being fitted with taps." Serane gestured at the dissolving city, the translucent market, the cathedral sinking into its own memory. "This whole kingdom was put in a vault a hundred years ago, and the crown has spent that century telling itself it was a mercy. It was a granary. And today the granary opened itself, and everything the crown stored here—" she spread her hand at the light pouring through the door, at the magic dispersing into the waking world like dawn into a dark room— "is going free. Into the soil. Into the water. Into every child born next spring from here to the coast. You can't chain that, Kraye. You can't even *weigh* it. All you can do is decide whether you're going to be standing here when the last of these hymns finishes coming undone."

One of the junior engineers made a small sound. Another dropped his stylus and did not pick it up.

"The Marshal will have you all tried for desertion," Kraye said, but her voice had lost its floor.

"The Marshal will be lucky if the crown survives the season," Serane said. "Go home. Kiss someone you love. Learn a song. That's an order."

For a long moment nobody moved. Then the youngest engineer—nineteen, maybe twenty, with ink-stained fingers and the look of a boy who had signed on because artifice paid better than farming—turned and walked. Then another. Kraye held out longest, staring at the gate as though she could argue with it, as though enough discipline applied to reality might make reality apologize and revert. But the chain at her feet slid loose from the stone with a sound almost like relief, and dissolved into a thread of gold light, and rose, and joined a slow upward drift of such threads rising all across the dying city—Casper's songs going home to the world, one strand at a time.

Kraye picked up her kit. She walked away without saluting, which Serane noted, distantly, was probably the most honest thing anyone in crown service had done all century.

* * *

By late afternoon the plaza before the gate was empty of everything except the leaving.

Serane stood at the edge of it with her sword still sheathed—it had been sheathed since the bridge, since the moment on the Bridge of Nailed Songs when she had drawn steel and then, for the first time in her life, put it away unfinished—and counted what remained of her command.

Forty-one soldiers had crossed into Casper. Eleven still answered when she called the roll. The rest had scattered into the memory-streets during the collapse, some fleeing toward the breach, some simply walking off into districts that no longer existed, swallowed by the very country they'd been sent to dismantle. She found herself unable to mourn them and unable to judge them either. Men who run into fog are at least honest about the fog.

Her eleven formed up badly, in a loose crescent, faces streaked with ash and worse. Corporal Dane, who had held the third perimeter alone for an hour. The twins, Rill and Maret, who had joined the same week eleven years ago and had not spoken since the engineers began to come apart. Young Fenn, who kept looking at the gate as if it owed him money, or an apology, or both.

"Report," Serane said.

"Gate's stable at half-open, ma'am," Dane said. "The girl—Pemberton—she's holding it. Been holding it for hours. The choir-master's conscious again. The last of the Casper folk are through except a handful of singers staying to the end." He hesitated. "There's, ah. There's a question forming, ma'am. Amongst the men. About what happens when we're recalled."

"What happens when you're recalled," Serane repeated.

"Yes, ma'am. Whether we report that we stood down. Whether we report what the Marshal ordered and what actually happened here. Whether—" Fenn's voice cracked through, young and furious. "Whether any of it goes in a ledger at all, ma'am, or whether we were never here, the way *they* were never here. The way Casper was never here. A hundred years, and the whole kingdom written out of the rolls like it never—"

"Fenn." Dane's warning tone.

"No, I want to hear her say it," Fenn said, and turned to Serane with the naked challenge of the very young, the kind she had once crushed in interrogation rooms for practice. "What do we tell them, Inquisitor? What's the official truth? Because I've been thinking about it, and I don't think there's a version we can write down that isn't a lie."

The wind off the dying kingdom moved through the plaza. Somewhere beyond the arch, the ghost-choir had begun the last verses—the leaving-song, the one Aldric had whispered about with tears in his ruined voice, the song Casper had waited a hundred years to finish. The threads of golden light rose thicker now, streaming up through the dissolving rooftops like reversed rain.

Serane unbuckled her sword belt. She did it slowly, so they could all see, and laid the belt on the stones at her feet with the buckle facing the gate.

"There is no official truth," she said. "There hasn't been since before any of us were born. Here's what I'm going to do. I'm going to walk through that gate last, after the last singer, after the last thread of this place has gone into the world. And then I am going to send my resignation to the Marshal by courier, and when the warrant for my arrest arrives—and it will—I'll be somewhere he can't reach, learning how to live in a country where the gifted don't have to hide." She looked at each of them in turn, eleven tired faces, her last command, her final duty. "Any of you who want to stand with me when it comes to that, follow me. Any of you who want to go back and take your chances with the ledger, go now, with my thanks and no mark against you. Both choices are honorable. Only the lie is not."

Nobody moved for a moment. Then Rill unbuckled her belt. Then Maret. Then Dane, with the sigh of a man setting down something he'd carried so long he'd forgotten its weight. One by one, all eleven, until the pile of white-and-gray steel on the stones looked like the shed skin of something that had finally grown too large for it.

Fenn was crying and pretending not to. Serane let him.

"Ma'am," Dane said quietly. "Where do we go? If we're not crown anymore. If we're not anything."

Serane looked through the gate. On the far side, in the green and ordinary light of the waking world, she could see the valley of Vell—terraced fields, a mill with its wheel turning, smoke rising from chimneys of people who had spent generations perfecting the art of not looking. She could see villagers gathering at a cautious distance, watching the procession of the impossible come down out of the archway and into their wheat fields. She could see an old woman step forward and offer bread to a Casper child, hesitantly, the way you offer your hand to a dog you've been taught to fear.

And further up the slope, at the mouth of the gate itself, she saw Freya Pemberton turn and look directly at her—as if she'd felt the weight of eleven sets of eyes settle on her—as if the door itself had whispered her name.

"She's still deciding," Serane said, and found, to her surprise, that she was smiling. "Ask her."

She picked up her pack, left the sword lying in the dust of a kingdom that no longer existed, and led her eleven soldiers through the door—out of the crown's service, out of the crown's story, into whatever was being written next.
