## 22. Coda: The Choir Remembers

The first voice belonged to a miller's boy named Tam, who was nine years old and could not have told you why he opened his mouth at the harvest festival and sang a note no one in Vell had taught him.

It happened like this: the fiddlers had finished their third reel, the cider had gone around twice, and the whole village stood in the square beneath strings of lanterns, breathing the smell of woodsmoke and crushed apples. And Tam, waiting for his mother by the bake-stall, heard something under the noise. Not a melody exactly. A shape. A place where a note wanted to be and wasn't yet.

He sang it.

It was a small sound, thin as wire, and it should have died there among the lanterns and the laughter. Instead an old woman selling honey cakes turned her head. Then a shepherd. Then three of the Casper-folk who had come down from the gate that spring, people with faces like carvings worn soft by rain, who had spent a year learning how to buy bread without flinching when coins changed hands.

One of them—a broad-shouldered woman named Maren, who had been a wall-mason in a kingdom that no longer existed—took up the note a fifth above him.

Then someone else. Then someone else.

Nobody decided to sing. That was the strange thing, the thing people would argue about for years afterward in the taverns and the orchards and the doorways at dusk. Nobody decided. The song simply remembered itself, the way water remembers downhill, and within a minute half the square was carrying a tune that had no name in either country—half of it valley, half of it Casper, braided somewhere in the middle so tightly you couldn't say where one strand ended and the other began.

Freya Pemberton stood at the edge of it all with a cup of cider going cold in her hand, and she did not sing.

She never sang. Everyone knew this about her now, the way everyone knew which houses kept bees and whose dog would follow you home. Freya did not sing, and she stood at the edges of things, and if you asked her why she would smile in a way that answered nothing.

But she listened. She always listened. And as the song swelled over the square—Tam's thin wire of a note still holding at its center, stubborn as a nail—Freya closed her eyes and felt the ground hum beneath her feet, very faintly, the way it had hummed once beneath a moss-covered arch when a stray melody found it after years of silence.

Some doors, she thought, are only ever closed from one side.

* * *

Aldric heard it too, from the little house behind the gate where he'd lived since midwinter.

He was mending a chair—one leg cracked, his hands better at fixing things than they had ever been at anything else—and when the sound came over the hill he set down the glue pot and sat very still until the last of it faded.

His voice had not been the same since the riot in the memory streets. A shard of collapsing stone, or perhaps a scrap of the crown's iron hymns—it had never been clear which—had torn through his throat on the day he threw himself between the Inquisitors' blades and the last singers of the Ash Choir. The healers said he was lucky to speak at all. He could speak. He could even, on good days, carry a simple air across a room without it splintering halfway.

But he would never again hold a choir together with nothing but breath and will, the way he had in the capital's great cathedral, the way he had crossing the Bridge of Nailed Songs with a frightened girl beside him whose harmony had fit his own so perfectly that both of them had wept on the far side and pretended they hadn't.

He missed it the way a man misses a limb. Not constantly. But in certain weathers. In certain hours.

Tonight, though, listening to the village sing itself whole across the darkening fields, Aldric found he didn't mind the crack in his voice so much. Because what came over the hill was not a performance. It was not art. It was the sound of two peoples discovering they had always been singing variations on the same few songs—lullabies that shared a bone structure, work songs with matching rhythms, griefs that rhymed—and that the century between them had been thinner than anyone believed.

That had been the point all along, hadn't it? He'd spent thirty years believing music was a weapon, because in the capital it had to be. A key to pick locks with. A blade to cut wards with. A country carried in the throat because there was nowhere left to put it down.

Here, tonight, it was just a thing people made together because being alone was worse.

He picked the chair back up and went on mending it, humming the festival tune under his breath, letting it fray and mend and fray again in his scarred throat, and finding, to his own mild surprise, that the frayed version was also beautiful.

* * *

Serane Voss did not attend festivals.

This was not quite true anymore, and she knew it, and the knowing annoyed her. She attended them. She stood at the back, arms crossed, in plain gray clothes with no insignia, watching the valley people watch the Casper-folk and the Casper-folk watch them back, cataloguing every flicker of suspicion and every slow thaw with the same professional attention she had once given to interrogation rooms.

She told herself she was assessing risk. A year ago she had stood down forty crown soldiers on the steps of a dissolving kingdom and chosen exile over orders, and the habit of assessment dies hard in a woman trained since childhood to see threats everywhere.

But the truth—the truth she had finally stopped running from, somewhere around the fourth month, when Maren the wall-mason had shoved a bowl of stew into her hands and said *you look like a woman who hasn't eaten in front of anyone in years*—was simpler.

Serane came because she did not know how to belong anywhere, and she had decided, in her grim and methodical way, to learn.

She had been born in Casper. This fact had been taken from her before she could walk, along with her mother's face and her first language and the lullaby that should have been hers, and the crown had built an Inquisitor out of the empty space. For a year now she had been slowly furnishing that space with recovered things: words in the old tongue, learned badly from Maren; songs, sung worse; a memory of a harbor she had never seen that nonetheless made her chest ache like homesickness.

Tonight she stood at the edge of the square with her arms crossed, watching the miller's boy Tam conduct half the village with a wooden spoon, and she caught herself mouthing the harmony.

Maren appeared at her elbow with two cups of cider and handed her one without asking.

"You're smiling," Maren said.

"I am not."

"You're doing the thing where your mouth almost does it and then you arrest it. Like a door you keep catching before it opens." Maren drank. "You know the whole village can see you, Voss. They've got a bet going about when you'll finally laugh."

"A bet."

"Tam's holding the stakes. Three coppers and a knife."

Serane stared at the child in question, who was currently standing on a bench beating time with the spoon and grinning at her with the fearless directness of the young, and felt something in her chest do a thing she refused to name in front of witnesses.

"The knife," she said, "is mine. I gave it to him last month for whittling."

"Then your coppers are already committed," said Maren, "and you may as well laugh."

Serane did not laugh. But later, when the big song rose again and the whole square was carrying it, she sang—quietly, at the back, in a voice trained for precision and ruined by tenderness—and nobody arrested her for it, and nobody looked at her strangely, and the door in her chest, the one she had been catching for a year, stood open.

* * *

Above the gatekeeper's house hung a tuning fork of white iron.

It was not a grand thing. It hung from a leather cord on a nail above a plain wooden door that had replaced the moss-covered arch, because the arch had gone with everything else when Casper unmade itself—dissolved into the waking world the way frost dissolves into morning, leaving the valley wet with magic and blinking in the light. What remained was a door. Oak, iron-banded, ordinary. Aldric had built it himself, badly, and Maren had quietly rebuilt it the following week while he was at market, and neither of them ever mentioned it.

The fork hung there because the ghost-choir's last singer—an ancient voice named Ilse, who had stayed three months after the exodus to teach the valley's children the old harmonies before fading out like a candle—had pressed it into Freya's hand and said:

*A kingdom is not walls. It was never walls. It is this. Strike it whenever you forget.*

So the fork hung above the door, and once in a while—on the solstice, on the anniversary of the opening, on nights when the wind off the hills sounded like voices—someone would climb up and strike it against the doorframe, and the note would ring out pure and silver across the valley, and dogs would lift their heads, and somewhere in the square below someone would begin to sing.

Tonight, during the festival, it was Tam.

He'd climbed the hill with two friends on a dare, or so they claimed, though all three of them had been careful to bring the little wooden mallet Ilse had left for exactly this purpose. He struck the fork against the frame. The note rang out over the fields, over the lantern-lit square, over the heads of dancers and bakers and a former Inquisitor drinking cider, and Tam stood there with his hand still raised, feeling the vibration buzz through his palm and up his arm and into the place behind his sternum where songs live.

"It's warm," he said to his friends, wonderingly. "Why's it warm? Metal doesn't stay warm."

His friends had no answer. Neither, when they got home and told everyone, did their parents. Only Freya, when Tam worked up the courage weeks later to ask her directly, smiled her non-answering smile and said:

"Doors remember who knocked."

* * *

Which brings us to the child.

Her name was Wren. She was six, the daughter of one of the Casper families, born inside the gate during the frozen century and therefore, technically, older than everyone in the valley—a joke her parents told constantly and she understood not at all. She had grown up in a country of repeated days, among streets that were memory and neighbors who were echoes, and she had come through the door into the waking world at the age of five, into a life where bread actually got eaten and rain actually fell and nothing, mercifully, ever happened exactly the same way twice.

Wren adored the festival. Wren adored everything. Wren was the kind of child who asked questions the way other children threw stones—frequently, enthusiastically, and without regard for where they landed.

She found Freya at the edge of the square near midnight, when the dancing had gone loose and slow and the lanterns were burning low. Freya was sitting on the low wall by the well, alone, the way she was always slightly alone even amid a crowd, and Wren planted herself in front of her with the gravity of a small ambassador.

"You're the door lady," Wren said.

"I'm a door lady," Freya agreed.

"My mama says you gave away your name. To open the door." Wren frowned, working through the arithmetic. "Did it hurt?"

Freya considered the question with more seriousness than most adults would have mustered. Around them the last verses of the big song drifted and frayed, and the night was full of the sounds of a valley learning to be two places at once.

"No," she said at last. "It was like setting down something I'd been carrying so long I'd forgotten it was heavy."

"But then—" Wren's brow furrowed. "What do we call you?"

"You just called me the door lady."

"That's not a *name*, though." Wren stamped her foot with six-year-old finality. "Names are yours. Mama says. Nobody else can give you one, not even the king, not even a priest. You have to *decide*." She peered up. "So what did you decide?"

And Freya Pemberton—who had been Freya of the Hollow Gate, keeper of the last seam, last living key to a vanished kingdom, the girl who sold her happiest memory to a broker and surrendered her name to a dying door—looked down at this small, impossible ambassador from a country that existed only in songs now, and gave the answer she had been giving all year, the answer that was not a deflection at all but the truest thing she knew:

"I'm still deciding."

Wren studied her for a long moment, the way children study things, without mercy and without malice. Then she nodded, satisfied, as if this were the most reasonable answer in the world.

"Good," she said. "Deciding takes forever. Mama says I'm still deciding whether I like turnips, and I've been deciding my whole entire life." She grabbed Freya's hand, tugging her toward the square, toward the music, toward the lanterns. "Come *on*. They're starting the last song. Everybody has to be in it. That's the rule."

"What rule?" said Freya, letting herself be pulled.

"The rule of the song," said Wren, as if this explained everything, and maybe it did.

* * *

Later—much later, when the lanterns had guttered and the dancers had drifted homeward and the valley lay quiet under a sky salted with stars—two people walked up the hill to the plain wooden door.

They did not walk close, exactly. But they did not walk apart, either. Somewhere between those two facts lay a year of mended chairs and shared suppers and long silences that had stopped needing to be filled, and neither of them had ever put a word to what it was, because some harmonies are spoiled by being named.

At the door, Aldric paused. His breath fogged in the cold. Above them the white iron fork caught the starlight, faintly warm, faintly ringing with a note that had finished sounding an hour ago and somehow had not stopped.

"They'll want a festival next spring too," he said. "Bigger. Tam's already organizing. There's talk of a proper choir. Mixed—valley voices and Casper ones. He asked me to lead it." A dry pause. "I told him my voice wouldn't survive a verse."

"And?"

"And he said—" Aldric's mouth twitched. "*Cracked bells still ring.* Six years old. Where do they get it?"

"From us," Freya said. "We're all still deciding. They're just faster about it."

She reached up and touched the tuning fork, lightly, two fingers against the white iron. It hummed against her skin, barely, a thread of sound more felt than heard—the note that had started everything, a stray melody in a silent valley, a gate remembering it was a door.

Below, in the darkened square, a single window was still lit. Someone was singing a lullaby to a baby—not a valley lullaby, not a Casper one, but something new, something braided, something that had never existed until this year and would exist forever now, passed mouth to mouth down the generations like a coal handed carefully between cupped hands.

Aldric listened to it. So did Freya. Neither spoke.

Then Aldric pushed open the plain wooden door, and held it—for her, as he always did, as he probably always would—and Freya stepped through into the warmth of the house, and the door swung shut behind them.

It locked.

Of course it locked. It was, after all, only a door.

And outside, above the lintel, the white iron fork hung in the cold and the starlight, humming its one unbroken note to no one—to everyone—waiting, patiently, for whoever came knocking next.
