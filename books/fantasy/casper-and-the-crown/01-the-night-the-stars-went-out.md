## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

There is a particular hour — somewhere past the second pot of tea, somewhere before the candle gutters — when a map begins to tell you lies. Not big lies. Not the kind that get you hanged. Small ones. A river that has wandered a hundred yards from where you drew it. A pass that looks wider on vellum than it ever was in stone. I call them lies, but really they're confessions: the map admitting it was made by a person, and the person admitting the world doesn't hold still.

I had been arguing with the Thornfell passes for six hours that night, and the passes were winning.

The chart spread across my worktable was a smuggler's chart, which is to say a chart of everything the crown's cartographers were paid to leave off the official ones. Goat tracks that could carry a man and forty pounds of untaxed wool over the spine of the mountains. The dry creek beds that flood in spring and swallow wagons. The ward-stones along the Thornfell border, marked in my own careful hand with the little star-symbols that meant *warded, safe, do not cross after dark*.

Forty-one ward-stones. I had counted them twice, once on foot and once from the back of a very patient mule, because a cartographer who guesses is a cartographer who gets people killed. Forty-one stars stitched along the border like buttons on a coat, each one fed by the old magic, each one holding the dark on the far side of the mountains where the dark belonged.

I was redrawing the easternmost cluster when the candle did something strange.

It leaned. Not toward the window, the way a flame leans when a draft comes under the door. It leaned *up*, as if the flame had suddenly remembered it wanted to be somewhere else entirely, and for one absurd moment I thought: *the wick's gone hollow, buy better candles, Vesper, you're a professional.*

Then every star in the sky went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded over. I know cloud. Cloud is soft, it's a blanket, it comes with a smell of rain and a certain muffled quality to the world. This was not cloud. This was forty-one ward-stars and every one of their ten thousand sisters extinguished in the same instant, the way a room goes dark when someone snuffs the candle — deliberate, total, *willed*.

The ink on my chart crawled.

I want to be precise about this, because precision was my trade and it is the only language I trust when the world stops making sense. The ink did not move like ink moves when paper is wet. It moved like something alive that had been pretending to be ink for a very long time and had briefly forgotten itself. The star-symbols along the border — my forty-one careful little marks — shivered, and one of them, the easternmost, the one I'd touched last with my pen, simply unraveled into a blot.

I sat very still.

There is a rule in cartography older than any guild: *if the map changes and you didn't change it, leave.* Every apprentice learns it with a ruler rapped across their knuckles. Maps change for three reasons. Someone redrew them. The land changed. Or the map was never honest to begin with. But there is a fourth reason, the one we don't teach the children, the one the old ward-keepers used to mutter about over their pipes: sometimes the map changes because *the world has forgotten what it was*, and the ink, poor loyal creature, is trying to keep up.

The wards snapped.

I felt it before I heard it, which I know sounds like the sort of thing a person says to make themselves seem mystical, and I want to state for the record that I am not mystical. I am a professional. But a border ward is a tension, the same way a bridge is a tension, the same way a held breath is a tension, and when forty-one of them let go all at once, the release travels through the ground and up through the legs of your chair and into your back teeth. It sounded — the sound arrived a heartbeat after the feeling — like rope parting under load. Not one rope. A whole rigging, cut in a single stroke, every line going slack at once.

The candle righted itself. The flame settled. Outside my window, the valley lay black as the inside of a hat.

I did the sensible thing. I did what any sensible professional does when the impossible happens in their place of work: I made tea, on the theory that the world could not have truly ended while the kettle still worked.

The kettle worked. The tea was mediocre. The stars stayed dead.

* * *

By the time the grey came up — a wrong grey, a grey with no gold in it, the color of ash stirred into milk — I had done three things. I had walked the length of my lane and back twice, looking up like an idiot. I had checked my instruments, all of them, twice, because a compass that lies is a tragedy but a compass that's *right* about a world gone wrong is worse. And I had unrolled every chart I owned across the floor of my workroom until I was standing in a paper sea, looking down at a border that had, overnight, begun to forget itself.

The ward-stone symbols were fading. Not all at once — the western ones still held their little stars, stubborn as weeds — but moving east they grew fainter, and past the Thornfell spine they were simply gone, as though my own hand had never drawn them. In their place, in ink I did not own and did not remember buying, a new line was forming. A route. It ran up through the passes, over the spine, toward a place I had never marked because no honest chart marked it: the citadel in the high moon-country where the old king slept his enchanted sleep, the one they tell children about, the one that is a bedtime story the way a wolf is a bedtime story.

The route was drawing itself toward the citadel. And at the end of the route, in that same borrowed ink, three words were appearing one letter at a time, slow as frost:

*before the thirteenth.*

I rolled the chart up so fast I creased it. Then I unrolled it again, because I am a professional, and professionals do not look away from evidence no matter how much they'd like to.

That was when I heard the road.

* * *

The valley road below my cottage had been quiet for three years, ever since the toll-house burned and the surveyors moved on. It was not quiet now. It had the sound of a road carrying more than it was built for — wheels, feet, a low animal hum of fear, the particular creak of a cart loaded past all sense with everything a family owns and several things it doesn't.

Refugees. Coming down out of the high valleys, out of the villages that sat under the shadow of Thornfell, moving away from the border the way water moves away from a lifted gate.

I went down with my chart-case on my shoulder because I am, apparently, incapable of going anywhere without it, and because a part of me — the professional part, the part I hide behind when the rest of me is afraid — insisted that somewhere in that flood of people there would be information, and information is just geography that hasn't been drawn yet.

They were a mixed lot. Farmers with their seed-grain in sacks, which told me they expected to be gone a long time or not come back at all, because no farmer abandons seed-grain for anything less than the end of the world. A midwife I knew by reputation, hauling two goats and a child who appeared to be asleep standing up. A pair of miners from the eastern diggings, their faces still black in the creases, walking with the flat, fast stride of men who have decided that looking back is a luxury.

I stopped the third cart that passed and asked the question you ask: *what did you see?*

The driver was an old woman with a face like a walnut and the unimpressed stare of someone who had buried two husbands and did not intend to be buried by a cartographer. She looked me over — the ink on my fingers, the chart-case, the general air of a person who asks questions from a standing start — and decided I was worth exactly one answer.

"Went out," she said. "All of 'em. Same breath."

"The stars?"

"All of 'em." She flicked the reins. "You didn't see? Where were you, under a rock?"

"Under a roof."

"Same thing, these days." And the cart rolled on, because everyone on that road had somewhere to be and none of it was here.

I walked the line of them for an hour, asking my question, collecting my answers, and the answers braided together into a shape I didn't like. The ward-stones along the eastern pass had gone dark — not broken, not toppled, *dark*, the carved stars in them gone grey as old bone. A shepherd swore he'd seen the dark on the far side of the border move, which shepherds say, shepherds have always said, and everyone smiles at shepherds, except that this shepherd wasn't smiling. A tinker had a cousin who worked the citadel road, and the cousin had sent one message before the message-lines went quiet: *the king's mountain is humming.*

And one boy, no older than ten, sitting in the back of a wagon between a loom and a barrel, told me the thing that settled it. He said it matter-of-factly, the way children say the truest things, because they haven't yet learned which truths are expensive.

"The dark's drinking the light," he said. "That's what my gran says. It's not that the stars died. It's that something's *drinking* them."

His grandmother cuffed him gently and told me not to listen to the boy. I thanked her. I did not stop thinking about it.

* * *

By midday the road had a rhythm to it, and I had a decision, and the two of them were at war.

The sensible decision was south. South was the capital, the guild, the Arch-Cartographer's tower with its thick walls and its famous indifference to anything that couldn't be invoiced. South was where you took evidence like a self-drawing chart and a dead sky, handed it to men with better seals than yours, and let them decide what it meant. South was the professional choice. I had made the professional choice perhaps four hundred times in my life, and it had never once required me to climb toward the thing everyone else was fleeing.

The other decision was sitting in my chart-case, drawing itself in ink I didn't own, pointing up the valley toward the Thornfell passes and the sleeping king and the three words: *before the thirteenth.*

I spread the chart on a milestone, right there on the road, with refugees streaming past on either side of me, and I studied it the way I'd studied a thousand charts, and I asked it the question I ask every map: *what are you trying to tell me?*

A map is an argument. That's the first thing they teach you and the last thing you believe. Every map argues that the world has a shape, and that the shape can be known, and that knowing it will keep you alive. My chart was arguing that I should go up. My chart, made by my hands, in my style, down to the little hooked serifs I put on my north arrows — my chart, which I had locked in my workroom behind a door I'd locked with my own key, was arguing with a borrowed voice.

A professional knows when a map has stopped making sense. It's in the lines. The routes don't connect. The distances lie. The scale drifts. And a professional also knows — this is the harder lesson, the one that costs — that when a map stops making sense, the map is usually still telling the truth, and it's the *world* that's stopped.

The stars hadn't died. The boy's grandmother was wrong about that, and the boy was right. Dead is an ending. This was a *theft*, ongoing, patient, with a route leading to it like a trail of crumbs. And somewhere at the top of that route was a crown on a sleeping head, and somewhere behind me was a guild full of men who would take one look at this chart and lock it in a drawer, and somewhere ahead of me was a thirteenth turn of a moon that was, at that moment, hanging fat and wrong in the ash-grey sky like a coin someone had bitten.

I looked up the valley. The Thornfell passes stood against the sky, black where the wards used to glitter on their summits, and past them, higher, the moon-country, and higher than that — though you couldn't see it, though no honest chart marked it — the citadel.

"Right," I said, to the milestone, because there was no one else to say it to. "Before the thirteenth."

A woman herding three children past me gave me the look people give you when you talk to milestones, which I deserved, and kept walking, which was sensible of her.

I rolled up the chart. I checked my ink, my pens, my spare nibs, the little folding rule my master had given me twenty years ago, the compass — the plain brass compass, north-seeking, reliable, boring, the most trustworthy object I owned.

The compass needle was not pointing north.

It was pointing up the valley. Toward the passes. Toward the citadel. It trembled there against the glass like a small caged thing, insistent, certain, and quite, quite wrong.

I stood on the road in the middle of the end of the world, holding the one instrument that had never lied to me, watching it lie, and I understood — with the particular, cold clarity that comes to professionals in the hour when their profession stops protecting them — that whatever had gone out with the stars had not gone out entirely.

Some of it had come looking for a cartographer.

I put the compass in my pocket, pointed up the valley road, and started to climb.
