## 3. The King in the Dark

I have been dreaming so long that I have forgotten which part is the dream and which part is the man.

That is not entirely true. I remember the man. I remember his name — Aldric, Aldric of the line of Veyra, seventh of that name to sit the Moonlit Throne — the way you remember a word in a language you no longer speak. It sits in my mouth like a coin. I can turn it over. I can feel its weight. I cannot spend it on anything.

Here is what I can tell you with perfect clarity, in case you wondered what a king keeps when everything else is taken: in the eleventh year of my reign I levied a tithe of two coppers per hearth on the river towns of the Vail, to fund the dredging of the Miren channel, and it raised four thousand, one hundred and sixty-three crowns, and the dredging ran nine months over schedule, and the foreman's name was Oskell, and he had a limp from a pike wound he'd taken in my father's war, a war I did not start and did not finish but inherited like the throne, like the crown, like everything.

I remember that. I remember the exact sum.

I cannot remember my mother's face.

I know she had one. I am fairly confident of this. Faces being generally standard issue among mothers. But when I reach for it — and I reach for it often, believe me, I have nothing else to do — what I find instead is the tithe. The coppers. Oskell's limp. The dredging schedule. My own memory has been reorganized by a librarian who hates me, who has shelved everything useless where the precious things used to be and burned the precious things for warmth.

Or not burned. Kept. That's the word that keeps surfacing, cold as a stone in a stream: *kept*.

* * *

The dark here is not empty. That is the first thing I would tell you, if there were a you, if there were a telling. The dark is full. It is the fullest place I have ever been. It presses against me like a crowd at a coronation, and like a coronation crowd it is mostly silent, mostly waiting, and it wants something from me that I am no longer sure I have.

I walk. There is nothing to walk on and nowhere to walk to, but walking is a habit of forty years and habits outlive their purpose. I walked the borders once — the real borders, the ones with mud in them. I walked Thornfell in the rain with the ward-keepers, and they showed me the old wards, the ones laid down before my grandmother's grandmother, and I put my hand on one of the standing stones and felt it hum like a struck bell that had been ringing so long the ringing had become a kind of silence.

The wards are quiet now. I know this the way you know weather through a window. Something snapped — I felt it go, felt it like a rope parting somewhere behind my ribs — and after the snapping came a hunger in the dark, a great slow appetite turning over in its sleep, and I understood, in the wordless way you understand things here, that the appetite was wearing me.

I would like to state, for the record, that I never asked to be worn.

* * *

Sometimes she comes to me. My mother. Or the shape the dark makes when I ask it for my mother, which is not the same thing, and I know it is not the same thing, and I go to her anyway, because a drowning man does not inspect the rope.

"Aldric," she says, and her voice is right, her voice is exactly right, warm and slightly amused, the voice of a woman who found her son's seriousness funny and loved him for it. "Aldric, you're working too hard again."

"Mother," I say. "What did your face look like?"

She smiles. She must smile; I can feel the smile the way you feel sun through cloth. "Silly boy. You know what I looked like."

And I do, for a moment — I get a flash of something, high cheekbones, a scar on the left eyebrow from a falcon she swears was her sister's fault — and then it drains away like water through fingers, and what remains is the tithe of two coppers per hearth, and Oskell's limp, and the nine months over schedule.

"You're not her," I say, when I can speak again.

"No," the shape agrees, and it does not even have the decency to sound sorry. "But I am what's left of where she was. Isn't that nearly as good?"

It is not nearly as good. It is not even nearly nearly as good. But there is no one here to complain to except the thing wearing my mother, and I have a king's training, which means I know how to be polite to monsters. It is, when you come down to it, most of the job.

* * *

The crown talks to me.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is one of the few possessions I have not yet been relieved of. It does not talk the way people talk. There is no voice, exactly. There is a pressure that arranges itself into meaning, the way frost arranges itself into feathers on a window. And it is patient. That is the worst of it. I have met impatient men — I have *been* an impatient man, I once threw a goblet at my chancellor over a grain tariff, and I regret it, I regret it with the one part of me that still regrets things — and impatience is a weakness, it shows you where a man's walls are thin. This thing has no thin walls. It has been hungry for longer than my dynasty has existed, and it can wait out a king the way winter waits out a tree.

"You are comfortable," it says, or means, or is.

"I am being eaten," I say.

"Those are two descriptions of the same arrangement." A pause, the kind of pause that in a person would be a smile. "You have always been good at arrangements. That is why you were chosen."

"I was not chosen. I was crowned. There is a difference, and the difference is that one of them I agreed to."

"You agreed to hold the realm's memories safe." The frost-feathers shift, rearrange. "You have done so. admirably. Everything is here. Nothing has been lost. The tithe of two coppers per hearth. The falcon scar. The dredging of the Miren channel. All of it, kept, safe, *held*. You are the finest vault your realm has ever built."

And here is the thing I do not say, because saying things to it gives it threads to pull: I notice what it lists. The tithe. The scar. The channel. Always the same inventory, always the ledger-items, always the things a king *records* rather than the things a man *keeps*. It is showing me its shelves. And on those shelves, where my mother's face should be, there is a gap, dust-free, recently swept, the way a shelf looks when something has been taken down and is being used.

It is not storing my memories. I understood this a while ago — a while being a unit of measurement that has no meaning here, a while being possibly a year, possibly an afternoon.

It is *spending* them.

* * *

I dream of the observatory sometimes. The real one, in the citadel's high tower, where the court astronomers kept their great brass orreries and argued about the moons — thirteen of them, counting the little grey one the sailors call the Penny, which isn't really a moon at all but no one has the heart to tell it so.

I am not an astronomer. I went up there perhaps six times in my reign, and five of those were to get away from my chancellor. But I remember the room. I remember the smell of oil and cold brass, and the astronomer — the old one, the one with the ink-stained cuffs — who told me once, unasked, in the tone of a man reporting a leak in the roof: "Your Majesty, the stars are a kind of memory. The light is old. When you look up, you are being remembered by things that are already gone."

I thought it was a pretty thing to say. I gave him a stipend.

Now I lie in the dark that is full, and I understand him, and I would give anything — I would give the tithe, I would give the channel, I would give Oskell and his limp and every dry ledger in my skull — to be remembered by something that is already gone.

Because the stars are out. Not dimmed. *Out*. I felt them go, one breath after another, like candles snuffed by a hand moving down a long table, and in the moment after the last one went I felt the dark around me grow *fuller*, and I understood that the light had not been destroyed.

It had been brought here.

It is here with me. All of it. Every star in the sky is in this room, this dark, this crown, hoarded like grain against a famine that will never come, and the hunger that wears me is sitting on the hoard the way a dragon sits on gold — not because it needs it, but because needing is the only shape it has.

* * *

"Tell me about the cartographer," the crown says one day — one *when* — and I go very still, the way you go still when a predator has stopped looking at the horizon and started looking at you.

"I don't know a cartographer," I say.

"You knew a cartographer. Hands in white gloves. An oath. A set of maps of the feeding routes." The frost-feathers turn, considering. "I am asking because I cannot find them. The memories are not on my shelves. Someone took them down and carried them out of me, and I want to know which door they used."

And I want to laugh, because it is the first time the great patient hunger has ever sounded anything but patient — it sounds, if I am honest, *annoyed*, the way my chancellor sounded when a figure wouldn't reconcile — and there is a small, mean, human part of me that wants to savor it.

But under the meanness there is something else, something that catches like a fishhook: *hands in white gloves. An oath.*

I don't remember. Of course I don't remember; remembering is not on offer here. But somewhere under the ledgers, in the flooded cellar where the crown has not yet finished its inventory, something heard *an oath* and *white gloves* and turned over in its sleep, and a name almost surfaced, almost — a name that tasted like ink, like maps, like a door I once held open and then, for reasons I paid dearly to forget, held shut.

"Find your own thief," I say.

"I intend to." The pressure withdraws, unhurried, and the dark closes over the place where it was. "It is climbing toward us even now. Maps have a way of arriving at their destinations. And when it comes, little vault, you and I are going to have a very interesting conversation about what you paid to forget — and whether it was worth the price."

* * *

I lie in the full dark, in the crown that wears me, and I count what I have left.

Two coppers per hearth. Four thousand, one hundred and sixty-three crowns. Nine months over schedule. A limp. A falcon scar that may have been my mother's, or her sister's, or no one's.

And one name I cannot say, in a cellar I cannot reach, that the hunger wants back and cannot find — which means that somewhere, out past the wards, out past the starless sky, there is a person walking around with a piece of me in their head.

I do not know who they are. I do not know why they ran, or what I paid them to carry, or whether they are friend or thief or something that is both, the way most things are.

But they are climbing toward me. The crown said so, and the crown does not lie; it has no need of lies. It has ledgers instead.

*Hold on,* I say to the name I cannot remember, to the gloves I cannot see, to the thief in the dark between us. *Hold on, and whatever you do —*

*— do not give it back.*

* * *

The moon, when it turns above the citadel, turns like a key.
