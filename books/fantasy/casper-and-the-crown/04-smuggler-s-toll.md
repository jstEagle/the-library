## 4. Smuggler's Toll

Hollowmere smelled like wet wool, tallow, and the particular desperation of a town that had just realized the sky was not going to fix itself.

I came down off the pass with the refugees, which was a humbling experience for a woman who had once drawn those very passes and charged a duke's ransom for the privilege. The road into town was churned to soup by cart wheels and frightened feet, and I walked it in the middle of a river of people who kept looking up at the empty dark as though the stars might come back if enough eyes asked politely.

They wouldn't. I'd been watching them go out since before Thornfell. One by one, then in clusters, like candles snuffed by a draft no one could feel. And now nothing. Just a black ceiling, close and low, pressing down on the world like a lid on a pot.

The wards snapping had been worse. You don't hear a ward break so much as feel it — a wrongness in your back teeth, in the soles of your feet, in that old animal part of you that knows when the fence around the sheep has stopped being a fence. Every mapmaker worth her ink felt it that night. Half of us pretended we hadn't. The other half started drinking early.

I did both, in sequence.

Hollowmere's one street ran downhill toward a mere that wasn't hollow at all but black as spilled ink, reflecting a sky with nothing in it to reflect. Lamplit windows. Shutters going up early. A tavern called the Drowned Lantern doing brisk business out of pure human stubbornness.

And moored at the mere's edge, half-hidden under canvas and fishing nets: a barge. Flat-bottomed, shallow-drafted, built to slip through waterways that officially didn't connect to anything. I knew that hull. I knew the patched sailcloth and the lazy list to port and the way the name *Mercy of Coin* had been repainted over so many times it now read, if you squinted, *Mer y of Co*.

Brannic's boat. Which meant Brannic was in town, which meant my luck was either turning or setting a trap.

I went looking for him the way you approach any dangerous animal: slowly, from downwind, with something to offer.

* * *

He was in the back corner of the Drowned Lantern, eating stew with the focused hostility of a man who expected the stew to betray him. Brannic Oakhewn is built like something carved out of a cliffside by a sculptor who got bored halfway through, all mass and rough edges and one eyebrow permanently raised in suspicion of the universe. He saw me coming from thirty feet away, because of course he did, and he did not stop chewing.

"Vesper Vane." He said it the way other men say *plague*. "They told me you were dead."

"They tell everyone that. It's good for business."

"Whose?"

"Mine, mostly. Fewer debts get collected." I pulled out the chair across from him. He made no move to stop me and no move to welcome me, which was, for Brannic, practically a hug. "Is that mutton?"

"It's a mystery. Everything's a mystery now. Cook swears it's chicken." He pushed the bowl an inch toward the center of the table, which from Brannic was hospitality bordering on sentiment. "You're thinner. You look like a compass needle someone stepped on."

"You always did have a poet's soul."

"I have a smuggler's soul. Poets starve slower." He finally set down his spoon and looked at me properly, and I watched him do the arithmetic behind his eyes — the same arithmetic every man who'd ever worked with me did. *What does she want, what will it cost me, and can I get out of the room before she finishes asking?* "No," he said.

"I haven't said anything yet."

"You were going to say 'Brannic, I need passage.' And I was going to say no. Let's not waste each other's evening. Yours is clearly worthless anyway, but mine isn't."

"Where are you headed?"

"Nowhere."

"The Mercy of Coin doesn't sail nowhere."

"She does when nowhere's the only place with a bounty on her hull. Starless sky, Vane. Do you know what that's done to shipping? Nobody trusts a night run anymore. Nobody trusts a chart. Half my colleagues are beached, and the other half are praying, and I don't pray, so I'm sitting in a town full of refugees drinking watered ale and eating mystery meat." His voice dropped. "So whatever you're about to ask me, the answer's still no."

I leaned forward. "The Thornfell passes are open."

That landed. I watched it land. Brannic's whole career was built on knowing which doors were closed, and a closed door opening where no door should be is either a fortune or a funeral, and a smuggler's entire life is deciding quickly which.

"That's not possible," he said. "The wards—"

"Snapped. Two nights ago. All of them, along the whole range, like someone cut the strings." I turned my cup slowly on the table. "Someone cut the strings, Brannic. Deliberately. And everything east of Thornfell is now unguarded, unmapped, and unsupervised. There are routes through those mountains that appear on exactly three charts in the world. I drew two of them."

"And the third?"

"Rewrote itself last night while I watched. But that's a longer story and involves more screaming than either of us needs after this stew."

He sat back. Rubbed his jaw. Outside, somewhere down the street, a child was crying and a mother was telling her the stars were just resting, which was either a lie or a prophecy, depending on how the month went.

"Why me?" Brannic said finally. "There are other boats."

"Not going where I'm going."

"Which is where?"

"Citadel of the Moon."

He laughed. It was a short, ugly sound. "Nobody goes there. That's not a place, that's a warning with walls."

"Aldric's been sleeping up there for sixty years, and two nights ago his crown ate the sky. I intend to climb up and have words with it."

"With a *crown*."

"With a crown."

Brannic looked at me for a long moment, and then he did something worse than refusing: he got thoughtful. Thoughtful Brannic was expensive Brannic. "Here's my problem, Vane. It's not that you're mad. Mad I can price. It's that you owe *me*."

"I don't owe you."

"The Greywater job. Three years back. Whose name was on the manifest when the customs cutters boarded?"

"An alias."

"*My* alias. My face. My neck, nearly, until you vanished into the marshes with the good maps and left me explaining myself to a magistrate with a sense of humor." He jabbed a scarred finger at me. "You owe me a debt, and people who owe me debts only show up when they want to make the debt *bigger*."

"That's not fair. Sometimes I show up to make it smaller."

"When?"

"...This might be one of the bigger ones."

He sighed, long and theatrical, the sigh of a man negotiating with himself and losing. Then he held out one enormous hand. "Passage to the foothills below the citadel. Not the citadel. I am not dying on a mountain so you can argue with jewelry. Foothills. After that you walk, and I never see you again, and we call the Greywater debt settled."

"One condition."

"There it is."

"I need you to keep moving at night. The roads will be watched — refugee traffic, bandits, things worse than bandits. Your whole career is sailing dark waters nobody else will touch. This is that, but vertical."

"Night runs past Thornfell." He chewed on it. "With no stars to steer by."

"You'll have me."

"That's supposed to comfort me?" But his mouth twitched, almost against its will. "Fine. Fine. We leave at second bell, before the toll-wardens wake up. And Vane—" He pointed that finger again. "If I find out this is one of your old games, some scheme you buried so deep even you've forgotten it—"

"I don't play games anymore, Brannic."

"No. You look like a woman who's stopped playing and started *losing*." He waved for another bowl of the mystery stew. "Eat. You'll need your strength for all the walking I'm not doing for you."

I ate. It was, definitively, mutton. Some mysteries resolve themselves, given time.

* * *

We loaded the barge by lantern-light, which felt obscene and necessary at once. A town without stars learns to guard its small flames like dragons hoard gold; every lamp in Hollowmere burned behind shutters or smoked-glass, hoarding light the sky had abandoned.

It was Brannic's deckhand, a wiry girl called Nix with river mud to her elbows, who noticed the frost first.

"Bosun," she said — she called everyone bosun, including me, including, once, a heron — "bosun, look at the rail."

Frost had crept up the *Mercy of Coin*'s gunwale in a perfect ring. Not scattered crystals, not wind-feathered ice — a clean circle, etched around the capstan like a signature. And inside the ring, the wood was dry.

"Frost in rings," I said, before I could stop myself.

"You know it?" Brannic crouched by the rail, breath fogging.

"I've seen it once. In an old survey. Frost that grows in circles instead of feathers — they called it turnfrost. The old ward-keepers marked it on their calendars." I touched the ring. Cold bit my fingertip, cold with a strange *intent* to it, cold that felt less like weather and more like a clock. "It means the moon's begun counting."

"Counting what?"

"Turns." I looked up at the moon, hanging pale and alone in the starless black, and for the first time it occurred to me that it looked less like a companion in the darkness and more like the last coin left on a table. "Thirteen of them, if the survey's right. And then something happens that the surveyor couldn't bring himself to write down. He just left the margin blank and drew a little picture of a man kneeling."

"Cheerful fellow."

"Cartographers usually are. It's all the solitude."

Nix had gone quiet, hugging her arms. "Bosun," she said to me, "the ring's getting bigger."

She was right. Slowly — so slowly you'd swear you weren't seeing it — the frost circle was widening around the capstan, patient as a tide. I thought of the ward-keeper in the pass, coughing blood into the snow, pressing his broken compass into my hands. *It eats what it cannot hold,* he'd said. *But mind you, cartographer — it holds what it eats. Forever.*

At the time I'd assumed he meant the king.

Standing on that deck, watching winter draw its careful little circle around us, I began to suspect the inventory was longer.

"Second bell," Brannic said quietly. "Get below, both of you. Whatever's counting—" He glanced up at the naked sky, and for one unguarded moment the suspicion fell off his face and something older showed underneath, something that looked a lot like a boy on a boat, looking up for stars that used to be there. "Whatever's counting, I'd rather it counted us moving."

* * *

We cast off at second bell, silent as thieves, which was redundant since we were thieves. Hollowmere slid away behind us, lamplit and shuttered, and the black mere opened ahead like a page no one had written on yet.

I stood at the stern and took out the broken compass. Its needle swung, twitched, and settled — pointing not north, not toward the citadel, but off to the northwest, toward a stretch of hills I had no reason to visit and every instinct to avoid.

"Holding course," Brannic muttered beside me, checking the sweep of the pole. "Vane. What's that thing pointing at?"

I watched the needle tremble, eager as a hound at a scent, and felt the first true cold of the season slide down my collar, independent of any frost.

"Ask me again tomorrow," I said. "I'm hoping the answer changes."

The needle held steady. Somewhere behind my ribs, in a place I didn't have a chart for, something answered it — a pull, faint and familiar, like a hand closing around a memory I didn't know I'd lost.

The moon rose higher over the water, white and watchful, beginning to count.
