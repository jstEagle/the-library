## 6. The Crown's Servants

I've been robbed by better men than these, and I want that on the record.

It was the frost that woke me — rings of it, spreading across the *Kestrel*'s deck like someone had poured a circle of white ink and let it bleed outward. Third ring since we'd taken on Vesper and their impossible cargo of maps. The moon sat low over Hollowmere's rooftops with a thin halo around it, and I didn't need Old Nan's weather-bones to tell me the halo was getting brighter each night. Things were counting down. I just didn't know to what, and I've learned the hard way that when you don't know what's counting down, you check your cargo holds and your escape routes in that order.

So there I was, at an hour no honest smuggler is awake, doing inventory of my own ship because my own crew sleeps like drugged bears, when the bells started.

Not Hollowmere's bells. Those are fat, lazy things that ring for market days and fires and the occasional wedding nobody attends twice. These bells were thin. Wrong. They came from the ridge road, high and clear as glass cracking, and under them I could hear hooves. A lot of hooves, moving in formation, which told me everything I needed to know before I saw a single rider. Bandits don't ride in formation. Pilgrims don't ride at night in starless armor.

And they were wearing starless armor. That was the part that stopped me flat on the quarterdeck with my hand halfway to the signal lamp. Every soldier in that column down the ridge road wore plate so black it didn't shine. Not dull black — *hungry* black. Torchlight went into it and didn't come back out. I've seen obsidian, I've seen lacquered warplate from the southern principalities, I've seen a man paint himself with grave-soot to frighten toll collectors. This was none of those. This was armor that had been dipped in the same dark that had eaten every star in the sky three weeks ago, and I stood there with my breath smoking in the cold and understood, in the way you understand things you'll spend years pretending you always knew, that the sky going out and this column coming down my road were the same event wearing two coats.

"Hollowmere," I said to nobody, because saying it made it real. "They're going for Hollowmere."

Hollowmere has maybe four hundred souls, a toll bridge, a shrine nobody important prays at, and the best eel pies in three valleys. It also has me, currently anchored midstream with a hold full of untaxed brandy and one passenger who paid in a map instead of coin and then had the nerve to redraw the map while I watched.

I did the arithmetic any sensible man does. Four hundred strangers against one Brannic. Even weighted for charm, that math doesn't come out in Hollowmere's favor. My plan, such as it was, formed itself with admirable speed: wake Vesper, cut the mooring lines quiet as prayer, drift downstream on the current with the lanterns dead until the bend hid us, and let the crown's fancy riders sort out the eel pie situation on their own.

That was the plan. I want that on the record too.

* * *

Vesper wasn't in their bunk.

Their bunk was there, blankets thrown back, boots gone, and on the little table bolted to the bulkhead lay the compass — the broken one, the dead ward-keeper's legacy that Vesper carried everywhere and consulted more often than most men consult their consciences. Its needle was spinning. Slowly, steadily, around and around, like a dog chasing its tail in its sleep.

I picked it up. Don't ask me why; I'm a smuggler, we touch things that aren't ours professionally. The case was warm. That was the wrong part. Brass sitting in an unheated cabin on a frost-ringed night should have been cold enough to stick to skin, and this thing was warm as a held hand, and the needle spun and spun and then — I swear this on my mother's good name, which she'd dispute — it stopped. Pointed. Straight through the cabin wall, upstream, toward the town. Toward whatever Vesper had gone into town to do at the exact hour an armored column crested the ridge.

"Of course," I said. "Why would anything be simple."

The bells got louder. Down at the landing stage, the first torches were coming through the gate arch, and I could see now that the riders weren't alone. They moved through Hollowmere's streets in pairs, unhurried, methodical, the way tax assessors move, or plague doctors. And behind each pair walked people. Hollowmere folk, barefoot in the frost, hands bound not with rope but with strips of pale cloth that glowed faintly, the way snow glows after sunset. None of them were screaming. That was the detail that put ice in my stomach alongside everything else. When soldiers take a town, there's screaming, there's running, there's the whole ugly music of it. This was silent. The townsfolk walked where they were pointed, faces empty as fresh paper, and the starless soldiers collected them like a man collecting letters he already knew the contents of.

Unmapped. The word arrived in my head from nowhere, and I trusted it about as far as I trust anything that arrives from nowhere, but it fit. I'd heard talk in the last week, tavern murmur, refugee gossip from up-valley: the crown's hunters weren't taking everyone. They were taking the ones who couldn't be predicted. The ones whose lives didn't run along grooves anymore. Everyone else got left standing in their doorways, watching, and here's the thing that kept me up after — the ones left behind didn't look relieved. They looked *faded*. Like the difference between a map and the blank margin around it.

I am, by trade and temperament, an unmapped man if ever there was one. No fixed address, no registered cargo, a name I've worn like a coat over at least two other names. By any reasonable reading, I should have been first through the gate.

Instead I stood on my own deck, holding a warm compass, and thought about Vesper.

* * *

Here's what I'll admit, since we're being honest in the privacy of my own skull: I didn't go into town for loyalty. Loyalty is expensive and I price everything. I went in for self-interest, which is the same thing wearing cheaper clothes. Vesser owed passage on my ship, and more than that, Vesper owed me — two years back, before the stars went out, before any of this, I ran a cargo through Thornfell that I will describe only as delicate, and the ward-wardens caught me, and it was a certain cartographer's forged survey chart, beautiful enough to hang in a duke's hall, that got me out of a cell and back onto open water. I don't forget debts. Debts are the only currency that never devalues. If the crown's collectors took Vesper off my deck, I'd never collect, and worse, I'd spend the rest of my life knowing I'd been anchored forty yards away while it happened, checking my escape routes like a man with sense.

A man with sense stays on the boat.

I rowed in anyway.

* * *

Hollowmere's main street runs from the gate to the shrine square, cobbles under frost, and I came up it hugging the eave-shadow with my hood up and my gait arranged into the particular slouch that says *drunk, harmless, not worth the paperwork*. The pairs of hunters worked the doors. Up close, the armor was worse — up close I could see it wasn't reflecting anything at all, not even firelight, like each breastplate was a little hole cut out of the world. One of them turned its head as I passed, and where a face should have been behind the raised visor there was nothing but a smooth dark curve, and I kept walking, drunk and harmless, heart going like a drum corps.

At the well in the shrine square, they'd set up a table. An actual table, folding legs, with a ledger on it and a clerk — a living clerk, thank all forgotten gods, a grey man with ink-stained fingers and spectacles frosted over, reading names aloud from a list while hunters brought their catch forward and the glowing bindings flared.

"Marta Ellery." A baker's widow stepped forward, dazed, gentle. "Confirmed. Unmapped as of the ninth turn. Take her."

"Tomas Bell. Confirmed."

"Denna Marsh. Confirmed—"

The list went on. I pressed into the shadow of the shrine wall, close enough to hear, far enough to run, and told myself I was memorizing names for ransom purposes later, which was a lie so comfortable I almost believed it.

Then the grey man turned the page, and his spectacles caught the torchlight, and he read:

"Brannic Corr."

My own name, in a bored civil-service voice, in a crown ledger, in a town I'd chosen precisely because nobody in it knew my name.

"No confirmation," said one of the hunters, its hollow helm tilting toward the clerk. "Subject not located within the collection perimeter."

"Noted," the clerk said, and licked his thumb, and turned the page, and read the next line, and the world went very narrow and very quiet, the way it does in the half-second before a blade comes down.

"*Vesper Vane.* Cartographer. Priority designation. Confirm capture at any cost. All other collections secondary."

All other collections secondary. Marta Ellery and Tomas Bell and Denna Marsh and the four hundred souls of Hollowmere — *secondary*. There was a priority target in this town tonight, and the crown wanted it at any cost, and the priority target was sleeping — not sleeping, was supposed to be sleeping — aboard my ship, except it wasn't aboard my ship, it was somewhere in this frozen town following a broken compass toward its own past, and every hunter on this street would burn Hollowmere to its foundations to reach it.

I looked down at my hands. Steady, surprisingly. I looked at the compass-shaped absence in my pocket where I'd left it on the boat, because apparently even my instincts had been drinking.

And I did the only piece of honest accounting available to me: Vesper's debt, weighed against the smartest decision of my career, and found — to my lasting professional disgust — that the debt outweighed it. Not because Vesper had earned it. Because two years ago a forged chart had walked me out of a cell, and a man who forgets that kind of thing starts forgetting other kinds, and then one morning he's the kind of man who reads a name off a list without blinking.

I'd seen that morning coming for other men. I wasn't going to stand in line for it.

* * *

Finding Vesper took less skill than I'd have liked credit for. The compass was on my boat; Vesper's habit was to follow the compass; therefore Vesper would come back to the boat, eventually, by some route, and the smart play was to get there first and be leaning on the rail looking insufferably calm when they did. Which is exactly how it played out, near enough — I slipped back down the water-stairs as the collection column began moving its silent cargo toward the ridge, and I was aboard, casting off the stern line, when a shape rose over the gunwale dripping river and swore at me in Vesper's level, exhausted voice.

"You're leaving."

"I'm evacuating strategically," I said. "There's a difference, and it's the difference between men who die old and men who die interesting. Get below. Hunters in the streets, and before you ask — they're not here for the town."

"They're taking the town."

"They're *collecting* the town. There's a list. Names, designations. Yours is on it." I watched that land. Vesper went still in the particular way of someone whose whole body has become a listening instrument. "Not on it the way mine's on it. Mine's a footnote. Yours is the headline. *Priority designation, confirm capture at any cost, all other collections secondary.* Their words, not mine. Whatever you're carrying in that head of yours besides those cursed maps, the crown wants it badly enough to empty a town to get it."

The frost rings on the deck had grown another band while we talked. Fourth turn of the moon, if Old Sable's hermit arithmetic meant anything, and I was starting to suspect it meant everything.

"So," I said, and here's where I made the choice I'll be defending to my own reflection for years, "we have two options. One: I put you ashore at Grayford, wish you luck, and you go climb toward that citadel alone with your spinning needle and your missing past, and I go back to being a man with sense. Two—"

"Two," Vesper said.

"I hadn't finished describing it."

"You were going to say you'd take me upriver yourself. Past the checkpoints. You hate it. You're going to charge me double."

"Triple," I said. "And I want it noted that option one was on the table."

"It's noted." Vesper looked back once at Hollowmere — at the lit windows, the emptying square, the thin bells still ringing their wrong note off the hills — and something crossed their face that I recognized from men who've read their own death warrants and filed them anyway. "Brannic. Why?"

Because you pulled me out of a cell with a pen stroke. Because nobody deserves to be a headline. Because the alternative was becoming the kind of man who turns pages.

"Debts," I said. "You wouldn't understand, you're a cartographer."

But that's not the whole of why I stayed at the tiller while the *Kestrel* slid upstream into the dark, past the checkpoint fires, toward the citadel and the thirteenth turn of the moon. The rest of why is this: when I read that list by torchlight, under Vesper's name, in small clerical script beneath the priority designation, there was a second line. A note. Three words that have been sitting in my chest like swallowed ice ever since, and that I haven't yet told Vesper, because some cargo is too dangerous to declare at the border.

The note read: *Return the fugitive.*

Which means whoever wrote that list knows Vesper ran.

Which means somebody, somewhere ahead of us, has been expecting this journey longer than we have.
