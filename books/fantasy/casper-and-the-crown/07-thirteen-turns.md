## 7. Thirteen Turns

The hermit's hut sat where no hut should have been able to sit — a shelf of rock jutting out from the mountain's shoulder like a misplaced bookmark, reachable only by a goat track that the goats themselves seemed embarrassed by. Brannic refused to climb the last hundred yards on principle, so I went alone, hands and boots finding holds in the dark, the broken compass in my pocket tugging faintly upward, as if it approved of the elevation.

I should say something about the dark, since it had become the only weather we had. Four days without stars. The moon still hung up there, but wrong — thinner each night than any almanac could account for, as if something were drinking it too. And the frost had started coming in rings. I'd noticed it first at Hollowmere, neat concentric circles on the shutters, on standing water, on the faces of the dead they couldn't bury fast enough. Rings, like ripples. Like something dropped into the world from a great height.

The door of the hut was made of map-leather stretched over ribs of some animal I chose not to identify. Before I could knock, a voice said: "You're late."

"I didn't know I was expected."

"Nobody ever does. That's how I know." The door swung inward. "Come in before the rings find you. They've reached the treeline. You walked through at least six of them getting here, and you're luckier than you deserve."

* * *

Inside was every cartographer's fever dream and every hoarder's cautionary tale. Charts hung from the ceiling on wires, layered over one another so the room felt like walking through a forest of paper. Desks crowded the walls, buried under brass instruments, dividers, calipers, ink pots sealed with wax older than me. A stove burned something that smelled of pine and secrets.

And in the middle of it all, small and sharp-eyed and wrapped in three coats despite the stove, stood Old Sable.

I knew the name. Every working mapmaker knew the name, though most assumed Sable was dead, or legendary, or both. The *Thornfell Survey*. The *Coastal Corrections of the Second Age*. Half my craft was built on foundations this person had laid, and here they were, five feet nothing, squinting at me like I'd tracked mud across their floor.

Which, to be fair, I had.

"You're Vesper Vane," Sable said. Not a question.

"The compass told you?"

"The compass doesn't talk. It points. At you, mostly, these last few years — I'd wondered why. Now here you are, climbing toward the citadel with a dead ward-keeper's needle in your pocket and frost rings on your boots." Sable poured something amber into two cups and pushed one at me. "Drink. It tastes terrible. That's how you know it's medicine."

I drank. It tasted terrible.

"Now," Sable said, settling onto a stool that groaned under the dignity rather than the weight, "ask me what you climbed all this way to ask. And don't waste my time asking whether the crown is real. I watched them forge it. That's a conversation for after the drink."

My mouth went dry despite the whiskey. "You watched—"

"Forty-one years ago. I was younger than you. Better with an instrument, worse with people, same as now." Sable's eyes had gone somewhere far away, somewhere with better lighting. "Ask."

"What is it doing? To the king. To the sky."

Sable looked at me for a long moment. Then they got up, crossed to the tallest stack of charts, and pulled one free with the care of someone lifting a sleeping child.

It was a map of the realm as it had been drawn forty years ago — but not the political version, not the pretty one with borders in gold leaf. This was the deep survey. Ley-lines. Ward-anchors. And at the center, where the citadel stood, an annotation in faded brown ink that I initially took for a stain until I saw it was writing, tiny and dense, circling the mark like a halo:

*Holds. Do not feed.*

"Hold what?" I asked.

"Everything," said Sable. "Sit down. This takes the whole night, and you're going to want to be sitting when we get to the part about you."

I did not like the sound of that. But I sat.

* * *

"A crown is not a hat," Sable began, pacing between the hanging charts like a lecturer who had waited four decades for a classroom. "That's the first thing everyone gets wrong. A king wears a crown; fine, ceremonial, gold and jewels and a good excuse for a parade. But *the* Crown — capital letter, the one Aldric wears — is a vessel. It was forged to hold memory."

"Whose memory?"

"*Everyone's.*" Sable let that land. "Think, Vane. What happens to a kingdom's memories? Wars get rewritten by whoever wins. Famines get forgotten by whoever recovered. Whole generations decide their grandparents' stories are embarrassing and let them rot. A realm forgets itself the way a man forgets his own face — slowly, comfortably, fatally. So the founders commissioned a vessel. One object, warded past warded, that would hold the true record. Every road as it truly ran. Every oath as it was truly sworn. Every name."

"And the king wears it because...?"

"Because a vessel that size needs an anchor. Living, willing, rooted in the land it records. Aldric volunteered. He was young. He thought he understood what he was offering." Sable stopped pacing. "For thirty years it worked beautifully. The realm hummed. Wards held. The stars—" they gestured upward, at the ceiling, at everything above the ceiling, "—the stars were never just lights, boy. They are the sky's own memory. Every one of them a fixed point, a landmark the world navigates by. And the Crown, anchored to Aldric, drew on starlight the way a mill draws on a river. Gently. In balance."

I was beginning to see the shape of it, and I didn't like the shape at all. "And then?"

"And then the wards fell."

"The Thornfell wards. Three nights ago."

"Before that." Sable's voice had gone flat, the way voices go when someone has rehearsed bad news too many times. "The outermost ring failed months ago — quietly, the way old things fail. The Crown felt itself start to leak. Memory bleeding out through the cracks. And here is the thing about a vessel, Vane, that the founders never planned for: a vessel that has held everything for thirty years cannot bear to hold nothing. It wasn't built to endure emptiness. It was built to be full."

The stove ticked. Somewhere outside, something moved along the treeline, and Sable glanced at the shuttered window with an expression I'd learned to read on smugglers' faces: *later. We'll deal with that later.*

"So it started feeding," I said.

"It started *drinking.* Starlight first — closest thing to its proper diet, easy to take, and who would miss a few stars? Then the moon's light, which is why your almanacs have gone mad. But starlight is thin fare. It kept the vessel alive without keeping it *full.* And there, right below it, asleep and trusting, was the greatest concentration of memory in the realm." Sable met my eyes. "A living king. Thirty years of anchoring. Every day of his reign stored behind his own eyes."

"He's eating him."

"The Crown is consuming Aldric's memories to keep itself full. Slowly, at first — a face here, a childhood summer there. That's why he sleeps. There's less and less of him left to be awake." Sable picked up the old chart again, tapped the haloed mark. "But here is the arithmetic you climbed a mountain for. A king is finite. The Crown's hunger is not. When Aldric runs out — when the vessel has drunk the last of him — it will not simply stop and die. Vessels fear emptiness more than anything in this world. It will turn outward. To the realm."

"How long?"

Sable smiled, and it was the worst smile I have ever seen on another human being, patient and pitying and absolutely certain. "You've noticed the moon."

"Frost rings. Thinner each night."

"The moon turns thirteen times before the devouring completes. We are at the fourth. Nine remain. After the thirteenth turn, Aldric is finished, and the Crown goes looking for its next meal among everyone whose memories it hasn't already catalogued." Sable set down the cup. "Which brings us to the rule, Vane. The one rule. Write it on the inside of your skull."

I waited.

"*A crown binds by holding.* That is its nature and its mechanism. It binds a king by holding his memory. It binds a realm by holding its record. Everything it has power over, it holds. Everything it holds, it owns." Sable's finger came up, sharp as a divider point. "So whatever plan is forming behind those tired cartographer's eyes of yours — and I can see one forming, it's what you people do instead of sleeping — understand that you do not beat it with force. Blades pass through it. Fire has no grip on it. You unbind a thing that binds by holding by giving it something it must hold forever."

"That's not a plan, that's a riddle."

"All the best ones are." Sable turned back to the charts, and I thought the audience was over, and I was already halfway to relief when they said, quietly, without turning around:

"You asked why the compass points at you. Sit back down. We haven't gotten to the part about you yet."

* * *

I sat back down. Brannic would tell you I'm not a brave man, and Brannic would be right, but there is a specific kind of courage required to stay seated when a legend starts talking about you in a tone usually reserved for terminal diagnoses.

"The Crown holds the realm's true record," Sable said. "Every road. Every route. Every path by which anything moves anywhere." They let me arrive at it myself, which was cruel of them, and effective, which was worse.

"...Including the routes it feeds by," I said slowly. "The paths starlight takes. The ways in and out of the citadel."

"Now you're mapping."

"And someone would have had to draw those routes. Someone with access. Someone trusted." My voice sounded strange to me, distant, like hearing it through water. "Sable. Who was the Cartographer Royal?"

Silence, except for the stove, except for my own heartbeat, which had developed opinions.

"I don't know," Sable said. "The records of that office are held in the Crown itself, which means they're held nowhere anymore. But I'll tell you what I know. Forty years ago, at the forging, there was a young surveyor present. Gifted beyond sense. The founders' favorite. And when the outermost ward-ring began to fail — months ago, quietly, the way old things fail — that surveyor was the first person in the realm positioned to notice." Sable finally turned around. "And the first person in the realm to disappear."

The compass in my pocket had gone warm. Not pointing. Just warm, like a hand resting there, like something patient waiting to be remembered.

"I don't remember any of that," I said. Which was true. What I remembered was an apprenticeship in Lowmarsh, a modest career, a modest life, maps of river fords and smuggling cuts. Ordinary. Mine.

"No," Sable agreed softly. "You wouldn't. The Crown eats what it cannot hold, and it holds what it will not lose — and it would not lose its cartographer. If that surveyor fled, the Crown would have reached into them and taken the knowing of it. Left the skill and stripped the history. You'd still be able to draw a pass at midnight, Vane. You just wouldn't know why you can."

I thought about the smuggler's chart I'd been working on the night the stars went out. How the routes had come to my pen faster than thought. How the dying ward-keeper had looked at me — not at Brannic, not at the refugees, at *me* — before pressing that broken compass into my hands. How the ink had begun rewriting itself.

"Why give me this?" I asked, holding up the compass with a hand that mostly didn't shake. "If I was theirs. If I ran. Why arm the deserter?"

"Because," said Old Sable, and here they smiled again, and this time there was something underneath the smile that might have been hope, or might have been grief, and I genuinely could not tell which frightened me more, "a vessel that holds everything is also a vessel that can be *filled.* You can't fight it, boy. But maybe — maybe — you can feed it. Give it a memory so vast, so binding, that it must hold that forever and let go of the king. The founders built it to hold. Hold is all it knows."

"And what memory could possibly—"

"Not what." Sable looked at the compass in my hand, and then at me, and the temperature of the room seemed to drop several degrees. "*Who.*"

Outside, the frost rings crept audibly up the mountainside, a sound like the world being traced in outline. Nine turns of the moon remained. And in my pocket, the broken compass stirred against my palm like something waking, pointing — for the first time since Hollowmere — not at me, but past me, through the wall, toward the ruined observatory on the western ridge.

Toward whatever I had chosen to forget.

"We leave at first light," I heard myself say. "Brannic will hate this."

"Oh," said Old Sable, pouring a third cup of the terrible medicine, "he already does. He's been listening at the door for ten minutes. Come in, smuggler, and bring my ladder back where you found it."
