## 9. Cells Without Windows

They took my boots first.

I want you to understand that this was the insult that broke me. Not the hood, not the manacles, not the long blind ride in a cart that smelled of wet wool and fear — the boots. A man can survive losing his freedom, his coin, even his dignity, if he keeps his boots. Take a smuggler's boots and you've taken his trade, his roads, his whole argument with the world. I sat in the dark of that cell and mourned my boots like other men mourn their mothers.

The cell had no windows. That's not a complaint; it's an observation about the architecture of despair. Whoever built this place understood that windows are hope with glass in them. There was a slot in the door for food, a bucket for everything else, and walls of grey stone fitted so tightly that not even a draft could find its way through to keep me company. Somewhere beyond those walls, Vesper Vane was running through the mountains with my barge, my crew, and half my cargo of self-respect.

I'd told myself I was covering their escape when I turned and walked back into the hunters' line. I'd said it out loud, even: *Go on, then, you map-mad fool. I'll hold them off.* And for perhaps four seconds I had held them off, which is three seconds longer than most men manage against six soldiers in armor with no stars on it.

Then someone hit me with something heavy and polite, and here I was.

* * *

The first thing I learned about the crown's dungeon was that it was quiet in a way dungeons shouldn't be. Prisons are supposed to have noise in them — weeping, cursing, the mad singing of men who've given up on dignity. This place had none. Just breathing. Dozens of people breathing, slow and even, like a congregation asleep in church.

The second thing I learned came three days in, when they moved me to a larger cell because — as the jailer put it, without a trace of apology — "you're crowding the ones who still have names."

That was how I met Tam.

He was sitting cross-legged in the corner of the shared cell, a thin man with a farmer's hands and the eyes of a man reading a book that wasn't there. When I was shoved through the door he looked up at me with genuine, uncomplicated friendliness.

"Hullo," he said. "Are you new? I'm—" He stopped. His mouth stayed open for a moment, working at nothing, like a fish testing the air. "I'm," he tried again. Then, brighter, as if the failure hadn't happened: "Hullo."

"That's all right," I said, because what else do you say. "Names are overrated. I'm Brannic, and I already regret mine."

"Brannic." He tasted it. "That's a good name. Strong. I wish I could remember if mine was strong." He patted the floor beside him, inviting me to sit, host of a kingdom of grey stone. "They take them at night, you know. The names. The jailer comes around with his book and reads them out, and then they're gone till morning."

"Reads them out where?"

"Here." Tam tapped his own temple, then, after consideration, mine. "He remembers us for us. That's his job. Someone has to, or else—" He frowned, groping after the end of that sentence the way a man gropes after a stair in the dark. "Or else," he concluded, satisfied.

I looked around the cell properly then. There were eight others besides Tam. Not one of them looked frightened. That was the thing that raised the hair on my arms, more than the windowless stone or the silent breathing. They were *content*. A woman by the far wall was humming to herself, some tune she'd forgotten the name of but not the shape. Two men were playing a game with pebbles whose rules changed every time one of them forgot them, which was often, and both found this endlessly delightful.

"They're happy," I said.

"Oh, aye." Tam said it the way you'd say *the harvest came in well*. "Happy as lambs. The jailer says that's how he knows it's a mercy. You can't grieve what you can't hold."

"And do you believe him?"

Tam considered this with the seriousness of a scholar. "I don't know what I believe," he said. "I only know what he tells me. It's rather convenient, actually. You should try it."

* * *

The jailer came at what I judged to be evening, though in a place without windows evening is just a rumor. He was a big man gone soft around the middle, with kind eyes and a keyring that sang when he walked, and under his arm he carried a ledger bound in grey leather — the same grey, I noticed, as the walls, as the stones, as everything in this place the crown had gotten its fingers into.

He went down the cell reading names aloud, softly, like a father checking on sleeping children. "Maren Ostwick. Joss Fen. Aldous Reed." Each time he spoke a name, one of the prisoners would surface from wherever they drifted — blink, smile vaguely, murmur *that's me* or sometimes just *oh* — and then sink back down like a stone into a pond. It took him perhaps two minutes to remember eight human beings, and he did it with the tenderness of a man saying prayers.

Then he got to Tam and paused.

"You're new," he said to me. It wasn't a question. "Brannic. Smuggler. Taken at Hollowmere crossing, four days past." He closed the ledger partway. "You kept your name. Interesting."

"I'm attached to it."

"Mm." He studied me with those kind eyes, and I realized they weren't kind at all, or rather they were, in the way a man's eyes are kind to the animal he's leading to slaughter because it isn't the animal's fault. "Most aren't. Most fight it, at first. Holding a grudge takes memory, though. Give it three weeks."

"What did they do to them?" I nodded at Tam, who had fallen into a light doze, smiling at nothing. "He's a farmer. Look at his hands. What's a farmer done to earn this?"

"Nothing." The jailer said it flatly, and that flatness was the first honest thing I'd heard since the boots. "That's what no one understands. It isn't punishment. The crown collects the unmapped — people who don't fit its patterns, who move in ways it can't predict. Smugglers. Wanderers. Your cartographer friend, most of all." Something flickered across his face when he said *cartographer*, quick as a fish under ice. "It holds them somewhere safe until it decides what they're for."

"And while it decides, they forget?"

"While it decides, *it* remembers for them." He lifted the ledger slightly. "Every name. Every mother's face. Every debt and love and grudge. It's all in here, safe, exactly as they left it. Nothing is lost, Brannic. Nothing at all. It's only... elsewhere."

"That's a fine distinction."

"It's the only distinction there is." For a moment the softness dropped away and I saw what was underneath: a very tired man standing in a very quiet building, doing arithmetic with souls. Then it settled back into place, smooth as a mask. "Your supper's coming. Eat it. The ones who eat adjust faster."

He turned to go, and I did the stupid brave thing, because apparently I'm constitutionally incapable of the smart cowardly one.

"The woman who was taken with me," I said. "Vesper Vane. Cartographer. Is she alive?"

The jailer stopped. He didn't turn around right away. When he did, his face had gone careful, the way a river goes still before it drops over a ledge.

"Why do you ask?"

"Because I pulled her out of a burning customs house in Marrowgate seven years ago, and she repaid me by being the most infuriating passenger on my barge, and because if your master decides what she's 'for,' I'd like to know whether to grieve now or later. I hate scheduling grief twice."

For a long moment he just looked at me. Then he glanced at Tam, dozing, and at the humming woman, and something in his jaw worked like he was chewing on a sentence he couldn't swallow.

"She's alive," he said quietly. "She's in the deep cells. The crown wants her whole. Unforgotten." He said the word like it cost him. "Which is more than I can say for anyone else down here."

And he left, and his keys sang, and I sat down next to Tam and stared at the wall where a window should have been.

* * *

It took me five more days to work up the nerve, and I'll be honest about why: not fear for myself. Fear of arithmetic.

Here's what I'd worked out in the dark, counting breaths. The jailer remembered everyone. Which meant the jailer could be *asked*. And a man who carries other people's memories like a packhorse carries salt is a man with a terrible thirst to set the pack down — I'd seen it in his face, that flinch when he said *unforgotten*. But anything he gave me, he gave at risk of whatever passes for his neck in an organization run by a hungry piece of headwear. So the price would be steep, and the currency would be information, and the question was what I knew that was worth a life.

The answer, when I finally laid it out honest, was: quite a lot, actually. Seven years of Vesper muttering over charts in my cabin. Routes they'd traced and burned. The way they talked in their sleep about white gloves and an oath and a tower full of instruments. Things a smuggler files away without meaning to, because a smuggler knows that someday everything is worth something to somebody.

I just hadn't decided yet whether *somebody* included me.

On the sixth day the jailer came alone, out of schedule, and stood at the bars looking at me with his tired eyes, and saved me the trouble of opening negotiations.

"You asked about the cartographer," he said.

"I ask about lots of things. Weather. Boots. The philosophical nature of porridge."

"The crown has begun asking questions about you, Brannic. Why a smuggler would abandon his cargo and walk into a hunter's line. Why he keeps asking about a prisoner in the deep cells." He wrapped his hands around the bars, close enough that I could see the ink stains on his fingers — years of ledger-work, remembering strangers. "I can't answer those questions for you. But I can tell you that men who interest the crown stop being prisoners and start being materials."

"Charming. And if I were a man who wanted to avoid becoming material?"

"Then he'd need to give the crown something it wants more." He lowered his voice, and now I heard it plain, underneath the weariness: shame. Deep, old, well-kept shame, like water under a floor. "It wants to know where Vesper Vane is going. The mountain road. The citadel route. Its hunters lost them at the treeline, and its maps —" he smiled, and it was a terrible smile — "its maps have stopped making sense. It needs eyes that know the old ways through Thornfell."

"So you want me to sell her the pass routes."

"I want you to buy her life," he said. "Let's be precise about what we're doing, you and I. We're neither of us men who can afford pretty words."

Behind me, Tam stirred in his sleep and murmured something that might have been a name, or the ghost of one.

I thought about Vesper on the deck of my barge, arguing with me about tide tables at three in the morning, insufferable, certain, alive. I thought about the hunters' list with their name written on it, and about the jailer's ledger, and about the difference between a memory held and a memory kept — the only distinction there is, he'd said, and I was beginning to hate how much sense he made.

If I gave up the routes, the crown's hunters would be waiting at every turn between here and the citadel. If I didn't, Vesper died in a deep cell, whole and unforgotten, which struck me as the cruelest possible way to preserve a person.

Unless.

Unless the routes I sold were true enough to satisfy and false enough to mislead. Unless a smuggler's knowledge of the Thornfell passes — every deadfall, every washed-out switchback, every gorge that floods with the spring melt — could be bent just so, the way you bend a lie around a spine of truth until it walks like honesty.

The jailer watched me think. He was good at watching, this man. He'd had practice, holding eight lives in a ledger while the bodies sat empty in front of him.

"Well?" he said.

"There's a route through the Grey Stair," I said slowly, "that doesn't appear on any crown survey. Old smuggler's road. I'll draw it. Every mile. In exchange, the cartographer goes on the transfer list — out of the deep cells, into general holding. Where a man with keys might, conceivably, misplace a prisoner."

His eyes narrowed. "That's not the bargain I offered."

"No," I agreed. "But it's the bargain I'm selling. You want the Grey Stair. I want Vesper somewhere your keys can reach. And you—" I leaned toward the bars, and I made sure my voice was gentle, because cruelty would have closed him like a fist, "—you want, just once, to open this ledger and lose something on purpose. Don't tell me I'm wrong. I can smell it on you. You remember them all so they don't have to, and nobody remembers *you*, and every night you read out those names like prayers to gods who never answer."

The silence stretched so long I heard Tam turn over.

"That's not how it works," the jailer said at last, hoarse.

"It's how it works tonight."

He stared at me. And then, with the care of a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was heavy, he unlocked the cell door and brought in paper and ink, and I began to draw.

* * *

I drew the Grey Stair true. I drew the shepherd tracks above it true. And then, with a steady hand and a heart going like a drum, I drew the ford at Miller's Bend a quarter-mile downstream of where it truly lies — into the narrows where the gorge walls pinch together and the water runs fast enough to drown a horse, let alone six hunters in starless armor.

It's not murder, I told myself, scratching away. It's geography. Nobody makes anybody take the low road. And if Vesper's map-reading is half what they claim, they'll never come within ten miles of my little amendment anyway.

I finished near dawn, or the rumor of dawn. The jailer took the chart, checked it against his own memory — and there was a horror I hadn't prepared for, watching him verify my betrayal against the perfect recollection of a hundred conversations I'd never known were catalogued — and nodded once.

"The transfer order goes through tomorrow," he said. "The deep cells, to general holding. If the crown asks why, I'll say the prisoner's cooperation earned leniency." He paused at the bars. "Brannic. When you get out of here — and the crown will spit you out eventually, it always does — when you find your cartographer—"

"Yeah?"

"Don't tell them what you traded." His voice was barely a whisper. "Some prices are kinder unseen."

He left. His keys sang. And behind me, Tam woke, smiled his lamb's smile, and said, "Hullo. Are you new?"

"Aye, friend," I said, and lay back on the cold stone, and stared at the wall where a window should have been, and did not sleep at all.

Because here was the thing I hadn't let myself think while the ink was drying: Vesper's map rewrites itself. Sable's letters had said so, and I'd laughed at it, and I'd seen it with my own eyes on the barge — ink crawling into new routes overnight like frost growing backward.

Which meant that sooner or later, that uncanny chart of theirs was going to show them the ford at Miller's Bend.

And it was going to show them *my handwriting* on it.

Somewhere above the valley, under a sky with no stars in it, Vesper Vane was climbing toward a citadel with a liar's compass and a traitor's map — and now, thanks to me, a third thing to carry: a smuggler's chart, drawn in a dungeon without windows, that would either save their life or hang us both.

I put my hands over my face and waited for morning, real or rumored, to come find out which.
