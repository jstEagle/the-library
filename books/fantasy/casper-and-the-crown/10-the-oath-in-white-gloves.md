## 10. The Oath in White Gloves

The compass had been leading me by the nose for three days, and I was tired of pretending I hadn't noticed.

A broken compass, Old Sable had said, doesn't point north. It points to what it's missing. At the time I'd taken that as a riddle worth chewing on. Now, watching the needle strain toward a ridge of black stone like a dog at the end of a leash, I understood it as an accusation. It wasn't pointing to something *out there*. It was pointing to something it thought belonged to me.

"You could ignore it," Brannic said. He was walking behind me, which he'd been doing since Hollowmere — smuggler's habit, letting someone else find the ambush first. "Compasses break all the time. We could go around the ridge. There's a valley south of here, warm, low, full of sheep and people who don't ask questions."

"The map says we should climb."

"Your map," Brannic said, with the particular flatness he reserved for my map, "has been wrong four times in six days."

"It's been *redirected* four times in six days. That's different." I didn't believe it either, but saying it out loud kept the other thought — the one where the ink crawled into new shapes because it wanted something from me — safely on the far side of my teeth.

We climbed anyway. Of course we climbed. I told myself it was strategy: high ground, sightlines, a chance to see the frost rings spreading across the valley below and count how many turns of the moon we had left. But my feet knew better. They climbed the way a man walks in his sleep, sure of every handhold before his eyes found it, taking a goat track I had no business knowing existed, cutting left where the rock offered a seam only someone who'd been here before would trust.

"You've walked this path," Brannic said. Not a question.

"I haven't been north of Thornfell in years."

"That's not what I asked."

I didn't answer. The needle pulled harder.

* * *

The observatory sat in the saddle of the ridge like a broken tooth — white stone gone grey, its great dome split open to the sky, the brass ribs of the telescope arm jutting up through the wound like the fingers of something that had died reaching. I knew it. That was the horror of it. Not remembered it — *knew* it, the way you know the layout of your own house in the dark. My boots found the collapsed stair without looking. My hands knew which stones were loose.

There were no stars overhead. There never were anymore. But the dome's split opened onto a sky so black and close it felt less like night than like a held breath, and standing under it, in that ruin, I felt watched by something patient.

"Vesper." Brannic's voice, low. He'd stopped at the threshold. Smugglers have instincts for thresholds; they spend their lives learning which doors are worth the toll. "This place is wrong."

"This place is mine," I said, and then heard myself say it, and wished I hadn't.

The needle spun.

Not wavered — *spun*, wild and screaming, the little metal heart of it whipping around so fast it blurred, and the cold that came off it bit through my glove like January. I dropped it. It hit the flagstones still spinning, ringing against them like a struck bell, and the sound didn't fade the way sound fades. It *opened*. Each ring of it peeled something back, and —

* * *

— white gloves. My hands, in white gloves, holding a pen that weighed more than a pen should, laying ink down on vellum so fine it drank light. Around me a room I knew from dreams I'd been telling myself were nothing: vaulted, star-charted, lit by lamps that burned cold blue. And a voice behind me, courteous as frost:

*You've given us the northern passes, Cartographer. Show us where the light comes in.*

*The light comes in everywhere,* I heard myself say — my voice, but younger, prouder, the voice of someone who had not yet learned what service costs. *Starlight isn't a road, Majesty. It falls.*

*It falls,* agreed the crown, *and pools. And pools can be drained.*

And my pen moved. Gods help me, my pen moved, and I drew the routes by which a crown could drink a sky.

* * *

I came back to myself on my knees on the observatory floor, and Brannic was crouched over me saying my name in the tone men use when they're not sure the person will answer to it anymore.

"How long?"

"Minutes. You were talking. Not to me." He helped me up, or tried; my legs had opinions of their own. His face in the dark had gone careful, the way it went careful when a job turned bad. "Vesper. What did you see?"

I told him. Some of it. The gloves. The room. The voice like frost. The pen moving. I did not tell him the part that mattered most, because I hadn't finished understanding it yet, and some things, once spoken, become too true to take back.

But the floor gave me no such mercy. Because while I'd been kneeling, the dust had cleared from a stretch of wall near the shattered telescope mount, and there — incised deep, worn shallow at the edges by years of weather — was a seal. A crown above crossed calipers. And beneath it, cut in a cartographer's neat capitals:

**VESPER VANE, CARTOGRAPHER ROYAL**
*Mapped the Feeding Routes, Years 1–9*
*By Their Own Hand, By Their Own Oath*

Brannic read it twice. Then he looked at me, and whatever he saw in my face confirmed it faster than the stone could.

"You're the architect," he said quietly.

"I don't remember being the architect."

"No," he said. "You remember *not* remembering. That's worse."

He was right, and I hated him for it, and I hated the stone more. Nine years. Nine years of routes drawn in my own hand — every pass where starlight pooled, every valley where memory gathered thick enough to harvest, mapped and measured and delivered up to a crown that drank them until the sky ran dry. The border wards hadn't failed on their own. I had drawn their weakness into them. Somewhere in nine years of work there was a line, one clean confident stroke of my pen, and when the wards snapped like cut rope on that first terrible night, it was my stroke that snapped them.

Every refugee on the valley road. Every nameless prisoner in a cell without windows. Aldric, sleeping, being eaten alive by the thing I had fitted to his head.

My hands. My white-gloved, steady, celebrated hands.

"There'll be a reason," Brannic said, and it wasn't comfort, exactly. Brannic doesn't do comfort. It was arithmetic. "People don't build a thing like that and run unless something happened. You fled. Sable said so. Fled means you turned against it somewhere between year nine and now."

"And erased myself doing it."

"That part I'd guess cost you."

"It cost me everything except the guilt," I said. "That apparently ships free."

He almost smiled. Almost. In Brannic that's a eulogy.

I picked up the compass. It had gone quiet, the needle resting, satisfied — pointed not north, not at the citadel, but straight at my own chest. It had brought me home. It had shown me the address where I'd been living all along, the one I'd burned the deed to.

"We keep going," I said.

"Do we?" Brannic looked out through the split dome at the starless dark. "Because from where I stand, the crown isn't hunting you despite what you know. It's hunting you *for* what you know. And if you're the one who drew the roads, Vesper — who better to draw the one that undoes it? Or the one that leads us all into its mouth. Your map's been herding us for a week. Whose hand do you think is moving the ink?"

I had no answer. That was the truth of it, laid out on cold stone under a dead sky: I had spent my life believing maps made sense of the world, and now the world's worst map was me, redrawn by my own request, and I could not swear which lines were mine.

Below us, far down the valley, frost bloomed in a perfect new ring. The moon turning. Counting down.

And the compass needle, resting against my chest, began — very faintly, very patiently — to beat. Like a second heart. Like something knocking at a door I had locked from the inside, asking, in a voice like frost:

*Shall we finish the oath, Cartographer?*

* * *

That night we made camp in the observatory's lower chamber, because the ridge gave shelter and neither of us would admit we were too wrung out to move. Brannic slept badly, one hand on his knife, the way he always did. I didn't sleep at all.

Instead I built a fire in a hearth my hands remembered how to unblock — the flue stone lifted out, set aside, replaced after, all of it done smooth as breathing by a body that had lived here — and I fed it pages torn from the back of my own chart book. Blank ones. I needed the light to think by, and I needed to burn something, and the two needs got along fine.

Here is what I assembled, page by burning page:

Old Sable's teaching: *a crown binds by holding memory.* The ward-keeper's dying whisper: *the crown eats what it cannot hold.* The seal on the wall: *by their own oath.* And underneath all of it, the thing I'd been circling since Hollowmere like a moth around a lamp it suspects will kill it: the compass didn't point to memories. It pointed to *mine*. Which meant the memories weren't gone. They were *held*.

Held where?

By what?

"You're muttering," Brannic said from his bedroll, without opening his eyes.

"I'm strategizing."

"You're muttering with a knife in reach, so keep it strategic."

I threw a page at him. He caught it without looking, which was insufferable, and set it on the fire without reading it, which was somehow worse.

"If you were the crown's cartographer," he said, watching the flame eat my handwriting-that-wasn't-mine-anymore, "you'll have left records. A woman like you doesn't work nine years without keeping copies."

"A woman like me erased herself."

"A woman like you," Brannic said, "kept the compass. Broke, useless, worthless to anyone else — and she kept it, and carried it, and made sure it ended up in the hands of a dying ward-keeper who'd pass it to a stranger on the road." He finally opened his eyes and looked at me across the fire. "That's not erasure, Vesper. That's insurance. Whoever you were made sure whoever you became would find the trail. You didn't destroy your past. You hid it. From yourself. Along a route only you could follow."

I stared at him. The fire popped. Somewhere above us, through the split dome, the dead sky pressed down like a lid.

"Which means," I said slowly, "the route isn't finished."

"No."

"Whatever I buried, I buried it in pieces, and this was only the first."

"No," Brannic said again, and rolled over, and added, muffled, with terrible smuggler's pragmatism: "So try to be somebody I like by morning. The next piece might be worse."

It was worse. Of course it was worse. But I didn't learn that until two nights later, when the compass woke us before dawn dragging us east, and the second piece of my buried self turned out to be waiting in a prison I had once drawn the plans for — and the jailer who kept it had been waiting even longer to give it back.
