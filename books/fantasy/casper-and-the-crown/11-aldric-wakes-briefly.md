## 11. Aldric Wakes, Briefly

The hour begins with the taste of copper and the smell of my mother's kitchen, which is how I know it's a lie.

Mother never cooked. Mother was Queen Maren of the Ninefold Vale, and her hands smelled of ink and cold iron, not bread. But the crown has been rummaging through what's left of me again, patching holes with approximations, and somewhere in its vast indifferent arithmetic it decided that a king ought to remember his mother's kitchen, so it built one. It built it badly. The bread smells like wet stone. The window over the basin shows a garden I never had, growing roses in the wrong season.

I stand in the false kitchen and I am grateful anyway.

You learn gratitude for strange things when you've been asleep for — how long? I don't know. That's the first theft you stop counting, because counting requires knowing the number, and the number is gone. The crown took the calendar out of me early. It kept the taxes. Of everything it might have carried off into its cold hoard — my wedding day, my father's voice, the name of the hound I loved — it kept the tax ledgers of the eastern provinces, complete down to the farthing. Some nights — if they are nights; there are no nights here, only turns of a moon I can feel but not see — some turns I lie in the dark of myself reciting grain tariffs because they are the only thing that still belongs to me whole.

A king is his people's memory, my father told me once. I thought he meant it as a duty. He meant it as a warning.

* * *

Here is what waking feels like: imagine you have been underwater for years, and someone does not pull you up but simply lets the water remember, briefly, how to be air.

It starts at the edges. My fingers first — real fingers, heavy ones, resting on the arm of a throne I can suddenly feel beneath me, cold stone worn smooth by other kings' shifting. Then breath. Then weight, the honest miserable weight of a body that has been sitting too long, which means my knees ache, which means I am alive, which means —

Which means the crown has slipped.

I do not ask why. Asking questions inside your own head, while that thing is coiled around your thoughts like frost around a windowpane, is a good way to lose the hour before you've spent any of it. Instead I open my eyes.

The throne room of the moonlit citadel is exactly as I remember it, which is to say wrong. It was built by my great-grandmother to face east, toward the morning, and the architects put windows in the east wall tall enough to walk through. Now the windows show nothing. Not darkness — nothing, a color that has no name because naming it would require light. And above, where the ceiling opens into the old star-well, where every coronation in my line had been held under open sky so the stars could witness the oath —

Above me, pressed against that opening like moths against glass, are stars.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. They are not shining down on the room. They are being held. I can see it now, from the inside, in the way you can only see a thing once you've become part of its machinery: the stars are not dead in the sky. They are here. Swallowed. Hoarded in the crown's gullet like a dragon's pile, dimmed by proximity to each other, crowded, suffocating. The realm below thinks the heavens went out. The heavens didn't go anywhere. They were *taken*, and I am sitting in the vault.

And the crown is distracted.

I can feel its attention pulled thin, stretched south and west, following roads, following — someone. Someone is climbing toward me. I felt them days ago the way you feel a splinter in a finger you'd thought long numb: small, insistent, impossible. The crown hates them. I could taste its hatred, cold and specific, and hatred is a form of attention, and attention is a form of absence, and absence is an hour.

One hour. Maybe less. I will not waste it standing.

* * *

Walking after years of stillness is its own humiliation. My legs hold — barely. I make it three steps before the throne room tilts and I catch myself on the arm of the throne like a drunk leaving his own feast. Fine. A king who cannot stand can still kneel, and kneeling puts my hands on the floor, and the floor is stone, and stone remembers.

Everything here remembers. That is the joke at the center of all this, the one nobody alive is left to laugh at. The crown was forged — I know this much, I pried this much loose over uncounted turns — the crown was forged to be the realm's memory. A vault. A keeper. Every oath sworn under open sky, every border drawn, every grave named: held safe, forever, so no king's death and no war and no burning library could ever take them. My ancestors wore it as a duty. *We keep what the realm cannot afford to forget.*

But a vault needs a wall to stand against, and when the Thornfell wards fell and the dark came down, the crown had nothing left to hold itself apart from. A memory-keeper with no memories outside itself begins to eat inward. First the stars, because stars are the oldest witnesses and their light is memory of the oldest kind. Then the king, because I was nearest, and because a sleeping king is just a very large memory wearing a body.

So it eats me turn by turn, patiently, the way rot eats a beam from within — and it keeps the tax records. Because the tax records are useful. Because hunger always keeps what's useful.

I drag myself along the wall toward the star-well. Above me the stolen stars press downward, and I think: *you are witnesses.* Every coronation oath in my line was sworn beneath you. You saw my father crowned. You saw me. You are not treasure. You are witnesses, and a witness stuffed in a vault is a hostage, and hostages wait.

* * *

Halfway across the floor I find the first crack made visible.

There is a place where the crown's grip thins — I can't explain it better than that. Like ice over a stream where the current runs fast beneath: you can see the water moving under the white. Something has worried at the crown from the outside. Someone has been *climbing*, and every mile they gain loosens the frost by a hair. Through this thin place I can hear — faintly, the way you hear neighbors through a wall — the crown thinking.

It does not think the way we do. There is no *I* in it. There is hunger, and there is inventory, and there is a single preoccupation repeated like a bell tolling: *the cartographer, the cartographer, the cartographer.*

Cartographer.

I knew a cartographer once. Before. White gloves, because she — he? — they — handled the oath-charts, and the oath-charts could not bear the oil of human skin. I remember gloves. I remember an oath spoken in this room, under this well, witnessed by these stolen stars. I remember being afraid of how competent they were. A king should be afraid of competence; it's the only honest fear left to him.

The crown wants them. Not dead — the crown doesn't hate the climber, whatever I tasted earlier. Hatred was my reading, and I read it wrong. What the crown feels is *want*, vast and precise: it wants a hand. It wants someone who can draw the map of its own appetite. It wants to be infinite and it knows, in its cold inventory way, that infinity requires a cartographer.

Well. So do I.

Because here is what I have understood, lying in the dark of myself for longer than I can count: you cannot kill this thing. I have watched it eat attempts to kill it. Warders marched on the citadel in the early dark, brave and doomed, and the crown drank their courage like broth and wore their armor afterward — the starless patrols, yes, those are our soldiers, hollowed and repurposed, which is a sentence I intend to answer for if I live to govern again. Destruction feeds it. Destruction is just memory being made available.

But a vault, my father said, holding the crown before ever it touched my head — a vault binds by keeping. Whatever it holds, it must hold *forever*. That is not its choice. That is its forging, older than its hunger, deeper than its greed. It cannot let go of what it keeps.

Which means the way to free a prisoner of the crown is not to break the vault open.

It is to give the vault something it must keep forever, so completely, so absolutely, that keeping it uses up the vault's whole capacity — and everything else falls out of its hands like sand from an overfull fist.

One memory. Held eternally. Everything else released.

I do not know whose memory could do that. Mine won't — mine are already half-eaten, full of holes, patched with false kitchens and wet-stone bread. But the person climbing toward me, the one the crown calls *cartographer* with something like longing — they have a past. And from the little the crown lets me glimpse of its thoughts, their past has a shape the crown finds interesting indeed.

They'll figure it out. Or they won't, and the thirteenth turn comes, and the crown finishes me and turns south with its new map and its new mouth. I cannot make them understand. I cannot send a letter. I have no voice the living can hear, no hands but these, shaking, braced against cold stone.

But I have an hour. And I have the stars.

* * *

Stitching starlight is harder than it sounds, and it sounds absurd.

The hoarded stars are not entirely inert. Pressed together this tight, they leak — thin threads of light escaping between each other like steam from a covered pot, and where the threads touch the stone of the star-well's rim, they cling, faintly, the way frost clings. I have watched it happen for turns beyond counting from inside my prison, unable to move, unable to speak. Unable until now.

I climb — crawl, honestly, dignity abandoned somewhere back at the throne — until I'm beneath the well's rim, and I reach up, and I take a thread of escaping light between two fingers.

It burns. Good. Pain means the hour is real.

Now: how does a king write a letter to a stranger? Not with words — words fade, words are the crown's currency and it will collect any I leave lying about. No. It must be written in the thing the crown cannot easily repossess without noticing the theft: pattern. Star-patterns. Constellations. I spent my boyhood learning them from my father on the palace roof — the Plough, the Sisters, the Long Wake, the Shepherd's Eye — and my father taught me they were maps before they were stories, routes home for travelers and sailors and smugglers and anyone else who trusted the sky.

So I will write in routes.

Thread by thread, finger burning, I lay the escaped light into the old stones of the well's rim, working fast before the crown notices its hoard leaking. A line south-west, following the passes — the true passes, not the mapped ones, the smugglers' cuts that no crown chart records because no crown chart *can* record them; they exist only in the memory of people the crown never managed to hold. Toward Hollowmere. Up through the high shrine country. To the ruined observatory, and past it, to the citadel's one unguarded throat.

And at the end of the route, where the line arrives at the base of the walls, I write the message itself. Not in letters. In the oldest sign-language of the sky, the one every stargazer learns first:

The Shepherd's Eye, closed. The Plough, broken — its handle snapped, its blade turned inward. And last, the sign my father called the Unyoking: two stars joined by a yoke-line, and then the yoke-line struck through, and the two stars set free to drift apart.

Not rescue. Release.

*Don't save me,* the sky-signs say, to whoever climbs beneath them and thinks to look up at the hole in heaven where the stars used to be. *Don't save me. Unbind me. Find what must be kept, and give it, and let everything else go.*

My hands are shaking. The burn has spread up both arms and I suspect I am bleeding on the stones, which the crown will notice eventually, though blood is such a small dull thing among all its hoarded light that maybe — maybe — it will overlook the stain.

* * *

The crack is closing. I feel it the way you feel a fever break in reverse — the frost creeping back across the windowpane of myself, the false kitchen waiting behind my eyes with its wet-stone bread. The crown's attention swings back toward the citadel like a lighthouse beam, and I have perhaps minutes, perhaps less.

I use them to sit. Properly, this time, on the steps below the throne instead of on it — a small rebellion, but I find I mean it. If I am to be a vault's contents, I will at least be contents that slouched.

Before the dark takes me back, I look up one last time at the star-well, at my crooked, burning, amateur constellation stitched into the ancient stones. It's ugly work. My father would be ashamed. But somewhere south of here a cartographer with white gloves and a rewritten map is climbing through a world gone dark, and when they come high enough to see the empty socket of the sky — and they will, climbers always look up — they will see that the stars aren't dead.

They're held. And held things can be given.

The frost closes over my eyes like water over a drowned lamp. The last thing I carry down with me is not hope exactly — hope implies expectation, and I've been disappointed too thoroughly for expectation — but something cheaper and more durable: the certainty that I spent my hour well. That for one hour, the prisoner worked.

Below, faint through miles of stone and snow and turning moon, I feel the climber pause on the road. Look up.

And begin, slowly, to climb faster.
