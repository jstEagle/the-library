## 12. The Turncoat's Road

The compass stopped shaking the moment I decided.

I'd been holding it wrong, I think — treating it like a tool, something you point and follow. But it had never been pointing north, or toward the citadel, or even toward my own buried past. That night in the ruined observatory, after the truth of what I'd been came up out of the ground like a root pulled bleeding from soil, the needle had settled into a steady, patient pull toward the crown's garrison at Hollowmere's edge. Toward Brannic.

It wasn't guiding me anymore. It was agreeing with me.

"You're sure about this," Sable said. The old cartographer sat by the fire with her bad leg stretched out and her maps spread around her like a dragon's hoard, all of them annotated in a hand so like mine it made my teeth ache. She'd trained me once, before the gloves, before the oath. Or someone with her face had. Memory is a country I'm still learning to read.

"I'm sure I'm not leaving him," I said.

"That's not the same as sure."

"No," I admitted. "But it'll do."

She snorted and pushed a scrap of vellum across the floor to me with her boot. On it was a single line of ink that hadn't been there an hour ago — one of her self-writing charts, the kind I used to believe were impossible, back when I believed the world was honest. The line wound down through the hills east of Hollowmere and ended at a small, ugly symbol: a door with a keyhole drawn where the handle should be.

"What's this?"

"The reason you're going to survive tonight." She tapped the keyhole. "There's a road under the garrison. Not a tunnel — a road. Built by smugglers, mapped by smugglers, and then—" she looked at me over the fire, "—mapped again by someone better. Someone the crown trusted to know every secret way in the realm."

My stomach turned over. "Me."

"You. You drew the master chart of the hidden roads, Vesper. Every bolt-hole, every toll-tunnel, every door cut into mountain stone. And because you drew it, you're the only person alive who knows which ones the crown never found." Her eyes glinted. "They burned your memory. They couldn't burn your lines."

I looked down at the vellum. My handwriting stared back at me from the margin — small, precise, confident. *Enter by the cistern gate. The guards count nine, not ten; they've never counted the tenth.*

"Did I write that?"

"You wrote everything," Sable said. "That was the problem."

* * *

The moon was three-quarters through its fourth turn when I reached the ridge above the garrison, and the frost rings showed it plainly. They spread out from the citadel now, visible even at this distance — perfect concentric circles of white creeping across the valley floor like ripples from a drowned bell. Each ring meant another fraction of Aldric devoured. Each ring meant less time. Old Sable had done the arithmetic for me twice: thirteen turns, and we'd spent four of them arguing about whether I was brave or merely stupid.

Both, probably. Brannic would have said both, and then charged a fee for the diagnosis.

I lay flat against the frozen heather and studied the garrison below. Starless armor moved along the walls in patterns too regular to be human — the hunters kept their rounds like clock hands, and there was nothing clocklike about their faces, because they didn't have any. Just smooth dark steel where features should be, the crown's hunger given legs and orders. They had taken Brannic two nights ago, dragged him out of the burning safehouse with his hands bound and his mouth still running insults at a speed that, frankly, I admired. He hadn't told them anything. I knew that because he was still alive. The crown's jailers only killed you after you'd run out of things worth remembering.

Which raised the question I kept circling: what did Brannic have worth remembering?

The answer, according to the compass, was me.

I checked the vellum again, then the ground. There. The cistern gate — a slab of mossy stone set into the hillside behind the garrison's old well-house, exactly where my past self had promised, exactly as forgettable as my past self had designed it to be. I'd walked past this hillside on the way into Hollowmere and never seen it. Neither, apparently, had the crown. Somewhere in the vaults of my erased life, the man I used to be had built a door into his enemy's house and then thrown away the key along with everything else.

I wondered if he'd known he'd need it someday. I wondered if he'd known he wouldn't be himself when the day came.

The tenth guard wasn't a guard at all. He was a deadfall — a section of wall rigged to collapse on anyone who stepped on the pressure plate beneath the loose flagstone, and it was still armed, still oiled, still patient after who knew how many years. I stepped over it the way my own handwriting told me to, left foot wide, right foot on the seam, and felt something in my chest that wasn't quite pride and wasn't quite grief. Competence, maybe. Borrowed competence. A stranger's skill living in my hands.

The tunnel beyond smelled of cold stone and old lamp oil. I lit my stub of candle and followed the line on the map — down, then left at the junction marked with a tiny smuggler's rune meaning *quiet here*, then down again, deeper than any cellar had a right to go. My own marginal notes appeared every few dozen paces, written in that same assured hand: *Low ceiling. Mind your head.* *Rats, but tame ones.* *If you can smell smoke, you've gone too far.*

Whoever I had been, he'd been thorough. And chatty.

The passage ended at a wall of fitted stones with no visible door — except that my map showed a hairline crack shaped like a keyhole, and when I pressed my palm against it, the stones sighed apart on counterweights so perfectly balanced they made no sound at all. Beyond was torchlight, and voices, and the particular echo of a place built to hold people who weren't meant to leave.

I put out the candle and listened.

"—told you already, I don't remember his face because there wasn't one, it was *armor*, do you people have ears under those helmets or just more helmet—"

Brannic. Alive. Insulting his captors with the fluency of a man who'd been doing it professionally for days.

A second voice answered him, low and tired, and something about it snagged my attention — not its words but its weight, the sound of someone carrying far too much. "The list says you traveled with the cartographer. Vesper Vane. Tell us where they went and the crown will consider your debt paid."

"My debt?" A laugh, ragged but real. "Mate, you've got the ledger backwards. I owe *them*. There's a difference between owing and being owned. You lot wouldn't know it — you work for a piece of jewelry."

Then silence, and the soft tread of boots leaving, and my heart hammering hard enough that I worried the whole garrison could hear it.

I gave them a hundred heartbeats. Then I slipped through the gap.

* * *

The cells were cut into the bedrock, windowless, each door iron-banded with a small square hatch at eye height. I counted six along the corridor, and from five of them came nothing — no light, no sound, no breathing I could detect. From the sixth came humming. Badly. Something that might once have been a drinking song before it was run over by a cart.

I knelt at the hatch and knocked the smuggler's knock — three fast, two slow, one fast — the rhythm that meant *friend in the dark, say nothing loud.*

The humming stopped. "Either you're a ghost," Brannic whispered, "or the most idiotic woman alive."

"Man."

"Even worse. Stand back from the door."

"I don't have a key."

"You don't need a key." His voice dropped lower, and I heard him shift close to the bars. "You need to tell me how you got in here, because if the answer is 'walked,' I'm going to need a moment to reassess several life choices."

"Smuggler's road. Under the cistern gate. Tenth guard's a deadfall."

Silence. Then, very quietly: "Nobody knows about the cistern gate. I ran that route for eleven years and I only ever heard rumors of it. Rumors with a price attached."

"Well." I pressed my palm to the lock mechanism I could feel but not see — a counterweight release, of course, because my past self had apparently never met a problem he didn't solve with physics. "Consider the rumor paid off."

The lock opened with a click soft as a knuckle cracking. The door swung, and Brannic filled it — thinner, bruised along one side of his face, his wrists raw where the bindings had been, and grinning like a man arriving at his own surprise party.

"You're late," he said.

"You're loud."

"I'm *consistent*." He stepped past me, glanced down the corridor, and his grin faded into something sharper. "Vesper. Listen. Before we run — the jailer. The one who questioned me. He's not like the armored ones."

"I noticed the voice."

"He remembers." Brannic said it carefully, the way you'd handle something with edges. "For the others. The prisoners in the quiet cells — they've forgotten their names, their faces, all of it, and he keeps it for them. Carries it. I heard him talking to one of them through the door, telling a woman her own name like it was a lullaby." His jaw worked. "He cried, Vesper. Afterward. When he thought no one was listening. What kind of prison has a jailer who grieves?"

"The kind the crown builds," I said, and hated how easily the understanding came, how much of it felt like remembering rather than deducing. The crown ate what it could not hold. So it held. It held and held until the holding became its own kind of hunger, and it made its servants hold too. "Come on. We can grieve on the move."

We went back through the gap in the stones, Brannic folding his tall frame through sideways and muttering about architects with grudges against shoulders. Behind us, from one of the silent cells, came a sound I will hear for a long time: a voice, thin and wondering, asking the darkness who was there. Asking if anyone knew its name.

I didn't stop. That's the part I keep returning to. I knew the route, I knew the timing, I knew that stopping meant both of us ending up in a quiet cell with a jailer to carry our names — and none of that knowledge made walking away feel like anything other than theft. Brannic's hand landed briefly on my shoulder as we climbed. He didn't say anything. He didn't need to. Some debts are acknowledged best in silence, and some silences are heavier than the things they acknowledge.

* * *

We came out of the hillside a mile east of the garrison as the moon finished its turn.

I felt it before I saw it — a shiver through the ground, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall. Above the valley, the fourth frost ring completed itself with a soundless finality, white meeting white, circle closing on circle. Somewhere north, in a throne room under a hoarded sky, a king lost another piece of himself to the thing on his head.

"Thirteen turns," Brannic said quietly. He was looking at the rings too, arms crossed against the cold. "Four down. How long does a turn take, exactly?"

"Nine days. Give or take."

"So we have eighty-one. Minus whatever the crown spends chasing us." He blew out a breath that hung in the air like a small ghost. "When I took your coin at Hollowmere, the job description was 'guide to the citadel.' It did not mention breaking into garrisons, insulting jewelry, or racing celestial bodywork."

"You can renegotiate when we stop moving."

"Oh, I intend to. Hazard rates. Emotional damages." He fell into step beside me as we started down toward the tree line, and for a while we walked in the companionable silence of two people who have recently saved each other's lives and haven't yet worked out the accounting. Then he said, without preamble: "The compass pointed you to me. Didn't it? That's how you found the garrison so fast."

"It agreed with me," I said. "There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"With that thing? I'm starting to hope so."

He grunted. We crossed a frozen stream, ice groaning under our boots, and on the far bank he stopped and turned to face me, and the humor drained out of his face entirely, which on Brannic was roughly equivalent to another man weeping.

"Back in the cell," he said, "they offered to wipe my debt. Clean slate. All I had to do was give them your name and direction." He held up a hand before I could speak. "I know. You're thinking *of course he refused, he's the loyal friend, that's his role in the story.* But I want you to understand it wasn't loyalty. It was arithmetic. Everything I've got — the routes, the contacts, the name I've built across half the realm — it's all built on knowing which people are worth owing. And you—" he jabbed a finger at me, "—you are the single most valuable debt I have ever carried. A cartographer the crown erased, carrying a map that rewrites itself, heading for a king nobody's managed to reach in years? Vesper, you're not a passenger. You're the whole cargo."

"Flattering."

"It's not flattery, it's *pricing.*" But something in his voice softened despite itself. "Though I'll admit the pricing has developed complications. Complications with a face and an irritating habit of walking back into prisons for people."

"Is that trust?"

"That's *concern,*" he said firmly, and started walking again. "Trust comes later. Trust is earned at market rates."

I smiled, and for the first time since the stars went out, it didn't feel like lying.

* * *

We camped that night in a hollow beneath a stand of black pines, and I unrolled the map to check our road north. The ink had shifted again — it did that now, always a step ahead of me, drawing routes I hadn't chosen toward places I hadn't named. But this time, studying the new line, I noticed something that stopped my breath.

In the margin, in my handwriting, in ink I had not touched:

*The ninth cell. Ask the jailer his name.*

"Vesper?" Brannic looked up from the fire. "What is it?"

I stared at the words. My hand. My letters. And yet a message from someone I had never been to someone I barely was — or perhaps the reverse. Perhaps the map wasn't herding me anymore. Perhaps the man who'd built these roads, the one in the white gloves, was leaving notes for the woman who'd inherited his hands, the way Sable left notes for me. A relay across an erased life.

"There's a jailer," I said slowly. "At the garrison. Brannic says he carries the prisoners' memories for them — keeps them safe, gives them back in pieces. He grieves the carrying." I traced the margin note with one finger, feeling the faint groove of the pen beneath the ink, as though whoever wrote it had pressed hard. "And my map wants me to ask him his name."

Brannic was quiet for a moment. Firelight moved on his scarred face. "And do you trust it?"

I thought about the cistern gate. The tenth guard. The counterweights singing open at my palm. Everything my past self had built had held — had *worked* — precisely because he'd understood what the crown would never understand: that the strongest things are the ones nobody thinks to look for.

"He's never lied to me yet," I said. "Whoever he was."

Above the pines, through a tear in the clouds, I saw it — one star, alone, burning low over the northern peaks. Not returned. *Released.* As if something vast and hoarding had let one small light slip through its fingers, careless, or testing, or afraid.

The compass in my pocket began to tremble. Not pulling north. Not pulling toward the citadel, or the past, or Brannic.

Pulling toward the jailer.

Toward a man who remembered everything, and wanted, more than anything in the starless dark, to be allowed to forget.
