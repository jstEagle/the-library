## 13. The Jailer's Grief

There are two hundred and six cells in the deep archive of the crown's dungeon, and not one of them has a window. I know this because I have counted them four hundred and eleven times, and I will count them again tomorrow, and the day after, because counting is what I do now. Counting, and remembering.

My name is Corvel Ash. I tell you that freely, because it is the last free thing I own.

I was a monk of the Order of the Ledgered Soul once — a small order, an honest one, sworn to keep records of the dying so that no one left the world unwitnessed. We wrote names on vellum. We lit candles. It was quiet work and it suited me, because I have always had a memory like a well: things fall in and never come back up, but they are all still down there, every one, whole and cold and perfectly preserved.

The crown found me three years ago. Or rather, its servants did, when the starlight began to thin and the old keepers of the dungeons started to forget which prisoners were theirs. A jailer who cannot remember his prisoners is worse than useless. A jailer who cannot remember his prisoners releases monsters by accident and punishes innocents by design. So they came to my monastery with their starless armor and their polite voices, and they asked me if I would mind holding some things for them.

I said yes. That is the sin I carry, and unlike everyone else in this place, I carry it with my eyes open.

* * *

Here is how the work is done.

When the crown takes a prisoner's memories — their name, their mother, the road home, the reason they weep at certain songs — those memories do not die. Nothing the crown takes dies. It swallows, and it holds, and somewhere in that cold infinite appetite there is a shelf with your childhood on it. But a shelf needs a keeper. The crown is vast, but it is also hungry, and hungry things do not like to be bothered with small administrative tasks.

So it deputized me.

Every night at the turn of the hour, the taken memories come to me like birds returning to a roost. They settle into my head and I hold them. I hold a fisherman's memory of teaching his daughter to gut her first catch. I hold a bride's memory of the morning she decided she loved her husband, a decision made over burnt porridge, for reasons she could never afterward explain. I hold the name of the woman in cell ninety-four, who has asked me every day for two years what her name is, and every day I tell her, *Maren*, and every day her face does something complicated and then goes smooth again, because the name means nothing to her. It means everything to me. I say it like a prayer. I am, I suppose, the only congregation Maren has left.

Two hundred and six prisoners. Two thousand, nine hundred and forty-one held memories as of last night's accounting. I could recite every one. Sometimes I do, walking the corridors, whispering them into the dark like a man scattering seed on stone.

You will want to ask why I don't simply give them back. I asked myself that in the first month, standing outside cell ninety-four with all of Maren's life folded neat behind my teeth. The answer is simple and it is the crown's answer and I hate that it works: a prisoner who remembers why she fights is a prisoner who fights. The namelessness is not cruelty for cruelty's sake. It is the lock itself. Give Maren back her daughter's face and Maren will tear these walls down with her fingernails, or die trying, and either way the crown loses a holding and I lose my post and someone else, someone worse, gets the keys.

That is what I tell myself. I want you to understand that I believe it, most days, the way you believe a roof will hold. Not because you've tested it lately. Because the alternative is looking up.

* * *

They brought a new prisoner through three nights ago — the smuggler, Brannic Vole, big and loud and unrepentant, the kind of man who treats a cell like an inn room with poor service. And with him came word that traveled through the guards like grease through a goose: the cartographer had come. *Vesper Vane.* The one the crown wanted above all others, the one whose name sat alone on a list I was made to memorize and then forbidden to speak aloud.

And then, last night, the impossible thing. The smuggler's cell stood empty at morning count. Open door, missing prisoner, and the guard on duty swearing he'd seen a corridor that doesn't exist — a smuggler's cut-through, a route that predated the crown's renovations. Drawn by someone who knew this dungeon before it was a dungeon.

Someone who had been here. Someone who had drawn it.

I stood in that empty cell for a long time. And I felt something I had not felt in three years, which was hope, and hope in this place is a splinter working toward the heart.

Because here is what I know, and what I decided before dawn: the cartographer is climbing toward the throne. The cartographer was once the crown's own. And if anyone alive can unmake the arrangement that keeps two hundred and six people nameless and one monk drowning — it is not me. I have tried. God knows I have tried. I once walked to the edge of my jurisdiction, to the stair that leads up out of the archive, and I got as far as the third step before my own held memories turned on me, every grief I carry rising at once like floodwater, until I crawled back down and was sick in a corner and counted cells until my hands stopped shaking.

I cannot carry myself out. But maybe someone can carry the well.

* * *

I caught up with them at dusk, in the long gallery above the flooded cisterns, where the crown's light comes through the grates in pale bars and everything smells of wet stone and old iron. The smuggler moved like a man re-entering a bad dream, and the cartographer —

I had been told what to expect. Star charts and certainty. A professional. What I saw instead was a person with shadows under their eyes deep enough to store things in, moving with the particular caution of someone who has recently learned their own hands cannot be trusted.

"Vesper Vane," I said, and stepped out of the dark with my ring of keys held open-palmed, the universal grammar of surrender. "Don't run. Running wakes the wardens, and the wardens don't sleep anyway."

Brannic had a knife out before I finished the sentence, bless him. The cartographer put a hand on his arm and studied me.

"You're the jailer," Vesper said. "The one Brannic mentioned. The one who remembers."

"For them, yes." I set the keys on the floor between us and stepped back from them, deliberately, so they could see I was unarmed except for the worst weapon of all. "Corvel Ash. Formerly of the Order of the Ledgered Soul. Currently custodian of two thousand nine hundred and forty-one stolen lives. I've come to make you an offer, and then, I expect, to beg."

"Begging's new," Brannic muttered. "Guards usually skip straight to the stabbing."

"I'm not a guard." I looked at Vesper and made myself say it plainly, because plainly was the only currency I had left. "I'm a vault. The crown took their memories and gave them to me to hold, because I remember everything, cartographer. Everything. Do you understand what that means? Every name. Every face. Every last ordinary evening of two hundred and six people's lives, filed in my skull, and none of it mine, and none of it returnable. There's a woman in cell ninety-four named Maren who asks me daily who she is, and I know, I *know*, down to the scar on her knee and the song she hummed at her wedding, and I cannot give it back to her without giving her back her grief and her rage and her reason to burn this place down, and the crown has convinced me that would be a catastrophe, and the crown might even be right, and I no longer know the difference between prudence and cowardice. That is my situation."

Silence, except for water dripping somewhere in the cisterns below, patient as arithmetic.

"What's the offer?" Vesper said finally.

"You're going to the throne room. Everyone knows it now; the whole dungeon can feel it, the way animals feel weather. When you reach the crown — if you find some way to unbind it, to break its hold —" My voice did something humiliating here. It cracked. Three years of ledgered souls, and it was my own name that nearly undid me. "Then take me with you. Take the memories. All of them. Let me carry them to the king, let me pour them back where they belong. I'll walk into whatever fire is required. I only want to stop being the shelf. I want, just once, to forget something."

* * *

I want to record what happened next honestly, because honesty is the last duty of a ledger-keeper and I may not get another chance to perform it.

Vesper looked at me for a long moment, and I saw the refusal arrive before it was spoken. I have spent three years learning to read faces through memory rather than expression — I know what longing looks like from the inside — and what crossed the cartographer's face was not cruelty. It was arithmetic. The same arithmetic the crown had taught me.

"If I take you," Vesper said slowly, "you're carrying three thousand memories through the most dangerous ground in the realm, to a crown that eats memory, that *hunts* memory, that hoards starlight in its own skull. If it touches you, it doesn't just take your life, Ash. It takes two hundred and six lives. Everyone you hold. Every name. Gone into that thing forever, with no keeper and no key." A pause. "Right now you're the safest place those memories exist. You're a locked room that walks. I can't unlock you in the middle of a siege."

"They're not safer in me," I said. "They're *only* in me. One skull, cartographer. One fever, one fall, one warden's blade, and two hundred and six people become nobody, permanently, with no one left who even knows to mourn them. At least in their owners' heads they'd be *lived*. Worn. Fought for. Isn't that what memory is for? Not storage — use?"

"Yes," Vesper said quietly. "It is." And then, with the terrible gentleness of someone delivering a prognosis: "But I need you here. When the binding breaks — if it breaks — someone has to be inside who knows where every prisoner is, who can open every door before the wards collapse and bury them. You're not a hostage to me, Ash. You're the plan."

Do you see the craftsmanship of it? It was a good refusal. It was a *true* refusal. Every word of it correct, every consequence honestly weighed, and it still functioned, in the end, as exactly the same sentence the crown had been saying to me for three years: *not yet, not you, the cost is too high, be the shelf a little longer.*

"No," I said.

"Ash—"

"No. You misunderstand what I'm asking." I heard my own voice go strange, go young, go back to a monastery garden where I had once argued theology with a man who smelled of beeswax. "I'm not asking you to consider the strategy. I'm asking you to look at me. I am thirty-one years old and I contain a wedding, a funeral, a child's first steps, and a woman's whole name, and I cannot put any of it down. I have not slept a full night in three years because when I sleep, they dream, cartographer. Their memories dream *in me*. Maren dreams of her daughter and I wake up crying for a child I never had, grieving a loss that isn't mine, loving people I've never met, and I love them, that's the rot of it, I *love them all*, two hundred and six people, and not one of them knows my face by morning." The keys on the floor between us seemed suddenly very loud. "So I'm begging. Formally. As one keeper of maps to another. Find another plan. Open the doors yourself. Learn the layout. Anything. Just don't leave me as the shelf. Don't be the crown's last argument for why I must remain one."

Vesper's face did something complicated — I recognized it, later, as the face of a person watching their own reflection appear in someone else's ruin — and then it went smooth, the way Maren's goes smooth, and I understood in that instant exactly what I was looking at. Someone else who had learned to file their own grief away for later. Someone else being prudent.

"I'm sorry," Vesper said. "No. Not yet. If we survive the throne room, I'll come back for you. I swear it on the map."

Everyone in this kingdom swears on something. It's never anything the swearer can afford to lose.

"Then we're done," I said, and picked up my keys, and became the jailer again, because the shape was there and I fit it, the way a hand fits a shackle. "Cell block C is half-flooded; go around by the cistern stairs. The wardens change at moonrise; the fourth turn is tonight, and they'll be restless. And cartographer —" I made myself say it, the last gift I had, the one true thing I could still give away — "when you meet the crown, it will offer you something. It always offers. It offered me my monastery, my candles, my quiet vellum life, whole and restored, in exchange for one small continued service. Check the offer against the people you love. If the offer requires them to stay lost, it's lying."

Brannic tugged Vesper's sleeve and they melted backward into the gallery dark, and I was alone with the dripping water and the pale bars of light and two thousand nine hundred and forty-one reasons I couldn't sleep.

* * *

Here is the regret, and I set it down plainly.

I lay awake in the warden's alcove doing what I always do — reciting names, Maren, Tomas, Ilsette, the fisherman, the bride, all the way down the ledger — and somewhere past midnight I reached the memory I always save for last, the oldest holding in my collection: a boy's memory of his mother teaching him to draw borders in sand, telling him *a line is a promise, little one, draw nothing you wouldn't live inside.*

And I realized, with the particular horror available only to a man with my memory, that I had refused the cartographer using the crown's own argument, delivered in the crown's own gentle voice, and that I had watched someone else's face go smooth with prudence and had thought: *ah, there it is, the lock,* without once wondering who had built the lock into me, and when, and whether the crown had taken more from me than it ever entered into the ledger.

Because here is what I know now, in the small hours, holding my well of other people's lives: I remember everything I was given to hold. Perfectly. Forever.

But I cannot remember the exact moment I agreed to any of it. The oath I swore in the starless armor's presence is gone — not held, not shelved, simply absent — and I had never noticed, because a man with a perfect memory does not check his own pockets.

Something was taken from me that was never recorded. Which means the ledger is incomplete. Which means the crown lies to its own bookkeeping. Which means —

Which means I owe the cartographer an apology, and a warning, and I intend to deliver both, even if I have to break every rule I've hidden behind for three years to do it. At moonrise the wardens change. At moonrise the frost begins its rings. And at moonrise, Corvel Ash, former monk, current shelf, is going to climb the third step, and the fourth, and however many it takes, carrying the only thing I have left that is truly mine:

the intention to forget.
