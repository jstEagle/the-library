## 14. Frost Rings and False Kings

The frost rings had reached the seventh turn by the time we made camp in the ruins of a shepherd's bothy, and I had stopped pretending the cold was ordinary. It wasn't. Ordinary cold bites your fingers and steals your toes. This cold *counted*. It sat outside the fire like a tax collector, patient, making note of everything we burned.

Brannic had his boots off and was inspecting a blister with the gravity of a man reading his own death warrant. "Fourth pair of socks gone through," he said. "I want it noted that I owned three pairs when this started."

"Noted."

"And that I've been robbed by weather, which is a new one even for me."

"You've been robbed by kings, priests, a river baroness, and at least one goose," I said. "Weather's just joining a distinguished list."

The map lay open on my knees, doing its slow, oily work — ink crawling at the margins like something alive under glass. Since Hollowmere it had been leading us upward in long, obedient switchbacks toward the citadel, and every night I checked its arithmetic twice, because a map that lies once will lie again. Tonight the route bent east around a frozen tarn called, according to the ink, *Mere of the Second Silence*.

"That name wasn't there yesterday," Brannic said.

"No."

"Maps don't usually grow names overnight."

"This one does whatever it likes." I traced the tarn's shoreline. The ink was still wet. Still warm, if I'm honest, though I'd stopped saying that part out loud after Brannic gave me a look normally reserved for men who claim their horses talk to them. "It wants us to go around the water instead of across the ice."

"So we cross the ice."

"So we cross the ice," I agreed, and felt the map shudder faintly against my knees, like a cat being carried somewhere it disapproved of.

We crossed it at dawn. The ice held. The map sulked.

* * *

The mentor appeared at noon, walking out of the snowfall as casually as a man stepping out for tobacco.

I knew him before I saw his face. I knew him by the walk — that rolling, heel-first stride he'd used to cross thirty years of border country without ever once hurrying. Corvus Dane. Dead eleven years, drowned in the Greywash with half a smuggling fleet, and here he came up the frozen track with snow in his grey beard and his old oilskin cape thrown over one shoulder exactly wrong, the way he always wore it, because he said capes were for people who wanted to look heroic and he preferred to look *expensive*.

Brannic's hand went to his knife. Mine didn't. That should have frightened me more than it did.

"Vesper Vane," Corvus said, and his voice was right too, gravel poured over honey. "Still drawing other people's roads for them, I see. Some things never change."

"You're dead," I said.

"I get that a lot lately." He smiled. And there it was — the smile. Warm at the corners. Eyes that crinkled. A smile that said *I'm glad to see you*, that said *you've done well*, that said all the things mentors are supposed to say.

That was the first thing wrong with him.

The second thing wrong arrived about four seconds later, when he said: "Come here, lad. Let me look at you."

Lad. He had never once called me lad in six years of apprenticeship. He'd called me *Vane*, or *cartographer*, or, in his darker moods, *the expense*. The closest he'd ever come to affection was the day I finished my master chart of the Thornfell passes and he looked at it for a long time and said, "Hm. You've wasted less paper than usual." I had treasured those seven words for a decade. I'd have died for another seven like them.

This thing wearing his face said, "You don't know how proud I've been of you," and my skin crawled so hard it nearly left without me.

"Frost rings," I said to Brannic, conversationally, not taking my eyes off the dead man. "Seventh turn. Sable said the crown gets cleverer with each one. Learns what it can't predict."

"It can't predict me," Brannic said. "Everyone says so."

"It learned you fine. It's learning *me*." I stepped off the track, putting a frozen boulder between myself and my mentor's ghost. "And it went looking through my head for the thing that would stop me walking. And it found you."

Corvus-Dane-thing spread his hands. Snow settled on his shoulders and did not melt, which was answer enough, but I let him talk anyway, because a liar talking is a liar spending information. "I know how it looks—"

"It looks like a corpse with good posture."

"—but I got out of the Greywash. Swam clear. Been following you since Hollowmere, trying to catch you alone, away from—" he flicked a glance at Brannic, distaste performed beautifully, "—your current associates. Vesper. Turn back. There's nothing up that mountain but a king who's past saving and a crown that will eat you alive. I've seen what it does. Come south with me. We'll take the coast road. I'll teach you the deep-water charts, the ones I never got time for."

And there it was. The hook inside the bait. Because that offer — *the charts I never got time for* — was the dream I'd carried at seventeen, the one I'd never told anyone, not even Sable, not even myself out loud. The crown hadn't just read my memories. It had read the *shape* of them, the architecture, the load-bearing griefs.

"Say the thing," I said quietly.

"What thing?"

"The thing you actually said. When I showed you my first finished survey. Word for word. You remember it. You're supposed to be memory-perfect."

He hesitated. One heartbeat. Two. Then, warmly: "I told you I was proud of you."

"You told me I'd wasted less paper than usual." My voice didn't shake. I was almost impressed by that. "You never once told me you were proud. Not once in six years. I used to lie awake hating you for it. And then you drowned, and I hated you for *that*, and then I spent eleven years becoming the kind of cartographer who might finally have earned the sentence, from a man who was no longer available to say it." I took a breath. The cold counted it. "So no. Whatever you are, you're not him. Because the real Corvus Dane would never have been kind to me, and whoever built you thought kindness is what I wanted."

The smile stayed exactly where it was while the warmth drained out from underneath it, like tide leaving a beach. What remained was smooth and patient and interested in me the way a fisherman is interested in a lure's performance.

"You would refuse your heart's desire," it said. Its voice hadn't changed, but its *weight* had — the same gravel-honey sound coming from somewhere much larger. "Curious. The others took the copy. Every one of them. They wept and turned back and walked into our keeping, grateful."

"Then you've been fishing in shallow water."

"We have been fishing in *memory*, Cartographer Royal." The title landed like a stone dropped down a well. Behind it, the snowfall thinned, and I saw that we were no longer alone on the track — shapes stood among the drifts, starless armor drinking the grey light, a dozen of them, maybe two dozen, arranged with a precision that hurt to look at. Herded. That was the word. The map had herded us onto this track, and the track had herded us into this bowl of snow, and the ghost had been the soft part of the trap before the hard part closed.

Brannic moved up beside me, knife out, and said, cheerfully, in the tone he used for negotiating with people who intended to kill him: "Right. Before anyone does anything regrettable — I'd like it noted I offered to cross the ice."

"They crossed the ice," the impostor-Corvus said, and something in the vast patience behind the voice turned, briefly, toward my knees, where the map hung in its case. "The instrument resists us. Interesting. It was ours once."

"It was mine once too," I said. "I'm told we're both reformed."

"You misunderstand your position." The armored figures began to walk forward, unhurried, their boots making no sound in the snow at all, which was somehow worse than sound. "You cannot fight what remembers every fight you have ever fought. We know your feints, Vesper Vane. We know which hand you favor and which knee aches in the cold. We know the oath you swore in white gloves and the road you fled down afterward. Surrender the compass and the chart, and we will be — generous. Your associate walks free. Your debts are paid. And you may keep your memories whole, unedited, forever. Isn't that what you climbed all this way to recover?"

And here is the thing I learned on that frozen track, standing in the bowl of the trap with my dead teacher's face smiling at me and the crown speaking through his mouth: grief is a weapon only if you pick it up.

They'd built him perfect. Perfect walk, perfect voice, perfect wrongness of cape. But they'd built him from *my* memory of Corvus, and my memory of Corvus had been curated by eleven years of longing into something softer than the man ever was. The real Corvus would have looked at this ambush and said, *Vane, you've walked into it, well done, now get us out of it,* and then he'd have already been moving while he talked. The false one stood waiting for an answer, because the crown thinks answers matter. The crown is made of held things. It believes everything can be held.

Including, apparently, me.

"Brannic," I said. "The tarn."

"The frozen one? The one your map didn't want us near?"

"The very one."

"Any particular reason?"

"A map that lies tells you where the truth hurts it." I pulled the chart from its case and let the wind take it flat, and the ink screamed — actually screamed, a thin dry hiss like a quill dragged wrong — because the route it had drawn bent desperately away from the tarn, away from the ice, away from the Mere of the Second Silence. "Whatever's under that water, it doesn't want us looking. And I've spent my whole life looking at the places maps avoid."

The impostor's face finally did something honest. It went blank.

"Take them," it said, and the armor came on at a run.

What followed was short, ugly, and deeply undignified, and I will render it honestly because I am, whatever else has been edited out of me, a professional. We did not fight two dozen hunters and win. We ran. Brannic threw his last fire-pot sideways to crack the lake ice behind the lead rank of armor, and the crown's soldiers — who remember every fight but perhaps not every *lake* — went into the black water in a tangle of starless steel, silent even drowning, which I will hear at night for the rest of my life. The rest slowed to drag them out, because the crown holds what it takes and apparently that includes its own drowned men, and in that slowing, Brannic grabbed my collar and we went up the scree like rabbits with a debt collector behind them.

Behind us, the impostor stood unmoving in the snowfall, Corvus's face tilted slightly, studying me as I fled. Just before the ridge cut off the view, it raised one gloved hand. Not in threat.

In *wave goodbye*.

* * *

We didn't stop until full dark, in a hollow beneath an overhang with a view of the valley, because I refused to camp anywhere my back couldn't see the whole approach. Brannic got a fire going small and low, the way smugglers do, a fire you could cover with a hat.

He fed it sticks for a while. Then, without looking up: "You knew. From the start. Before he even spoke."

"The walk was right. The smile was wrong."

"His smile was wrong?"

"His smile was *kind*." I watched the flame eat the stick. "Corvus Dane once made a boy of fourteen cry by telling him his lettering was 'adequate, which is worse than bad, because bad can be fixed.' He charged me rent for the privilege of apprenticeship. Real rent. He deducted it from my wages with a ledger, and the ledger was beautiful, flawless ruling, and I loved that ledger more than I ever loved him." I heard my own voice go strange and stopped, restarted. "Eleven years dead, and I still wanted him to say the sentence. So did whatever's under that mountain. It reached into my head, found the wanting, and built me a version of him that would finally say it. And for about four seconds, I nearly went with it. Four seconds, Brannic. That's how long it takes to lose everything."

Brannic was quiet. Then he said, "For what it's worth, I'd have known too."

"How?"

"He offered to teach you for free." He poked the fire. "No smuggler alive teaches anything for free. Dead giveaway."

I laughed. It came out cracked and surprised, and it hurt my ribs, and it was the best minute of the last three days. Grief weaponized, Sable would have written in one of his cramped marginal hands. Survived by knowing the dead better than the living do. Or possibly: survived by spite. Both entries were probably true.

But underneath the laugh, something had hardened in me, cooling like slag. The crown had shown me its method. It reads, it copies, it offers you your own heart back at interest. It had tried my love. Which meant next it would try my guilt — the oath in white gloves, the roads I mapped for it once, the thing I'd fled. Somewhere ahead, between here and the citadel, there was a version of that conversation waiting, and it would be much harder than a dead man's smile, because guilt is the one memory that's *true*.

I opened the map. The ink had redrawn itself again, sullenly, and the new route no longer avoided the tarn — it ran straight up the mountain's spine, direct and confident and helpful, as if the last day had never happened.

"It's stopped lying badly," I said. "Now it's going to start lying well."

"Or," Brannic said, "it's given up and decided honesty's the best policy."

"From a parasite?" I looked north, toward the citadel, where above the black peaks the moon hung ringed in its seventh halo of frost, thin and bright and counting down. "Brannic, when a thing like that starts telling you the truth, it's because the truth is a bigger trap than the lie."

The moonlight caught the fresh ink, and for just a moment I could have sworn the lines of the new route weren't drawn at all.

They were *written* — in a small, cramped, familiar hand, marching up toward the throne room of the sleeping king like a sentence being completed:

*Almost home, Cartographer Royal.*
