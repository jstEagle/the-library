## 15. The Citadel Below the Moon

**Vesper**

The moon's ninth turn caught us on the last mile of the Grey Stair, and I will say this for the frost: it made the climbing honest. Every handhold glazed white, every foothold announced itself with a crackle under my boot, and the whole mountainside became the kind of map I could have made in my sleep — if I still trusted my sleep, which I don't, and my hands, which I trust less.

"You know what I miss?" Brannic said behind me, breath steaming. "Inns. Inns have doors. Doors keep things out. This is a mountain with no doors."

"This is a mountain with one door," I said. "That's the problem."

Below us the valley had gone silent in the way that silence gets when it isn't empty but listening. The frost had come down the slopes in rings, exactly as Old Sable's notes had promised — perfect concentric circles spreading from the citadel like a stone dropped in a frozen pond. Nine rings now. Four more and the moon finished its turn, and whatever was up there finished its meal.

I had Sable's notes bound against my chest, wrapped in oilcloth, because the last three people who'd read them carelessly had apparently forgotten their own mothers' faces, and I had enough trouble with faces as it was. The compass rode in my pocket, pointing — as it had for two days — not north, not to any memory I could name, but up. Always up. Toward the citadel below the moon.

That's not a contradiction. You'll understand in a moment. I'm still not sure I do.

The Grey Stair ended at a shelf of black rock, and when we pulled ourselves over the lip of it, Brannic swore with the fluency of a man who'd been saving it up for nine turns of the moon.

I didn't swear. I wished I had the breath.

The citadel didn't sit on the mountain. It hung above it — a vast inverted shape of pale stone, its spires pointing *down*, its foundations lost in cloud, suspended beneath the moon as though the sky had grown a tooth. Moonlight poured over it and did not fall past it. It stopped. It *collected*, running down the spires like water down a funnel, and at the crown of the inverted keep — which is to say, its lowest point, hanging closest to the world — something drank.

"That's not possible," Brannic said.

"No," I agreed. "But there it is."

I'd drawn impossible things before. Cartographers all do; it's how we get paid. A river that forks around a haunted islet. A coastline that curves to flatter a duke. But I had never drawn a building that hung from the sky, and standing in front of the real thing, I felt the old professional shame of a man whose imagination had been too small.

The compass in my pocket was trembling.

* * *

**Brannic**

I've smuggled brandy past three customs houses, a fugitive countess past a wedding, and once — my proudest work — an entire orchestra past a war. I know what it looks like when a thing is too big to run from. You get a feeling in your back teeth, like a storm coming, except the storm is a fact and you are a mistake.

That was the feeling, looking up at the citadel hanging over us like the underside of the world.

Vesper stood at the shelf's edge with their notebook out, sketching, because of course they were. Nine turns of frozen hell, two ambushes, one betrayal by their own map, and the moment they see the impossible fortress they start *drawing it*. I used to think it was bravery. I've since decided it's a disease, and I've stopped trying to cure it because the disease keeps saving my life.

"Notes say there's a gate on the north face," Vesper said, not looking up. "Sable marked it. 'The Warden's Door, sealed from inside, opens for the crown's own.'"

"Charming. Do we have a crown?"

"We have a compass that used to belong to one."

The wind came off the ice fields then, and it was wrong. I want that written down somewhere. It was *wrong*. Wind should push you. This pulled — a slow, patient tug at the spine, like the whole mountain was leaning toward the citadel, and I caught Vesper's arm and felt the same lean in them.

"Tell me you feel that."

"I feel it," Vesper said. "Sable's notes call it the Draw. Everything up here leans. Snow, light, thought." A pause. "Memory most of all."

Lovely. A building that pulled at your thoughts. I'd known women like that, but at least they'd had the courtesy of a door you could leave through.

We moved along the shelf, north, toward the Warden's Door, and the closer we got the more I noticed the light. Not the absence of it — the *gathering* of it. The moonlight didn't scatter up here. It ran in thin bright threads along the rock, all flowing one direction, all flowing *in*, and when I looked closely at the citadel's hanging walls I saw them: veins. Faint silver lines running through the stone like the veins in a leaf, all converging on the crown-point where the keep hung lowest.

"Vesper."

"I see it."

"Is that—"

"Light. Yes." Vesper's voice had gone flat, which I'd learned was their voice for *I am frightened and doing arithmetic about it*. "Sable wrote that the stars didn't go out. He wrote it three times, underlined. I thought it was an old man's denial." They looked up, and up, and their face did something complicated. "He wasn't denying. He was right."

* * *

**Vesper**

Here is what Sable's notes said, and here is what my eyes confirmed, and I set them side by side now because I have spent my life teaching people to trust maps over their own lying eyes, and this once, the map and the eyes agreed, and I wished with everything I had that they hadn't.

*The stars did not die,* Sable wrote. *Nothing dies that quietly. I watched the night the wards fell. The stars did not go out — they were taken. Watch the citadel on a clear night. Watch where the light goes.*

And the light went *in*. Through the veins in the stone, down the hanging spires, into the crown-point, and I understood — the way you understand a landslide, all at once and uselessly — what we were climbing toward.

Every star in the sky. Hundreds of years of them, the fixed lamps of the Thornfell sky that every map I'd ever drawn had marked with their old smuggler's names — the Lantern, the Sisters, the Thief's Eye that Brannic swore by. They weren't dead. They were *hoarded*. Pulled down through the collapsed wards, threaded through the mountain, and stored inside the crown like a miser's coins, and the king who wore it was the vault they were kept in.

"It's not eating him," I said aloud. I hadn't meant to speak. "It's not just eating him. He's the *larder*. The stars are kept in him, and it feeds on them through him, slowly, the way you'd feed on stores in winter."

Brannic was quiet for a long moment. "How many stars?"

I did the arithmetic I'd been doing since the shelf. "Every one we've lost. All of them. Nine turns of moon, and the sky's been black the whole time — that's not a famine, Brannic, that's a *harvest*. Someone gathered the entire harvest of the sky into one room, and the room is a man."

The compass burned against my leg through the pocket. Pointing up. Pointing in. Toward the vault, or toward whatever part of me the vault wanted. I tried not to think about which.

* * *

**Brannic**

Here's a thing about Vesper Vane nobody tells you, because nobody who knows them is alive to tell it: they talk about horror like it's a surveying problem. *The stars are hoarded in a man.* Said the way you'd say *the bridge is out, we'll need the ford.* I used to find it cold. Somewhere on the Grey Stair I understood it was the opposite of cold — it was a person holding themselves together with professional language the way you'd bind a wound with whatever's on hand, and if they ever stopped talking like a cartographer, whatever was underneath was going to come out, and I did not want to be standing there when it did.

The Warden's Door was exactly where Sable's notes promised: a slab of black stone set in the north face, carved with a crown, and around the crown a ring of empty sockets like a setting missing its jewels. Star-sockets. I'd have bet my ship on it.

Vesper stood before it with the compass out, and the needle — the broken, lying, memory-pointing needle — swung once and settled, pointing straight at the crown carved in the door.

"Opens for the crown's own," Vesper read. Their voice was steady. Their hands were not. "That's me. That's what the flashbacks have been telling me. White gloves, an oath. I was the crown's own."

"You were," I said. "Past tense. You ran. There's a difference between a key and the thing that cut the lock."

"Is there?" They looked at me, and for once there was no surveying-problem voice at all. Just a person, asking. "Brannic, if the door knows my hands — if everything up here knows my hands — then maybe I didn't run far enough. Maybe I was always walking back."

I've lied to customs men, to captains, to a countess on her wedding night. I opened my mouth to lie to Vesper — *you ran clean, you're free, the past is a country you emigrated from* — and what came out instead was the truth, because apparently nine turns of the moon will do that to a man.

"Then we walk in, we do the job, and we walk out, and if the past wants a word with you, it can take it up with me. I've thrown better men than the past out of worse bars than this."

Something moved behind Vesper's face. Not quite a smile. The place where a smile keeps its coat.

"Put your hand on the crown," I said. "Let's see if the door remembers you kindly."

* * *

**Vesper**

I put my bare hand on the carved crown, and the mountain remembered me.

Not the door — the *mountain*. The frost rings shivered. The silver veins in the hanging stone brightened, all of them, a thousand threads of stolen starlight pulsing once like a heartbeat, and the Warden's Door ground open with a sound like a word being unsaid. Cold air poured out, smelling of dust and moonlight and — I cannot describe this any other way — of *remembering*. The air inside the citadel was thick with memory the way marsh air is thick with rot. You could taste other people's childhoods.

"Charming hospitality," Brannic muttered, and drew his knife, which is Brannic's version of a prayer.

We stepped through, and the door sealed behind us, and I want it noted for whatever record survives that I did not flinch. Much.

Inside, the citadel was not dark. That was the horror of it. Every hall we crossed was lit — lit from within the walls, the silver veins running bright through the stone, and the light wasn't the citadel's. I know light. I've mapped it by every name the sky has for it. The Lantern's gold. The Sisters' cold blue. The Thief's Eye's red flicker. They were all here, all flowing through the walls around us, a whole sky's worth of light in captivity, and it moved the way prisoners move in a yard: slowly, in circles, together and alone.

We came out onto a gallery — a balcony of pale stone hanging over an interior vast beyond sense, the inside of the inverted keep, and I stopped drawing. I want that recorded too. Vesper Vane, who sketches ambushes mid-ambush, stood with an empty hand.

Below us, filling the keep's hollow like water fills a cup, were the stars.

Not images of them. Not reflections. *Them.* Hundreds of points of light drifting in slow suspension in the dark air of the keep, each one burning its own true color, and around each one a faint shell of frost — the same rings that were climbing the mountain outside, writ small, one ring per star, one turn of the moon per ring. Counting them down. Keeping them. And at the center of the drifting constellation, far below, on a throne of black stone at the crown-point of the inverted world, sat a small still figure wearing a shape of light that moved and fed and *waited*.

The crown. And the king beneath it, so far away I couldn't see his face, only the posture — the terrible patient posture of a man who has been sitting still for a very long time because the thing on his head prefers him that way.

"That's the whole sky," Brannic said quietly. "That's every star there ever was, in one room."

"And four more turns," I said, "and it finishes him, and then it starts on us. On everything. Sable said it — a crown binds by holding. It's been holding the sky. When the king's spent, it'll need a bigger vault."

Brannic looked at me. "How do you fight a thing that owns the night?"

"You don't fight it," I said, and the compass was hot in my pocket, and the memory behind my eyes — white gloves, an oath, my own handwriting in a margin I couldn't read — pressed close as a hand on my shoulder. "You *unbind* it. That's what the king's message said. Don't save me. Unbind me."

"And do you know how?"

I looked down at the captive stars, at the small hunched king, at the crown that had hoarded a heaven and was still hungry, and I heard myself answer in a voice that did not feel entirely like mine — older, surer, the voice of the person I'd been before I cut myself out of my own life.

"Yes," I said. "I think I built the lock."

Far below, on the black throne, the crown turned — slowly, deliberately, the way a great beast turns its head — and every star in the hollow flickered at once, and I understood that it had noticed us coming, and that it had been expecting us for a very long time.

* * *

**Brannic**

The crown looked at us. I don't care what Vesper says about crowns not having eyes. It *looked*, the whole room of stolen sky bent toward us like grass under wind, and every hair I own stood up and filed a complaint.

"Well," I said, and checked my knife, because a man should do something with his hands. "It knows we're here. Any chance it's shy?"

"No," Vesper said, already moving toward the stair that spiraled down into the hollow, into the dark, into the middle of a caged sky, walking like a person walking home. "It's not shy. It's *patient*. There's a difference, and it's worse."

I followed. Of course I followed. Nine turns of the moon I'd been following this disaster of a cartographer, and the moon had four turns left, and I'd be lying if I said I knew which of us was leading.

The stair went down, and the stars closed overhead like a lid.
