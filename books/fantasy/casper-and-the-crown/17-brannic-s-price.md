## 17. Brannic's Price

I've been robbed before. Professionally, I mean — it's a hazard of the trade, and I like to think I take it with grace. There was a fence in Marrowgate who lifted my entire winter cargo while shaking my hand. There was a customs man in Pell who confiscated my boat and then had the decency to look embarrassed about it. I have lost gold, goods, a ship called the *Kind Regret* (the name was aspirational), and once, briefly, my dignity in a card game against a bishop.

None of that prepared me for watching a woman made of frost offer Vesper Vane everything they'd ever lost, and watching Vesper say no.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Brannic rule one: tell it in order, or you'll lie by accident.

* * *

We'd made the citadel's shadow by the ninth turn of the moon, which sounds impressive until you understand what the ninth turn does to a body. The cold down in the valley wasn't weather anymore. It was arithmetic. Every turn added a ring of frost, and every ring took something — feeling from your fingers first, then your toes, then whatever part of you still believed spring was a real thing that happened to real people.

We holed up in the ruins of a shepherd's bothy halfway up the last ridge, and I did what I do best: I built a fire out of things that weren't meant to be burned. Fence posts, mostly. A door. A chair that some shepherd had probably sat in for forty years watching his flock, and which now gave us maybe three hours of heat. I like to think he'd have approved. Shepherds understand sacrifice. They spend their whole lives trading their comfort for something dumber than them.

Vesper sat by the window with the broken compass in their lap and didn't talk. That was normal, by then. The compass pointed at memories instead of north, and lately it had been pointing at Vesper like a dog pointing at its own tail. They'd gone quiet in the way people go quiet when they're carrying more than they can lift and are too proud to put any of it down.

I talked enough for both of us. That was our arrangement. It had always been our arrangement, back when we were just the smuggler and the mapmaker splitting profits on routes through Thornfell, and it was our arrangement now that we were two half-frozen idiots climbing toward a crown that ate stars.

"You know what I miss?" I said, feeding the chair to the fire leg by leg, like amputating it. "Wine. Real wine. Not this vinegar they brew in Hollowmere that tastes like a churchyard feels."

Vesper grunted.

"And I miss being warm. And I miss not being on a first-name basis with frostbite. And I miss — " I paused, because the next one was true and true ones cost more. "I miss knowing where I stood with you."

That got a look. Not much of one. Vesper's looks were rationed like everything else on this mountain. But they turned from the window, and the compass needle trembled in their lap, and they said, "You know where you stand with me, Brannic."

"Do I?" I said. "Because here's where I stand: I broke a man out of a dungeon for you. I watched him forget his own daughter's face while a jailer recited it back to him like a prayer. I traded away the location of every cache I own between here and the coast to buy your life, and I'd do it again, and the thing that scares me isn't the debt. The thing that scares me is that I don't know what you're planning to pay it with."

"I'm going to unbind the king," Vesper said. Flat as a surveyed line.

"Aye. And then what? Crowns don't come off, love. Sable told us that himself. You bind it or it binds you, and either way somebody's paying." I jabbed a stick into the fire. "So let's hear the price."

Vesper looked at the compass. The compass, traitorous little thing, pointed steadily at Vesper's own chest.

"That's what I thought," I said, and left it there, because pushing is for fences and I needed Vesper whole for another four turns of moon.

* * *

The crown came for us at midnight. Of course it did. Everything on this mountain ran on midnight; it was the only hour the dark trusted.

It didn't send soldiers this time. It didn't need to. The frost on the window thickened, and the frost became a shape, and the shape became a woman — tall, grey-white, her hair moving like smoke underwater, her eyes the blue you see at the bottom of a glacier right before it kills you. She stood in the doorway of the bothy without opening the door, which is the sort of trick that makes even an old criminal's skin crawl, and she spoke in a voice like snow settling on snow.

"Cartographer," she said. "And the smuggler. How convenient. I have terms for both of you."

"We don't deal with crowns," I said. "Bad credit history."

The frost-woman ignored me, which stung, but fair. Her eyes never left Vesper. "I know what you intend. Unbinding. You believe you can reach the throne room before the thirteenth turn completes. Perhaps you can. I am not here to stop you." Something moved across her frozen face that might, on a living woman, have been a smile. "I am here to make you a better offer."

"Here it comes," I muttered.

"Every prisoner in my cells," the crown said. "Every soul I hold. The mapped and the unmapped alike. The nameless ones who have forgotten their faces — they will have them back. Their names. Their mothers. Their children. All of it returned, whole, tonight. In exchange—" and here her voice went gentle, which was worse, "—you will serve me. As you served me before. You will map my hunger, Cartographer. You will chart the roads by which I feed, and I will never hunger past what you draw for me. The realm keeps its borders. The king keeps his sleep. And everyone I have taken comes home."

The fire crackled. Somewhere outside, wind moved through dead heather, making a sound like whispering.

I looked at Vesper. And here is the thing I will carry to whatever grave accepts me: so help me, for one full breath, I wanted them to say yes.

Because I'd been in those cells. I'd walked those corridors under escort, hands bound, listening to the jailer recite names to prisoners who stared at him like he was reading letters addressed to someone else. I'd seen a woman ask the wall who she was, politely, twice, in case the wall had changed its mind. I'd seen a boy — fourteen, fifteen — who couldn't remember his own mother and cried anyway, because grief apparently survives memory the way a scar survives the knife. I'd traded my caches to get Vesper out of that place, and I'd have traded my other caches, and my ship-that-wasn't, and my teeth, if it came to it.

And now the crown was offering to open every cell. Every one. Including the nameless. Including the boy.

All it cost was Vesper.

* * *

Let me tell you how a smuggler thinks, since everyone assumes it's all greed and gutting knives. A smuggler is an accountant of the impossible. We price things. That's the whole trade. You look at a cargo, a risk, a road, and you put a number on it, and the number is usually ugly and you live with it, because living with ugly numbers is what separates professionals from corpses.

So I priced it. Sitting there in the chair-fire light with frost growing up the inside of the windows, I priced it like any cargo.

Cost: Vesper Vane. One cartographer, used, stubborn, prone to self-sacrifice, currently owned by no one. Value: incalculable, but let's be honest, mostly to me.

Return: every prisoner freed. The realm spared. The valley unfrozen. The boy gets his mother's face back. The woman stops asking walls. Aldric sleeps on, dreaming whatever kings dream, and nobody has to die, and nobody has to forget, except the one person volunteering.

Any honest accountant signs that contract. Any honest accountant would already be reaching for the quill.

The problem — the whole rotten problem — is that I'd stopped being honest somewhere around chapter nine, when I traded my caches for a person instead of a profit, and I hadn't noticed until right now, sitting across from Vesper Vane, hating them, hating the crown, hating arithmetic itself.

"Well?" said the frost-woman.

"Don't answer yet," I said, and my voice came out wrong, gravel over glass. "Vesper. Listen to me. Listen to the numbers."

"They're not numbers, Brannic."

"Everything's numbers." I stood up. My knees cracked like the fence posts I'd burned. "Thirty-some souls in those cells. Maybe more. Maybe hundreds by now — the crown's been collecting unmapped folk since the wards fell. Hundreds of people who get their lives back tonight if you sign. Against that: you. One person, who signed on for this climb knowing the bill might come due, and here it is, itemized, and it's cheaper than anyone feared." I spread my hands. "Take the deal."

The compass in Vesper's lap swung. Pointed at me, then. Then back at them.

"Take the deal," I said again, louder, because I could feel the yes dying in the room and I was trying to resuscitate it. "You want to be noble? This *is* the noble option. This is nobility at a discount. You serve a crown that — fine, fine, it ate the stars, it's monstrous, I'm not defending it — but it's offering terms, Vesper. Terms! Monsters don't offer terms. And the terms are *everyone goes free.*"

"Except me."

"One of you versus all of them. Even you can do that sum."

Vesper stood too. Slowly. They held the broken compass in one hand and their knuckles were white around it, and their face in the firelight was the face of someone standing on a bridge deciding whether the river or the rope is lying to them.

"The crown doesn't keep bargains," they said quietly. "It edits them."

"It edited *you,*" I shot back, "and you're still standing here arguing with me, which proves it does sloppy work—"

"It erased my whole life, Brannic. Do you understand what it's actually buying? Not a cartographer. A cartographer who agrees that erasing people is acceptable. It wants me to become the thing that did this to me, with my signature on it." Vesper's voice didn't rise. That was the worst part. It stayed level, surveyed, mapped. "And the prisoners — you think it frees them? It *holds* memory. That's what it *is.* It doesn't release anything it holds. It'll give them back borrowed shapes of themselves, long enough to walk out the gate, and then it will own them twice over, because they'll owe their selves to it. It's not selling freedom. It's selling the *story* of freedom, and it needs my hand to draft the map."

"You don't know that."

"No," Vesper admitted. "I don't. I know nothing. That's rather the theme of my life." They looked down at the compass, then up at me, and something in their expression made my chest hurt in a way frostbite never managed. "But I know you, Brannic Odo Marsh. I know you traded every cache you own for a person you claimed was just a fare. I know you sat up three nights with the jailer's list of names memorizing it, because you said somebody should remember them even if the crown doesn't. Don't tell me to become the crown, and then act surprised when I refuse."

The frost-woman watched us like weather.

"You have until the fire dies," she said.

* * *

We argued. Gods, how we argued. I threw the numbers at them and they threw the truth back, and neither of us landed a clean blow because we were fighting different wars. I was fighting for the prisoners. So were they. That's the filthy joke of it — we wanted the same thing and it tore us apart anyway, because wanting the same thing isn't the same as agreeing on the price, and friendship, I have learned late and expensively, dies less often from betrayal than from arithmetic done in front of each other.

"Say I refuse," Vesper said at last. "Say we climb on, and I fail. The thirteenth turn comes. The crown finishes eating Aldric, and then it eats the realm. Everyone in those cells starves or freezes or worse. Is that your preferred ledger?"

"At least the ledger stays honest!"

"Honest," Vesper repeated softly. "You sound like Sable."

"Sable's dead, probably. Sable lived alone on a mountain explaining things to goats. Let's not canonize him."

"He told me the crown can only be unbound by giving it a memory it must keep forever. He told me there was a third road. Not service, not destruction. *Unbinding.*"

"He told you a riddle. Riddles are what wise men leave behind when they don't want to be blamed for the outcome."

"And you'd have me take the certain road instead of the possible one?"

"I'd have you take the road where fewer people bleed!" I heard my own voice crack and hated it. "Gods' teeth, Vesper, I carried you out of a dungeon. I'm not — I did not climb this mountain to watch you volunteer for a lifetime of it. There are other cartographers. There will be other chances. Take the deal, buy the realm time, and we *find* the third road from inside — you, alive, at the crown's side, with your memories back, looking for Sable's loophole with resources. That's strategy. That's the smuggler's play."

"And if there is no loophole? If I map its hunger and it grows past every line I draw?" Vesper stepped closer. The firelight caught the frost on their eyelashes; they'd been crying, or the cold had been doing it for them. "Then I've built the cage that holds the world, Brannic. With my hands. Again. Because that's what I did before, isn't it? That's what the compass keeps dragging me back to. I mapped the feeding routes. I built this. Me. And you're asking me to do it again and call it mercy."

The fire settled. A fence post collapsed into embers.

And there it was. The thing underneath all my shouting, laid bare between us like a bone through skin: Vesper remembered — in fragments, in flashbacks, in white-gloved hands and an oath they couldn't place — that they had helped forge this darkness. And I had been asking them to walk back into it, and calling it strategy, and maybe part of me had even believed that, and the rest of me had just been unable to watch them choose the cliff.

"Vesper," I said, quieter. "I'm not asking you to save yourself. I'm asking you to let me save you. It's different work. Worse pay. But it's mine."

They closed their eyes. "I know," they whispered. "I know, Brannic. And I can't."

The fire died.

The frost-woman unfolded her hands. "Then we are concluded," she said, and there was no anger in it, which somehow made it obscene. "The offer stands until the twelfth turn. When you kneel — and you will, Cartographer, they all do eventually — I will reopen negotiations." Her gaze slid to me, and the temperature dropped hard enough to ache in my fillings. "As for you, smuggler. You have given me much information freely of late. I have decided to repay you. Consider it a gift."

"What gift?" I asked, and my mouth had gone dry as old paper.

She smiled with a face that had never learned how. "In three days, you will learn what I have done with the prisoners you care about most. I find that gifts land best when the recipient has time to anticipate them." And she poured herself backward into the frost on the window and was gone, leaving only cold and the smell, faintly, of starlight burnt down to ash.

* * *

We didn't speak for a long while after. I rebuilt the fire from the last unburned fence post, and Vesper sat with the compass, and the mountain creaked around us like a ship taking water.

It was Vesper who finally broke it. "You meant it," they said. Not accusing. Just checking, the way you check a knot. "You wanted me to take it."

"I priced it honestly," I said. "First honest pricing I've done in years. Felt terrible. Wouldn't recommend it."

"And now?"

I fed the fire and watched it catch. "Now I'm thinking about what the frost-bitch said. About her gift. Three days." I looked up. "Vesper — the twelfth turn is six days off. Why would she give us three days to learn something? Why give a warning at all?"

Vesper went very still. I watched the thought move through them like a current under ice.

"Because she doesn't think it's a warning," they said slowly. "She thinks it's a *leverage.* Something she believes will break me before the thirteenth turn." Their grip tightened on the compass until I heard the casing creak. "Which means there's something about those prisoners she doesn't want me thinking clearly about. Which means—"

"Which means whatever she's hiding," I finished, "is worth climbing faster for." I stood, knees screaming, and started kicking dirt over the fire. "Get your boots on, mapmaker. We're walking tonight."

"In this cold?"

"Especially in this cold." I grinned at them, and it felt rusty, like a gate after a long winter, but it worked. "Rule two of smuggling, Vesper: when a monster gives you a deadline, treat it as a map. She just told us exactly where she's weak."

Vesper shouldered their pack. At the door of the ruined bothy they paused, and without looking back said, "For what it's worth — I'm sorry I wouldn't take the deal."

"For what it's worth," I said, "so am I. Ask me again tomorrow and I'll probably mean it differently."

And we stepped out into the dark below the stolen stars, toward whatever the crown had spent three days preparing for us — and I swear on the *Kind Regret's* nonexistent grave that the broken compass, clutched in Vesper's fist, was trembling. Not spinning. Trembling. Like a heart.

Pointing at the citadel. Pointing at the cells beneath it.

Pointing home.
