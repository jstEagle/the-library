## 19. The Thirteenth Turn

### VESPER

The throne room had no roof.

That should have been the first thing anyone told me, and no one had, and I resented it the way you resent a map that leaves out the one river you're standing in. Above us, where rafters or vaulting ought to have been, there was only the sky — the starless, scraped-clean sky — and the moon, hanging low and swollen, its final ring of frost already blooming across its face like a stain spreading through silk.

The twelfth turn was nearly done.

Brannic stood at the base of the dais steps, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a man who has argued himself hoarse and lost on purpose. "Last chance," he said. "You walk out of here, we take the coast road, we drink something terrible in some port where nobody's ever heard of a crown that eats the sky. I'll even carry your bag."

"You hate my bag."

"I hate what's in your bag. The bag itself is fine. The bag has never betrayed anyone."

"The bag led us into an ambush."

"The bag was *compromised*," Brannic said, with the wounded dignity of a man defending a colleague. "There's a difference."

I almost laughed. That was the trick of Brannic — he could put a laugh in your mouth at the exact moment you needed somewhere to put the terror. I would remember that. I intended to remember everything, one last time, which was rather the point.

Because the moon was about to finish its turn, and I was about to hand over every memory I owned.

Aldric lay on the throne itself — not beside it, not beneath it, but *in* it, sunk into the stone like a coin pressed into soft wax. The crown sat on his head and it did not look like gold. It looked like a hole in the shape of gold. Around it, faint as breath on glass, the stolen stars turned in slow orbits, hundreds of them, thousands, a galaxy swallowed and kept. They were beautiful. That was the worst part. The crown was beautiful the way a famine is quiet.

I climbed the dais.

"Vesper." Brannic's voice had lost its humor. "Sable said *give it a memory it must keep*. She didn't say give it everything."

"Sable also said the crown can't be destroyed, only out-holded. Out-*held*. Whatever." My hands were shaking. I made fists of them. "It's been holding the king's memories for thirteen turns and it's still hungry. A scrap won't feed it. A scrap will just buy us a fortnight and a very well-fed crown. It needs a whole life. It needs a life it can't finish digesting."

"And yours is the one on offer because—"

"Because I built its feeding roads." The words came out flat and clean, like stones laid in a wall. "I mapped the routes by which it drinks starlight and memory out of this realm. Every pass, every ley, every shrine. I drew the veins. It's my hand in all of this, Brannic, even the parts I paid to forget. Especially those." I looked down at Aldric's sleeping face — young under the dust of years, exhausted in the way of a man who has been dreaming of his own unraveling. "So it's my debt. And a debt should be paid with the whole purse, not the loose change."

Brannic was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice had gone rough. "You know what you're asking me to watch?"

"Yes."

"You know I'll be standing right here, and afterward you'll look at me like I'm a stranger, and I'll have to decide whether to be one?"

"I know."

"Fine." He scrubbed a hand down his face. "Fine. But I want it noted — *noted*, for posterity — that this is the worst toll I have ever paid on any road, and I once paid a troll in teeth."

"Noted," I said. "Posterity is listening."

Posterity, as it happened, was the crown.

I felt it the moment I set my foot on the top step — that cold, patient attention turning toward me like a lighthouse beam. The frost on the moon thickened. Somewhere below us in the citadel, the last of the hoarded stars shivered in their orbits.

*Cartographer,* it said, and its voice was not a sound but a subtraction, a place where sound should have been and wasn't. *You come to bargain.*

"Not to bargain," I said. "Bargaining implies you get something you didn't already have."

*Everything is something I do not yet have. That is the nature of hunger.*

"Then here." I pulled off my gloves — Brannic's gloves, actually, borrowed in Hollowmere a lifetime and four turns ago — and pressed my bare palms against the arms of the throne, one on either side of the sleeping king. The stone was cold as the inside of a star. "Every memory. All of it. The childhood I've been reconstructing from other people's stories. The observatory. The oath in the white gloves. The maps I drew for you. The night I ran. The night I chose to forget, and every night since. The guilt — *especially* the guilt. It's the heaviest thing I own and I am so tired of carrying it."

*And what do you ask in return?*

"Nothing. That's the point. That's what makes it a holding and not a meal." I leaned closer, until I could see myself reflected — small, warped — in the dark curve of the crown. "You bind by keeping. So keep. Keep all of it, forever, perfectly, the way you were forged to keep the realm's memories safe before someone — before *I* — taught you to eat instead. You don't get to digest this. You don't get to use it. You hold it, whole and undiminished, for as long as there is a moon to turn and a sky to hang stars in. That is your work now. That is your *purpose*. A crown that holds one life faithfully instead of devouring a kingdom badly."

The silence that followed had texture. It had *weight*. The frost on the moon crept a finger's width further, and I thought, absurdly, *it's considering the offer*, and then I thought, *of course it is — I taught it to consider things. I wrote its appetite into the passes. Some of its patience is mine.*

*You will be empty,* the crown said. Not mocking. Almost — almost — gentle. *You will not know why you climbed. You will not know his face. You will stand in a world you saved and be a stranger to your own name.*

"Yeah," I said, and my voice cracked on it, and I let it. "But he'll be awake. And the stars will come home. And somewhere in whatever I am afterward, there'll be a map with a blank in the middle of it, and I'll draw around the blank, and that will have to be enough." I swallowed. "Besides. You're forgetting something."

*Am I.*

"You'll know everything I ever was. Every stubborn, petty, cartographically obsessive thing. You'll hold me so completely that some part of me will *always* exist — just in you. That's more immortality than most people get. Try not to ruin it."

The moon's last ring closed.

And the crown said: *Accepted.*

### ALDRIC

I had been dreaming of my mother's hands for thirteen turns, and in the dream I could never see her face, and in the dream this did not seem strange, which was the cruelest trick of all. The cold thing in the crown had eaten her face first. It had started with the things I loved, because those were softest, easiest to swallow. Taxes it left me. Tax ledgers, grain quotas, the exact wording of the Border Accord of my grandfather's reign — the crown had no taste for those, so they sat in my skull like furniture in a flooded house, preserved and useless.

I had learned, over the turns, to drift toward the surface of my own mind the way a drowning man learns which way is up. Mostly there was nothing there to rise *to*. Mostly the surface was the crown, and the crown was hunger wearing my shape.

But tonight — tonight something was different.

Tonight there was a crack in the ceiling of my prison, and through the crack came a voice I did not know, bargaining with my jailer as though hunger were a merchant and memory a shipment of cloth.

I pressed myself toward the crack. Thirteen turns of practice, and all of it for this: I put what was left of me — the tax ledgers, the grain quotas, one fragment of my mother's laugh, hidden so deep even the crown had missed it — and I *pushed*.

*You will be empty,* the crown was saying, to the voice, to the cartographer. *You will not know why you climbed.*

*Yeah,* the voice said. *But he'll be awake.*

*He,* I thought. *He. That's me. I'm the he.*

*Accepted,* said the crown.

And the crack became a door, and the door became a *tearing*, and thirteen turns of held breath went out of me all at once.

I woke the way a river wakes when the ice breaks — not gently, not all at once, but in groaning, cracking, irresistible motion. The first thing I felt was my own name, which I had not been able to reach in so long that I said it aloud just to feel it in my mouth. "*Aldric.*"

The second thing I felt was the crown, and the crown was — *full*.

I felt it the way you feel a pack on your shoulders: heavy, but *carried*. Something vast and complete had settled into it, a whole life with edges and weight and a stubbornness that ran through it like a spine. It was not eating. For the first time since it had settled on my head, the crown was not eating. It was *holding*, the way it had been forged to hold, the way some smith with better intentions than mine had meant it to — and whatever it held, it held so fiercely, so jealously, so *completely*, that there was no room left in it for hunger.

The stolen stars shivered.

And then, one by one, they began to leave.

They rose out of the crown's dark curve like sparks off a bonfire, like a flock startled from a tree, and they went *up* — through the roofless throne room, through the scraped and starving sky — and where they went, they stayed. I watched a star settle into its old place in the north and felt something in the realm's bones click back into joint. Another. Another. The frost on the moon stopped growing. Then, slowly, like a held breath released, it began to fade.

The woman at the throne's arm was crying without any expression at all, as if the crying were happening to someone slightly behind her face.

I knew her. That was the terrible, wonderful thing — the crown's grip had broken, and everything it had taken from *me* came flooding home, and among the returning tide was her: Vesper Vane, Cartographer Royal, who had stood in this room with white gloves and a steady hand and drawn the roads of my devouring, and who had run, and who had come back. I knew all of it. I knew the guilt she had just handed over. I knew it because I had watched it being taken from her, the way you watch a thief carry your neighbor's furniture out into the street.

She would not know me at all.

I pulled the crown from my head.

It came away easily — too easily, after everything, like a key finally turned — and in my hands it was only an object, cold and heavy and *occupied*, holding its one life the way a miser holds a single perfect coin. I set it on the throne. I would decide later what to do with it. Some part of me understood that it could never be destroyed, only honored, or at least kept, and that this too was a kind of sentence.

Then I stood, on legs that had forgotten standing, thirteen turns of sleep draining out of me like water out of a breached dam, and I looked at the woman who had bought a kingdom with her own past.

She was looking at the sky. The stars were coming home in their hundreds now, and their light was coming down with them, and it fell on her upturned face, and she was saying, in a voice gone small and wondering:

"I don't — I don't know why I'm crying. Brannic. *Brannic*, why am I crying?"

And Brannic — gods keep him, whatever gods are left, and there were more of them now, you could feel it, the sky filling back up like a wound closing — Brannic came up the dais steps and put a hand on her shoulder and said, gruffly, gently, in the voice of a man paying a toll he'd argued about and lost:

"Because you won, Vesper. That's all. You just won."

She frowned at him, at the name, at the word *won*, like a cartographer studying a coastline that had moved in the night. "Vesper," she repeated, tasting it. "Is that mine?"

"It's yours," I said.

She turned. She looked at me — at the king she had climbed a mountain and broken herself open to wake — and there was nothing in her face but courtesy and confusion and the particular alertness of someone who has just discovered the map is blank and is already, helplessly, wanting to draw on it.

"I'm sorry," she said politely. "Do I know you?"

I knelt.

A king kneeling to a stranger in an empty throne room under a sky refilling with stars — it was not dignified, and I found I did not care. "No," I said. "Not anymore. But you knew me once, better than anyone alive. And everything you were is safe." I nodded at the crown on the throne, at the small fierce light it held. "It's being kept. Perfectly. Forever." I held out my hand. "So you're free to begin again. And I would be honored — *honored* — if you'd let me watch you draw the first line."

She looked at my hand for a long moment.

Then the last star came home, and the sky was full, and she said:

"First line of *what*, exactly?"

* * *

That, I would learn, was the question that would shape the rest of my reign — and, though neither of us knew it yet, the first line of a far stranger map than any she had ever drawn.
