## 21. A Map of No One

I woke to the smell of bread and ink, which are two smells that should never share a room and yet have defined my entire adult life.

The ceiling above me was timber, smoke-darkened, crossed by a beam that had been patched with a plank of clearly inferior wood. I lay still for a long moment and studied that plank, because it was the first thing in days — weeks? months? — that asked nothing of me. It was just a bad repair on an old roof. It did not want my memories back. It did not require me to remember who had taught me to draw coastlines, or why I flinched at the color white, or what I'd traded away in a throne room while a moon finished turning.

It was just wood. I decided I liked it very much.

"You're up," Brannic said from somewhere behind me. "Don't do anything dramatic. You've earned maybe a week without drama."

I turned my head. He sat by the window of what appeared to be a small room above what appeared to be a small tavern, mending a strap with the intense concentration of a man who wanted very much not to be having a conversation. Frost still furred the corners of the glass, though the frost-rings were gone now; the moon hung outside like an ordinary moon, unremarkable, doing its slow honest work. Through the gap in the shutters I could see a scatter of stars. Not all of them. Not yet. But some — three, four, a handful of stubborn lights holding their ground over the western hills, as if they'd never left.

"Those are new," I said.

"Old," Brannic said. "Returning. There's a difference. Sable would explain it, but Sable would take four hours and insult your grandmother."

"I don't know if I have a grandmother."

He set down the strap. For a moment he looked at me with an expression I couldn't read — which, given that I currently couldn't read much of anything, felt almost fair.

"You will," he said. "Or you won't, and you'll be someone else entirely, and the realm will limp along either way. Realms are good at limping." He stood, stretched until his back cracked like green kindling, and jerked his chin toward the table under the window. "There's bread. And there's — other things. Take your time. That's an order from your captain."

"You're not my captain."

"Everyone's my passenger until they pay their toll," he said, and went downstairs, and left me alone with the bread and the other things.

* * *

The other things were laid out on the table with a care that Brannic would deny under torture: a folded chart weighted at the corners with four mismatched objects — a compass (broken), a stub of red wax, a knife I recognized the way you recognize a scar, and a river stone worn smooth as a knuckle. Beside them, a sheaf of loose papers covered in handwriting.

My handwriting.

That was the strange part. Not that the papers existed — a cartographer's effects are a cartographer's biography, or so I'm told — but that when I looked at the script, I knew it was mine the way you know your own reflection in a dark window: familiar in shape, wrong in every particular. The hand slanted hard to the right. It abbreviated ruthlessly. It made decisions — *this pass is a lie in winter*, *count the cairns twice, they breed* — with a confidence I could not locate anywhere in myself.

I sat down across from my own past like a stranger at a toll booth and began to read.

*The Thornfell wards fail where the stone forgets its bedding,* said one marginal note, cramped into the margin of a chart of the northern passes. *Stone remembers longer than men. Ask the masons, not the priests.*

*I don't know any masons,* I thought. *I don't think I know any priests.*

Another page: a sketch of Hollowmere's rooftops, annotated in that same slanting hand: *Brannic's route through the tannery row is genius and he knows it, which is the problem.*

I laughed. It came out of me unexpectedly, a short bark that startled us both — me and the stranger whose hand had written it. Because I didn't remember the tannery row. I didn't remember Brannic owing me a debt, or trusting me less, or choosing loyalty over coin in a burning inn while hunters in starless armor kicked in doors. Brannic had told me these things, in fragments, on the walk down from the citadel, carefully, like a man feeding a fire one stick at a time so it wouldn't flare and die. But being told is not remembering. Being told is a map of a country you've never walked.

And this — this page, this ink, this confident slanted hand — was the walking itself. It was evidence of a person. She was clever. She was wry. She loved her work with the specific, slightly obnoxious devotion of someone who talks about drainage gradients at dinner parties. She had been afraid of things I couldn't name and brave in ways I couldn't claim.

She had also, according to a note tucked between two sheets of the smuggler's chart, mapped the feeding routes of a crown that ate starlight and memory, in exchange for — something. The note didn't say what. Just a line drawn through a sentence, hard enough to tear the paper, and beneath it, in smaller script:

*Some roads you burn behind you. Some roads you burn ahead. Choose fast.*

I put that page down gently, as if it might go off.

* * *

The trouble with reading yourself like a chart is that charts invite correction.

By the third day I had spread everything across the tavern floor — the old maps, the self-rewriting one (still now, mercifully, its ink lying quiet as a sleeping dog), Sable's notes, the pages of my own hand. I told myself I was organizing. Archiving. A professional instinct.

What I was actually doing was conducting an argument with a dead woman.

Because that's what she was, in every way that mattered. Vesper Vane, Cartographer Royal, oath-taker in white gloves, architect of the crown's hunger, the woman who climbed a mountain to give a monster the only thing it could never digest — she had spent herself paying for it, every memory down to the price of bread, and what sat cross-legged on the floor of a tavern above the valley road was not her. It was whoever came after. A blank margin. A map of no one.

"The worst part," I said aloud, to Brannic, who was pretending to inventory rope in the corner, "is that she's better than me. Look at this." I held up a sheet. "*'The Ashwater ford lies about its depth in spring. Locals know. Locals charge.'* That's funny. That's better than anything I've ever — " I gestured vaguely at myself, all of it. "I've been awake six days and my most notable quality is liking bread."

"She once talked about drainage gradients through an entire dinner," Brannic said. "You're better company already."

"How do you even know that?"

"Sable told me. While you were sleeping off the end of the world." He coiled rope with unnecessary violence. "Point is, you keep talking about her like she's someone to live up to. She isn't. She's someone you were spared from becoming twice over — first by the crown taking it, then by you giving it. Both times, that cost bought something. Stop auditing the purchase."

"That's easy for you to say. You got to keep your terrible memories."

"All forty years of them." He finally looked at me, and there it was again, that unreadable thing. "Vesper. Do you know what I'd give for one week where the worst thing I remembered was nothing?"

I didn't answer. Outside, another star came out — I'd begun keeping count, a small tally scratched into the tavern table, five, then six, each one a small private festival — and we both looked at it for a moment, the way you look at a candle in a house where the lights have been out for a long time.

"Seven," I said.

"Aye," said Brannic. "Seven."

* * *

On the ninth morning I found the letter.

It wasn't a letter, exactly. It was a half-page folded inside the backing of the old smuggler's chart, sewn there — sewn, which meant deliberately, which meant she'd known someone would eventually peel the chart apart looking for exactly this. My hands shook a little getting it open, and I noted, distantly, that the shaking was mine. Whatever else was missing, the fear was fully stocked.

*If you're reading this, it worked,* the letter began. *Or it failed, and Aldric is wearing me like a glove, in which case stop reading and run, you idiot, the west stair, always the west stair.*

I kept reading. It worked.

*So. Hello, successor.* The hand here slowed, the letters rounder, as if she'd been choosing each one. *I've thought a great deal about you — about whoever wakes up on the far side of this. I tried to leave instructions. I burned them. Then I wrote more and burned those too, and finally I understood that instructions are just another kind of map, and I have made enough people follow maps they never chose.*

*So instead, three observations. One: you'll grieve me. Don't fight it, but don't mistake it. Grieving a self you never met is just homesickness with nowhere to point. It passes if you feed it work instead of sorrow.*

*Two: Brannic will stay. He'll pretend it's about the debt. It was never about the debt. Let him pretend. It's how he loves.*

*Three:* and here the ink blurred, whether from water or pressure I couldn't say — *you are not what happened to you. I know everyone says that. But I am living proof of the opposite too: I was what happened to me, for thirty years, and look where it got the sky. So start blank. Blank is not empty. Blank is the part of the map where the cartographer gets to decide what's real.*

*Draw well. — V.V.*

I read it four times. On the fourth reading I noticed the postscript, crammed sideways up the margin in a rush:

*P.S. — the ford at Ashwater really does lie about its depth. Check it yourself. Trusting my notes is how the last one of us got into trouble.*

I laughed again, wetter this time, and Brannic appeared in the doorway with two cups of something hot and steaming and the elaborate casualness of a man who has been listening at doors since before I had a personality.

"So," he said. "She leaves good notes?"

"She leaves insufferable ones." I took the cup. "Apparently you're going to stay."

His ears went faintly pink, which on a face like weathered oak is nearly a confession. "Debt's not settled."

"What debt?"

"Exactly," he said, and drank his tea, and that was the end of the discussion, and it was the most honest conversation we'd ever had.

* * *

We stayed through the thaw. Or rather, I stayed, and Brannic stayed, and neither of us named what that arrangement was, because naming it would have required vocabulary neither of us owned.

I began to work. Not the old work — not yet, maybe not ever; my hands knew the shapes but the knowledge behind them lived in a crown somewhere, holding a kingdom's memories forever, and I found I didn't want it back. What I wanted was smaller and stranger. I wanted to walk the valley road and see what was true about it now. I wanted to ask the masons about the stone, because my own dead handwriting had told me to and because checking her notes against the world felt like a way of meeting her without grieving her.

I started a new chart. It had no borders yet. It began at the tavern door — I drew the door, the bad patch on the roof beam visible through the upper window, the well with its crooked crank — and it went outward from there at the speed of walking. The first road I drew was the one to Ashwater, because of the ford, because of the postscript, because I suspected the whole letter had been built backward from wanting me to go and check.

Brannic watched me ink the first lines with the wariness of a man observing someone handle a weapon.

"It won't rewrite itself?" he asked.

"It's not that kind of map." I blew on the ink. "This one only changes if I change."

"Huh," he said. "Terrifying."

"Yes," I agreed. "Completely."

Outside the window, the eighth star of the evening cleared the ridge, steady and white and unimpressed with itself, and below it the valley lay thawing, rivers running loud with melt, refugees become travelers become, gradually, neighbors. Somewhere north, a king in a plain iron circlet was learning to sleep without dreaming of taxes. Somewhere colder than that, a crown held everything I had been, patient as winter, keeping its one eternal memory the way it had once kept the stars.

And here — at a scarred table, over bread and ink, in a hand that slanted not at all — a new cartographer drew a door, and then the road beyond it, and then stopped, pen lifted, at the edge of everything known, which was approximately twelve feet past the well.

"Brannic," I said, "how do you feel about Ashwater?"

His grin was slow and crooked and entirely his own. "Depends who's charging at the ford."

"We are," I said, and drew the next line.
