## 22. The First New Road

The ink froze in the well twice before I got a single line down.

That's Thornfell for you. Even with the stars back — even with the sky stitched whole again like a coat someone finally bothered to mend — the mountain mornings still bite. I held the little pot over the brazier and watched the surface shiver, and I thought: *this used to be easy.* There was a time when I could have drawn the whole of the northern passes from memory, blindfolded, drunk, insulted by bandits in three dialects.

There was a time when I was somebody else entirely, apparently.

"Still fighting it?"

Brannic ducked through the doorway of the old watchtower without knocking, because Brannic has never knocked on anything in his life except possibly doors he intended to rob. He carried two cups of something steaming and smelled faintly of pine smoke and the particular stubbornness of a man who has decided to be optimistic against his own better judgment.

"Fighting what?" I said. "I'm drawing."

"You're staring at blank paper and blaming the ink."

"I'm *considering* blank paper. It's an important part of cartography. Ask any mapmaker worth their salt."

"I did. Old Sable says you're being dramatic."

Old Sable, it turned out, had not died in the starless dark after all. The crown's fall — its *unbinding*, we're supposed to call it now, though half the realm still whispers *the Night the King Was Freed* — had left the hermit cartographer blinking in his cave like a mole dragged into daylight. He'd come down from the high country a month later, declared that my maps were "sloppy but honest," which is the finest compliment anyone has ever paid me, and installed himself in Hollowmere as the town's self-appointed correction bureau. He corrects my work. He corrects Aldric's proclamations. Last week he corrected a goose.

So the paper sat there, white as frost, and I sat there, and Brannic set both cups down on the table and looked at what I hadn't drawn.

"It's supposed to be a road," I said, defensive already, which he noticed, because he notices everything except when to stop talking about it.

"A road where?"

"Here." I tapped the table. Then, because that wasn't helpful, I tapped my chest. "From here to... somewhere."

He pulled up a stool and sat across from me, forearms on knees, the way he does when he's about to say something practical and hates it. Six months of this. Six months since the citadel, since the throne room, since I walked out of there hollow as a gourd with a king stumbling behind me and stars raining home one by one like embers returning up a chimney. Six months of waking up every morning and meeting myself for the first time.

You'd think it would get easier. Some days it does. Some days I look at my hands and think, *well, they seem capable,* and leave it at that. Other days I find notes in my own handwriting — cramped, slanted-left, impatient — and feel like I'm reading letters from a stranger who knew me better than I do.

Today was a letter day. I'd found one tucked inside a chart of the southern coast, in the margin, small:

*If you're reading this, you gave it away. Don't go looking for it back. Draw forward.*

Draw forward. Fine words. I still don't know if past-me meant them kindly or just wanted future-me to shut up.

* * *

The thing nobody tells you about losing your memory on purpose is that everyone else remembers *for* you, and they all remember differently, and they all look at you sideways while doing it, waiting to see if you'll flinch.

Brannic tells me stories over dinner. He's careful with them — or as careful as a man built entirely of sharp edges can be. He tells me about the pass town and the smuggler's toll, about the raid, about the dungeon where men without names shuffled in circles while a jailer wept over everything he carried for them. He does not tell me the story the same way twice. At first I thought that meant he was lying. Then I understood: he's editing. Deciding each night how much of me I can hold. Which is its own strange kindness, and its own strange theft, and I have never worked out which one dominates.

Aldric doesn't tell me stories at all. The king — former king; he abdicated in the spring, to general astonishment and one very loud objection from a duke who was subsequently corrected by Old Sable — sits with me sometimes in silence, watching me draw, and once he said only this:

"I forgot my mother's face. You forgot everything. Between us we've learned the same lesson, I think."

"Which is?"

"That a person is not their memories. A person is what they do next."

He said it simply, the way he says most things now. The crown took more than his years of sleep; it took the polish, the performance, the whole gilded apparatus of kingship, and what climbed off that throne was a tired, honest man with frost-scars on his hands. He wears plain iron now. He had the old crown melted down — the parasite's empty shell, harmless as a dead beetle once it had a memory to hold forever — and reforged into a circlet so plain that a blacksmith apologized for it.

"It's perfect," Aldric told the apologetic blacksmith. "Power should look uncomfortable."

I asked him once, carefully, whether he remembered me. Before. Whether the man who served the crown — the Cartographer Royal who mapped its feeding routes, whose guilt is now locked in cold metal for eternity — whether that person and I were the same.

He was quiet a long time. Then: "They had your hands. And your temper. But they didn't have this." He nodded at my half-finished cup, my frozen ink, my blank paper. "Whatever you are now, you're new. The realm needed the old you gone more than it needed them kept."

High praise from a man I freed at the cost of myself. Or maybe just an honest assessment. With Aldric it's hard to tell the difference, which I suspect is exactly how he wants it.

* * *

Here's what I know about who I was, assembled from fragments, margins, other people's recollections, and one extremely detailed account from a jailer who now tends a shrine in Hollowmere and remembers nothing but his own grief, which he says is enough:

I was the crown's cartographer. I drew the roads it fed along. I told myself I was preserving knowledge, safeguarding the realm's memory, serving the sleeping king — and then one day I saw what the feeding actually looked like from the ground, a valley going grey and forgetting itself, and I ran. I cut my own memory out rather than carry the knowledge of what I'd built, because carrying it would have meant using it, and using it might have meant becoming useful to the crown again.

Past-me chose ignorance as armor. And then, months later, standing in the throne room with the thirteenth turn bearing down, past-me chose to hand over even that armor — every reclaimed memory, every crime, every hour of service and every moment of flight — to give the parasite something true enough to hold forever.

*Don't go looking for it back. Draw forward.*

The trouble with drawing forward is that paper expects a starting point.

* * *

In spring, the first refugees-turned-settlers came back to Thornfell. In summer, the passes cleared and the trade routes reopened, and I discovered that people still needed maps — real ones, honest ones, maps that didn't rewrite themselves overnight — and that my hands remembered how to make them even when my head didn't. My hands are loyal that way. They remember things my mind refuses. Sometimes mid-stroke they'll draw a contour I couldn't have named and Old Sable will squint at it and say, "Correct," in a tone of profound irritation, and I'll feel like a ghost shaking hands with its own corpse.

But this map — the one under my frozen ink, the one Brannic was watching me fail to start — this one wasn't for sale, or for the realm, or for the archive in Hollowmere.

It was just mine. And I had no idea what went on it.

"Start with the tower," Brannic said.

"This isn't a survey."

"Everything's a survey. You taught me that." He sipped his drink. "Tower. Well. The pine with the lightning scar. Sable's cave. The road to Hollowmere. Put down what's actually around you, and see what shape it makes."

"That's not a life, Brannic, that's a—"

"A map." He shrugged. "You keep saying you don't know who you are. So mark where you've been these six months and read it back. That's how you always found out where anything was. Walking toward it and writing it down."

I wanted to argue. Instead I dipped the pen.

The first line was the watchtower, square and stubborn, with the brazier marked as a small angry dot. Then the path down to the stream where I wash my brushes and pretend to fish. Then the pine — lightning-scarred, half-dead, still going — because Brannic was right, and I hated it, and the line came anyway. Then the track south toward Hollowmere, and I let my pen wander along it further than my eye had planned, past the fork, past the shrine where the jailer keeps his candles, out toward the low hills where—

Where the road bent, and my hand stopped.

Not hesitated. Stopped. The way it used to stop, according to Brannic, whenever I'd found something true on the page.

"What?" he said.

"I don't know." I looked at the line. It curved around a hill that didn't need curving around, toward a valley that had no settlement and no reason to attract anyone, and my hand had drawn it anyway, sure as breathing. "My hands know something I don't."

"They usually do." He leaned over, studying it. "What's down there?"

"Nothing. Farmland. An old waystation, maybe." I frowned. "Or—" And there it was again, that flicker behind my eyes, not a memory, never a memory, just the *shape* of one, like hearing footsteps in a house you thought was empty. "Or something past-me thought mattered."

Brannic sat back. "We could ride out tomorrow."

"We?"

"I've got nowhere better to be." He said it lightly, but we both knew he'd turned down three lucrative contracts this year to stay within a day's ride of a forgetful cartographer, and neither of us had ever acknowledged it aloud, because acknowledging it would require vocabulary neither of us owned. "Besides. If your old self hid something in a valley, I want first pick of whatever's buried."

"That's very romantic."

"I'm a smuggler. Romance and larceny share a border."

* * *

Evening came down the mountain in stages — gold, then violet, then the deep clean blue that still stops my heart some nights, because for one terrible year there was no blue at all, no stars, just a ceiling of nothing. I stepped out onto the tower's broken balcony with my unfinished map and watched the sky do its slow, extravagant work.

The stars come back in order. That's the strange part. Astronomers — actual astronomers, suddenly employed again after a year of professional obsolescence — confirmed it: the crown released them in reverse order of consumption, oldest first, like a ledger being unwound. The last ones returned in early autumn, and the sky has held ever since. Aldric watches them every clear night from the porch of the modest house he insists on living in, down in the valley among people who mostly treat him as a neighbor with an interesting past. Bounded. Chosen. Crowned in iron he can take off whenever he likes.

Some nights I climb down and stand next to him and we say nothing together, which is the best conversation either of us gets.

Tonight I stayed up top. I laid the new map flat on the balustrade, weighted the corners with stones, and looked at what my day had made: a tower, a stream, a scarred pine, a road bending toward a mystery my hands wouldn't explain.

It wasn't much. It was, I realized, exactly as much as I actually have — and unlike every chart I'd ever drawn before, nothing on it was borrowed. No inherited borders. No routes designed by something hungry. Just places I'd walked since becoming whoever I am now.

*Draw forward.*

"All right," I told past-me, out loud, to the empty air, the way I've started doing when the margin-notes get bossy. "Forward."

And then — because the evening was warm, and the ink was finally behaving, and a king below was watching his stars hold steady, and a smuggler was probably downstairs stealing my good boots — I drew the next line.

It curved down off the edge of the known map, into the blank space at the bottom of the page, toward the valley my hand had chosen, and beside it, in my own unfamiliar handwriting, I wrote the only label that seemed honest:

*Road begins here.*

Far off, down where the road bent around the hill, a light flickered in the dark where no light should be. One flash. Then gone.

Brannic appeared at my shoulder with the boots — my boots, unrepentant — and followed my gaze to the empty valley.

"Tomorrow, then," he said.

"Tomorrow," I agreed, and reached for fresh ink.

The stars held. The road waited. And for the first time since I woke up as a stranger in my own life, the blank space ahead of me didn't look like loss.

It looked like territory.
