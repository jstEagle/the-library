## 1. The Child Who Spoke First

The charms Odile sold were lies, mostly.

That was the trick of it—Greyhollow's law forbade magic but permitted superstition, and so she sat beneath her striped awning in Tallow Market selling knotted red thread for colicky infants and river-stones drilled through with holes to hang over doorways against bad dreams. The knots were ordinary knots. The stones were ordinary stones. She spoke her little incantations in the flat market-tongue of the city, words that meant nothing, that had never meant anything, and the customers paid their coppers and went away comforted, and everyone was safe.

She had been Marren the charm-wife for ten years. Marren had a limp she affected on cold mornings and a dead husband she had invented in careful detail—a cooper, drowned in a flood, poor soul—and hands that trembled only when no one was looking. Ten years of being small and dull and harmless. Ten years of silence, which was its own kind of prayer, though she would have bitten her tongue black before admitting it.

The morning the witch-finders came to Tallow Market began like any other: fog off the canal thick enough to chew, the fishmongers shouting, a beggar child stealing an apple from her table because he always did, every third day, and she always let him. She was wrapping a knot of thread for a woman whose daughter was to be married when the shouting changed pitch.

Not alarm. Not yet. Something worse than alarm—*attention*. The particular hush that fell over a crowd when it decided, all at once, to become invisible.

Odile looked up from her thread.

They came down the market row four abreast, grey cloaks buttoned to the throat, their faces behind lacquered masks shaped like closed eyes. The witch-finders of King Aldric did not carry swords. They carried long silver wands tipped with hollow glass, and as they walked they touched those tips to things—the awning posts, the fishmonger's scale, the forehead of a sleeping drunk—and the glass filled faintly with light or it did not. When it did not, they moved on. When it did—

A pot-seller two stalls down flared green in the glass. Odile watched them take him by both arms, watched him protest in a voice cracking with bewilderment—he was nothing, he was a *pot-seller*, his wife made the glazes—and watched the crowd look away in one practiced motion, ten thousand pairs of eyes suddenly fascinated by their own shoes.

Her own hands were steady. That was the horror of it. A decade of hiding had taught her body to be calm while her mind screamed.

*They are not sweeping randomly.* She knew this the way she knew weather coming off the sea. The sweep moved in a spiral, tight and deliberate, working inward toward a point. Toward *her* end of the market. Someone had given them a bearing. Some thread of the old divinity had flickered somewhere near Greyhollow, and Mauveth's hounds were casting for it the way sharks cast for blood.

She packed slowly. Never run; running is a confession. She wrapped the threads, stacked the stones, folded the canvas, all with the unhurried hands of Marren the charm-wife, while inside her chest something very old and very frightened pressed its face against the walls of her ribs and whispered in a language she had sworn never to answer.

*Vael,* it whispered. *They're hunting what's left of Vael.*

"Fine day for it," said the fishmonger beside her, meaning nothing, meaning everything.

"Every day's fine if you keep your head down," Odile said, and limped home through the fog.

* * *

Her rooms above the dye-shop smelled of mordant and tallow, three narrow floors of someone else's life. She lit no lamp. She stood at the window watching the witch-finders' lanterns move through the streets below in their patient spiral, closer now, and she took stock of herself the way a soldier takes inventory before a battle she already knows is lost.

One knife. One vial of true consecrated oil, hidden in the heel of her boot for ten years, never used, never poured out, never *spent*—hoarded the way a miser hoards coin that could have bought bread. Ten years of unspoken prayers dammed up behind her teeth. Enough devotion to light a candle, if she dared. Not enough to matter.

Below, the lanterns paused outside the dye-shop.

Odile stepped back from the window. Her heart did not race; it simply stopped, politely, the way it had learned to.

Then came the knocking.

Not at the shop door below. At her door. Her own door, three floors up, where no one knocked, where Marren the charm-wife received no visitors after dark. Three small knocks. Then a pause. Then three more, spaced wrong—not the rhythm of a fist but of something counting, something reciting, and Odile knew that rhythm the way you know your own heartbeat: *knock, knock, knock; and silence, which is also speech.* It was the pattern of the Ninth Hour vigil. It was how a postulant summoned her superior to prayer.

No one living used that pattern. No one living *remembered* it.

She opened the door with the knife already in her hand.

The child standing in her doorway was perhaps seven years old, thin as a winter branch, barefoot despite the season, with mud to the knees and a cut over one eyebrow gone brown with dried blood. His hair was the pale gold of old straw. His eyes were the problem. They held a light that did not belong in any living face—low and banked and orange, like coals seen through a grate, and when he blinked the light did not blink with him.

He looked at the knife without fear. He looked at her face, and tilted his head, the way a bird considers something bright.

"You've been hiding," he said.

In Vaelish. In the pre-kingdom liturgical tongue, the language of the silent god, the language Odile had sworn on her knees before a drowning altar that she would never speak again—and here it was falling out of a child's mouth whole and perfect, each vowel carrying the weight of a thousand years of dead priests.

"I don't know what you mean," she answered in market-tongue. "You have the wrong house, little one."

The coal-light in his eyes flared, gently, reproachful.

"Eriksen-of-the-Ninth-Vow."

Her name. Not Marren. Not the name she had worn like borrowed clothing for a decade, but her name—her *true* name, the name given at her swearing, spoken in the old grammar that bound title to soul. No one alive knew it. She had buried it with her sisters. She felt it land in her chest like a stone dropped down a well, falling and falling, striking water somewhere very deep.

"How do you—" Her voice failed. She tried again, and it came out in Vaelish, traitor tongue, the words rising in her throat unbidden after ten years of drowning them: "*Who told you my name?*"

"No one." The child swayed on his feet. Up close she could see he was shaking, exhaustion written in the grey of his lips, and still that banked fire burned steady in his eyes. "I dreamed it. I dream all of it. I dream the others going out, one by one, like windows going dark in a great house." He lifted one small hand and pressed it, mud-streaked, against her breastbone, directly over her heart, and through the contact Odile felt it—a pulse, faint and rhythmic and *wrong*, a second heartbeat nested inside the boy like a swallow's nest in a rafter.

"They sent me," he said. "What was left of them that could still send anything. They said: find Eriksen-of-the-Ninth-Vow. Find the one who kept her vow too well. Tell her the silence is ending, whether she speaks or not."

Behind him, down in the street, a lantern swung into view at the mouth of the alley. Then another. The spiral had found its center.

The child's eyes rolled back, and he pitched forward, and Odile caught him—caught all of him, this feather-light vessel full of burning—and stood in her doorway holding the last ember of her god while the witch-finders' boots rang on the cobbles below.

She should put him out in the street. She should close the door. She should let the tide take him, the way she had let everything else go dark and quiet, and live another ten years as Marren, and die safe.

The boy stirred against her shoulder and murmured, half-dreaming, in the tongue of the silent god:

"*Answer us, sister. We are so few now.*"

Odile Eriksen stepped backward into her house and barred the door.
