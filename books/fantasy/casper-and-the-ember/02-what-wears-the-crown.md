## 2. What Wears the Crown

The river gate took their last silver, and the ferryman asked no questions, which was the only kindness left in Greyhollow after dark. Odile sat with her back against the wet planks of the hull and the boy pressed to her side like a coal banked in ash, watching the city's lamps recede behind them—hundreds of small fires, each one a household that would burn a charm-seller if the witch-finders paid enough.

Casper was talking.

He had not stopped since midnight. The words came out of him in that old cadence, rising and falling like water finding its level, and Odile had spent the whole crossing trying not to listen, because listening was the first step toward answering, and answering was the first step toward everything she had buried under ten years of false names and cheap tin charms.

*Eriksen-of-the-Ninth-Vow.* He had said it once, on her doorstep, and her hands had remembered how to make the warding sign before her mind could stop them.

Now, on the black water, she let herself listen.

It was not the tongue of any living nation. It was older than Greyhollow, older than Aldric's line, older than the idea of kings. The liturgical language of Vael—the Silent God—who had never spoken a word since the world was made, whose priests therefore learned to speak *to* silence rather than through it. She had sworn those vowels into her own throat at nineteen, kneeling in cold water up to her chin while the temple flooded around her, and she had not used them in a decade, and they came back anyway, unbidden, the way grief comes back.

*Ember,* he was saying. *Ember, scattered, seven-fold. I am the fourth. They are eating us.*

Her breath fogged and hung in the air between them.

"Who," she said, and her voice came out rusted. "Who is eating you, little one?"

He turned his face up to hers. In the dark his eyes were wrong—not feral, as the market children had whispered when he stole bread from the shrine stalls, but luminous, faintly, the way coals stay lit under grey ash. He said a word she knew. A word every priest of the Ninth Vow knew, though none had dared say it aloud in a hundred years, because naming it was half an invitation.

Mauveth. The god-eater. The hunger that walked before the temples fell, that drank divinity the way drought drinks rivers, that the silent god had gone to war against at the end of all things—and lost, or won too dearly, or simply stopped, because Vael did not explain itself. Not then. Not ever.

Odile looked down at the boy—an ember of her god, sitting in a stolen coat two sizes too large, gnawing the last heel of bread she'd bought him—and felt the full architecture of her cowardice assemble itself around her like a cathedral.

She had thought hiding was neutrality. Ten years of it: no prayers, no signs, no vows answered or refused, just a woman named Marta selling love-luck and fever-knots to people who couldn't tell true work from tin. She had told herself the war was over, that silence meant safety, that whatever had broken the temples had broken them for everyone equally.

But embers were still falling. Still burning. And something was still hunting them, patient as rot, one by one by one—and every year she had spent invisible was a year the hunter hunted unopposed.

"I hid," she said, not entirely to Casper. To the river, maybe. To whatever fragment of Vael still listened, if anything did. "I hid, and I called it survival."

Casper leaned his head against her arm. He did not comfort her. Embers didn't; they only burned, and waited to be gathered.

Behind them, on the far shore, a bell began to ring—not the hour, but the hunt. Three strokes, pause, three strokes. The witch-finders were loose in the streets, and they were not searching the city anymore.

They already knew which way the child had gone.

* * *

King Aldric watched his own hand turn the page of a book he had not chosen, and could not remember beginning, and could not put down.

That was the shape of it now, most days. A dimness at the center of himself, like looking at your reflection through smoked glass—you knew the outline was yours, but the details belonged to someone else. He sat in his body the way a man sits in an inn's common room while strangers argue over his head: present, ignored, unable to leave.

*Mauveth.*

Even thinking the name felt like shouting down a well. Sometimes, in the deepest hours, when the thing wearing him slept—if it slept—Aldric could move a finger. Curl it. Once he had written a single letter in spilled wine before the hand went flat and obedient again. He had spent eleven days deciding what the letter should be. He had chosen *R*, for Renza, his daughter, dead six years, because it was the only word left in him that Mauveth could not use.

The thing did not speak inside his skull. That was the horror of it. It simply *was*, vast and unhurried, and his thoughts moved through it like fish through deep water—unnoticed, irrelevant, occasionally swallowed.

But tonight the great attention turned, and even a passenger feels it when the carriage changes course.

Below, in the map room, the witch-finders knelt in their grey hoods. Aldric could see them through eyes that were not only his: nine figures, and above them the Grand Finder, holding a scrap of linen that had touched the child. The scrap was glowing. Faintly, goldly, like a coal wrapped in cloth.

*There,* the presence thought, and its thinking was not sound but pressure, a slow tide dragging everything toward one point on the map. *There, and there, and there. Four remain. Three have gone into me. The fourth runs with a woman who smells of vows.*

Aldric raged, silently, in the sealed room of himself. He threw himself against the walls of his own skull until something tore—but the tear did not free him; it only let a little of the darkness in, and he learned things he did not want to know. That the witch-finders had been Mauveth's for years, longer than his possession, perhaps. That the purges, the burnings, the outlawing of magic across three kingdoms—all of it had been a harvest, cutting away everyone who might recognize an ember, might shelter one, might kneel to one. That his reign had been a long, slow clearing of the field.

*The drowned temple,* the presence mused, turning the map's western edge. *She will run where she was made. They always do. Sentiment is a road, and roads are for following.*

And beneath that vast, cold planning, so faint Aldric almost missed it—a thread of something else. Gold. Old. Patient in a different way. Like a fire banked under ash, waiting for someone to breathe on it.

The king clung to that thread the way a drowning man clings to driftwood. *If You are still there,* he thought, pouring every scrap of himself into it, *if anything of You remembers my grandfather's crown, my daughter's name, my one good decade—take this body. Take it and burn what's wearing it. I release it. Do You hear me? I release it.*

The presence paused. For one terrible moment, the smoked glass thinned, and Aldric saw the map room clearly with his own eyes: the candle guttering, the Grand Finder's hood lifting, grey lips moving.

"The woman has a name," the Grand Finder said. "Eriksen. Of the Ninth Vow."

Somewhere far off, in a body walking a dark road beside a burning child, a woman he had never met shivered without knowing why.

* * *

Dawn found them in the hills west of the city, where the road gave up and became a sheep track and the track became a memory. Odile carried Casper on her back now; he burned hotter with every mile, and she could feel it through the wool of her coat, a heat that had nothing to do with fever.

He was murmuring again. Directions, she realized. Liturgy and directions braided together, the way temple lessons were always taught: doctrine in one ear, the way home in the other.

"Drowned temple," she said aloud, testing it, and hated how her heart lifted at the words. Home was a trap dressed as a sanctuary. Mauveth would know she'd go there. Any priest would.

But there was nowhere else. The embers needed water and stone and vow-ground to rest in, and there was only one place in the world where all three had been joined.

She crested the ridge and looked back once at Greyhollow, small and smoky in the valley, and at the distant palace on its crag, where the king's banner hung limp in the windless morning.

"Ten years I kept my head down," she told the boy sleeping against her shoulder. "And the whole time, the wolf was wearing the shepherd."

Far below, thin as a needle, the witch-finders' horn sounded on the road behind them.

They had maybe a day.
