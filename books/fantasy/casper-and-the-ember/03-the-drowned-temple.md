## 3. The Drowned Temple

The temple did not rise out of the lake so much as kneel there, half-sunken, its bell tower tilted like a mourner too long at a graveside. Odile had not seen it in eleven years, and it had not waited for her. The water had taken the colonnade to the shoulder; the carved faces of the Nine Vows stared up through green glass with their mouths full of silt.

Casper stopped at the shore and would go no farther. His bare feet had left bloody prints on the stones behind them — she'd carried him the last mile without asking permission, and he had let her, which frightened her more than anything the witch-finders might do. A child who let himself be carried was a child who had nothing left for walking.

"You know this place," she said. Not a question. He had been steering them for three days now, tugging her sleeve at crossroads, pointing down goat tracks no map remembered, and every track had led true.

The boy looked at the drowned faces under the water. When he spoke, it was the old tongue again, that liturgical cadence that made the hair on her arms stand like hackles: *Vael-ennath, vael-ennath, korim.* The god hears. The god hears. Come home.

"I'm not coming home," she said. "I'm coming back. There's a difference."

He turned and looked at her then, and his eyes were wrong — not feral-wrong as they had been on her doorstep, but lit from inside, faint gold at the rim of each iris like embers breathing under ash. He was burning faster now. She could see it in the way the air around him shimmered when he shivered, in the frost melting off the reeds where he passed. Ten years ago she had been taught what an ember was. She had never been taught how long one could hold itself together before the fire ate the wick.

* * *

They waded in at low evening light, up the flooded nave, water thigh-deep and cold enough to make her teeth ache. The sanctuary still stood above the surface — the choir dais, the altar stone where she had knelt at nineteen and sworn silence into service. She lifted Casper onto the altar because there was nowhere else dry, and because some part of her, treacherous and old, wanted him exactly where vows were made.

"Listen to me." She took his face in her hands. His skin was fever-hot. "Whatever happens, whatever they offer me — you stay here. You burn slow. Do you understand? Slow."

He understood. That was the horror of him; he understood everything, this child who spoke a dead language and had come out of nowhere to find the one woman in Greyhollow who could hear it. He pressed his small hand flat against her sternum, over the heart, and said one word in the common tongue — his first, and she suspected his only:

"Hold."

Then the dogs came.

Not dogs. She knew that even before they breached the tree line — shapes that moved like hounds but cast shadows that moved like men, seven of them, wading the shallows without ripples. Behind them, walking across the water as if it were marble, came a figure in king's armor dulled to the color of drowned iron, and above the gorget, where Aldric's face should have been, something else looked out. It wore his face the way a glove fits a hand. The eyes were wet and black and patient.

Mauveth.

Odile put herself between the altar and the water and drew the only weapon she had kept these ten years: a charm of silence, pressed wax and grave-dust, worth perhaps three heartbeats of quiet against a god-devourer. She held it anyway.

"Eriksen-of-the-Ninth-Vow." Mauveth's voice was Aldric's voice, but slowed, deepened, spoken from somewhere beneath the man. "You hid well. Ten years selling luck to fishwives while your god starved. I ate three of his embers last winter. They tasted of sermons."

"What do you want?"

"The same thing you want, priestess. An ending." The thing gestured, and the shadow-hounds halted, sitting in the shallow water like obedient beasts at a master's heel. "The child burns. You've seen it. By dawn he will be cinders wearing a boy's shape, and all that holy fire will scatter into the air and be wasted — or I will gather it, and finish what I began, and your god will be gone so thoroughly that even grief will forget him. Or—"

It paused. It knew how to pause. Ten years of silence had taught her the weight of a pause.

"—or you take him. Take the ember into yourself, as you were trained to do, as the Ninth Vow was made for. All that power, freely given by a dying vessel. You would be more than a battle-priest. You would be the god's whole remainder, walking. Your standing restored. Your name unburied. Greyhollow's outlawry meaningless against what you would be."

The words went into her like hooks going into a fish.

Because it was true — that was the filth of it, the genius of it. She *had* been trained for this. The Ninth Vow, the vow of vesselhood, sworn by one priestess in each generation, the one who would carry divinity in a mortal body if the god ever needed hiding among men. She had broken that vow not by fleeing but by fleeing *wrong* — by hoarding her devotion instead of spending it, ten years of prayers swallowed instead of given, a reservoir behind a dam of fear. Mauveth was offering her the key to her own dam and calling it mercy.

And the boy. She could feel the heat of him at her back like an open oven door. If she took the ember into herself, she might live. She might even win. And Casper — Casper would simply stop burning. Stop everything. Ash on an altar, and her walking away whole, powerful, *restored*, with the only person who had ever called her by her true name dead by her own hand and called salvation.

She thought about the charm-stall. The false name. The little wax hands and bottled luck sold to women who needed them, magic smuggled into an outlaw city one kindness at a time — because she had told herself it was cowardice, hiding, failure, when all along it may have been the only vow she never broke: *give, don't keep.*

That was the test. It had always been the test. The silent god had never demanded silence; it had demanded giving, and she had mistaken the two for the same thing and buried herself alive for a decade.

"No," Odile said.

Mauveth tilted Aldric's head. Somewhere under the black eyes, the real man screamed — she heard it, faintly, a sound like wind through a keyhole.

"I don't want it back," she said. "I want it *gone*. Given. Spent. Every prayer I ever swallowed, every year I hid, every scrap of devotion I hoarded like a miser — I'm done keeping." She turned to the altar. "Casper. Hold."

His hand was already on her chest. Her hands closed over his.

She opened the dam.

It did not feel like power. It felt like bleeding — ten years of unspent faith leaving her in a single torrent, every swallowed hymn and unsaid grace pouring down her arm into the small burning body on the altar. She gave him the vigil-candles she'd never lit. The fasts she'd broken alone. The night she'd wept in a market gutter because a witch-finder's boot had crushed her charms and she'd thanked him. She gave it all, and it was not enough, and so she gave the thing underneath it all — the love she'd refused to feel since the day she ran, love for a god who never asked for her silence, poured out until there was nothing left in her but the giving itself.

Casper blazed.

Light stood up out of the boy like a pillar, white-gold, and the lake around them caught it and burned green to the far shores. The shadow-hounds unraveled mid-yelp. Above the water, Mauveth recoiled, Aldric's armored feet carving furrows in the surface, and for the first time the thing in the king's face looked afraid—

—and Vael, reignited in a child's body, opened its single eye upon its devourer, and the world held its breath between one heartbeat and the next.
