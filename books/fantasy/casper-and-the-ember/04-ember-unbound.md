## 4. Ember, Unbound

*Odile*

The water of the drowned temple rose to her ankles, black and patient, and Casper stood in the center of it burning.

Not with flame—she had learned that distinction long ago, kneeling at this same altar with her forehead pressed to cold stone. This was the other fire, the one Vael's liturgy had no clean word for because the silent god never wasted words on what could not be mistaken: the fire that consumed the vessel first. The boy's eyes were white as salt. His small hands were open at his sides, palms up, in the posture of a man being executed who has decided to forgive it.

"Give it to me," Mauveth said from across the flooded nave.

It wore Aldric like a coat worn too long—the king's face sagging around something older, something that smiled with teeth that did not belong to any human mouth. The witch-finders stood along the broken colonnade behind it, forty of them, their iron brands dark, waiting for an order. Water dripped through the collapsed roof in slow silver threads. Somewhere below the surface, Odile knew, the old mosaic still lay intact: Vael's nine-pointed star, drowned but unbroken.

"You want nothing," Mauveth said, and its voice was kind, which was the worst thing about it. "That is what I have always admired about you, Ninth-Vow. Ten years in Greyhollow selling love-knots and luck-charms to fools, wanting nothing, needing nothing. I can give you back everything you threw away. Your vows restored. Your voice in the god's ear. You need only do what you have always done." It extended Aldric's hand, palm open, gentle. "Give me the child."

And there it was. The temptation was not power—it was permission. Permission to go on hiding, dressed up in vestments. She looked at the hand, and she understood, finally, why she had failed her test ten years ago: not because she had fled, but because fleeing had felt like humility. Silence had felt like devotion. It had been fear wearing faith's clothing, and she had let it fit her for a decade.

Casper made a sound. Not the dead tongue this time—a child's sound, thin and human, and his knees buckled, and the water around him began to steam.

Odile waded toward him.

"No," said Mauveth.

"Yes," said Odile, "you've misunderstood something fundamental," and she knelt in the flood and took the boy's burning face between her hands.

*Casper*

There was a door inside him. He had lived his whole life knowing it was there without knowing the word *door*. Behind it, something vast turned over in its sleep like a banked coal, and every night he dreamed in a language the waking world had buried, and every morning he woke hungrier than sleep could explain.

Now the door was breaking, and the thing behind it was coming through whether he consented or not, and he was going to come apart like wet paper—

—and then a woman's hands, cold from the floodwater, held his face, and she was speaking the dead tongue, and she was speaking it *wrong*, stumbling over the vowels the way a traveler stumbles over stones, but she was speaking it anyway. To him. At him. Into him.

She said: *I kept it. All these years I kept it, and told myself keeping was the same as giving.*

He didn't know what she meant. He didn't need to. Something in her opened—not her hands, not her mouth; something deeper, some cellar of the soul where a person stores what they refuse to spend—and it poured into him like light poured into water.

It hurt. It hurt like warmth hurts frostbitten fingers. Ten years of unspent devotion, ten years of prayers bitten off before they finished, ten years of a charm-seller's small mercies that had really been tithes paid to a god she pretended wasn't listening—all of it, all at once, flooding through a boy built to hold an ember and finding room made for a bonfire.

The door inside him didn't break.

It opened from the other side.

*Odile*

She felt herself emptying and did not stop. That was the whole of it; there was no ritual left in her, no liturgy, only the raw transaction: *take what I hoarded, take what I withheld, take the ten years, take them all.* She had thought giving would feel like dying. It felt like setting down a weight she had forgotten she carried, so vast that her body swayed without it, and she laughed—actually laughed—as the water around them went gold.

Vael woke.

She had expected thunder. There was none. The god rose through the boy and through her and through the drowned mosaic beneath their feet, and the nine points of the star ignited under the floodwater like a city seen from far above at night, and the light did not roar—it *stood*. It stood up out of the temple like a pillar of dawn, and Mauveth recoiled from it, dragging Aldric's body backward across the stones, and for the first time the devourer's voice lost its kindness.

What happened then was not a battle. Battles have two sides. This was a burning-out—a hearth-fire meeting a rot and simply continuing to burn where the rot had been. Odile saw Mauveth try to hold Aldric, saw it pour itself deeper into the king's bones to keep its purchase, and saw Vael's light follow it down without hurry, without hatred, the way morning follows a thief.

Aldric screamed. Then someone else screamed—something with no throat, wearing a scream it had borrowed—and then the light went through the king like water through a net, taking nothing of him, leaving nothing of *it*, and Mauveth was gone. Not destroyed. Odile understood that even now. Starved. Scattered. Reduced to hunger with nowhere to hang it.

The witch-finders stood blinking along the colonnade like men waking from a shared dream. One by one, without a word, they dropped their brands into the floodwater—iron hissing—and walked away into the rain.

*Casper*

The quiet afterward was different from every quiet he had ever known.

Before, quiet had been the world refusing to answer him. Now the quiet was full—full of a god sitting down inside its own silence the way an old man sits down after work, deliberately, having chosen it. Casper felt it settle. Felt the ember in his chest bank itself low, low, warm as a stone kept near a bed, and felt—this was the part that made him cry, though he was almost eleven and had sworn off crying—the feeling of being *set down*. Of being put somewhere safe by something enormous.

The woman was still holding his face. Her eyes were wet. She was saying something in the dead tongue, over and over, and this time he understood it perfectly because it was simple:

*You are not a weapon. You are not a ward. You are mine, if you'll have me.*

"I'll have you," he said, in Greyhollow speech, in plain grey words, and her face broke open like the morning.

*Odile*

They walked home through a changed city, because there was no other way to say it: the city had changed, or rather the city had stopped pretending. In the streets of Greyhollow, charms were being sold openly—a girl at a corner stall braiding luck-threads without hiding her hands; two old men arguing theology on a doorstep with the fervor of men who had waited thirty years for permission. King Aldric, they heard as they passed the square, had addressed the crowd at dawn in his own voice, hoarse and halting, telling them what had worn him and what had freed him, and asking—asking, not decreeing—what magic freely given might build here.

Rain had washed the sky clean. Casper walked close enough to her that their sleeves touched, and neither of them moved away.

"Eriksen-of-the-Ninth-Vow," he said solemnly, testing it.

"Just Eriksen," she said. "Or Odile. Or—" she glanced down at the feral, ember-warm, impossible child beside her, and found that her voice wanted to be gentle and let it, "—or *home*, if you're calling for me."

He considered this with the gravity of a scholar. "Home," he agreed.

Behind them the drowned temple settled back into its flood, its star dimming by choice, keeping watch in the dark. Ahead, Greyhollow smoked and glittered with ordinary evening fires, and somewhere among them a door waited that would need feeding, and sweeping, and answering when knocked upon—for word, Odile already suspected, traveled fast in cities, and there would be others like Casper. Others who spoke the dead tongue. Others with doors inside them, some closed gently, some nailed shut, some already smoking at the hinges.

She took the boy's hand. They walked in out of the rain.
