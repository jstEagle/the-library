## 1. The Taster's Table

The poison arrived in the third course, as poisons often did.

Xanthe Dunmore knew it before the silver spoon touched her lips — knew it by the way the sauce had gone glassy at the edges, a faint iridescence that no honest reduction of wine and marrow should carry. She had learned to read food the way priests read scripture: closely, suspiciously, line by line. The kitchen boys thought her fastidious. The steward thought her vain. Only she knew that she was reading for murder.

She lifted the spoon anyway.

That was the job. That was the whole of it. The king's taster ate first, and if the taster lived, the king ate after. Three years she had sat at the long side-table below the dais, close enough to watch His Majesty chew, far enough that his eyes never once found hers. She was furniture that swallowed. A chair with a mouth. The courtiers spoke over her head, around her, through her, and she let them, because invisibility was the other half of her wage.

The sauce of braised eel slid across her tongue — rich, buttery, faintly sweet with honeyed leeks — and beneath it, coiled like a serpent in grass, the bitter green kiss of crowshade.

She did not spit. Spitting was a confession, and confessions brought questions, and questions brought the witch-hunters, and the witch-hunters asked questions in rooms without windows. Instead she swallowed, set down the spoon with a delicate click, and folded her hands in her lap while the poison began its work inside her.

Crowshade killed a grown man in the space between courses. It seized the heart, sent it hammering into arrhythmia, and stopped it before the victim could finish saying how much he'd enjoyed the meal. Xanthe knew this because she had made herself an expert in every poison the kingdom's apothecaries could brew, buy, or steal — not from books, though she'd read those too, but from the slow, deliberate, three-year project of drinking them all.

Small doses. Smaller than small. Doses measured in grains of dust, in the residue left on a mortar's lip, in the ghost of bitterness on a fingertip. Week after week, month after month, she had fed herself the kingdom's arsenal one drop at a time until her blood had learned each poison the way a soldier learns an enemy's face. Her body catalogued them. Recognized them. Refused them.

Mostly refused them.

The crowshade hit her like a fist behind the ribs. Her pulse stuttered, galloped, stumbled. A wave of heat crawled up her throat and pooled behind her eyes, and for four seconds — she counted them, she always counted them — the banquet hall went soft at its edges, the candle flames blooming into halos, the music smearing into something underwater.

Then her heart remembered itself. Found its rhythm. Held.

She sat very still through the fourth course so no one would notice her hands trembling, and when the steward came to clear the plates he found hers empty and gleaming, and declared the table safe. The king ate his eel. He ate it with evident pleasure, dabbing his mouth with a napkin embroidered in gold thread, and nothing happened to him, because nothing was in it anymore except dinner.

No one thanked her. No one ever thanked her. The taster who lives is invisible; the taster who dies is briefly famous, mourned in a footnote, replaced by morning.

Later, in the stone corridor outside the hall, the under-steward pressed a coin purse into her palm — eleven silver marks, docked a mark for the chipped goblet someone else had broken — and murmured, almost kindly, "Rough course tonight, Mistress Dunmore."

"You have no idea," she said, and walked on.

* * *

Her lodgings were three streets and a world away from the palace, up a stair that smelled of tallow and old fish, behind a door with a lock that only pretended to work. She climbed it slowly, one hand on the wall, letting the last ghost of the crowshade drain out of her limbs. By the time she reached the top she was steady again. Steady, and alone, which was the condition she trusted most.

The room was narrow as a coffin stood on end. A cot. A brazier, cold until she lit it. A chest with a false bottom, and beneath the false bottom, the other life.

She knelt and lifted the boards out one by one. Wrapped in oilcloth lay her inventory: charms, dozens of them, each one outlawed under the Edict of Ash on pain of the pyre. Knots of red thread stitched with patterns no licensed craftsman would touch. Iron nails bent into rings. Pouches of grave-dirt from the paupers' field, which kept debtors from the door. Salt charmed against nightmares. And luck — luck above all — because luck was what the poor bought when they had nothing else left to spend.

Selling them was death if she were caught. Not prison. Death. The Edict of Ash had been read aloud from every pulpit in the city seven years ago, after the last great burning: no charms, no cantrips, no whispered words over water or thread or iron. Magic belonged to the crown alone, and the crown, the edict said, used none. What the king did not use, no one might.

So Xanthe sold it in the dark, quietly, to people who knocked rather than rang.

Tonight's knock came before she'd even finished unwrapping the oilcloth — three soft taps, then two, the pattern she'd taught perhaps a dozen souls, each sworn to teach no one else. She blew on the coals until they caught, shuttered the window, and opened the door a hand's width.

Marta Voss. The laundress from the tannery row, a big raw-knuckled woman who scrubbed other people's linens until her own hands cracked and wept. Xanthe hadn't seen her since midwinter.

"You shouldn't be here," Xanthe said, before Marta said anything at all. "It's been months. If you were followed—"

"I wasn't followed." Marta's voice was thick, urgent, the voice of someone who had run out of time to be careful. "I had to come. I'm sorry. I know you said never unless — but I couldn't wait for the market night. I couldn't."

"Come in. Quickly."

Marta filled half the little room by herself. She sat on the edge of the cot with her cap wrung in her fists, and in the brazier-light Xanthe saw what winter and worry had done to her: hollows under the cheekbones, a tremor in the jaw, eyes rimmed red from sleeplessness.

"It's my boy," Marta said. "Tam. He's eight. He took a fever off the wash-water nine days ago and it won't break. The physician wants six marks just to look at him, and I've got two, and the fever's climbing. He talks to things that aren't there now. Last night he swore the fire in our hearth spoke to him." She laughed, horribly, and the laugh broke in the middle. "I told him fires don't speak. And then I thought — maybe I'm wrong. Maybe there's something in the fire, and maybe there's something that can tell it to let him go."

Xanthe looked at her a long moment. There was no good answer here, and she knew all the shapes a bad answer could take. Fevered children died; that was the oldest arithmetic in the city, older than the Edict, older than the walls. A charm would likely do nothing. But Marta hadn't come for certainty. She'd come for the last thing she owned to trade, and for permission to hope.

"What have you got?" Xanthe asked.

"Three marks now. My mother's wedding ring besides." Marta held it out — a thin band, worn nearly to wire, warm from her grip. "I know it isn't enough. I know you've got your own rent. But please. Whatever you have. Anything."

Xanthe reached into the oilcloth and drew out a knot of red thread, stitched through with iron filings and a single dried rowan berry, closed with a whisper of words she would deny under torture having ever spoken. Against fever it was worth less than most things in her stock. Against despair, it was the strongest thing she carried.

"This is a fever-knot," she said. "Tie it above his wrist, left arm, tight enough to feel but not bite. When the sweat comes — and it may not come — don't cut the thread. Let it fall off on its own or it takes the working with it." She hesitated, then added the part she almost never added: "And Marta — keep giving him water. Boiled water, cooled. Charms fail more often than they hold, and water doesn't."

"You think it'll work?"

"I think it's what I have." She took the three marks and the ring, weighed the ring in her palm, and gave it back. "Keep this. Sell it only if he worsens and you need the physician after all. I'll take the coins."

Marta's eyes went bright with something between gratitude and shame. "You're — I'll bring the rest. When Tam's well, I'll bring the rest, I swear it on his life—"

"Don't swear on his life." Xanthe's voice went flat and hard, and she heard the hardness herself and hated it a little. "Swear on nothing. Just get home before the patrol turns onto Tanner's Lane. Go."

At the door, Marta paused. "Why do you do it?" she asked. "The charms. You've got a post at the palace. Silver every week. Why risk the pyre for the likes of us?"

Xanthe looked at her — at the cracked hands, the frantic love, the whole desperate weight of her — and found she had no honest answer that wasn't also a wound. Because the silver didn't fill the room. Because the palace thought she was furniture. Because somewhere in three years of swallowing poison for strangers, she had forgotten how to swallow anything else. Because a woman who trusts no one still has to be something to somebody, even if it's only for the price of three marks and a lie about hope.

"Because I'm good at it," she said finally, which was true, and insufficient, and all she had.

Marta went. The bolt slid home behind her, and Xanthe sat alone by the brazier, counting the coins into their hiding place, and felt the familiar silence settle back over the room like ash.

That was the shape of her life, and she had believed it would hold: mornings of poison, nights of charms, and between them a woman carefully unmade, trusting no one — not the court, not her customers, not Merrick the fence who moved her goods and skimmed her coin, and least of all herself, because she had seen exactly what her own body could survive and knew it was not the same as being safe.

She banked the fire. She lay down. She blew out the candle.

And in the dark, through the wall, through the floor, through the whole sleeping tenement, she heard it — faint, impossible, unmistakable — a child's voice speaking words in no language she knew, and every charm beneath the floorboards answering it, humming like struck bells.
