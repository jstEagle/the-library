## 2. A Knock in the First Tongue

Xanthe did not sleep. She lay on her narrow bed with a knife under one hand and the loose floorboard under the other, listening to the humming.

It was not loud. That was what frightened her most — its quiet. The charms beneath her floor were small things, cowardly things, each one warded and wrapped and buried under three years of careful silence, and yet they sang to that voice like children answering their mother across a crowded market. She could feel them through the mattress slats, a vibration in her back teeth, in the marrow of her ribs.

*Words in no language she knew.*

She knew every language worth money in Ashfall. The gutter cant of the ash-market, the trade-tongue of the river barges, enough High Speech to read a writ of seizure and forge one besides. Her mother had sung her lullabies in old Lowmarsh before the fever took her voice along with everything else. Xanthe had made a life out of knowing what people said when they thought no one understood.

This was none of those. This was older. It moved like water moving underground — you didn't hear it so much as feel it passing through the foundations of things.

And it knew where she lived.

She told herself, in the flat practical voice she used for inventory: *A child. A lost child who wandered into the wrong stairwell. Children say nonsense; charms are tin and bone and wishful thinking; nothing hums.* But she had spent three years training her body to tell truth from lie at the level of the blood, and right now her blood said *something is under your floor singing back*, and her blood had never once been wrong.

The humming stopped sometime past midnight.

Xanthe lay awake until the grey came, then rose, dressed, and pulled up the floorboard.

The charms lay still. All nine of them, in their oilcloth and their wax, inert as buttons. No scorch marks. No cracks. Whatever had spoken to them had spoken gently.

That somehow made it worse.

* * *

She went to work because going to work was what she did, the way the tide goes out, the way smoke goes up. The palace kitchens swallowed her at dawn — forty fires, two hundred mouths to feed, and one woman with a plain grey dress, plain brown hair, and a face designed by God specifically to be forgotten. She tasted the king's breakfast: porridge from the communal pot, honey from the sealed jar, milk from the churn the dairymaid swore by her sainted aunt no one had touched. Nothing. She tasted the midday broth for the queen's table — the queen being a title more than a woman these days; she hadn't appeared in public since midwinter. Nothing there either, unless you counted too much salt, which was an insult rather than an assassination.

Master Corvelin, the head cook, watched her work as he always did, arms folded, flour to his elbows.

"Quiet night?" he asked.

"Lovely," said Xanthe. "Slept like the dead."

He grunted. He was one of perhaps four people in the palace who knew her name, and he used it rarely, the way you'd use a knife you weren't sure was yours. "There's talk upstairs," he said. "Witch-hunters came to the gate this morning. Asking after charmsellers."

"They ask every week." She rinsed her mouth with water from her own flask — never the kitchen's water; a taster who trusted the kitchen's water deserved what she got. "Did they find any?"

"One. Old woman off Tanner's Row." He said it carefully, watching her. "They walked her out through the yard. She was still praying."

Xanthe kept her face doing nothing, which was its full-time occupation. "Sad," she said, and meant it, and let neither show. Inside, cold arithmetic clicked along: Tanner's Row was four streets from her tenement. The hunters were circling closer than usual. And something had sung to her floorboards all night.

Finish the shift. Go home. Decide.

She finished the shift.

* * *

The decision made itself, the way decisions do when you've already made them and are only waiting for your courage to catch up. She climbed her stairs in the last light, and there — sitting against her door as if it were a bench and not the threshold of the only secret she had left — was a child.

Soot-streaked. That was the first thing. Soot in his hair, soot ground into his knees and knuckles, a black smudge across one cheek like a thumbprint. He couldn't have been more than eight or nine. His clothes had been good once — a coat with real brass buttons, now burned through at one cuff — and his eyes were the colour of banked coals, which is to say they looked like nothing until you looked directly at them, and then they looked like everything.

"You're late," the boy said.

Xanthe stopped three steps down. The landing was narrow; the stair rail was rotten; the window at the far end gave onto a drop into the midden-lane. She catalogued all of it without deciding to, the way she catalogued every plate set before her.

"This isn't your door," she said.

"No." He said it simply, without apology, as if doors belonged to whoever needed them. "Your name is Xanthe Dunmore."

Her spine went cold from the nape downward, vertebra by vertebra, like ice dropped down her collar. Nobody knew that name. *Dunmore* was dead and buried under six years of ash and a false surname bought from a forger who'd since hanged himself, conveniently. On the palace rolls she was Xanthe Vey, orphan, licensed taster, unremarkable. In the ash-market she was nothing at all, which was safer.

"Wrong girl," she said. "You want the next landing."

"Dunmore," the boy repeated, patient, terrible. "Of the Dunmores of Lowmarsh. Your mother called you Thia when you were small and cried about it, so she stopped. You keep a knife under your bed on the left side because you're left-handed but you trained your right, so no one can take it off you. And under your floor—" he tilted his head, listening to something she couldn't hear, "—there are nine small voices, and they're all awake now, and they know me."

The knife was under her apron before she'd decided to move. "Get away from my door."

"You should invite me in instead." He stood, slowly, the way old men stand, joints creaking with a weariness no child should carry. Up close she could see the soot wasn't only soot — there was grey in it, ash-grey, and his breath, when it reached her across the narrow stair, smelled faintly of a fire long gone out. Like a hearth cold for days. Like the inside of a chimney. "Because they're coming, and I can't knock forever, and I'm very tired, Xanthe Dunmore."

"Who's coming?"

"Hunters." He said the word the way other children said *wolf*. "And worse than hunters."

"I don't know you. I don't help strangers. I especially don't help strange children who know things they shouldn't—"

"Casper," he said.

"What?"

"My name. You asked. Well — you didn't, but you wanted to, and lying to yourself gives you a headache." He rubbed one soot-black eye with the back of his wrist. "Casper. I had another name before. Everyone did. Before the Edict."

Something turned over in Xanthe's chest. She hated it. She hated him, briefly and sincerely, for putting it there.

"The Edict of Ash has been law for thirty years," she said. "You're eight. You weren't alive 'before' anything."

"Not me." He pressed one small fist against his own sternum, and through the burnt hole in his cuff, between the blackened fingers, she saw it — saw it and felt it, heat blooming against her own breastbone like a swallowed coal. "Him. What I'm carrying. It was alive before the first brick of this city. It was alive before your Edict, before your king, before the last dragon folded its wings over the mountains and died of grief. And it's dying now, in here, and when it dies—" his voice cracked, went young for just a moment, a little boy's voice, exhausted and afraid, "—when it dies, I think I die with it. And I think worse things happen too. Worse things for everyone."

Down in the street, a lantern swung past. Boots on cobbles. Voices — the clipped cadence of the watch, or someone wearing the watch's cadence like a coat.

Casper's head snapped toward the sound. When he looked back at her, his coal-coloured eyes had gone bright, genuinely alight, and the shadows of the stairwell leaned *away* from him as though the darkness itself had taken a step back.

"He knows my name too," the boy whispered. "The thing behind the king's mouth. It's been looking for me since midwinter. Every hunter in Ashfall is hunting a child, and none of them know why except the thing that sent them." He held out his hand — small, filthy, steady. "Nine charms under your floor, Xanthe Dunmore. Every one of them outlawed. Every one of them enough to hang you twice over. You've spent three years pretending you're nobody." His fingers curled closed, and the heat in the air guttered like a candle in wind. "I'm offering you the chance to be somebody, one time, at the worst possible moment. Which is the only time it counts."

The boots were on the stairs now. She could hear them — two men, heavy, unhurried, the tread of men doing routine work. Checking doors. The old woman off Tanner's Row was still warm, wherever they'd put her.

Every instinct she owned — every instinct she had *built*, plank by plank, over three years of tasting death in small sips — screamed the same instruction: step back, close the door, be plain grey Xanthe Vey, forget the boy on the landing, survive.

But beneath the floorboards, nine outlawed charms began to hum.

And Casper, soot-streaked and burning-low, looked up at her with the patience of mountains and said, in the language that was no language she knew — the language that moved like water underground, the language her bones understood before her ears did:

*"Open the door, taster. Time to taste something true."*
