## 3. What the Crown Wears

The woman's house smells of copper and thyme and something older underneath — ash that has never been rained on.

Casper knows her name before she says it. *Xanthe Dunmore.* The name tastes like a struck flint in his mouth. He has carried it across three provinces and one river full of drowned things, and now he is here, standing on her floor with his shoes leaking soot-water, and she is looking at him like he is either a miracle or a rat, and he does not yet know which.

She asks him questions. Where are your parents. Who taught you to speak like that. What is that light under your skin.

He tries to answer but the words come out wrong-shaped, because the coal in his chest is waking up, and when it wakes it does not want talk. It wants *showing.*

So he shows her.

* * *

It begins the way it always begins: with a door.

Not a real door. A door made of the space between two heartbeats. Casper steps through it the way he has stepped through it every night since the fire came to him — falling forward into the dark behind the world, where things wear their true shapes because there is no light left to lie with.

Xanthe comes with him. He can feel her hand in his, small and callused, gripping hard enough to hurt. She is saying something — *what did you do, what is this* — but sound works differently here. Her voice arrives as heat against his ear. Her fear arrives as a taste: vinegar and iron.

*Don't be afraid,* he tells her, which is a lie, because he is afraid too, every time, and the coal likes that. The coal feeds on fear the way a wick feeds on oil.

They fall.

* * *

First he shows her the palace, because that is where it lives, and you cannot kill a thing until you have seen its den.

The throne room of Aldermarch, but not the throne room from the coins and the proclamations. This is the throne room as the coal sees it — colors bleeding wrong, gold running like yolk, banners hanging dead as shed skins. And on the dais, the throne itself, and on the throne —

A man.

Except.

*Xanthe, look at his hands,* Casper thinks at her, because here thinking is speaking. *Look at them properly.*

The king's hands are beautiful. That is the first horror. They are long-fingered and clean, the hands of a man who has never hauled anything heavier than a pen, and they move with terrible grace as they draw the quill across parchment after parchment after parchment. Warrants. Sealing orders. Edicts. The hands sign and sign and sign, and each signature lands with a sound like a knuckle cracking inside an empty room.

But the man attached to the hands —

The man is *flickering.*

That is the only word Casper has ever found for it. The king sits on his throne and flickers like a candle in a draft, present and absent, solid and see-through, and when the light passes through him just right Casper can see straight through the royal chest into the hollow behind it, and the hollow is not empty the way a room is empty.

The hollow is empty the way a *shell* is empty.

*He isn't dead,* Casper tells Xanthe, who has gone very still beside him, her breath coming fast and shallow. *Dead would be kinder. Dead is finished. He's — used.*

And then, because she needs to understand, because everyone needs to understand and no one ever does, he lets the vision tilt and shows her what sits inside the shell.

* * *

It is old.

That is the first thing, and the only true thing, and everything else about it is a shape it borrows so that minds can hold it without breaking. Old. Older than the kingdom. Older than the word for kingdom. It was old when the dragons were young, and the dragons were old when this city was mud and reed-huts, and it remembers all of it the way a scar remembers the knife.

In the vision it has no face. It has a *mouth.* A mouth like a crack in stone, widening, narrowing, tasting the air of the throne room, and every word the king speaks pours out of that crack first. Every decree. Every sentence of death. The parasite shapes them in the dark behind the crown and pushes them up through the king's throat like a puppeteer hauling on strings, and the king's mouth opens, and the king's voice — a real voice once, Casper can almost hear its ghost, warm and tired and human — says the words it is made to say.

*By order of His Majesty, the practice of charm-craft is forbidden throughout the realm.*

*By order of His Majesty, the witch-hunters are granted authority of life and death.*

*By order of His Majesty, let the ash-markets burn, and let none mourn what is lost in them.*

Xanthe makes a sound. Small. Like something tearing quietly.

*You've heard those words,* Casper says. *Everyone has. But listen to how they're said. Listen to what they're for.*

Because here is the thing the coal has shown him, night after night after night, until he stopped sleeping and started simply watching: the parasite is not cruel for pleasure. It is cruel for *harvest.* Every edict, every burning, every family dragged into the street by Veyra's hunters — all of it is threshing. Fear is the grain. Grief is the grain. The parasite drinks the terror of a whole city the way Xanthe drinks her morning tea, slowly, daily, with an appetite that never once diminishes. And magic — magic most of all. Every charm confiscated and fed to the flames feeds it twice, once in the burning and once in the despair of the one who made it.

It wore kings before this one. Casper has seen them, in the deep layers of the vision: a procession of crowned shells stretching back past the founding of Aldermarch, each one flickering, each one hollow, each one signing and signing until it wore out and was replaced by the next willing body. Kings die. The thing behind the mouth does not. It simply waits in the dark between reigns, patient as rot, for the next hand to climb onto the throne and offer itself up.

*Why him?* Xanthe's voice, in the vision, is thin as paper. *This king. Why did it choose him?*

And Casper, because the coal insists on honesty even when honesty is a wound, shows her.

* * *

He shows her a garden.

Twenty years ago. The same throne room, but alive — windows open, sunlight lying in honest bars on the floor, and a younger man kneeling in the corner of it with dirt under his fingernails, planting something in a clay pot. A private joke, maybe. A king who grew basil by his own throne because his mother had grown basil, because some part of him refused to become the kind of man who only touched gold.

The parasite came for him the way it comes for all of them. Not with claws. With an *offer.* In the vision Casper hears it speak its opening words, the same words it has spoken to every king since the first: *You are so tired. Let me carry it for you. Let me be your strength, and you will never need to doubt again.*

And the young king — tired, grieving, newly crowned over a kingdom already cracking — had said yes.

That was all it took. Yes.

The pot of basil is still in the throne room. Casper shows her that too: a dried brown skeleton of a plant, twenty years dead, gathering dust behind the throne while the shell of the man who planted it signs away the lives of his people. No one waters it. No one dares touch anything near the throne. The plant died the week the king stopped being the king.

*He's still in there,* Casper says, and hates how much he wants it to be true. *Underneath. I can hear him sometimes, at the bottom of the hollow. He counts. All day, every day, he counts — the names on the warrants. He knows every name. He counts them like penance and then his hand keeps signing anyway.*

*Can it be undone?*

He doesn't answer right away. In the vision, silence is a color, and the color of this silence is the grey of banked coals, which is the exact color of the thing sleeping in Casper's chest.

*There's a way,* he says finally. *There's always a way. Fire unbinds what fire bound. But the fire that's left in the world is—*

He stops. Because the coal in his chest has done a thing it has never done before.

It has gone quiet.

Not resting-quiet. Listening-quiet. And Casper, who has lived inside this fire since the night his village burned and everything he loved went up in light, knows with sudden sick certainty what it is listening to:

Him. His hesitation. Measuring whether the boy who carries it is strong enough for what must come, or whether it will need to find another vessel — a newer one, a warmer one — before the work is done.

* * *

The vision tears.

Not gently. The throne room peels back like burnt paper, and beneath it there is only the dark between heartbeats, and in that dark something vast turns its attention toward them — not the parasite, not yet, but the vision itself, or whatever stands behind visions, and Casper feels Xanthe's grip crush his fingers white.

*Enough,* she says, and it is not a question. *Casper. Enough. Bring us back.*

He tries. The coal does not move. For one long, endless moment Casper hangs in the dark holding a stranger's hand, and the coal holds *him*, weighing him like a taster weighing a spoonful, deciding if he is worth keeping—

Then he bites down on the inside of his cheek until blood blooms, and pain is a language the coal respects, and the world slams shut around them like a book.

* * *

Floorboards. Copper and thyme. Lamplight.

Casper comes back to himself kneeling on Xanthe Dunmore's kitchen floor, both their hands pressed flat to the boards as if the house might drop away beneath them. His nose is bleeding. Hers is too — the vision takes tolls from everyone who walks it. Somewhere outside, the watch-bell rings the hour, ordinary and stupid and beautiful.

Xanthe pulls her hand back slowly. She wipes her nose with her wrist. She looks at him the way she must look at dishes that have come out of the king's kitchen smelling faintly of almonds — measuring how bad it is, and whether there is any antidote in the world for it.

"The king," she says. Her voice is steady. Her hands are not. "The king I have sworn my immunity to. The law I have spent three years hiding from. You're telling me neither of them was ever real. That the thing signing the warrants isn't even *him.*"

"It's wearing him."

"And you." Her eyes drop to his chest, to the place where his skin glows faintly through the soot-streaks of his shirt, ember-pink, pulsing slow. "That fire in you. It's the same kind of old. Don't tell me it isn't. I saw its face in there."

Casper opens his mouth to deny it.

The coal rises instead, warm against his ribs, patient as rot, and says nothing at all — which is answer enough.

Xanthe stands. She crosses to the window, checks the street the way hunted people check streets, and when she turns back her face has closed into something hard and practical and terrified, and she says the words that will set everything after them burning:

"There's a man who moves things the crown doesn't want moved. Charm-broker. Smuggler. If anyone can get you out of this city before the hunters find that glow under your shirt, it's Merrick." A pause. Her jaw tightens. "And if you're wrong about any of this — if you've led that fire *here* — I will never forgive myself. So we go tonight, and we pray Merrick is the man I think he is."

Casper says nothing. He is listening to the coal.

It is humming, very softly, a note like a struck bell far underground.

It already knows Merrick's name.
