## 4. The Fence's Price

Merrick kept his counting-house behind the tallow-chandler's on Rope Walk, where the smell of rendered fat masked every other scent in the district — including, Xanthe had always supposed, the smell of guilt. She had traded with him for three years. He had never once asked where her charms came from, and she had never once asked what happened to them after they left her hands. That was the contract. That was the whole beauty of it: two people who agreed to know as little of each other as possible.

Now she stood at his door with a child's hand in hers and needed to know everything.

"Merrick." She knocked twice, then once — their old signal. "It's Xanthe. Open."

The bolt scraped back and there he was, round-faced and soft-eyed as ever, a man who looked like he'd been built for comfort and had simply never gotten around to it. His gaze went to her face first, then dropped to Casper, and something flickered behind the softness. Calculation, maybe. Or fear.

"Inside," he said. "Quickly."

The counting-house was warm and close, lit by a single lamp that turned everything the color of old honey. Shelves ran floor to ceiling, but no coins sat on them — only jars. Charms in jars, hundreds of them, each tagged in Merrick's neat clerk's hand: *love-knots, mild; fever-bones; one (1) storm-glass, cracked, discount*. The great gray market of forbidden things, catalogued like buttons.

"You look terrible," Merrick said, which from him was nearly tenderness.

"I need a favor that will make you hate me."

"Those are the only favors worth doing." He poured three cups of small beer without asking, and Xanthe watched Casper take his and hold it in both hands, not drinking, watching Merrick with those unsettling still eyes. The boy hadn't spoken since the vision — if that was what it was — except once, at dawn, when he'd said in his rusted little voice: *"He signs while he sleeps. The hand does not rest."*

She hadn't slept either.

"There's a child," she said. "He needs to leave the city. Quietly, tonight, through a gate nobody watches. I'll pay. I'll pay more than I have, which means I'll owe you, which you'll enjoy."

Merrick turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "What kind of child needs that kind of leaving?"

"The kind the Edict would burn."

The lamp guttered. Outside, somewhere down Rope Walk, a dog began to bark and was kicked quiet.

"Xanthe." Merrick set the cup down. His voice had changed — lost its warmth, gained weight. "Do you know what the hunters have been doing this month? Not sweeping. *Buying.* Pardons. Full pardons, sealed by the Lord Protector himself, for any broker who delivers a source. A real source. Something above street-charms." He smiled, and it was the saddest smile she had ever seen on him. "I told them I had nothing. I meant it. I have nothing."

"But," Xanthe said.

"But you just walked into my house and told me you're harboring exactly that." He wouldn't meet her eyes now. He studied his own hands, those soft white hands that had counted her charms so gently for three years. "They came yesterday, Xanthe. They showed me my name on a pardon scroll. They said all I had to do was wait. And here you are. Here you both are."

The floor did not move, but Xanthe felt it tilt anyway. Three years. Three years of careful, lonely competence, of trusting no one — except, apparently, in the place beneath the trust where she'd kept one name after all.

"You could lie to them," she said quietly. "Say you never saw us."

"And if they search me? If they find out later? A pardon keeps a man alive, girl. It doesn't keep him safe." He finally looked up, and his eyes were wet, which somehow made it worse. "I'm sorry. I truly am. You were the best supplier I ever had. Run. I'll give you until the bell — I'll swear I heard you heading for Rivergate—"

Casper stood up.

He didn't say anything. He simply set down the untouched beer, took Xanthe's sleeve, and pulled, and there was such absolute certainty in the gesture that she moved with him before her mind caught up. They reached the back door as the front one burst open — not knocked, *burst*, hinges shrieking — and lamplight flooded the counting-house along with boots, six pairs at least, and a voice like a drawn blade:

"Witch-finder Veyra. Nobody moves."

Xanthe didn't move. She ran.

* * *

The ash-market at night was a different country. By day it was the city's grudging concession to the Edict of Ash — the one legal market where nothing enchanted might be sold, where vendors sold ordinary knives and ordinary bread and swore oaths over their stalls to prove their goods were clean. By night it was abandoned, stall-shutters rattling in the wind off the foundries, the ground glittering faintly with slag and cinder that no broom had ever fully conquered.

Xanthe knew its geometry the way she knew the palace kitchens: every alley mouth, every dead-end, every gap between the tanner's wall and the cooperage where a thin adult got stuck and a thin child slipped through like water. She shoved Casper ahead of her into the first of these and followed, skinning her shoulder raw on the boards, hearing behind her the crash of hunters pouring into the market square.

"Split and sweep! Torch the stalls as you go — flush them out!"

*Torch the stalls.* Her stall. The little corner pitch she'd maintained under a cousin's name, where she sold — had sold — nothing but honest soap and honest candles, her camouflage, her cover. She heard the shutters splinter and felt the loss like a tooth coming loose.

"They're burning it," Casper whispered. It wasn't a question. He had stopped at the mouth of the tanneries' lane, looking back, and the firelight touched his soot-streaked face so that for a moment he seemed carved out of it, a child made of cinders.

"It's just wood," Xanthe said. And then, because lying to him felt suddenly impossible: "It was the last thing I owned that was mine."

Behind them, orange light climbed. The hunters were working methodically, stall by stall, and the fire was spreading faster than method should allow — the wind off the foundries feeding it, the dry summer turning the whole market into a wick. Somewhere a man screamed, a vendor who'd been sleeping in his pitch, and Xanthe's stomach clenched. This was the arithmetic of the Edict: burn ten innocent stalls to catch one guilty woman, and call the ten collateral.

"This way." She hauled Casper left, past the rendering sheds, into the labyrinth of the old tannery yards where the vats stood empty and the lime pits breathed their ghost of rot. Her lungs burned. Her legs shook — not from running, she realized, but from something older. From the vision still lodged in her like a splinter. *The king is an empty vessel. Something older than kingdoms speaks through his mouth.*

And Merrick's face as he refused to look at her. *Here you both are.*

She had spent three years believing the danger wore a crown and carried edicts. She had never imagined it wearing a friend's face and holding a cup of small beer.

Casper stopped dead ahead of her, and she nearly bowled him over.

Ahead, at the far gate of the tannery yard — the one that opened onto Cooper's Stair and freedom — stood two hunters with torches, silhouetted against the glow of the burning market. Blocking the way out. And behind Xanthe, at the way they'd come, more light bloomed. They weren't chasing her anymore. They were *herding* her. Veyra hadn't burst into Merrick's counting-house hoping to catch them. She'd let them run because a running target chooses its own path, and Xanthe had chosen exactly the path a hunted charm-seller would choose.

The underground wasn't broken, she thought, cold settling into her bones. The underground had been bought. Every fence, every smuggler's gate, every favor she might call in — purchased in advance with pardons. There was no exit from the city that didn't lead through the crown's hands.

Casper tugged her sleeve. He pointed — not at the hunters, not at the fire, but down, at a grate in the tannery floor, half-hidden under spilled oak bark. Old drainage, leading toward the river. Toward the dark.

And then he spoke, softly, in that rusted voice, and the words were not in the common tongue. They were in the First Tongue, the language that made her hidden charms hum in their jar at home, and though she understood none of it, she understood *this*: he was telling her the grate was safe. Telling her the way the dragons' language tells things — as fact, as law, as something older than doubt.

Above them the fire roared, and through it, cutting like a blade through smoke, came Veyra's voice, amplified by some charmed horn, rolling across the ash-market:

"DUNMORE! You've seen what we do to shelters. Come out with the child, and I burn only you!"

Xanthe looked at the grate. At the dark beneath it. At the child who knew her true name and was offering her the only road left, down into the black and the wet, away from every life she had built.

She knelt and put her fingers under the grate's edge.

It was heavier than it looked. Of course it was. Everything worth lifting was. She braced, spat on her hands, and pulled — and as the iron groaned free, she heard Casper say one word more in the First Tongue, a word that sounded like a struck bell, like an ending, like a beginning:

*"Down."*
