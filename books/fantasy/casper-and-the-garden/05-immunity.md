## 5. Immunity

The tanneries stank of lime and rot, and Xanthe had never been so grateful for a smell in her life.

No one came to the old tanneries. The pits had gone dry a generation ago when the river shifted and took the trade downstream with it, leaving behind a honeycomb of stone vats and curing sheds that the city forgot rather than abandoned. The air itself was a kind of ward — thick with tannin and ammonia, sour enough to make even the witch-hunters wrinkle their noses. Superstition clung to places like this. People said the old tanners' ghosts still walked, still scraped the hides of anyone who trespassed.

Let them believe it. Fear kept better watch than any charm she could sell.

Xanthe lowered herself into an empty vat — one of the smaller ones, its stone walls worn smooth as river rock — and pulled Casper down beside her before she dared light the stub of candle she'd stolen from a shrine niche on the way through the ash-market. The flame wavered green for a heartbeat, tasting of the smoke they'd run through, then steadied.

Behind them, the market burned. She had heard it in their flight: the crackle of her stall going up, Merrick's voice raised high and clear over the chaos — *there, there, that's the charmer, that's the poison-witch* — and boots, so many boots. She had not looked back. Looking back was how people died in stories, and Xanthe Dunmore had no intention of becoming a story.

Casper sat pressed against her side, small and shaking, soot streaked from his hairline to his chin like he'd wept ash. His chest rose and fell too fast. She put two fingers against his sternum, out of habit more than anything — the same gesture she used checking a patron's pulse at the table — and felt it: that dying coal beneath his ribs, faint but furious, flaring against her touch like a banked ember breathed upon.

"Slowly," she said. "Breathe slowly. Panic burns fuel."

He looked up at her. "You sound like the dragons."

"I've never met a dragon."

"You talk like them anyway." He closed his eyes and made himself breathe — four counts in, six counts out, a rhythm she recognized from her own worst nights after the first year of dosings, when her body had tried to reject everything and she'd taught herself to outlast the cramps by counting. Someone had taught him the same trick. Or something had.

She let him rest. She did not let herself.

Her hands were still trembling, and she hated them for it. Three years of steady work undone in one night — not the poisons, those she could handle, those were just chemistry and courage — but the betrayal. Merrick. Twelve years she'd dealt through him, twelve years of splitting coin and keeping each other's secrets because mutual ruin was the only loyalty either of them trusted. And he'd sold her for a pardon. Sold *her*, and worse, sold the boy — a child, a fire-child, straight into the hands of men who burned things like him in Copper Square while crowds sang hymns.

She should have known. That was the bitterest cut of it. She *should* have known, because she'd built her whole life on knowing that everyone had a price, including herself. She'd simply assumed Merrick's was higher.

The candle guttered. Somewhere above them, water dripped through the collapsed roof into a dry pit, patient as a metronome. Casper's breathing slowed. Sleep took him between one breath and the next, sudden as a trapdoor, the way children sleep and soldiers don't.

Xanthe stayed awake and thought about the hunter-captain's face.

Veyra. She'd seen her only once before tonight, across the width of the Edict Court, and even then the woman had radiated certainty like a stove gives off heat. A true believer. Those were the dangerous ones — not the corrupt hunters you could buy, but the ones who genuinely thought the world would be cleaner without people like Xanthe in it. Tonight Veyra had stood in the burning market and looked directly at where Xanthe crouched behind a collapsing awning, and her hand had risen, and she had spoken a word —

— and nothing had happened.

Xanthe turned her own left hand over in the candlelight now, examining it as if she'd never seen it before. Calluses on the fingertips from handling taster's tools. Old chemical stains in the creases that no pumice would lift. Nothing special. Nothing marked.

And yet Veyra's word — and Xanthe knew enough now to know it had been a word of the First Tongue, or something wearing its shape — had broken against her like a wave against a seawall. She'd felt it come. That was the thing she couldn't stop turning over. She'd felt the spell arrive, felt it press against her skin, cold and grasping, hunting for purchase, for a seam to slip into, for the little hook every charm needed to catch flesh and mind —

— and found nothing. Found her.

Three years of poison. Arsenic Mondays. Belladonna on feast days, graduated doses timed to the king's menus. The slow accumulation of things meant to kill her, taken willingly, survived stubbornly, until her blood was less a river than a moat. She'd built her immunity the way a mason builds a wall — one stone at a time, each dose a little worse than the last, until the mortar set.

But she had never considered what else might be locked out by a wall like that.

Enchantment was poison. Wasn't it? That was the whisper she'd heard in Casper's vision of the hollow king — the parasite wore the crown and spoke through the mouth, and every edict signed in the king's hand carried a thread of that ancient will in it, binding judges and hunters and whole congregations to obedience without their ever feeling the leash. Magic didn't knock. It seeped in, quiet as arsenic, and remade a person from the marrow outward.

And her marrow was a graveyard of every poison the kingdom's alchemists could brew.

She sat very still in the dry vat, listening to the drip of water and the far-off murmur of the burning market, and let the idea finish assembling itself.

The parasite's magic could not grip her.

Not the hunter's word-spells. Not the edicts, if edicts carried enchantment in their ink. Not the thing in the king. Her body — ruined, hardened, three-years-poisoned body — refused all of it the way a full cup refuses more wine.

Merrick had called her damage. The court physicians had called her constitution remarkable and moved on. The Edict had called her a criminal waiting to be caught. It had taken a feral child and a burning market for Xanthe Dunmore to understand that the poison she'd swallowed for a living was the single most valuable substance in Ashfall City.

Her damage was her armor.

A laugh climbed up out of her chest — small, cracked, disbelieving — and she strangled it before it woke the boy. But it left something behind in its wake, something warm and unfamiliar sitting low in her belly.

Purpose had a taste. She knew tastes. This one was iron and green wood, and she wanted more of it.

* * *

Casper woke at grey dawn, instantly alert the way animals are, and found her already sitting cross-legged at the vat's rim with a stick and the flat dust of the tannery floor spread smooth before her.

"Teach me," she said.

He blinked. "Teach you?"

"The First Tongue. All of it. Everything you know." She tapped the dust. "We'll start with whatever word the captain threw at me last night. I want to know what nearly killed me."

"It wouldn't have killed you." He crawled up beside her, knees dirty, hair a crow's nest. He studied her face with that unnervingly direct gaze he had, as if reading text written under her skin. Then, softly: "It bounced off you. I saw it. Like rain off slate."

"So I'm told. Teach me anyway."

"Why?" And there it was — the wariness, old beyond his years. He'd been handed from person to person, used and hunted; he knew better than to give power away free. "Everyone wants the Tongue. Hunters kill for hearing it. Kings empty themselves to hold it. Why do *you* want it?"

Xanthe considered lying. It was her reflex, her craft — a plausible half-truth, shaped and polished, cheap as a luck-charm and worth about as much. She looked at the boy instead, at the soot and the exhaustion and the ember breathing under his ribs, and she gave him the truth, because he was the first creature in three years she'd owed truth to.

"Because last night a woman pointed magic at me and it broke. Because I spent three years swallowing poison to keep a man alive who turns out to be a puppet, and I am done surviving other people's designs. Because if I learn your language, I can do more than duck and run." She turned her palm up. The calluses caught the dawn light. "Because everything I am is armor, and armor is wasted sitting in a hole. I want to walk into that palace and say something that *cannot be ignored*. And I suspect your tongue is the only way to do it."

Casper stared at her. Something shifted in his face — the feral caution easing by a degree, replaced by something almost like hope, which was more frightening to him than any hunter.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. First lesson."

He drew a line in the dust. It wasn't a letter, quite — more a gesture frozen mid-motion, a stroke that seemed to lean into being spoken.

"This is *vessh*," he said. "It means *I refuse*. It's the first word, because the dragons said everything began when fire refused to be owned." He glanced up. "Say it."

"*Vessh.*"

"Not with your mouth. With here." He pressed his small fist below his own sternum, where the coal lived. "The Tongue isn't sounds. Sounds are just the shadow of it. You have to mean refusal with your whole body. Can you do that?"

Xanthe thought of Merrick's voice ringing over the burning market. Of the king signing death warrants with a borrowed hand. Of three years of swallowing poison with a polite smile while everyone congratulated her on her strong stomach.

"Yes," she said. "*Vessh.*"

The word left her strangely — not as sound but as pressure, a pulse that pushed outward through the tannery dust and scattered the letters they'd drawn. The candle flame snapped upright. In the vat behind them, the stagnant rainwater shivered though nothing touched it.

Casper went very still. His eyes were enormous.

"That's not supposed to happen," he whispered. "That takes most people years. The Tongue doesn't answer strangers." He reached out and touched her forearm, wonder cracking his voice wide open. "It answered *you*. Xanthe — it answered you like it knows you."

Above them, faint but unmistakable, came a sound that emptied the wonder from the morning: iron-shod boots on stone, many of them, moving with the unhurried discipline of soldiers who believed they could not fail.

A voice followed, level and carrying, echoing off the vats:

"Xanthe Dunmore. Captain Veyra of the Edict Court. Come out, and the boy keeps his fire another day."
