## 6. Hunters at the Gate

The tanneries stank of lime and rot and old money's indifference, and Xanthe had never loved a smell more.

She crouched in the shadow of a drying shed, Casper pressed against her side, watching the lane that wound up from the ash-market. Smoke still rose from the direction of her stall — her stall, her awning, her little kingdom of outlawed charms — and the smoke was the wrong color for wood alone. They had burned everything. Her stock. her ledgers. The false bottom where she kept the good things.

"They'll come here," she murmured. "Tanneries are the only place left with no eyes on it."

Casper didn't answer. He hadn't answered much since the market, since Merrick's face had gone smooth and sorry as he'd said *I have children, Xanthe* while the hunters closed like a fist. The boy just watched the lane with those ember-dark eyes, one hand curled over his chest as if he could cup the coal inside him and keep it from guttering.

The First Tongue words he'd taught her sat in her mouth like stones. Three of them. *Vash. Kelum. Oren.* Fire, door, open. She turned them over the way she'd once turned over poison antidotes, learning their weight, their interactions, the ways they might kill her.

Hooves on cobblestone. Not one horse. Six.

"Down," she breathed, and pulled him into the reeking ditch beside the shed, into water gone gray with lime and hair and worse.

The riders came slow, the way Veyra's riders always came — no shouting, no torchlight theatrics. Captain Sable Veyra did not need theater. She needed only to arrive, and the city arranged itself around her like iron filings around a lodestone.

Xanthe counted them through a gap in the shed boards. Six riders, and a seventh figure walking ahead of the horses on foot, unhurried, lantern held high.

Veyra herself.

* * *

Sable Veyra had burned forty-one witches in eleven years, and she remembered every name.

That was the part the pamphlets never printed, the part the crowds didn't want to see: she wrote each name in a ledger bound in gray calfskin, with the date, the crime, and the charm recovered, because the Edict of Ash was not cruelty. It was memory. It was the city refusing to forget what magic had done when it was free — the Dragon Wars, the burning decades, the fields that still would not grow. A witch-finder who forgot her dead became a butcher. Veyra refused to be either.

Tonight's entry already half-written itself in her head. *Dunmore, Xanthe. Taster to His Majesty. Crimes: unlicensed charm-craft, distribution to commoners, harboring an uncategorized entity.* She'd liked Dunmore, in the distant way one likes a well-made lock. Competent. Quiet. The kind of woman the city depended on without ever thanking.

Which was precisely why it cut so deep. The dependable ones were always the worst. They knew every crack in the wall they were selling.

"Captain." Sergeant Illo reined in beside her at the tannery gate. "No lights. But the dogs won't come past the second pit."

"The dogs are smarter than we are," Veyra said. "Lime burns their noses. Spread out. She's cornered, and cornered poisoners do desperate arithmetic."

"And the boy?"

Veyra looked at the dark sheds, the hanging hides ghost-pale in the lantern light, like a field of flayed souls. "The boy is the whole reason we're here, Illo. Whatever Dunmore is, she's small. What she's carrying is not." She dismounted, boots sinking in the muck, and drew her iron rod from its holster — plain, blackened, unadorned, the tool of her trade. Iron grounded magic. Iron made the law physical.

Her grandmother had died in the Dragon Wars' last year. Veyra had been told this so many times it had stopped being a memory and become a bone in her body. Everything she did, she did so that no child would ever again watch the sky catch fire.

"Take the east row," she said quietly. "And Illo — if the boy runs, take him alive. Whatever he is, he's a victim before he's a criminal. That's the order of things."

It was the last order she gave as a captain whose word meant what she believed.

* * *

They heard the hunters fanning out through the sheds — boots in lime sludge, the soft clink of iron rods against belt buckles.

Casper's hand found hers. Small. Cold. Trembling slightly, or perhaps that was the fire in him shivering.

"I can burn them," he whispered, and his voice had that doubled quality again, the child's voice with something vast underneath it. "I don't want to. But I can."

"No." The refusal came out of her faster than thought. "You burn one hunter tonight and there's no story left but dragon. No trial. No mercy. They'll hunt you until the end of the world, and they'll be right to." She swallowed. "We run, or we hide. That's the arithmetic."

"You always talk about numbers," Casper said. There was almost a smile in it.

"Numbers keep people alive."

Boots crunched closer. A lantern glow swept the gap between sheds, ten feet away, then five. Xanthe's mind ran its cold calculations — two exits, both watched; lime water to the knees; three words in a language that made her hidden charms hum through the wet cloth at her back. *Vash. Kelum. Oren.*

Fire. Door. Open.

She had never used the charms on a person. Never once, in six years of the trade. It was the line she'd drawn, the one clean thing about her dirty living — *I sell luck, I sell wards, I sell small mercies, but I am not a witch.*

The lantern light touched the edge of their shed.

"Casper," she whispered. "Teach me the fourth word."

His head came up sharply. "There isn't—"

"There's always a fourth word."

He stared at her, and the coal behind his ribs flared visible through his shirt, a faint orange pulse like a heartbeat seen through skin. Then, very softly, in the language that had once commanded dragons:

"*Sorath.*"

"It means 'unmake,'" he said. "It means you give something permission to stop being what it is. You can't take it back, Xanthe. Nothing you say after *sorath* is ever unsaid."

The shed door burst inward.

* * *

Veyra saw the woman first — mud-slick, gray-lipped from the lime water, hands empty and raised, exactly as compliant as a poisoner pretending surrender. And then she saw the boy, and the ledger in her mind went blank.

The child glowed. Not metaphorically. Beneathan soaked shirt, a light pulsed slow and deep, the color of coals at the bottom of a dying fire, and the air around him tasted — Veyra actually tasted it, copper and hot stone — of something out of the oldest pages of her grandmother's stories.

"By the Ashes," Illo breathed behind her.

"Dunmore." Veyra kept her voice level, her iron rod steady. Eleven years of practice. "Step away from the child."

"He hasn't done anything. He's seven years old, Captain. He's starving and terrified and he speaks a language your Edict doesn't even have a word for yet."

"That's what worries me." Veyra took one step forward, muck sucking at her boots. "Give me the boy, and I swear on my grandmother's grave you'll see a tribunal, not a pyre. I don't burn children, Dunmore. Whatever you've been told about us."

"She's telling the truth," Casper said suddenly, staring at Veyra with those banked-fire eyes. "She believes it. All of it. She thinks the fire is the monster."

"Of course I believe it," Veyra said. "I've read the histories. I've walked the fields that still won't grow—"

"The fields won't grow," the boy said, "because your king salted them himself. Four hundred years ago. Before the parasite wore him."

The lantern flames all leaned sideways at once, toward the child, as if listening.

Veyra felt something shift in her chest — a splinter of doubt, thin as a needle, sliding between the plates of eleven years of certainty. She crushed it down. Doubt was how witches worked. Doubt was the first charm they ever sold you.

"Iron, now," she snapped. "Take them both—"

And Xanthe Dunmore spoke.

Not pleading. Not the desperate arithmetic Veyra had predicted. She spoke four words in a language that made Veyra's teeth ache and the iron rod in her hand go suddenly, impossibly heavy, and the world *changed*.

* * *

*Sorath,* Xanthe said to the iron.

To the law itself, made metal, made physical, made *grip*. Give it permission to stop being what it is.

The rod in Veyra's hand softened like wax, sagged, and poured itself into the muck as a puddle of dull slag. Around the yard, five more rods died the same death, and the hunters' shouts turned to something higher, rawer — fear, the specific fear of armed men feeling their arms leave them.

The lanterns guttered. The lime-water in the ditch began to steam.

Xanthe hadn't moved. Her hands were still raised. But they weren't raised in surrender anymore; they were raised the way a conductor raises them, and the light coming off Casper had spread to her, or through her, a faint gold threading under her skin like veins of ore.

"Sergeant!" Veyra's voice cracked on it. "Netters! Ropes! Anything that isn't—"

But Casper had already taken her hand, and the boy said one more word — *kelum*, door — and the drying shed behind them simply opened, its boards swinging wide like a mouth, and beyond the boards wasn't the tannery at all but the night river, black and silver, thirty yards of impossible distance folded into a stride.

Veyra lunged. She was fast — eleven years of chasing made her fast — and her fingers actually brushed the boy's shoulder as the doorway swallowed them both.

Then the shed was a shed again, ordinary, stinking, empty, and her hand closed on nothing but lime-damp wood.

For a long moment nobody moved. The hunters stood in a ring around their captain, weaponless, breathing hard, watching the puddles of their own melted law cool in the mud.

Veyra looked down at the slag that had been her iron rod. Her grandmother's Edict. Her life's whole architecture, unmade in four syllables by a food-taster she'd once respected.

"She saved the boy," Illo said hoarsely. "You saw it. She could have burned us. Any of us. She melted the irons and nothing else."

"I saw." Veyra's voice came out flat and strange. She knelt, touched the cooling metal, and found herself thinking, absurdly, of her ledger. Of forty-one names. Of which column this night belonged in now — and whether the columns themselves had been drawn by the right hand.

Because the boy had said *parasite*. And the lanterns had listened.

"Captain?" Illo prompted. "Orders? Every gate, every ferry — we can still close the net."

Veyra rose slowly. In the gray calfskin ledger of her mind, a pen hovered over fresh ink, and for the first time in eleven years, she did not know what crime she was writing down.

Or whose.

"Get me the palace archives," she said finally. "Everything on the king's health. Sealed records. Tonight."

Illo blinked. "Captain, that requires a warrant from—"

"From the king. Yes." She looked north, toward the palace hill, where the royal banner hung motionless in the windless dark — and she realized, with a cold that had nothing to do with the lime water, that the banners had been motionless for weeks. That the king had not been seen in public since midwinter. That the edicts had kept signing themselves in his hand, hour after hour, as if the man never slept.

As if the man never *ate*.

"Dunmore poisoned kings for a living," Veyra murmured, half to herself. "Maybe she finally tasted something worth spitting out."

She mounted, and the hunters followed their captain away from the river, away from the gates, toward the one place none of them had ever dared to look too closely.

Above the palace, unseen by any of them, a single spark crossed the night sky — small, orange, stubborn — heading west, toward the old tannery road, carrying a taster, a child, and the last coal of dragon-fire toward the throne that hungered for it.
