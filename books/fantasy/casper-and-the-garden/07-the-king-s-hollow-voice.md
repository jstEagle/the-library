## 7. The King's Hollow Voice

The kitchens of Ashfall Palace still smelled the way they had three years ago, when Xanthe Dunmore had first walked through them with a taster's satchel and a forged letter of introduction: rendered fat and scorched onions, bay leaves gone brittle in the heat, the sour ghost of a hundred spilled wines. She stood in the shadow of the flour store with her back against cool stone and let the smell do what it had always done — settle her, steady her, remind her that she knew this place the way a surgeon knows a body.

She had come in through the ash-gate, hood up, carrying a basket of eels like any market woman with a delivery for the palace cook. No one had looked twice. No one ever did. The people who fed kings were invisible to kings, and that invisibility was the only armor she had left.

Above her, muffled by floors and marble and the weight of a kingdom's fear, the court was sitting. She could hear it in the rhythm of the servants' feet — faster than usual, heads down, no gossip. The king had called a session. The king had signed something. The king wanted witches.

*The king*, she thought, and the word tasted like copper.

Casper was two miles away in the tanneries, locked behind a door Merrick didn't know existed — one of the few mercies of Merrick's betrayal being that Merrick had never known everything. The boy had pressed his small hot hand to her cheek before she left and said something in the First Tongue that made the charms sewn into her hem shiver like struck bells. She hadn't asked what it meant. Some words you carry better unopened.

*The king is a door,* Casper had said instead, in trade-tongue, the night before. *I will show you the thing behind it.*

He had shown her. She had seen it — the throne room from above, as a dream sees, the crowned man at its center flickering like a candle in a draft, and something vast and patient coiled through him the way ivy coiled through a dead tree. She had woken with her teeth aching and her hands shaking and one thought repeating like a pulse: *it signs with his hand, it signs with his hand, it signs with his hand.*

Every edict. Every pyre. Every name on every warrant — including, soon, hers.

So she had come to see the door for herself.

* * *

The head cook was a woman named Berta whom Xanthe had once saved from a bad batch of elderflower wine — a small kindness, a drop of antidote slipped into a cup, never explained, never repaid until now. Berta took one look at her under the flour-store lamp and went grey.

"They said you were dead," she whispered.

"They say that about everyone eventually." Xanthe set the eel basket down. "I need the service stair to the royal apartments, and I need you to have never seen me."

"Xanthe—"

"Berta." She kept her voice gentle. "You remember the wine. I remember the wine. Let's leave it at that."

Berta's jaw worked. Then, with the practicality of a woman who had fed three coronations and outlived two poisonings, she wiped her hands on her apron and jerked her chin toward the far passage. "The stair by the buttery. Guards change at the hour bell — they're lazy and they take the change at the top of the stairs, not the bottom. You'll have forty heartbeats. Maybe fifty, if the wine at their post is good."

"Is it?"

"It's the king's own red." Berta's mouth twisted. "It's been good all year. That's the only reason half the household sleeps at night."

Xanthe paused. "Meaning what?"

Berta looked at her — really looked, the way women who work with heat and knives look, seeing straight through to the bone. "Meaning the king hasn't eaten in front of anyone in a year. Meaning he takes his meals alone and the plates come back untouched. Meaning the servants draw lots to deliver them, and the losers cry." She crossed herself with a flour-white hand, an old country gesture the Edict had never managed to burn out. "Whatever you're going to do up there — do it quick. That place eats people. I've watched it do it."

*So have I,* Xanthe thought. *From the outside. I just never knew it was eating him too.*

* * *

The service stair was narrow and smelled of tallow and old beeswax, and she climbed it with the eel knife in her sleeve and every charm on her body humming low and constant, like a hive in winter. At the top, a single door. Beyond it, the royal corridor, and the guards' post — she heard them now, the shuffle of dice, the wet click of a stoppered bottle. The hour bell began to toll.

She counted heartbeats. At thirty-one, the dice stopped. At thirty-eight, boots climbed the stair above — the change happening at the top, exactly as Berta promised, lazy men taking the long way to their leisure. At forty, she was through the door and into the king's corridor, and at forty-six she had slipped into the shadow of a tapestry depicting the First King's triumph over the last dragon, and stood with her heart slamming against the charms, waiting.

The tapestry was older than the dynasty. She'd never noticed that before. The dragon in it wasn't dying — it was bowing.

* * *

The royal apartments were warm. That was the first wrong thing. Ashfall Palace in the grip of the Edict was a cold place — fires banked by decree, candles rationed, the king's own austerity performed for a starving city. But here the air was hot and dry as a forge-room, and it smelled of something under the incense, something mineral and ancient, like the inside of a hill.

The second wrong thing was the light. Every lamp in the outer chamber had been lit, and every lamp had been turned to face the wall, so that the whole room glowed with reflected, sourceless light and the center of the room sat in a pool of shadow.

The king sat in the center of it.

He was smaller than his portraits. That was her first stupid, human thought — *he's smaller than his coins*. The man on the silver penny was broad and golden-bearded, a conqueror's jaw. This man was narrow in the shoulders, and his beard had gone grey at the edges, and his hands lay in his lap like two dead birds. He was wearing full ceremonial robes at midnight, alone, crown and all, as if he had dressed for a court that wasn't there.

He was talking. That was the third wrong thing. Low, continuous, in a voice like a man reading aloud to keep himself from screaming — and when Xanthe's eyes adjusted, she saw that he was talking to the empty chairs arranged before him. Six of them. He was holding session with no one.

"—and the petition from the weavers' guild is denied, the petition is denied, the petition is—"

His voice snagged. Stopped. He said, in a different tone entirely, thin and cracked and young: "Is someone there?"

Xanthe's whole body told her to run. She stepped out of the shadow instead.

The king looked up. And she saw what Casper had shown her, but from the inside this time, and it was worse. His eyes were wrong — not evil, not mad, but *flickering*, like a lamp about to gutter, and in the space between one flicker and the next she watched a man surface in them the way a drowning man surfaces, gasping, reaching, and go under again.

"You're the taster." His voice was hoarse with surprise. "The girl. Dunmore. They said you were dead."

"They say that about everyone eventually."

Something moved behind his eyes — not the parasite, she realized. Something smaller and more human. The corner of his mouth. Almost a smile. "You're not afraid of me. Everyone is afraid of me. Even the ones who aren't are lying about it."

"Should I be afraid of you?"

He was quiet for a long moment. The lamps hissed against the walls. When he spoke again, the voice had changed — lower, smoother, with a resonance no human throat should hold, and every charm on Xanthe's body went silent at once, as if the sound had pressed them flat.

"The witch of the ash-market," it said. "The poisoner who cannot be poisoned. We have read your file. We have written your file." The king's dead-bird hands lifted, turned palms up. "Come to see the law's own face? Come closer. It is very handsome. It has a crown."

Xanthe's hand closed on the eel knife. "I've come to see the man."

The flicker. The surfacing. For three heartbeats the king's face was only a face — exhausted, terrified, forty years older than the coin — and his eyes found hers with the desperation of a man looking up through ice.

"Three years," he whispered. "Three years it's been me and the dark and my own mouth saying things. I taste everything it makes me sign. Did you know that? It makes me *taste* them. Every name. Every warrant. It puts the ink in my mouth and I have to hold it there until my hand stops shaking, and then I sign, and it swallows." His breath came ragged. "You taste for a living. You of all people. You know what it is to have something put in your mouth that you cannot refuse."

Xanthe's throat had gone tight. "I know."

"The child." His fingers clawed at the armrests. "The fire-child. It's afraid of the child. I felt it afraid — do you understand, I *felt it feel*, and its fear was the first true thing I've been given in three years. It wants the child's coal. It will wear the boy the way it wears me, and the boy is small, and the boy is *bright*, and it will burn him down to the wick—"

The smooth voice rose through him like water filling a boot-print: "—the petition from the weavers' guild is denied—"

"—*please*." The king's hand shot out and caught her wrist, and his grip was nothing, a dying man's grip, but his eyes were everything, blazing up through the flicker one last time. "You're the only thing in this palace it can't grip. I watched it try, through my own eyes, when you were brought here to taste. It reached for your blood and your blood *refused it*. Three years of poison, girl. Three years of dying a little every day. It made you immune to everything — including *it*." His voice broke apart. "I don't remember my wife's face. I don't remember my son's voice. But I remember the First Tongue — it makes me remember, it makes me *speak it* when it signs, the old words, the binding words — I can give them to you, the ones it uses, and if you know the words it uses, you can—"

The lamps went out. All of them. Not blown — *drained*, light sucked backward into the walls, and the room went the color of the inside of a closed mouth.

When the nearest lamp guttered back to life, the king was sitting straight, hands folded, face smooth and golden in the reflected glow, and he was smiling at her with the coin's smile.

"You should not be here," he said pleasantly. "The Edict of Ash provides for visitors such as you. Captain Veyra has been *so* eager."

Xanthe stepped back toward the service door, and the king watched her go, and in the last flicker before she slipped through, she saw the man surface one final time — saw his lips move around three words of the First Tongue, slow and deliberate, shaped for her to read rather than hear.

She didn't know what they meant. But she knew they were the beginning of something, because every charm sewn into her clothes woke at once and sang.

She had the words. She had the man's blessing, and the man's plea, and the man's grief.

What she didn't have was time — because somewhere below her, in the ash-market dark, a bell had begun to ring, and it was not the hour bell.

It was the fire bell.

And it was ringing from the direction of the tanneries.
