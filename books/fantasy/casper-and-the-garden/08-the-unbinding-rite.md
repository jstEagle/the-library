## 8. The Unbinding Rite

The throne room of Vhalcrest smelled of lilies and old smoke, and Xanthe knew both scents were lies.

She had come up through the kitchens, as she had promised herself she would — the servants' stair behind the buttery, past the scullery boys who averted their eyes from her ash-stained cloak, because even now, with hunters at every gate and her face on the edict-boards in every square, no kitchen hand would betray the woman who had once pulled a poisoned plum from a serving tray with her bare fingers and kept walking. The palace trusted its poison to her. Old habit dies harder than kings.

Now she stood at the great doors of the audience hall, Casper's small hand swallowed in hers, and listened to the court murmur beyond like a hive of something winged and hungry.

*You can still turn around,* said the coward in her chest. *You have survived three years by never being the loudest thing in any room.*

But Casper looked up at her, soot still ground into the creases of his knuckles, and his eyes held that coal-light — dimmer now, she could see it, guttering like a candle in a draft — and he said, in the First Tongue, the word that meant *begin*.

It sounded like a door opening.

"Stay behind me," she whispered. "Whatever happens. Whatever I do. Stay behind me."

He nodded once, the way he did everything: without waste, without fear, as if fear were a currency he had never been taught to count.

The guards opened the doors because protocol demanded it — because a woman in a taster's grey could not be turned away from the king's hall, not when half the court still remembered her tasting for them, wine after wine, course after course, the little death-defying ritual they'd clapped for between acts of music. She walked down the length of the floor as three hundred faces turned to her, silk and velvet and gilded vanity, and somewhere among them Merrick stood with his wrists newly unbound, wearing the pardoner's white ribbon, and he did not look away.

That was the worst of it. He watched her come, and his eyes were wet, and he hated himself, and it changed nothing at all.

"Your Majesty," called the chamberlain, bewildered, "a taster has entered the—"

"The king will hear her." The voice came from the throne, and it was the voice she had heard through walls and keyholes for three years, smooth as poured lead, and it made every hair on her arms rise. "Let her approach. Let us all hear what the witch has to say."

A ripple went through the court. Not shock — relief. They wanted this. They wanted someone named, someone burned, someone small enough to blame for the cold in the granaries and the coughing in the lower city and the king's long strange silence. Xanthe understood then how easy tyrannies were to build: people *wanted* the fire. It warmed them.

She stopped ten paces from the dais and bowed, precisely, the bow of a servant. Beneath her cloak, against her sternum, Casper's charm-charcoal scratched faintly — alive, listening.

"Majesty," she said. "I have tasted your food for three years. I know what poisons you."

A laugh from the gallery — some young lord who thought wit was a weapon. The thing in the king's body did not laugh. Its head tilted, slow, birdlike, and the king's mouth smiled with too many degrees of curve.

"Do you," it said. "And here I believed you only knew what poisoned everyone else. Tell me, taster — what does a woman taste when she tastes for a god?"

"You're not a god." Her mouth had gone dry. She forced the next words out anyway, because terror was just another flavor and she had trained herself to swallow all of them. "You are older than gods and smaller than worms. You wear him because you cannot be anything yourself. And I am going to take him back."

The temperature of the room changed. Not dropped — *tightened*, as if the air itself had been gripped by a fist. Torches guttered. Somewhere a woman began quietly weeping without knowing why.

"You cannot," the parasite said pleasantly. "I have written the law against it. Edict of Ash, ninth clause: any tongue that speaks the First Tongue within the king's hearing shall be forfeit. Do you feel it, Xanthe Dunmore? The clause closing around you like a ring?" And it raised the king's hand, and the guards moved — six of them, halberds low, advancing with the terrible calm of men whose wills were not entirely their own. "I wrote you before you ever learned my name. Every word you speak here convicts you. Proceed."

*Proceed.* It wanted her to. Of course it did — it thought she had come alone, a lone witch with a stolen charm and a speech, something it could break in front of the court to seal its reign another decade. It did not know about the boy. It could not smell the fire yet; Casper had banked it, wrapped it in ash and silence, exactly as he'd taught her the First Tongue worked — *hide the ember inside the ordinary words.*

Her heart slammed against her ribs. This was the point of no return and she knew it the way she knew cyanide: instantly, bodily, completely.

She began to speak.

Not Vhalcresh. Not the trade-cant of the charm-markets. The First Tongue came out of her rough and mispronounced, a child's grammar in an adult's mouth, but the stones heard it anyway. She felt the hall answer her — a shiver through the flagstones, a hum in the iron chandeliers, the same answering hum her hidden charms had given off the night Casper first knocked. Words Casper had drilled into her over three days in the tanneries while hunters beat the streets above:

*What was bound, I name. What was bound, I see.*

The king screamed.

It was not the parasite's voice that screamed — that was the horror of it. It was *the king's* voice, raw and human and rusty from years of disuse, and the whole court flinched as one animal. For one heartbeat the man on the throne was visible: gaunt, gray-lipped, eyes wide and drowning, hands gripping the armrests as if holding onto a raft.

"Guards!" shrieked the chamberlain. "Seize her! In the king's name—"

They came fast. Xanthe kept speaking, louder, the syllables cracking in her throat, and Casper stepped out from behind her — disobeying her, the first disobedience of his short fierce life — and spread his arms, and the coal in his chest flared.

Light rolled off him like heat off a forge. Not flame — the court saw light and would swear forever after they'd seen flame — and the guards stopped as though they'd hit glass. One dropped his halberd and sat down where he stood, blinking like a man waking from a fever.

"Edict of Ash!" a voice cut through, high and official, and Xanthe knew it before she turned. Merrick, stepping forward, pardon-ribbon bright at his throat, testimony paper shaking in his grip. "This woman has trafficked in forbidden charms for three years — I sold them for her, I confess it, I've confessed it all — and this child is a vessel of dragon-fire, unregistered, unlawful, *unnatural*. By clause four, clause nine, clause eleven — seize them both, burn them both, the law is clear—"

His voice broke on *clear*. He was crying as he condemned her. She found, examining herself with a taster's detachment, that she felt almost nothing. Pity, maybe. The way you pity a rat that takes the bait because the alternative was starving.

"You sold me," she said to him, without stopping the rite, the words of the First Tongue continuing underneath her Vhalcresh like a second pulse. "Three years of splitting coin in the dark, Merrick. And you couldn't split one risk."

"They were going to take my hands," he whispered. "Xanthe, they were going to take my *hands*."

"Then you understand," she said, "exactly what you sold."

The parasite recovered. She felt it gather itself the way she'd once felt a slow poison gather in a dish — a thickening, a settling, intent pooling toward one purpose. The king rose from the throne, joints moving wrong, and when he spoke it spoke through him in a voice that filled the rafters and made the stained-glass saints seem to lean away:

**"STOP SPEAKING."**

The command hit the room like a physical blow. Courtiers dropped to their knees. The guards pressed their palms flat to the floor. Even Merrick folded, gasping. The words carried weight — the weight of every edict ever signed with that hand, every warrant, every burning, compounding interest on three centuries of signed cruelty.

Xanthe's jaw clenched shut. Her own muscles betrayed her, obeying the older law, the deeper signature. She stood rigid, throat locked, eyes streaming, and across the space between them the king-thing smiled.

"I have commanded kings," it said softly, conversationally, savoring. "Do you think I cannot command a cook? Your tongue is mine. Your lungs are mine. Kneel, taster, and I will let you live to taste again — there is always use for a woman who cannot die of what she drinks—"

*Casper.*

She couldn't say it aloud. She didn't need to. He had been watching her throat fight itself, and he understood, because he had spent his whole life understanding things no child should have to. He took one step forward, two, until he stood between her and the throne, a soot-streaked scrap of a boy facing a hollowed king, and he drew breath and spoke the First Tongue fluently, effortlessly, terribly — the language coming home to its last native speaker —

—and the command shattered.

Xanthe's jaw unlocked. Air flooded her lungs. And she saw the cost immediately, the way you see blood in water: the light around Casper dimmed. The glow that had stopped six guards shrank to a corona no wider than his shoulders. His knees buckled; he caught himself on his palms; and when he lifted his head, the coal in his eyes had gone from furnace-orange to the dull red of a dying stove.

One flame left, she thought. Maybe two. He's spending himself to keep my voice free.

*Keep speaking,* his eyes said, furious and pleading at once. *Don't you dare stop.*

So she didn't stop.

She planted her feet on the flagstones of the hall where kings had been crowned and witches had been judged, and she threw the words of the unbinding at the thing in the throne like a woman hurling poison into a well — every syllable Casper had taught her, every phrase she had practiced until her lips bled in the dark of the tannery loft, the naming, the seeing, the loosening:

*What was bound, I name. What was bound, I see. What was stolen, I call home. What wears the crown, I unmake.*

The parasite screamed with the king's mouth, and the king screamed with his own, and the two voices braided together into a sound the court would describe for the rest of their lives and never once get right. The throne's gilt cracked. The edict-boards hanging in the hall — EDICT OF ASH in letters of black iron — began, one by one, to drip molten.

The thing abandoned pretense. She felt it *leap* — a cold rushing presence pouring out of the king's body like smoke from a kicked hive, arrowing across the hall, straight for the smaller, brighter vessel standing guard before her.

Straight for Casper.

"No—" She lunged, arms out, knowing even as she moved that flesh was no wall against such a thing, that she was a woman of meat and poison throwing herself in front of ancient hunger—

—and it gripped her instead.

Cold closed around her spine, her skull, her heart, a hand of ice reaching for the levers of her — and found nothing to pull.

Because her blood was arsenic and nightshade and three years of every death the kitchens could brew, and whatever she was, she was not a vessel. She was a cup full of ruin. And the parasite had just drunk deep of a body that had spent thirty months learning to kill what lived inside it.

The cold convulsed. Screamed without a mouth.

Xanthe smiled through frozen lips, tasting copper and victory, and spoke the last line of the rite into the silence of a hall full of kneeling witnesses — the words that would either free a king or end her.

Behind her, Casper's final flame flared like a struck match in a dark room.

* * *

The light went white, then gold, then out.
