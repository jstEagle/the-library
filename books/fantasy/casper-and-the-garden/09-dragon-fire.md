## 9. Dragon-Fire

### Xanthe

The word left her mouth and the throne room stopped breathing.

She had spoken the First Tongue in fragments before — a syllable here, half a phrase there — the way a swimmer tests ice. But this was whole. This was *unbinding*, the rite Casper had taught her in the tanneries by candle stub and whisper, his small hands shaping the sounds like a boy folding paper birds: *this one opens, this one holds, this one lets go.*

The court did not understand her. That was the horror of it. Two hundred lords and ladies in silk gone grey with candle-smoke, and they heard only a woman's voice speaking nonsense at their king, and their faces showed nothing but confusion — because whatever the thing wearing the crown allowed them to feel, it felt first.

The king stood up from the throne.

No — the king was *pulled* up from the throne, like a puppet whose strings had remembered they existed. His spine arched. His jaw dropped too far. And from that opened mouth came a sound that made every torch in the hall gutter sideways, a sound with no throat behind it, vast and old and *hungry*.

**YOU DARE SPEAK TO ME IN THE TONGUE OF MY DEATH.**

Xanthe's knees wanted to fold. Her hands wanted to cover her ears. Three years of poisons had burned most of her fear out of her body the way lye burns wool, but what remained was enough to make her shake.

Casper's hand found hers. Small. Burning hot. He had insisted on being there — she had argued until her voice gave out — and now he stood beside her in the great hall in Merrick's borrowed coat three sizes too big, and his eyes were fixed on the king with something worse than fear.

Recognition.

"It knows me," he whispered. "Xanthe. It's looking at me."

The parasite turned its stolen face toward them. The king's eyes were wrong — not hollow as she'd imagined but *full*, brimming with a darkness that moved like oil on water, and when it spoke again it used the king's voice properly this time, warm, reasonable, the voice of a father to wayward children.

"Come here, boy," it said gently. "You've been carrying my inheritance all these miles. Let me take the weight of it."

"Veyra," Xanthe said, without turning her head. "Now."

Across the hall, the witch-hunter captain drew breath — and hesitated. One heartbeat. Two. Xanthe saw the war behind her eyes, the law she had served for twenty years cracking down the middle like river ice. Then Veyra did the thing no hunter had ever done in the history of the Edict of Ash.

She knelt. To a charm-seller. In front of the entire court.

"The hall is yours," Veyra said hoarsely. "Guards — *stand down*. That is an order given under my authority, and I will answer for it with my neck."

For a moment nothing moved. Then, one by one, spears lowered. Somewhere in the ranks a young soldier began quietly weeping, and Xanthe understood why: they had all felt it, hadn't they, all these years? The wrongness at the center of things. The edicts that punished kindness. The warrants signed in a hand that never tired of death. You can gaslight two hundred people only so long before some part of them starts screaming.

The parasite knew it too. She watched its composure crack like glaze on old pottery.

**THEN WE DO THIS THE OLD WAY,** it said, and the king's body lunged off the dais toward Casper faster than any human thing should move.

* * *

### Casper

It came at him like winter coming — not fast, exactly, but *inevitable*, cold pouring across the space between them, and Casper did the only thing he knew how to do.

He let the fire out.

Not much. Not all. A coal-flare, a dragon's cough, the smallest possible spending of the last true fire in the world. It caught the parasite mid-lunge like a wall of orange glass, and the king's body slammed into an invisible boundary and rebounded, smoking, shrieking in two voices at once.

But Casper felt the cost immediately. It was like a tooth pulled from his chest. The warmth he had carried since before memory — through the burning village, through the ash-market, through Xanthe's door — dimmed. He could feel the edges of it now. Before, the fire had been endless because he had never seen its end. Now he could see it. It was very close.

*Casper.* Xanthe's voice, inside his head somehow, or maybe just so familiar it might as well be. *Don't spend it all on walls. Save it.*

"Save it for what?" he shouted back, and then he understood, because the parasite understood it at the same moment, and he felt its attention slide off him —

— onto her.

*Oh,* he thought. *No.*

* * *

### Xanthe

It hit her like a wave hitting a seawall.

That was the sensation — pressure, immense and total, a darkness trying to pour itself into every opening she had. Into her mouth, her lungs, the spaces between her thoughts. Every person it had ever worn had opened for it the way a house opens for weather, doors and windows and chimneys, because that is what living bodies do; they receive.

Xanthe Dunmore's body did not receive.

Three years of hemlock and arsenic and nightshade, measured in grains on a jeweler's scale, taken every morning on an empty stomach while the palace cooks watched her lips go grey and her hands tremble and her eyes water — three years of teaching her flesh that anything foreign was an enemy. Her blood was a border with soldiers on it. Her marrow was a locked gate.

The parasite poured itself against her and her body said **no**.

She felt it happen — felt the darkness recoil from her bloodstream like a hand snatched back from a stove. Felt it try again, deeper, more desperate, flooding into her veins, and felt her poisoned blood do what poisoned blood does best.

It attacked.

The thing that had worn kings for nine hundred years had never once been poisoned, because who poisons a god? It had no defense. It had never needed one. And now it was inside the one body in the world built as a trap for it, and her blood — her beautiful, ruined, meticulously murdered blood — closed around it like a fist.

**WHAT ARE YOU,** it screamed, from inside her own skull, in her own voice, and she laughed because the sound was terrible and wonderful.

"I'm the taster," she said. "I taste everything. Everything you are — I'm tasting right now."

And she burned it.

* * *

### Casper

He had never seen anything burn like that.

Fire, real fire, ate things and left ash. What happened inside Xanthe was different — it was light without heat, a glow rising under her skin like sun through closed eyelids, spreading from her heart outward along her arms, her throat, her face. The parasite came out of her in pieces, tearing free, shrieking in voices that were not voices — old men's voices, dead queens' voices, the voice of every person it had ever been and emptied out — and each piece that left her blackened and curled and came apart in the air like burnt paper.

The court was on its feet. Nobody had told them to stand. They stood because something older than law told them to.

The king lay crumpled at the base of the dais steps, and around him the darkness pooled and writhed, wounded, searching — Casper saw it turn, saw the oil-dark attention sweep the hall and find him, the newer vessel, the easier door.

It came for him.

He didn't run. There wasn't time, and anyway — anyway, he understood something now, watching Xanthe stand glowing in the wreckage of the oldest evil in the world. He understood what the fire was *for*. It had never been meant to be kept. It had been meant to be spent, the way a seed spends itself becoming a tree, and keeping it had only ever been a longer, slower way of losing it.

So when the darkness reached for him he opened — not like a house, like a *forge*.

"CASPER!" Xanthe's voice, raw, terrified. "Don't — you'll have nothing left—"

"I know," he said.

He gave it everything. All of it. Nine hundred years of hoarded dark rushed into the last vessel of dragon-fire expecting a hollow boy to fill, and found instead a furnace, and the furnace was glad of the fuel.

The light that followed was the color of the First Tongue. Everyone in Ashfall who lived past that day would remember it differently — gold, white, green, the red of dawn — but Casper remembered it as a word, spoken once, by the world itself:

*Enough.*

* * *

### Xanthe

When she could see again, the throne room was quiet.

Ash hung in the air like grey snow, sifting down over the court, the banners, the empty throne. Where the parasite had been there was nothing — not even smoke. Things that old don't leave bodies. They just stop, the way a lie stops when everyone finally hears the truth underneath it.

Casper was on the floor.

She ran to him and gathered him up and he was light, so light, a child made of sticks and soot, and his skin was cool for the first time since she'd known him. His eyes fluttered open. They were just brown now. Just a boy's eyes.

"Gone?" he whispered.

"It's gone." Her voice broke on it. "It's finished."

"And my fire?"

She couldn't lie to him. She had lied to everyone for three years and she would not lie to this. "Spent," she said softly. "You spent it saving me, you little fool."

He considered this with the grave seriousness he brought to everything, and then he smiled — a gap-toothed, soot-streaked, entirely ordinary smile.

"Worth it," he said. "You tasted awful."

She laughed, or sobbed, or both, and held him while the court stood frozen around them, and neither of them saw the king stir.

But Veyra saw. Veyra saw the man on the floor open his eyes — his *own* eyes, wet and blinking and nine hundred years newly awake — and look at his hands as though meeting them for the first time. She saw him touch the crown on his brow with fingers that shook. She heard him speak, in a rusted whisper, his first words as a free man in nine centuries:

"What... what year is it?"

And she heard herself answer, captain of the witch-hunters, breaker of her own oath: "Whatever year it is, Your Majesty — it isn't yours anymore. It belongs to them now."

The king looked at Xanthe and Casper kneeling together in the falling ash, and something moved across his ancient, exhausted face that might, given time and mercy, learn to become gratitude.

Outside the palace windows, dawn was coming up over Ashfall — and for the first time in living memory, it looked less like ash than like morning.
