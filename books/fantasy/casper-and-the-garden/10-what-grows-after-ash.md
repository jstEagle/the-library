## 10. What Grows After Ash

The Edict of Ash did not burn. That was the strange part. Xanthe had expected it to go up like her stall had — a roar, a column of black smoke, something the whole city would feel in its teeth. Instead it simply stopped being true.

Weeks after the throne room, the edicts were still nailed to every gatepost and market pillar, their wax seals glinting dully in the morning light. But no one enforced them. The witch-hunters' barracks stood empty half the day; Veyra had dismissed two-thirds of her company herself, telling them to go home and be neighbors again. The parchment curled at its edges, rain-softened, and children peeled the letters off one by one for games, spelling out words like *forbidden* and *heretic* in puddle-mud until the words lost all their teeth.

Xanthe walked through the ash-market on a morning that smelled, for the first time in three years, only of bread.

She still woke some nights with the taste of the parasite in her mouth — that cold mineral tang, like licking a blade left out overnight. She still dreamed sometimes of the grip, the way it had closed around her heart in front of the whole court, certain of its prize, and then begun to *burn*. Her blood had done what three years of poison had taught it to do: refused everything. She had stood in the center of the court's screaming with a god's death rattling down her throat, and she had swallowed.

The king — Aldric, he was called, though he'd nearly forgotten it himself — had wept when it was over. Not the hollow weeping of the puppet, but the raw, ugly crying of a man waking from nine years inside his own skull. He had abdicated within the month. There was talk of councils now, of wards electing their own voices, of justice written by hands that had actually held bread and shovels and sick children instead of scepters. It was slow. It was loud. It was, Xanthe thought, the first honest thing this city had done in her lifetime.

And Veyra.

Xanthe found her where she always was these days: standing before the assembly hall in her plain gray coat, no captain's braid anymore, arguing with the council of the Lower Ward about the trials. The trials were endless. Every warrant the parasite had signed, every family burned under false accusation, every fence and taster and hedge-witch who'd been squeezed into informants — all of it had to be named aloud, entered into the record, grieved properly.

Veyra had testified first. That was the thing people couldn't stop talking about. The captain who had hunted Xanthe across half the city had stood in the assembly with her own confession written out fair and read it in a voice that didn't shake until the end. *I believed the law was the wall between us and the fire. I did not ask what wore the law.* They'd offered her a seat on the new council. She'd refused it twice. She said someone had to keep walking the streets, learning faces, unlearning fear.

"Joubert," she called as Xanthe passed, using her surname the way she'd started doing weeks ago — an apology made into a habit. "Your laundress filed a complaint."

Xanthe stopped. "About the luck-charm? It worked."

"About the price." Veyra's mouth twitched. "You charged her nothing. She says charity is an insult to a working woman and demands you let her pay."

"Tell her the exchange rate is bread."

"She'll bankrupt you." But there was warmth under the dryness now, careful and new, like a plant grown through a crack. Veyra glanced past Xanthe toward the far end of the market, where a small figure was already crouched in the dirt. "He's been out here since dawn. I told him frost could still come."

"He told me frost can mind its own business." Xanthe hesitated, then asked the question she'd been circling for weeks. "The hearing next week. Yours. The last one. You're sure you want me in the gallery?"

"I put your name in my testimony myself, Dunmore. Least I can do is look at you while they read it back." Veyra adjusted the strap of her sword — she still carried it, though the Edict's badge had been pried from the scabbard. "Besides. Half the ward wants to hear how a food-taster killed a god with bad blood. Let them see you buy turnips afterward. It does more good than the testimony."

* * *

Casper had laid out the garden beds in lines so straight they looked ruled.

He worked the way he'd done everything since the fire went out of him — with a fierce, methodical patience, as if the discipline itself were a way of keeping faith with what he'd spent. Nine years old and he turned soil like a man settling a debt. His sleeves were rolled past thin elbows; ash still lived in the creases of his knuckles no matter how he scrubbed, and Xanthe had privately decided it should stay there, a mark like a brand reversed — not what had been taken from him but what he'd carried through.

"You're late," he said without looking up.

"You're bossy."

"The First Tongue has a word for that too." He grinned, quick and crooked, then sobered and held out his palm. In it lay a seed — plain brown flax, nothing rare, bought from a grain-wagon with coins Veyra had pressed on him against his protests. "Ready?"

Xanthe knelt beside him in the dirt. Around them the ash-market was rebuilding — stalls rising on scorched frames, awnings dyed bright over smoke-blackened beams, the char itself left visible on purpose. Merrick's old corner stood empty. He'd been tried already, one of the first; he hadn't argued, hadn't begged, had only looked at Xanthe once across the assembly floor and then down at his own hands for the rest of it. They'd given him labor on the aqueduct repairs. She didn't hate him anymore. That had surprised her most of all — not forgiveness exactly, but the discovery that hatred needed feeding, and she was tired of feeding things that only took.

"Now," Casper said. "Say it with me. Slowly."

He spoke, and the old language moved through the morning air like water through a dry channel. Xanthe's accent was still terrible. Casper corrected her vowel sounds with the gravity of a scholar and the exasperation of a boy, and together they bent over the seed and spoke the charm — the first magic spoken openly in this market square in living memory, with no stall to hide behind, no glance over the shoulder, no buyer's desperate whisper.

*Grow,* they said. *Small thing, green thing, wake.*

Nothing dramatic happened. That was the point. The seed trembled faintly, warmed, and sank into the earth as if it had decided the dirt was worth trusting. By midsummer there would be flax here, blue flowers in rows, and Casper had plans for beans along the north edge and a cutting of plum tree that the baker had promised.

"That's it?" said a voice nearby — one of the fishwife's daughters, maybe seven, watching with her fists on her hips. "It just goes in the ground?"

"It waits," Casper said seriously. "That's the hardest kind of magic. Waiting magic."

"Can I do one?"

Casper looked at Xanthe. And there it was — the question underneath everything, the one the whole city was asking without quite daring to say: *is it allowed? Is it ours?*

"It's yours," Xanthe said. "All of it. That's what we fought for. Come here, little one. Cup your hands."

* * *

They stayed until the light went long and gold over the rooftops. Word traveled the way it does in markets — sideways, by elbow and murmur — and by afternoon a loose ring of people had gathered at a respectful distance, watching the garden beds take shape. No one hid a charm. A tinker braided a finding-knot right there on his cart, muttering the words aloud like a man testing ice. An old woman who had sold Xanthe eggs for years pulled a weather-charm from around her neck and hung it, weeping quietly, on the plum-tree stake. Nobody burned. Nobody fled. The ash-market breathed.

Casper planted until his hands gave out, and then he sat back on his heels and looked at the sky for a long time.

"Does it hurt still?" Xanthe asked quietly. "The empty place."

He considered this with the honesty he never rationed for anyone. "Like a tooth that came out. My tongue keeps going to the gap." He flexed his fingers, and for a moment — only a moment — Xanthe saw the ghost of heat in them, memory-fire, the shape of what had poured out of him to save her life. "But I dreamed last night. Real dreams, not the burning ones. I dreamed about the plum tree full-grown. I don't think the coal dreamed anything. It was too busy staying lit." He shrugged, nine years old again, ash in his knuckles. "I like the dreams better."

Xanthe put her arm around his narrow shoulders. She had stopped being afraid of that, somewhere in the weeks since — touching, being touched, letting one person all the way in. Trust, she'd written once in her ledger of charms, was the only spell she'd never learned. She was learning it now, badly, slowly, one morning at a time.

"Tomorrow," Casper said, "we do the north bed. And—" He stopped.

Down the lane, past the fish-stalls and the mended awnings, a rider was dismounting at the well. Gray coat, travel dust, a satchel heavy with documents. One of Veyra's runners — but the runner wasn't looking for Veyra. He was scanning the crowd, and when his eyes found the garden, his face did something complicated, something between relief and dread, and he started toward them at a pace that meant the news could not wait for courtesy.

Casper's hand found Xanthe's. Hers tightened around his.

The runner reached the low wall of the garden bed, breathing hard, and held out a folded letter bearing the new seal of the Lower Ward council — and beneath it, in the old style, unmistakable even half-scraped away, the seal of a king.
