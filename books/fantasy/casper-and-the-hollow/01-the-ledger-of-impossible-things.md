## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

There were four hundred and twelve entries in the Scribes' Guild autumn ledger, and Odile Montrose had found the one that did not belong before the second bell of the morning.

She found it the way she found everything: by refusing to be hurried.

The scriptorium above the Counting House smelled of tallow, iron-gall ink, and the particular dust that accumulates when paper is older than the people handling it. Rain worked at the windows in long grey strokes. Forty scribes bent over their desks in rows, their quills whispering like insects in a wall, and at the far end, at the smallest desk, at the desk nearest the door — because the door was where debt-bonded clerks belonged, where they could be watched and fetched and dismissed — Odile balanced the books of the most powerful institution in Vellamare.

She was good at it. She was, though no one would ever say it aloud in a room where the Master Scribe might hear, the best of them. Precision was the one currency she owned outright. Every figure in her hand sat straight as a soldier. Every column closed. Every discrepancy was run down like a criminal and brought to justice in the margin, in her small severe script, with the date and the cause and the correction. The guild had purchased eleven years of her life for the price of her brother's schooling, and it had purchased, whether it knew it or not, the finest bookkeeper in the city.

So when the entry in the third ledger of October refused to make sense, she did not do what the other scribes would have done. She did not smooth it over. She did not assume her own error and correct the world to match her tiredness. She sharpened her quill a second time, trimmed the nib with the little knife she kept for that purpose, and read the line again.

*Paid to the House of Aldous, in settlement of the Vane Street matter: 300 marks, and one memory, category: first love, condition: unspoiled.*

She read it four times.

The ink was not faded. It was not a later addition — the hand was the ledger's own hand, the same brown-black iron-gall, the same pressure, the same slight rightward lean that belonged to Senior Scribe Halloway, who had kept this book from the feast of Saint Corvin to the feast of Saint Ambrose. It was not a joke, because Halloway had no sense of humor and less imagination; Odile had audited his marginalia for three years and the most whimsical thing he had ever written was *see prior page*. It was not a metaphor, because ledgers did not traffic in metaphor. Ledgers were the one place in the world where words meant exactly, mercilessly, what they said.

*One memory, category: first love, condition: unspoiled.*

Three hundred marks she could account for. Three hundred marks was the going rate for settling a matter like Vane Street — she knew because she had priced a dozen matters like it. But the memory was listed in the column reserved for *goods in kind*, the column where one might record a bolt of silk or a cask of wine or, in lean years, a horse. The column had a sum at the bottom. The sum was not zero.

She turned back through the pages.

The second entry was on the twenty-ninth of Saint Corvin's month. *Received from the Undercroft tithe: 1,400 marks, and forty-one memories, assorted, condition: mixed.* The third was a week earlier. *Paid to the Undercroft: 200 marks, and nine memories, category: childhood, condition: unspoiled.* And then another. And another. Going back — she turned the pages faster now, her finger running down the goods-in-kind column like a woman running down a dark stair — going back through October, through September, through the whole of the summer, through the spring, back and back until her finger stopped at a page near the beginning of the year and would not go further, because further back the entries changed. Further back the goods-in-kind column was full of honest things. Wax. Vellum. Lamp oil. A mule.

The first memory entry in the guild's books was dated the ninth day of the first month of this year.

Eleven months ago. Eleven months ago, someone had begun paying debts in memories, and no one had stopped them, and no one had noticed, and the column had grown the way rot grows in a beam — quietly, from the inside, until the whole structure rested on it.

Odile sat very still.

Rain ticked against the glass. Somewhere below, in the Counting House proper, coins were being weighed; she could hear the small music of the scales through the floor. A junior scribe two desks over coughed and blew his nose and bent again to his work, and the quills went on whispering, and the world continued to believe itself to be an ordinary world.

She did the thing she always did when her hands wanted to shake: she made them work. She drew a fresh line in her own audit book — the private one, the one she kept in her head's own cipher, the one that would cost her her remaining hand if it were ever found — and she copied out the entry exactly. Date. Sum. Category. Condition. Then the next. Then the next. Forty-one memories, assorted. Nine memories, childhood, unspoiled. One memory, first love, unspoiled. She built a column of them, and she summed the column, and the sum was a number she had never seen before and would not be able to stop seeing now: two hundred and six memories, bought and sold and received and paid, in eleven months, by the Scribes' Guild of Vellamare.

Two hundred and six. She wrote the figure and looked at it and felt the particular cold that comes not from fear but from the moment fear arrives, punctual, to meet a suspicion that had been waiting for it.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing she had told no one, the thing she kept folded up small beneath her competence the way other women kept letters beneath their linens.

Memories could be taken. She knew this the way one knows a scar.

She had been nineteen, and her brother Tomas was dying of the lung-rot, and the physicians wanted a fee that did not exist in the world she lived in, and then a man had come to the door of their rented rooms wearing grey and carrying a case, and he had explained, gently, the way one explains the rules of a game to a child, that there were other currencies. That the Guild bought. That the Guild paid well for certain categories. That a memory of her mother — her mother, dead two years, whose face Odile already had to work to hold — was worth, in that season, exactly the cost of a winter of the lung-cure.

She had signed. She had sat in a chair in a grey room while a man with kind eyes and a cold instrument touched her temple, and she had watched her mother walk away out of her own head, and she had wept without knowing why, and Tomas had lived, and she had never once been able to regret it, and she had never once stopped grieving. That was the cruelty of it. That was the design of it. They took the memory and left the grief, the way a thief takes the coin and leaves the purse.

And then, because a girl who has sold her mother's face has nothing left to sell but her labor, they had offered her the debt-bond. Eleven years of service to discharge the *residual fee* — a phrase she had signed without reading closely, because grief is a kind of blindness and hope is a kind of debt. She had been twenty now for five months. Six years remained.

Six years, and every day of them spent inside the institution that had eaten her mother's face, and she had never — she realized this now, with the ledger open under her hands — she had never once thought to look for the trade in the guild's own books. She had thought of it as a wound in herself, private, singular, a strange and terrible thing that had happened once in a grey room to her alone. She had not understood that it was a *business*.

Two hundred and six memories in eleven months.

She closed her eyes and did the arithmetic of horror. The guild's ledgers recorded transactions with perhaps a third of the city's trading houses directly; the rest flowed through intermediaries, through the Counting House's client books, through the tithe rolls. If two hundred and six appeared in the guild's own records, then the true number — the number moving through Vellamare like blood through a body — would be in the thousands. Tens of thousands. A city of two hundred thousand souls, and some uncountable portion of them walking around with holes in their heads where their childhoods used to be, and calling the holes prosperity.

*Condition: unspoiled.* She hated that phrase. It had the voice of a grocer describing fruit.

"Montrose."

She did not flinch. Eleven years had trained the flinch out of her, or rather had trained it inward, where it did its work on her alone. She looked up with the face she kept for Senior Scribes: attentive, mild, slightly tired, the face of a woman who is competent and knows it and is not proud of it.

Halloway stood at the end of her desk. He was a long grey man with ink-stained cuffs and the eyes of someone who had spent forty years reading other people's numbers and had forgotten his own. He looked at her open ledger, and then, for a moment longer than was comfortable, at her face.

"Third ledger of October," he said. "You've flagged nothing."

"No, Senior Scribe."

"The goods-in-kind column closes clean?"

"It closes," Odile said, "exactly."

Something moved behind his eyes. Not guilt — she would have known guilt; she saw it every day in the faces of men who came to settle debts they could not pay. This was older and more tired than guilt. This was the look of a man standing at a door he has long ago stopped trying to open.

"Good," he said. "The Master will want the autumn books by week's end. Unremarkable. He is very particular that the autumn books be unremarkable."

"Of course," Odile said.

He lingered one moment more. Rain ran down the window behind him and made the light shiver. Then he turned and went back up the room between the desks, grey coat, grey hands, a man carrying something heavy in a place no one could see, and Odile understood, with a certainty that settled into her bones like cold water into a cellar, that Halloway knew. That Halloway had written those entries in his own hand and had carried them for eleven months the way a man carries a stone in his mouth. That *unremarkable* was not a description. It was an instruction.

She bent back to her work.

* * *

The afternoon passed the way afternoons in the scriptorium always passed: in ink and lamplight and the slow erosion of the light at the windows. Odile closed the autumn ledgers. She made her columns true — truly false, now, she thought, and the thought sat in her chest like a swallowed coal. At the fifth bell the scribes rose, and stretched, and put on their coats, and went out into the rain to their suppers and their families and their unbroken lives, and Odile remained, because debt-bonded clerks remained. There was a second shift. There was always a second shift. The Counting House never entirely slept, because debt never entirely sleeps.

By the eighth bell she was alone with the ledgers and one guttering lamp, and she did what she had been wanting to do all day with the patience of someone who has learned that wanting things is a private sport.

She opened the archive.

The guild's older books lived in a vault beneath the Counting House, and clerks could request them through the proper channels with the proper signatures, but the proper channels were closed at the eighth bell, and Odile had spent eleven years learning the building the way a prisoner learns a wall. The archive stair was behind the map cabinet. The lock was old and honest and had one weakness, which was that it had been installed by a locksmith who believed locks were for honest men, and Odile was in and out in less than a quarter hour with a single book under her coat: the client register for the current year, the one that recorded who the guild *bought from*.

Back at her desk, she found what she was looking for in under a page.

*Undercroft.* The word appeared again and again in the receipts column, always in the same hand, always for the same categories. *Received from the Undercroft: forty-one memories, assorted. Received from the Undercroft: nine memories, childhood. Received from the Undercroft: — * and here her finger stopped, because the next entry was different. The next entry was not a receipt. The next entry was a payment, and it was not paid *to* the Undercroft. It was paid *for* it.

*Paid, being the covenant tithe, due at the turning of the year, to the Undercroft, per the original terms: 4,000 marks.*

*Covenant.* She said the word without sound, letting it move its shape in her mouth. It was a church word. It was an old word. It belonged in the front of holy books and in the oaths of kings, not in a receipt column between lamp oil and wax.

And beneath it, in the margin, in a hand she did not recognize — smaller than Halloway's, older, pressed deep into the paper as though the writer had been afraid the words would not stay — a single line:

*It is not a purchase. It never was a purchase. We are paying a debt, and the debt is not ours, and the well is running dry.*

Odile sat back.

The lamp had burned low. The rain had thickened; it came against the glass now not in strokes but in sheets, and somewhere far below — below the Counting House, below the vault, below the foundations, below anything the building admitted to having — she heard, or thought she heard, a sound that did not belong to rain or to the city. A long, low sound, like water moving in a place water had been forgotten. Like a bell rung under the earth.

She looked down at the ledger, at the neat and terrible columns, at the two hundred and six souls who had been spent out of existence to keep some covenant she had never heard of, and she felt the thing she had been holding down for eleven years rise at last and put its hand on the back of her neck.

She was tired of being precise inside a lie. She was tired of closing columns that closed over graves. She was twenty years old and she owed six more years to the men who had sold her mother's face by the ounce, and she was suddenly, violently, unable to bear one more day of it — and she was also, she admitted, in the small honest room at the back of herself where she kept the things she could not afford, *afraid*. Because a clerk who asks the wrong question about a covenant does not get answers. A clerk who asks the wrong question gets a grey room and a cold instrument and a chair, and comes out the other side with a hole where the question used to be.

The lamp guttered.

And that was when the knocking started — not at the great doors of the Counting House, which were locked and barred at the eighth bell, but at the small door at the bottom of the scriptorium stair, the clerks' door, the door that no one used at night.

Three knocks. A pause, as if the knocker were gathering what remained of his strength. Then three more, weaker, dragging, like a man knocking from the far side of a long fall.

Odile put her hand flat on the ledger, over the covenant tithe, over the margin, over the well that was running dry. Her heart was going like a second set of footsteps behind her own.

She went to the door, and lifted the bar, and opened it.
