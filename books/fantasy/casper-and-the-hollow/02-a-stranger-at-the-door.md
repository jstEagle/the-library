## 2. A Stranger at the Door

The rain had been falling since noon, and by the time the bells rang Vespers it had worked itself into the grain of everything — the stone steps of the Scribes' Guild slick and dark as poured ink, the windows fogged from within so that the lamplight bled outward in soft yellow halos, the very air in the scriptorium gone damp and heavy, smelling of wet wool, tallow, and the iron-gall ink that Odile Montrose had been breathing since she was nine years old.

She did not look up when the door groaned.

That was the first thing she would remember afterward, the thing that would return to her in the small hours for the rest of her life: that she had heard the door open and had not looked up, because doors opening meant nothing in the scriptorium. Doors opening meant apprentices with armloads of fresh vellum. Doors opening meant the evening shift shuffling in with their cold-red knuckles and their complaints about the weather. The scriptorium was a room where the world came and went as a matter of course, and Odile had spent eleven years training herself to treat the world's comings and goings as background noise, like the scratch of nibs or the drip of the eaves.

So she kept her eyes on the ledger — Third Quarter, Disbursements, Column Seven — and her nib kept moving, and the stranger crossed the room behind her without her seeing him at all.

It was the sound that finally broke through. Not footsteps — there were no footsteps, which should have been strange enough — but a wet, dragging breath, the kind of breathing that has forgotten how to be quiet because it is too busy simply continuing. A sound like a bellows with a split seam.

Odile set down her pen.

She turned on her stool, and there he was: a man standing three feet behind her, swaying like a mast in crosswind, one hand pressed flat against his ribs where the grey guild-cloak he wore had gone black and shining with blood. He was young — younger than her, maybe twenty-two — with river-tan skin and a courier's cropped hair plastered to his forehead by the rain, and his eyes were the problem. His eyes were wrong. They had the fixed, over-bright look of a man who had decided not to die *here*, in front of witnesses, and was holding that decision together with both hands.

"Montrose," he said. "You're Montrose."

Not a question. Her name landed in the middle of the scriptorium like a dropped weight, and two heads turned — old Brother Fenwick at the restoration table, his magnifying loupe still screwed into one eye; Sister Amalie by the drying racks, a sheet of vellum half-raised in her hands like a shield.

"I'm afraid you have me confused—" Odile began, because eleven years of institutional instinct answered before she could stop it. *Deny first. Ask later. The Guild teaches its clerks that names are currency, and currency is not given away.*

"You balance the ledgers." He took a step and his knee buckled; he caught himself on the edge of her desk, and the whole thing jumped, and her inkwell chattered against the wood. "Third Quarter. Disbursements. You found them."

The words went through her like a nail through the palm.

She had found them six days ago. Entries that should not exist. Payments recorded in a hand she knew — Master Corvane's own careful script — for sums that were not sums at all. In place of figures, in place of coin, the entries read like this: *Paid to the Underward, one summer afternoon, age seven, the smell of bread.* *Paid, one first kiss, unnamed.* *Paid, the color of a dead wife's dress.* She had assumed forgery. Then madness. Then — on the sixth night, sitting alone in the counting room with a lamp burned down to a stub — she had assumed nothing at all, and simply copied the entries out in her own hand onto a scrap of paper she now kept folded inside her left boot, because some part of her had understood, even then, that knowing this was dangerous, and that a copy hidden against her skin might be the only proof she'd ever get.

"How do you know that?" she said quietly.

"Because I carried the original." His mouth was going grey around the edges. "Because they've killed three couriers this month trying to make sure no one else knows, and I am the last, and I have been running since dawn, and I need you to take it before—"

The door banged open.

Two men in oilcloth cloaks stood in the entryway, rain streaming off their shoulders, puddling at their boots. They wore no livery, but they didn't need to. Odile knew the walk of guild enforcers the way she knew the walk of debtors: the deliberate unhurried tread of people who have never once needed to run. The taller one carried a ledger-satchel of stiff black leather, and something about the way he held it — casually, by one strap, like a man carrying a lunch — made the hairs rise along Odile's arms.

"Evening," the tall one said pleasantly. "We're looking for a courier. Ran off toward this quarter. Seen him?"

Brother Fenwick looked up from his loupe. Sister Amalie lowered her vellum. And Odile sat very still at her desk with a dying man's hand inches from hers, and understood, with a clarity that felt almost holy, that the next ten seconds would decide what kind of person she was.

*Deny first,* said the institution in her blood. *Ask later. Give up nothing. The Guild provides.*

But the Guild, she thought, had provided her with a debt-bond instead of wages. Had taken eleven years of her life at compound interest. Had let her mother die owing, and had entered the funeral costs into the bond like any other line item, neat and justified and due. The Guild had taught her arithmetic so thoroughly that she could not help doing it now, in the space of a heartbeat: *what is owed, what is paid, who pays, and who profits.*

The courier's blood was dripping onto her floorboards with soft, patient ticks.

"No one's come through," Odile said. "Only us, and the rain."

The enforcer smiled. It was a good smile, well-practiced, and it did not touch his eyes, which moved past her across the room — across Brother Fenwick's restoration table, across the drying racks, across the long shadows between the stacks — with the slow thoroughness of a hand searching pockets.

"We'll just have a look about, then."

* * *

Later — much later, in the years when she told this story to herself in the dark, revising it, sharpening it, unable to stop — Odile would understand that the scriptorium had saved her by accident. It was a room built for concealment without anyone intending it: high shelves of ledgers rising to a vaulted dark, carrels walled shoulder-high, the great restoration table hung all around with sheets of drying vellum that swung gently on their lines like pale ghosts. A maze of paper. A forest of other people's secrets, eleven centuries deep.

The enforcers walked the aisles. Their boots rang on the stone. Odile sat frozen at her desk, quill in hand, writing — actually writing, her body committing fraud on its own initiative, copying out a column of legitimate figures while her heart slammed against her ribs like something trying to get out. Beside her thigh, below the lip of the carrel, the courier crouched in the shadow beneath her desk, folded impossibly small, his breath coming in thin silent pulls through his teeth. Blood crept across the flagstones in a slow black tongue. She watched it spread and thought, absurdly, of the ledgers. *Every entry must balance,* Master Corvane always said. *Something in, something out. Nothing appears from nowhere.*

Here was an appearing. Here was blood on her stones that no ledger would ever record.

The tall enforcer stopped at the end of her row.

"Working late, mistress clerk?"

"The ledgers don't balance themselves." Her voice came out steady. She marveled at it from a great distance, as if someone else were speaking through her mouth. "Quarter's end is four days off."

"Diligent." He drifted closer. She could smell him now under the rain-smell — tallow and leather and something faintly chemical, like the fixative they used on fresh ink. His gaze slid over her desk, her papers, her face. "You're Montrose, aren't you? Odile Montrose. Corvane speaks well of you."

"He's generous."

"Mm. Says you're the best eye in the building. Says nothing gets past you." The smile again, wider now, showing teeth. "Is that true?"

"Master Corvane flatters me, sir. I copy figures. That's all."

For a moment — one full breath, in and out — the enforcer looked at her, and she looked back, and between them passed something wordless and ancient: the appraisal of a buyer deciding whether a thing is worth the trouble of taking. Then, from the far end of the scriptorium, Brother Fenwick knocked over his jar of pounce powder, swore with tremendous feeling, and began berating Sister Amalie for leaving it at the table's edge, and the spell broke.

"Nothing here," the tall one called back to his companion. "Check the refectory. He'll be making for the water gate if he's got any sense."

They left the way they'd come, and the door closed, and the rain filled the silence behind them, and nobody moved for the count of thirty. Then Brother Fenwick said, mildly, to the room in general: "Amalie, my dear, I believe my spectacles require cleaning," and he and Sister Amalie gathered their things and departed with the elaborate unconcern of two people who intended, forever after, to be able to say they had seen nothing at all.

Odile slid from her stool and knelt.

The courier had slumped sideways against the carrel wall, and the last minutes had cost him dearly; his lips were the blue-white of skimmed milk, and when he tried to speak, the sound came out as a rattle. She took his shoulder and eased him down flat, pressing hard on the wound beneath his ribs, and her hands came away black to the wrist.

"Don't talk," she said. "I'll fetch—"

"No." His hand shot up and caught her wrist with a grip that astonished her, iron in a dying man's fingers. "No doctors. No guild healers. They own every healer in this city, do you understand? They'd know within the hour." His eyes fixed on hers, and the fever-brightness in them was terrible to see, a lamp burning down its last oil all at once. "Listen to me. My name is Perrin Voss. I was sworn to the covenant. Do you hear me? *Sworn* — the old covenant, the true one, the one your ledgers are bleeding dry."

"I don't know what you—"

"You do." He almost smiled. "Six days ago you found the payments. Don't insult us both."

The folded paper in her boot seemed suddenly to weigh pounds. Odile knelt there in the spreading blood with this stranger's life leaking through her fingers, and felt the solid floor of her world tilt beneath her like a deck in weather. Eleven years of columns and quarters and balanced sums, and underneath it all — underneath *everything* — a word she had never once seen written in any guild record, spoken now in a dying voice:

Covenant.

"What is it?" she whispered. "What's breaking?"

His laugh was a wet thread of sound. "Everything. That's rather the point." He fumbled at his cloak, at an inner pocket, and she helped him — her hands moving without permission — and drew out a scroll no longer than her forearm, wrapped in oilskin, sealed at the middle with a blob of dark wax stamped with a device she did not recognize: a circle bisected by a descending line. A stair, she thought distantly. A seal shaped like a stair going down.

"Original map," Perrin Voss breathed. "Terms of the covenant. Blood-ink, unforgeable, the only copy the guild doesn't hold. They've been selling the Hollow's memories for forty years to pay their debt — forty years of theft dressed up as bookkeeping — and the covenant's come due, and it can't be paid in stolen coin. Someone has to carry this down. Someone outside the guild. Someone who owes them nothing and knows exactly what they've taken." His fingers tightened on her wrist. "I ran the routes for nine years. I know every courier they've bought, turned, or buried. But you — you found the entries. You *read* them. Nobody reads anymore, Montrose. Nobody looks."

"Give it to the magistrates—"

"The magistrates bank with the guild." His head rolled; his voice was fraying. "Give it to the temples—"

"The temples lease their relics through guild brokers. I process those contracts."

"Then you already know." His eyes were losing focus, drifting toward the ceiling, toward somewhere past it. "There's no one clean above ground. That's why it goes *down*. That's why it was always supposed to go down." His grip spasmed, relaxed. "Take it. Hide it. Find the stair that isn't there. And Montrose — listen — the map chooses its bearer, they used to say. Old courier superstition. It was warm in my pocket the whole way here. It's never been warm before."

He pressed the scroll into her hands.

And it *was* warm. Faintly, unnaturally warm, like a stone kept near a fire, like a living thing asleep. Odile sat back on her heels with the oilskin cylinder cradled in her bloody palms and felt its heat soak through her skin and into her bones, and somewhere very far away, beneath the rain and the ringing bells and the whole vast weight of the city, she imagined she could feel something turning over in its sleep.

"Perrin," she said. "Stay with me. Tell me about the Hollow. Tell me where the stair is."

But his eyes had gone past her, fixed on the high dark of the rafters, and whatever he saw there made his face go young and surprised, like a boy catching snowflakes.

"Oh," he said softly. "Oh, I'm late. Father will be furious—"

Then the light in him guttered, and settled, and went out, and the scriptorium was very quiet except for the rain, and Odile Montrose knelt alone in another man's blood, holding the warmest, most dangerous thing she had ever touched.

She should call for help. She should summon the wardens, report the intrusion, surrender the scroll to Master Corvane and let the institution do what institutions did, which was absorb and conceal and continue. That was the procedure. That was eleven years of training. That was every instinct the guild had grown into her like grafts into a tree.

Instead she wiped her hands on her apron, wrapped the scroll in a sheet of blotting paper, and slipped it into the false spine of a disused tax ledger from the year of her birth, where no auditor had looked in nineteen years and none ever would.

Her heart was a drum. Her thoughts were astonishingly clear.

*This is a choice,* she realized, standing there in the lamplight with ink and blood on her hands. *Whatever comes next — this was the moment I chose.*

She had just blown out her lamp when she heard the boots again. Two sets, unhurried, returning through the rain — and beneath them, fainter, a third tread from the cloister side, the direction of the Master's stair.

The enforcers had remembered something. Or someone had told them.

Odile picked up the old tax ledger, heavy as a paving stone, and walked, calm as Sunday, toward the archive window that opened onto the drainage channel behind the counting rooms — the one window in the Scribes' Guild, she happened to know, whose lock had been broken since before she was born, because she was the one who had broken it, three years ago, just to prove to herself that she could.

Outside, the city waited, drowned in rain and lamplight, full of stairs she had never dreamed existed.

Behind her, the scriptorium door opened.
