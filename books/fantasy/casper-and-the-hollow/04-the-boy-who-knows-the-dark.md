## 4. The Boy Who Knows the Dark

Casper had learned to read the city by its smells before he could read a word of ink.

Bread and horse from the market streets above. Tallow and wet stone in the middle tiers, where the poor burned what they could afford. And below that — beneath the sewers, beneath even the old cistern roads the city had forgotten it owned — there was a smell with no name. His father had called it *the deep breath*. Earth that had never seen rain. Cold that had never been warmed by anything but passing bodies. Casper knew it the way other boys knew their mothers' voices.

He was running through it now, lungs full of it, boots striking the old flagstones three steps ahead of the lantern-light behind him.

"Down the throat!" someone shouted behind him, and the words came back doubled off the cistern walls, so that for a moment it sounded like the dark itself was calling him home.

*Throat* was collector's slang. Every guild man on the night shift knew the undercity only as a set of openings — throats, gullets, mouths — places where debtors went down and rarely came up. They didn't know the veins between the mouths. That knowledge lived in exactly one head now, and the head belonged to a seventeen-year-old boy pressed flat against a wall of green-slicked stone, listening to four grown men with iron-shod boots decide which direction not to go.

Casper counted their footsteps the way his father had taught him. Not the sound — the *weight*. Collectors walked heavy when they were sure, light when they were guessing. These ones were guessing.

Left, then. Toward the Drowned Chapel.

He let them pass, waited until the last lantern-glow bled around the corner, and went the other way.

* * *

His father's maps were wrapped in oilcloth against Casper's chest, and he touched them the way other men touched amulets.

Twelve sheets of good rag paper, drawn in a hand so fine it looked like embroidery: every tunnel, every stair, every door in the undercity, marked in three colors of ink. Black for what the guild believed existed. Red for what actually existed. And a third color, a brown-red darker than rust, that Aldous Reyes had never explained to his son, used sparingly, at the edges of things, along routes that ended in blankness or in little annotations like *not on the ledger* and *she will know* and once, memorably, *do not wake this one.*

His father had been dead eleven days.

They had found him in the canal basin with his satchel rifled and his hands empty, and the guild had called it an accident, because the guild called everything an accident that it did not wish to sign for. But Aldous Reyes had swum the canal basin since before Casper was born. Aldous Reyes had known every current, every submerged grate. Men who knew water like that did not drown by accident, and they especially did not drown eleven days after refusing, loudly and publicly, in the Scribes' Hall itself, to surrender a certain sealed commission to a certain Master Scribe.

Casper had stood at the funeral — a small thing, seven mourners, most of them route-runners too frightened to speak above a whisper — and he had felt something happen inside his chest that he would spend the rest of the night trying not to name. It wasn't grief exactly. Grief was soft. This had edges. It sat behind his ribs like a folded blade, and every time he thought of his father's face in the water, the blade opened another degree.

The collectors wanted the maps. That much was plain; they'd torn apart the loft twice while Casper watched from a rooftop across the lane, chewing his sleeve to keep silent. What Casper couldn't work out was why. Route-maps were worth money, yes, but the guild had its own surveyors, its own charts. Unless — and here the folded blade turned — unless his father's maps showed something the guild's charts were carefully missing.

*Not on the ledger,* the brown-red ink said.

Eleven days of sleeping in drain-junctions and eating pigeons' eggs raw had given Casper a great deal of time to think about those three words.

Tonight he'd made a mistake. He'd gone back for the twelfth map — the one sheet his father had kept separate, hidden in the loose brick behind the stove, and which Casper had left there originally because carrying all twelve at once felt like carrying all his eggs in one basket. The brick was empty when he got there. The collectors had been waiting in the dark of the loft, patient as spiders, and Casper had gone out the window instead of the door, down the drainpipe, into the gullet, and now here he was, heart slamming, in the long cold vein beneath the tannery district.

They had the twelfth map.

But they didn't have him, and they didn't have the other eleven, and as long as he kept breathing, the blade behind his ribs stayed sharp.

* * *

The vein ran north toward the Weepers' Stair, where groundwater bled through the limestone in a hundred slow silver threads. Casper loved this stretch. His father had loved it too; the map called it *the choir*, and in summer you could stand in the middle of it with your eyes shut and hear the water ticking off the stone in a rhythm almost like speech.

He was halfway through the choir when he heard the bell.

Not a street bell. A scriptorium bell — high, quick, triple-struck, the alarm pattern. Even down here, filtered through forty feet of stone, he knew it. Everyone in the lower city knew it. When the Scribes' Guild rang that pattern, somebody had done something unforgivable.

Casper stopped, water ticking around his ankles, and did the arithmetic. The scriptorium annex was six streets east and two levels up. If he climbed the Weepers' Stair he could reach the maintenance gallery above the annex roof in perhaps four minutes — five if the ladder rungs were iced, and tonight they would be, because the cold came down the shaft like a poured liquid.

He told himself he was going out of caution. A bell meant enforcers moving in numbers, and enforcers moving in numbers swept the undercity afterward, standard procedure, his father's notes said so in red ink. He needed to know which exits would be watched.

He told himself that. He climbed anyway, faster than caution required, because some part of him — the part with the blade in it — already knew that on the night the guild rang its alarm, anything that ran was something he wanted to see.

* * *

From the gallery above the annex roof, the world was all steam and lamplight and falling snow, snow that melted before it landed because the scriptorium vents breathed heat like a sleeping animal. Casper lay flat behind a chimney breast, breath shallow, and looked down into the courtyard.

Three figures.

One was a woman — young, narrow-shouldered, dressed in clerk's grays that marked her as guild property down to her buttons — standing beside the annex's side door with her arms wrapped around herself. The second was a courier, or had been; he was crumpled against the doorframe now, and even from the gallery Casper could see the dark spreading under him on the snow, black in the lamplight, spreading the way spilled ink spreads, faster than a body should allow.

The third figure wore the Master Scribe's colors, and Casper's teeth ached from clenching.

He was too far away to hear words. He didn't need them. He'd grown up watching this exact theater from rooftops: the collector's patience, the debtor's fear, the careful choreography of a trap closing. The woman had something. Whatever it was, she'd had it long enough for the courier to bleed out defending the delivery, and long enough for the Master Scribe himself to come down from his tower on a snowy night, which the man had not done in living memory for anything less than a covenant-grade secret.

She ran.

Casper nearly laughed aloud at the audacity of it. Clerks didn't run. Clerks balanced books and died of dust in the lungs at fifty. This one went over the courtyard wall like she'd practiced it — badly, catching her elbow, sliding down a drainpipe with all the grace of a dropped sack — and hit the alley beyond at a dead sprint, and the Master Scribe did not shout after her, which was worse than shouting. He simply raised one hand, and from the shadowed archways of the alley, collectors unfolded themselves like furniture coming alive.

They knew the streets. They did not know the veins.

Casper was already moving along the gallery, matching her flight below, reading her line the way his father had taught him to read water. She was fast and terrified and completely lost. Within three streets she'd take the Tanner's Cut, because it looked open, and the Tanner's Cut dead-ended at a grate that hadn't opened in sixty years, and then they'd have her, and whatever she carried would go back upstairs in the Master Scribe's gloved hand, and the blade behind Casper's ribs would have another degree of angle to it, because he could have helped and hadn't.

He dropped through the coal-chute into the alley ahead of her.

* * *

Later — much later, when he had time to be embarrassed — he would admit that his plan had consisted entirely of the words *follow me* delivered in a confident voice.

What actually happened was this: she came around the corner at full speed, saw a boy-shaped shadow detach itself from a wall, and swung at his head with a leather tube she'd apparently been carrying like a club. Casper caught her wrist, mostly by reflex, and they stood there in the freezing dark, breathing hard at each other, her eyes wide and white-rimmed and furious.

"The grate," he said. "Tanner's Cut is a trap. Come on."

"Why would I—" Another glance over her shoulder. Lantern-light, boot-noise, the particular whistle collectors used to signal each other across courtyards. "Who are you?"

"Someone who knows where the doors are." He pulled her wrist, and either terror or sense won out in her, because she came.

They went down through the coal cellar, through the hatch his father had called *the keyhole* — a door so narrow they had to turn sideways, edged with rust that printed orange stripes on their clothes — and into the warm dark of the steam galleries beneath the bathhouse quarter, where the pipes sang and no pursuer's boots made any sound worth hearing. She kept up. That surprised him. Clerk's grays weren't made for crawling, and she tore both sleeves and one knee and never once complained, which in Casper's experience put her ahead of most route-runners he'd known.

It was in the steam galleries, in a pocket of quiet where the pipes fell silent and the only light came up green through a broken grating, that she finally stopped and looked at him properly.

"You knew the hatch. You knew the cut was a dead end. Who are you?"

"Casper."

"That's a name, not an answer."

"Then we're even," he said, "because you're wearing guild buttons and running from the guild, and that's not an answer either. It's a riddle."

Something moved across her face — too quick to name, too tired to hide well. Her hand had gone, without her seeming to notice, to the leather tube at her side. Holding it. Shielding it.

And the tube had a seal on it.

Casper's breath stopped. Wax, black, stamped with a mark he had seen ten thousand times and expected never to see again outside his own memory: a stylus crossed with a key, ringed in a circle of tiny letters worn nearly smooth by a thumb that had worried it the way his father worried everything he cared about. Aldous Reyes had pressed that seal into every sworn commission he ever carried. There was a wax impression of it on the mantel of a loft the collectors had already torn apart twice.

"Where," Casper said, and his voice came out strange, flattened, like it had traveled a long way to arrive, "did you get that?"

Her hand closed tighter around the tube. "A dying man gave it to me. In the scriptorium. Why? Do you recognize the seal?"

"Do I—" The laugh that came out of him had nothing funny in it at all. Eleven days. Eleven days of drains and raw eggs and sleeping sitting up with his back to the stone, and here was the answer to everything, hanging off the wrist of a runaway clerk in a stolen gray coat. "That seal belonged to my father. Aldous Reyes. The courier whose body they pulled out of the canal basin eleven days ago and called an accident."

The woman went very still. Steam drifted between them, smelling of sulfur and hot copper.

"He wouldn't give it up," she said quietly. "Even dying. He — they were asking him for it, I think, before he reached me. He chose where it went."

*He chose where it went.* Casper looked at her — really looked, past the torn sleeves and the terror and the guild buttons — and understood that his father, in the last minutes of his life, or in whatever arrangement had led to those minutes, had picked this stranger out of the whole city. Had trusted her with the one thing worth killing for. Which meant one of two things: either his father had known her, or his father had judged her, and Aldous Reyes's judgments were the closest thing to scripture Casper had ever been given.

Above them, faint through forty feet of stone, a whistle sounded. Then another. The collectors were fanning out over the bathhouse quarter, patient as tide.

"They'll sweep the galleries by morning," Casper said. "There's one way out they won't think of, because it isn't on their charts. It is," he added, tapping the oilcloth bundle at his own chest, "on mine."

"Where does it lead?"

"Down." He held her gaze. "All the way down. My father drew these tunnels for twenty years, and the parts he drew in brown-red ink — the parts he marked *not on the ledger* — they don't go anywhere on any map the guild owns. They go somewhere else. Somewhere my father died rather than talk about." The blade behind his ribs was fully open now, and it felt, for the first time in eleven days, like purpose instead of grief. "Whatever that seal opens, whatever he was carrying, I'm going to finish the delivery. You can come or you can take your chances with the sweep. Those are the terms."

She looked at him for a long moment. Snow-melt dripped somewhere in the dark, counting seconds.

"Odile," she said at last. "My name is Odile Montrose. And I suppose I'm coming."

"Good." Casper shouldered past her toward the crack in the eastern wall — a seam barely wide enough for a thin boy, invisible unless you knew the brown-red line on the twelfth map ended here, at a door that wasn't there. He pressed his palms to the stone, feeling for the draft his father had promised lived behind it, cold and old and smelling of the deep breath.

"Stay close," he said, as the seam gave, as the dark beyond it exhaled against his face like something waking. "And whatever you hear down there — don't answer it."
