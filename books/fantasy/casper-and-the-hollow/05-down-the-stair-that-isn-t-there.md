## 5. Down the Stair That Isn't There

*Odile*

She had been running for two days on no sleep and half a stolen loaf, and the city had stopped being a city somewhere around the third bell of the night. It had become a mouth — alleys opening like gullet after gullet, swallowing her deeper into the districts where even the guild's lanterns gave up.

The map was strapped against her ribs beneath her coat, wrapped in oilcloth, warm from her own body heat. She had not dared open it since the scriptorium. She had not dared stop moving at all, except to breathe, and lately even that felt like a transaction she might be billed for later.

They were behind her again. She could tell by the way the beggars vanished. In the Warrens, the poor read the streets faster than any scribe read a ledger, and tonight every doorway that should have held a sleeping body was empty, every begging bowl withdrawn. The guild's coin moved ahead of its hunters like a tide going out.

Odile turned down a stair she did not know — a narrow stone throat between a shuttered chandlery and a wall of old posters promising things no one had ever received — and heard, above her, boots. Two pairs. Unhurried. That was the worst part; they weren't hurrying because they knew something she didn't. They knew the stair ended.

It ended. A landing, a bricked-up archway furred with soot, and nowhere left to go but back up into their hands.

Her hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against the brick, as if she could feel through it whatever the map knew, some secret geometry, some door — *a door that exists only at certain hours*, the courier had gasped, blood on his teeth, *the covenant keeps one* —

The courier. The dying man who had chosen her out of everyone in that scriptorium. Why her? Because she balanced the books? Because she was the only one who'd flinched?

Behind her, the boots reached the top of the stair.

"Ledger-keeper Montrose." A voice like a receipt: itemized, final. "Master Scribe Veyl extends his regards."

She turned. There were indeed two of them, tall men in gray coats the color of wet paper, and the nearer one held something small and glinting — a bond-sigil, her name stamped in lead, the thing that made her legally, contractually theirs until her debt burned off sometime around her fortieth year.

"There's been an accounting error," Odile said, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "I'd like to dispute it."

The hunter smiled without warmth, the way the guild taught all its arithmetic to smile. "Then come upstairs and file a grievance."

And that was when the wall behind her opened.

There was no fanfare. No grinding of hidden gears. The brick simply stopped being a wall — the way a word stops meaning anything if you say it too long — and where the soot-furred archway had been there stood a doorway, and beyond the doorway, stairs, going down into air that smelled like rain on stone that had never been rained on.

Odile did not think. Thinking was a luxury itemized elsewhere. She stepped backward through the door as the hunters lunged, and the last thing she saw of the surface world was the leaden sigil of her own name falling from the nearer hunter's fingers and ringing on the stone like a dropped coin.

Then the doorway closed behind her, gently, the way a book closes when you fall asleep reading it.

* * *

*Casper*

Casper had been following the smell of blood and expensive paper for six streets, which was a strange combination, but then his whole life had been a strange combination since the night his father came home with a knife wound shaped like a guild seal.

He kept to the rooflines where he could. His father's route-maps rode inside his coat, thirty years of tunnels inked by a dead man's patient hand, and Casper had spent two days cross-referencing them against the fleeing girl below him — the ledger-woman, the one from the scriptorium, the one whose map bore his father's seal pressed into green wax.

That seal had done something to him that grief hadn't managed in two years. Grief was weather. This was a compass needle.

Because his father had died refusing to hand over exactly such a map, and now here was a guild accountant carrying what looked very much like the original, sprinting through the undercity like a woman who had finally read the fine print on her own life.

She turned down the chandlery stair. Dead end — Casper knew that stair, had slept in its lee one frozen winter. He swung down a drainpipe to watch the trap close, already calculating whether he could drop onto the second hunter's shoulders before either of them reached her.

Then the wall opened, and the girl walked backward into it, and the hunters screamed a word Casper hadn't heard since his father's funeral:

"*Stairsunder!*"

The door sealed itself like water finding its level. The hunters beat on the brick with fists and then with a pry-bar, and the brick took it the way a sleeping man takes being called — slowly, grudgingly, not at all.

One of them ran off toward the guildhouse. The other stayed, prying, cursing, and Casper crouched in the dark above him doing the fastest arithmetic of his life.

His father's maps marked places like this with a small symbol: a circle with a line through it, and beside it, in the margins, times. *Open at slack-water only. Open between bells. Ask nothing twice.* His father had called them the honest doors — doors that didn't lie about being doors, that simply refused to exist most of the day, which was more honesty than the guild ever managed.

And this one was open *now*, which meant it would not be open long.

Which meant the girl was on the wrong side of a closing door, alone, carrying a map that had gotten his father killed.

Casper swore softly, comprehensively, in the dialect of dockside children, and went over the edge while the hunter was still prying.

He hit the landing running. The hunter whirled — big man, slow surprise — and Casper gave him both boots to the chest, off a barrel he'd positioned in his head three seconds earlier, and the hunter went down among the old posters promising things no one had ever received.

Then Casper put his palms flat against the brick, feeling for the seam the way his father had taught him — *you don't look for the door, boy, you ask the wall if it remembers being one* — and the wall, mercifully, still remembered.

It opened. Cold air breathed out, smelling of rain that had never fallen and something older underneath, something like a library and something like a grave.

"Hey!" he said into the dark, because shouting your intentions into unknown darkness was another thing his father had taught him. "Ledger-woman! I'm coming down! I'm not with them — my name's Casper, my father was Aldous Vane, and his seal is on your map!"

Silence. Then, faintly, from below: "Prove it."

"How?"

"Tell me what the seal smells like."

Casper blinked. Then he laughed, once, despite everything, because it was true — his father chewed bay leaves to keep his breath sweet for the wax, and every letter he'd ever sealed smelled faintly of it, like a kitchen garden that had learned cryptography.

"Bay leaf," Casper said. "And pipe smoke. He pretended he'd quit."

A pause in the dark. Then, reluctantly, the way trust always sounds at first: "...Come down, then. Quickly. I don't think it stays."

It did not stay. He felt it behind him as he descended — a soft pressure at his back like a hand deciding — and when he glanced up, the doorway was brick again, and the hunter on the other side was hammering on a wall that had never been anything else.

* * *

*Odile*

The stair went down farther than the building above had height, which Odile noted with the detached professionalism of someone who had spent nine years balancing accounts that did not balance. She counted steps because counting was how she prayed.

Forty steps. Eighty. At one hundred and twenty, the light changed — not brighter, just *different*, the gray of deep water instead of the gray of lamplit fog — and the air began to taste like something.

Not like anything. Like forgetting.

She couldn't have described it if she'd tried, and she did try, because description was her trade. It tasted the way a word feels when it's on the tip of your tongue. It tasted like walking into a room and standing there, empty-handed, knowing you'd come for a reason. Every breath took a little edge off her, sandpaper on the varnish of herself, and she caught her mind reaching idly for her mother's face and finding it — blurred. Not gone. Blurred, like a watermark seen through cheap paper.

She stopped counting steps.

"You feel it too." The boy's voice behind her, low. He'd caught up without making a sound, which should have frightened her more than it did. "Don't fight it. Fighting makes it worse. Breathe shallow."

"You've been down here before." Not a question. She could hear it in how he moved — no hesitation at the turns, weight placed like a man following music he'd memorized.

"Born down here, near enough. My father ran these routes for twenty years." A pause, and when he spoke again the casualness had a hairline crack in it. "Until the guild asked him for something and he said no."

"The map."

"You know what it is?" Quick, hungry.

"I know it's why two men are trying to kill me. Beyond that—" She touched the oilcloth at her ribs. "I know it's the original covenant. The courier told me the covenant is breaking, and then he told me to run, and then he died on my floor. Those are all the facts I have. I'm an accountant, not a prophet."

"A what?"

"An accountant."

"In the undercity."

"Yes."

"...Huh," said Casper, with the particular reverence of the undercity for people with respectable trades, and Odile discovered she was almost smiling, which was absurd, which was impossible, which was — she noticed with alarm — exactly the kind of thing the air might be doing to her. Making the terrible seem funny. Loosening the rivets.

"Breathe shallow," she said, and heard him huff something that was almost a laugh, and hated herself a little for the warmth of it, and kept walking.

* * *

*Casper*

At the bottom of the stair there was no bottom. The steps ended and the dark continued at the same angle, smooth as poured glass, and Casper's stomach performed the specific lurch of a body realizing the world has stopped agreeing to rules.

"Hold the wall," he said. "Left hand. Trust the stone more than your eyes down here."

"Is that from the maps?"

"That's from my da. Rule three." He recited it the way other families recited grace: *"The Hollow doesn't hate you, but it won't remember you, so you remember yourself."*

Ahead, the ledger-woman — Odile, she'd finally said, when he asked twice, and only after he'd given his surname first, which told Casper she understood debts better than most debtors — put her palm against the stone and kept walking, and the dark kept letting them.

An hour, maybe two. Time behaved strangely; his heartbeat insisted it had been minutes, his legs insisted it had been a day. Twice they passed openings where faint light moved like light through curtain-gauze, and once, distantly, they heard singing — many voices, thin as thread, a melody that seemed to be missing notes the way a moth-eaten coat is missing patches.

Odile stopped walking. "Do you hear—"

"Don't answer it," Casper said quickly. "Whatever it sounds like. Da's rule seven."

"What's rule seven?"

"If something down here calls you by name, it wants the name more than it wants you."

She was quiet for a moment. Then, dry as ledger-paper: "Your father sounds like he would have been good at audits."

"He was good at *leaving*. Same skill set." And then, because the dark made talking easier, the way it made everything else harder, Casper heard himself keep going. "He carried messages between up there and down here. Covenant business. Sworn courier, last of the line. When the guild decided the covenant was more useful broken, they needed the original map destroyed, and he wouldn't give it up, and—" His jaw did something he couldn't control. "Two years ago. Knife wound shaped like a guild seal."

Odile turned in the dark. He couldn't see her face well — just the pale oval of it, and the shine of her eyes, which were dry, but only barely.

"And now his map has found me," she said. "Or I found it. Or a dying man chose me out of a room full of scribes because—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I found entries in the guild ledgers that shouldn't exist. Payments recorded in memories. I thought it was a cipher error. I corrected it." A sound that was not quite a laugh. "I *corrected* it. Nine years of balancing their books, and the moment I find the one column that matters, I fix the math and hand them the proof."

"They would've found out anyway."

"You don't know that."

"No," Casper admitted. "But I know my da said the same kind of thing once. *If I'd just kept my head down.* Everyone who ever mattered says it right before they do the thing that mattered." He shrugged, awkward, surprised at his own mouth. "Point is. You didn't sell them out. They sold *you*. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference."

The silence that followed was different from the other silences. Warmer by one degree. He'd take it. Down here, one degree was a fortune.

* * *

*Odile*

The door, when they found it, was already looking for them.

That was the only way she could describe it. After hours of smooth dark, the air ahead thickened into a suggestion of architecture — a frame, a lintel, the ghost of hinges — and through it she could see the Hollow.

She had expected, without admitting it, hell. What she saw was a city at dusk that never finished becoming dusk: rooftops of slate-blue stone, windows lit with a light like the memory of candlelight rather than the fact of it, gardens growing silver instead of green. Beautiful the way a portrait is beautiful — beautiful *of* something, and fading from its edges inward, like a photograph left in the sun.

The air through the door tasted stronger here. Richer. She breathed it and lost, briefly, the name of her brother, and panicked, and clawed it back — *Tam. Tam, who is eleven, Tam who burns toast* — and held it with both hands like a bird.

"It gets worse," Casper said quietly, watching her. He'd gone still in a way that wasn't calm; it was the stillness of a man at a graveside. "First day, it takes little things. Song titles. What you ate yesterday. My da used to write our names on his forearm in ink before every run." He pushed up his sleeve. There, faded but stubborn, in a dead man's careful hand: CASPER. ALDOUS. He tapped the letters. "Ink fades. Memory fades faster. We write everything down from here. Every hour. Names, faces, why we came."

"Why we came," Odile repeated, and unslung her satchel, and found a stub of pencil and the back page of a debt schedule she'd never finish.

She wrote: *Odile Montrose. I am twenty-three. I owe the Scribes' Guild nine hundred crowns, which they will never collect. I had a mother who sang off-key and a brother who burns toast. I am going to save a world I didn't believe in yesterday.*

Casper watched her write, then took the pencil and added, beneath, in blunt carpenter's letters: *Casper Vane. Son of Aldous. I came because of a seal on a map. I'm staying because of the woman writing this.*

She stared at it. "That's sentimental."

"It's *documentation*," he said. "Rule three. The Hollow won't remember us. So we remember each other."

And because the air was pulling at her, and because fear shared is fear halved, and because she had spent nine years being precise about things that didn't matter and wanted, desperately, to be precise about something that did — Odile held out her hand.

"Wary truce," she said. "You know the dark. I know the covenant. Nobody sells anybody out."

He shook it. His grip was rope-callused and warm. "Nobody sells anybody out," he agreed, and then ruined the solemnity entirely by adding, "Also you snore, probably. I'll document it."

"I do not—"

Somewhere below them, across the silvered roofs of the fading city, a bell rang — a cracked, patient bell, tolling a note that Odile felt in her teeth rather than her ears. And under the note, woven through it, came that thin many-voiced singing again, closer now, and this time she could hear what was wrong with it.

The voices were singing names. Hundreds of names. And every few bars, a name would simply stop — mid-word, mid-breath — as if the singer had reached for it and found the shelf empty.

Casper's face had gone gray. "That's the Lament," he said. "My da heard it once, from the far side. He said when the singing stops, the covenant's nearly paid out. When it goes quiet altogether—"

"—there's nothing left to sing," Odile finished.

The bell tolled again. Below them, in the dusk that never finished, a child appeared on a distant stair, small and bright as a struck match, running toward the door as if she'd been waiting for it all her life — and Odile's hand tightened on the map, and the two of them stepped together into the Hollow.
