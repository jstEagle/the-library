## 6. The Hollow Below

The last stair let go of us like a hand that had held on too long.

I felt it as a loosening behind my ribs — the sense of a door deciding, at last, to be a door only when it wished to be — and then we were through, and the air changed.

I had expected cold. I had expected the mineral breath of deep stone, the damp dark of cellars and crypts, the smell of a city's forgotten underside. What I got instead was warmth. A slow, green warmth, like the inside of a greenhouse in late summer, thick with the scent of loam and something sweeter beneath it — overripe fruit, candlewax, rain that had fallen somewhere far away and long ago.

"Do you feel that?" Casper whispered behind me. His voice had gone strange, flattened with awe. "That's not air. That's *remembering*. The whole place is remembering."

I did feel it. It pressed against my skin like a second atmosphere, faintly electric, faintly sad. Every breath carried fragments — a snatch of a lullaby in a language I didn't know but somehow almost understood; the ghost-smell of bread baking in a kitchen that belonged to no one; grief, old and worn smooth as river glass, drifting past like pollen. The Hollow breathed memories the way the surface world breathed smoke.

"Keep your hood up," I said, because saying something ordinary kept me from screaming. "And keep walking."

We came down out of the stair onto a shelf of black rock, and the Hollow opened below us like a held breath.

There was no sky. There was instead a vast ceiling of stone hung with lights — thousands upon thousands of them, small and soft and gold, clustered like fruit, like stars that had grown tired of being distant and come down to live among their people. Beneath that false constellation, the land rolled away in gentle terraces: gardens of pale silver grass, orchards whose trees bore blossoms and lantern-fruit at once, a river that ran without hurry, its water so clear it seemed less like water than like the *idea* of water, remembered by someone who loved it. Houses of white stone and dark timber climbed the hillsides, their windows glowing amber. A bell tower stood at the center of everything, slender and patient, though no bell rang from it.

It was beautiful. That was the first thing. The first thing, and the cruelest, because the beauty was real — and so was the dying.

I saw it in the light itself. The lantern-stars overhead did not shine evenly. Some blazed warm and steady, but others guttered, browned at the edges like paper near a flame, and here and there whole clusters had gone dark, leaving holes in the heaven where light should have been. The silver grass grew patchy. In the low places it hadn't turned grey exactly — it had turned *less*, thinning into transparency, so that the black rock showed through it like bone through skin. One orchard tree stood leafless and colorless, its branches so faint I could see the hillside behind them.

"It's going out," I said. My voice sounded small and stupid against all that silence. "The whole realm. It's going out like a lamp."

"Not going out." Casper came to stand beside me, his father's route-map crushed against his chest. He was seventeen and trying very hard to be older, and for once he wasn't pretending. "Being *taken*." He nodded down at the nearest terrace, where figures moved among the fields. "Look at them. Look properly."

I looked properly, and horror arrived in my chest like a swallowed stone.

They were beautiful too — the people of the Hollow. Tall, graceful, dressed in clothes woven from what looked like dyed lamplight, moving through their dimming fields with unhurried grace. But they were *thin* in a way that had nothing to do with flesh. Their edges blurred. When one woman bent to gather fruit, her hand passed slightly through the branch before finding it, the way a dreamer's hand fumbles a cup. An old man sat by the roadside mending a net that wasn't there, his fingers working patiently at empty air, and I realized with a lurch that he had forgotten the net but not the mending. A child chased something invisible across the grass, laughing — and the laugh echoed wrong, arriving a half-second after her open mouth, as if even sound had to strain to remember her.

"They're fading," I said. "Not dying. Fading."

"The guild calls it attrition." Casper's jaw was tight. "My father called it theft. Same thing, if you're the one being robbed."

We went down into the Hollow because there was nowhere else to go, and because the map — blood-inked, sealed, humming faintly against my ribs where I'd bound it beneath my coat — pulled at me like a fish on a line. Down through the silver grass, along the memory-river, toward the bell tower that never rang. And as we walked, the people noticed us, and this was worse than anything.

They noticed us *wrong*.

A woman standing at her gate watched us pass with polite, puzzled eyes, the look one gives strangers on a road — except her gaze slid off Casper twice before it caught, and each time it caught she blinked hard, as though focusing on him cost her something. A man carrying firewood nodded to us and said, "Good evening, travelers," in the tone of a man who says it forty times a day because he cannot remember whether he has already said it. An elderly pair on a bench held hands and smiled at us with such perfect, vacant sweetness that I had to look away, because their smiles were the smiles of people who had been happy once, in a story they could no longer quite recall.

"They've lost so much," I murmured. "What does the guild even take? How do you sell a memory?"

"By the pound, apparently." Casper's bitterness had an edge of grief underneath it, and I understood suddenly that this was not new knowledge to him. "Father said the ledgers price them like wine. First loves go high. Weddings. The smell of your mother's kitchen. Grief sells best of all — people up there pay anything to feel someone else's sorrow, just to know they still can." He kicked a stone from the path, and the stone faded where his boot struck it, which unsettled us both. "They take the big things first. Then they get greedy. Then they take whatever's left."

"And what's left," I said quietly, watching the old man mend his invisible net, "is this."

A bell tower rose ahead of us at the meeting of three roads, and beside it, a well — dry, ringed with carved names so worn they were barely letters anymore. Around the well, the Hollow folk had gathered for what seemed to be a market, though nothing was bought or sold. They simply traded: a song for a song, a name of a flower for the name of a season, small kindnesses of recollection passed hand to hand like coins worn nearly smooth. One man offered a memory of snow. Three children crowded around him, eyes wide, mouths open, receiving winter like communion.

I stopped walking. I couldn't help it.

"That's the market," I said. "That's what they have left. They trade memories back and forth because none of them can keep hold of their own."

Casper said nothing. He had gone very still, staring past me, and I turned to follow his gaze.

Near the well, a mother stood with her daughter.

The girl could not have been more than six, small and solemn, wearing a dress the color of dusk. She held her mother's hand the way children do — absolutely, without thought, as if that hand were the fixed point around which the world turned. The mother knelt to straighten the girl's collar, and she was doing it correctly, tenderly, every motion right, and her face was kind and lost.

"Mama," the little girl said clearly, in a voice that carried across the hush of the market, "who are you?"

The mother's hands stilled on the small collar. For a moment she only looked at her daughter — looked at her with love, real love, undimmed, the love surviving like a root beneath a burned field — and I watched her search herself for the answer and find the shelves empty. I watched her realize she did not know this child's name. I watched her understand, in some deep animal place beneath the forgetting, that this was the worst thing that would ever happen to her, and that she would not be permitted to remember it happening.

"I'm—" the mother began. Her voice cracked like ice on a pond. "I'm the one who takes care of you."

"But who *are* you?" The girl's lip trembled. Her grip tightened on that beloved, unfamiliar hand. "You used to sing the blue song. In the kitchen with the round window. You used to sing it and I knew all the words and now I don't know any of them and *you*—"

"Hush." The mother gathered the child against her, folding her in, hiding her own face in the small hair. "Hush, little one. I'm yours. Whoever I am. I'm yours."

The girl clung to her. Around them the market went on gently, songs changing hands, snow falling invisibly into small cupped palms, and no one looked, because looking was a thing they had learned not to spend themselves on.

I discovered I had put my hand over my mouth. I made myself lower it.

"This is what we buy," I heard myself say. My voice came out flat and terrible. "Upstairs. This is what the prosperity rests on. Every lamp in Ashgrave burning bright, every guild ledger balanced to the copper — and underneath, a mother who doesn't know her daughter's face." I turned to Casper, and my eyes were hot, and I did not care. "I balanced those books. Eleven years. I found the entries and I told myself they were rounding errors. *Memories, paid in kind.* I read those words and I thought they were code for grain. For coin. I was good at my work, Casper. I was so *good* at it, and my goodness was the lid on all of this."

"You didn't know."

"I chose not to know." The words tasted like ash and honesty. "There's a difference, and the difference is the whole of me."

He looked at me for a long moment — this boy with his dead father's maps and his fists full of inherited grief — and then he did something I did not expect. He reached out and touched the bundle at my side, where the map lay hidden, briefly, like a vow.

"Then we unbalance the books," he said.

Before I could answer, a sound moved through the market — not a sound exactly, more a change in the quality of the hush, the way air shifts before a storm. The Hollow folk had turned as one toward the far end of the square, where a broad avenue climbed toward a palace of pale stone that seemed, impossibly, to be built partly of *light* — walls of it, columns of it, glowing softly and flickering in places, dimming in others, like everything else here.

"The regent's court," Casper murmured. "Father wrote about it. The one who signed the covenant, ages back. If anyone remembers its terms—"

"No one will," I said bitterly, thinking of the old man and his net. "That's rather the entire crime."

But the crowd was parting, and through it came a procession, and the procession was wrong in a way that made my skin crawl. Six guards in armor of tarnished silver walked with ceremonial slowness — and their armor fit them badly, loosely, like clothing made for larger men, as though their bodies were shrinking inside their histories. Behind them, borne on a litter of woven light, came a figure robed in white gone grey at the hem.

The regent was fading faster than any of them.

I could see the palace steps *through* her shoulder. Her face was noble still, carved by long centuries of duty, but the features had softened at the edges like wax left near a flame, and her eyes — her eyes were the worst. They were clear and ancient and utterly unfocused, gazing out at her people with an expression of vast, courteous love for a world she could no longer quite perceive. As the litter passed, she lifted one translucent hand in blessing. A child waved at her. The regent smiled at the space just left of the child, and the child's face fell, and the guards' blank helms turned away in perfect shame.

"She doesn't remember either," I whispered. "She signed it. She *sealed* the covenant with her own hand, and she's sold so much of herself keeping the payments flowing that she can't remember what she promised."

"Then the map is all that's left," Casper said. "The original. Blood-ink. The one thing the guild never managed to buy." He glanced at me sideways, and in the strange gold light his young face looked suddenly fierce. "Which means, Odile Montrose, that you are currently the most dangerous person in two worlds. And the guild knows it."

The words settled over me cold, despite the Hollow's warm remembering air. I thought of enforcers' boots on scriptorium stairs. Of a courier's blood spreading across the floor tiles. Of my debt-bond, called due, my name written in a ledger I had once believed was only arithmetic.

And then the map spoke.

There was no other word for it. Against my ribs, beneath my coat, the blood-sealed vellum warmed suddenly, sharply, like a hand pressed flat against my heart — and from somewhere inside the folded layers, in a voice like ink drying, like a promise being kept, I heard a single word that was not a word, a pulse of meaning rather than sound:

*Toward.*

Toward the palace. Toward the flickering court. Toward whatever remained, at the top of those shining steps, of the person who had made the bargain that was killing a world.

The regent's litter paused at the foot of the palace stair. And the regent — blind, faded, courteous, gone — turned her head with sudden, terrible precision, and her ancient eyes found me across the whole length of the square, through the whole depth of the crowd, and fixed there.

She raised that translucent hand again. Not in blessing this time.

In *recognition*.

"Casper," I breathed. "She sees the map. She sees *it*, even if she can't see me."

The guards were turning. Slowly, ceremonially, the way everything here moved — but turning.

"*Run toward her,*" the map pulsed against my heart, warm and insistent and utterly certain. "*Toward.*"

So, with hunters behind us in the dark above and a fading queen beckoning us forward, I ran.
