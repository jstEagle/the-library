## 7. What My Father Collected

My father kept his secrets the way other men kept coin — close to the skin, counted in the dark, never spent where anyone could see.

I learned that early. I was seven when I found the first one, wedged behind a loose brick in the chimney of our rooms above the rope-walk: a strip of oiled leather, and inside it a page of paper so thin it seemed embarrassed to exist. It was covered in my father's handwriting — the same careful, upright script he used for his route-maps, but denser, folded over itself like a moth hiding its wings. I couldn't read most of it. The words were old, or wrong, or both. But at the bottom, drawn small and precise as a spider's signature, there was a seal I knew better than my own name.

A circle broken by a vertical line. A stair descending through it.

The seal of the covenant.

* * *

I didn't tell him I'd found it. That was the first rule of loving Aldous Vane: you did not ask about the work. You mended his coats when they came home torn. You learned which of his silences meant *tired* and which meant *followed*. You ate supper across from him and watched him count, with his eyes alone, the number of people in the room, and you pretended not to notice, because noticing out loud would have made it real.

He was a courier. That's what he told the neighbors, what the guild's rolls said, what I repeated at school when the other boys asked. *He carries things.* It was even true. He carried things. What he carried, and for whom, and down how many stairs into how much dark — those were questions he answered only once, near the end, when answering them cost more than silence ever had.

But I'm ahead of myself. Down here, in the Hollow, where the air tastes like the moment before you sneeze and every shadow has a second shadow underneath it, it's hard not to be ahead of yourself. Grief does that. It runs forward into all the empty rooms the dead leave open.

* * *

We had been walking for hours when Odile finally asked me.

She'd held out longer than I expected. She was a ledger-woman, after all — she understood that some entries you don't read until the audit forces you. But the tunnel we'd taken off the Stair had narrowed into something older, walls slick with moisture that wasn't quite water, and carved into the stone at intervals were marks. Route-marks. My father's route-marks.

She ran her fingers over one — three short lines beneath a broken circle — and looked back at me with that expression she wore instead of asking questions, her eyebrows doing the arithmetic her mouth refused to.

"My father's," I said, because she'd earned that much. She'd bled on the Stair beside me. She hadn't screamed when the door opened onto nothing.

"Your father was Scribes' Guild?"

"He was *sworn*." I said the word carefully, because it mattered. "There's a difference. Guild scribes keep accounts. Couriers keep promises. The covenant had couriers — sworn ones — men and women who carried messages and payments between the surface and..." I gestured at the dark around us, at the whole breathing dimness of it. "...here. Before your guild decided promises were just debts with better handwriting."

"And he marked the routes."

"He mapped them. Every stair, every hour they existed, every door that was only a door at certain times. His maps were the only complete set in existence." My voice came out flatter than I wanted. Flat is what happens when feeling gets pressed too long under too much weight. "The guild burned what they found after he died. They thought they got all of them."

"But they didn't."

"No. He hid them. All over the city, all under it. Behind bricks. Under chapel floors. Inside the false bottoms of tea chests in houses whose owners never knew they were keeping them." I touched the mark on the wall, three lines and a broken circle, worn shallow by decades of damp. "I've been finding them since he died. One at a time. It's slow work. It's the only work I have."

Odile was quiet for a moment. In the Hollow's half-light her face was all planes and shadows, sharp as a ledger line. "Casper. What did your father carry?"

And there it was. The question. The one I'd been carrying myself for eleven years, packed tight as anything he'd ever strapped to his back.

* * *

Here is what I knew before he told me, from fragments, from overheard arguments, from the shape of his fear:

That there was a realm below the city older than the city. That the two realms had struck a bargain centuries ago — a covenant, written in blood-ink by hands on both sides, binding each to sustain the other. The Hollow gave the surface its deep foundations, its clean water, the strange good luck that made the city prosper while other cities starved. The surface gave the Hollow... something else. Something the covenant called *remembrance*. Payment rendered willingly, freely, on schedule, sealed and carried down by sworn couriers.

That somewhere in the last hundred years, the willing part had rotted away.

That the Scribes' Guild — the institution built to guard the covenant — had discovered it could take instead of receive. That memories could be drawn out of sleeping debtors, bottled, sold. That selling them was profitable, and paying them down was expensive, and the difference between those two facts was enough to corrupt an order of monks.

That my father figured out the theft. And that when they ordered him to hand over the maps — the true maps, the original covenant-map and every route to it — he said no.

I knew all of that the way you know the shape of a room in the dark. What I didn't know, until six weeks before his death, was why he'd stayed loyal so long. Why a man who saw the rot didn't run.

* * *

It was winter. He came home late, smelling of lamp oil and cold stone, and he sat down at our table without taking off his coat, which he never did, and he looked at me for a long time — I was fifteen, all elbows and anger in those days, furious at him for secrets I could feel but not name — and he said:

"Cas. Sit down. I'm going to tell you what I collect."

Not *carry*. Collect.

"The covenant isn't paper," he said. "Paper's just the receipt. The covenant is a flow. Like blood in a body, or coin in a market. The Hollow gives the surface its luck — the deep luck, the old luck, the reason our wells don't poison and our walls don't crack. And the surface pays it back in memory. Living memory. Given gladly. That's the term that matters — *given gladly*. The Hollow can't survive on stolen goods any more than a man can live on transfused blood taken by force. It sickens. It forgets itself. It starts to die."

He turned his hands over on the table. Courier's hands, scarred and steady.

"For thirty years I carried payment down. Little vials of it. A fisherman's joy in his daughter's first steps, given free because he loved the Hollow that kept his boat safe. A widow's memory of her wedding, donated because her mother had been born below and she wanted the gift to go home. Do you understand? People used to know about the Hollow, Cas. Not the way the guild knows — the way neighbors know. There were shrines at the wellheads. Offerings left at doors that weren't doors. The payment flowed because everyone gave a little, and no one missed it, and the Hollow bloomed."

"What changed?"

His jaw worked. For a moment he looked not like my father but like an old soldier remembering a retreat.

"Greed dressed up as necessity," he said. "About eighty years ago there was a bad decade — plagues, fires, the harbor freezing. The guild's masters panicked. They started drawing memories without asking. Justifying it. *Emergency measures.* And the emergency never ended, because stolen memories sold beautifully up top. Rich merchants bought their dead wives' voices back in bottles. The universities bought genius. The banks bought... certainty. Whole lifetimes, lad. Whole lifetimes in little glass tubes, priced by quality." He smiled, and it was the worst thing I'd ever seen, that smile. "The city calls it prosperity. Down below, they call it being eaten."

"So you stopped."

"I tried to stop it properly." He shook his head. "You don't fight the guild with refusal, Cas. Refusal is a single entry; they'll just write around it. I spent years copying everything. The covenant's terms. The original map — the true one, written in blood-ink, the one that shows where the Well of Names is and what reforging requires. Every hidden route, every stair that exists only at certain hours, every door that's a lie except at dusk. If the covenant could be reforged — if someone could get down there with the truth — then maybe..."

"Maybe what?"

"Maybe the theft could be reversed. Maybe the payment could flow again, freely, the way it was meant to." He reached across the table and gripped my wrist, and his hand was shaking, which frightened me more than anything he'd said. "Listen to me. If they come for me, you find the maps first. Before them. There's a key hidden in each one — a piece of a phrase, a fragment of the sealing words. Alone they're nothing. Together—"

The knock came at the door then. Three knocks, evenly spaced, patient as arithmetic.

I have relived that night ten thousand times. I have rebuilt it in my head with different endings — endings where we went out the window, where we ran for the river stairs, where I was faster, or braver, or simply awake enough to understand what the knocking meant before the door came down. In none of those versions do I save him. I know that now. Knowing it hasn't stopped me from trying.

They took him from our rooms while I stood in the corner like furniture, because I was young and he'd put himself between me and the enforcers' blades and told me, with a look, to be nothing, to be no one, to be a boy who'd seen nothing. He went with them quietly. He'd already given me everything that mattered.

They brought him back three days later. What was left of him. Officially, he'd fallen down a stair in the guild archives. Unofficially — in the way the neighborhood understood, in the way his old contacts went silent overnight, in the way the enforcers spent a week tearing apart our home brick by brick looking for papers they never found — he had been asked to surrender the map, and he had refused, and the guild had remembered that they were also very good accountants of another kind entirely.

The kind who balance ledgers with lives.

* * *

"—and that's why," I finished, "when I saw his seal on your map, I followed you. Not for the guild. Not for the reward they'd have paid me if I'd dragged you back upstairs, which, for the record, I considered for almost no seconds at all."

"You considered it," Odile said.

"I considered it the way you consider jumping. Briefly, and mostly to prove you won't."

Almost, she smiled. Down here in the dark, on her side of our small fire, it nearly happened. Then it passed, the way light passes behind clouds, and she looked at the route-mark on the wall again, and I watched her do sums behind her eyes. She was always doing sums. I was beginning to understand that the sums were how she prayed.

"They killed him for a map," she said quietly. "And I've been carrying it in my coat like a grocery list."

"You didn't know what it was."

"I balanced their books, Casper. For nine years. I recorded payments in and payments out, and when the columns didn't match, I assumed *I* had erred, because the guild doesn't err." Her voice had gone strange — scraped thin, like something dragged over stone. "I sat at that desk and I made the theft *legible*. I made it clean. I wrote down, in beautiful figures, exactly how many memories they took this quarter, and I never once asked where the column labeled ACQUISITIONS came from." She stared into the fire. "Your father refused to hand over one map and died for it. I handed over thousands of pages and got promoted."

The fire popped. Somewhere far off in the tunnels, water dripped with the patience of something that had been dripping since before the city above had a name.

"You want to know the worst part?" she said. "When the courier put that map in my hands, my first thought — my honest, immediate first thought — was *this will unbalance everything.*"

I thought about my father then. About the last time I'd seen him alive, standing in our doorway with his collar torn and his eyes already saying goodbye, and how his last look at me wasn't fear. It was instruction. *Find the maps first,* it said. *Finish the route.*

"It should unbalance everything," I said. "That's what truth is. It's the entry that makes the whole book honest."

This time she did smile — briefly, crookedly, gone as fast as it came. But it counted. I logged it. I was my father's son; I kept accounts too, just of smaller, stranger things. Every kindness Odile Montrose let slip past that ledger-keeper's armor of hers, I wrote down somewhere inside myself, in ink I was pretty sure was permanent.

* * *

We walked on. The tunnel widened, then narrowed, then opened without warning into a gallery where the ceiling rose into darkness and the walls were covered — floor to vanishing point — with names.

Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Carved, painted, chalked, scratched, renewed over and over across centuries by hands that must have come here generation after generation to say, in whatever medium they had: *I was here. We were here. Remember.*

Near the base of the wall, whole sections had gone smooth. Not eroded. Smoothed. Deliberately effaced, the stone rubbed blank as if the names themselves had been drawn out of it — harvested, bottled, sold. And beside one of those blank patches, fresh chalk, shaky and recent:

*she was called Maren. she liked rivers. please remember she liked rivers.*

Someone had tried to save a name with a sentence. Someone down here was losing their mother or their sister or their self, and all they could think to do was leave a note.

I stood there a long time. My father had walked this gallery — I was sure of it, sure the way you're sure of a voice you'd know anywhere. He'd stood where I was standing, and he'd seen what I was seeing, and he'd gone home and copied maps by candlelight for years because of it. This was what he collected, in the end. Not payments. Not secrets.

Obligations.

"Come on," I said, and my voice came out rougher than I intended. "The regent's court is a day further, if the map holds. And Odile—" I turned back to look at her, firelight and fading names and all that terrible quiet stone between us. "When we get there. When we show them the covenant. I need you to understand something."

She waited.

"My father didn't die protecting paper. He died protecting *them*." I nodded at the wall, at Maren and her rivers and every smoothed-over name. "Every step of this route, he walked it knowing what it cost and what it was for. So whatever the guild offered you upstairs, whatever they're hunting us with, whatever this costs — I'm finishing his route. All of it. To the Well of Names, if that's where it goes."

"That's where it goes," Odile said softly. She had the map out, and even from across the dead gallery I could see the blood-ink catch the light, dark red as a heartbeat. "Casper — the seal. Look at the seal."

I took it from her. And there at the bottom margin, in my father's unmistakable hand, small as a confession, was a line I had never seen on any of his route-maps. A dedication. A delivery address for everything he'd ever carried:

*For whoever finishes the route: the last payment was never made. It waits at the Well. It has been waiting eighty years. See it delivered.*

The tunnel ahead breathed cold against my face, and somewhere below us, faint as a pulse, I heard water moving — a lot of water, moving with purpose, like something vast turning over in its sleep.

The Well of Names was listening.
