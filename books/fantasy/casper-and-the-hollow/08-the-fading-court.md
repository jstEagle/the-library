## 8. The Fading Court

The road to the regent's court was paved with names.

I didn't understand that at first. Casper led me through streets that seemed drawn in charcoal — the outlines of houses present but the interiors smudged, as though some great hand had rubbed at them until only the suggestion of home remained. And along the gutters, caught in the cracks of the pale stone like seeds after a flood, were letters. Small ones. A *t* here, an *ell* there, half a word crumbling into dust where someone had stepped on it.

"Careful where you walk," Casper said quietly. He'd bound his wound with a strip torn from his shirt, and he moved with his left arm held close, protectively, like a man carrying something breakable inside his ribs. "That's not litter."

"It's writing." I crouched despite his warning and looked closer. The letter nearest my boot was an *O*. My own initial, or one of ten thousand others'. "It's everywhere."

"That's what happens when a name comes loose." He didn't stop walking, so I rose and followed. "Down here, your name isn't just what people call you. It's what holds you together. Take enough of it away—" He gestured with his good hand at the smudged doorways, the blurred windows, the street that seemed to have forgotten its own direction halfway down the hill. "—this happens."

"And you know this how?"

"My father's maps have notes in the margins." His voice went flat, the way it always did when his father surfaced between us, which was more often than I liked and less often than he could help. "'Names are load-bearing,' he wrote. I used to think that was a joke."

It was not a joke. Nothing in the Hollow was.

We climbed for what felt like hours, up a stair cut into the spine of some enormous root — or bone; I decided not to ask which — until the air changed. Below us the Hollow's air had tasted of forgetting, thin and grey, like watered wine. Here it thickened. It smelled of candlewax and lilies and something older underneath, something like the inside of a locked drawer. The light came from lanterns that burned without flame, each one a soft gold egg of glow, and beneath them stood the court of the Hollow's regent.

I had imagined palaces before. I was a Scribe; I had catalogued descriptions of them by the hundred — marble halls, thrones, banners, all the furniture of power. The Fading Court was none of that. It was a hall built of glass and dusk, its walls so thin I could see the dark earth pressing against them like a sea held back by courtesy alone. The pillars were candles the height of trees, each one burning low, and I understood without being told that every candle was a life. Some burned tall and steady. Others had guttered to blue nubs, barely holding their shape, their light flickering in rhythms that were almost — almost — like breathing.

Between the pillars stood the people of the court: courtiers, heralds, guards in armor so old it had gone soft as cloth. They were beautiful the way a photograph is beautiful, all edges softened, faces like portraits left out in rain. A woman in a gown of grey silk turned to watch us pass, and her eyes were kind and utterly empty, twin lamps with no oil.

"They can't see us," I whispered.

"They see shapes," Casper said. "They remember there used to be things worth seeing." He swallowed. "Keep moving."

At the end of the hall, on a dais of white stone worn smooth as river pebbles, sat the Regent of the Hollow.

He had been magnificent once. You could see the architecture of it still beneath the ruin — the broad shoulders, the long hands, the crown that was not metal but a circlet of living script, letters circling his brow in slow orbit like birds that had forgotten how to land. But the man himself had gone translucent. I could see the throne through his chest. When he breathed, the candlelight passed through him and came out the other side, faintly bent, the way light bends through water.

His eyes were open. They were fixed on nothing, or on everything, or on some memory so far back that it had become the whole sky.

Casper knelt. After a moment's hesitation — kneeling is not a Scribe's instinct; we bow to institutions, not to persons — I knelt beside him.

"Your Grace," Casper said. "My name is Casper Vane. My father was Aldous Vane, courier of the covenant. I've brought the map."

Nothing. The regent's lips moved, shaping some word too faded to carry sound.

"Your Grace. The covenant is breaking. The surface guild has been selling your people's memories for decades. We've come to—"

The regent's head turned.

It moved slowly, with the terrible patience of a clock hand, and when his gaze found us I felt it like cold water poured down the back of my neck. There was awareness in those eyes. Not much. A puddle's worth, in a man who had once been an ocean. But it was enough to recognize that we were real, and speaking, and waiting.

"You," he said. The voice came out like paper being unfolded. "You are... the petitioners."

"We are," I said, because Casper had frozen, staring. "Your Grace, I'm Odile Montrose, of the Scribes' Guild. I—" The title stuck in my throat. Of the Scribes' Guild. As if I still belonged to them. As if they hadn't marked me for death three days ago. "I keep accounts. I found the payments. That's why we're here."

"Accounts." Something flickered across his ruined face. It might have been a smile. It might have been grief wearing a smile's clothing. "Yes. There were accounts. There was..." His brow furrowed, and the circlet of letters above it shuddered, two or three of them dimming like snuffed wicks. "There was a debt. We owed. Or they owed. Someone owed someone. There was ink."

"There was a covenant," Casper said gently. "Do you remember its terms?"

The silence stretched. In it I heard the candles burning — a sound like distant surf, like the Hollow itself breathing through a thousand small throats. One of the blue nubs behind us guttered out entirely, and a courtier simply wasn't there anymore. No body. No cry. Just an absence shaped exactly like a person, which folded politely into the floor and was gone.

"The terms," the regent repeated. He said it the way a scholar says a word in a dead language — correctly, reverently, and with no idea what it meant. "Terms are... given. Terms are received. There was a well." Hope leapt in my chest. "Or a door. There was a thing that was a place, and the place had a name, and the name—" His hands rose, translucent fingers spread wide, grasping at the air as if the sentence were a bird he might catch before it flew. "The name is written somewhere. It was written *here*, in me, and now..."

Now it was sold. I finished the sentence for him in the privacy of my skull and felt sick doing it. Somewhere in the guild's vaults, sealed in a vial and priced by weight, sat whatever memory had made this man a king. Probably filed under *miscellaneous antiquities*. Probably bought by some merchant who wanted to feel regal at dinner parties, who wore the regent's coronation day like a borrowed coat, feeling the warmth of it without ever asking whose heat he was stealing.

"Your Grace," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved. "The map. The original covenant, written in blood-ink. If we show it to you, will you know it? Will it give you back what you've lost?"

"I don't know," the regent said.

It was the most honest answer anyone had given me in years, and I hated it completely.

Casper unslung his satchel anyway, wincing as the strap crossed his wounded shoulder, and drew out the map. Even here, even in this hall of dying light, it seemed to generate its own gravity. The blood-ink had dried to the color of rusted iron, but the lines still moved — I had seen them move before and never gotten used to it, rivers of script crawling along the map's borders like ants carrying the alphabet somewhere important. He laid it on the steps of the dais and turned it to face the throne.

"Look," he said. "Please. Look and remember."

And the regent looked.

For a long moment nothing happened. Then the circlet above his brow flared — actually flared, gold-white, throwing shadows sharp as knives across the hall — and every courtier in the room turned at once, a movement so synchronized it raised the hair on my arms. The regent's translucent hands came down onto the armrests of his throne, and where they touched, the white stone *remembered itself*, grain blooming back into it like frost forming in reverse.

"I remember," he whispered. "I remember *ink*."

"Yes," I breathed. "Yes, Your Grace—"

"I remember signing. I remember the price. Both realms pay, both realms live, freely given, freely—" His voice cracked like ice over deep water. "Freely given. Freely *given*."

And then, like a wave receding, it went out of him. The flare died. The letters overhead dimmed to embers. His hands sank back into transparency, and the stone beneath them faded with them, and his face went smooth and vacant as a pond in winter.

"No," Casper said. He climbed two of the steps, heedless of protocol, heedless of the guards who stirred like sleepwalkers. "No, you were *there*, you remembered, it's right in front of you—"

"He can't hold it." My own voice sounded far away. I understood, suddenly and totally, in the way one understands a ledger line that finally balances: the map could show him the truth, but it couldn't make him keep it. Memory isn't information. Information can be copied. Memory has to be *held*, by a mind with enough of itself left to do the holding. "Casper. Casper, look at him. There's not enough of him left to store what he's seeing. Showing him is like pouring water into a broken jar."

"Then we fix the jar!" Casper whirled on me, and his eyes were wet and furious, and he was so young in that moment, younger than me, young enough to still believe that wanting something badly enough was a form of force. "The memories exist. You said so yourself — the markets, the vaults, the vials. They're sitting in the guild's cellar gathering dust while he—" He gestured at the regent, at the empty courtiers, at the child-shaped absence I'd glimpsed in the streets below, at all of it. "While everyone *drains*."

"So we steal them back." I said it flatly, testing the weight of it. "Break into the vaults. Take the vials. Return them."

"Yes!"

"And then what? We pour a stolen past back into a stolen people and call it restored?" I shook my head. "You saw the map's margins same as I did. Your father underlined it twice. *Freely given.* The covenant doesn't run on memories, Casper. It runs on consent. That's why the guild's version keeps failing — that's why the Hollow keeps fading no matter how much they sell. Theft doesn't seal a covenant. It just delays the bill."

Casper stared at me. Behind him, the regent had begun humming — tunelessly, sweetly, some fragment of a coronation anthem with the melody missing. Around him his court stood in perfect, patient stillness, waiting for a decree their king could no longer compose.

"You sound like a ledger," he said finally. Quiet. Wounded.

"I am a ledger," I said. "That's the only thing I've ever been allowed to be. So believe me when I tell you the numbers don't work. Stealing it back won't save them. It will just change who owes whom."

"Then what does work?"

I opened my mouth to say *I don't know* — and stopped. Because I did know. Or rather, the regent knew, or had known, five minutes ago, before the knowledge slid off him like rain off glass. *There was a well,* he'd said. *A thing that was a place, and the place had a name.*

"A place," I said slowly. "He said the covenant was sealed at a place. A well, or a door. If the original sealing site still exists, the covenant might be reforgeable there. By whoever holds the map." I looked down at the blood-ink lines, crawling patiently along their ancient routes, and noticed — for the first time — that they weren't decorative. They were directions. Every border of script on the map was a road, and every road converged, somewhere near the map's heart, on a single symbol I had taken for a signature.

A circle. A vertical line through it.

A well, drawn in blood, at the center of everything.

"Casper," I said. "Where's the Well of Names?"

But before he could answer, the far doors of the hall boomed.

Not opened — *boomed*, struck from outside, a sound I knew in my bones because I'd heard it through the guild's own doors my entire life. The knock of the enforcers' staff. Three blows, measured, official, final.

Every candle in the hall leaned toward the sound, flames bending like grass in wind, and the last warm light in the Fading Court went the color of a bruise.

"They followed us down," Casper whispered. His hand found mine in the dark, and it was shaking, and so was mine, and neither of us let go. "Odile. They're *here*."

* * *

We ran.

There was no dignity in it, no strategy worth the name — just Casper hauling me sideways off the dais as the doors burst inward and guild lantern-light, that flat grey light I had been born under and thought I'd escaped, came flooding up the hall like dirty water into a cellar. Enforcers in wax-grey cloaks. Six of them. Eight. Their boots rang on the white stone, and where they stepped, the names in the pavement died.

"This way — the servants' stair — my father mapped it—"

"Casper, wait—"

The regent's voice stopped us both.

It wasn't loud. It was barely a thread. But it carried, the way a bell carries under water, and it was aimed at me — at me specifically, at the map clutched against my chest, and when I turned I saw that his eyes, those puddle-deep fading eyes, had cleared for one final moment. One last coin of awareness, spent all at once.

"Scribe," he said. "Keeper of accounts. Listen." His translucent hand rose, trembling, and pointed — past us, past the enforcers, through the wall of his own hall, at something only he could see. "The well takes only what is *owed*. Nothing borrowed. Nothing stolen. Nothing given secondhand." The light in him was going out even as he spoke, syllable by syllable, like a man walking backward into a dark room. "Find what is truly yours to pay. And child — whatever you find —"

The doors broke. The first enforcer stepped through, and his lantern swept the hall, and the regent's face went smooth as milk.

"—don't pay it alone," the darkness where he had been seemed to finish, in my imagination, in my guilt, in the voice I would hear for weeks.

Then Casper pulled me through the servants' door, and the Fading Court closed behind us like a book, and somewhere in its pages the hunters began to turn.
