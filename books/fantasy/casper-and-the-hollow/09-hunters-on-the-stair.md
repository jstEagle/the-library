## 9. Hunters on the Stair

I learned to count footsteps before I learned my letters.

My father taught me on the long walks down into the undercity, his hand heavy on my shoulder: *Listen first, boy. The dark tells you everything if you let it speak.* Every stair in the deep roads had a voice — the wet slap of moss-grown treads near the cisterns, the dry clatter of old brick by the ash-chutes, the hollow ring of the marble steps that had fallen down from some rich man's house and never been missed. I could walk the whole of the beneath with my eyes shut and know where I was by sound alone.

So when the Hollow's air went wrong that evening — when the dim gold light along the Well Road flickered like a candle in a draft — I heard what Odile did not.

Boots. Six pairs, maybe seven. Descending.

They were still far off, up past the door that only opened at certain hours, but sound carries strangely in the Hollow, the way it carries in a bell. The realm itself seemed to pass the noise along, from stone to stone, as though it were frightened too.

"Cas." Odile looked up from the map, her fingers still tracing the blood-ink lines. She'd taken to holding it even when she wasn't reading it, the way you hold something you're afraid will be taken. "What is it?"

"Hunters." I was already moving, pressing my ear flat against the cold wall of the archway we'd sheltered under. The Well Road curved away ahead of us, lined with those pale fruiting trees that grew from nothing, their branches hung with little glass bells that rang without wind when someone remembered something. The bells had gone quiet. All of them at once. "Guild boots. Iron-shod. They're coming down the stair."

"That's impossible. The door only opens—"

"At certain hours. And it's dusk above right now, which means—" My stomach turned over. "Which means somebody told them exactly which hour."

She understood faster than I wanted her to. The guide. Wexlen, with his kind grey face and his stories about his daughters who no longer knew him. He'd walked with us two days through the root-tunnels, laughing at my father's old route-maps, correcting them gently — *this passage flooded three winters ago, boy* — and all the while he'd been counting our steps for someone else.

Odile's hand closed around mine. Hers was cold. "How long?"

"An hour, if they're careful. Less if they're not."

"Then we run."

* * *

We ran, and the Hollow ran with us.

That was the thing about the dying realm nobody above ever understood — it wasn't empty. It was *peopled*. As we fled down the Well Road, doors opened in the hillsides themselves, round doors set into the roots of the pale trees, and faces looked out at us. Fading faces, translucent at the edges like paper held against a lamp. A woman with silvered skin pressed a bundle into my arms without a word — bread, actual bread, dense and grey and warm. An old man stood in his doorway and sang, low and cracked, a song I somehow knew without knowing, and as he sang the glass bells overhead shivered and rang.

"They're giving us what they have left," Odile panted beside me. She ran like she did everything — precisely, economically, her braid whipping behind her like a ledger ribbon. But her eyes were wet. "Cas, they barely remember how to bake. That woman gave us her own—"

"Don't think about it."

"I *always* think about it. That's the whole problem with me."

Despite everything — the boots behind us growing louder, the stitch in my side, the wound of Wexlen's betrayal still raw in my chest — I laughed. Just once, just a breath of it. She glanced at me, startled, and something passed between us there in the failing light of the Well Road, quick and unspoken: the understanding that two people who should never have met had somehow become the sort of people who could make each other laugh while running for their lives.

Then the light died behind us, and I stopped thinking about anything but survival.

The hunters had torches. Real fire, brought down from above, and fire in the Hollow is a violence all its own — the pale trees recoiled from it, their branches curling away like fingers from a hot stove, and where the flames passed, the grass along the road went grey and stayed grey. They weren't hunting us anymore. They were burning a road through a world that couldn't fight back.

"There." I caught Odile's sleeve and pulled her left, off the road, toward a cleft in the hillside that my father's maps marked with a symbol I'd spent my whole childhood wondering about: a circle with a line drawn through it. *The Stitch,* he'd called it, once, when he was drunk enough to talk about the deep roads. *A seam where the world was sewn shut. Never go in, boy. Things fall apart in seams.*

"Your map says never go in," Odile said, reading it upside down in my hands as we scrambled up the scree.

"My map also says the guild would never find this place. Maps are advice, not law." I squeezed sideways into the cleft, dragging her after me. "And right now I'm advising us very strongly."

The Stitch was exactly what my father had promised: a crack in the world, narrow enough that we had to turn our shoulders, deep enough that the light behind us failed within ten steps. The rock here felt wrong under my palms — too smooth in places, rough in others, like something had been knitted together badly and left to heal. The air tasted of iron and old rain.

Behind us, muffled by stone, came the sound of the hunters reaching the Well Road. Shouting. The crackle of torches. Then a voice, amplified by the Hollow's strange acoustics until it seemed to come from inside my own skull:

*"The girl carries stolen property of the Scribes' Guild. The boy is an accessory. Take them breathing. Anything else you may take as spoils."*

Spoils. Of a dying realm. Of fading people who had just given us their last bread.

Something in me went very still and very hot, the way a banked coal goes before it flares.

"Casper." Odile's whisper came out of the darkness close to my ear. "That voice. Do you know it?"

"No." But I knew its *kind*. Master-Scribe-trained, the clipped vowels, the certainty of a man who had never once been told no by anyone who mattered. "Keep moving. There's a junction ahead where the seam opens out. After that I don't—"

The wall beside us exploded.

Not all of it. Just a section, a slab of the smooth wrong stone shearing inward as if punched by something enormous, and through the gap poured torchlight and smoke and the silhouettes of men in grey-and-crimson guild livery. Three of them, then four, climbing through the breach they'd made with whatever breaking-iron the guild used to open locked places. Of course they had tools for it. The guild had spent a hundred years opening things that were never meant to be opened.

"*Down!*"

I shoved Odile forward into the dark and turned to meet them, because someone had to be between her and the torchlight, and I had known since the night I saw my father's seal on her stolen map that it was going to be me.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about your first real fight: it doesn't feel like the stories. It feels like being late. Like every decision arrives already overdue, like your body is a step behind your fear the entire time.

The first hunter swung a weighted chain at my head and I ducked it mostly by accident, and the second one's fist caught me in the ribs hard enough to fold me in half. I heard myself make a sound I didn't recognize. The torchlight strobed across my vision. Somewhere behind me Odile was shouting my name, and somewhere ahead of me the seam of the world went on into blackness, and I was in the middle, which was where I had always been — the debt-collector's son, the boy in the middle, the boy who carried other people's papers through the dark.

But I knew the dark. They didn't.

I grabbed a handful of gravel from the broken wall — the Stitch's strange smooth stones, light as bone — and threw it full into the nearest torch. Sparks burst. The flame guttered. And in the half-second of darkness I moved the way my father had drilled into my legs on ten thousand midnight walks: *low, boy, always low, the dark belongs to the low.*

I hit the lead hunter at the knees.

We went down together onto stone, and his head struck the seam-rock with a crack I felt through my own skull, and then the second man was on me, and there was no more thinking, only weight and breath and the terrible intimacy of fighting — his elbow in my cheekbone, my teeth finding his wrist, the two of us grappling in the dirt like animals while the third hunter circled with a short blade, waiting for a clean strike, patient as a clerk.

The blade found me.

Not deep. Not a killing blow. It slid along my left side, below the ribs, a bright cold line that took a moment to become pain, and when the pain arrived it arrived completely, white and total, and I screamed — I'll admit that, I screamed like a child — and rolled away clutching my flank, and my hand came away wet.

Through the roaring in my ears I heard Odile.

She had picked up a fallen torch. This small precise woman who balanced impossible ledgers and apologized to furniture she bumped into was standing over me with a burning brand in both hands, swinging it in wide desperate arcs, and the hunters — trained men, armed men — actually fell back, because fire in the Hollow behaves strangely, feeds strangely, and the flames had begun to burn *gold*, the color of the realm's own dying light, and I think it frightened them more than any blade could have.

"The next one who touches him," she said, in her exacting ledger-clerk voice, shaking, "goes in the books. As a loss."

God help me, I loved her a little, right then, bleeding on the floor of a broken seam in the world.

But there were four of them, and more coming — we could hear the boots on the Well Road, doubling back, answering the shout — and my side was pumping warmth through my fingers with every heartbeat, steady as a dripping tap, and I knew arithmetic when I saw it. Four hunters, two of us, one wound that wouldn't wait.

"Odile." I got my feet under me, using the wall. "The junction. Fifty meters ahead, the seam opens, there's a fork — take the left passage. It floods, but it's low water now, and they won't follow you into water with those torches."

"We're not splitting up."

"You're taking the map to the regent, and I'm buying you the time to do it." I pushed the bundle into her arms — the map, my father's route-book, everything. "That's not a suggestion. That's the terms."

"I don't accept those terms."

"You accepted worse ones from the guild." I smiled at her, and I felt the smile come out crooked, and I didn't care. "Go left. Count forty stairs down. Don't stop for anything, not even me. Especially not me."

For one moment — one long, hanging moment, with the hunters regrouping in the torchlight and the boots thundering closer and my blood ticking away between my fingers — she looked at me with an expression I will carry into whatever comes next. Not pity. Not gratitude. Something fiercer than either. The look of a woman memorizing a debt she fully intended to collect.

Then she kissed my cheek, quick as sealing a letter, turned, and ran into the dark.

* * *

What happened after that happened fast, and I remember it in pieces, the way you remember a storm.

I pulled my father's knife — his real knife, the courier's blade with the seal worked into the hilt — and I put my back against the narrowest point of the seam, where only one man could reach me at a time. That was the whole of my strategy. Narrowness and stubbornness and a dead man's steel.

The first hunter came in overconfident and left holding his broken wrist. The second was smarter; he feinted high and drove his boot into my wounded side, and the world went white and sideways, and I went down to one knee with my back screaming and my front screaming louder. His blade rose.

It never fell. The wall itself reached out.

I swear this is true, though I know how it sounds. The Stitch — the sewn-shut seam of the world, the place my father warned me never to enter — groaned around us, and tendrils of that smooth wrong stone curled from the broken gap like slow green shoots, and they wrapped the hunter's arm and wrenched him backward into the dark beyond the wall, and his scream cut off abruptly, like a door closing. The remaining hunters froze. So did I.

The Hollow had chosen a side.

Maybe it was the blood — my blood, soaking into the seam-stone, a payment of a kind. Maybe it was the map, or the covenant stirring in its long sleep. Maybe the realm simply recognized, at last, the difference between thieves and couriers. I've had weeks since to wonder, and I still don't know. What I know is that the last two hunters looked at the moving wall, and at each other, and did the sensible thing, which was to run.

I meant to follow Odile immediately. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger keeps accounts of intentions: I *meant* to follow her.

Instead the floor tilted, or I did, and the smooth stone came up gentle as a mother's hand, and the last thing I saw was torchlight guttering gold at the mouth of the seam, and the last thing I heard — faint, from far down the left-hand passage, past forty stairs, through water and dark — was Odile's voice calling my name.

Calling it like she was afraid it might fade.

Like she already knew what names cost in this world, and what it means to spend one on someone else.

Then the Hollow folded its dark around me like a page turning, and I stopped hearing anything at all.
