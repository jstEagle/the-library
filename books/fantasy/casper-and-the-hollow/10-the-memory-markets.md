## 10. The Memory Markets

The tunnels changed the way a voice changes when it lies.

Casper felt it first — I saw it in the set of his shoulders, the way he paused with one hand flat against the tunnel wall as though listening for a heartbeat in the stone. "Smell that?" he asked.

I couldn't, at first. Then it came to me on the next breath, faint and wrong: something sweet, like sugar left too long near a candle flame, like the ghost of honey in an empty jar. Beneath it ran another scent entirely — old paper, ink, the dry mineral breath of archives. It was a smell I knew better than my own name, and finding it here, three hundred feet beneath the city whose Scribes' Guild had employed me for nine years, made my stomach turn over slowly, like a page being flipped by an unkind hand.

"Paper," I said. "And preservative. The kind we used for—"

"For keeping things," Casper finished. His face had gone careful and closed, the way it did whenever we spoke of what the guild actually did down here. "For keeping things a long time."

We walked on. The passage widened by degrees, the rough-hewn dark giving way to dressed stone, then to brick laid in patterns I recognized from the guild's older buildings — herringbone work from before the Fire Ordinances, the kind of masonry you only found in places built to outlast their builders. Someone had spent money here. Someone had spent a great deal of money making sure no ledger entry would ever point this way.

The light came next. Not torchlight — nothing so honest. It seeped from seams in the ceiling, a pale gold luminescence without any heat in it at all, and where it touched the walls I could see they were lined with shelving. Miles of it, I thought dizzily. Shelving running away into the gloom on both sides, tier upon tier, each shelf crowded with objects that caught the strange light and held it: glass vials in wooden racks, each one stoppered with wax, each one glowing faintly with a color that shifted when you looked at it directly, like light on the inside of a shell.

Casper stopped walking altogether.

"What is it?" I whispered.

"I know this place." His voice came out flattened, pressed thin. "My father's maps. He drew a junction here — he marked it *not for travel* and never said why. I thought he meant it was flooded, or collapsed." He swallowed. "He knew. He drew the door to it and he never told me."

There was no time to answer him kindly, because at that moment we heard voices.

We did what we were becoming practiced at doing — we folded ourselves into the shadow behind a rack of shelving, and I pulled my cloak around us both, and we waited. Two figures passed along the aisle opposite, carrying a lantern between them, though the lantern seemed almost redundant in that luminous air. They wore no guild livery, but they moved with the unhurried confidence of men who owned everything they surveyed.

"—the Ashwell lot goes up tomorrow," one was saying. "Merchant family. Third purchase this year."

"Quality?"

"Thin. Childhood mostly. First snows, a dead dog, a wedding. Nothing structural." A pause, and then a laugh I felt in my teeth. "They never miss the structural ones. That's the trick of it, isn't it? Sell a man his griefs and he thanks you for the lightness."

They passed. Their laughter faded into the humming dark, and neither Casper nor I moved for a long moment.

"They're selling them," I breathed at last. "Here. Like — like bolts of cloth. Like spice." My hands had gone cold inside my gloves. Nine years I had kept the guild's books. Nine years I had balanced columns to the last copper and told myself that precision was a virtue, that a well-kept ledger was a form of honesty. And all the while the true accounts had been kept here, in glass, in wax, in the color of other people's yesterdays.

"Not just selling," Casper said quietly. He was staring down the aisle with an expression I couldn't read. "Look at the racks. Look at how they're organized."

I looked. And because I was who I was — because precision was the only prayer I had ever been taught — I looked properly.

The shelves were not arranged by size or by date of acquisition. They were arranged by district. I saw the little brass plaques nailed to the uprights, and I read them the way I read everything, quickly and completely: *Ashwell*. *Corvane Row*. *The Tanner's Quarter*. *Sallow Street*. District after district, street after street, the entire city catalogued and shelved like a library of lives. And within each section, smaller labels: *Griefs*. *Joys*. *Faces*. *Years*.

"It's a market," I said. "A whole market. But where are the buyers? Where are the stalls?"

"Below us, maybe. Or—" Casper stopped. His eyes had fixed on something past my shoulder, and when I turned, I understood why.

Set into the wall beside our hiding place, half-hidden by a hanging curtain of oiled canvas, was a counter. A proper shopkeeper's counter, worn smooth at the edges by years of leaning elbows, with a bell-pull above it and a slate board hung behind. On the slate, written in a clerk's tidy hand, was a list:

*THIS WEEK'S ARRIVALS —
First kisses (assorted), 40 lots.
Sea-voyage, complete, one owner, excellent condition.
Mother's voice, singing (partial).
Summer of the plague year, expurgated.
Childhood, ages four through nine — see specialist.*

*NO REFUNDS ON OPENED MEMORIES.*

I read the list twice. The second reading was worse than the first, because on the second reading I stopped seeing categories and started seeing people. *One owner.* A sea voyage belonged to someone; it had been lived inside a particular skull on a particular deck under a particular sky, and now it hung here in a jar between a mother's singing voice and a summer someone had survived.

"Odile." Casper's hand found my sleeve. "Odile, your face. What is it?"

I didn't know yet. That was the terrible part — some instinct older than thought had gone rigid inside me, the way a dog goes rigid before the ground tells it why. I stepped out from behind the curtain, and Casper hissed a warning I ignored, and I walked to the nearest rack and took down the brass plaque that labeled its row.

*S. MONTROSE & ASSOCIATES, LEDGER-HOUSE, CORVANE ROW.*

My own name. My own ledger-house — the small counting office I had signed my debt-bond to serve, the office whose books I balanced every evening by lamplight, believing myself to be the most honest woman on Corvane Row.

The rack behind the plaque held perhaps two hundred vials. Each was tagged with a paper label in that same tidy clerk's hand, and the tags bore dates, and the dates spanned nineteen years. Nineteen years ago I had been six years old.

"Casper," I said. My voice sounded like it was coming up from underwater. "Bring the lantern."

He brought it. He held it high, and the light fell across the rows of little glowing vessels, and I began to read the tags aloud because if I did not say them they would stay inside me and crack me open from within.

"Winter, age seven. The pond on Gildersleeve Lane, frozen. First skate." A vial the color of morning ice. "Age eight. Mother teaching the cipher alphabet. Kitchen table. Bread burning." Amber-gold, that one, warm even through the glass. "Age nine—" I stopped.

Age nine was the year my brother Tomas fell ill. Age nine was the winter of the fever carts and the quarantined houses, the year I learned that love could be weighed against survival and that the scales did not care which side trembled.

There were eleven vials dated from my ninth year alone.

My hand went to them the way a hand goes to a wound. I lifted one — the fourth, tagged *Fever house, window seat, watching for the doctor — offered voluntarily* — and even through the stoppered glass I felt it answer me. Not warmth exactly. Recognition. Something in the vial knew me, leaned toward me the way a candle leans toward a draft, and the color inside it swam and gathered and for one vertiginous instant I was standing again in that cold room with the smell of vinegar and boiled linen, watching the road through fogged glass, waiting for a man on a cart to tell me whether my brother would live.

I put it back. My fingers would not entirely obey; they wanted to hold on.

"Odile." Casper's voice was very gentle now, the gentleness you use near broken glass. "These are yours."

"Some of them." My throat was raw. "Not all. Some of these are—" I scanned the neighboring tags and felt the floor tilt. "Tomas. These are Tomas's. His skating lesson. His — Casper, this one says *mother*, it's tagged with her name, she sold—" No. She hadn't sold. My mother had died penniless with a debt-bond of her own, and the guild had collected, and collections of that kind were always described in the ledgers as *voluntary settlement*. I had entered those words myself, once, in another life, in another column, believing them.

"The fever year," I said. "The guild bought my ninth year while my brother was dying. While I sat in that window. They took the worst year of my life and they *priced* it." The laugh that came out of me was an ugly thing, and I was glad there was no one to hear it. "And do you know what the worst part is? Do you know why they took eleven vials from that year instead of one? Because suffering keeps. Grief holds its color. A happy memory fades on the shelf — but heartbreak, heartbreak is *stable*. Heartbreak is an investment."

Casper had gone pale in the lantern light. "Your brother," he said carefully. "Is he—"

"Alive. In Corvane Row. He thinks our childhood was mostly happy." I stared at the rack, at the hundreds of small colored lights, at the museum of a girl I had been. "He thinks that because the parts that weren't are sitting in a cellar wearing price tags. When I get home from balancing the guild's books, when I sit with him at supper, I am sitting across from a man whose past has been *edited*. And I never noticed. Nine years, Casper. I noticed a misprinted folio number at forty paces, and I never noticed my own history had been thinned out like broth."

I reached for the vial again — *fever house, window seat* — and this time I made my fingers stop an inch short of the glass.

Because I remembered what the courier had said. I remembered the map pressed into my bleeding hands, and the covenant, and the word that ran through all of it like a vein of ore: *return*. What was stolen must be returned. And I understood, standing in that humming golden dark with my childhood racked and labeled before me, what returning would mean. Not rescue. Not reunion. If the covenant was reforged, these vials would go back — back into the heads that had housed them first, back into the city's mind, back into mine. Every grief I had been spared. Every night I had slept through because the worst of it had been decanted and sold. I would have to drink down my ninth year whole, unexpurgated, with interest.

And I wanted it. God help me, some starved thing deep under my ribs *wanted* it, the way the Hollow's fading people must want their names. That was the violation underneath all the others: they had not only taken my past. They had made me grateful for the theft.

"We should take yours," Casper said. "Tonight. Now. While—"

"No." I said it more sharply than I meant to. "Look at the seals, Casper. Guild wax, guild countersigns — every vial logged, every removal recorded. We take one and the shortfall shows by morning. You don't steal from a ledger-house by taking. You steal by *balancing*." I was surprised to hear my own voice steady, surprised to find the accountant still alive in me somewhere beneath the shaking girl with her hand an inch from the glass. "When the time comes — and it's coming — we take everything, openly, or we take nothing. That's the only theft they'll never see. The honest kind."

Casper looked at me for a long moment. "You sound like your ledgers," he said at last, and there was something like awe in it.

"I *am* my ledgers." I straightened my gloves. "It's the only weapon they ever gave me. Come on. If they catalogue by district, they catalogue by debt-bond, and I need to know how far the inventory goes. I need to see whether there's a rack in here with the Hollow's name on it — whether they've been shelving a whole world."

We moved deeper into the stacks, past *Ashwell*, past *Sallow Street*, past districts I had only ever seen as column headings, and the aisles went on and on, and the sweet rotting-honey smell thickened until I could taste it on my teeth. Somewhere ahead, beyond the shelves, I began to hear it: a murmur of many voices, the chime of glass, the cadence of commerce. The actual market, awake below us, buying and selling the interior weather of an entire city.

Then Casper caught my arm, hard, and pulled me down behind a shelf end, and pointed.

At the aisle's far terminus, apart from the city racks, stood a single case under glass — a curator's case, locked and lit with its own cold lamp. And inside it, arranged on velvet like crown jewels, lay a row of vials that glowed no color at all. They shone silver. Silver like moonrise on water, silver like the map's blood-ink turned to light, silver like something that had never belonged to the surface world at all.

The plaque beneath the case bore no district name. It bore a single word, in the old script, in the guild's oldest hand:

*HOLLOW — RESERVED.*

And beneath it, smaller, in fresh ink that had not yet dried:

*Final lots. Auction upon confirmation of covenant collapse.*

Casper made a sound low in his throat, half grief and half fury, and I gripped his wrist to hold him still — because down the far aisle, drawn by that same sound or by some sixth sense of ownership, a lantern was swinging toward us, and behind the lantern, unmistakable even at fifty paces by the stoop of his shoulders and the jingle of master-keys at his belt, walked a figure I knew.

Nine years I had signed my name beneath his.

The Master Scribe had come to count his inventory.
