## 11. A Name I Sold

The vault did not look like a place where childhoods were kept.

It looked like a chapel, if a chapel had been designed by accountants. Row upon row of glass-fronted cabinets rose toward a ceiling lost in shadow, and in each cabinet, in each neat wooden cradle lined with grey velvet, lay vials — thousands upon thousands of them, slender as swallows' eggs, glowing with the faint amber light of other people's remembering. Some shone bright as festival lanterns. Some had dimmed to the color of old honey, or worse, to the pale grey of watered milk.

Odile stood at the entrance with Casper's lamp cupped behind her shoulder and felt her breath go strange in her chest.

"They're *labeled*," she whispered.

They were. Little paper tags, tied to each vial's neck with thread, written in the tidy copperplate hand she knew better than her own — the Scribes' Guild hand, the hand she had practiced for six years until her letters matched the masters' exactly. She leaned close to the nearest cabinet and read:

*First snowfall, age four. Seller: unnamed. Grade: gentle.*

*Wedding day, whole. Seller: unnamed. Grade: radiant.*

*A mother's voice singing, fragmentary. Seller: unnamed. Grade: tender. Reserved — Guildmaster's table.*

Reserved. As though a mother's voice were a wine.

"Odile." Casper's voice came low from behind her. He had been quiet since they slipped past the sleeping doorwarden, quieter than his usual quiet, and when she glanced back at him she saw that he was looking at the vials the way he looked at his father's route-maps — like a man reading a language of loss he'd grown fluent in without wanting to. "How many do you think?"

She did the arithmetic before she could stop herself. It was what she was for; numbers arrived in her head unbidden, arranged themselves into columns, demanded balancing. Cabinets visible: perhaps sixty. Vials per cabinet: perhaps two hundred. And this was only one vault of however many the guild kept beneath its honest counting-houses above.

"Twelve thousand," she said. "At least."

"Twelve thousand *whats*?"

"Memories. Moments. People's—" She stopped. Her throat had closed around the word *lives*, and she would not say it here, in this hushed and reverent theft, where saying it aloud might feel like prayer instead of accusation.

Casper touched one cabinet door, very lightly, the way you touch something asleep. The glass was cold. "My father said the guild paid well for certain kinds," he murmured. "First memories. Last words. He called them the premium grades." His jaw worked. "I thought he meant spices."

* * *

They moved down the aisles because moving was easier than standing still. The vault had its own weather — a cool stillness, a hush so complete that Odile could hear the faint chime of the vials themselves, a sound below sound, like bells heard through deep water. Each memory hummed at its own pitch. Bright ones sang. Dim ones sighed. And some, she noticed as they went deeper, made no sound at all, and those frightened her most: vials gone clear as tap water, empty of everything but the shape of having once held something.

Sold out completely. Used up. Somewhere on the surface, some person was walking around with a hole where a wedding had been, or a funeral, or a face, and paying rent with the interest.

"The ledger said the Hollow survives on what we sell it," she said, mostly to keep her own courage fed. "But these—these were never bound for the Hollow. These are surface stock. These are for us. For banquets and boardrooms and—" She stopped at a tag that read *A daughter's forgiveness, sought and granted* and found she could not finish the sentence.

Casper caught her elbow. Not gently — urgently. "Odile. Look at the aisle markers."

She looked. Brass plaques above each row, lettered in the same copperplate. The aisles were organized alphabetically. By surname.

Her stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

"M—" she read, walking faster now, past *Lachance*, past *Marchand*, past families whose names she knew from tax rolls and debt schedules, all of them sellers, all of them unnamed on their tags because the guild preferred its merchandise anonymous, "Merrow, Montclair—"

Montrose.

The aisle was long. Her footsteps echoed off the glass like someone following her.

There were eleven vials under her family name.

Eleven. She counted twice, because counting was how she held on to things, and both times the answer was the same, and both times it was unbearable. Eleven moments of her life, taken out of her like teeth, sitting here in velvet cradles under paper tags in a dead man's handwriting.

She lifted the first one. *A kitchen in winter, bread rising.* She remembered no such kitchen — which was itself an answer, wasn't it, a hole shaped like warmth. The second: *Rain on a schoolroom window.* Nothing. The third—

The third vial was brighter than the others. It threw a small gold circle on the shelf around it, and the light trembled, alive, urgent, like something knocking to be let out. The tag read:

*Debt-bond signing, age nineteen. Seller: Montrose, O. Grade: pivotal. Note — retained for audit.*

Retained for audit. Kept so that the guild could check its work. So that some master scribe could revisit, whenever the ledgers needed reconciling, the exact expression on her face the day she signed away six years of her life.

Her hands were shaking. She watched them shake with a strange detachment, as though they belonged to a clerk she was supervising. Behind her, Casper said something — her name, probably — but it reached her from very far away, because the vial was warm now against her palm, warm as a living thing, and there is a kind of memory that does not wait politely to be remembered.

She pulled the stopper.

* * *

Bread. First — absurdly, mercifully — bread. A kitchen in winter, steam on the windows, flour to her elbows, and her mother humming off-key in the way that made Tam laugh, and the light coming down gold and slow through frost-feathered glass, and everything so ordinary and so entire that adult-Odile, watching from inside her own nine-year-old body, wanted to scream *this, this is what you sold, you fools, you absolute thieves, THIS—*

Then the memory turned.

Because it was the signing, of course. The audit-grade moment. The kitchen fell away like a page turning, and she was nineteen again, standing in the Master Scribe's study with rain hammering the windows and her hands folded the way she'd been taught to fold them, and Tam —

Tam was fourteen and coughing blood into a handkerchief he hid in his sleeve because he didn't want her to see, and the physicians' bill sat on the desk between her and the Master Scribe like a third person in the room. Above the city the guild lamps burned; below the city, though she did not yet know it, a realm was starving on sold rememberings. She knew only what the Master Scribe told her, in his kind dry voice, tapping the contract: that the debt-bond carried terms generous beyond custom, room and board and a wage, six years of service in exchange for which the guild would settle her brother's medical accounts in full.

"And the alternative?" her younger self asked, in a voice she barely recognized — steady, always steady, steady as a ruled line.

"The alternative," the Master Scribe said, "is that love remains free, and your brother dies of it."

In the memory she signed. In the memory she watched herself sign, pen scratching, ink beading on the cheap paper, and the Master Scribe sliding across the desk — almost as an afterthought, almost kindly — one additional document, smaller, a rider clause she never read closely afterward because reading it hurt too much:

*Seller further cedes, as surety, one (1) memory of seller's choosing, to be drawn at Guild discretion should default occur.*

Her mother's humming. Her mother's off-key, terrible, beloved humming, in a warm kitchen in winter — that was the memory the guild had drawn when they decided she needed softening, or testing, or simply auditing. That was why no kitchen lived in her head anymore. She had traded her mother's voice to save her brother's lungs, and she had done it in a single signature, half-asleep with grief and fear, and then she had spent six years calling it *necessity* and *duty* and *the sensible thing*, dressing it in ledger-language because ledger-language didn't bleed.

The memory ended. The vault returned.

Odile was on her knees on the stone floor. She didn't remember falling. The empty vial rolled from her fingers and spun, wobbled, settled against Casper's boot.

"Hey." He crouched beside her, and his hand hovered near her shoulder without landing, the way you hover near a bird with a broken wing — wanting to help, afraid of making it worse. "What did you see? What was in it?"

"My mother," she said. Her voice came out sanded flat. "I sold my mother."

"You what?"

"Not — not all of her. A piece. A morning. She was baking bread and she was singing and I—" The sentence broke apart in her hands. She gathered it again, because sentences were also things she balanced, things she kept level when everything else tipped. "When Tam got sick. There was no money. There was nothing. The guild offered to pay if I bonded myself, and I signed, and buried in the contract was a clause letting them take one memory as surety, and they took her. They took the best morning we ever had, and I let them, and then I worked for them for six years and balanced their books and never once asked what was in the vaults, because I think—" her breath hitched, ugly and honest, "I think some part of me knew. I think some part of me knew exactly what we were selling down here, and I didn't want the number in my head. Because once a number is in your head, you have to carry it."

The vault hummed around them, twelve thousand small voices through water.

Casper lowered himself fully to the floor beside her, cross-legged, shoulder nearly touching hers. When he spoke, his voice was rough. "My father took courier work to feed us. Same math." He turned the map-case over in his hands — the wax-sealed original, the covenant, the thing everyone bleeding in this story was chasing. "He knew what the guild was. Knew better than anyone. And he kept carrying their packages anyway, right up until the package was this, and carrying it killed him." He laughed, short and joyless. "I've hated him a little for that. For being brave too late. For leaving me the routes and not the reasons."

"I don't hate my mother for dying," Odile said, and then corrected herself, quietly: "I mean — I don't hate myself. Or. I do. That's the problem. I've hated myself for six years in a very orderly fashion. Line by line. Interest accruing." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, furious at them. "Do you know what I did, the first week in the scriptorium? I told myself it was arithmetic. One brother, alive, against one morning, forgotten. Any auditor would call that a favorable exchange. Any *sane* clerk."

"Was it?"

She looked at him. His face was thin and stubborn and young in the lamplight, and there was flour-dust — no, chalk, from the tunnel walls — in his hair, and he waited for her answer the way he waited for everything: like a boy who had learned that people lie to fill silences, and who would rather have the silence.

"No," she said. "No, it wasn't. Because a morning isn't a unit. You can't net it against anything. My mother's voice isn't a debit. It was — it was the whole ledger, Casper. It was the book itself." Her hands closed on her knees. "And I sold the book to save one page."

The words hung there. And then — slowly, like light reaching the bottom of a well — something shifted in her chest, some load-bearing grief giving way to something that was not yet peace but was at least a foundation.

"But," she said, and her voice steadied, found its footing, "he lived. Tam lived. He's up there right now, probably annoying his landlady, alive, because his sister made a monstrous bargain in good faith with monsters." She breathed. "And I am done pretending the bargain was clean. But I am also done pretending I made it out of wickedness. I made it out of love, badly. Out of love with bad accounting." A sound escaped her that was half laugh, half sob. "That's going in the ledger. *Love, bad accounting of.*"

Casper was smiling — a real one, crooked and startled, the first she'd seen from him. "You're crying and doing bookkeeping."

"It's how I pray."

He picked up the empty vial, weighed it in his palm, and set it upright on the shelf among its full siblings — a hollow tooth in a mouth of gold. Then he nodded down the aisle. "There are ten more of yours here. Do you want them?"

She considered it. Truly considered it — the temptation was enormous, ten mornings of restoration, ten pieces of herself re-shelved into her skull. And then she looked at the dimming vials further down the row, the grey ones, the silent ones, belonging to sellers who had no idea their pasts were sitting underground waiting for rescue, and she understood with sudden total clarity what the covenant must ask of whoever reforged it.

"Not yet," she said. "Not while everyone else's are still locked up. We take proof. We take the tags, the grades, the Master Scribe's own handwriting — enough to show the regent what's been stolen, and how. And then—" she stood, and her legs held, and she was surprised by how much she sounded like herself, a new version of herself, one with the first crack of self-forgiveness running through her like gold through broken porcelain, "—and then we come back for all of them. Every vial. Every name. Including mine."

"Including yours," Casper agreed, and pocketed three of the paper tags like a man collecting evidence, which was exactly what he was doing.

They turned back toward the aisle's end, toward the dark, toward the Well Road and whatever waited on it. Odile took one step, two — and then stopped, because far off in the vault, deeper in than they had dared go, a bell rang.

Not a chime of memory. A real bell. Brass, hard, human. An alarm.

Somewhere behind them, doors were opening that were supposed to stay shut, and boots were finding the stair.

"Casper," she whispered. "They know we're here."
