## 13. The Regent's Last Memory

The regent's palace had once been a cathedral of light. Now it was a cathedral of dusk.

We climbed toward it through streets that remembered being grander — avenues of pale stone where the lamps still burned but burned thin, their flames the color of weak tea. The Hollow's people watched us pass from doorways and windows, and I tried not to flinch at what I saw in them. A woman whose left side was slightly translucent, so that the lamplight passed through her shoulder like water through glass. A man who paused mid-stride, brow furrowing, as though he'd forgotten which direction he was walking — or why he was walking at all. A child chasing something invisible, laughing, while her mother stood behind her with an expression I recognized from my own mirror: love stretched over terror.

"They're worse than last week," Casper murmured beside me. His arm was bound tight against his ribs where the hunters' blades had found him on the stair, and every few steps he pressed his palm flat to the bandage as if holding himself together by force of will. "My father's maps used to mark the fading districts. Red ink. He called them *the bleeding edges*. I thought he meant floods."

"He meant this." My voice came out flat. I had run out of room for anything else.

The palace doors were taller than our whole scriptorium on the surface — black wood banded with silver that had gone grey as ash — and they stood open, because there was nothing left inside worth guarding except a dying man.

* * *

The throne room smelled of lilies and dust and something older underneath both, something like the air in the memory markets: forgetting made breathable. Light fell through windows of colored glass, but the colors had drained from the panes themselves, so that what should have been ruby and sapphire came through as shades of grey, painting the floor in a monochrome mosaic of saints I could not name and neither, I suspected, could anyone else anymore.

The Regent sat on a dais beneath a canopy that had once been cloth-of-gold. He was small now. That was the first cruelty of him — I had imagined a king, vast and terrible, and instead there was this: a thin man in robes too large for his diminishing body, his hands folded in his lap like folded paper, his eyes open and fixed on some middle distance where perhaps the Hollow still existed in its fullness.

He was fading faster than his people. It radiated off him. The edges of his silhouette shimmered faintly, the way heat shimmers above summer stone, and when Casper knelt — wincing, dragging me down beside him — the Regent looked at us with the polite blankness of a man greeting strangers at his own funeral.

"Visitors," he said. His voice was soft and pleasant and terribly wrong, like a bell with a crack running through it. "How kind. How... kind."

"Your Grace," I began, and stopped, because I didn't know the forms. On the surface we had protocols for everything — how to address a guildmaster, how to seal a letter, how to apologize for an error in a ledger. No one had ever taught me the etiquette of dying kings.

Casper knew better. He drew the map from the oilcloth wrapping inside his coat and held it out across his knees, and I saw the moment the Regent's gaze touched it — saw something move behind those faded eyes like a fish turning under ice.

"Where," the Regent said slowly, "did you get that?"

"It was my father's," Casper said. "Tomas Vale. He carried for the covenant. They killed him for it."

For a long moment the Regent said nothing. Then, with the terrible slowness of a man whose body has become a foreign country, he leaned forward and touched the map's edge with one fingertip.

"Tomas," he said. "Tomas Vale. Yes." And then, quieter, with wonder: "I remember Tomas. Isn't that strange. I remember almost nothing, and I remember Tomas."

I felt Casper go rigid beside me. Grief does strange things to a person's spine; his grief straightened him like a blade being drawn.

"You remember him," Casper said carefully, "but you don't remember your own covenant."

"I remember what I paid to keep," the Regent said simply. "That is the trick of it, boy. We are all of us ledgers here. Whatever you hold dearest, that is the first thing they take — no. Forgive me." His hand fluttered. "The first thing we *sell*. They only take what we refuse. Your father refused everything. So they took..." He trailed off, frowning, searching. "They took his—"

"His life," Casper said. His voice did not shake. I admired that more than I can say.

"Yes," the Regent agreed gently. "Yes, that would be the final payment, wouldn't it."

* * *

I had come expecting answers, and I suppose I got them, though not the way I'd imagined. I had pictured a king rising from his throne, seizing the map, declaring terms, marshaling his people. Instead the Regent asked us to help him stand, and then asked us to walk with him in his garden, because the garden was where he kept the last of himself.

The garden lay beyond the throne room through doors of fretted silver, and it broke my heart in a way the fading streets had not. Someone had loved this place once — terraces of night-blooming flowers, a fountain shaped like a sleeping giant, trees whose leaves glowed faintly with stored daylight, a trick of old Hollow craft I'd read about in guild archives and never believed. But half the beds stood empty, not dead but *absent*, the soil smooth and featureless as if nothing had ever grown there. The fountain ran dry. The glowing leaves guttered one by one as we walked beneath them, going out like candles after a vigil.

The Regent moved along the paths with his hand trailing the low hedges, and wherever his fingers passed, the leaves brightened briefly — a little borrowed warmth, a little remembered green — and then dimmed again behind him.

"My wife planted the white ones," he said, gesturing with vague pride at a bed of pale blossoms. "Or perhaps I dreamed that. There was a woman. There was certainly a woman. She sang while she worked." He turned to me, and his eyes — I will write this plainly, because I owe the truth to whoever reads this — his eyes were the color of watered milk, and looking into them was like looking into a well from which someone had drawn up nearly all the water. "Do you know her name? You seem like a girl who knows things. Ledgers. You have ledger-eyes. Do you know her name?"

"No, Your Grace. I'm sorry."

"Mm." He nodded, unsurprised, unbroken. "Neither do I. I know that I loved her. The shape of loving remains even when the face goes. That is the cruelest economy of all — they leave you the debt and take away the reason for it."

Beside me, Casper made a small sound. When I glanced at him his jaw was set hard, and I understood: the Regent was describing Casper's own inheritance, the shape of a dead father's love with the man himself gone out of it.

"The map," I said, as gently as I could, which was not very. Gentleness is not among my talents; precision is. "Your Grace, the covenant is breaking. Your people are fading. We've brought the original map — the blood-ink map. We think it holds the terms. But we need to understand them before the guild hunts us down, and they are hunting us, and they are close."

The Regent lowered himself onto a stone bench beside the dry fountain, moving like a marionette whose strings had mostly been cut, and patted the space beside him. We sat. Above us the last glowing tree shed its light in slow, dying pulses.

"Blood-ink," he said. "Yes. I signed it in blood-ink, a hundred years ago. Or my grandfather did. Or—" He laughed, a small cracked sound. "You see? Even that. Even *who I am*. They took the top layer first. Names, dates, faces. Then they took the reasons. What's left is..." He spread his hands, translucent at the edges. "What's left is obligation. Duty without memory. A debt whose creditor I cannot recall." He fixed me with sudden, startling clarity. "But you said terms. Ask your question, ledger-girl. While I am still here to answer. Some hours are better than others. This hour is good. This hour I am almost entirely myself."

So I asked. I told him everything — the ledgers on the surface, the entries paid in memories, the courier dying in the scriptorium with the map pressed into my hands, the Master Scribe's calm face as he called my debt-bond due, the markets below where childhoods hung in vials like preserved fruit, my own among them. I told it quickly and precisely, because precision was all I had to offer a dying king, and when I finished, the Regent was silent for a long time, watching the fountain that did not flow.

"The Well of Names," he said at last.

"What?"

"That's where it must be sealed again. The covenant. If the map shows you the way — and it will, blood-ink never loses its way — then the sealing happens at the Well of Names. Every covenant between the realms has been sealed there since before either realm had names to give." He smiled faintly. "Which is rather the point."

"Tell me," I said. "All of it. Exactly."

And he did, in the measured voice of a man reciting something carved so deep in him that even the guild's knives couldn't reach it — the one thing, perhaps the only thing, they had never managed to sell, because it wasn't his to sell. It belonged to the covenant itself.

"The covenant is a promise made of payment," he said. "Not words. Payment. Both realms must give willingly, both realms must receive willingly, and the giving must happen at the Well, where names are minted. A name freely surrendered binds tighter than any oath. That is the old law. The surface gave its dreams; we gave our depth. Each year the exchange renewed itself, and each year both worlds grew richer for the honesty of it." His hands tightened on his knees. "Then someone on the surface learned a shortcut. Why wait for willing gifts? Take. Bottle. Sell. The covenant didn't break all at once, girl. It broke the way a bridge breaks — quietly, joint by joint, while everyone kept walking across and calling it sound."

"And to reforge it?" I asked, though some part of me already dreaded the answer. "To seal it again. What's required?"

"A debtor," the Regent said. "Someone who owes nothing."

The words went into me cold, like a needle finding the space between ribs.

"I don't understand."

"Everyone owes something," the Regent said. "That is the condition of being alive. Debts of love, debts of guilt, debts of breath — every soul arrives owing and departs owed-to. But the Well requires a seal-bearer with a clean slate. Not innocent — innocence isn't the same as owing nothing. It requires someone whose accounts balance perfectly. Who has taken exactly what they've given. Who stands at zero." He looked at me with that awful gentle clarity. "Can you say that of yourself, ledger-girl? Can any of us?"

I thought of my brother, alive because I had sold our mother's memory of me. I thought of my debt-bond, twenty years of servitude inked into guild parchment. I thought of Casper's father, dead on a stair somewhere in the dark, and the son sitting beside me carrying that death like a second wound. I thought of the courier whose blood had dried on my scriptorium floor because I had been too slow, too careful, too afraid to open the door sooner.

"No," I said.

"And you, boy? Tomas's son?"

Casper was quiet. When he spoke, his voice was rough. "I owe my father a finished errand. I owe these people every road my family ever walked for them. I owe—" He stopped. Shook his head. "No."

"There," the Regent said softly. "There it is. Hope curdling into impossibility. I know the taste. I drink it every morning."

* * *

The light in the garden was failing. Whether that was evening coming or the garden itself finishing its long surrender, I couldn't tell, and I suspect the distinction no longer mattered down here.

"Wait," I said, because I am Odile Montrose and I do not accept impossibilities on the first reading. "There must be a way to zero an account. Debts can be paid. Settled. Forgiven. If the Well needs someone who owes nothing, then we make someone who owes nothing. Tell me how debts cancel."

"Some cancel," the Regent allowed. "Debt against debt, gift against gift. But the great ones — guilt, grief, love — those aren't coin, girl. Those are weight. You cannot pay a weight. You can only carry it or put it down, and putting it down—" his translucent fingers brushed the dry rim of the fountain, "—is what the guild taught everyone to do. Put it down. Forget. Look how beautifully that worked."

"Then who?" My voice cracked on it, which I hated. "Who in either realm could possibly owe nothing?"

The Regent gazed at the darkening sky above the garden, where stars were appearing — real stars, or Hollow echoes of stars, I couldn't say — and for a moment his fading face looked almost amused.

"Once," he said, "it might have been anyone. Once, before the selling began, most souls hovered near balance — a kindness here, a theft there, roughly even. But a century of the guild's economy has seen to it that everyone owes. The sellers owe for what they stole. The buyers owe for what they bought. The forgotten owe their rescuers. The rescued owe their dead." He sighed, a sound like wind through empty rooms. "No. Whoever seals the covenant now must be someone the world has never entered into its books at all. Someone with no entries. No credits. No debits. No—"

He stopped.

I watched the thought arrive in him like a lamp being lit in a far window of a dark house — watched hope rise in his ruined face and curdle, even as it rose, into something else. Something like horror. Something like pity.

"Girl," he said. "Ledger-girl. What is your name?"

"Odile," I said. "Odile Montrose."

"Odile," the Regent repeated, tasting it. And then, gently, in the tone one uses to deliver a terminal diagnosis: "Are you certain?"

The garden seemed to lean in. The last glowing leaf overhead pulsed once, twice, and went out.

"What do you mean, am I certain?"

"In all your guild records," he said, "in all your ledgers and bonds and filings — has anyone ever written your name correctly? Has anyone ever written it at all? Or only your debts? Only the bond, the interest, the penalties, the account number?" His milk-pale eyes held mine. "Because I begin to wonder, child. I begin to wonder whether the world has ever owned enough of you to owe you anything back. Whether there is anything of Odile Montrose left outside the guild's columns at all — or whether you have spent so long balancing other people's books that your own page stands..."

He didn't finish. He didn't need to.

*Empty*, I finished, in the silence of my own skull. *Blank. Zero.*

Somewhere far above us, muffled by a hundred feet of stone, came a sound I had learned to dread on the surface and had hoped never to hear again below: boots. Many boots. And beneath them, thinner and colder, the particular ringing hum of guild-forged steel waking up.

Casper was on his feet instantly, hand pressed to his wounded side, eyes finding mine in the dark. "They've found the palace."

The Regent reached up with a fading hand and closed my fingers around the map.

"Well of Names," he whispered. "Run, girl who owes nothing. Run, and pray you never learn what that costs."
