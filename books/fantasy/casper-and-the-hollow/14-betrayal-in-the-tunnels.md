## 14. Betrayal in the Tunnels

The guide's name was Wrennel, and Casper should have known from the first hour that a man who smiled that easily in the dark was lying about something.

They met him on the third morning below, at the edge of a district the Hollow folk called the Whispermarket, where stalls sold bottled birdsong and secondhand shadows and no one made eye contact for longer than a breath. Wrennel had appeared out of an alley mouth like smoke finding a draft — a narrow man, gray at the temples, with hands stained to the knuckles in lamp-black and a voice soft as silt settling. He said he'd known routes through the Undervaults since before the guild's hunters learned to read maps. He said he'd carried messages himself, once, in the old days.

He looked at the blood-ink map slung against Casper's chest exactly once, and then never again, and that too should have told Casper something. Honest men look twice.

*You don't get to judge him,* Casper reminded himself, trudging behind the guide's lantern through a corridor whose walls sweated a faint phosphorescent moss. *You followed a dead man's seal into the dark. You'd sell your own shadow for one more hour of your father's voice.* But there was a difference, he thought, between wanting a thing and selling someone else to get it, and the difference was the whole width of the world, and he kept the thought like a stone in his boot: small, constant, grounding.

Odile walked ahead of him, close enough to touch. She hadn't slept more than an hour at a stretch since the markets, since she'd found the vial labeled with her own childhood and drunk down the shape of what she'd given away. She was quieter now, but it wasn't emptiness; it was the quiet of a ledger being balanced line by line, and when she glanced back at him her eyes had that ledger-light in them, arithmetic and resolve. She still carried the map. She always carried the map. They had agreed on that after the hunters came: if they were separated, the map went with her, because she could read the covenant's terms and he couldn't, and because — though neither of them said it aloud — she was the one who owed nothing except everything, and he was the one who would fight.

The tunnels changed as they went deeper. The air thickened until breathing felt like drinking; the moss-light gave way to veins of true luminescence, blue-white as frost on a window, running along the ceiling like rivers seen from underneath. Once they crossed a bridge over a chasm where names drifted up out of the dark — actual syllables, audible, half-formed, the exhalations of the Well Road far below — and Wrennel stopped and took off his cap and stood with his head bowed for a long moment, listening.

"My daughter's name comes up here sometimes," he said. "Mirelle. She faded two winters past. Not gone. Just... thinned. I could walk past her in the street and she'd see straight through me, polite as fog."

"I'm sorry," Odile said, and Casper believed she meant it.

Wrennel put his cap back on. "The regent will see you," he said. "What's left of him. And after that—" he gestured downward, toward the drifting syllables "—the Well Road runs another league to the Well itself. If you mean to do what I think you mean to do."

"We mean to reforge the covenant," Odile said. "Return what was taken."

"Ah." Wrennel smiled, and his teeth were very white in the frost-light. "Then you'll want the eastern stair. The direct road is watched. Guild hunters have been crawling through the upper tunnels like lice in a seam since the vaults broke. But there's a way — an old courier's way — my father used it, my grandfather before him. It lets out a hundred paces from the Well."

Casper's father had taught him the first rule of route-reading before he'd taught him letters: *a shortcut offered freely costs more than the long road bought honestly.* He opened his mouth to say so, and Odile caught his eye, and whatever she saw in his face made her say instead, smoothly, "We're grateful. Lead on."

Later — much later, in the bad hours afterward — Casper would take that moment apart a hundred times and find it unchanged every time: the moment when he'd swallowed his instinct out of trust in her judgment, or exhaustion, or the simple animal need to believe that a grieving father wanted the same thing they did. The moment before the trap closed.

* *

The eastern stair was real. That was the cruelest part. It existed — a corkscrew descent cut into living rock, its steps worn hollow by generations of feet, its walls hung with courier-marks burned into the stone: the double-chevron of the sworn couriers, the circle-and-line of the covenant's old guard. Casper ran his thumb over one as they passed, the same mark his father had inked into the inside of his wrist, and grief rose in him like water into a well-shaft, steady and cold.

"Your father was sworn," Wrennel observed, watching him.

"He was the last one," Casper said. "They killed him for the map."

"They killed a lot of people for the map." Wrennel's voice didn't change, didn't sharpen, and that flatness lodged under Casper's ribs like a splinter. "Strange, isn't it. One scrap of skin-paper. Whole graves full of people who died because of what it knew."

"It's not the paper," Odile said quietly, not turning around. "It's the terms. The covenant can't be broken while the original exists. Everything the guild has built for thirty years stands on that page staying lost." She paused on a step. "So they killed everyone who might remember it."

"And now two children come walking down out of the light carrying it," Wrennel said, "like lambs carrying their own slaughter. Bold. Or foolish."

"Both," Casper said. "Usually both."

Wrennel laughed — a real laugh, short and surprised, and for a moment Casper almost liked him. That was the second cruelty: that the liking was probably real, all the way up until the moment it wasn't.

They camped that night in a side chamber off the stair, a courier's rest with soot-blackened walls and a hearth that still drew, some deep flue pulling air from who-knew-where. Wrennel produced dried fish and a twist of salt and a bottle of something amber that tasted of iron and honey. They ate. Odile slept first, curled against the wall with the map strapped beneath her coat, her hand resting on the strap even in sleep, and Casper sat up to keep the watch because he didn't trust the dark down here and because watching Odile sleep was the closest thing to peace the Hollow had left to offer him.

"You love her," Wrennel said, from across the coals. Not a question.

"She saved my life on the stair. I'm saving hers down here. We're square."

"That's not what I asked."

Casper fed a twig to the fire. "No," he said. "It isn't. And it's none of yours."

Wrennel nodded slowly, as if Casper had answered anyway. Then he said, softly, "If you could buy back one memory — one — for someone you loved. Would you? Whatever it cost anyone else?"

The question hung in the smoky air. Casper thought of his father's face, which was going soft at the edges in his mind no matter how hard he held it; thought of the vials in the market, ranked and labeled like preserves; thought of Mirelle, thinned to fog, walking past her father without seeing him.

"Yes," he admitted. "That's the trap, isn't it? Everyone would say yes. That's how the guild got away with it for thirty years. They never asked anyone to be a monster. They just asked everyone to be a little bit selfish, one vial at a time, until the selfishness added up to a dying world."

Wrennel stared into the fire for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice was almost gentle. "You sound like your father."

"You knew him?"

"I knew *of* him. Everyone below knew of Aldous Vane." He rose, brushing ash from his knees. "Sleep. I'll take the middle watch. The last league is hard walking."

Casper slept. That was the third cruelty — that he trusted enough to sleep, and the sleep was dreamless and warm, and he woke to lamplight and cold iron.

* *

There were six of them.

Casper came awake to the pressure of a knee between his shoulder blades and the bite of a cord at his wrists and the specific, unforgettable stink of guild-hunter oil — tallow and silver nitrate, a smell designed, he'd always suspected, to be remembered. His father's route-map was torn from his shirt. Someone wrenched his head back by the hair and he got one clear image: lanterns, six of them, arranged around the chamber like a stage; hunters in gray lacquered leather, faceless behind smoked visors; and Wrennel standing apart, cap in hand, looking at the floor.

"Wrennel." Casper's voice cracked on the name. "Wrennel, look at me."

"Don't," the guide said.

"Look at me! You ate with us. You showed me the courier-mark on the stair. You bowed to the names in the chasm — your daughter's name, Wrennel—"

"MIRELLE IS THINNING!" The words tore out of him like something ripped, and the chamber went silent except for the guttering lamps. Wrennel's composure was gone, all of it, stripped back to a raw-faced ordinary man with wet eyes and shaking hands. "Two winters. Two winters she's been fog, and every year less, and the Scribes' Guild sits on ten thousand vials with her name on half of them, and you—" his voice dropped to a rasp "—you two were going to burn them all. Every vial. Return *everything*, you said. The stolen memories go back to the Hollow and the surface forgets its profits, and maybe Mirelle gets her years back and maybe she doesn't, and meanwhile the guild offered me — tonight — they offered me her restoration. Certain. Signed. Her whole self, poured back, starting tomorrow."

"They're lying," Odile said. She was awake — must have been awake since the first hunter's footfall — sitting with her hands bound in front of her and her chin level, and her voice was terrible in its calm. "They'll pour back a paste of borrowed memories and call it Mirelle. A girl who smiles at the right times. You know what restoration from a guild vault looks like, Wrennel. You've seen the restored ones in the market districts. They're *waxworks.*"

"They're *alive.*"

"They're inventory that walks!"

A hunter moved — a small motion, gauntleted hand lifting — and Casper twisted against the cords, shouting, and a boot found his ribs and the world went white at the edges. When it cleared, Odile was on her feet somehow, though he'd swear no one had helped her up, facing the senior hunter with the frost-light catching the sweat on her temple.

"The map," the hunter said. A woman, by the voice, bored as a clerk. "And the debt-bonded thief. The Master Scribe wants both. The boy is surplus."

"He's not surplus," Odile said. "He's the son of the last sworn courier, and if you touch him the covenant's remnant will know the guild murdered courier-blood a second time, and every fading soul in this realm will have a name to hate you by."

"Fading souls can't lift a fork, let alone a blade."

"No?" Odile tilted her head. "Then why send six of you?"

The silence stretched one heartbeat too long, and Casper understood, in the way you understand a route-map all at once, that Odile was doing what she always did: reading the terms, finding the flaw, buying seconds. Six hunters meant fear. Fear meant the Hollowfolk uprising in the market districts hadn't been put down as cleanly as the guild's criers claimed. Fear meant leverage.

"Take the map," the huntress said at last, tiredly. "Leave the boy trussed. If the Hollow wants him, the Hollow can dig him out."

Two hunters crossed to Odile. One reached for her coat, for the strap beneath it, for the blood-ink page that thirty years of corpses had failed to bury —

—and Odile moved.

Casper never fully pieced together what happened next; it happened in the grammar of violence, fast and fragmentary. She drove her bound fists up into the nearest hunter's visor. The man staggered; the other grabbed for her; she dropped, rolled, came up with a courier's knife — Wrennel's knife, snatched from his belt, gods knew when, gods knew how long she'd been palming the possibility — and slashed the strap holding the map and *ran*, down the eastern stair into the dark, bare feet slapping stone, the blood-ink scroll clutched to her chest, and the hunters boiled after her with a roar like water into a breach.

"CASPER!" she screamed, once, from the dark, his name cracking in half — and then the stair swallowed her, and the lamplight swung and shrank, and he was alone with Wrennel and two hunters who had stayed behind to hold the prisoner.

He fought. Of course he fought. He got a shoulder into the nearer one's sternum and heard something crack pleasantly and nearly made the stair himself before the butt of a halberd caught him across the temple and the floor came up generous and slow.

He surfaced once, half-conscious, cheek against cold stone, and Wrennel crouched beside him. Up close the guide's face was wrecked — grief and relief fighting openly across it, neither winning.

"They'll restore her," Wrennel whispered. It wasn't an excuse. It wasn't even an apology. It was a prayer repeated to a god who wasn't listening. "They signed. I watched them sign."

"They signed in *guild ink,*" Casper rasped. Blood filled his mouth; he swallowed it. "My father taught me the seals, Wrennel. Guild ink dissolves. It's written to dissolve."

He watched the words land. Watched them land and break something. Wrennel made a sound like a man taking a blade without armor, low and involuntary, and for a moment his hand hovered over Casper's bonds as if it might do anything at all.

Then the huntress's voice came down the stair, echoing: *"Prisoner secured?"*

And Wrennel closed his eyes, and stood, and walked away up the tunnel toward his waxwork daughter, and Casper lay in the dark with the taste of blood and the sound of his own name still hanging in the air where Odile had screamed it, and he laughed — actually laughed, a horrible bubbling sound — because she'd escaped, she'd gotten clean away, the map was safe, the covenant had a chance, and every single thing he'd done for three weeks below the city had ended here: trussed on a cold floor, alone, in the dark, worth exactly nothing to anyone.

*Except,* said a small stubborn voice in him, the one that sounded like his father, *she heard you scream. She'll come back. She's a Montrose. She balances everything.*

*She shouldn't come back,* he answered it. *If she's smart, she won't.*

But he already knew she wasn't smart that way. Neither of them was. It was, he thought, drifting down into the black, the only inheritance either of them had ever been glad of — and somewhere above, muffled by leagues of stone, he thought he heard the far-off crackle of fire, and the rising, ragged sound of a great many forgotten voices learning, all at once, to shout.
