## 15. The Price Offered

They brought me up through a door I had walked past ten thousand times without ever truly seeing it.

That was the first thing the guild taught its apprentices, though no one said it aloud: the city is full of doors that are not doors, and the highest art of a Scribe is knowing which ones to keep shut. The stair behind the archive wall — the one disguised as a seam in the masonry, the one Casper had bled to defend — ended in a wine cellar beneath the Scribes' Guild itself. Of course it did. All roads led home to them. All debts did too.

Two hunters flanked me with the particular courtesy of men who have been paid not to bruise the merchandise. My wrists were bound in front of me with ledger-cord, the pale braided string we used to tie off completed accounts, and there was something almost funny in that — almost — until I remembered what else that cord was used for, and then my hands would not stop shaking.

The cellar smelled of dust and vinegar and old ink. Casks lined the walls, each stenciled with a year and a district: *Ashmarket, 41st year. Lantern Row, 38th.* I had spent my whole life walking over neighborhoods like those, buying bread from bakers and ribbon from ribbon-sellers, never once suspecting that beneath their feet the guild kept vintages of *them* — of their weddings and their griefs and the smell of their mothers' kitchens — racked like wine and sold by the dram.

I had known for weeks now. Knowing again, here, in the dark among the casks, was different. Knowing is a thing you do with your mind. This was my body learning it, the way a body learns a fall.

The stairs ahead climbed into lamplight, and from above came a sound I knew better than my own heartbeat: the scratch of a pen.

He was writing while they brought me to him. Of course he was. Master Scribe Aldous Vane had held his post for thirty years, and in all that time, so far as anyone knew, he had never let an hour pass unrecorded.

* * *

His study sat at the top of the guildhouse, where the morning light came through windows of real glass — not the cloudy green panes the rest of us got — and fell across a desk so orderly it looked less like furniture than an argument. Every pen in its stand aligned. Every blotter square to the desk's edge. Behind him, shelves rose to the ceiling, filled not with books but with ledgers, thousands of them, spines labeled in his own small exact hand.

He did not look up when I entered. He finished his line. He blotted it. Only then did he raise his eyes, and I made myself meet them.

"Odile Montrose." He said my name the way he read figures — checking it against something. "You've cost this guild a great deal this season."

"I've cost you nothing," I said. "You were already spending what wasn't yours."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. It might have been approval. That was the worst part, how much of him I recognized. He had signed my apprenticeship papers. He had praised my columns once, years ago, in front of the whole scriptorium — *this girl balances like breathing* — and I had carried that praise like a coin against my heart for a decade.

"Sit," he said.

"There's no chair."

"There will be, if you sit on the floor, and you'll find the floor honest at least." He set down his pen. "Stand, then. You'll want your legs under you for what I'm about to say."

The hunters withdrew. The door closed. And Master Scribe Vane folded his hands and looked at me with something I had not expected and could not immediately name, until I realized it was *weariness*. Deep, geological weariness, like a man standing at the bottom of a well he had dug himself.

"Do you know why the covenant exists?" he asked.

"To bind the realms. To keep the Hollow alive."

"To keep *both* realms alive," he corrected softly. "You've been below, Montrose. You've seen what the Hollow is. A realm runs on belief the way a lamp runs on oil, and theirs ran out generations ago. When I was a young scribe — younger than you are now — I was sent down with the last delegation. I saw a city the size of ours going dark street by street. Children fading mid-laughter. Do you understand? Not dying. *Unhappening.*" His voice stayed level, but his hands tightened on each other. "The covenant gave us terms. Payment freely given, from both sides, renewed each year at the Well of Names. And the surface — do you know what the surface said, when the regents of this city were told the price?"

I said nothing. I could see the shape of the answer coming down the years toward me like a landslide.

"They laughed," Vane said. "They were asked to give up memories — willingly, honestly, enough of them to matter — and the merchant princes and the guild elders looked at their ledgers and their new grand houses and their daughters' dowries, all built on forty good years of borrowed prosperity, and they said no. They said, *let the dead below stay dead.* So the guild chose. We chose the only door left open. We took instead of asking. We have taken for thirty years, and every year the taking has been easier, because forgetting is the one trade where the customer never complains."

"You killed my father's generation of couriers," I said. "You killed Casper's father."

"We killed men who tried to hold the city to a promise the city had already broken," he said. "Yes. I will not dress it as anything else. Write it in your ledger however you like; I've written it in mine." He unfolded his hands at last and gestured, gently, to the map-case on his desk — empty, its lock sprung. "But none of that is why you're here."

"No," I said. "I'm here because you want the map."

"The map is already mine. Your friend carried it into my cellar, and my hunters carried him out of it. He's alive, before you ask — damaged, but alive; I need him coherent for the trial. What I want—" and here he leaned forward, and the lamplight caught his eyes and made them briefly, terribly kind, "—is your signature."

* * *

He slid a single sheet across the desk.

I knew paper like that. Heavy cream stock, guild watermarked, the kind reserved for instruments of debt. But the text on it was not standard. It had been drafted by hand, in his own ink, and I read it the way I read everything — twice, three times, hunting the trap — and the trap was that there wasn't one. Or rather, the trap was the truth of it.

*Be it known: Odile Montrose, debt-bonded, is released from all obligations to the Scribes' Guild, principal and interest forgiven. Her record is struck clean. Further: one Thomas Montrose, currently indentured to the Foundrymen's Compact in settlement of his sister's bond, shall be discharged and conveyed home within the week, his own accounts closed.*

Tam. My brother's name, in ink, in forgiveness. Ten years I had carried his debt — the debt I'd made when I traded our mother's memory to the guild's market to pay for the surgeon who saved his life, the debt that had chained me to this scriptorium since I was seventeen years old. Ten years of balancing other people's impossible columns to work off a payment I'd already made and could never stop owing. And here it was, dissolved. Here he was, free. Home within the week.

My eyes burned. I hated that they burned.

"And in exchange?" My voice came out steadier than I deserved.

"The original covenant map," Vane said. "Surrendered voluntarily. And more than surrendered — renounced. You will sign a statement attesting that what you found below was a forgery, planted by dissident elements, and that the memory-trade continues under legitimate covenant authority. The guild will restore order. The markets will reopen. And the two of you—" he inclined his head slightly, including the absent Casper in the arithmetic, "—will live. That is the full price. No blood. No trials beyond the necessary theater. One signature."

"And the Hollow?"

"Will continue," he said, "as it has continued."

"Fading. A child down there didn't know her own mother's face, Master Scribe. I watched a woman sing a lullaby to a daughter who looked at her like a stranger. That's your *continuing*."

"That is the surface's continuing," he said, and for the first time his voice hardened, and I heard thirty years in it. "Four hundred thousand people above us, Montrose. Four hundred thousand, fed by granaries that shouldn't be full, healed by surgeons working miracles they don't understand, living in a city that should have collapsed into debt and plague decades ago — and doesn't, because every night we drain a little light from below to keep the lamps lit up here. You want to talk about children who don't know their mothers? Go up into Ashmarket and count the orphans of the collapse-that-never-happened. Count the plague pits we never dug. I have spent my life choosing which world starves slowly, because no one will let me choose anything kinder." He drew breath. "You think I'm the villain of this ledger. I am the only man willing to *keep* it."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below us, faintly, I could hear the scriptorium waking — pens scratching, apprentices shuffling, the ordinary music of an institution built on a lie so large it had become load-bearing.

And here is what I must confess, because the whole point of a ledger is honesty even about its keeper: for one moment — one long, warm, shameful moment — I wanted to sign.

I wanted it the way the drowning want air. Tam, home. My bond, gone. My name, mine again, clean, with nothing owed to anyone. I could walk out of this guildhouse into the morning and buy bread from a baker whose wedding was still his own, and I would never have to look at a fading child again, never have to carry the map, never have to decide anything larger than dinner. The temptation didn't arrive as weakness. It arrived as *relief*, dressed in my brother's face, and it stood in the room with us like a third person, and it was so heavy and so sweet that I had to grip the edge of the desk to keep my hand from moving toward the pen.

Vane saw it. He was a reader of ledgers; he knew what a trembling line meant.

"There's no shame in it," he said quietly. "Every person who has ever signed anything signed it first for the people they love. That's how the guild works, Montrose. That's how the whole world works. Love is the currency; we're merely the exchangers."

"No," I said. My voice cracked, and I let it. "Love is the reason people *pay*, not the reason people steal. That's the difference between a gift and a theft, and it's the whole difference, and you've spent thirty years pretending you can't tell them apart because telling them apart would mean admitting what you are."

"And what am I?"

"A man who found a burning house next to a full granary," I said, "and decided the grain mattered more."

* * *

He was silent for a long moment. Then, to my astonishment, he smiled — a small, tired, genuine smile — and took back the release.

"Do you know," he said, "in thirty years, eleven people have stood where you're standing with that document in front of them. Eleven. Couriers, archivists, one regent's son. And every one of them signed. Every one. Some of them cried while they did it. Some thanked me." He squared the paper against the desk's edge, precisely, the way I squared my own columns. "So you'll understand that your refusal, while predictable — I did write it in the margin last night, odds of four to one against you — is also somewhat refreshing."

"You knew I'd refuse."

"I hoped. There's a difference. Hope is just a wager you place on someone else's character, and yours, Montrose, has always been the soundest instrument in this building." He slid the release into a drawer and locked it, and I understood suddenly that he meant to keep it. That it was real. That some part of him had drafted it wanting to lose the wager. "Which brings us to the unpleasant portion of the morning."

"Casper."

"Your friend is in the lower cells. He'll be tried Thursday — formally, publicly; the guild requires a traitor this season and he's convenient. Unless." He looked at me over his folded hands. "Unless someone brings me the Well of Names' true terms. The actual covenant text, restored and ratified. Because I will tell you a secret, girl, since you're the only person in thirty years worth telling it to: I am *tired*. The taking gets easier and the remembering gets harder, and lately I wake at night unable to recall my mother's face, and I know exactly which drawer of which vault it sleeps in, and some mornings I stand in front of that drawer and I cannot make myself open it, because if I look, I'll have to admit what I've become." His voice dropped. "If the covenant can truly be reforged — if there is a way where the payment is honest — then bring me that way, and I will burn my own ledgers before I let the guild stop you. That is not mercy. That is a drowning man bargaining for a rope."

"And if I can't?"

"Then Thursday happens," he said simply. "For both of you."

They took me back down through the cellar, past the casks of stolen years, and put me in a cell that was, in the manner of the guild, impeccably clean. Through the wall, after a long time, I heard breathing that wasn't mine — ragged, careful, familiar — and I pressed my palm flat against the cold stone and felt, or dreamed I felt, it answer.

*I'm here,* I didn't say aloud. There were listening-spells in guild stone; I knew their architecture better than anyone alive.

Instead I lay back on the cot and did the only thing I knew how to do. I balanced.

On one side of the scale: freedom, Tam, safety, an ending — everything Vane offered, everything I had wanted for ten years, laid out shining and complete. On the other side: a child who didn't know her mother. A regent dissolving like sugar in rain. A boy with his father's seal and his father's stubbornness bleeding somewhere below me in the dark. And underneath all of it, quiet as a watermark, the truth Vane himself had handed me like a knife: *the covenant demands willing payment from both realms.* Someone had to pay. Someone always had to pay. The only question the universe had ever really asked any of us was *who*, and *willingly*, and whether love was a reason to give or a reason to take.

Vane thought I'd refused his offer out of principle. He was wrong, mostly. I'd refused it because I had finally read my own ledger all the way to the bottom, and I knew what was written there.

I owed the Hollow. I owed it from the day I first traded a memory, from the day the guild first paid my wages with someone else's mother's laughter. And a debt collector was coming Thursday, and I intended — whatever it cost, whoever it cost — to settle my accounts in full.

I just had to get out of this cell first. And I had exactly one asset left, one thing the Master Scribe wanted too badly to search me for, because he believed he'd already taken everything:

Casper had taught me, on the stair that isn't there, the hours when doors forget themselves.

Tonight, the guild's own cellar door would forget too.
