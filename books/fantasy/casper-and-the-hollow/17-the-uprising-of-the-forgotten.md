## 17. The Uprising of the Forgotten

### Odile

The fire began in the vial-house, and it was not the kind of fire that burned wood.

Odile saw it first as a light on the water — a golden shimmer rolling down the canal of the Hollow like spilled honey, and beneath it, a sound she had never heard in this dim country: cheering. Not the hollow, polite applause of the Fading Court, but the raw, cracked-throated cheering of people who had forgotten they were allowed to raise their voices.

"The markets," Casper said beside her, his bandaged shoulder stiff under her supporting arm. His face, lit by that impossible glow, looked ten years younger and ten years more afraid. "They've gone to the markets."

They had gone to the markets. All of them — the fading mothers and the nameless children and the old men whose faces had gone soft as wet paper, the ones who had stood in the Well Road and watched a debt-bonded scribe from the surface offer up her leverage for a stranger's life. Odile had not meant to be a spark. She had only meant to save Casper. But the Hollow had been dry kindling for a hundred years, and all a spark has to do is fall.

They came down the Well Road behind the crowd, because the crowd moved like a river and rivers do not ask permission.

The memory markets of the Hollow were built into the ribs of a dead cathedral — Odile could see that now, in the strange light, the vaulted bones of some surface church swallowed whole by the founding of the city above, its saints' niches filled with shelves, its altar replaced by a counting desk. The guild had built its vaults in a house of worship and never once seen the joke. Thousands of vials gleamed in the dark, racked in rows that ran back farther than the eye could follow, each one stoppered with grey wax, each one holding somebody's wedding, somebody's grief, somebody's face of a child now grown or dead or faded past recognition.

And the guild's factors were there, a handful of them, grey-robed, standing at the counting desk with their ledgers open and their faces going the color of their robes as the crowd broke over the doors.

Odile had imagined this moment, in the way one imagines disasters — as chaos, as noise, as a mob doing terrible things. What she saw instead was something that frightened her more.

It was orderly.

The Hollow folk did not smash the vials. They *took* them. An old woman whose skin was so translucent Odile could see the shelf behind her through her hands climbed onto the counting desk, seized the great ledger of withdrawals, and read aloud, in a voice like a bell with a crack in it, name after name after name. And at each name, someone in the crowd came forward.

"That one's mine." A fisherman with gills of scar tissue where his face had begun to dissolve, holding out both hands. "The winter my daughter was born. That one's mine."

"That one's mine." A boy no older than ten, who did not know his own name — Odile realized with a lurch that he was the child from the square, the one who had not recognized his mother — pointing at a vial with a faded label. "I don't know how I know. But it's mine."

"That one's mine. That one's mine. That one is *mine*."

The words came like hammer blows, like a litany, like a prayer said in reverse — not asking, *claiming*. The factors did not resist. What could they resist with? Ledgers. And the ledgers were being read aloud by the people they were written against, and it turned out that a debt spoken back to its keeper by the one who owes it is not a debt anymore. It is a bill.

Casper's hand found hers. His palm was hot, feverish — the wound was worse than he'd admitted, and she would deal with that later, she told herself, later — and he was shaking, but not with fear.

"I've seen riots," he said quietly. "My father's routes ran through all the worst parts of the surface. Riots are loud and stupid and everyone's just frightened." He nodded at the crowd, at the old woman on the desk reading names in her cracked bell-voice while the crowd repeated each one back to her, *so that the name would be heard, so that the name would exist again*. "This isn't a riot. This is an audit."

And then the first vault broke.

* * *

### Casper

The vault was a door of black iron at the back of the cathedral-ribs, and it was sealed with the guild's great seal — the quill crossed with the chain, the mark Casper had grown up hating in silence, the mark that had been pressed into the wax of the map his father died holding.

The Hollow folk had no battering ram. They had something better.

They had a man named Ellery Vane.

Casper had seen him in the Well Road crowd — a broad, stooped figure, a lamplighter by his calluses, one of the long-faded, so far gone that his features were suggestions rather than facts, a face like a thumbprint in wet clay. He had sold forty years of memories, one vial at a time, to keep his family fed in a country where food was memory and memory was food. He walked up to the black iron door, and he put his forehead against it, and he said, so quietly that Casper only heard it because the crowd had gone still:

"I want my wife's face back. I want the morning she died. I want the name I gave my son. I want all of it, and I want the *interest*."

Then he hit the door with the iron bar he'd carried up from the lamplighter's yard, and the sound it made was the sound of a bell the size of a mountain, and the door — which had held against a century of fading patience — cracked down the middle like an egg.

Because the door was not just iron. Casper understood that in the moment it broke. It was iron *and covenant*, and the covenant was already breaking, had been breaking since a courier bled out in a scriptorium, since a scribe with ink-stained fingers chose the stair that wasn't there, since a crowd learned its own names again. The vault was only as strong as the lie it protected, and the lie was dead, and the door knew it.

Vials poured out of the broken vault like a flood of frozen light.

The Hollow folk did not trample each other for them. That was the thing Casper would remember all his life, the thing he would write down later, in another life, in a ledger of his own: they did not *trample*. They knelt. They knelt in the flood of their own stolen years and picked up vials with the tenderness of people handling infants, reading labels, calling out — "Whose is this? Whose is 1789, the harvest? Whose is the wedding on the bridge?" — and passing each one hand to hand until it found its owner, and each owner, drinking, *came back*.

Casper watched it happen. He watched a woman drink her mother's laugh and suddenly *have* a mother again, have forty years of her, have the smell of bread and the scold and the song, and she sat down right there on the vault floor and wept with her whole body, and around her the fading — the terrible translucent fading that had eaten the Hollow for a century — *reversed*. Not all at once. Not completely. Color came back into her hands like dawn coming into a window.

It works, he thought, and the thought hit him like the lamplighter's bar. *It actually works. Give the memories back and the Hollow lives.*

And if it works in this direction—

He grabbed Odile's wrist. "The surface," he said. "If returning them restores the Hollow, then the surface has been running on stolen fuel this whole time. When the vaults empty—"

"When the vaults empty," Odile said, and her face went pale in the vial-light, "the city above starts to remember what it actually is. What it actually was. Before."

Before the prosperity. Before the ledgers balanced themselves with other people's lives. Before the guild.

Somewhere above them, through a hundred feet of stone and root and dark, Casper fancied he could hear it beginning: the great city of Vael Corren, waking from a hundred-year dream, remembering its debts.

* * *

### Odile

But a broken vault is not a healed world, and a crowd with vials in its hands is still a crowd, and crowds can be turned.

Odile saw the grey-robes regrouping at the far end of the market hall — not the factors, who had fled or knelt or simply stood weeping among their own ledgers like men watching a flood take their houses, but the *hunters*. Six of them, maybe eight, in their chain-stitched hoods, and behind them, at the mouth of the corridor that led down toward the deeper vaults, a figure she knew.

The Master Scribe's under-secretary. Vess. The one who had read out her debt-bond in the scriptorium with a voice like a man reading a menu.

Vess raised a hand, and the hunters raised their quills — the terrible writing instruments of the guild, quills that could write a person out of existence, and Odile had seen what a written-out man looked like, had seen the courier's body in the scriptorium with its name already smudging.

"No," she said, and she did not know she was going to say it, and then she was walking forward, out of the crowd's shadow, into the open floor between the crowd and the hunters, and her voice came out of her the way the old woman's bell-voice had come out of her, cracked and enormous. "You are standing in a house of worship that you turned into a pawnshop, and you are holding pens, and you think that makes you strong. Look at them." She pointed at the kneeling crowd, at the woman with color coming back into her hands, at the boy holding his name. "Look at what a hundred years of your ledger *cost*. You want to write someone out? Write *me* out. My name is Odile Montrose, debt-bond number nine thousand and forty-one, and every entry in your books about me is a lie you told to make theft look like arithmetic. Come and collect."

The hall went silent. Even the fire in the vial-house, guttering somewhere above, seemed to hold its breath.

And Vess — Vess did the last thing Odile expected. He laughed.

"Scribe Montrose," he said, and the title was a knife wrapped in courtesy. "You still don't understand what you've broken open. Yes. Take the memories. Watch your Hollow bloom. And then climb the stair and watch the surface *starve* — watch the city forget how to make its steel and its bread and its laws, watch a hundred years of borrowed skill evaporate in a week, watch the plague-years come back because no one up there remembers the cures we bought. The covenant was a lie, yes. But it was a lie holding up a *roof*. You've pulled out the pillar. Both realms come down. The Master Scribe tried to tell the Court this for a century. The Court forgot to listen. Will you?"

The crowd murmured. Odile felt the murmur move through them like a current — the terrible arithmetic of it, the truth inside the lie. She had no answer. Not yet. She had a map in blood-ink against her ribs and a half-formed plan about a well, and between her and the well stood a question she could not yet answer.

But Casper could. Casper stepped up beside her, grey-faced, holding his wounded arm, and he said:

"Then the covenant gets *reforged*. Willingly. Both sides. That's what the map says — my father died carrying the terms, and the terms say willing payment from both realms, not theft. The guild never reforged it because reforging it means the surface gives back honestly, means the guild stops skimming, means *you*—" he pointed at Vess, "—stop being rich. Don't tell us the roof can't be rebuilt. Tell us why you never tried."

And the crowd roared. Not the murmur now — a roar, a hundred throats, a thousand, the sound of people who had spent a century being *quiet* discovering the full capacity of their own lungs, and the hunters' quills wavered, and Vess's face for the first time showed something underneath the courtesy, something that looked almost like fear, and he and his hunters melted back down the corridor toward the deep vaults, toward the Master Scribe, toward the last ledgers and the last locks.

Toward the Well of Names. Odile knew it in her bones. If the guild could not stop the uprising, it would go for the covenant itself — poison the well, seal the stair, burn the map's twin, do *something* down there in the dark where the covenant was written.

"We have to move," she said. "Now. Tonight."

* * *

### Casper

They went down through the burning market while the Hollow folk drank back their lives.

It was not a clean thing, the uprising. Casper did not want to remember it clean. There were factors who had hidden vials of the *best* memories — children's first words, the faces of the dead — and when the crowd found the hiding places, some of the crowd did ugly things, and Casper watched an old factor be pressed against a shelf until his hood came off and he was only a frightened man, and the crowd around him wanted blood, and got a little of it before the lamplighter Vane pulled them back with his cracked bell of a voice: "*We are not them. We are not them. That's the whole point of us. We are not them.*"

There were vials that broke in the fire, and the memories inside them burned out into the air unclaimed — a century of weddings and funerals and first snows going up as smoke, and people stood in that smoke breathing it in, weeping, catching the last of strangers' joys on their tongues because it was all that was left of them. Grief everywhere. Grief like water. But underneath the grief, everywhere, the fury, and the fury was not the ugly kind, not in its heart. It was the fury of a bill come due. It was the fury of the forgotten, and it was *clean at the center*, and it carried them.

They reached the mouth of the deep corridor as the bells of the Hollow began to ring — real bells, salvaged from the drowned cathedral, rung by hands that had not rung anything in a hundred years. The sound rolled down the tunnel ahead of them like a promise.

Odile stopped at the threshold and put her hand against the wall, where the blood-ink map, pulled from her coat, showed the way down: the Well of Names, the sealing place, the heart of everything. And below the well, in ink so old it had gone brown, a line of the original covenant that Casper had read a dozen times and never understood until this moment:

*The well keeps what is freely given, and gives back what is freely asked, and the keeper of the ledger shall owe nothing, or the well will know.*

"The well will know," Casper murmured.

"Then we'd better make sure it knows the right things," Odile said.

They went down into the dark, following the bells, and far below them — deeper than the vaults, deeper than the markets, down where the guild's oldest secrets were kept in the oldest ink — Casper heard, very faintly, the sound of a great door being sealed.

The guild had reached the Well of Names first.
