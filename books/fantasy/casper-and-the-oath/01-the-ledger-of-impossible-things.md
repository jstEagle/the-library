## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The vault beneath the Ashgrove Guild did not have windows, which Casper Vale considered its single honest feature. Everything else in the place lied by design — the lamps burned without oil, the locks opened for voices that had died decades ago, and the ledgers themselves breathed. He had worked among them long enough to stop finding it unsettling and start finding it tedious, which was, he supposed, what mastery looked like: wonder ground down into routine.

He sat at the audit desk with his sleeves rolled to the elbow, a pot of cold tea at his right hand and a jeweler's loupe screwed into his left eye, working through the night's entries the way a surgeon works through a body — methodically, respectfully, and with the quiet certainty that something in there was going to surprise him.

The ledger open before him was *Volume IX: Years*, one of forty-one volumes cataloguing everything the guild had ever traded. Memories were the bread and butter of the Ashgrove inventory, but memories came in flavors, and each flavor had its own book. *Names* was Volume II. *Debts* was Volume XVII and required two witnesses to open. *Years* — whole years, lifted from lives like fruit from a branch — was Volume IX, and it was Casper's favorite, because years were the most honest commodity they dealt in. A stolen name could be argued with. A stolen debt could be disputed. But a year, once taken, was simply gone from the person who'd lived it, and no amount of cleverness brought it back.

Tonight's audit covered eleven entries. He read each one twice, checked the provenance seals, matched the ink weights against the guild's reference standard, and initialed the margin in his small, severe hand.

Entry 9,447: three years traded by a cooper in Marrow's Reach for a house that did not burn down. Standard extraction, clean edges, no residue. The man would wake tomorrow and remember building barrels but not the daughter who'd died in the fire he no longer remembered surviving. The guild called that a kindness. Casper had stopped calling it anything at all.

Entry 9,451: a widow's grief, sold in installments. Fourteen payments over as many months, each one lighter than the last, until she'd be able to stand at her husband's grave and feel only the weather. Clean work. Someone else's clean work — the seal belonged to Vessic, the guild's best extractor, a woman who could lift a memory so gently the donor never felt the departure. Casper envied her the gentleness. His own extractions were precise but never painless; he took things the way a key takes a lock, with a small violence of turning.

He was halfway through entry 9,453 when he noticed the ink.

It was a minor entry, barely worth the page. A ferryman on the Graywater had traded a single afternoon — one afternoon, nothing more — in exchange for passage debts forgiven. The memory itself was unremarkable: sun on water, the smell of tar, a passenger who never gave a name. Casper had audited hundreds like it. He was reaching for his stamp when he saw that the ink of the entry was moving.

Not drying wrong. Not smudging. *Moving* — creeping outward from the letters like frost across glass, thin tendrils of black spreading past the ruled lines of the page, pooling in the margins where no ink should be. And the color was wrong. Guild ink was iron-gall; it aged brown, went brittle, flaked. This was black the way the space between stars is black, and it was wet. It glistened under the lamp like something freshly spilled.

Casper set down his stamp and looked at it for a long moment, the way you look at a dog that has begun speaking in a human voice.

Ink did not bleed in the vault. Ink was the one thing here that obeyed. Every substance in the Ashgrove collection was bound by contract — the contracts were literally written into their molecular structure, which was why the lamps burned without oil and the doors knew dead voices. Ink that moved was ink that had escaped its contract, or worse, ink that had never signed one.

He pulled the loupe from his eye and leaned close. The tendrils had reached the margin now, and in the margin they were doing something worse than spreading. They were forming characters. Small ones, cramped, slanting left against every convention of script Casper knew. He couldn't read them, but he recognized the *style* of them, the way you recognize handwriting before you recognize words.

Someone was writing in the margins of the guild's ledger, and whoever it was wrote like no living hand.

"Right," he said aloud, to the empty vault, because saying things aloud kept the loneliness at a manageable distance. "Let's see what you are."

* * *

There were protocols for anomalies. Casper had written half of them himself, back in his first decade, after the incident with the ledger that had begun dreaming. Protocol said: isolate the volume, log the anomaly, notify the guildmaster, and touch nothing with bare skin until the archivists had run the binding rites.

Casper touched it with bare skin.

Not out of recklessness — or not only. Out of the particular hunger that had driven him to the guild in the first place, twenty years ago, a hungry apprentice with quick hands and nothing in the world that anyone wanted back. The vault was full of other people's lives, thousands of them, shelved and labeled and dusted, and Casper had spent two decades handling them like a priest handles relics: reverently, expertly, and always, always belonging to someone else. He knew the taste of a stranger's wedding day. He knew the smell of a childhood he'd never had. He was the most well-remembered man in the city and none of the memories were his.

So when a page began bleeding impossible ink, protocol said report it, but instinct said *this one is addressed to me*. Things in the vault rarely announced themselves. When they did, it paid to listen first and file paperwork second.

He pressed two fingers flat against the wet margin.

The vault vanished.

Not dimmed, not darkened — *vanished*, the way a word vanishes when you say it too many times, all at once and completely. In its place: heat, wind, gold light going amber at the edges, and wheat. Wheat everywhere, taller than his waist, bending in long silver waves around him, and the smell of it — dry, sweet, dusty, like summer deciding whether to become autumn. Somewhere far off, a bird made a sound he had no name for, three notes descending, patient as dripping water.

And there, alone in the middle of the field, stood a door.

No wall. No frame beyond its own frame. No house it might belong to, no ruin it might have outlived. Just a door, upright in the wheat, painted a green so deep it looked wet, with a brass handle polished bright by use. It cast a shadow, which meant there was a sun somewhere above the haze, though Casper couldn't find it. The door was closed. It looked, more than anything else in the world, *expectant* — the way an empty chair looks expectant, the way a set table looks.

He walked toward it. The wheat parted around him with a sound like whispered counting. Ten steps. Twenty. The door did not recede, exactly, but it did not arrive either; it held its distance the way the horizon holds its distance, present and unreachable in the same breath. He could see the grain of the wood. He could see his own reflection, faint and warped, in the brass handle. He reached out—

—and the vault snapped back around him like a trap closing.

He was standing. He didn't remember standing. His tea had gone fully cold and his hand still hovered over the ledger page, fingers curled around the shape of a handle that wasn't there. His heart was going hard enough that he could hear it in his ears, and his mouth tasted of wheat dust, which was impossible, because there was no wheat within four hundred miles of the vault.

On the page, the bleeding ink had stopped. The tendrils lay still in the margin, and now that he looked at them properly — now that he'd seen whatever they'd wanted him to see — they resolved into legibility, the way a face resolves once you know it. Not foreign script after all. Numbers. A date, written small and cramped and slanting:

**the ninth of the waning moon.**

Casper knew dates the way priests knew prayers. It was part of the trade; every memory came stamped with when it happened, and misdating an entry was the gravest sin an auditor could commit. So he did the arithmetic without wanting to, automatically, the way you count stairs.

The ninth of the waning moon was tonight.

* * *

He finished the audit. That was the discipline of him — the thing the guildmaster praised and everyone else quietly resented. Whatever shook him, he finished the work first. He initialed the remaining entries, sealed Volume IX, logged the anomaly in the incident book with a description so dry it read like a recipe, and then, and only then, allowed himself to sit very still in the lamplight and consider what had happened.

A stranger's memory had shown him a door. That much was clear, or clear enough. The ferryman's traded afternoon — sun on water, an unnamed passenger — had been the surface of the extraction, but memory is layered, and somewhere beneath the ferry ride there had been a field, and a door, and whatever waited behind it. Extractions sometimes carried sediment. Casper had seen it before: pull a stone from a riverbed and sometimes another stone comes up stuck to it.

But sediment didn't bleed. Sediment didn't write dates in margins. Sediment didn't reach up through a century-old ledger and take a thief by the fingers like a hand extended in a crowd.

He thought about the door. He thought about how it had looked at him — and it had looked at him, he was sure of it now, sure in the way thieves are sure about being watched, an instinct older than his apprenticeship. Doors don't look. But that one had been waiting for him specifically. He'd felt it the way you feel your own name spoken in another room.

He thought, too, about the ferryman. Entry 9,453. A man who'd sold a single afternoon and gone home lighter by one sunset. Casper had audited the extraction seal, verified the payment, filed the paper. Somewhere across the city, the ferryman was sleeping the sleep of the unburdened, and he would never know that the afternoon he'd sold had contained a door, and that the door had been bought by a guild, and that the guild's auditor had just put his fingers in it.

That was the trade. That was always the trade. Somebody's smallest afternoon became somebody else's whole world, and neither of them ever met, and the ledger recorded the exchange in iron-gall ink and called it fair.

Casper looked at his reflection in the dark window-glass of the vault's inner door. Forty-one years old, mostly. Quick hands, gray coming in early at the temples, eyes the color of the tea he never drank hot because hot tea meant sitting down and sitting down meant stopping and stopping meant the silence got in. Twenty years in the vault. He could appraise a memory's worth in seconds. He could tell you what a grief weighed, what a childhood cost, what a first love fetched on the open market versus what it was actually worth, which were never the same numbers. He was, by any measure the guild kept, excellent at his work.

And every night he climbed the stairs out of the vault, crossed the courtyard where the lamps burned without oil, let himself into his narrow rooms above the counting-house, and ate dinner alone at a table set for one, surrounded by books full of other people's best days.

Restless competence, the guildmaster called it. Casper knew what it actually was. It was a man very good at holding things who had never once been handed anything.

He locked the incident book, banked the lamps, and stood.

Then he heard it — faint, from the shelf where the restricted volumes lived behind their iron grille. A sound like a page turning in an empty room. He crossed the floor, keys already in his hand, and found the grille shut, the locks fast, and every volume exactly where it should be. Except one. *The Prophecy Codex*, the oldest book the guild owned, chained to its lectern since before the guild had a name, had turned its own page.

And in the fresh margin, in the same cramped, slanting, impossible hand, the ink was still wet, and it was waiting for him, and this time it wasn't numbers.

This time it was a date, written out in full, a hundred years old and still bleeding:

**tonight.**
