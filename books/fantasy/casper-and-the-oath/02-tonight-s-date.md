## 2. Tonight's Date

Casper Vale did not touch the page.

That was the first rule of the vault, older than any of its locks: *you do not touch what bleeds.* Ink that moved was ink with an appetite, and every ledger-keeper before him had learned it the same way — by losing a fingertip's worth of memory to a margin that wanted feeding. There was still a hollow place in the third drawer of the archive where a man named Ollivan Peck used to keep his wedding day, until he reached for a wet entry and forgot he'd ever been married at all. They'd found him sitting on the stairs, smiling politely at everyone, unable to say why.

So Casper stood back from the lectern, hands folded behind his back like a man at a funeral, and read.

**tonight.**

One word. No year. No hour. Just the date, written out in the Codex's cramped slanting hand, and beneath it — because the Codex had never once stopped being cruel — the ink continued, fresh lines crawling across the vellum as he watched, like frost spreading up a window:

*the keeper who counts shall open the book from inside.*

*the door remembers him already.*

"Absolutely not," Casper said to the empty room.

The room did not answer, which he chose to find encouraging.

He did what he always did when the world misbehaved: he got a second opinion. Not from a person — people in the guild were mostly opinions wearing coats — but from the ledgers themselves. The Ashgrove Guild kept records the way other institutions kept prisoners: thoroughly, jealously, and with an eye toward eventual ransom. If tonight's date meant anything anywhere, it would be cross-referenced somewhere in the stacks, filed under some impossible category like *Days That Were Promised* or *Debts Owed by Time*, because the guild had categories for everything, including things that hadn't happened yet.

He fetched the day-book first. His own hand, his own entries, three years of them, neat as stitching. He turned to today's date and found it exactly as he'd written it at dawn: inventory, audits, one routine acquisition of a retired clockmaker's Tuesdays. Nothing strange.

Then he checked the ink.

It had dried wrong.

Not smudged, not faded — *wrong*, the way a word is wrong when you've stared at it too long. He held the day-book under the lamp and tilted it, and the entries for today shifted, very slightly, the way water shifts over a stone. Underneath his tidy accounting, faint as a watermark, another line ran through the paper in handwriting that was not his and was also, horribly, almost his.

*he will check twice,* said the ghost-hand. *he always checks twice.*

Casper set the day-book down with exaggerated care, the way you set down something that might be sleeping.

"All right," he told it. "You have my attention. Try not to be smug about it."

* * *

The Prophecy Codex sat chained to its lectern in the restricted alcove, iron links long enough to permit reading and short enough to discourage theft, though Casper privately suspected the chain wasn't there to keep the book from being taken. It was there to keep it from leaving on its own.

He brought the lamp close and began the work he was actually good at: comparison. Cross-referencing was a kind of lockpicking, if you thought about it right. Every document had a mechanism, a tumblers-and-springs logic underneath the words, and if you could feel how a text fit together you could feel where someone had forced it. The Codex was no different. A hundred years of prophecies, most of them vague in the way prophecies are when they want deniability — *a city shall hunger for its own name; a daughter shall carry her father's debt across water* — all of it written in the same slanting hand, all of it dated, all of it accounted for.

Except the dates didn't account for anything.

That was the thing he found at half past eleven, with the vault clock ticking down toward midnight like a patient creditor. He'd gone through the Codex front to back, then back to front, running dates against dates, looking for the pattern, and the pattern was worse than any prophecy. Every prediction in the book had come true — fine, the guild knew that, that was why the Codex lived behind iron — but the dates were *impossible*. Entries describing events from two centuries ago were dated after the events they described. Entries about things yet to come carried dates decades old. The whole book kept its chronology the way a drunk keeps his balance: technically upright, fundamentally lying.

And every impossible date, without exception, resolved itself to a single day.

Tonight's.

Casper counted them. He counted because counting was the only prayer he knew — forty-one entries, spread across a hundred years, each one stamped with a different impossible date, each one pointing like a compass needle at the twenty-third of the month, the day the vault ledger bled, the day the Codex turned its own page and wrote **tonight** in ink that hadn't finished drying since before the guild had a name.

Forty-one prophecies, and they weren't predictions at all. They were *installments*. A century-long payment plan, each verse a little more specific than the last, the way a debt gets more insistent as it comes due.

The earliest entry, a hundred years old, read only: *a thief will come who keeps accounts.*

The next, ninety years later: *he will count what should not be counted.*

The third: *he will find the page that bleeds.*

They got closer together near the end. In the last decade the Codex had grown chatty, gossipy, almost fond. *He checks twice.* *He works alone and calls it a preference.* *His mother taught him the names of stars and he has sold all of them except one.* Casper read that line four times before his hands steadied enough to turn the page. It was true. It was so precisely, privately true that it felt less like prophecy than burglary — someone had broken into the smallest room of his heart and left the window open behind them.

And then the final entry. Tonight's. Still damp. Still bleeding.

*the keeper who counts shall open the book from inside.*

"Keeper," Casper said aloud, tasting the word like a coin he suspected was clipped. "I'm a thief. Get it right."

The ink shivered, and added a line:

*in Vell, those are the same trade.*

He had never heard the word Vell in his life. He was nearly sure of that. And yet it landed somewhere below memory, in the cellar of him, with the weight of something he'd been carrying for years without noticing — the way you stop feeling a stone in your shoe until you take the shoe off.

The vault clock struck quarter-to-midnight.

* * *

Guildmaster Hesperine kept late hours the way other men kept grudges: out of habit, and because letting go seemed somehow more expensive. Casper found him in the counting-room above the vault, doing what he always did after midnight — reconciling the guild's holdings against the guild's promises, a nightly ritual that consisted of weighing small glass vials of memory on a jeweler's scale and frowning at the discrepancies only he could see.

Hesperine was old the way oak is old, all grain and weather, with eyes the color of ledger-paper. He didn't look up when Casper entered. "Vault's locked."

"The Codex turned its own page."

Now he looked up. Slowly, the way heavy things move. "Show me."

"It's writing tonight's date," Casper said. "It's been writing tonight's date for a hundred years. Forty-one entries, all converging. It knows my name, Guildmaster. It knows about my mother." He heard his own voice go flat and precise, the voice he used for appraisals, and hated it a little. "It called me the keeper who counts."

For a long moment Hesperine said nothing. He set down the vial he'd been weighing, aligned it with the others on the tray — all twelve, evenly spaced, a little parade of stolen days — and folded his hands.

"Codices drift," he said at last. "Old ink settles. Margins blur. You've been alone down there three years, Vale. Books get lonely, and lonely books get imaginative."

"It quoted my childhood."

"Then it's been reading your day-books." The old man shrugged, vast and slow, a hill deciding something. "Everything in this guild is traded, boy. Everything. Including, eventually, privacy. Some clerk bought a memory of your mother thirty years ago, sold it to a broker, the broker's estate came to us in settlement, and now the Codex has chewed it into poetry. That's not prophecy. That's *inventory*."

Casper stood very still. It was a good explanation. It was a beautiful explanation, actually — bureaucratic, unromantic, entirely consistent with everything he knew about how the world worked. Things got traded, things got resold, meaning leaked sideways between documents like water between stones.

"You don't believe that," Casper said.

"I believe it's cheaper to believe," Hesperine said, and returned to his scales.

But as Casper reached the door, the old man spoke again, without turning around, in the tone he used for bad debts he intended to collect personally.

"If it writes your name in full — first name, family name, the way we write the dead — you come to me before you come to the book. Do you understand? Before the book."

"And if I'm already at the book?"

"Then be quick," said Hesperine, "and count carefully."

* * *

The stairs down to the vault seemed longer on the return. Casper took them anyway, because of course he did, because checking twice was the only religion he'd ever practiced faithfully.

The lamp in the alcove had burned low. The Codex waited on its lectern, chained and patient, and the page was still open to tonight's date, and the ink —

The ink had grown.

While he'd been upstairs arguing bureaucracy with an old man in denial, the margin had filled. What had been a handful of lines was now a column, dense and slanting, and Casper made himself read it top to bottom, standing well back, hands locked behind him like a prisoner of his own caution.

*a thief will come who keeps accounts.*

*he will find the page that bleeds.*

*he will ask the old man, who will tell him it is nothing, because telling himself otherwise costs more than he pays.*

*his name —*

And there it was. Mid-margin, mid-column, the letters forming even as he watched, stroke by stroke, wet black rising out of dry vellum like a bruise surfacing through skin:

*C —*

*A —*

*S —*

"No," Casper said, and heard how thin it came out. "No. We're not doing this."

*S —*

*P —*

He grabbed the lamp. Old instinct, old rule — *you do not touch what bleeds* — but rules were for ledgers, and this wasn't a ledger, this was a book writing him into existence letter by letter, and there were exactly three ways to stop a document from completing itself: burn it, seal it, or steal it. The Codex couldn't be burned; three guildmasters had tried, and their ashes were filed under *Lessons*. It couldn't be sealed; the iron grille was proof of that failure.

Which left theft.

He was reaching for the sand-tray — the emergency dousing box chained beside the lectern for precisely this contingency, though no one in living memory had needed it — when he saw the rest.

Because the column hadn't stopped at his name. Below the forming letters, in smaller script, almost gentle, the Codex had appended something new. A time. An instruction. A courtesy, really, the kind of note a host leaves for an expected guest:

*midnight. bring nothing. you are already carrying it.*

The clock in the dark above him began to strike.

Twelve strokes, and on the first one the lamps guttered though there was no draft, and on the fourth the ink in the margin began to move faster, letters tumbling over each other now, his name assembling itself with the eagerness of a debt finally coming due, and on the ninth Casper understood — with the calm, terrible clarity of a man reading his own contract — that the Codex had never been predicting tonight at all.

Prophecies predict. This was *scheduling*. A hundred years of installments, paid punctually, and tonight was the date the account matured, and he hadn't signed anything, except that he had, hadn't he, every day for three years, every audit, every count, every careful double-check — the whole time keeping the books of a guild whose oldest book was keeping him.

On the twelfth stroke, the last letter of his name settled wet and shining into the vellum.

**CASPER VALE**, complete, dated a century ago, witnessed by no one.

The vault folded.

Not collapsed — *folded*, the way a page folds, the walls swinging inward like covers closing, shelves bending toward him like fingers, and the last thing Casper Vale saw of the world he had catalogued so carefully was the Codex itself, open, satisfied, its margins finally dry.

The last thing he felt was the ink taking hold — patient, proprietary, warm as a handshake — and a single line arriving in his mind in that slanting, impossible hand, no longer written anywhere but simply *known*:

*the keeper who counts has opened the book from inside.*
