## 3. Pulled

The codex did not want to be read.

Casper had learned this the way he learned most things — through the body, not the mind. His fingers hovered above the open page and the paper *leaned* toward him, a hunger in it, the way old ledgers sometimes seemed to lean when an entry was overdue. He'd spent twenty minutes on the prophecy that bore tonight's date, tracing the faded script with one finger held a careful inch above the vellum, never touching. *The keeper who counts shall come at dusk of the appointed day.* The margins had rewritten themselves around his name an hour ago, ink crawling like something waking from a long sleep, and he had not touched them either.

He was very good at not touching things.

That was the whole trade, really. A memory-thief who touched everything was a thief with no inventory. You learned to stand at the edge of other people's lives and lift what you needed without disturbing the rest — a year here, a name there, the smell of someone's dead father's coat lifted clean out of their grief like a coin from a pocket. Casper Vale could walk through a crowded room and leave it lighter without anyone feeling the draft.

But the codex was not a crowded room. The codex was watching him back.

"Appointed day," he murmured, because saying things aloud made them smaller. The vault heard him anyway. It was built for hearing — shelves of ledgers rising forty feet into dark rafters, each spine holding its own small thefts, the air thick with that particular silence of a place where thousands of memories sat shelved and breathing. Somewhere above, the guild's night-bells would be counting down to midnight. He'd have maybe an hour before the vault-warden's rounds brought lamplight down the eastern stair.

The anomalous ledger still lay open beside him. Page 401, entry dated ninety-one years ago: *one door, standing alone in a wheat field, traded for nothing recorded.* And beneath it, in fresh ink that should not exist, bleeding up through the paper from nowhere: *he is coming.*

"I'm not," Casper told the book. "Whatever I am, I'm not coming."

The ink spread another fraction of an inch.

He should have gone to the guildmaster again. He had gone once already, and gotten the flat look that meant *you are a tool, Vale, and tools do not interpret their own readings* — the same look that had followed him through nine years in the Ashgrove Guild, ever since the night they'd found him, seventeen years old and hollow-eyed, having stolen his way out of a debt he couldn't name even then. The guildmaster kept him because he was precise. Precision did not require being believed.

So instead of going upstairs where people were, Casper stayed down here among the books, which was where he belonged, which was the thought that always arrived dressed as comfort and tasted like ash.

He checked the hour by feel — a thief's habit, keeping time in your wrists — and decided he would copy the prophecy page. Not take it. Copy it. There was a difference, and the difference was that taking left a gap, and gaps in the guild's collection got noticed, and noticed things got audited, and audits ended careers or worse. He took out his kit: fine brush, distilled ink, a sheet of blank vellum soft as skin.

His hand shook. He looked at it shaking, mildly offended.

"You're being ridiculous," he told himself. "It's a book."

The book turned its own page.

Not metaphorically. Not a draft from some vent. The vellum lifted and flipped with the deliberate weight of a hand turning it, and Casper went backward off his stool with the sound of his own heartbeat loud in his ears, and landed among the shadows between shelf and floor, and lay there looking up at the codex as it turned another page, and another, faster now, pages riffling past like wind through wheat —

— like wind through wheat.

"No," Casper said.

The bells began to strike midnight.

He felt the first note in his teeth. The second note arrived in the floorboards, humming up through his spine, and by the third note he understood that the sound wasn't coming from the tower above at all. It was coming from inside the codex. Each bell-stroke was a page settling, and the vault was answering — the shelves leaning inward, all of them, the whole cathedral of stolen memory bowing toward the little table where he sat, the way a crowd leans toward a stage.

The fourth bell. The shelves were moving. Forty feet of ironwood and vellum, groaning inward like the covers of a closing book, and Casper scrambled up and ran three steps and understood, in the calm detached way of a man reading his own obituary, that there was nowhere to run because the room itself was folding shut around him.

The fifth bell. The far wall came down like a falling curtain of spines. Dust fell upward. That detail lodged in him absurdly, unforgettably — dust drifting *up*, defying every law he'd ever pickpocketed, and he thought: *this is what it looks like when a thing is being remembered wrong.*

The sixth bell, and the codex opened to the width of a doorway, and the doorway was full of dusk.

He had one instant — one long golden instant hanging between bell-strokes — to understand that he was not falling into a book. Books don't pull. This was a country reaching through its only remaining window, and it had been waiting a hundred years, and it was hungry for its keeper the way the vault was hungry for debts: patiently, totally, without malice.

Then the seventh bell struck, and Casper Vale stepped off the edge of his own world.

* * *

Falling through a codex, it turns out, is not like falling.

There is no stomach-drop. There is no wind. There is only the sensation of being *read* — of passing line by line through a sentence much larger than yourself, each word brushing against him like a stranger's fingers searching his pockets. He felt things lifted from him as he fell. Small things, at first. The taste of this morning's tea. The exact blue of the vault's warden's eyes. Then larger things, and he clawed at them — his mother's voice singing off-key in a kitchen that smelled of burnt sugar; the first lie he ever told well; the weight of the first coin he ever stole, warm from someone else's palm — and the words let him keep those, but he felt them *weighed*, measured against some scale he couldn't see, catalogued, entered somewhere.

*I know that feeling,* he thought, tumbling. *I've done that to other people. I'm being done unto.*

The irony arrived with perfect comic timing, which was somehow the worst part.

Then the sentence ended, and he hit ground that smelled like rain that hadn't fallen yet.

Casper lay on his back in a wheat field under a sky the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom. Dusk — real dusk, permanent dusk, the sun hung low and huge and red on a horizon it never seemed to sink below, painting everything gold and rust and shadow. Wheat surrounded him to the height of a man's chest, stalks heavy-headed and motionless in air that didn't stir. No wind. No birds. No insects. Just the wheat, and the light, and a silence so complete he could hear his own blood working.

And there, thirty paces away, exactly as the stolen memory had shown him: a door.

It stood alone in the field. No wall, no frame, no house — just a door, upright, oak and iron-banded, closed, patient as a headstone. Its shadow stretched long across the wheat toward him, and the shadow was the only thing in the entire landscape that moved, swaying gently though nothing else stirred, as if it were breathing. As if it were waiting to be knocked on.

Casper got up slowly, the way you rise in a room where you're not sure you're alone. Every instinct he owned fired at once — *inventory, exits, valuables* — and returned a report so bleak it was almost funny. Inventory: one thief, slightly lighter than before. Exits: one door, destination unknown, possibly the trap itself. Valuables: none, unless you counted the memories still in his head, and something in the way the wheat stood perfectly still suggested that everyone here counted those.

"Okay," he said aloud. His voice fell flat in the windless air, died a foot from his lips. "Vell."

The name rose out of him unbidden — out of the ledger entries he'd memorized, the codex margins, the bleeding ink. Saying it changed the field. The wheat rippled once, all together, like a sleeper shifting under blankets. The door's shadow shivered.

Somewhere behind him, a church bell rang six times.

Six o'clock. Evening bells. Except the sky said evening had been true for hours, days, possibly decades, and when Casper turned to look for the village the sound must come from, he saw lights strung along a valley below — lantern-glow, chimney smoke standing perfectly straight in the windless air, the geometry of a town at evening. So he walked toward it, because walking toward light is older than sense, and because the alternative was knocking on the door in the field, and he wasn't ready for doors yet.

The town was called Marrowfen, according to a sign whose letters had been painted over so many times the original name showed through in ghost-strokes underneath. The streets were cobbled and crooked and utterly lovely in the low red light, and the people filling them were the strangest thing Casper had ever seen, and he had seen a woman sell her wedding day for a jar of youth.

They were repeating themselves.

He saw it within moments, once he knew to look. A fishmonger argued cheerfully with a customer about the price of eels — and then, ten paces later, near the fountain, the same fishmonger argued the same words with the same customer, same tilt of the head, same laugh. A boy chased a dog around a well, caught it, let it go, and rounded the corner to begin the chase again. Two old men played draughts in a doorway, and one moved a piece, lost it, groaned, and reset the board — and Casper, watching from the shadow of a lintel, saw the game had been reset so many times the board's squares were worn smooth in the pattern of that single recurring loss.

Everyone wore the same clothes. Everyone carried the same baskets, the same bread, the same armloads of the same flowers. And nobody — this was the part that crawled coldly down Casper's spine — nobody looked tired of it. They laughed at the same jokes with genuine delight. They grieved the same griefs freshly. A woman stood at a doorstep holding a small coffin-sized bundle wrapped in linen, and she wept with the raw, total anguish of the first loss in the history of the world, and across the square, when the moment circled back around, she wept it again, identical, pristine, endless.

"The same day," Casper whispered. "They're living the same day over and over, and they don't—" 

He stopped. Because a girl selling apples had just looked directly at him.

Only her. Only for a moment. Her eyes passed over him the way eyes pass over furniture, and slid on, and she resumed calling her prices in a voice worn smooth by centuries of the same cry. But for one heartbeat she had *seen* him, and the seeing had cost her something — a flicker of confusion crossing her face like a crack across glaze — and then the loop closed over her again and she was apple-seller, only apple-seller, forever.

*Don't touch anything,* Casper thought. *Don't disturb the display. You're the anomaly here. You're the thing that doesn't belong in the ledger.*

Which was when he realized someone was standing behind him.

He hadn't heard footsteps — there were no footsteps to hear; he would stake his career on the fact that this street made no sound underfoot that it didn't make every day, forever — but a presence had simply assembled itself at his shoulder the way dusk assembles, gradual and then total. He turned.

She was perhaps fifty, or perhaps five hundred — the dusk-light made age impossible, smoothing everything to bronze. Tall, gray-streaked hair pinned with what looked like a quill, dressed in traveling leathers gone soft with a century of wearing, and carrying herself with the particular stillness of someone who had stopped expecting surprises a very long time ago. Her eyes were the color of banked coals, and they were fixed on him with an expression he recognized from mirrors: the look of a professional examining a forgery and finding, to her astonishment, that it might be real.

"You're late," she said.

Her voice was rough, like a door unused. Casper's mouth, trained in nine years of talking his way past wardens and creditors and worse, produced: "Traffic."

Something crossed her face that was almost a smile and didn't survive the journey. "Ninety-one years since the ledger wrote *he is coming*. A hundred since the codex named the keeper who counts. I have watched every midnight from every angle this country has." She stepped closer, and up close he could see the exhaustion under her composure, deep as geology. "You will forgive me if I don't find *traffic* amusing."

"You're Yara Ashgrove," Casper said. It wasn't a guess. The guildmaster's face had flickered — genuinely flickered — when Casper mentioned the exiled heir, and exile apparently meant *here*, meant *waiting*, meant a hundred years of midnights.

"And you're the keeper." She said it like a diagnosis. Her gaze went over him the way he'd go over a lock — assessing, dismissive, recalculating. "They sent me a memory-thief. Of course they did. The Oath has a sense of humor; I helped write it, I should know." She turned away, gesturing at the frozen square, the weeping woman, the wheeling boy, the whole golden machine of the repeated day. "Do you understand what this is?"

"It's a loop. They're stuck in one day and they don't know it."

"They know nothing. That was the mercy we bought them." She said *we* like it cost extra. "A plague of forgetting was eating Vell alive — people losing their children's names mid-embrace, whole villages waking up strangers to themselves. My father's generation swore the Oath rather than watch it happen. The Oath hid the country inside its own remembering, wound every soul here into this single safe day, and set a price on the keeping of it." She looked back at him, coal-eyed. "The price is you. The codex says a keeper comes, and counts, and the counting holds the country together. I've spent a century making sure I'd recognize him."

"And how do you know it's me?"

"Because," Yara Ashgrove said, "the margins of the codex rewrote themselves around your name an hour before you fell, and I felt it from here — the first new writing in this country in a hundred years. Because you walked past that woman's grief without stealing it, which took more discipline than you know. And because—" she paused, and something in her voice shifted, some door opening a crack onto a room he wasn't invited into yet, "—because the Door in the wheat field has been closed since the day I sealed it, and tonight, when you landed, it breathed."

Behind her, across the rooftops, the great red sun hung exactly where it had hung since he arrived. It would hang there tomorrow. It would hang there for the baker, the fishmonger, the grieving mother, forever, until someone counted them free or the country finished forgetting itself.

Casper looked at the dusk, at the looped and innocent lives, at the woman who had waited a hundred years to say *you're late* to a stranger, and felt vertigo open under him like a second fall.

"I haven't agreed to anything," he said.

Yara Ashgrove smiled at last — thin, tired, and terrible.

"No," she said. "But your page is getting longer. Come and see."
