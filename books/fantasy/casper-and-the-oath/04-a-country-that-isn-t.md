## 4. A Country That Isn't

Yara had learned, over the course of a hundred years, that there were only two ways to watch a man fall out of the sky into your country. You could panic, or you could pretend you'd been expecting him.

She had been expecting him for a century, which made the pretending easy.

He lay in the wheat stubble at the edge of the Dusk Road, groaning softly, patting his own ribs as though checking whether they had survived the journey. The codex that had carried him was already gone — swallowed back into whatever place books went when they finished using people. Above them both, Vell's permanent evening hung low and amber, the sun nailed an inch above the hills like something a child had glued to the sky.

"Take your time," she said. "The ground isn't going anywhere. Nothing here goes anywhere."

The thief sat up. He had vault-keeper's hands — ink-stained at the nails, careful even now, even concussed by prophecy. His eyes did what all guild-trained eyes did when they woke somewhere wrong: they counted. The fence posts. The crows, frozen mid-wheel overhead. The light that refused to finish setting.

"You're Ashgrove," he said. Not a question. "You have her chin."

"I have my own chin," Yara said. "It merely resembles hers."

"Casper Vale," he said, and got up, and offered his hand as though they were meeting at a wedding instead of at the end of the world. She didn't take it. He withdrew it without offense, which told her he'd expected exactly that. "Keeper who counts," he said. "That's me, apparently. Written down a hundred years before I was born."

"Yes."

"And you're the one who's been waiting."

"A hundred years, four months, and eleven days," Yara said. "But who's counting." She turned and started down the road, because if she watched his face any longer she would see the question forming in it — *why would anyone wait a century* — and she wasn't ready to answer questions tonight. Or ever. "Keep up, Master Vale. I'll explain while we walk. There's less of Vell every year, and I'd rather not waste any of it standing in a field explaining things you could steal out of my head anyway. That's your trade, isn't it?"

He fell into step beside her. "I don't take from colleagues."

"No," she agreed. "You only take from strangers. And ledgers. And countries, if the ledgers ask nicely."

Behind them, the crows finished their wheel and began it again, exactly as before.

* * *

The Dusk Road ran through country that remembered being loved. That was how Yara thought of it, though she'd never said it aloud: stone walls built by patient hands, orchards heavy with fruit no one harvested, doorways carved with initials of couples whose grandchildren should have been old now and were not, because there were no grandchildren, because there was no time, because there was nothing here but the same amber evening poured over the same day, forever, like wax over a coin.

Casper walked slowly through it, touching things. A wall. An orchard gate. The carved lintel of a well house, where someone had once chiseled two names inside a heart.

"Mira + Tomas," he read aloud. "1889?"

"The year doesn't matter," Yara said. "Every year is 1891 here. The last one."

"Because of the plague."

She stopped walking. It was a small road, this stretch, hemmed by hawthorn, and when she turned to face him the low sun put her shadow long across the stones behind him, as though she were already leaving.

"What do you know about the plague?" she asked.

"That it eats memory," Casper said. "That's why the guild exists — or part of why. Forgetting isn't natural, whatever the philosophers say. Natural forgetting is slow. This was fast. Whole villages waking up as strangers to themselves. Mothers looking at children like furniture. It moved like fire moves, jumping from mind to mind, and nobody knew—" He caught himself. Looked embarrassed at how much he knew. "I keep the ledgers. I've read the incident reports from the border provinces. They're the worst reading in the archive."

"They're the truest," Yara said. "Go on."

"It reached Vell's borders in the winter of '91. Everyone knew what came next. And then—" He gestured at the amber sky, the frozen crows, the whole impossible afternoon of it. "Then Vell vanished. From the maps first. Then from the treaties, the trade routes, the histories. Then from minds. My own guild's records call this region 'the Unclaimed Flats.' There's nothing in the ledgers between the winter of 1891 and about forty years ago, when someone found a single page that bled ink and couldn't be explained." His eyes narrowed slightly. "That page is why I'm here. Someone wrote Vell back into existence inside a book of prophecies. Inside *memories*. This whole country—"

"—exists now only in recollection," Yara finished. "Yes. Stolen memories, traded memories, forgotten debts. Every scrap of Vell that survived the Oath survives because the Ashgrove Guild, in its infinite venality, happened to deal in exactly the commodity the plague consumed." She smiled, and knew it was not a kind smile. "My family's crime saved this place. Remember that, when I tell you the rest. We are villains and saviors in the same ledger line, and the ink hasn't dried on either."

They walked on. The hawthorn gave way to open downs, and on the brow of the hill stood the town: Harrow's End, three hundred rooftops, chimneys smoking the same smoke they had smoked for a hundred years. Church bells rang the six o'clock hour somewhere below, though it had been six o'clock since before Yara's grandfather was born.

"So tell me the rest," Casper said quietly. "The Oath. Your father swore it, the reports say. Aldous Ashgrove, guildmaster, entered Vell in the winter of '91 and was never recorded leaving."

"My father didn't swear it alone." The words came out level. She had practiced them level for a hundred years; practice holds even when everything else fails. "There were three signatories. My father. His partner in the guild — a man named Emeric Voss. And me."

Casper stopped dead on the road.

"You were a child," he said.

"I was sixteen," Yara said. "Old enough to hold a pen. Old enough to mean it. Which is what the Oath required — meaning. It wouldn't take the signature of someone who didn't understand what they were signing away. So my father made me understand. He spent three days making me understand. The plague was nine miles east and moving faster than armies move, and there was no cure, no ward, no wall that would hold it. Memory-fire doesn't respect stone." She looked out over the town, at the lamplighters beginning their rounds in streets that had seen ten thousand identical evenings. "So we went into the archive-cathedral, the three of us, and we opened the Codex — the true one, not the copy your vault keeps — and we asked it what it wanted. Because the Codex is older than forgetting, Master Vale. It remembers *everything*, and it can hold what mortals cannot. But nothing is given. Everything is traded."

"And it wanted Vell."

"It wanted Vell's *existence*. All of it. Maps, minds, histories, names — the whole weight of a nation's being-in-the-world, handed over as payment. In exchange, the Codex would fold Vell inside itself, into the space that memory occupies, where the plague couldn't reach. A country hidden in the one place the fire couldn't burn, because the fire *was* forgetting, and you cannot forget what has already been surrendered." Her voice stayed flat. She was proud of that. "Three million people, saved by ceasing to have ever been. No one would mourn them because no one could remember them. No one would grieve Vell because grief requires a name, and the name was the first thing to go."

"But the ledgers kept pieces."

"The guild trades in stolen years and borrowed names. Our vaults held fragments of Vell in a thousand memories — a soldier's recollection of these hills, a merchant's memory of the harbor at Lowmark, a grandmother's story about a town called Harrow's End. The Oath couldn't erase what had been *sold out* of Vell before the signing. Loopholes. My father was a guildmaster before he was anything else; he built the loophole in deliberately. Scattered Vell across ten thousand strangers' heads so that someday, someone might gather enough of it to find the Door."

Casper was staring at her with an expression she recognized and hated: the look of a man doing arithmetic with someone's soul. "And you signed," he said. "Sixteen years old. Why you? Why did it need you?"

"Because the Oath demanded a blood-price from the ruling line," Yara said. "And because my father believed — correctly — that I was the only one of the three of us who would still be alive when the debt came due." She let that sit between them on the road. "Emeric died within the decade. My father lasted thirty years. The Oath takes its toll from the signatories in order. I am the last name on the page, and I have been paying interest for a very long time."

"What kind of toll?"

"I can't leave," she said simply. "And I can't be *in*. The Oath erased me along with my country — my name went into the Codex with everything else — but it left me standing here, a keeper with nothing left to keep. I am a hole shaped like a person, Master Vale. The citizens don't see me unless I speak first. The houses forget I've slept in them by morning. I erased myself further, after — deliberately, piece by piece, so I could walk among the looped and learn the shape of the trap. There is not one soul in this country who remembers my face." She heard how it sounded and hated that too. "Don't look at me like that. Pity is just theft with better manners."

"I wasn't going to pity you," Casper said. "I was going to ask what happens when the toll finishes collecting."

And there it was — the question under all questions, the one she had circled for a century. Yara resumed walking, down toward the town gate, where the lamps were being lit by a man who had lit them at this hour every day since before her birth.

"When the last installment is paid," she said, "there won't be a Yara Ashgrove at all. Not erased-from-memory like the rest of Vell. Erased outright. The Codex keeps what it's owed, and I am the collateral on my own signature." She glanced sideways at him. "Which brings us to you, and to why a century of waiting ends tonight, with a thief falling out of a book into my wheat field."

* * *

Harrow's End received them the way it received everything: without comment. Citizens nodded to Yara as she passed — a courtesy the loop extended even to holes shaped like people — and looked straight through Casper, because he was new and new things slid off Vell like rain off oiled cloth. He noticed. Of course he noticed; he was guild-raised. She watched him catalogue it, file it, refuse to flinch.

She took him into the market square, where the fountain stood dry because it had stood dry for a hundred years, and where a notice board held proclamations in a hand she recognized as her father's, weathering without weathering.

"Here's what you need to understand about the Door," she said. "The Oath locked Vell's exits the way a miser locks a strongbox. One way out. One. A door that stands in a wheat field west of here — you've seen it, don't pretend you haven't, it's written on your face that you stole a peek at it in my guild's vault before you ever fell into my country." His expression didn't change, but something behind it did, and she allowed herself the small satisfaction. "The Door opens for almost no one. It opened for no one for a hundred years. It will open only for someone who is *recorded in both worlds* — written into the ledgers of the outer world *and* present in the inner one. Do you understand what that means? Every soul born in Vell was unwritten from the outside. They exist here alone. Half-recorded. The Door reads them as incomplete and stays shut. I am worse than incomplete — I am a signatory; the Door reads me as *collateral* and stays doubly shut. But you—"

"You're recorded out there," Casper said slowly. "Guild rolls. Ledgers. Incident reports. I'm in the books."

"You are in the books *and* in Vell. The first complete person this country has contained since the winter of 1891." Yara folded her arms. "The Door will open for you, Master Vale. That is the entire architecture of the prophecy your codex has been scribbling for a century. *The keeper who counts comes from outside and leaves by the one way.* The Codex wrote you into itself because the Oath's terms demand a witness from beyond the erasure — someone who can carry word of Vell's survival into the world that forgot it. That's the mercy clause my father negotiated with his dying breaths. Vell gets one messenger. One. Chosen by the ledgers, delivered by the books, gone through the Door with the truth in his keeping."

"And the truth is what, exactly? What do I carry out? 'Country hidden in a book, send help'? Who would believe it?"

"You carry the proof that it *worked*," Yara said, and heard her own voice crack, just barely, on the second word — a hundred years of level practice failing by a syllable. "You carry out the fact that three million people did not die screaming as their minds burned. They live. They laugh and bake bread and light lamps and bury — no. They don't even bury. They live the same gentle day forever, safe, sealed, *alive*, and the world outside may have forgotten them but they are not lost, they are *kept*—" She stopped. Breathed. The lamplighter passed them, nodding his empty nod, lighting lamps against a sun that would never set.

Casper was watching her the way thieves watch safes.

"There's more," he said. "You're giving me the shape of the plan but not its cost. Guild habit — I know how contracts hide the price. So I'll ask plain, Ashgrove. If the Door opens for me, why does the codex need a *keeper who counts*? Keepers stay. Messengers leave. Those are different words."

The bells rang six o'clock again, somewhere, impossibly, the same hour arriving twice.

"Because the Oath is a contract," Yara said, "and contracts require a signatory on each side. When Vell was hidden, the Codex took custody of the country — but custody needs tending. The loop must be wound. The dusk must be held. Someone must stand inside the erasure and keep the terms alive, or the whole arrangement decays and the plague finds what's left of Vell like water finding a cracked hull." She met his eyes and gave him the truth at last, the piece she'd been carrying like a stone in her chest since the moment she read his name in the margins a century ago — gave it to him clean, because she was many things, but she had never been a liar. Not to the people she was about to fail.

"For a hundred years, that keeper has been me. And my installments are nearly paid, Master Vale. When the Codex collects the last of Yara Ashgrove, the post stands empty. The prophecy says a keeper comes from outside. The ledgers chose you. The Door opens *outward* for a recorded man — but the Oath's fine print has always had a second clause, and I have read it ten thousand times, and I have never once managed to make it mean anything except what it says."

"Which is?"

"*The keeper who counts shall remain, that the count continue.*"

The words settled over the square like the amber light itself. Somewhere down a lane, a woman was calling a child home to supper, the same call, the same child, the same supper, forever. Casper stood very still, and Yara watched the arithmetic move behind his face — the same terrible arithmetic she had performed at sixteen with a pen in her hand.

"Well," he said finally, lightly, in the voice of a man buying time with counterfeit coin. "Good thing I'm a ledger-keeper and not a prophet. Let's go find this Door of yours. I'd like to see the fine print for myself."

Yara turned toward the western hills, where the wheat grew gold in a sun that would never finish setting, and did not tell him that she had already read the fine print — that she had read it so often the words lived behind her eyelids — and that the clause had a second half, and the second half was the reason she had waited a hundred years, four months, and eleven days, hoping the ledgers would choose anyone, *anyone at all*, other than the one person she was beginning to be afraid she couldn't bear to spend.

Behind them, unremarked, the lamplighter paused at the fountain, frowned at nothing, and lit a lamp that had never once, in a hundred identical years, needed lighting.
