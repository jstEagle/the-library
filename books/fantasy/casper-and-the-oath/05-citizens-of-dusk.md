## 5. Citizens of Dusk

The baker opened her shutters at the same moment the dusk settled, and Casper understood — with the particular nausea of a man who has spent his life noticing what others miss — that she had done this before.

Not yesterday. Not last week. *This same evening*, over and over, in a country that could not turn its face away from one hour of one day.

He stood across the lane from her shop, half-hidden behind a rain barrel that never rained, watching. The light in Vell did not come from any sun he could find. It pooled instead, amber and low, as though the whole sky were the inside of a lantern, and it never changed. No dawn had come since he fell through the codex. No true night either. Just this long bruised gold, this held breath, this single evening stretched like dough until it thinned to nothing.

The baker's name was Maren Ostrow. He didn't know how he knew that yet — he only knew it the way he knew the weight of a coin in his palm, instantly and without permission. Names came to him like that sometimes. It was the thief's gift: things people carried carelessly, he caught.

She was perhaps fifty, broad-shouldered, flour to her elbows even at dusk, working loaves into the oven by feel while her eyes kept going to the small chair by the window. The chair was empty. A child's coat hung over its back — grey wool, brass buttons, sleeves worn shiny at the cuffs where small hands had pushed through them ten thousand times.

On the counter beside the till sat a funeral wreath of white flowers, fresh as if cut that morning.

Casper watched her glance at the wreath, and watched her face do something terrible and ordinary at once: it crumpled, and then it smoothed, and then it crumpled again, each grief arriving as though for the first time, each forgetting following on its heels like a loyal dog.

"Excuse me," he said, before he could stop himself.

She looked up. Her eyes found him and there was no suspicion in them at all — only the mild, warm confusion of a woman interrupted mid-thought. "Oh! You'll be wanting bread, love. I've the last of the seed loaves, but they're good ones." She wiped her hands on her apron. "I'd have more tomorrow, but—" and here her voice caught, snagged on something just beneath the surface of her own mind, "—but we're burying my Liss today. So it'll have to wait."

"I'm sorry," Casper said. His mouth was dry. "When is the funeral?"

"Four o'clock," she said, gentle, as though explaining to a child. "At Saint Aldhelm's. You'd be welcome. Everyone comes." She smiled, and the smile broke his heart clean down the middle, because it was a smile that had been broken so many times it had learned to reassemble itself wrong. "Everyone always comes."

*Everyone always comes.* Present tense. Always tense.

Behind her, through the doorway to the back room, Casper could see a table set for supper. Two plates. One of them already had a small cloth napkin folded into the shape of a bird — the kind of thing a child does, or a mother does remembering a child — and beside the plate lay a hair ribbon, red as a wound.

"How old was Liss?" he asked quietly.

Maren's hands went still in the flour. "Nine," she said. And then, with the strange bright certainty of someone reciting scripture: "She'd have been ten at midwinter. She wanted a cake shaped like a swan."

Casper's vision blurred. He blinked it clear. In his trade you learned to keep your face still while other people's memories moved through you — joy that wasn't yours, terror that wasn't yours — but this was different. This wasn't a memory he'd been paid to carry. This was a woman standing in front of him, alive and breathing, drowning in the same inch of water every single day and calling it weather.

"Thank you for the bread," he said, because he could not say anything else.

"Two coppers," she said. Then she frowned, faintly, like a clock hesitating between ticks. "No — take it. Take it. Liss would want—"

And there it was again. The catch. The snag. Grief rising, cresting—

—and smoothing over, like water closing after a stone.

She handed him the loaf. She turned back to her oven. She glanced at the small chair. The coat on it shifted slightly in a draft that shouldn't exist, and Casper saw — he *saw*, with the clarity that had made him famous in certain rooms he could never speak of — the exact shape of the loop.

Every dusk, the funeral. Every dusk, the fresh wreath. Every dusk, the two plates, the red ribbon, the swan-shaped cake never baked. Every dusk, Maren Ostrow lost her daughter for the first time, and forgot by morning that there had ever been a morning, and began again.

* * *

Yara found him an hour later sitting on the steps of the well in the market square, the untouched loaf beside him, watching the townsfolk move through their eternal evening.

They were beautiful, he thought. That was the worst part. They were so *alive*. A fiddler played the same eight bars on a corner and the crowd around him laughed at the same joke in the music every time it came round, delighted anew, forever. Two old men argued chess over a board whose pieces neither of them ever actually moved. Children chased a hoop down the lane, shrieking with laughter that never wore thin, never tired, never grew up.

A town painted on the inside of a bell, ringing the same note.

"You followed me," Yara said, settling onto the step beside him. She said it without accusation, the way one notes the weather.

"You told me to stay close."

"I told you to stay *near*. There's a difference in Vell." She nodded at the square. "You've been watching."

"There's a baker," Casper said. "Maren Ostrow. Her daughter died — nine years old — and she buries her every evening. Four o'clock, Saint Aldhelm's. Everyone comes." He heard his own voice go flat and precise, which is what it did when feeling got too large to hold any other way. "She doesn't know. She can't know. By the time she reaches the churchyard she's already forgotten why she's carrying flowers, but her feet remember the way."

Yara said nothing. In the amber light her face was unreadable, but her hands — her hands were knotted together in her lap like rope under strain.

"The whole town's like that?" Casper asked.

"The whole country." Yara's voice was low, level, worn smooth as a riverstone from saying it. "Vell has been living this evening for a hundred years. Not the same hour — the Oath isn't cruel enough for that, or not clever enough. It gives them drift. Small variations. The fiddler plays a new song sometimes. The children grow, very slowly, and forget growing. But the anchor holds. The funeral. The dusk. The forgetting." She looked out at the laughing crowd. "It's mercy, Casper. That's what no one outside understands. The plague of forgetting was killing them *whole* — names first, then faces, then the will to eat, to breathe. My father's generation swore the Oath so their people would lose only one day at a time instead of everything at once."

"And the baker's daughter?"

"Liss Ostrow died in the last true winter, ninety-eight years ago. The plague took her in six days, and the sixth day it took her mother's memory of her, and Maren woke with a grief she couldn't source and a house full of small coats." Yara closed her eyes. "So the Oath gave her back the funeral. It was kinder than leaving her hollow. It let her bury Liss properly, once, and then it — kept her there. Where the love still worked."

Casper stared at her. "That's not mercy. That's—"

"Don't." The word cracked out of her sharper than anything he'd heard her say. Then, softer, with a hundred years of practice pressing it flat again: "Don't judge it until you've watched a country die the other way. I have. I watched it from the inside of the Codex."

He let it go. But his thief's eye had already been at work, and both of them knew it, and neither of them said *that* either.

* * *

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing that had been sitting in Casper's chest since the baker's shop, warm and horrible and familiar as a lockpick in his palm:

He could fix her.

Not the country. Not the Oath, not the century, not the great machinery of the prophecy grinding its slow gears beneath the dusk. But *her*. Maren Ostrow, fifty years old, buried her daughter every evening at four o'clock — he could take it. The grief. The loop of it. He'd lifted worse from harder minds. He had lifted a warlord's memory of his mother's dying face; he had lifted a bride's memory of the words she wished she'd said; he had lifted, once, for a fee he'd never forgiven himself, a hanging man's last prayer. Grief was heavy, yes, but grief was *simple*. It had a handle. It came out clean if you knew where to grip.

And what would she be, afterward? Free. Waking into an ordinary sorrow — a mother who had lost a child long ago, long enough ago that the wound had scarred over, that she could bake her bread and hang the coat somewhere out of sight and live. Live forward. Time moves for people who aren't nailed to one evening.

He could do it tonight. He could do it before the four o'clock bell.

He was still turning it over — feeling its weight, the way he felt the weight of any theft, testing the balance of it — when Yara spoke again, quietly, without looking at him.

"I can hear you thinking, keeper."

"Can you?"

"I've had a century of practice listening for it." Now she turned, and her eyes in the lantern-light were steady and terribly kind. "You're wondering what her grief weighs."

Casper said nothing.

"It weighs less than you think," Yara said. "That's the trap. Taking it would be easy. You'd barely strain. And she'd wake tomorrow free, grateful, light as a woman with no past — and the Oath would notice."

"The Oath notices a lot, apparently. It hasn't noticed me."

"It noticed you before you arrived. You're written in the Codex; you're the loudest thing in this country." She rose, brushing dust from her dark coat. "Listen to me, Casper. Vell runs on a ledger too. Did you think the Ashgrove Guild invented double-entry? The Oath keeps accounts. Every memory that belongs to Vell — every grief, every name, every funeral — is collateral. It's what the Codex holds against the country's survival. Steal one item and the ledger doesn't merely shrink. It *rebates*. The Oath compensates. It takes something else to balance the column."

"Takes what?"

"Something adjacent. Something loved." She said it flatly, but he saw her throat move. "Steal Maren's grief and the Oath might reach for the next nearest weight. The fiddler's music. The children's laughter. Another mother's daughter — some other family's anchor-day, pulled loose, and a hundred people suddenly grieving something they can't name, in a country with no room left for unaccounted sorrow." She met his eyes. "You wouldn't free her. You'd open a vein."

The four o'clock bell rang.

Casper hadn't noticed any church tower, but the sound rolled through the square all the same, bronze and patient, and he watched the townsfolk set down their cups and their games and turn, all together, all gently, toward Saint Aldhelm's — a congregation moving not in terror but in the soft automatic tenderness of habit. The fiddler laid his instrument down. The chess players rose. Children took their mothers' hands.

And Maren Ostrow came out of her shop with the white wreath cradled in her arms like a sleeping child, and her face was wet, and she was smiling, because everyone always comes, everyone always comes, and Casper stood up so fast the loaf fell into the dust and he didn't pick it up.

"I could take it," he said. His voice shook. "One theft. One. I've done it ten thousand times and the world kept turning. You're telling me this one country is too fragile to survive an act of kindness?"

"I'm telling you," Yara said, "that in Vell, kindness has a price column. Everything does. I know better than anyone alive." Something moved behind her face then — old, private, gated — and was gone. "Come. We walk her to the churchyard, we stand at the back, we do nothing. That's the discipline of this place. Doing nothing is the hardest work in Vell, and if you mean to survive here, you'd better learn it before your hands learn otherwise."

They walked at the rear of the procession, two strangers in a town of the same strangers, past shuttered shops and lamplighters lighting lamps that would never need putting out. At the churchyard gate, Maren paused. She turned — Casper could not have said what made her — and looked directly at him across forty heads.

And for one instant, one impossible, splintering instant, the loop *stuttered*. He felt it the way you feel a skipped stair. Her eyes widened. Her lips parted. The wreath trembled in her arms, and something moved up through her — ninety-eight years of buried dawns, all the mornings that never happened, the swan-cake, the tenth birthday, the wedding Liss never had, the granddaughters never born — all of it surging toward the surface like a drowned body finally letting go of the riverbed—

Her mouth formed a word. Two syllables. It might have been *help*.

Then the Oath closed its hand, and her face smoothed, and she smiled her broken reassembled smile, and turned, and carried her daughter into the churchyard to bury her for the ten-thousandth-and-something time.

Casper stood shaking in the lane.

"That," Yara said quietly beside him, "hasn't happened before."

"She almost woke."

"Yes." Yara was looking at him strangely now — not with warning but with something hungrier and more afraid, something that looked, to Casper's professional eye, very much like hope. "You touched the loop just by standing near it. Do you understand what that means? You're not just written in the Codex, Casper Vale. You're *reading it back*."

Far off, beyond the rooftops, beyond the wheat fields, in the direction of the archive-cathedral, a bell that Casper had not yet learned to fear began to ring — slow, iron, arrhythmic, counting something out.

Yara went rigid. All the hope drained out of her face at once, replaced by a stillness he recognized from guild veterans hearing the vault alarm.

"What is that?" Casper asked.

"The Blanks," she said. "They've started hunting."
