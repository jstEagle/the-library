## 8. What Thieves Keep

They came for him at the hour when Vell's dusk went thickest, when the amber light pooled in the streets like old honey and the shadows of the archive-cathedral reached all the way across the square.

Casper heard them before he saw them — a sound like paper being smoothed flat, over and over, patient and endless. He had learned the city's sounds during his three days of hiding: the baker's shutters at dawn-that-never-changed, the bell that rang six times no matter the hour, the fountain that ran backward. But this sound was new, and every instinct he owned stood up at once and told him to run.

He pressed himself into the alcove behind the census shelves, among records of citizens who had never been born and never would be, and watched them come through the cathedral doors.

There were four of them. They looked like clerks. That was the worst of it — they moved with the tired, dutiful gait of people late for a filing deadline, coats buttoned to the throat, ledgers tucked under their arms. But where their faces should have been there was nothing. Not shadow, not bandage, not absence-with-a-shape. Nothing, the way a page is nothing after ink has been scraped from it. The parchment of their skin stretched smooth over the place where a face goes, and Casper's stomach turned over with something worse than fear: recognition. He knew that smoothness. He had seen it on ledger pages where entries had been removed by guild discipline, erased so thoroughly that even the memory of the entry was gone, leaving only a faint gloss where the words used to live.

The Blanks moved down the aisles, trailing their fingers along the spines of books. Where they touched, the spines faded. Titles vanished. Whole volumes went pale as bone. One of them paused at the census shelf ten feet from Casper's alcove and began, methodically, to erase it.

* * *

He had hidden well, these three days. He was proud of how well he'd hidden, in the way of a man who has not yet understood that hiding is not the same as surviving.

The first day he had spent in the belfry, watching the loop turn beneath him like water under ice. The second day Yara had found him — found him too easily, he'd thought at the time — and given him the rules. *Don't touch the citizens. Don't touch the memories. Stay off the Oath's streets after the sixth bell.* She had said it in her flat, commanding way, guilt worn like a uniform, and he had obeyed because she knew this country and he did not.

But tonight the sixth bell had come and gone while he was deep in the cathedral's lower stacks, following a thread. His own ledger page — the one that had grown longer each hour he stayed, the one that now unrolled from his satchel in a pale ribbon whenever he dared look at it — had begun writing things he hadn't done. *The keeper counts,* it said, in his own handwriting, which was somehow the most frightening part. *The keeper counts what remains.*

And beneath that, in fresher ink still drying: *They are coming.*

So he had run for the archive, because if anything in Vell could hide a thief, it was a building made entirely of things the country had forgotten to forget. He had been wrong. The Blanks didn't search the archive. They simply erased it, aisle by aisle, shelf by shelf, walking the labyrinth of Vell's memory into nothing, and Casper understood with cold clarity that they weren't looking for him at all. They were removing the places he could be.

The Blank at the census shelf finished its work. The shelf was bare wood now, unstained, as if no book had ever leaned against it. It turned its smooth face toward the alcove.

Casper felt its attention like a hand entering his pocket.

He ran.

* * *

The cathedral's upper galleries were a warren of catwalks and hanging shelves, and Casper took them at a dead sprint, boots hammering planks that groaned with age. Behind him the sound of smoothing paper grew louder — patient, unhurried. They didn't run. Why would they? He was an entry in a book, and entries do not escape their pages; they are only turned to.

A catwalk ended at a ladder bolted to the wall of the scriptorium tower. He went up it two rungs at a time and came out into a circular room walled with glass cases, each one holding a single sheet of vellum under lock. Moonlight — real moonlight, the only true night Vell permitted, a brief silver hour the Oath hadn't thought to erase — fell through the tower windows and lit the cases like lanterns.

He recognized the room's purpose instantly, the way a locksmith recognizes a lock. These were confessions. Testimonies. The last statements of people who had known they were about to be forgotten, written down and sealed away because even the Oath's founders had flinched at total silence. Casper pressed himself into the narrow space between two cases and pulled out his tools.

Here is what a memory-thief knows, and what no one else understands: fear is just another kind of inventory. When you cannot fight and cannot flee, you take stock. Casper took stock. Four Blanks below, closing on the stair. One exit above — a window, a drop into the dark, likely broken ankles, certainly found. And one asset he had never used inside Vell itself, because Yara had forbidden it, because every theft unraveled the country a little more.

But the country was unraveling toward him either way. And the Blanks were coming up the stairs with their patient paper-smoothing sound, and Casper Vale had survived his whole life on one principle: *when cornered, take from your captor.*

He chose the lead Blank — the one whose footsteps had entered the tower first. He fixed his attention on it the way he'd fix his gaze on any mark, breathed once, and reached.

Stealing a memory is like picking a lock you can feel with your whole mind. Most minds are crowded — rooms full of furniture, doors, noise. He braced himself for the same struggle he always felt, the resistance of a life defending itself.

There was no resistance. There was nothing to resist.

He reached into the Blank and found a vault that had already been emptied, scoured clean, and at the very bottom, wedged into the last seam of the thing like a coin fallen behind a wall, a single memory remained — too small, too stubborn, too *heavy* to scrape out. Casper took it, and it poured into him like ice water, and he understood everything.

* * *

Her name was Maren. She had been the keeper of Vell.

She had been a baker's daughter from the low town, quick with figures, hired young because keepers were hired young — chosen for good penmanship and no family, because a keeper belonged to the country or no one. For eleven years she had sat in a stone counting-room beneath the archive and written down everything: names, debts, weddings, harvests, the exact color of the sky each evening, so that when the forgetting came — the plague of forgetting, the slow tide the founders had bargained against — someone would hold the country safe inside a human head.

She had loved a man who brought her bread. She had planned, in the careful margins of her ledgers, a garden. She had counted the days since she'd seen the sun rise, because the dusk never lifted, and the count was the only thing that proved time still moved.

And then she stayed too long.

That was the truth of it. No battle, no punishment, no dramatic failure. Just arithmetic. A keeper holds the country's memory, but a body holds only so much; every year the country poured more of itself into her, and she gave more of herself back, trading her own recollections to make room — her mother's voice, the bread-man's face, the garden — until the day she reached for her own name and found the shelf empty. She walked out of the counting-room and her face came off like wet paper, smooth and blank, and she picked up a ledger because her hands still remembered wanting one, and she joined the other smooth-faced clerks, and none of them remembered why.

The Blanks were keepers. Every one of them. Men and women who had held Vell inside themselves until there was no room left for *themselves*, and the Oath — efficient, obedient, indifferent — had repurposed what remained: hands that could carry ledgers, feet that could walk the aisles, and a hunger where a self used to be. They erased things because erasing was the last shape their emptiness could take. They hunted intruders because somewhere in the fine print of the Oath, in language no living person had read, it said they must.

Maren's last scrap of memory — the coin behind the wall, the thing too heavy to scrape — was this: standing at a window in the counting-room, watching a stranger arrive through a door that shouldn't exist, thinking, with the last of her name: *Run, keeper. Count yourself out before the country counts you in.*

Casper came back to himself kneeling on the scriptorium floor, shaking, his thief's senses raw and ringing. Below, the paper-smoothing sound had stopped.

In its place: footsteps on the stair. Unhurried. Patient. Coming up.

* * *

He did the cowardly, sensible thing. He climbed out the window.

The drop was shorter than he'd feared; the gallery roof caught him, rolled him, dumped him gasping into a rain gutter full of ash. He lay there listening to the tower above him go quiet — the Blanks reaching the empty room, finding nothing, beginning, without haste, without anger, to erase the confessions one case at a time. He thought of Maren's testimony dissolving under a smooth hand, and something in his chest clenched hard enough to hurt.

Then he got up and limped toward the quarter where Yara kept her preserved house, and he made a decision that sat in him like a swallowed stone.

He would tell her nothing.

It wasn't strategy — not at first. Strategy came later, dressing the choice in reasonable clothes: *if I tell her the keepers become Blanks, she'll stop trusting the plan; she'll panic; she'll send me out the Door alone.* But under the strategy, honest as a wound, was simpler arithmetic. Yara had carried this country for a hundred years. She had exiled herself from her own home, scrubbed herself out of its memory, worn her guilt like armor. If Casper told her what waited at the end of the keeper's road — that the road didn't end, it just emptied you onto it, forever, walking — he would be taking the last thing she had left.

Hope was a currency. He knew currencies. And some coins you don't spend, because spending them buys nothing but grief.

Besides — a colder voice said, the voice that had kept him alive in the guild's vaults for twenty years — besides, keeper. Look at your ledger page.

He looked. In the ash-light of the gutter, by the door of Yara's house, he unrolled the ribbon of his own record and read what had grown there since midnight. The handwriting was still his. The entries were not things he had done. They were things he had *counted*: the number of bells, the number of Blanks, the number of steps between the cathedral and here, the number of days — eleven years, said a line in fresh ink, *the average span* — and beneath it all, in letters growing clearer by the hour:

*The keeper counts, and the country keeps the count.*

His hands were steady. That frightened him most of all. Twenty years a thief, and his hands had only ever gone this calm before a job he had already decided to finish.

Inside the house, Yara had lit a lamp. He could see her silhouette moving behind the curtain, cataloguing, planning, believing — he could see it in the set of her shoulders — that she had finally found the answer after a hundred years, that this thief from outside was the loophole, the way out, the end of the Oath.

Casper folded his ledger page away, straightened his coat, and put on his best face — smooth, competent, unreadable, a face with nothing behind it.

He knocked.

The door opened, and lamplight spilled over him, and Yara Ashgrove looked at him with the first hope he had ever seen her wear, and Casper Vale smiled the smile he saved for marks, and said:

"We need to talk about the Door."

Behind his eyes, in the vault where thieves keep what they steal, Maren's last thought lay coiled and cold: *Count yourself out before the country counts you in.*

He had decided, very quietly, not to listen.
