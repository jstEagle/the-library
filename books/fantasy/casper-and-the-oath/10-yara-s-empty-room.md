## 10. Yara's Empty Room

The rain in Vell fell upward.

Casper noticed it on the walk from the archive-cathedral, thin silver threads lifting off the cobblestones and climbing back into a sky that couldn't decide whether it was dusk or something older. He had stopped flinching at impossible weather somewhere around the second hour of this world. That worried him more than the weather did. A thief who stopped noticing the strange things was a thief about to be robbed.

"Keep your hood up," Yara said without turning around. She walked ahead of him through streets that bent politely out of her way, as if the city itself remembered her and preferred not to look. "The Blank patrols double after the cathedral alarm. They'll be sweeping every threshold in the district."

"They didn't see my face."

"They saw your *page*." She said it flatly, the way you'd say *they have your blood*. "In Vell, that's worse."

He pulled the hood lower and said nothing. Behind them the archive-cathedral still glowed faintly against the dark, its thousand shelves lit from within like a lantern made of paper, and he could feel his ledger page growing there — another line added with every hour he breathed this country's air. He hadn't told her how fast it grew. He hadn't told her what the Blanks became, either, but that secret sat deeper, in a pocket of himself he'd sewn shut.

Yara stopped before a narrow house wedged between a shuttered chandler's and a wall of ivy that grew in perfect, deliberate rows — planted by someone who cared, maintained by no one for a hundred years.

"This is where I lived," she said. "Before."

She said *before* the way other people said a dead relative's name: carefully, once, and then not again.

The door wasn't locked. Nothing in Vell locked anymore; locks were for keeping things out, and nothing came to Vell except forgetting. Inside, the air tasted of dust and rosemary and something underneath both — a preservation-spell's ozone sweetness, the scent of a moment held too long. Lamplight bloomed obediently as they entered, though Casper saw no wicks to light. The house remembered being warm and kept performing the part.

"You'll sleep here tonight," Yara said. "The Blanks won't search a preserved residence. They consider it... finished." She hung her coat on a peg by the door, an act so ordinary it looked obscene in a country stuck at the end of its last day. "There's bread in the tin that isn't bread anymore. Eat it anyway if you're hungry enough."

"And you?"

"I have work." She was already moving toward the back hall, toward whatever century-old errand she carried instead of sleep. "Don't open the door at the end of the upstairs corridor."

Casper looked at her.

"Don't," she repeated, and climbed the stairs, and a door clicked shut above him like a full stop.

* * *

A thief's first instinct upon entering a stranger's home is inventory. Casper had never managed to kill it, and tonight he didn't try. While Yara's footsteps moved overhead in slow circuits — pacing, he thought, or reading aloud very quietly to herself — he catalogued the ground floor.

A kitchen with copper pots hung by size, each one polished to a dull refusal to tarnish. A sitting room with two chairs angled toward a cold hearth, one chair's arm worn smooth by a habitual hand. Books everywhere, most of them ledgers — of course, he thought, even here, even her — filled with entries in a hand he now recognized as hers: tight, upright, economical, each numeral drawn as if it might be audited by God.

And photographs.

That was the word his mind supplied, though these were memory-prints, the kind the Guild charged fortunes for: moments fixed in silver-and-salt, viewable only by touch. A man with grey in his beard and Yara's exact stubborn jaw stood laughing in most of them. Her father, then. The guildmaster's old partner, the architect of the Oath. In one print he held a girl of maybe ten on his shoulders while wheat bent golden around them both. In another, the same man stood beside a woman whose face Casper's eye slid off of, some protective blur baked into the image itself.

Every print held Yara. Every print held her father. Not one held anyone looking *at* her — no friends caught mid-laugh at her shoulder, no lover's hand at her waist, no neighbor waving from a doorway. She existed in this house only as someone's daughter, framed and frozen, adored and alone.

Casper set down the last memory-print with exaggerated care, as though it might report him.

Upstairs, the pacing had stopped.

* * *

He told himself he was checking for Blank-signs. Entry points. Escape routes. These were true statements the way a forged signature is true handwriting.

The corridor at the top ran past three doors. The first opened onto a child's room preserved mid-growth: chalk drawings on slate walls, a bed with a quilt stitched from wheat-colored scraps. The second was a study, its desk buried under Oath-drafts in two hands — the father's bold strokes interleaved with young Yara's careful ones, arguments conducted in margins across years.

The third door was at the corridor's end, and it was wrong.

Not locked. Not marked. Just *scrubbed*, the way a sentence is scrubbed when someone has gone through a document with a blade and ink-eater, leaving paper thin and ghosted. The wood around the frame had been polished by repetition — a palm pressed to it nightly, maybe, or a forehead. But the door itself bore no nameplate where the others had small brass ones. No wards. No scratches, no dents, no history at all, as if the door had been manufactured yesterday specifically to lead nowhere.

Yara had said *don't*. Casper Vale had spent thirty-one years treating *don't* as a unit of measurement: how much a thing was worth opening.

He turned the handle.

* * *

The room inside was empty the way a wound is empty.

Bare floorboards, pale rectangles on the walls where pictures had hung — not recently; the surrounding wallpaper had faded evenly around the absences, meaning the removals were as old as everything else in Vell. A window with clean glass looking out on the upward rain. And in the corner, a single object: a child's wooden horse, painted white, its paint worn to bare wood along the back where small hands would have gripped.

No bed. No trunk. No clothing, no letters, no hairpins, no boot-scuffs worn into the boards. He knelt and ran his fingers along the floor and found them — grooves, faint, where furniture legs had once stood. Something had lived here. Something had been *removed* from here so thoroughly that even the room had agreed to forget it.

Casper had broken into vaults, palaces, a cardinal's private reliquary. He knew every species of hidden room: the guarded, the trapped, the shameful, the treasured. This was none of those. This was a room that had been *edited*.

And he understood — standing in the pale rectangles' light, the little horse watching him with its one surviving painted eye — exactly what kind of edit removes a bedroom's contents but leaves the room itself standing.

An erasure of a person. Performed by that person.

"She erased herself," he said aloud, and his voice sounded obscene in the hush. "From her own house."

Because that was what the Oath required, wasn't it? She'd told him on the road, in her clipped, ledger-keeper's cadence: *I wrote myself out so I could move. A country can't hunt what it can't remember.* He'd nodded along like it was tactics. Strategy. The cold arithmetic of survival that thieves respect.

He hadn't thought about what it meant that the erasure started here. That before Vell forgot her, before its maps and bells and neighbors lost her name, she had gone room by room through her own childhood and unmade herself — lifted the portraits down, burned the letters, scraped the initials out of the window ledge (he found those grooves too, near the sill: two sets of letters, one set gouged away with something sharp, the survivor's half left intact because it belonged to someone else) —

He crossed to the windowsill and read the remaining letters by the failing light.

*E— & YARA*

Someone had carved their name beside hers. Someone who had loved her enough to carve it into wood, in a child's uneven hand. And Yara — fourteen? fifteen? already drafting oaths at her father's elbow — had taken a knife to her own half and cut herself out, leaving the other person's declaration orphaned on the sill.

*Casper,* he thought, *you are the worst kind of guest.*

He stayed anyway. Thieves always stay.

* * *

It took him an hour to find what remained, because whoever had done this was thorough, and Yara Ashgrove did nothing without thoroughness. Under a loose floorboard beneath the window — the same trick his own mother had used for hiding coins, which struck him with sudden, irrational grief — lay a shallow cavity containing three things.

A ribbon, green, gone grey at the edges.

A tooth, small, human, kept the way parents keep teeth.

And a scrap of paper folded eight times, soft as cloth from handling, though the handling must have happened a century ago and by someone else, since Yara could never have touched it again without undoing her own work. He unfolded it with a burglar's reverence.

Child's handwriting. The same hand as the windowsill carving.

*yara says when the plague comes we will all forget everything but i say i will NOT forget her and she laughed and said dont make promises to a country yara is the best at everything and tomorrow we are going to race to the mill and i am going to let her win because she likes winning better than i do*

No name signed. The writer had been forgotten so completely that even this testimony had lost its author — the ink itself seemed slightly blurred, as if the words were struggling to remember who had written them. Casper recognized the phenomenon from the Guild's deepest vaults: memories abandoned too long began to eat themselves, author first.

But the paper had survived. Because Yara had saved it — no. He frowned. Because Yara *couldn't* save it; touching it would anchor her back into Vell's memory. Someone else had tucked it under the floorboard. Someone who then, presumably, woke up one morning inside the loop and forgot why they'd hidden a treasure, and forgot the treasure, and forgot Yara entirely, and went on baking bread or forging nails for a hundred repetitions, carrying a hole shaped like a friend and calling the hole *nothing*.

Casper sat down on the floor of the erased room, in the pale rectangle where a bed had been, and held the scrap of paper, and felt something in his chest perform a maneuver he had no training for.

This was his trade's oldest rule: *never carry what isn't yours.* He had stolen griefs, guilts, first kisses, deathbeds — always for coin, always handing them onward, never letting one settle in him like sediment. A thief who keeps what he steals stops being a thief and becomes something heavier.

He folded the paper back along its eight creases.

Then he did the thing he would later understand was the first crack in the ledger of himself: he did not put it back.

He slipped it inside his coat, over his heart, in the inner pocket where he kept the one item he'd ever stolen for free — a pressed wheat stalk from a field in a vision, which suddenly made a different kind of sense, because the field in his first stolen glimpse had been burning, and burning fields mean someone tried to destroy the evidence, and evidence of *her* was scattered all through this country like seed.

*You have no one,* he thought, addressing her through the ceiling, through the floorboards, through a century of careful self-deletion. *You wrote yourself out of every book in Vell, and the people who loved you got folded into a loop that can't hold your name, and your father traded you like currency, and the guild that bears your family's title exiled you, and I am — what? What am I to you? The prophecy's delivery boy. The keeper who counts. A stranger with a hood up eating bread that isn't bread.*

But he was also, he realized, the only person in any world currently holding proof that Yara Ashgrove had been loved. The ribbon. The tooth. The racing-to-the-mill promise. It all fit in one coat pocket. An entire life's worth of being known, light enough to steal.

His eyes stung, and he let them, alone, briefly, in the empty room. Then he wiped his face with the heel of his hand, replaced the floorboard so precisely that not even the dust pattern broke, and took the horse.

He couldn't have said why. Later he would say it was tactical — an anchor-object, leverage, insurance. All lies, and competent ones, which is what made them convincing. The truth was simpler and stupider: the horse was the only thing left in the world that had touched her every day and still remembered the shape of her grip, worn into the paint along its back, and he could not stand for it to spend one more century in a room that had been taught to deny her.

He tucked it under his arm and went downstairs.

* * *

Yara was waiting at the bottom of the stairs.

She stood with a lamp in one hand and her face arranged into the particular stillness she used for interrogations, and behind her the front door stood open on the upward rain, and beyond the rain, far down the crooked street, three figures moved with the terrible smooth gait of clerks who had no faces to arrange into expressions.

"The Blanks passed the square twice," she said. "They're methodical. We have until they finish the district, no longer." Her eyes flicked to the shape under his arm, and something crossed her face — not recognition, recognition was scrubbed out of her, but the ghost of recognition, the ache where a memory used to attach. "What is that?"

"A wooden horse," Casper said. His voice came out steady. Thieves lie beautifully; it was the only gift he'd ever trusted. "I found it downstairs. Thought it might be worth something."

"It's worthless." She said it instantly, reflexively, the answer of someone protecting a wound she couldn't locate. Then, quieter, with the first fracture he'd ever heard in her: "Why does my chest hurt when I look at it?"

Because you erased yourself so well you deleted the reason you're sad, he almost said. Because somewhere under the Oath there's a girl who raced to the mill and liked winning, and you buried her alive in this house and called it strategy.

"We should move," he said instead.

She nodded and stepped out into the rain, and he followed, and as they went — quickly, hood to hood, fugitives in a country that was forgetting itself — Casper's free hand rested flat against his coat, over the folded scrap of paper, over the wheat stalk, over the small hard shape of the horse.

Three objects. One heartbeat's weight.

He was composing, without quite deciding to, the sentence he would eventually need: *I know who loved you, and I can prove it.* But the Blanks reached the end of the street just then, and Yara seized his sleeve and pulled him into the dark, and the confession stayed where confessions live in thieves — sealed, patient, accruing interest.

Behind them, in the house that pretended no one had ever lived there, the lamp Yara had left burning guttered once and went out, as if the building, too, had finally agreed to forget.

And in the inner pocket over Casper's heart, folded eight times, a child's promise to a country kept quietly refusing to die: *i will NOT forget her.*

Neither, it was beginning to appear, would he.
