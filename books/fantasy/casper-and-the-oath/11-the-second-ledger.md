## 11. The Second Ledger

There were two ways to enter my father's study: through the door, which no longer existed, or through grief, which did.

I chose the door anyway. Old habits die slower than countries.

The house stood where I had left it a century ago — preserved the way amber preserves the fly, beautiful and dead and honest about neither. Dust in Vell didn't fall so much as hesitate, caught mid-decision between settling and vanishing. I walked the hallway with Casper's footsteps behind me, lighter than mine, quicker, the walk of a man who had spent his life entering rooms he wasn't invited into.

"You said there was something you never showed me," he said. "When we passed this street before. You walked faster."

"I always walk faster here."

"That's not an answer."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

My father's study was at the end of the hall, behind oak that had been grown, not built — the Ashgroves planted their houses as seedlings and shaped them over decades, so that every doorframe curved like a held breath. I pressed my palm to the grain. It remembered me. That was the cruelty of Vell: it remembered everything except itself, and me least of all, because I had made sure of it.

"The Oath wasn't written on paper," I told him. "Everyone thinks it was. There are copies, hundreds of them, in the cathedral archive — beautiful copies, illuminated, bound in leather the color of wine. But those are translations. Interpretations. The original was written here, in this room, in a book my father kept under the floorboards, and the book was not paper at all."

"What was it?"

"Skin," I said. "Vell's skin. The country gave it willingly, back when giving still meant something here. A ledger needs a surface that can hold what's written on it, and ordinary paper forgets. This doesn't. This *keeps*."

Casper, being Casper, looked less horrified than professionally interested. "A ledger that keeps. In my trade we'd call that a competitor."

"Your trade stole from people one memory at a time. My family's trade stole from an entire nation all at once and called it mercy." I knelt and found the seam in the floorboards by touch, the way my father had taught me when I was nine years old, his hands over mine, his breath smelling of ink and clove smoke. *Someday this will be yours, little ash. Pray it never is.* "The Oath consumed things to power itself. Everyone knows that part. What no one knows — what I didn't know until forty years after I'd already lost everything worth losing — is that the Oath kept accounts."

The floorboard lifted.

Beneath it, in a cavity lined with lead — because even then, my father had feared something would read over his shoulder — lay a book the size of a paving stone. Its cover was pale grey, faintly veined, warm to the touch the way a sleeping animal is warm. Not metaphor. Warm.

Casper went very still beside me. Thieves know danger the way sailors know weather; it arrives in the body before the mind. "That's a ledger," he said quietly. "That's a real ledger. Like the ones in your guild vault."

"Worse than the ones in the guild vault. The guild ledgers record trades. Debts entered, debts paid. This one records *costs*." I lifted it out. It weighed more than its size explained, the way guilt weighs more than its causes do. "Every time the Oath erased something — a name from a sign, a face from a portrait, a year from a life — it had to put the stolen thing somewhere. Conservation, my father called it. Nothing in Vell is ever destroyed. It's only moved. Into here."

"And who pays the interest?"

I looked at him. He already knew. I could see him knowing, and hating that he knew, the way a man hates recognizing his own handwriting in a forged letter.

"The keeper," I said. "The keeper holds the account. That's why the position exists. That's why the Codex demands one. Someone has to carry the weight of everything the Oath took, or the whole structure collapses and Vell comes apart like wet paper. My father designed it that way. He designed a country that could only survive if one person agreed to be its grave."

*

We carried the ledger to the kitchen table, because kitchens are for hard truths. Mine had been scrubbed clean of me decades ago — Yara's chair gone, Yara's cup gone, the notch Yara's brother cut into the doorframe at age seven gone, smoothed over by wood that healed itself rather than remember her. Casper had seen that room upstairs, the empty one, and had the decency not to mention it again. But I watched him look at the kitchen the way he'd looked at it, cataloguing absences, and I decided that if he was going to help me commit the largest theft in the history of nations, he deserved to see the inventory first.

I opened the second ledger.

The first page was my grandmother's handwriting, small and severe. The entries were simple at first. *One well, district of Marrowfen. One bell, tower of Saint Elsin. One summer, 614 days of it, returned to the sky.* The Oath had started small — it had to, to learn its own appetite. A plague of forgetting was moving through Vell in those years, eating memories out of living heads, and the founders had made their terrible arithmetic: give the Oath the country's past, and it would leave the country's people their present. No one would remember Vell's history, but everyone would survive to have a tomorrow.

What they hadn't understood — what *I* hadn't understood, at nineteen, holding the pen while my father dictated — was that the Oath would keep eating after the plague died. Erasure is easier than restraint. And every erasure needed somewhere to go.

The handwriting changed down through the pages. My grandmother. My father. Then, halfway through, a hand I knew almost as well as my own and had never once let myself think about in ninety years.

Casper noticed before I could stop him. His finger hovered over the page. "Whose writing is that?"

"My father's partner," I said. "His co-author, if you want the truth. The Oath has two clauses that matter — the clause that takes, and the clause that keeps. My father wrote the taking. This man wrote the keeping." I turned the page with more care than the gesture required. "He was the last keeper. Before the position sat empty for a hundred years."

"The one whose vacancy I'm filling."

"The one whose vacancy the Codex has been advertising for a century, yes. It doesn't advertise twice. When a keeper dies or fails or is emptied, the Oath waits. It can wait longer than patience. It waited for someone recorded in both worlds — a man with entries in the guild ledgers *and* a name the Codex had begun to write around. You, Casper Vale. You were not chosen. You were *matched*, like a key to a lock that was cut a long time ago."

He absorbed that with the flat expression thieves wear when the job turns out to be bigger than the briefing. Then his eyes went back down the page, and I watched him do what he does — read a ledger the way other men read faces.

"These aren't just objects," he said slowly. "These later entries. Look." He turned toward the final third of the book, where the script grew cramped, hurried, the ink thinning as though the writer were running out of himself to write with. "*One voice, the singer Aldous Renn, taken mid-song. One grief, unnamed, belonging to the baker's wife. One promise, made at a well, broken and therefore free.*" Casper's throat worked. "It's not recording what the Oath erased anymore. It's recording what the *keeper* fed into it. To keep it running. He was paying the interest himself."

"Yes."

"In pieces."

"Yes."

"How long—"

"He lasted eleven years," I said. "The average is three. My father lasted four and wrote about it afterward like a man describing a war he'd won, which tells you everything about my father. But this man—" I stopped. Ninety years of not thinking about him, and my voice still betrayed me on the first try. I tried again. "This man believed the keeper's job wasn't to endure the cost but to *witness* it. Every piece he surrendered, he surrendered knowingly, and he wrote each one down first, so that the thing being taken would exist in words even after it stopped existing in him. He said a memory written down honestly can't be fully stolen. It leaves a shadow. A shape. He was wrong, mostly. But it was a beautiful wrongness, and I loved him for it, in the way you love a teacher, which is to say completely and without ever saying so."

The kitchen was quiet. Somewhere outside, the dusk that never ended leaned against the windows.

Casper turned the pages gently now, one by one, reading a man's disassembly in reverse — the keeper growing thinner on the page as the Oath grew fatter, entries shortening from sentences to phrases to single words. *One lullaby. One river, remembered cold. One mother's hands.* And then, near the end, the handwriting deteriorating into something barely legible, letters leaning like drunks:

*One name, mine, given freely.*

And beneath it, in a final line pressed so hard the skin of the ledger had scarred:

*tell her.*

*

Casper read it aloud. I think he needed to hear it in a voice that wasn't mine, because mine had stopped working.

"*Tell her.*" He looked up. "Tell her what?"

"I don't know."

"You never found out?"

"There was no one left to ask." I kept my voice level. Level voices are a discipline, like archery; you practice until the shaking happens somewhere no one can see. "By the time I found this ledger, the keeper had been gone sixty years. My father was gone. The Oath had sealed the borders — nothing left Vell, not even questions. And 'her'—" I laughed, briefly, and hated the sound. "Do you understand how many women that could be? A country full of them. A sister. A daughter. A woman he loved and never told. The last keeper emptied himself into a machine to keep three hundred thousand people breathing, and his entire remainder, the very last thing he owned, was two words addressed to somebody, and I don't know who, and I never will."

"Unless she's alive."

I stared at him.

"Vell doesn't kill anyone," Casper said. He had that thief's cadence now, the one that means the lock is almost open. "You told me yourself — conservation. Nothing destroyed, only moved. If the Oath couldn't destroy people, it couldn't destroy her either. She'd be in the loop. Walking around. Buying bread. Living the same day forever, same as the baker, same as everyone." He tapped the scarred line of ink. "She's here, Yara. Whoever she is. She's been here the whole time, carrying whatever he meant to tell her, and she doesn't even know she's waiting."

The hope hit me like cold water — sudden, total, painful. I had spent a century rationing myself to zero hope precisely so that nothing could be taken from me, and here was this pickpocket from another world cracking open the vault I'd welded shut, holding up the one thing I'd forbidden myself to want.

"She might be dead," I said. "Time passes strangely inside a loop. People fray."

"People fray," he agreed. "But you said it yourself. Written honestly, a memory leaves a shadow. So does a message. Two words, pressed that deep?" He shook his head slowly. "Men don't press that hard for strangers."

I closed the ledger. My hands were not entirely steady, and I let him see it, because some debts are paid in visibility.

"When we break the loop," I said, "when we wake them — I'll find her. I'll stand in whatever square she wakes up in, and I'll say: someone loved you enough to spend himself down to nothing keeping you alive, and his last words were for you, and I've carried them a hundred years, and I am sorry they're late."

"And if 'tell her' isn't a message," Casper said quietly. "If it's a request. Tell her *something*. Something he never got to say."

"Then I'll say that too. Word for word, if I can find it. And if there are no words—" I touched the cover of the second ledger, warm as a sleeping animal, patient as a grave. "Then I'll tell her what kind of man writes his own name down before he gives it away. That much, at least, I can testify to."

Casper was silent a moment. Then, in the tone of a man opening a door he suspects leads off a cliff: "Yara. If the last keeper fed himself into the Oath piece by piece — and if the Oath conserves everything it takes — then where did his pieces *go*? They weren't released. The loop's still running. Which means somewhere inside this country, there's a storehouse with eleven years of a man in it. His songs. His griefs. His lullabies. Maybe—" his eyes came up, bright and terrible, "—maybe his unfinished sentence."

I felt the floor of the world tilt, very slightly, the way it does before an earthquake decides whether to happen.

"The Blanks guard the cathedral archive," I said slowly. "Faceless clerks. Empty clerks. I always assumed they were constructs. Tools the Oath grew for itself." My mouth had gone dry. "But the Oath doesn't grow tools. It *conserves*. It repurposes. It takes a thing and moves it somewhere useful."

We looked at each other across the second ledger, and I watched the same conclusion arrive in his face that had arrived in mine, wearing the same horror, dressed for different funerals.

Somewhere in the dark above us, in the cathedral at the heart of Vell, a hundred faceless clerks filed silently through the stacks — and one of them, perhaps, still had two words folded away inside whatever remained of him, waiting a hundred years for a delivery that had never come.

"We need to get into the archive," Casper said.

"I know," I said.

"Sooner rather than later."

"I know," I said again, and reached for my coat.
