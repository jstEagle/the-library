## 12. A Day Worth Stealing

The rule was the first thing they taught you in the Ashgrove Guild, before locks, before ledgers, before you were ever allowed to hold a memory that wasn't yours: *a thief never gives back.* Not because giving was dangerous — though it was — but because giving meant the memory had mattered to you, and anything that mattered could be used against you. Guildmaster Vess said it like a proverb. Casper had repeated it like one for eleven years.

He stood outside the bakery on Wren Street at dusk-that-was-not-dusk, and broke it.

The shop looked the same as it had every evening he'd passed it this week and every evening it had passed for a hundred years. Warm light in the windows that came from nowhere, since Vell's sun never quite set and never quite rose. The smell of bread that no one baked anymore, rising from ovens that remembered hands. And in the doorway, sweeping a step that never gathered dust, Marta Coven — the baker with flour in the creases of her knuckles and her daughter's funeral waiting for her tomorrow morning, the same funeral she had buried herself inside every single day since the Oath closed over this country like a lid.

Casper knew her grief's exact weight. He had audited it, in a manner of speaking. He'd watched her knead dough with shaking hands while her mouth moved through words she didn't know she was saying: *Ilse, Ilse, mind the proofing, don't let it catch, my love, my girl—*

He should have kept walking. Yara had been explicit. Every theft from Vell pulled a thread, and every thread was holding up something. He'd felt it himself when he took small things — a soldier's parade, a child's first snow — each time the whole country shuddered like a sleeper disturbed by a draft.

But tonight Marta Coven had dropped her broom in the middle of the step, sat down on the cold stone, and begun to cry without knowing why, and Casper Vale — keeper of impossible ledgers, thief of things no one else would miss — found he did not have it in him to walk past.

*One day,* he told himself. Not a theft. A gift. There was no entry in any ledger for that. That was exactly what frightened him about it.

* * *

He went back to the ruined house they sheltered in — Yara's house, though she wouldn't call it that — while she was out scouting the Door road, and he sat in the kitchen where her family had once eaten meals no one remembered, and he opened his own chest the way a thief opens someone else's.

This was the part no guild training covered. Taking from yourself.

His memories lived in him the way coins live in a purse — sorted by weight, by shine, by how often his fingers found them in the dark. He laid them out along the counter of his mind, one by one, and refused them all until he reached the bottom of the purse, where the true ones lived. The ones that weren't stolen. The ones that were *his.*

There weren't many. Thieves are poor in their own currency.

His mother's face — he touched it and flinched away. Not yet. Not that one. He wasn't sure he had a right to spend it even now, and besides, if he gave that, there would be nothing left of him but the ledger.

Then: a summer afternoon, age nine or ten, before the Guild found him in the workhouse line. A fair on the river. His mother had bought him a paper boat painted blue, and they'd raced sticks against it under the willows, and she'd laughed — not politely, not tiredly, but with her whole chest, the laugh of a woman who had forgotten to be sad for an entire afternoon. He could smell the river mud. He could feel the string of the boat cutting a little groove in his finger. He could hear her say, *Winners get the last honey cake,* and lose anyway, and give it to him regardless.

That one. It was the truest thing he owned.

His hands were steady as he worked, which surprised him. In the guild vaults, when he lifted a memory from a sleeping stranger, his hands always trembled slightly — the body's objection to theft. But peeling this out of himself felt less like stealing and more like paying a debt he'd owed someone all along. He folded the afternoon down into itself, compressed the river and the willows and the laugh into a coin-sized gleam, and held it in his cupped palms, warm as fresh bread.

It already hurt. He noticed that distantly, the way you notice weather. Where the memory had been there was now a smooth pale absence, like a tooth socket. He tested the shape of it with his attention and found nothing — no boat, no willow, no honey cake. Just a fact without furniture: *I was loved once, I think.*

Gods. Was that worth it?

Marta Coven sat down on her step every dusk and wept without knowing why. Yes. Yes, it was worth it. Some arithmetic needed no ledger.

He carried the little light of it through streets gone violet with perpetual evening, past lamplighters relighting lamps that were never lit, and knocked on the bakery door.

* * *

She answered like a woman expecting bad news — everyone in Vell answered doors that way, he'd learned, braced for a blow that had already fallen and would fall again tomorrow.

"Forgive the hour," Casper said. "You're Marta Coven."

"I'm—" She frowned, and he watched the name land strangely on her, a coat that fit but hadn't been worn in years. "Yes. Who's asking?"

"A traveler." The lie came easily; lies were his native tongue. "I've come a long way, and I'm told your bread is the best in Vell."

"There's no bread." Her voice cracked down the middle. "There's no — I keep meaning to bake. I keep meaning to." She looked at her own hands as if they belonged to a stranger who had disappointed her. "Come in anyway. It's cold."

Inside, the ovens ticked with remembered heat. He asked her about her daughter without asking — mentioned a girl he'd supposedly seen at market, dark-haired, quick-laughing — and watched Marta's face do something terrible and tender, the way a door does when wind catches it.

"Ilse," Marta whispered. "My Ilse. Why can't I—" Her hand pressed flat against her breastbone. "She's gone. I know she's gone. I bury her every morning and by supper I've lost her again, and I can't even remember losing her, I just know the hole is there, I can feel the shape of it—"

"Marta." He took her flour-rough hand and turned it palm-up, and set the memory in it.

Not physically. Physically there was only a faint warmth, a shimmer like heat off summer stone. But she gasped as if he'd pressed a coal into her skin, and her eyes went somewhere else entirely.

Casper watched her take the river afternoon. Watched her become, for the length of its unfolding, a nine-year-old's mother on a bank under willows — no. That was wrong, that was the flaw in the gift, it was *his* mother in the memory, his mother's laugh, his mother's voice saying *winners get the last honey cake.* Marta was hearing a stranger. But grief doesn't check provenance. Love doesn't ask whose face it wore first. She laughed — Marta Coven, who buried her daughter daily, laughed with her whole chest in the middle of her empty bakery — and tears ran freely down into the flour creases, and she said:

"Oh. Oh, I remember days like that. I had days like that with her. I thought I'd lost them too."

"You haven't lost them," Casper said quietly. "They're still yours. All of them. They're just — held somewhere. Held for safekeeping." Like everything in Vell. Like the whole country, filed and shelved and gathering dust in the dark. "This is one of them. On loan."

She gripped his hand then, hard enough to hurt, flour and bone and desperate strength. "Who *are* you?"

"Nobody important," said Casper Vale, keeper of the ledgers, liar. "A man settling a debt."

* * *

The stutter began as a sound.

Not a loud sound — a structural one, the way a house makes noise at night when it settles. Somewhere beneath Wren Street, beneath the whole city, something vast shifted its weight. The perpetual dusk flickered. Once. Twice. For half a heartbeat the light through the bakery window was gold instead of violet, honest late-afternoon gold, and Casper saw the street outside as it must have looked a hundred years ago: people walking with purpose, awnings snapping in real wind, a dog trotting after a cart with its tail up.

Then the dusk snapped back like a bone setting, and the country *shivered.*

He felt it in his teeth. Felt it in the ledger-sense the codex had grown in him during his weeks here — the awareness that Vell was a text and he was reading it. Whole paragraphs of the day lurched, reordered themselves, tried twice to happen. Across the square, the lamplighter relit his lamp and then stood staring at it, frowning, because he had suddenly done two evenings in a row and some animal part of him knew it. A woman carrying water stopped mid-step and looked at the jug in her hands as though she couldn't account for it. An old man in a doorway said, aloud, to no one: "*Wasn't I just somewhere else?*"

Waking. All across the district, citizens of Vell were waking — briefly, groggily, the way sleepers surface between dreams — and looking around at their own lives with the dazed offense of people finding strangers in their houses.

And in the bakery, Marta Coven fell to her knees.

"It's *her*," she sobbed, both hands over her mouth. "It's Ilse, I can see her, I can see the whole day — the fever, the doctor, the — oh gods, the little coffin, I carried — I *carried*—" The gift had torn the loop open for her specifically; the borrowed afternoon had been a key turning in a hundred-year lock, and everything behind it came flooding out at once. Every funeral. Every forgetting. Every morning she'd woken with the wound fresh and no scar to show for it. "How many times? How many times have I buried her? Tell me. *Tell me how many times.*"

"I don't know," Casper said, and it was the truest thing he'd said all year. "I'm so sorry. I thought I was giving you a kindness."

"You gave me my daughter." She looked up at him from the floor, tear-streaked and blazing, and she was more alive than anyone he'd seen in Vell. "Do you understand? For one minute I had her back. One minute. You gave me that. Whatever it costs — whatever *you're* going to pay for it—" She seized his wrist again. "Don't let them put me back to sleep. Promise me. Promise me you won't let me forget her again."

Outside, the shiver was spreading. He heard voices in the square now, dozens of them, overlapping — confusion, fear, someone calling a name over and over, someone else laughing wildly at a lamp, someone screaming. The dusk overhead rippled like water with a stone thrown in. Far off toward the archive-cathedral, bells that had never rung in his time here began, hesitantly, to ring.

And far off in another direction — a direction that made the hair on his arms rise — came a different sound. Thin. Papery. The whisper of many pages turning in unison, and beneath it footsteps that made no sound at all but left frost-shaped absences on the cobbles where they passed.

The Blank had heard the stitch tear. The Blank were coming to mend it.

* * *

Yara found him in the square, and the look on her face stopped him three steps into flight.

She'd run — he could see it, her braid half-collapsed, breath ragged — and she'd arrived already knowing. Of course she had. She'd spent a century learning to read this country's tremors the way sailors read swells.

"What did you do?" Not shouted. Worse than shouted. Quiet, in the way of a woman conserving fury for accuracy.

Casper opened his mouth to lie. It was his craft; he could have lied beautifully, could have constructed a story with load-bearing details and a plausible villain. But Marta was standing in her bakery doorway behind him, weeping her daughter's name like a bell being struck, awake and grieving and gloriously, catastrophically alive — and Yara's eyes went to her, and widened, and something in Yara's face broke sideways into horror and longing so naked that lying to her would have been a kind of blasphemy.

"I gave her a day," Casper said. "One day. With her daughter. It cost me—" he almost said *nothing*, the reflex of every thief, and caught it, and hated himself for the reflex. "It cost me a true memory. Mine. Not Vell's. Nothing of the country's. I checked the arithmetic before I did it, Yara, I swear I thought—"

"You didn't think." She crossed the distance between them, and her voice finally cracked loose from its leash. "You *felt*, which is worse. Don't you understand what you've done? The loop isn't a cage, Casper, it's a *tourniquet*. Those people are bleeding out a hundred years of grief, and the loop is the only thing keeping them from drowning in it all at once. You just cut a hole in it. Do you see?" She grabbed his sleeve and turned him to face the square.

He saw.

The waking was spreading outward from Wren Street like ice cracking off a pond. Citizens stood frozen mid-task everywhere he looked, faces slack, then crumpling, then twisting through expressions too fast to name — recognition, loss, terror, joy, all of it arriving at once, a century of undelivered feeling hitting them in a single wave. A woman sank down beside her water jug and began to rock. Two men embraced each other and cried without seeming to know why. Somewhere a child woke crying for a parent, and Casper's stomach clenched, because he didn't know if the parent would wake remembering love or remembering loss, and neither outcome was mercy.

"They'll settle," Yara said, and now her voice shook. "Most of them will fold back into the loop — it has the gravity of habit, it always has. But some won't. Some are *awake* now, permanently, and every awakened citizen is a place where the Oath has torn, and the Codex sends the Blank to every tear." She turned on him. "How many did you wake? Hundreds? A thousand? You bought one woman one afternoon and the invoice is going to be written in—"

"In what?" Casper snapped back, and his fear came out as anger, the way it always did. "In what, Yara? Say it. In *people*. That's what you mean, isn't it? The Blank will empty whoever tore the page, the way they emptied every keeper before me, and you're angry because I've shortened the clock." He stepped closer. "Well, count it honestly, then. Count what I bought. That woman buried her daughter every morning for a hundred years. I gave her one hour of having her back. If that's a crime, fine — arrest me. But don't stand there and tell me it was *wrong*, because I watched her laugh, Yara. First time I've heard anyone in this country laugh at something that hadn't happened yesterday forever."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The bells rang their hesitant, rusty peals. The dusk rippled overhead like the underside of a held breath.

"That laugh," Yara said finally, very quietly, "is going to bring the entire Blank Court down on this district. And they don't arrest thieves, Casper. They *erase* them. I've watched it." Something moved behind her eyes — a century of watching it — and she shut the door on it fast. "We have minutes. Maybe less. Get Marta out the back way toward the cathedral, and pray the crowd covers your trail, because if they find the source of the tear—"

The frost-absences appeared at the far end of the square. Four figures. Five. Six. Tall, gray, featureless as unprinted pages, moving with the unhurried gait of clerks who know the paperwork is already signed.

"—they find *you*," Yara finished.

The nearest Blank raised its head. It had no eyes, and yet Casper felt its attention settle on him with the precise, dreadful weight of a finger stopping on a ledger line.

On his line. His page. The one that grew longer every hour he stayed.

The Blanks began to cross the square.
